## 20. Broadcast

Marcus Okafor had produced true-crime podcasts for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned exactly one universal law: the episode you're proudest of is the one that will ruin your sleep. So it was no surprise at all that on the night the final installment of *The Borrowed Will* went live, he sat alone in the studio at 11:47 p.m., watching the upload bar crawl like a man watching a fuse.

"Thirteen percent," he told the empty room. "Great. Terrific. Take your time."

The studio smelled like cold pizza and Sebastian's coffee, which was to say like ambition and mild despair. Her chair was pushed back from the mixing desk at her usual reckless angle, as if she'd been launched out of it by an idea. She'd gone home two hours ago — actually home, not the office couch — after he'd physically stood between her and the master file until she promised to let him press the button. It had taken some doing. Sebastian Quintana did not delegate detonations.

But this one, he'd insisted, was his to light. Not because she didn't care. Because she cared too much, and someone had to hold the match steady.

The final episode ran ninety-four minutes. Wren's voice ran through it altered — pitched down, time-smeared, layered under ambient recordings of tide and gull noise that Marcus had spent three days perfecting — but even disguised, there was something in it that got under the skin. A steadiness. The voice of a woman who had spent thirty years being nobody on purpose and had finally decided to be somebody for exactly one hour.

They'd recorded through the night, all four of them — Sebastian, Wren, Marcus at the board, and once, unexpectedly, Reyes, who had shown up at 3 a.m. with bad coffee and better questions and ended up sitting in the corner taking notes nobody asked her to take. Julian's corroboration was in there too, taped from his hospital bed, his voice thin but unflinching: *"My mother built the lie. I maintained it. I don't ask anyone to forgive me. I only ask you to understand what fear does to a house."*

And then Vivian's story, told whole for the first time. The falsified death certificate. The violent father. The staged disappearance. A seventeen-year-old girl who had looked at the options available to her — run and be hunted, or stay and be erased — and chosen a third thing: become a ghost on her own terms, with her little sister tucked under her arm.

Ninety-four minutes. Marcus had listened to it maybe forty times and it still made his chest hurt at minute sixty-one, when Wren described the last thing Vivian ever said to her: *"You remember the sea, Wrennie. Whatever name they give you, you remember the sea."*

The upload bar hit one hundred.

Marcus hovered over the publish key longer than any grown man should hover over a single keystroke. Then he thought about Tom Reilly, dying in a trailer park with thirty years of guilt and one good decision left in him. He thought about Ada Marsh and her hymnal. He thought about a bicycle carried toward a boathouse by someone small.

He pressed it.

* * *

Detective Ilsa Reyes heard the episode the way she heard most things these days: on her phone, in her car, parked outside the precinct at seven in the morning because going inside meant answering forty emails about why she'd spent department resources auditing a thirty-year-old missing persons case.

She'd had advance access, technically. Sebastian had sent her the final cut three days ago with a note: *Tell me if anything jeopardizes the prosecution. You have veto power.* Reyes had used it exactly once — a detail about the father's business partner that could taint a witness pool — and Sebastian had cut it without argument, which had surprised them both.

Hearing it finished, though, was different. Hearing it as the public would hear it.

By the time she'd driven the six blocks to her desk, the world had already changed shape. Her phone buzzed against her hip in the specific arrhythmic way that meant group texts multiplying. The department's media liaison was already standing outside her lieutenant's office wearing the expression of a woman watching a weather report for a hurricane with her own street name.

"Reyes," the lieutenant called. "A word."

"A word," she agreed, and braced.

What she expected: discipline. What she got: the lieutenant sliding a printout across his desk — a news alert, timestamped nine minutes ago. **HEIR TO ASHCORNE FORTUNE ARRESTED IN COLD-CASE INQUIRY.**

"They picked him up at six this morning," the lieutenant said. "Federal charges reopened. Fraud, obstruction, and they're talking to the state about the '94 evidence tampering. His lawyers are already screaming, but—" he tapped the printout "—ninety-four minutes of audio tends to drown out screaming."

"I didn't leak anything," Reyes said. "Everything in the episode came from public records or protected sources."

"I know." He leaned back. "That's what worries me. You did this by the book and it still worked. Do you know how offensive that is to this department?"

It was the closest thing to a compliment he'd given her in nineteen years, so she took it and left before he could reconsider.

* * *

By noon, Marcus's phone had achieved a state he privately called "hornet theology" — buzzing constantly, stinging unpredictably, humming with purpose.

Downloads for the finale passed two million in fourteen hours. The previous record for the show had been set by the Meridian case, Sebastian's celebrated closer, and it had taken that one a month to hit what *The Borrowed Will* did before lunch. Marcus watched the analytics dashboard the way other men watched sports, shouting numbers at Sebastian whenever she walked past his desk.

"Two point four million!"

"Mm."

"'Mm'? We broke the internet, Sebastian. There are think pieces. There are already think pieces about the think pieces."

"There will be worse ones by Friday." She dropped into the chair across from him, looking like a woman who hadn't slept but had, for the first time in months, slept *well*. "Show me the worst one."

He showed her. It wasn't hard to find. The headline read: **WHO EDITED THE TRUTH? QUINTANA'S CURATED CONFESSION SPARKS ETHICS FIRESTORM.**

"The gist," Marcus summarized, "is that you didn't give the audience everything. That Wren chose what to include. That you, quote, 'played documentarian instead of prosecutor,' end quote. One guy on Twitter is calling it 'true crime with a chaperone.'"

Sebastian read it twice. Marcus watched her face do something complicated — a flicker of the old hunger, the part of her that wanted to own every fact in every room — and then settle into something calmer.

"He's right," she said.

"He's — wait, what?"

"He's right. I curated it. Wren curated it harder. There are things in that night we left out because including them would have put a target on a woman who's been a target her whole life." She shrugged. "I'd do it again. But he's right that it's a choice, and choices get audited."

"You're not going to defend yourself publicly?"

"Oh, I'll defend myself. Just not today." She stood, stretched, and paused at his desk on her way out. "Today I'm going to sit somewhere quiet and feel weird about winning. Tomorrow I fight."

"That's very healthy," Marcus said. "I hate it. Also, Julian Ashcombe gave a statement from the hospital. It's everywhere. Want to see?"

She took his phone. Read. Marcus watched her eyes move down the screen and stop.

*"For thirty years I kept my mother's secret because I was afraid of her, and then afraid of myself. Ms. Quintana's series tells the truth as it happened. I corroborate every word of it. My sister Vivian was the bravest person this family ever produced, and the world owes her a memorial, not a mystery."*

Sebastian handed the phone back slowly.

"Huh," she said softly.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's just — the first time I met him, he threatened to have me removed from the property." A small, crooked smile. "People contain multitudes. Mostly terrible ones, in that family. But still."

* * *

Reyes closed the file on a Thursday.

Not metaphorically — literally, physically, with an actual stamp, because the records room still ran on paper for cases older than its software. The 1994 Ashcombe file had lived in a box whose cardboard had gone soft as old bread, and she'd had it open on her desk for three weeks while the federal case assembled itself around the father like a tide coming in.

There were forms for this. Of course there were forms. But the form asked for a disposition, and none of the checkboxes fit: *unfounded*, no. *Deceased*, yes, but — where? When? The form hadn't been designed for a girl who'd died of cancer at forty-six under a name that wasn't hers, having raised her sister in safety, having outlived the mother who buried her reputation and nearly outlived the father who made it necessary.

So Reyes wrote it longhand, in the margin, in pen, because some things shouldn't be typed:

**Vivian Ashcombe — located. Deceased 2023, at peace.**

She stared at it for a while. Nineteen years a cop, and she'd closed hundreds of files, and this was the first one that felt like setting down something heavy rather than shoving it into a drawer.

Her rookie year, she'd been the one who pulled the original Ashcombe box for a supervisor who never followed up. She remembered the photograph clipped inside — a girl on a cliff, wind-blown, half-smiling, wearing a locket. She remembered thinking, at twenty-three, *somebody knows*. She'd been right. Everybody knew. That had been the whole problem.

She stamped the page. Closed the box. Carried it back to the shelf herself, because she didn't trust anyone else to put it away gently.

In the elevator afterward, her phone buzzed. Sebastian.

*File closed?*

*Closed,* Reyes typed. *You?*

A pause, long enough that Reyes had reached her car before the reply came.

*I'm about to find out what closing costs.*

Which was, Reyes thought, starting the engine, either a figure of speech or a warning. With Sebastian Quintana it was usually both.

* * *

The reaction, over the following week, split exactly the way rivers split — gradually, then all at once.

On one bank: celebration. Vigils above the cove. Flowers zip-tied to the boathouse ruins' surviving fence post. A Change.org petition, signed by eighty thousand people, demanding Vivian Ashcombe's name be added to the family plot. Three separate members of Parliament — wrong country, wrong jurisdiction, nobody cared — called for inquiries into how a death certificate gets forged and stays forged for thirty years.

On the other bank: outrage, and not entirely unreasonable outrage. Editorials asked who elected Sebastian Quintana judge of what the public deserved to know. A former colleague of hers went on cable news and said, with real venom, "She found the truth and then she *negotiated* with it." The phrase stuck. *Negotiated with the truth.* It trended for two days.

Marcus tracked all of it with the grim diligence of a man bailing water. Sebastian, meanwhile, seemed almost serene, which frightened him more than the legal threats had.

"It's working," she said one evening, gesturing at the chaos on his monitor. "Look at it. Both sides are arguing about the truth. Loudly. In public. Nobody's arguing about whether it exists anymore. That's the win."

"The win has lawyers in it."

"Everything worth having has lawyers in it. That's how you know it's real estate." She stole a fry off his plate. "How's Wren?"

"Quiet. Which per her is good." Marcus lowered his voice out of pure habit, though the office was empty. "She watched the numbers come in from wherever she watches them. Sent one message through the intermediary. Want to hear it?"

Sebastian nodded.

Marcus read from his phone: *"'Tell the angry ones they're right. And tell the kind ones thank you. And tell Quintana the sea remembers.'"*

Sebastian laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly wet-eyed. "She's going to be insufferable when she comes out of hiding properly."

"She's already insufferable. She's just insufferable anonymously."

* * *

Reyes caught the tail end of it all from her side of the fence. The father, remanded pending trial, had begun issuing statements through counsel that grew steadily less coherent — the bluster of a powerful man discovering that power was a rental, and the lease was up. Eleanor's estate was frozen by court order; the trust's annual disbursements, those tidy payments to a redacted name, now sat in escrow like evidence in a jar. Julian, discharged from the hospital with a walking cane and a newly public conscience, gave an interview from the Ashcombe grounds in which he pointed at the burned boathouse and said, "This is what secrets look like eventually. This is always what they look like."

And on a gray Friday afternoon, Reyes drove out to the cove alone, off the clock, and stood where the tide chart in a dead woman's confession letter had once pointed. The water did what water does. Gulls argued about something. Down the coast, the boathouse ruins smoked faintly in memory if not in fact.

She thought about writing the notation again, just to feel it. *Located. At peace.* Two words doing the work of thirty years.

Her phone buzzed. Sebastian again.

*You free tonight? Bad coffee, my treat. There's something I need to tell you about the handwriting on the confession letter. Something I've been sitting on since day one.*

Reyes looked at the sea a moment longer.

Then she typed back: *I'll bring the coffee. Yours is a crime scene.*

She was halfway to the car when it occurred to her that Sebastian had said *something I've been sitting on* — past tense, deliberate, a woman choosing to hand over a weight. And whatever it was about that handwriting, Reyes suspected, was going to reopen a file neither of them had on their desks yet.
