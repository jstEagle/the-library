## 12. A Night at the Boathouse

The rain began at dusk, a fine gray sift that turned the lake to hammered tin and made the boathouse look like something drowned already, waiting only for the water to finish the work. Gideon Reyes sat in his car at the far end of the public lot, engine off, wipers lifted so they wouldn't tick, and watched the building through a windshield gone soft with condensation.

He had told himself he wasn't going to come.

That was the first lie of the evening, and Gideon had built a life on knowing other people's lies, so it offended him professionally to catch himself in one. He had known since the moment Pryce's hand had lingered half a second too long on Danny Voss's shoulder at Tuesday's staff meeting—known the way he knew when a guest's wedding ring came off before check-in and went back on before checkout—that something moved between those two men that had nothing to do with renovations. Men who were merely business partners did not touch each other like men holding a rope over deep water.

And now there was the photograph. The four young men on the dock, sun-bleached to ghosts, one face torn away. Three alibis accounted for. The fourth face lived upstairs in the manager's office of the Grand Meridian Hotel, wearing a résumé that had materialized from nowhere like fog off this same lake.

So here he was, in a rented sedan—he'd learned long ago never to conduct surveillance in his own vehicle; the valet boys noticed everything, and he'd trained them to notice everything, which meant they would notice him—sitting in rain that smelled of rotting leaves and cold stone, feeling like a man who had finally stopped reading the town's mail and started opening it.

The boathouse was dark except for one window on the second floor, where Danny's renovation office glowed amber. Through the rain-blurred glass Gideon could see the shape of scaffolding inside, plastic sheeting billowing faintly where the old doors had been removed. They were gutting it. Stripping the original planking, pouring new footings, hanging chandeliers where the oars had hung. By spring it would be *The Wren Pavilion*, Danny had announced at the chamber of commerce mixer—a name chosen, Gideon suspected, by someone who thought it sounded romantic rather than obscene.

A grave with a gift shop.

Gideon checked his watch. 9:47. He had been there two hours. His left knee ached in the way it always did after ten years of standing behind a front desk, and he flexed it against the floor mat and thought, absurdly, of Marta Ilic's hands around her teacup, trembling, telling him to let the dead stay buried.

*Some things don't stay buried,* he had answered. *They just wait for the ground to shift.*

The ground was shifting tonight. He could feel it the way you feel weather coming through an old scar.

* * *

At 10:16, headlights swept the lot.

Not Danny's silver Range Rover—that had been parked beside the boathouse since seven, visible whenever the motion light triggered. These lights came slow down the access road from the hotel side, running without high beams, and pulled into the shadow of the cypress stand at the lot's edge rather than the open spaces near the door. A dark sedan. Gideon didn't recognize the model, which itself was information; Pryce drove a car like a man who had read a manual on being unremarkable.

The engine died. The dome light did not come on—the interior bulb had been unscrewed or disabled—and for a full minute nothing happened at all. Then the driver's door opened with the deliberate slowness of a man who had done this before, and Peter Crane—Pryce, Gideon corrected himself, though the name felt like a costume now—stepped out into the rain without an umbrella.

He stood there a moment, letting the rain do whatever it was doing to him, face tilted up toward the boathouse window. Even at this distance, even in the dark, Gideon recognized the posture. It was the posture of a man visiting a place he hated and could not stop returning to. Thirty years of visits. Thirty years of envelopes.

Pryce crossed the lot with his collar up, let himself in through the temporary plywood door at the boathouse's landward side, and was swallowed.

* * *

Gideon's hands were not shaking yet. That came later.

What came first was the discipline. He took out his phone, checked the battery—eighty-one percent—checked that the camera was set to video, not stills, because video could not be dismissed as a trick of angle and light. He cleared the memory card of everything personal: forty photographs of ledger pages, the scan of the torn picture Marta had given him, a photo of his own handwriting practice comparing letter forms. If tonight went wrong, if the phone was taken from him, none of that could be allowed to surface. He moved it all to a card in his breast pocket, behind the laminated card that said GIDEON REYES, CONCIERGE, GRAND MERIDIAN — AT YOUR SERVICE, and almost laughed at the smallness of the words. *At your service.* Thirty years of serving, and this was what service had come to: sitting in the rain, waiting to catch respectable men being criminals.

The window above flickered. Shadows crossed the light—one broad, moving with the loose confidence of ownership; one narrower, precise, economical. Danny and Pryce. Their voices reached him in fragments when the wind turned, syllables without sense, but the tone carried perfectly: Danny expansive, laughing at intervals; Pryce quiet, clipped, the tone of a man delivering terms rather than conversation.

Then the tone changed.

It happened between one gust and the next. Danny's laugh cut off mid-note, and when his voice resumed it had dropped into a register Gideon knew intimately—not from guests but from marriages, from the lobby at 2 a.m., from every transaction where the mask slips and the customer underneath is revealed. It was the voice of a man who has just heard a number he doesn't like.

The shadows converged at the window. Stopped. Held.

Gideon raised the phone.

Through the lens, zoomed to its limit, the scene resolved into grain and glow: Danny Voss in shirtsleeves, arms folded, jaw set in the family mold—the same jaw as his father, the same jaw as the grandfather who had hosted the party in June of 1994 while Thomas Wren walked down to the water and did not walk back up. And facing him, holding out one hand, palm up, then turning it over to reveal what lay in it—

An envelope.

Even through the camera's compression, even across a hundred yards of rain, Gideon knew what it was. Not a contract—contracts came in folders, in courier bags, in the leather portfolios Danny carried everywhere. Not blueprints. An envelope, thick, unsealed, the kind of plain white vessel that had been passing between these families since before Danny was born. Hush money. Still flowing. Thirty years of quarterly payments, probably—Gideon did the arithmetic without wanting to—more than a million dollars spent keeping four boys' story airtight, and the payments hadn't stopped when the fathers retired or died. The debt had simply been inherited, like the hotel shares, like the jaw, like the guilt.

Danny didn't take it at first. That was the thing that made Gideon's stomach turn cold. Danny stood with his arms folded and looked at the envelope the way a man looks at a bill he resents, and Pryce stepped closer—close enough that their shadows merged into one shape on the plastic sheeting—and said something short and final, and pressed it into Danny's chest hard enough that Danny rocked back a half-step.

Then Danny took it. Slid it into his back pocket with the casualness of a man pocketing a receipt. Said something that made Pryce's shoulders drop, some joke, some deflection, and the light mood crawled back over both of them like weather clearing.

Gideon lowered the phone. His hands were shaking now.

* * *

He made himself sit there another twenty minutes, because a witness who leaves early is a witness who misses the ending, and endings were where the truth kept its office hours. But the ending was anticlimax: Pryce left first, alone, pausing at the plywood door to look back at the water for a long moment—Gideon filmed that too, the silhouette framed in the doorway, head bowed, a man at a graveside whether he admitted it or not—and then the dark sedan pulled away toward the hotel with its lights still cowardly-low. Danny stayed another hour. Gideon watched the shadow move around the upper room, pacing, stopping, pacing, and once—only once—going still at the window and looking out at the lake for so long that Gideon wondered if he was looking at the spot. If Danny knew. If the son had ever been told what the money was for, or whether he'd been raised on the fiction, the accident, the terrible tragedy, poor Thomas Wren, such a shame.

*Poor Thomas Wren.* Ruth Calloway had said it once, in Gideon's hearing, three years ago, in the lobby, signing a permission slip for a school trip. He hadn't understood then why her pen had paused on the line for *emergency contact*. He understood now. There was no emergency contact for Thomas Wren anymore. There was only a sister who had waited thirty years, teaching children to write their letters in a corrected left-handed slant, and writing letters of her own at night.

The upper light went out at 12:40. Danny emerged, locked the plywood door with a padlock, got into the Range Rover, and drove away whistling—Gideon could hear the tuneless thread of it even after the taillights vanished. Whistling. A man walking cheerfully over a grave, with an envelope warm in his pocket.

Gideon sat until the lot was empty and the rain had thinned to mist, and then he played the video back twice, frame by frame at the crucial moment, and saved it under a filename that looked like nothing: *boathouse_site_survey_1016.mov*. A site survey. Let them find it. Let them explain it away. He had spent fifteen years making himself invisible, and invisibility, he was learning, had tactical advantages no one had ever taught him in hospitality school.

Then he did the thing he had been avoiding all night. He opened the folder in his mind that he kept locked, the one labeled June 1994, and he laid tonight's evidence inside it next to everything else, and he let himself see the whole shape of it at last.

The mayor's father had killed a boy—or caused a boy's death, the distinction mattered legally and not at all morally—and three friends had helped him bury it. One of those friends had never left Ashcombe. One had grown rich on silence and was now paying the grandson of the killer to keep renovating over the body of the truth. And the money was still flowing, which meant the secret was still dangerous, which meant somewhere in the chain there was still a link that could break.

Marta Ilic. The last living witness. The woman who had pressed a torn photograph into his hands and begged him, with tears she was ashamed of, to let the dead stay buried.

She had seen something. She had seen enough to be afraid for thirty years, and fear like that doesn't come from hearsay. Fear like that comes from standing somewhere and watching.

Gideon started the car. The heater coughed and filled the windshield with fog, and he sat in the white blindness of it, thinking about Marta's small apartment above the bakery, the light that still burned there past midnight sometimes—he'd seen it on his walks, had filed it away without asking why an old woman couldn't sleep.

Tomorrow, he decided. Tomorrow he would knock on her door, and he would not leave until she gave him the other half of the answer.

Because tonight he had proof of the payments. But payments were only the echo. Somewhere in Marta Ilic's memory was the sound itself—the splash, the voices, the pact made on the dock while a boy floated face-down in the black water—and Gideon Reyes, keeper of ledgers, collector of other people's truths, was finally ready to hear it.

He put the car in gear. Behind him, through the thinning rain, the boathouse stood dark and gutted over the lake, and the water beneath it held everything it had ever been given, patient as a ledger, waiting to be read.
