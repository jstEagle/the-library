## 3. Bellwether Lane Remembers Nothing

Emrys had a rule about interviews, learned over ten years of night dispatch and four hundred closed cases: people lie with their hands before they lie with their mouths. The mouth is trained. The hands are not.

She stood at the end of Bellwether Lane at nine in the morning, which was technically her midnight, still in the clothes she'd worn home from the dispatch desk, holding both letters in a manila folder under her arm like a summons. The lane looked exactly as it had looked for as long as she could remember—six houses in a crescent around a cracked basketball hoop nobody had used since Marisol left, hydrangeas going purple along the Okonkwos' fence, Mr. Brandt's flag drooping on its pole like something that had given up waiting.

Ten years ago this lane had been torn apart by a missing girl. Police tape on the hedges. Channel 4 vans idling where the hoop stood. Neighbors crying on porches, holding each other up.

Today it looked like nothing had ever happened here at all.

That was the first thing that didn't fit. Grief leaves sediment. Even when a neighborhood heals, you can see the strata—the memorial garden that never got planted, the room that stayed shut. Bellwether Lane had no strata. It had been polished smooth, like a stone someone kept turning in their pocket until every rough edge wore away.

Emrys started at the Okonkwos', because Adaeze Okonkwo had taught her to drive, and because if anyone on this lane would tell her the truth out of sheer irritation at being asked, it would be Adaeze.

* * *

Adaeze answered the door already dressed for church even though it was Tuesday, in a blue wrapper and a headwrap the color of a storm front, and her face did something complicated when she saw Emrys—a flicker of warmth, then a shutter coming down so fast and so smoothly that Emrys might have missed it if she hadn't spent a decade listening to liars breathe.

"Emrys." Not *my daughter*, not *nne m*, which is what Adaeze had called her since Emrys was eleven and eating dinner at their table four nights a week because her own mother worked doubles. Just the name. Flat as a receipt. "You look tired."

"I work nights."

"You always work nights." Adaeze stepped back to let her in, and Emrys noted that too—the invitation came half a beat late, like a word remembered at the last second. "Sit. I'll make tea."

The Okonkwos' living room was unchanged in ten years, and that was the second thing that didn't fit. The same sofa, the same framed cross-stitch of Psalm 23, the same photograph on the mantel: the whole lane at Chidi Okonkwo's sixtieth birthday, everyone crowded and laughing, and there at the edge of the frame, half-cut-off, a girl with dark hair and a sunburned nose. Marisol. Nobody had taken the photo down. Nobody had cropped her out either. She'd simply been allowed to fade at the margins, which was somehow worse.

Adaeze brought tea and sat across from her with her spine straight as a hymnal.

"Auntie," Emrys said, using the old word deliberately, watching her hands, "I got a letter yesterday. Someone confessed to killing Marisol."

The teacup didn't rattle. That was the third thing. Adaeze Okonkwo had once dropped an entire casserole when Emrys said *boo* behind her at fifteen. Her hands now were perfectly, unnaturally still, resting in her lap like folded laundry.

"That's terrible," Adaeze said.

"It's signed by Denny Ault."

"Denny Ault has been dead three years." No hesitation. No *poor Denny*, no *God rest him*. Just the fact, delivered like testimony.

"So someone forged his signature. Or—" Emrys let it hang. "Auntie, I have to ask you about the night she disappeared. I know you talked to the police. I know you told them everything. But I wasn't listening then the way I listen now, and I'm asking you to tell me again. What happened that night?"

And here was the thing Emrys would turn over for the rest of the day, the thing that would keep her from sleeping when she finally went to bed at noon: Adaeze told her. Fluently. Completely. In paragraphs.

"We were all at Chidi's sister's engagement party, at the hall on Delmore Street. You were there, Emrys—you'd fallen asleep in the coat room and Chidi carried you to the car. We got home around one. Marisol had said she was staying at the Ferreiras' that night, studying with Lucía. When we heard she hadn't come home, Sunday morning, we called the police right away."

It was a good answer. It had dates, times, sensory details, corroboration built in. It was, Emrys thought, almost exactly the answer a person would give if they'd rehearsed it.

Because here was what Adaeze hadn't done: she hadn't grieved. Ten years ago, telling this story to detectives, she had wept so hard Chidi had to finish her sentences. Today she recited it like a safety briefing. And when Emrys watched her hands—not her face, her hands—they stayed folded, calm, controlled, the hands of a woman performing calm rather than feeling it.

"Do you remember anything else?" Emrys asked gently. "Anything you didn't tell the police? Anything about Marisol that summer—was she seeing anyone? Was anything wrong?"

"Nothing was wrong," Adaeze said, and her eyes went, for just a fraction of a second, to the window—to the lane, to the Ferreiras' roofline visible past the hedge—and then came back. "Marisol was a happy girl. We all loved her. We all miss her."

We all. Twice in two sentences. Emrys drank her tea and thanked her and hugged her goodbye, and felt Adaeze hold on one second longer than the performance required, and heard her whisper, so low Emrys almost lost it: *"Some doors, nne m, are locked for a reason."*

Then she pulled away, and her face was church-smooth again, and she showed Emrys out.

* * *

The Ferreira house was louder. It was always louder—Lucía's kids underfoot, a telenovela muttering from the kitchen TV, Gerald Ferreira's voice carrying from the backyard where he was, at nine-thirty on a Tuesday, inexplicably power-washing his driveway.

Gerald Ferreira was the lane's load-bearing wall. He'd organized the search parties ten years ago, coordinated with the police, fed the volunteers, handled the press so the other families wouldn't have to. He'd also, less officially, been the one who decided when the candlelight vigils stopped being held, and the one who'd told Emrys, six months after Marisol vanished, with a hand heavy and kind on her shoulder: *"It's time to let her rest, mija. She wouldn't want you ruining your life over it."*

He shut off the pressure washer when he saw her, and his face opened into the big warm welcome he gave everyone, and Emrys thought: *there it is. The face he puts on.*

"Mija! Look at you. You eat enough? You're skin and bone." He wiped his hands on a rag and embraced her, and she let him, and catalogued the details the way she'd catalogued Adaeze's: the rag already clean, the handshake of a man who'd been expecting company or at least prepared for the possibility; the glance over her shoulder at the empty street before he turned back; the way his smile arrived a half-second before his eyes agreed to it.

"Gerald," she said. "I need to ask you about Marisol."

The smile stayed. That was the remarkable part—it stayed exactly where it was, fixed, while everything behind it reorganized. Like a shopkeeper keeping the display intact during an earthquake.

"Ten years, and still that girl breaks hearts," he said, shaking his head. "Come inside. Lucía! Put the coffee on!"

Inside, he gave her the story, and it was Adaeze's story. Not similar—identical. The engagement party on Delmore Street. One o'clock. Marisol supposedly at the Ferreiras' studying with Lucía, except Lucía had gone to bed early and Marisol had never shown, or had come and gone, the memory fogging conveniently at the crucial hinge. Home by one. Called the police Sunday morning.

Word for word, in places. *We called the police right away.* *Marisol was a happy girl.* The same phrases, the same cadence, the same little shrug at the same moments, as if the two of them had sat down at some point—last week, yesterday, the morning after the letters went out—and agreed on a script.

People who remember the same event don't remember it identically. Emrys knew this from dispatch, from taking statements after accidents: five witnesses to one fender-bender produced five different streets, five different light sequences. Memory is a crooked mirror. Identical stories don't mean shared truth. They mean shared preparation.

"Gerald," she said, setting down her coffee, "who else have you talked to about this? Recently?"

His eyes did the window-thing. Adaeze's window-thing. Out toward the lane, quick, checking.

"Talked to? Mija, nobody talks about it anymore. It's old grief. Old grief is heavy and it doesn't feed anybody." He smiled again, warm as a furnace. "Why are you digging this up? Some cop put you up to this? You should let sleeping dogs—"

"I got a confession letter," Emrys said, and watched the smile finally die. "Signed by a dead man. Somebody wants this case open, Gerald. And somebody on this lane mailed it, because the postmark is from our own zip code, and the paper is from the stationery store on Kessler that closed in 2019, which means whoever bought it bought it years ago and kept it. Kept it like ammunition. Does any of that sound like old grief to you?"

For a long moment Gerald Ferreira looked at her, and beneath the beloved-neighbor face Emrys saw something else, something older and wearier and frightened, and she thought: *he knows. He doesn't know what I know, but he knows what he knows, and my questions are landing on it like rain on a buried wire.*

"You were like a daughter to this lane," he said quietly. "We raised you. All of us. Whatever you think you're doing—chasing ghosts, hurting old people with questions—ask yourself who raised you, and what you owe them."

There it was. Not a denial. A debt.

Emrys stood, thanked Lucía for the coffee she barely touched, and walked out into the bright indifferent morning, and did not let herself shake until she was three houses away.

* * *

Old Mr. Brandt's house was the last one, sagging at the crescent's end like the period at the end of a sentence. Harold Brandt was eighty-six, a retired railroad signalman, widowed twice, deaf in one ear, and the only person on Bellwether Lane who had never pretended to like everybody. He'd once told the entire neighborhood association, at the annual meeting, over the microphone, that their HOA president was "a tyrant with a riding mower." Emrys loved him the way you love a blunt instrument.

He made her shout her business twice through the screen door, then waved her in with a hearing aid adjusted like a dial turned up to receive her specifically.

"A letter," he repeated, when she'd explained. "Somebody confessing. Signed by Ault." He lowered himself into his armchair with the sound of a ship settling. "Denny Ault couldn't write a grocery list. Man signed his own divorce papers with an X and a stamp."

"You knew him well?"

"Knew him the way you know a dog that bites. From a distance, and always watching." Brandt's pale eyes were sharp despite the cataracts. "He lived over on Corliss, but he had business on this lane, if you follow me. Business with certain houses."

"What kind of business?"

"The kind gentlemen don't discuss and ladies don't report." He tapped his temple. "I was a signalman forty years, girl. My whole job was knowing which lights were lying. Red light says stop, green light says go—but the relay underneath tells you what the circuit's actually doing. This lane has been running on painted lights for ten years."

Emrys leaned forward. "Mr. Brandt, the night Marisol disappeared. Where were you?"

"Home. Awake. I don't sleep much." He said it without drama, a fact among facts. "Saw more than the police ever asked me about."

Her pulse ticked up. "What did you see?"

"I saw," Brandt said slowly, "that everybody on this lane tells the same story, and I've been alive long enough to know that when six households remember one night identically, they didn't remember it. They agreed on it." He fixed her with the pale eye. "You want to know what I actually saw that night? I saw Gerald Ferreira's truck parked at the end of the lane at two in the morning, engine off, lights off, sitting there an hour. And I saw him walk up the lane afterward like a man coming back from burying something."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere a clock ticked with the patience of geology.

"Why didn't you tell the police?"

Brandt looked at her for a long moment, and in his face she saw the same thing she'd seen in Adaeze's hands and Gerald's window-glance: the shape of a loyalty grown around a secret like a tree around a fence post.

"Because whatever he was burying, girl, I looked at this lane—at Adaeze, at Chidi, at Rosa next door, at all of them, good people, the people who fed you when your mama worked doubles—and I decided some holes are dug for mercy." His voice dropped. "But mercy and murder wear the same shovel, and I'm too old now to carry not-knowing. So you tell me, dispatcher. You're the one who closes cases. What's under the ground on Bellwether Lane?"

Emrys didn't answer. She couldn't. Because the honest answer was *I don't know yet*, and because a colder answer was forming underneath it, one she didn't want to say aloud in this room:

Six households. One story. Rehearsed, synchronized, maintained for ten years like a watch someone winds every night without asking why it must never stop.

This wasn't a neighborhood that had forgotten. It was a neighborhood standing guard.

She walked back down the lane in the full morning light, past the hydrangeas and the dead hoop and the smooth-polished absence where a girl used to be, and took out her phone, and did the thing she should have done first, the thing her pride in working alone had argued against all morning.

She called the police department and asked for the records division.

"This is Emrys Villalobos. I need the case file on Marisol Quintero, reported missing June 14th, ten years ago." A pause. "Yes, that Quintero. I'll come pick it up."

The clerk's voice came back flat and procedural, and then, just before hanging up, added a single unscheduled sentence that Emrys would carry with her into the records room and through everything that came after:

"Huh. Funny. You're the second person to request that file this month. And the first one found it wasn't all there anymore."
