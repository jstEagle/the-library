## 2. Twenty Years of Filed Secrets

The police kept August Yarrow's grey overcoat in an evidence bag for three days before returning it to her, and when they did, it came back smelling of formalin and someone else's cigarettes. She hung it on the back of her kitchen door anyway. She could not have said why. Perhaps because a coat that had spent twenty years on her shoulders deserved better than an evidence locker; perhaps because she wanted to look at it each morning and ask it the question the whole town was asking her: *who put you on that drowned man?*

On the fourth night, unable to sleep, unable to eat, unable to bear the way Mrs. Calloway at the bakery had looked through her rather than at her—August went down into the cellar of the house on Pilcrow Lane and unlocked the file cabinet.

It was not a remarkable piece of furniture. Four drawers, green steel, bought secondhand from the defunct office of Greywater's last insurance broker. What was inside it was remarkable only if you understood what a concierge actually was: not a woman who carried luggage and turned down beds, but a woman who stood at the exact point where every secret in a small town had to pass to get from one mouth to another. Twenty years of guests at the Meridian Hotel—and before that, twenty years of her father's guests—had taught August that people confessed to the invisible. They told the maid things they would never tell their wives. They told the desk clerk things they would never tell their lawyers. And August, who had inherited her father's silence along with his house and his handwriting, had written it all down.

Not everything. She was not a monster. But enough.

The top drawer held index cards, arranged by surname, cross-referenced by year in a system no one but she could read. The second drawer held letters—some sealed, some opened with steam and resealed with patience. The third held confessions proper: pages people had pressed into her hands at three in the morning, saying *keep this, keep this, I can't be the one holding it.* The fourth drawer she did not open yet. The fourth drawer was her father's.

She started where any sensible person would start: with the dead man's name, once the coroner released it. Daniel Reeve. But tonight she didn't have the name yet—tonight she had only the coat, the cufflinks, and the particular quality of humiliation that comes from being looked at by two hundred people who had spent two decades successfully not looking at you at all.

So she began with herself. *Yarrow, A.* One card. Her own handwriting, from years ago, in the grip of some dark night she barely remembered: *If anything happens to me, look here first.* Beneath it, nothing. She had never been able to imagine what could happen to her. Now she knew. Someone could dress a corpse in her life and send it floating home.

* * *

She worked until two in the morning, lamp pulled low, tea going cold beside her. She was not looking for the killer—not exactly. She was looking for the shape of the thing. When you cannot see a man's face, you can still see the hole he leaves in a room. Whoever had taken her coat—taken it from the Meridian's staff closet, where it had vanished sometime in October, a loss she'd logged without thinking, one more small theft in a hotel full of them—had known the closet. Had known which coat was hers among forty identical staff coats. Had known her initials were stitched inside the cuff, A.Y., in thread her mother had sewn before dying. That was not a thief. Thieves took coats to stay warm. This was a costume.

*Yarrow* gave her nothing. She moved to the hotel itself, pulling cards at random, letting her fingers walk the alphabet the way her father had taught her—*the files will dream for you, August, if you let them, the truth is always filed somewhere you weren't looking.*

It was near three o'clock, in the H drawer, that her finger stopped.

**HARROW, V. — 2004.**

The card was older than most, the ink gone brown at the edges. Her father's handwriting, not her own. She read it twice because the first time her eyes refused the information.

*V.H. requested the east wing be closed to all staff during the week of the fire. Paid double for discretion. Asked whether my daughter ever talks. Told her no.*

August sat very still. The cellar was cold enough that her breath should have fogged, and she noticed distantly that it did not—or that she could not see it, or that noticing anything beyond the card in her hand had become impossible.

*My daughter ever talks.* Vivienne Harrow had asked her father whether August talked. And her father—who had taught her the files, who had built her silence brick by brick like a mason building a well—had answered *no*, and had taken the money, and had filed the transaction like a hat check.

She pulled the next card. **HARROW, E. — 2005.** Edmund, then nineteen, home from school. *E.H. seen at the boathouse after midnight, twice, week of the anniversary. Crying. Said to me: "You saw nothing." I said: "I see nothing, young master. It's the family trade."*

Her father had said that. *It's the family trade.* He'd said it to her too, once, laughing, teaching her to steam a letter open over the kettle. She had thought it was a joke about hospitality.

The third card was not a card at all but a folded page, thin as onion skin, tucked behind the H divider where it did not belong—a misfile, and her father never misfiled. Unless he wanted it found. Unless he wanted *her* to find it, someday, if she ever learned to look.

It was unsigned. It read:

*The fire at Saltcrest was not the boiler. The boiler was serviced in September; I have the receipt. The fire started in the east sitting room, and there was a woman in it, and the woman was supposed to die, and everyone in this town agreed afterward that she had. I am writing this down because I cannot carry it and I cannot say it and if I burn it I will say it drunk within a year. Keep it. You're good at keeping.*

Below that, in different ink, added later, in her father's hand: *Lena Voss. Witness. See also: R.*

*See also: R.*

August's hands were shaking now, properly shaking, the fine tremor of a tuning fork. She went to the R drawer with the page still pinched between two fingers as though it might fly away and take the past twenty years with it.

**REEVE, D. — 2004.** Groundskeeper, Saltcrest House. *D.R. pulled from the bay the night of the Voss fire. Officially drowned saving her. Body recovered; closed casket at the family's request; buried in the Harrow plot. D.R.'s sister asked me privately why the casket was nailed shut before viewing. I told her some families grieve privately. She said: "Some families lie publicly." She left town in the spring.*

A drowned man who had already drowned once, twenty years ago, saving a woman who had died in a fire—except the fire hadn't killed her either, apparently, or the casket hadn't held her, or—

August pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations. Somewhere above her, the house ticked and settled, the sound it made every night, the sound she had heard all her life and never once questioned. Tonight it sounded like footsteps.

* * *

She did not sleep. At dawn she climbed the stairs, made coffee she did not drink, and stood in front of the kitchen window watching the bay go from iron to pewter to a thin, dishonest silver. The water that had given up Daniel Reeve lay flat and calm, keeping its face smooth the way Greywater itself had kept its face smooth for twenty years.

And it was there, in the grey light, with the taste of old paper still in her mouth, that the memory finally surfaced—the one that had been circling beneath her thoughts since the fisherman dragged the body ashore, the one she had been refusing to look at the way you refuse to look at a stain on a ceiling until one morning you simply do.

The box.

The autumn she was twenty-four—twenty years ago almost to the month—her father had burned a box in the yard behind the house on Pilcrow Lane. She remembered it with terrible clarity now: the galvanized washtub he used instead of a grate, so the fire would be contained, tidy, deniable. The box had been a records box, the kind offices used, cardboard with metal edges. He had fed it to the flames a handful at a time, not dumping it, *curating* its destruction, making sure each page caught fully before adding the next. She had asked him what he was burning. He had said: *old guest records, nothing anyone wants anymore.* He had stood over that fire for two hours in his good coat. He had watched every page curl. And when it was done he had stirred the ash with a poker until it was indistinguishable from any other ash, and scattered it in the garden, and come inside, and washed his hands at the kitchen sink for a long time, longer than soap required, and said, without turning around:

*"August. Some day you'll want to know things. Don't. Knowing is a debt, and debts get collected."*

She had thought he was being dramatic. Her father, dramatic—she had laughed, even, and he had not laughed back, and she had forgotten it, filed it away in the great unindexed drawer of childhood, where all the true things go to wait.

He had burned the box the same week as the fire at Saltcrest House. The same week Lena Voss died. The same week Daniel Reeve drowned the first time.

The past stopped being background. It stood up out of its chair, and it turned around, and it looked at her with her father's eyes.

August set down the coffee she hadn't touched. Her reflection in the dark window looked back at her, hollowed, lit from below like something rising out of water. *Whoever dressed that corpse knew your closet,* she thought. *Knew your initials. Knew your habits. Knew how this town sees you—which is to say, doesn't.* There were perhaps four people alive who knew her that well. One of them was dead. One of them was Vivienne Harrow, who had once paid her father to confirm that his daughter never talked.

And one of them, she realized, with a cold that had nothing to do with the season, might be whoever her father had been protecting all those years—might be the reason a records box had to burn, the reason a casket stayed nailed shut, the reason a town agreed, unanimously and without being asked, that a certain woman was dead and a certain fire was an accident and a certain family's fortune was none of anybody's business.

She went back down to the cellar. She opened the fourth drawer—her father's drawer—for the first time since his funeral.

It was nearly empty. Three items only: a receipt from a hardware store dated the week of the fire, for a washtub and two gallons of kerosene. A photograph of Saltcrest House, east wing, with a small x penciled against one second-floor window. And an envelope, unsealed, addressed in her father's hand to no one, containing a single line:

*If you are reading this, August, then the tide has brought something back, and I am sorry, and it was not supposed to be you.*

August stood in the cold cellar holding her father's apology like a live coal, and outside, far off across the flat grey water, she heard the ferry horn sound—one long note, the morning run to the mainland—and understood that the next door she needed to open was not in any drawer she owned.

It was at Saltcrest House. And Vivienne Harrow was waiting for her.
