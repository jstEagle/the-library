## 10. What August Yarrow Knows

The courthouse on Marsh Street had been built to hold two hundred people, and on the morning of Vivienne Harrow's arraignment it held three hundred, standing along the walls, spilling into the corridor, breathing the same salt-damp air. August Yarrow sat in the third row—third, because the first two belonged to the lawyers and the press, and because she had spent twenty years learning exactly how far back to position herself so that no one would look at her. It was the first time in her life the calculation failed. Every eye in the room found her anyway.

She had testified for four hours the day before. She had said the words *my father drove the car* in a voice that did not shake, and had been privately astonished by that. She had said *the body in the casket was not Lena Voss* and watched the town's collective face do something she had no card in her archive to describe. She had said *I opened the boathouse* and *here is what the tide kept* and *I am the one who knows*, and when it was over, when the judge told her she could step down, no one had thanked her. That was all right. She hadn't done it to be thanked. She had done it because there comes a moment when the ledger has to balance, and she was the only one left holding the pen.

Now she sat in the third row and watched the empire come apart.

It came apart quietly, which surprised her. She had expected thunder. She had expected Vivienne Harrow to stand in her good pearls and deny everything with that glacial certainty that had governed Greywater Bay for sixty years. Instead Vivienne sat very straight in her charcoal suit, her hands folded on the table like a woman waiting for tea, and said almost nothing. Her lawyer did the talking. Her face did the rest. August, who had spent two decades reading the small weather of other people's expressions, read hers easily: not guilt, not shame. Arithmetic. Vivienne was calculating what remained, and finding it insufficient, and that—more than any verdict—was what finally broke her.

The conviction came eleven days later. Embezzlement, conspiracy, obstruction, and the murder of Daniel Reeve, though the state, to its credit, had never believed Reeve was the whole of it. The prosecutor stood in front of the jury and said, *Lena Voss did not die in that fire, and the woman who buried her name is the same woman who put a dead man in August Yarrow's coat,* and August felt the sentence pass through her like cold water through cloth. Twenty years of lies, and it took the jury four hours.

* * *

The house went first.

August had known it would. She had signed the papers with a steady hand, sitting across from a lawyer who would not meet her eyes, and she had thought, absurdly, of her father teaching her to drive on the coast road, his hand correcting hers on the wheel, saying *you have to look where you want to go, August, not where you're afraid of ending up.* The house on Lantern Row had been payment for silence. She had lived in it her whole life without knowing what it cost, and when the court took it back—when the truth of the transaction surfaced, as truths do, in the papers, in the ledgers, in the testimony of a dying doctor who owed her one last favor—she found she could not grieve it. You cannot mourn a thing you never truly owned.

The town was less forgiving than the court.

It happened in small ways, the way erosion happens. Mrs. Calloway, who had confessed to August for nineteen years over the front desk of the Greywater Inn—her husband's debts, her sister's drinking, the night in 2011 August would never put on a card—stopped coming for coffee. The fisherman who had dragged the dead man from the bay, old Tomas Brand, looked through her at the market as though she were glass. Someone wrote *DRIVER'S DAUGHTER* in chalk on the inn's front steps, and August knelt in the grey morning light and scrubbed it off herself, because the boy who worked the desk was sixteen and should not have to carry it.

Half the town turned away. That was the arithmetic of it, and she had done the math herself long before the verdict: for every person who had whispered *thank you* in the courthouse corridor, two had decided that exposing the Harrows was a betrayal of Greywater itself, that some lies are load-bearing, that August Yarrow had pulled one stone out and let the whole wall come down on their heads. She understood. She had spent twenty years as one of those stones herself. She had held up the wall and called it loyalty.

What surprised her was the other half. The half that stayed. The doctor, gaunt now, who came to the inn on her last week and sat across from her and said, "You could have buried me with the rest of it. You didn't. That's the difference between you and your father, August. He kept secrets to own people. You kept them because no one ever taught you another way to be loved." She had cried then, in the empty dining room, for the first time since the bay gave up its dead. Not for the house. For the sentence. For being seen at last, and finding it did not kill her.

* * *

Edmund left on a Tuesday in late October.

August went to the ferry landing to see him off, because someone should, and because she had come to think of him—with the wary, unglamorous tenderness of two people who had used each other to survive—as the closest thing to a friend the year had given her. He stood at the rail with one suitcase and his grandmother's pearls in his pocket, which he told her, with a crooked smile, he intended to sell in Lisbon and scatter the money into the Atlantic.

"The whole fortune?" she asked.

"Every cent of what's left. It was never ours. It was hers stolen from other people, and then other people's blood on top of that." He looked out at the grey water. "I used to think inheriting it would make me real. You know what I mean. Visible." He glanced at her, and there was no charm in his face now, only the raw thing underneath, which she had always suspected was the true Edmund. "You were the realest person in that house, August, and they made you the invisible one. That's the trick, isn't it. They make the honest ones invisible so the liars can shine."

The ferry horn sounded. He gripped her hand—briefly, hard, like a man letting go of a rail—and said, "Write to me. Not about the town. About you. I never once heard about you." And then he was a figure at the rail, and then a smudge, and then the bay had him, and August stood a long time on the wet boards watching the water close over the wake.

* * *

Vivienne, they said, had asked for one thing in prison: a window facing the water. They gave her one. August thought about that more often than she expected to—about the old woman standing at the glass every day, looking out at the bay that had kept all her secrets and finally, at the end, given one up. There was a version of August that pitied her. There was a version that hated her. She was trying, these days, to be a woman who could hold both and not be torn apart by the holding.

* * *

She burned the archive on a Thursday in November, at low tide, on the shore below the inn.

She had carried the boxes down herself, one at a time, forty-one of them, the cardboard gone soft at the corners from years of handling. The tide was out far enough to expose the dark ribbon of wet sand where Tomas had found the body in his coat, and she built the fire there, in the exact place, because it seemed right that the ending should know its own beginning.

She started with the oldest cards. Mrs. Calloway, 2004. The mayor's brother, 2007. The doctor's bad night in 2009, which she had kept for eleven years without ever once using, and which had turned out, in the end, to be the only card that saved anyone. She fed them to the flames one at a time, not in fistfuls, because these were not trash; they were the architecture of a person, and a person should watch her own architecture come down with her eyes open.

Some of them she read one last time as they burned. A confession from a woman whose name she would carry to her grave and never speak aloud. A letter from a man who had paid her to forget something he should have been punished for, and which she had returned to him, unopened, the week of the trial. A note in her father's handwriting—*A.Y. will handle the rest*—that she had found in the last box, the one from Saltcrest, and which she held over the fire for a long moment before letting go. It curled. It went orange at the edges. It became, in four seconds, nothing, and August Yarrow stood on the cold shore of Greywater Bay and watched her father become nothing too, and felt—finally, after all of it—light.

The smoke went straight up in the windless air, a grey column visible, she supposed, from the road. Let them see. Let the whole town drive past and see the concierge burning twenty years of their sins on the sand. She was done being the place where secrets went to be safe. She was going to be a person now, with a face and a history and a name that meant something other than *discreet*.

The fire burned down to embers as the tide began to turn. She stayed until the water came for the ashes, which it did the way it did everything—without ceremony, without malice, taking what was offered. She had spent her whole life in this town believing the bay was a thing that took. She understood now that it only kept what you refused to release.

One card she did not burn.

It was the last one, the newest, written in her own hand six weeks ago in the ferry office that smelled of diesel and coffee: *Lena Voss. Ferry operator. Alive.* And beneath it, in Lena's blunt, unsentimental script, an address on the mainland coast, two hundred miles north, and a single line: *Come in the spring. I'll run you across myself. There's a lot of water between here and there and I've had twenty years to learn what it's good for. Bring nothing you're afraid to lose.*

August stood at the water's edge with the card in her hand until her fingers went numb, and then she put it in her coat pocket—the grey coat, the one they had buried the dead man in, the one she had taken back from the evidence room and had cleaned and kept, because a woman should not be made to fear her own clothes—and she turned and looked once at the town behind her, the inn with its lit windows, the courthouse dome, the crooked line of Saltcrest House dark on its hill.

Then she walked up the beach toward the road, toward the bus that left at six, toward a woman she had never truly met and a life she had never once tried on, and for the first time in twenty years, August Yarrow carried nothing that anyone else had given her to hold.

The tide came in behind her, patient and thorough, and took the rest.
