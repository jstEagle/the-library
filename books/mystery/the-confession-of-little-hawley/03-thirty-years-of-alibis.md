## 3. Thirty Years of Alibis

The county archive lived in the basement of the Hollow Creek municipal building, behind a fire door that hadn't been inspected since before I was born, and it smelled the way all archives smell—dust and old glue and the faint chemical ghost of toner from a copier that died a decade ago. I had a key. Of course I had a key. I was the coroner, and the coroner's office shared a wall with the records room, and for eleven years I had been the only person in town who came down here on purpose.

I liked it that way. I liked the order of it. Every box labeled, every box where it belonged. If you wanted to know what Hollow Creek was, you didn't ask the people. People forget, people lie, people round themselves down to something presentable. You asked the paper. The paper didn't have a reputation to protect.

I pulled the 1994 boxes at 7:40 on a Monday morning, before the building had anyone in it but me and the janitor, Ray, who nodded at me the way he'd nodded at me for eleven years and asked nothing, which is why I tolerated him.

The statute of limitations was the thing I couldn't stop doing the math on. I'd sat up half the night with my laptop and the state code, and the answer kept coming back the same. Ninety-one days. If a crime had been committed in the summer of 1994—if the shoe in Ambrose Hawley's coffin meant a crime, and not just a strange, sentimental lie—then the state had ninety-one days left to care. After that, whatever had happened to whoever had worn that shoe would become history, and history is a thing you curate, not a thing you punish.

I set the first box on the steel table and cut the twine.

Here is what you need to understand about municipal records: they are supposed to be boring. They are supposed to be redundant. A death certificate generates a burial permit, which generates a cemetery ledger entry, which generates a coroner's filing, which cross-references a county health department log. The redundancy is the point. Any one document can be lost. Any one document can be forged. But five documents that all agree, generated by five different offices that don't particularly like each other—that's the closest a town comes to truth.

The summer of 1994 agreed too much.

I noticed it first with the small things. The utility records for the Hawley property—Ambrose's house on Founders Row—showed water usage for June, July, and August of 1994 that was identical to the gallon. Not similar. Identical. 4,200 gallons in June. 4,200 in July. 4,200 in August. A house with a garden, in an August in Hollow Creek, when the temperature hit ninety-four degrees three weeks running, using the same water in August as it did in the mild rain of June.

Nobody uses the same water in August as June. Unless nobody was home.

I sat with that for a while. Then I pulled the coroner's log for that summer—my own office's log, the one I'd inherited from Coroner Aldous Prewitt, dead these nine years, whose handwriting I knew like my own because I'd spent years transcribing his files into the digital system.

Prewitt's log for June through August 1994 was eleven pages. I have read those eleven pages maybe fifty times over the years, in the ordinary course of work. I knew there'd been a farm accident in July, a drowning scare at the quarry that turned out to be nothing, a handful of natural deaths among the elderly.

The pages were there. All eleven. Neat as you please.

But the drowning scare entry—the one from August 9th, the quarry, the call that came in and was stood down—was written in a different ink than the entries before and after it. Prewitt used a fountain pen, black ink, the kind that fades to brown at the edges over thirty years. The August 9th entry was ballpoint. Blue. Fresh-looking, or at least newer-looking, sitting in the middle of that browned page like a stranger at a funeral.

Someone had rewritten one entry in my own office's log.

I photographed it with my phone, then photographed it again from different angles, then sat back down and made myself keep going, because the alternative was to sit there and feel the floor tilt, and I had a rule about that: when the ground moves, you catalog the movement. You don't run. You document.

The police files were worse. I didn't have the sheriff's department's internal files—those lived at the county seat—but I had the copies that flowed to the municipal clerk by statute: incident reports, permits, the sheriff's monthly activity summaries that Ezra Vann filed like clockwork for thirty-one years.

Vann's summary for August 1994 was one page. Every other summary in the archive was two to four pages. This one listed four incidents: a noise complaint, a barking dog, a traffic stop, and—"juvenile report, resolved." No name attached to the juvenile report. No follow-up. No case number. Just those two words, *resolved*, in Vann's blocky hand, and a signature.

I have signed thousands of documents in my career. I know what an honest signature looks like. It has a little carelessness in it, a little life. Vann's signature on that page was drawn, not written—each letter placed with the concentration of a man signing something he expected to be examined.

The town clerk's copies of the cemetery records were the last box, and by then I wasn't surprised, only colder. The burial permit for Ambrose Hawley, dated October 1994—wait.

I stopped.

I went back to the first box. The exhumation order, the one the developer's lawyers had served me with, listed Ambrose Hawley's date of death as June 2, 1994. That was the date on the monument, too. Everyone in Hollow Creek knew the founder had died in the early summer of '94; it was practically a civic holiday, the way the garden club still put peonies on his grave every June.

But the burial permit said October.

Four months. Ambrose Hawley, town founder, pillar of everything, had lain somewhere for four months before he was buried in the plot the whole town could see from the church steps. And in those four months, the water usage at his house had been a perfect, impossible 4,200 gallons a month.

Somebody had been home.

* * *

I called the town clerk at nine.

Marguerite Boyle was eighty-one years old and had run the clerk's office since 1971, and she was the only person in Hollow Creek whose memory I trusted more than the paper, because Marguerite had never in her life had a reason to lie and had never found one late in life either. She kept the office the way she kept her kitchen—everything reachable, nothing hidden, a jar of butterscotch candies on the counter for the kids who came in with their parents to pay water bills.

"Kestrel Dunmore," she said when I identified myself, and I heard something in her voice I'd never heard there before. Not surprise. Not warmth. A kind of bracing, like a woman hearing a creak in a staircase she'd hoped was done creaking.

"Marguerite. I need to ask you about the 1994 records."

A pause long enough that I checked the call hadn't dropped.

"What records in particular?"

"The burial permit for Ambrose Hawley. It's dated October. His death certificate says June."

"That would be an error," she said. "Clerical. Those things happened."

"Marguerite, I've read your filings for eleven years. You don't make clerical errors. You correct other people's clerical errors in red pencil and mail them back."

Another pause. When she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, slower, the voice of a woman choosing her words the way you choose footing on ice.

"You came down to my office yesterday," she said. "You stood at my counter and you asked me for the 1994 cemetery ledger and I gave it to you, because it's public record and you're the coroner and I have no cause to refuse you. So I'll say what I have to say now, and then I won't say it again. Are you listening?"

"I'm listening."

"Some doors stay shut for reasons," Marguerite said. "Not because of what's behind them. Because of what it does to the people standing in front of them when they open. You're a careful man, Kestrel. You've been careful your whole life. I've watched you. You keep your name small on your door and your head down at meetings and you know every grave in that cemetery by its first name, and I always thought that was a kind of modesty." She let that sit. "Maybe it's not modesty. Maybe it's just that you hadn't yet found the door with your name on it."

"I don't know what that means."

"No," she said. "Not yet. But you will, and when you do, I want you to remember that an old woman told you the truth in advance: there are things in 1994 that were put away by people who are dead now, and things that were put away by people who are not, and you should think hard about which kind you want to dig up. You of all people know that some things stay buried better for everybody."

"Is that a threat, Marguerite?"

The sound she made might have been a laugh, if laughter could be tired. "Lord, no, child. It's a warning, and warnings are the only inheritance I've got left to give. Come by Tuesday. I'll make coffee. I'll tell you about the permit, the parts that are mine to tell. But you go home first, and you sit with it, and you ask yourself whether you want the answer or whether you want the town. Because I don't think you can keep both."

She hung up before I could answer, which was probably the point. Marguerite Boyle had been hanging up on the powerful of Hollow Creek since before I was born, and she had a technique: she never left a silence you could fill.

* * *

I went home. I sat with it, like she said.

My house is the house I grew up in, on Larkspur Street, the one my father still won't come inside. Thomas Dunmore lives out at the edge of the county now, past the tree farm, and visits on the porch the way other men visit a bank—briefly, formally, transacting whatever fatherhood requires and no more. I sat at my mother's old kitchen table, because I'd never replaced it, and I laid out what I had: photographs of the water records. The ballpoint entry in Prewitt's log. Vann's drawn signature. The permit dated October under a death certificate dated June.

And the shoe. I kept coming back to the shoe. A child's leather shoe, small, the kind with the strap and the buckle, kept dry and dark for thirty years in a coffin built for a grown man. Someone had put it there on purpose. Someone had wanted it found—or wanted it kept, and the developer's lawyers had simply interrupted the keeping.

And the note. *Forgive me. He was never here.*

*He.* Not *it.* Not *the body.* *He.* A person, present tense of the heart even in past tense of the grammar. Someone had loved whoever *he* was, or feared for him, or both.

I made a list, because lists are how I pray. Everyone in Hollow Creek old enough in 1994 to have driven a truck, signed a permit, or rewritten a log entry. It came to maybe two hundred names. I put a star next to the ones who were dead: Aldous Prewitt, dead nine years. Ambrose Hawley, dead in the ground that wasn't his ground. A dozen others whose funerals I had personally attended, because that was my job—I stood at the graves of this town and said the words, and I had said them for thirty years without once wondering what the ground beneath me was agreeing to.

I put a different mark next to the living ones, and there my pencil slowed.

Ezra Vann. Retired sheriff. Bees, they said, out on the county road.

Marguerite Boyle. Clerk. Butterscotch candies.

My father.

I didn't put my father on the list because of anything. I want that on the record, in the archive of my own head, where I keep the things I can't file anywhere else. I put him on the list because he was a deputy in 1994, because he was twenty-eight years old and strong and available, because that's what a list is—a complete accounting, not an accusation. I have made ten thousand lists in my career and never once flinched at a name on them.

But I sat at my mother's table looking at his name, and I noticed that my hand had written it smaller than the others. As if the pencil already knew something I didn't.

* * *

I didn't sleep. Around two in the morning I went back down to the archive—not for more records, I told myself, just to re-photograph the permit, get the lighting right—and I stood in the aisle under the fluorescent hum and admitted the thing Marguerite had named before I could.

I wanted the answer. That was the flicker, the first spark of it, the thing that would burn the next three months of my life down to the studs. Not duty. Duty would have been satisfied with a report and a shrug. Not grief, even. It was want, plain and greedy, the collector's hunger of a man who has spent his whole life knowing where everyone in town is buried and has just learned that he doesn't know where one of them is—and that the not-knowing was manufactured, maintained, signed for, filed, and watered at 4,200 gallons a month.

Someone had built a lie with permits. Someone had filed it, cross-referenced it, made it redundant, made it *true* the way only paper makes things true. And they had done it well enough that it took an empty coffin and a child's shoe to crack it.

Which meant they'd done it for something that mattered more than paper. More than permits. More, maybe, than a town.

I turned off the archive lights and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the building settle, and I thought about Marguerite's question. The answer or the town.

I told myself I'd decide in the morning.

I already knew.
