## 7. The Founders' Ledger

The Hollow Creek Historical Society opened at ten on Wednesdays, which meant it opened at ten-forty, which meant Kestrel had twenty minutes to stand on the porch of the old Whitcomb house and read the brass plaque he'd read a hundred times before. *Established 1871. Home of the Founders' Collection.* The plaque had gone green at the edges. Everything in this town did, eventually. Brass, copper, memory.

He had called ahead. He had not called ahead in years—there had never been a reason—but he'd called Maribel Toss, the society's sole volunteer docent, and asked her, casually, what the society kept in its basement.

"Everything, dear," she'd said, in the tone of a woman who had never been down there herself. "Boxes and boxes. Ambrose's things are all down there somewhere. Why do you ask?"

"Curiosity," Kestrel had said, and the word had felt like a lie the moment it left his mouth, because curiosity was exactly what it wasn't. Curiosity was light. This was something with weight to it, something that had followed him home from the archives and sat in the passenger seat of his truck like a passenger he couldn't ask to leave.

He'd spent two days pulling the 1994 records, and what he'd found—or hadn't found—had rearranged the furniture of his mind. Missing pages. Missing months. A police log for August that read like a diary kept by a man with nothing to hide, which was itself the tell. Real logs had coffee spills and missed days and a drunk-and-disorderly on the fourth of July. Ezra Vann's August of 1994 had been a hymn.

Now he wanted the founders' ledger. Because if the town's records had been tidied, there was one place where tidiness would have been impossible—the one document kept by hand, by committee, by men who believed they were writing scripture.

The door opened at ten-forty exactly. Maribel Toss was eighty-one and dressed as though the society might receive visiting dignitaries: a brooch, a cardigan, a name tag laminated sometime in the previous century. She looked at Kestrel the way people in Hollow Creek had started looking at him since the exhumation—a half-second too long, a recalculation happening behind the eyes.

"Kestrel Dunmore," she said. "You've grown into your father's shoulders."

"Hello, Maribel. I'd like to see the founders' collection."

"Anyone may see it. That's rather the point of a historical society." She stepped back to let him in. The house smelled of dust and lemon polish and the particular staleness of rooms where nothing had been argued about in decades. "Though I'll say the basement's not been catalogued properly since Ruth Pell passed. She was our archivist, you know. Thirty-one years. When she went, the basement went with her, in a manner of speaking."

"Nobody's been down since?"

"Oh, people poke about." She waved a hand. "School groups, genealogy people, that developer's lawyers last spring—they were horrid, wanted everything photocopied, wanted *provenance*. As if Ambrose Hawley's hat needed a birth certificate." She smiled at her own joke and led him down a narrow hallway to a door painted the color of dried blood. "Mind the stairs. And mind the damp. And if you find Ruth's catalogue of the quilts, I'd be forever grateful. It's been missing since before Christmas."

She handed him a flashlight that worked on the third click and left him to it.

* * *

The stairs went down twelve steps into a concrete room that had once, judging by the shelving, been a root cellar. The society had lined it with steel racks and acid-free boxes, and the racks had rusted anyway, because Hollow Creek defeated everything eventually. Kestrel stood at the bottom and let his eyes adjust and felt the particular silence of a room full of other people's lives.

He knew how to work an archive. He was the coroner; he'd spent twenty years with other people's paper—death certificates, burial permits, the thin bureaucratic residue of lives. He knew that the truth of a town was never in its monuments. It was in its filing.

The founders' collection occupied three racks against the far wall. He found the boxes in order: correspondence, 1860s through 1880s. Land deeds and plats. The Hawley mill papers. Photographs, boxed and sleeved. And there, at the end, a box labeled in faded fountain pen: *FOUNDRY LEDGER & TOWN BOOKS — DO NOT LAMINATE.*

The ledger itself was a foot thick, bound in leather gone the color of a bruise, its spine cracked into segments like old bark. Kestrel carried it to the worktable under the single hanging bulb and set it down with both hands, the way he'd have handled remains. In a sense, that's what it was. The body of the town, embalmed in ink.

He opened it.

The early pages were magnificent. Ambrose Hawley's hand was a surveyor's hand—precise, unhurried, each entry ruled and dated. *Purchased of the Bellingham estate, 40 acres, water rights entire.* *Paid J. Creech for timber, sawn and cured.* *Laid out the common, four rods by six.* Kestrel read the town being invented, line by line, a place conjured out of a man's certainty. He felt the old pull, the one every kid in Hollow Creek felt—the myth of the founder, the man who'd looked at empty valley and seen a future. He'd believed in Ambrose Hawley the way you believe in weather. Not as a choice. As a condition of living here.

He turned forward through the decades. The hand changed, the ink changed, the entries thinned. By the 1950s the ledger had become a ceremonial object, updated a few times a year, recording ribbon-cuttings and centennials. By the 1970s it was nearly silent.

And then he reached 1994.

The pages stopped.

Not ended—stopped. There was a page dated March 1994, an entry about the society's spring meeting, and then the next leaf began mid-sentence in 1997, as though the years between had simply been excised from the town's memory. Kestrel ran his thumb along the gutter of the binding and felt it: the thin, hard edge of cut paper. Not torn. Cut. Someone had taken a razor or a very thin blade and sliced the leaves out flush against the spine, carefully, the work of an hour and a steady hand.

He counted. Between the surviving stubs, the missing run was consistent—roughly forty months, give or take, centered on the summer of 1994. The same summer the police log turned into a hymn. The same summer a boy walked out of the town's records and never walked back in.

Kestrel stood very still under the bulb.

People razored pages out of ledgers for two reasons. The first was shame. The second was fear, and fear was just shame that had learned to plan ahead.

He photographed the stubs with his phone, all four edges, the blade marks, the surviving March entry. Then he kept going, because the ledger wasn't the only thing in the box.

* * *

The photographs were sleeved in mylar, maybe two hundred of them, sorted by decade in Ruth Pell's small, exact handwriting. Kestrel went through them the way he went through everything now—slowly, looking for the thing he couldn't yet name. Ambrose at the mill opening, 1889, hat in hand. Ambrose and the first town council, all beards and gravity. Ambrose's funeral portrait, which stopped Kestrel cold for a moment—the man looked, in that formal nineteenth-century way, like he was listening to something the photographer couldn't hear.

Then the twentieth century, and a thinner run of images, and near the bottom of the box, in a sleeve marked *HAWLEY FAMILY — ASSORTED*, he found it.

A photograph, 4 by 6, color-faded to the orange of old peaches. Taken outside, on grass, in hard summer light. Ambrose Hawley—younger than Kestrel had ever seen him, though the face was unmistakable, that long jaw, those hooded eyes—stood with one hand resting on the shoulder of a boy.

The boy was maybe seven or eight. Small for his age, or maybe just slight, with fine hair and shorts and a striped shirt, one shoe on and—Kestrel leaned closer, his breath fogging the mylar—one shoe missing. Barefoot on one side. He stood slightly apart from Ambrose, angled away, the way children stand when the hand on their shoulder is a weight and not an affection.

And his face had been scratched away.

Not blurred. Not faded. Scratched—deliberately, furiously, with something sharp, the emulsion gouged down to the paper in a rough oval where the features had been. The scratches were old; the paper had relaxed around them, the edges softened by decades. Someone had done this a long time ago and done it with feeling. You don't scratch out a face absentmindedly. You scratch out a face because you cannot bear it, or because someone told you to, and both of those frightened Kestrel more than the empty coffin had.

The shoe. He couldn't stop looking at the missing shoe. A child's leather shoe, one of a pair, and here was the boy with one bare foot in the grass, and somewhere in a box in the county evidence room sat a single child's leather shoe, and Kestrel's hands began to shake, a fine tremor he couldn't command.

He photographed the picture front and back. On the reverse, in pencil, someone had written a date—*Aug. 1994*—and nothing else. No names. The date was the only caption a faceless boy was permitted.

He sat down on Ruth Pell's worktable stool and let the room be quiet around him for a long time.

*Little Hawley,* the town called him. The orphaned nephew Ambrose had taken in, the boy who vanished one August night in 1994, the story every kid in Hollow Creek learned the way they learned the flood year and the fire year—as a fact of the town's weather, a sadness with no edges, no grave, no name anyone could agree on. Kestrel had been eleven that summer. He remembered the searching, vaguely—the men walking the creek line, the flyers on the grocery store window going soft and gray in the rain. He remembered his mother crying in the kitchen one evening and his father's hand on her back and neither of them saying a word about why. He had thought, for thirty years, that he simply hadn't understood, because he was a child.

Now he was beginning to think he had understood perfectly, and that the not-saying had been the whole lesson.

* * *

He was repacking the box when he saw the dust.

The basement floor was concrete, and the concrete held dust the way old floors do—a fine gray felt over everything, disturbed only along the obvious paths. Ruth Pell had been dead four years. Maribel Toss did not strike Kestrel as a woman who went down twelve steps for anything short of a burst pipe.

The dust on the floor showed a trail of footprints. Not his. His were the fresh ones, sharp-edged, leading from the stairs to the table. These were older—days, maybe a week—and they ran not along the main aisle but along the founders' racks, back and forth, back and forth, the pacing pattern of someone searching. They ended at the rack where the ledger box sat.

Kestrel crouched. The footprints were size ten or eleven, work boot or hiking boot, the tread worn nearly smooth. He followed them with his eyes to the shelf itself, and there—on the steel lip of the shelf, in the gray felt of undisturbed years—were fingerprints. Fresh ones. The dust was thick enough that a fingertip left a perfect whorled print, and there were several, clustered where a box had been pulled out and slid back. Pulled out recently. The dust inside the old slide-marks was still settling.

He checked the other boxes on the rack. The land deeds had been moved—their box sat a half-inch proud of the shelf line, out of true with the others. The mill papers the same. Someone had gone through the founders' collection, box by box, with care and patience, and put everything back almost perfectly.

Almost.

Kestrel stood up slowly in the cold air and felt the temperature of the room change, though the bulb burned the same and the concrete held the same chill. He had thought, until this moment, that he was the only person in Hollow Creek asking questions. He had thought the silence around him was the silence of a town that didn't know what he knew.

But someone had been in this basement within the last week. Someone with steady hands and worn boot treads, going through the same boxes, looking—what? For the same pages? To make sure they were still missing? Or looking for the one thing Kestrel hadn't found yet?

He thought of Ezra Vann's honey jar on his kitchen counter, and the old sheriff's mild eyes, and the way Ezra had said *some doors stay shut for reasons* as though reciting a rule of physics. He thought of the developer's lawyers, horrid and hungry, last spring. He thought of his father, who had not returned his call from two days ago, which his father had never once failed to do in Kestrel's entire life.

He photographed the footprints. He photographed the fingerprints on the shelf, close, with the flash, though he doubted the county lab would lift anything from dust in a room this old. It didn't matter. The point of the photographs wasn't evidence. The point was that he wanted a record of the moment he stopped feeling like a man looking into the dark and started feeling like a man being looked at.

He repacked the ledger with the reverence he'd brought it out with, and the photograph last, sliding it back into its sleeve. Before he closed the box, he took the mylar sleeve holding the scratched photograph and, after a moment's argument with himself, did not put it back. He slid it inside his coat, against his chest, where it lay like a second heartbeat.

Maribel was at the front desk, dozing gently over a crossword. She started when he set the flashlight down.

"Find what you were after?" she asked.

"Some of it," Kestrel said. "Maribel—when did the developer's lawyers come through? Exactly."

"Last spring. April, maybe early May. Why, were they rude to you too?"

"And since then? Anyone else been down to the basement?"

She considered, and he watched her consider, and he saw the moment the question landed somewhere she didn't like. "Well," she said slowly, "there was a fellow. Three weeks back, perhaps. Said he was doing a history of the founding families for the sesquicentennial. Very pleasant. Very knowledgeable." She frowned at her crossword. "I never did catch his name. He had a card, I think, but I can't seem to find it now."

"What did he look like?"

"Tall. Gray at the temples. Nice boots." She said it without thinking, the way women of her generation noticed boots, and then she looked up at Kestrel and something in his face made her go still. "Kestrel? Is something wrong?"

"No," he said, and it was the second lie he'd told her, and he was getting better at them, which frightened him more than the footprints did. "Thank you, Maribel."

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of tin. Kestrel stood on the porch with the photograph pressed flat against his ribs and looked down the length of Main Street—at the monument, at the mill clock, at the tidy brick faces of the town Ambrose Hawley had invented—and felt, for the first time in his careful, invisible life, that the town was looking back.

Someone had been here first. Someone knew what he knew, or knew more.

He turned his collar up against a wind that hadn't existed an hour ago and walked to his truck, and only when he was inside with the doors locked did he take the photograph out and look at it again—the founder, the hand on the shoulder, the boy with one bare foot and no face.

*Aug. 1994,* the pencil said.

He started the engine. He was going to need to talk to the one person in Hollow Creek who had been a teenager that summer and had never once, in thirty years, said a word about it.

Delia Marsh still lived on Quarry Road.
