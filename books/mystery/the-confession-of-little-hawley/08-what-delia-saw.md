## 8. What Delia Saw

The truck came up the quarry road at eleven minutes past nine, and Delia Marsh knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat—by the rattle in the second gear, the one her father had been meaning to fix since the Nixon administration. She was seventeen years old, lying flat on the flatbed of a rusted Chevy that belonged to nobody in particular, sharing a warm bottle of Bartles & Jaymes with Bobby Trench, and she had come out to the quarry to do the thing seventeen-year-olds had been coming out to the quarry to do since the quarry was a quarry.

"Kill the light," she whispered, though Bobby hadn't turned one on. The moon was doing enough. It hung fat and orange over the rim of the pit, laying the old quarry out in silver and shadow—the terraced shelves of cut limestone going down and down to the black water at the bottom, water so still it looked poured rather than accumulated.

The truck stopped maybe forty yards from them, up on the second shelf, where the weeds had grown up through the cracks in the old loading platform. Delia squinted. Three doors opened. Not two. Three.

"That's the Hawley place's truck," Bobby breathed. "The old one. The blue one."

She knew. Everyone in Hollow Creek knew that truck. Ambrose Hawley drove it to church on Sundays and to the bank on weekdays and to the diner every morning at seven-fifteen, where he took the same booth and the same breakfast and tipped the same quarter, and the whole town arranged itself around those movements like iron filings around a magnet. Founder's blood. Founder's money. Founder's truck.

The third man was harder to make out. Big shape, moving slow. And then the moon came out from behind a cloud and she saw the third door was the passenger side of the second vehicle—the one she hadn't even registered as a vehicle at first, because it sat so quiet, nose pointed at the drop, engine ticking as it cooled.

It was Ezra Vann's department truck. And standing beside it, in the moonlight, with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral, was the sheriff himself.

"What are they doing?" Bobby whispered.

Delia didn't answer. She was counting. Ambrose Hawley, in his good coat, which was strange because it was August and hot as a skillet. Ezra Vann. And a third man who stepped into the light for half a second, and she would spend the next thirty years telling herself she hadn't recognized him, and the next thirty years knowing she had.

Thomas Dunmore.

Her father's friend. The man who'd coached her brother's Little League team. The man who'd carried her on his shoulders at the Founders' Day parade when she was six, all the way down Main Street, while she waved at everybody like a little queen.

The three of them stood at the edge of the second shelf, and something passed between them—something small, white, that Ambrose held up and then put away. A paper. A note. And then Ambrose Hawley said something she couldn't hear, and Ezra Vann laughed, and it was the wrong kind of laugh, the kind that comes out of a man when laughing is the only alternative to screaming.

Then the boy screamed instead.

It came up from the lower shelf, from near the water—a thin, high sound, a sound with a crack in the middle of it, the sound of a kid trying to be brave and failing at it. Delia's whole body went cold, colder than the night warranted, and Bobby's hand found her arm and gripped it hard.

"Little Hawley," Bobby whispered. "That's him. That's the Hawley kid."

Everyone knew Little Hawley. The orphaned nephew Ambrose had taken in after the parents died—car crash, some said; fire, said others; the stories changed every telling, which should have been the first clue. He was eleven or twelve, small for his age, with a cowlick that stood up no matter what, and he came into the diner on Saturdays and ordered a grilled cheese and ate half of it and wrapped the other half in a napkin to take home, and Delia had waited on him twice and thought, both times, that the kid had eyes like a dog that's been kicked by someone it loves.

The boy came up the switchback path from the water, stumbling, one hand out in front of him like he was feeling for something in the dark, and Ambrose Hawley went to meet him. And Delia watched—she watched, she would always have watched, she would see it behind her eyelids on her deathbed—Ambrose put his hand on the boy's head, gentle as a blessing, and the boy flinched away from it, and Ambrose's hand stayed there anyway, steering him, toward the truck, toward the shelf's edge, toward—

"Stop," Bobby said, and it wasn't clear whether he meant them or the scene.

What happened next took less than a minute and lasted thirty years. There was a scuffle at the truck. There was the white paper again, held out. There was the boy pulling back, shaking his head, backing toward the drop. And there was Thomas Dunmore, moving fast, catching the boy by the shoulders before he could go over, and holding him, and saying something low and urgent into his ear while the boy shook and shook and shook.

Then the boy stopped shaking. Whatever Thomas Dunmore said, it worked. And the boy got into Ezra Vann's department truck, and the truck sat there for a long moment with its lights off, and then it rolled away down the quarry road with its engine barely whispering, and Ambrose Hawley stood alone on the shelf in his good coat, and lit a cigarette, and looked up at the moon, and smiled.

Delia didn't see the smile. She had already pulled Bobby down flat behind the cab. But she heard the match scrape, and she heard the long exhale, and she heard him say, to nobody, to the moon, to the town that would spend decades believing whatever he told it:

"There. Now it's a story."

* * *

They never spoke of it, she and Bobby. That was the first payment—not money, but silence, offered and accepted, the way kids seal a pact without either of them quite admitting they've made one. They climbed down off the Chevy and walked the two miles back to town in the dark without holding hands and without speaking, and at the corner of Marsh and Vine, Bobby said, "We were at the drive-in," and Delia said, "Yeah. We were at the drive-in," and that was that.

The story arrived the next morning with the milk: Little Hawley had run away in the night. Ran off, the boy did, ungrateful after everything his uncle had done for him. Sheriff Vann had searched. Sheriff Vann was, quote, "very concerned." The town clucked and prayed and brought casseroles to Ambrose Hawley's big sad house on the hill, and Ambrose received them in his good coat with red-rimmed eyes, and by Labor Day the boy was a legend and by Christmas he was a lesson—*that's what happens when you don't appreciate what you're given*—and by the following spring, when Delia started her senior year, nobody said his name at all.

The money came in September.

A car she didn't recognize parked outside the Marsh house on a Tuesday, and Ambrose Hawley got out of it, and her father met him on the porch, and they talked for a while in the low, level way men talk when the subject is a thing that must never be a subject. Her father came inside afterward looking like a man who'd been hit in the face by weather.

"He wants to talk to you," her father said. "Alone. In the kitchen. I'll be right outside the door."

Ambrose Hawley sat down at their kitchen table like he owned it, because in a way he owned everything, and he put an envelope on the table between them, and he folded his hands on top of it.

"Delia," he said. "You were at the drive-in last month. August the fourteenth. You and Bobby Trench. You saw *The Flintstones* and you had popcorn and Bobby put his arm around you and you let him, and your father was very upset about it, and that's the story. That's what happened."

She was seventeen. She was holding a dish towel she didn't remember picking up. She said, "I don't know what you're talking about."

"No," Ambrose Hawley agreed pleasantly. "You don't. And that's a good quality in a person. Not knowing things." He slid the envelope across. It was heavy. It was so heavy. "There's more where that came from. Every August. On the fourteenth. For as long as you keep not knowing. College is expensive. Your mama's medical bills are expensive." He stood, and buttoned his good coat, and at the door he paused and looked back at her with an expression she would spend her whole life trying to name and would only find, at the very end, when it was too late to matter: relief. The relief of a man who has just set down something enormous.

"You're a good girl, Delia Marsh," he said. "You saw nothing. And one day, if you're very lucky, you'll forget it too."

She took the envelope. Her hands took it. That was the part she could never forgive—not the taking, but the hands, moving on their own, closing around the money like it was warm, like it was alive, like it was hers.

Her mother's treatments ran another eleven years on Hawley's August envelopes. Her college, her brother's wedding, the new roof in '02. She told herself, for thirty years, that she had spent the blood money on life, and that had to count for something, and some nights it did, and most nights it didn't.

* * *

The hospice nurse says I should rest. The hospice nurse says a lot of things. I've started keeping a list.

Delia Marsh, sixty-one years old, propped up in the good recliner by the window of the room that used to be her sewing room, wrote that line in a spiral notebook with a trembling hand and then sat for a while watching the light come through the cottonwood outside. Pancreatic. Four months, the doctors said, and one of those already gone. The morphine made the edges of things soft. She liked the edges soft. It was the middle of things that had always been the problem.

The envelope money was gone now—spent, mostly, in the last year, on the treatments that hadn't worked, which felt correct in a way she couldn't explain to anyone. The blood money, going back into the machine that was eating her. A circle closing.

Bobby Trench had died in 2019. Truck accident, on Route 9, and at the funeral she'd caught his widow's eye across the graveside and they had looked at each other for one long second, and neither of them had said a word, and both of them had known. Some silences are inherited like property. She wondered if Bobby had gotten the envelopes too. She'd never asked. She'd never asked anyone anything, for thirty years. That had been the arrangement. That had been the whole architecture of her adult life: a woman built around a hole, like a doughnut, like the quarry itself—everything arranged around the empty place where the truth used to be.

And now she was dying, and the strange thing—the thing the hospice nurse wouldn't understand, the thing she'd stopped trying to explain to Father Brennan, who kept offering her last rites like a man offering a raincoat in a drought—was that she wasn't afraid of dying. She was afraid of dying *with it*. Of carrying it down. Of Ambrose Hawley being right, after all these years, that she would forget, that everyone would forget, that the boy would dissolve completely into story and the story would dissolve into nothing and the nothing would just be the town, forever, the way the town had always been.

He was eleven, she thought. He was eleven years old and he had a cowlick and he wrapped half his grilled cheese in a napkin because his uncle fed him like he was a guest instead of a child, and the last night of his old life he was down at the water in the dark, and he was scared, and I watched, and I took the money, and I let them make him into a lesson.

She had started the notebook three days ago. Just fragments. *The blue truck. The second shelf. Three doors.* The date. The name she had never once written down in thirty years, and which her hand now wrote slowly, like a confession in itself: *Thomas Dunmore.* And under it, after a long pause, the other name, the one that had cost her the most to put on paper: *Kestrel.*

The coroner's boy. Thomas's boy. He was pulling the threads now—she'd heard it at the diner, the whole town humming with it, the exhumation, the empty coffin, the shoe. The town hummed the way it always hummed when something true got loose: too loud, too fast, everyone saying the same three sentences to each other like a spell to keep the thing at bay. But Kestrel Dunmore wasn't humming. Kestrel Dunmore was digging, in every way there was, and Delia had watched him at his mother's funeral years ago—watched him stand at the graveside with his jaw set and his hands folded, the most controlled grief she'd ever seen on a living person—and she had thought, even then: *that boy will find out. That boy doesn't know how to stop.*

She had a phone number for the coroner's office. She'd had it for two weeks, written on the back of a pharmacy receipt, and for two weeks it had sat on the table by the recliner like a loaded gun.

The hospice nurse came in, said her rest thing again, adjusted a blanket, left. The light moved across the floor. Delia looked at the receipt. She thought about her mother, eleven extra years bought with a child's erasure, her mother dying anyway, gently, in this same house, never knowing what had paid for the oxygen machine.

"Forgive me," she said out loud, to the empty room, to the boy, to nobody. It wasn't enough. She'd known for thirty years it wasn't enough. Forgiveness was a thing you had to be able to ask for out loud, to the right person, and the right person was eleven years old and thirty years dead or thirty years gone or—God, she didn't even know which, that was the thing, that was the rot at the center of it, she didn't even know if she'd watched a murder or a kidnapping or something else entirely, some third thing those three men had invented between them on the second shelf of the quarry under a fat orange moon.

She picked up the phone.

It rang twice. Then a voice she knew—Thomas Dunmore's jaw, his mother's careful quiet—said, "Coroner's office."

"Mr. Dunmore," Delia Marsh said, and her voice held, which surprised her, held steady as poured water. "This is Delia. Delia Marsh. I need to tell you what I saw at the quarry on August the fourteenth, 1994. All of it. And there's something else you need to know first—about the night your father drove that boy out of town." She closed her eyes. "He wasn't the only one in that truck, Kestrel. There was a woman in the front seat. And I think you've been looking for her your whole life."

The line went very quiet. Somewhere in the background, she could hear a file drawer rolling open.

"Tell me everything," Kestrel Dunmore said.
