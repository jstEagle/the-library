## 16. The Last Conspirator

The list of the living had gotten short enough to fit on an index card.

Kestrel wrote it out that morning at his kitchen table, because he was a coroner and lists were how he thought. Ambrose Hawley—dead, twice over, once officially and once in fact. Thomas Dunmore—alive but sealed shut, a man who had looked at his own son across a kitchen table and given him nothing but the flat gray stare of a man watching a door close. Marion Dunmore—his mother, gone eleven years now, her letters to a cousin in Montana the only testimony she'd ever left. Delia Marsh—dead in the spring, her confession delivered from a hospice bed with an oxygen tube rasping under her words like wind through a screen door.

That left one name.

*Ezra Vann.*

Kestrel sat looking at it for a long time. The card was small and white and ordinary, and on it rested the entire weight of thirty years of silence. Every other conspirator had slipped past justice the way people slip past weather—by dying, by leaving, by simply outlasting the interest of anyone who cared. But Ezra Vann was eighty-one years old, sharp as a tack driven flush into pine, living alone at the edge of town with forty hives of bees and a conscience he'd been feeding lies to since 1994.

He was chargeable. That was the word Kestrel kept coming back to. *Chargeable.* Obstruction, evidence tampering, falsification of records—the empty coffin alone was a felony with no statute long enough to cover it, because the coffin hadn't been staged once in 1994. It had been staged again when Ambrose died, whenever that actually was, and maintained by omission every single year since. The lie was ongoing. The lie was still breathing.

And Ezra Vann had put his signature on it.

Kestrel turned the card over. On the back, in smaller writing, he'd listed what he knew for certain, the way he'd catalog a body: cause of death, mechanism, manner. *The boy lived. His name was August Hawley. He died two years ago in Billings, under another name, and there is a cousin in Montana who has his letters.* Below that: *My mother helped hide him. My father helped bury the story. Ezra Vann signed the disappearance report knowing both.*

It read like an indictment already. All it lacked was a prosecutor.

He was still holding the card when the knock came at his door—not the office door, his home door—and he knew before he opened it who it would be, because in Hollow Creek nobody knocked on Wendell Quintana's door at eight in the morning unless they wanted something the town wouldn't give them politely.

Grant Halloway stood on his porch wearing a suit that cost more than Kestrel's truck, holding a leather folder the way a man holds a hymnal.

"Mr. Dunmore." Halloway smiled the way developers smile, all teeth and no eyes. "Got a minute?"

"I've got several," Kestrel said, and didn't move aside. "But I'm guessing you don't want to use them talking about the weather."

Halloway laughed, a good sport's laugh, and glanced pointedly at the open door until Kestrel relented and let him into the kitchen. He took the chair across from the table without being asked, which told Kestrel everything—the man had been briefed, coached, prepared. He set the folder down between them like a card he intended to play slowly.

"Can I get you coffee?"

"No thank you." Halloway folded his hands. "I'll be brief. I know you've been digging into the Hawley matter."

"The Hawley matter." Kestrel leaned against the counter. "You mean the exhumation you ordered. The one that opened up the biggest scandal this town has seen since its founding."

"The exhumation I was *required* to order," Halloway corrected gently. "A title dispute is a title dispute. I didn't ask for what came out of that box." He paused, and something flickered behind the smile—not quite honesty, but adjacent to it. "Though I'll admit, when my lawyers saw the shoe, we understood we were standing on something bigger than a property line."

"And?"

"And." Halloway opened the folder. "Hollow Creek Holdings has retained me to manage the development of the north parcel. Two hundred units, a commercial strip, infrastructure contracts going to firms in three counties. That project employs four hundred people during construction and supports maybe sixty permanent jobs after. It is, Mr. Dunmore, the only economic event of consequence to happen to this town in thirty years."

"Is there a question in there somewhere?"

"There's a problem." Halloway slid a printed page across the table. It was a news clipping, three days old, from the county paper: *CORONER PROBES 1994 DISAPPEARANCE OF TOWN FOUNDER'S WARD.* Someone had talked. Delia's niece, probably, or one of the hospice nurses. The story was thin but the headline did its work. "Lenders read headlines. So do insurers. So do the state agencies holding our permits. Right now, this project is viable because the Hawley story is a curiosity—a historical oddity. If it becomes a *scandal*, if it becomes a criminal investigation with a named suspect, the financing evaporates inside a week. I've watched it happen. Banks don't wait for verdicts. They wait for risk, and they run from it."

"So you're here to ask me to stop investigating."

"I'm here to offer you a consulting position." Halloway turned the folder around. Inside lay a single sheet, and beneath it a check, and the number on the check had enough zeros in it to buy Kestrel's house twice and the lot it sat on. "Historical consultant. Your knowledge of the town's burial records is, frankly, unmatched. We'd want you documenting the cemetery relocation—there are graves on the north parcel that have to be moved, and you're the only man alive who knows every one of them by name. It's real work. Respectful work. And it comes with a confidentiality clause, standard boilerplate, nothing sinister. You'd simply agree not to make public statements regarding matters under historical review while the project is underway."

"Which matters?"

"Hawley-adjacent matters."

Kestrel almost laughed. Almost. Instead he picked up the check and looked at it the way he'd look at an unidentified body—with professional detachment and a growing certainty that whatever it had been in life, it was not what it claimed.

"You know what I keep thinking about," Kestrel said quietly. "That shoe. A child's leather shoe, sitting in a founder's coffin for thirty years, and this whole town walked past that grave every Founders' Day and laid flowers on it and told their children the story of Ambrose Hawley, the great man, the generous man. Nobody wondered why the box was light. Nobody wanted to wonder." He set the check down. "And now you walk into my kitchen offering me more money than I've made in ten years, and what you're really selling is the same thing the town's been buying since 1994. A comfortable lie with a nice lawn built on top of it."

Halloway's smile thinned but held. "I'm offering you a choice, Mr. Dunmore. That's more than most people get. You can take the check, move four hundred families into homes they couldn't otherwise afford, and let a dead boy rest however dead boys rest. Or you can keep pulling threads, and when the financing collapses and the permits die and this town stays exactly as poor and proud and shrinking as it's been for three decades, you can stand in the ruins holding a file full of truth and tell yourself it was worth it." He stood, buttoned his jacket. "I'd think about which one your mother would have wanted."

Kestrel went very still. "You leave her out of this."

"Then I'll leave her *in* it, if you prefer." Halloway gathered his folder, unhurried. "Marion Dunmore helped hide that child. That's in your own notes, isn't it? You prosecute Ezra Vann, you prosecute the whole chain, and the chain runs through your parents' kitchen. The town won't hear 'the sheriff covered it up.' They'll hear 'the Dunmores hid a missing child and their son waited thirty years to sell the story.' That's how it'll land. That's how these things always land." At the door he paused, and for one moment the developer's polish dropped and Kestrel saw the older, tireder thing underneath—a man who had done this arithmetic many times and never enjoyed it. "I grew up here too, you know. Fourth generation. I'd rather build houses than funerals. Think about it."

The door closed behind him. Kestrel stood in his kitchen with the check on the table and the index card in his hand, and outside the window the town of Hollow Creek went about its morning—school buses, coffee at the diner, old men arguing softly outside the hardware store about rain. Four hundred families. Sixty jobs. A future, of a kind.

And underneath it, like a shoe in a coffin, a boy whose name nobody wanted spoken.

* * *

He drove out to the cemetery that afternoon without deciding to. His hands just did it, the way they always had when his head got loud—the truck finding the county road, the road finding the gate, the gate opening onto the rows he knew better than his own handwriting.

Every grave by name. That had been his pride, once. Alden Pruitt, 1911–1979, killed by his own tractor. Ruth Calloway, who outlived three husbands and buried a daughter and still brought canned peaches to every funeral for forty years. The infants' row along the east fence, stones so small the grass nearly swallowed them, each one known to him anyway—because someone had to know, and for twenty years that someone had been him, invisible, reliable, the keeper of names.

Now he walked the rows and heard Halloway's voice instead. *The town won't hear 'the sheriff covered it up.'*

Wasn't that the truth of it. He'd felt it already, in small doses, the way you feel frost coming before the thermometer admits it. Marge Ellerbee at the diner, who'd served him pie every Friday for fifteen years, suddenly serving it with a look. The county clerk taking an extra beat before handing over records, extra beat meaning *someone called*. Old Tom Bricker, who'd fished with his father, stopping mid-hello at the hardware store and settling for a nod. Not hostility. Worse than hostility. *Recalculation.* The town doing quiet math on the Dunmore name, deciding what it was worth, whether the son was the keeper of secrets or the seller of them.

They preferred the myth. Of course they did. The myth had a statue in the square and a holiday and a founding story clean enough to teach children. August Hawley had nothing—not even a stone, not even a name, just a shoe in a rich man's box and a lunchbox rusting in a drained quarry. The town had chosen, thirty years ago, and it was choosing again right now, in diners and church pews and county offices, choosing comfort over the boy the way it always had.

Kestrel stopped at the founders' monument, the granite obelisk where he knew—now, thanks to an old beekeeper's half-confession—the boy's true marker lay hidden beneath the foundation. He put his palm flat against the cold stone. Somewhere under his hand, under sixty tons of honored lies, was a small marker reading only *Our Boy*, carved by hands that couldn't say more.

"Not much longer," he said aloud. The wind took it.

His phone buzzed. A text from the county attorney's office, terse as a telegram: *Statute clock confirmed. 47 days remaining on obstruction window. Need formal complaint filing to preserve. Confirm intent by Friday.*

Forty-seven days. He looked at the check still sitting in his truck's passenger seat—he'd carried it out without meaning to, evidence or temptation, he wasn't sure which. Forty-seven days versus a number with enough zeros to erase a lifetime of careful invisibility.

He thought about his mother's letters, the ones she'd written from wherever she'd spent those three months, the ones that said *he is safe, he is sleeping, he ate well today*—a woman describing a hidden child in the language of a mother, because that's what she'd briefly become. She'd broken the law. She'd also kept a hurt boy alive when powerful men wanted him gone. Both things were true, and the town would only ever be able to hold one of them.

Ezra Vann could be charged. Only Ezra. The last conspirator, the fall the whole structure would land on, and everyone in Hollow Creek would breathe easier for it—one guilty old man, case closed, monument stays, myth survives, and the Dunmore name takes the secondary wound and bleeds quietly forever.

Or.

Kestrel got back in the truck. The check rode beside him all the way home, and when he pulled into his drive he picked it up, held it over the trash can in the kitchen—

—and stopped.

Because that was the easy gesture, wasn't it? The dramatic refusal, the torn check, the scene the movies taught you. And drama was a kind of performance, and performance was exactly what this town had been drowning in for thirty years. He didn't need to perform his integrity. He needed to *file* it.

He smoothed the check flat instead and tucked it into the case file, labeled it in his neat coroner's hand—*Exhibit: attempted inducement, G. Halloway, [date]*—and set it beneath the index card with the one name left on it.

Then he picked up the phone and dialed the county attorney directly, no text, no hedging.

"It's Kestrel Dunmore," he said, when the line connected. "Confirming intent. I'm filing the complaint against Ezra Vann. Full chain of custody on the evidence. And I want it on the record that I was offered money today to make this go away, and I'm entering that offer into evidence too."

A pause on the line. Then, dry as dust: "You understand what this costs you."

"Yes," Kestrel said, looking out the window at the town he'd served invisibly for twenty years, the town that preferred its myths, the town that was even now recalculating what a Dunmore was worth. "I've done the math. I'm filing anyway."

He hung up. Forty-six days now.

And somewhere out at the edge of town, forty hives hummed in the late light around an old man who didn't yet know he was the last name on the card—and Kestrel intended to be standing in front of him, hat in hand and file under arm, before the bees settled for the night.
