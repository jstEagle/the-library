## 1. Postmarked Yesterday

The trouble with running a bookshop, Coral Lockridge had decided somewhere around her fortieth cup of tea that morning, was that books were the only merchandise in the world that actively conspired against their seller. People came in, browsed for an hour, read entire chapters standing in your aisles like it was a public library with delusions of commerce, and then left with a bookmark from the pound shop down the road.

"Morning, Coral!" called Mrs. Pankhurst from the doorway, shaking rain off her umbrella with the enthusiasm of a woman who had never once considered that umbrellas were designed to be shaken *outside*.

"Morning, Edith."

"You've moved the poetry again."

"I have not moved the poetry, Edith. The poetry has been in the same place since March."

"Well, it's *somewhere else* now." Mrs. Pankhurst peered at her over her glasses, triumphant, and shuffled off toward the crime section, which — Coral noted with professional resignation — she would browse for twenty minutes and then purchase a large-print romance she'd already read twice.

Coral let the door chime settle and turned back to the mail pile on the counter. Three months into retirement and this was her kingdom now: a till that never quite covered the rent, a radiator that clanked like a prisoner demanding release, and a stack of envelopes that were, without exception, bills, more bills, or the quarterly newsletter of the Devonshire Ferret Fanciers' Society, which she had never joined but could not bring herself to cancel because its editor wrote with such genuine joy about ferret agility trials.

She sorted with the automatic efficiency of thirty years' habit. Electric company. Water rates. A flyer for a tarot reader in Torquay who promised to "reveal what the cards know about YOUR debts," which felt pointed. And then —

And then an envelope with no bill inside it at all.

It was ordinary-looking, which was the first wrong thing about it. Cream paper, good stock, the kind you'd buy at a stationer's rather than a supermarket. Her name and address written in a careful, upright hand, blue ink, no smudges. No return address. The postmark read:

**ASHCOMBE BEACH — 14 OCT**

Coral stood very still.

Three months ago, on the fifteenth of October, she had attended the funeral of Vivian Ashe. Not as a guest. She hadn't been invited, and Vivian's family wouldn't have wanted her there anyway — the detective who'd spent eleven months failing to find out who put their wife and sister and mother into the ground. She'd stood at the back of the churchyard in a coat that wasn't waterproof enough, watching Daniel Ashe hold himself together with the rigid dignity of a man who'd practiced his grief in a mirror, and she'd thought: *one of these people knows.*

She'd thought it every day for eleven months before that, too.

The case had gone cold the way cases do — not dramatically, not with a dramatic break or a dramatic dead end, but the way a fire goes out: slowly, and then all at once, and then everyone stops bringing wood. The Ashe file sat in a cabinet at Ashcombe CID with COLD CASE stamped across it in red ink that always struck Coral as unnecessarily theatrical. She kept her own copies. Of course she kept her own copies. Marcus Hale had told her once, gently, that letting go was part of retirement, and she'd told him, less gently, that letting go was what people said when they meant *give up*, and they hadn't spoken for a month afterward.

But the postmark. The postmark was dated the fourteenth of October. The day after the funeral.

A letter that had been sitting somewhere — in a sorting office, behind a dresser, in a coat pocket — for three months, and had chosen today, of all days, to arrive.

"Everything alright, dear?" Mrs. Pankhurst had materialized at the counter, holding a copy of something with a shirtless man on the cover. "You've gone the color of porridge."

"Fine, Edith. That'll be three ninety-nine."

"It was two ninety-nine last week."

"Inflation," said Coral, who knew perfectly well it had been two ninety-nine last week, and watched the old woman out the door before she allowed herself to pick up the letter opener.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected them to shake, and instead they were steady in the way they used to be steady, years ago, in interview rooms and at crime scenes — a steadiness she'd assumed had retired when she did, along with the warrant card and the habit of sleeping through the night.

She opened the envelope. Inside was a single sheet, folded in thirds, written in the same careful hand, front and back, no date, no signature.

*Dear Detective Inspector Lockridge,*

*I am writing to confess to the murder of Vivian Ashe.*

Coral set the letter down on the counter. She looked at it. She picked up her tea, found it cold, drank it anyway, and set it down again.

Then she read the whole thing.

*You will want to verify everything I tell you before you believe a word of it, so I will make it easy for you. Vivian Ashe died on the second of September. She was struck once, from behind, with a heavy object — you concluded a bronze bookend, though the weapon was never recovered. She was killed in her own kitchen between eight and nine in the evening, while her husband was at his Rotary dinner in Exeter and her daughter was, as far as anyone knew, still in Manchester.*

*There are things about that night that were never made public. I will give you three.*

*First: the kitchen window above the sink was open six inches. You believed it had been opened by the killer as an exit, but it was opened by Vivian herself, earlier that evening, because the smell of the lamb she'd burned had gotten into the curtains. She never got the smell out. It embarrassed her.*

*Second: she was wearing one slipper. The other was later found under the hall table, and you were told it was unremarkable. But she had taken it off to kick at the oven door, which stuck, and she swore at it — she swore beautifully, like a docker — and then she laughed at herself, alone in her kitchen, and that is the last thing she ever did.*

*Third:*

Coral stopped reading. Not because the letter frightened her, though it did, in the way a floorboard creaking at 3 a.m. frightens you — not with certainty, but with possibility. She stopped because her eyes had snagged on the next line and refused, for a long moment, to move past it.

*Third: you know all of this already, don't you, Magpie? You collected every shiny detail of that house and carried them home and lined them up on a shelf where nobody else could see them. The window. The slipper. The burned lamb. None of it was ever in a report. None of it was ever said aloud outside that kitchen. But you knew. You always knew, Magpie. You just couldn't prove any of it, and it ate you alive, and I am sorry for that. Truly. This letter is, in part, an apology.*

Coral became aware, distantly, that the radiator had started clanking again, and that somewhere in the shop Mrs. Pankhurst's replacement — a student named Priya who worked Tuesdays and Thursdays and had the disconcerting habit of knowing exactly where every book was — was reshelving with the soft thump of spines. The world was continuing. The world was under no obligation to stop just because a piece of cream-colored paper had reached across three months and thirty years and tapped Coral Lockridge on the shoulder.

*Magpie.*

Nobody had called her that in years. It had started as an insult, early in her career — some DS from another division sneering that she hoarded details the way magpies hoard anything that glitters — and she had done what she always did with insults, which was wear them until they fit like a favorite coat. Her team had adopted it. Her team, and only her team. It appeared in exactly one place in the official record: a witness statement from the Ashe investigation, in which a neighbor described "the detective — the one they call Magpie" going through the garden hedge with a torch. Marcus had fought to have it redacted. He'd lost. So it existed, technically, publicly — buried in a statement nobody had ever read, in a file nobody had ever pulled.

Except someone had pulled it. Or someone had been there. Or someone had watched her work that hedge, that night, with the torch beam jumping, and had known what the nickname meant to her — known it the way you can only know a thing from the inside.

The letter continued.

*You will ask yourself why now. Why three months after the funeral. Why not sooner, why not at the trial that never happened, why not pressed into the hands of someone still wearing a badge. I will answer that honestly: because I am a coward, and cowards postmark their courage and then lose their nerve. This letter sat finished for a long time. Something has changed. I cannot say what, yet. But you are the only person I trust to look at this properly, and I know that is unfair, because looking properly is what broke you.*

*One more thing, and then I will let you get back to your shop, which I hear is struggling, and which I am sorry about, because I know how much it means to you.*

*Vivian kept two ledgers. You found the first one — the green one, the accounts, the one full of her beautiful vicious handwriting tracking every penny of the Ashe family money. It logged nothing unusual, and you believed it, because there was no reason not to. But there was a second ledger. It was never logged as evidence because it was never found. It exists. I promise you it exists. Find it, and you will understand everything — including why I am writing to you instead of walking into your old station and putting my hands on a desk.*

*Burn this if you need to. I suspect you won't. Magpies don't burn anything shiny.*

*Yours,*
*A coward*

Coral read it twice more. Then a third time, standing behind her counter in her failing bookshop while the rain came down on Ashcombe High Street and Edith Pankhurst's umbrella dripped a small puddle onto her doormat, and by the third reading her hands had finally begun to shake — not from fear, she realized, but from something older and less dignified.

Relief. Horrible, shameful relief. Because for eleven months she had carried the Ashe case like a stone in her chest, and everyone — Marcus, her doctor, the well-meaning woman at the retirement seminar who'd recommended journaling — had told her to put it down. And here, at last, was proof that the stone had weight. That she hadn't imagined the shape of it. That the window had been six inches open and the slipper had been under the hall table and Vivian Ashe had laughed at her own swearing, alone, minutes before somebody killed her for reasons that lived in a ledger nobody had ever found.

Someone out there knew she'd been right.

Someone out there also, presumably, knew exactly how to hurt her, because they'd chosen the one name that opened her up like a lock.

"Coral?" Priya's head appeared around the biography shelf. "There's a man asking if we take second-hand paperbacks. Also, are you okay? You've read that letter about nine times."

"I'm fine." Coral folded the page along its original creases, precisely, and slid it back into its envelope. "Tell him yes, but I pay in store credit and judgment."

Priya laughed and withdrew. Coral waited until the chime announced the man's departure — with, she noted, a second-hand Le Carré he'd haggled down to a pound fifty — and then she locked the front door, flipped the sign to CLOSED, and stood in the middle of her empty shop surrounded by ten thousand books, every one of them containing a murder solved by someone smarter than she had been.

The green ledger. She remembered it vividly: Vivian Ashe's account book, recovered from the desk drawer in the study, its columns neat as stitching, documenting a household that appeared, on paper, to be entirely ordinary. Nothing hidden. Nothing glittering. She'd believed it because she'd wanted to believe it — because the alternative was another month of warrants and subpoenas and Daniel Ashe's lawyer smiling at her across a table.

But a second ledger. Hidden. Recording what?

Coral looked down at the envelope in her hands. Postmarked the fourteenth of October. Delivered today, the twelfth of January, to a woman who hadn't held a warrant card in over a year. Whoever had written it had either lost it for three months — unlikely, given the care in every stroke of that blue ink — or had chosen, deliberately, to let it age. To let the trail go cold and stale before handing her the match.

Or — and this thought arrived quietly, the way the worst thoughts always did — the writer had needed those three months. Needed something to happen first. Something that had changed, as the letter said. Something that had turned a coward's finished confession into a posted one.

Coral walked to the back of the shop, past the poetry (which had, she conceded privately, indeed drifted), into the little storeroom where the radiator lived and where, beneath a false bottom in a box marked WINTER SALE — DO NOT OPEN UNTIL DEC, she kept the private copies of the Ashe file. Thirty years of habit. Marcus called it her security blanket. She called it due diligence, and she had never once, in all her life, slept properly without it within reach.

She knelt, lifted the false bottom, and drew out the folder.

The paper smelled of dust and the particular staleness of unfinished business. On top lay the photograph of Vivian Ashe, taken for the missing-persons inquiry that had preceded the murder inquiry by exactly forty-eight hours — a handsome woman of sixty-one with her chin lifted, daring the camera to find fault.

"Right, then," Coral said aloud, to the radiator, to the rain, to the ghost of a burned lamb. "Let's see what you've got, Magpie."

She spread the file across the storeroom floor, and began — for the first time in three months, with the first real hope she'd felt since the day she'd handed in her badge — to look for the second ledger.
