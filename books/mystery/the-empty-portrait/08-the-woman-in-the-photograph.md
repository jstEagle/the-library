## 8. The Woman in the Photograph

There's a particular kind of silence that lives inside archives. It isn't the absence of sound — it's the presence of everything that was ever said and then filed away. I'd been sitting in the county records annex for three hours by the time I found it, wedged between a 1993 harvest festival program and a zoning dispute about a fence nobody ever built: a photograph of the Marchand estate's summer gala, printed in the *Verren County Gazette* on the fourteenth of August, 1993.

Two women stood on the terrace beneath the wisteria. The caption read: *Elise Marchand (left) with a guest at the annual Midsummer Gala.*

The guest's face had been scratched out.

Not blurred — the paper predated digital anything — but physically scratched, with something sharp enough to bite through newsprint and into whatever lay beneath. The strokes were deliberate, vertical, almost methodical. Someone had sat down with this photograph and taken their time. You don't do that in anger. Anger is fast. This was maintenance.

I photographed it with my phone, then bought the whole bound volume for forty dollars because the archivist gave me a look that said *we close at five* and I've learned to respect the small tyrannies.

* * *

Back at my apartment, I laid the image out on the light table I use for examining documents — a habit from my journalism days that podcasting never quite cured me of. The scratched woman stood slightly behind Elise Marchand, one hand resting on her shoulder. A gesture of intimacy. Sisters do it. Lovers do it. So do people who are standing very still while someone else decides what they're allowed to be.

The scratch marks were the problem. Newsprint is cheap and fibrous, and gouging through it tears as much as it removes. But here's what thirty years of true crime work teaches you: destruction leaves fingerprints of its own. Whatever was removed had been removed by someone who understood that a face is mostly geometry — the angle of a jaw, the set of an eye, the line where hair meets temple. They'd gone for all of it.

They'd missed the hands.

I zoomed in until the halftone dots swam like plankton. The scratched-out woman's left hand rested on Elise's shoulder, fingers relaxed, and on her ring finger — barely resolvable, three or four pixels of suggestion — there appeared to be no ring at all. Elise, married to Henri Marchand since 1988 according to every record I could find, wore hers plainly visible even at this resolution. Her right hand hung at her side, and the band caught the flash like a small accusation.

So the faceless woman wasn't the wife. Or she was a wife who didn't wear the ring. Or — and this was the thought I kept circling like a moth around a bulb — she was someone who didn't need to wear it, because everyone already assumed.

I called Delia Fontaine at nine the next morning. Delia runs a forensic imaging consultancy out of a converted garage in Portland and has the temperament of a woman who has spent twenty years explaining to lawyers why they can't just "enhance" things. She is also the only person I trust with photographic restoration, mostly because she tells me no more often than yes.

"You want me to reconstruct a face from a scratched-out newsprint photo from 1993," she said, before I'd finished the sentence.

"I want you to tell me if it's possible."

"It's not possible."

"Delia—"

"What I *can* do," she said, with the sigh of the perpetually conscripted, "is analyze the residual impression. When you scratch newsprint, the fibers deform but the ink doesn't fully leave. Under raking light and multispectral imaging, sometimes you get enough tonal variance to build a probability map. Not a face. A ghost of a face. And Vesper—" her voice shifted, professional to personal, which for Delia meant dropping maybe ten percent of the frost, "—if this is what I think it is, you need to understand what you're asking me to make. Once a face exists, it can't un-exist."

"That's rather the point of my entire career."

"Your career is a cautionary tale," she said, and hung up.

* * *

While I waited for Delia, I did what I always do when a case turns strange: I went back to the beginning and started counting things that shouldn't match.

The 1994 file on Elise Marchand's drowning was thin in a curated way — I'd written that phrase in my notes back in chapter two of this disaster, metaphorically speaking, and I stand by it. But thinness can hide in plain sight. What I hadn't done was count the photographs.

The estate had been generous with images over the years. Local histories, newspaper features, the B&B's website with its tasteful sepia gallery. Elise at her easel. Elise in the garden. Elise at the gala, alone, radiant. Elise and Henri cutting a cake at some anniversary. I pulled every image I could find, high-resolution where possible, and put them side by side on the light table.

Here's the thing about faces: they're supposed to change. Thirty years of photographs of the same person should show weight shifting, hairlines retreating, smiles deepening into character. What they should never show is *stasis*. And yet the woman in the 2005 B&B brochure — the innkeeper's beloved portrait subject, the Elise everyone remembered — looked, allowing for lighting and resolution, exactly like the woman in the 1993 gala photo.

Not similar. Identical. The kind of identical that makes your hindbrain whisper *twin* before your forebrain can slap it down.

Except Elise Marchand had no twin. I'd checked. Birth records, census entries, a 1971 profile in the Gazette headlined *THE PRODIGY OF VERREN COUNTY* that made charming mention of her being "an only child, raised among her father's books." No siblings. No twins. The article even quoted her mother saying, "We were blessed once and knew better than to ask twice."

Which meant either the woman in the recent photos had aged with the eerie perfection of a painting — and God, after what I'd found in that frame, I was no longer willing to laugh that off — or there had been two women all along, and the records had only ever counted one.

* * *

Delia called on Thursday. "Come look at this in person. I'm not emailing it."

That's never good. That's the forensic equivalent of *we need to talk.*

Her garage office smelled of coffee and cold electronics. On the monitor was the gala photograph, processed into something that looked less like an image than like a fever dream of one — tonal gradients rendered in false color, the scratched region glowing amber where the fibers held their secrets.

"I built a probability map of the underlying features," Delia said, tapping the screen. "Jawline, orbital structure, nasal bridge. Then I ran it against a reference." She pulled up the clear image of Elise from the same photograph. Two wireframe overlays appeared side by side, ghost-faces rendered in contour lines.

They were the same face.

Not "similar." Not "could be related." The measurements matched within tolerances tighter than most sibling comparisons I've seen. Same asymmetric tilt of the left eye — half a degree higher than the right. Same slight deviation of the nasal septum. Same jawline curve, same chin.

"Identical twins?" I asked, though I already knew the answer.

"Genetically identical, presented identically, aged identically? This reads as the same person." Delia leaned back. "But here's what kept me up last night. Look at the hands again — I cleaned up the whole frame, not just the face."

She enlarged the lower portion. The two women, side by side now, both fully resolved except for the amber ghost where a face used to be. Elise in her pale dress. The other woman in dark blue, hand on Elise's shoulder.

"The posture," Delia said. "Watch this." She overlaid the two figures' silhouettes, scaled to equal height. The outlines nearly coincided. Shoulder width, stance, the way each woman carried her weight fractionally on the left leg. "People mirror when they're comfortable together. Twins especially. But this—" she tapped the overlay, "—this is choreography. Like they'd practiced standing as each other."

Or practiced standing *in place of* each other.

I thought about the blank canvas in its ornate frame. About the innkeeper's serene insistence that there had always been a painting. About three townspeople describing three different women with the smooth confidence of people reciting lines they'd agreed upon. I'd been treating those as separate anomalies — a vandalized portrait, a curated file, manufactured memory. Standing in Delia's garage, looking at one woman wearing another woman's outline, they snapped together into a single shape, and the shape was terrifyingly simple.

What if the erasure hadn't started in 1994?

What if it had started years earlier, quietly, one photograph at a time?

"Vesper." Delia was watching me with the expression of someone who has delivered bad news before and learned to wait for it to land. "You went somewhere just now. Where?"

"If there were two of them," I said slowly, "and only one of them was ever in the records... then the question isn't who killed Elise Marchand. The question is which one of these women anyone was ever actually looking at."

Delia nodded slowly. "And the one whose face got scratched out?"

"Yeah."

"She's the one somebody wanted forgotten first."

* * *

I spent the drive home composing and deleting questions. If the faceless woman was real — a second woman, identical to Elise, present at the 1993 gala, intimate enough to touch her shoulder — then someone in Verren County remembered her. Someone had catered to her, danced with her, maybe loved her. Memory in that town had proven itself negotiable, but negotiation requires at least two parties, and somewhere there had to be a party who'd broken terms.

My client, Aldous Wren, had been the estate manager in 1993. He would have been at that gala. He might have poured the wine those two women drank.

But Wren was a locked door I wasn't ready to force, and besides, there was a nearer source. The innkeeper, Marta Osei, had run the B&B for eleven years and had lived in Verren County her whole life. When I'd asked her about the portrait, she'd lied with the calm of long practice. But people who lie about one thing often tell the truth about adjacent things, because adjacent things feel safe.

I stopped at the estate on my way through town. Late afternoon light, the wisteria over the terrace gone woody and gray with autumn. I found Marta refilling the bird feeder by the kitchen garden, and I didn't lead with the photograph. I led with hospitality.

"Do you ever get guests who ask about the history?" I asked. "Beyond the portrait, I mean. Old parties, old staff?"

"Sometimes." She scattered seed with an economy of motion that suggested decades of birds. "Mostly they want ghosts. Every old house gets asked if it's haunted. I tell them the truth — the house isn't haunted, it's just tired."

"And the galas? The Marchands used to hold them, I read."

"Mm. Before my time working here, but I came to a few as a girl. My mother did the flowers." Something moved behind her eyes — not guilt, exactly. Inventory. The look of a woman checking which shelves she was willing to open. "The last big one was '93. Beautiful summer. Everyone was there."

"Everyone," I said. "Who's everyone?"

"The family. Town families. The Wrens — Aldous ran the place, his little Celeste used to chase peacocks through the hedge maze." A faint smile, quickly folded away. "And Mrs. Marchand, of course. Elise."

"Just Elise? Just the one Mrs. Marchand?"

The seed scatterer went still. For a moment — half a second, maybe less — Marta Osei looked at me with an expression I have only seen twice before in my career, and both times it preceded a confession or a door slamming shut. It's the look of someone doing arithmetic with their own survival.

"There was only ever one Elise Marchand," she said. Each word placed like a stone in a wall.

"Of course," I said pleasantly. "Silly question."

I turned to go, and then, because I am constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone, I paused at the gate and said, without turning around:

"The photograph from that gala. The one in the Gazette. Did you know someone scratched out one of the faces?"

Silence. Long enough that I heard the peacocks — descendants, presumably, of Celeste Wren's quarry — calling from somewhere past the hedge maze.

When Marta finally spoke, her voice had changed. Flatter. Careful. "Mr. Joubert. You seem like a man who wants answers, so let me give you one for free. In this town, we don't talk about the year before Elise died. Ask me why, and I'll tell you I don't know. Ask anyone, and they'll tell you the same. And we will all be telling you the truth, because none of us know *why*. We only know that we agreed."

"Agreed with whom?"

"That," she said, setting down the seed tin with a small, final click, "is the question I've been afraid of for thirty years. Good afternoon."

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, so I did what insomniac investigators do: I made tea and interrogated my own notes. The apartment was quiet except for the radiator ticking and the cursor blinking on my episode draft like a slow heartbeat.

One woman or two. One woman twice. The possibilities kept folding into each other like reflections between mirrors. If there were two identical women, then the drowned artist, the painted portrait, the woman in the recent photographs — any of them could be either sister, and every record I'd trusted was a coin flip dressed as fact. If there was only ever one woman, then the second figure in the photograph was something stranger: a role, a performance, a person trained to be her. And either way, someone had taken a sharp instrument to a newspaper in 1993 or 1994 and carefully, patiently, erased a face — not out of rage, but out of policy.

The scratching bothered me most, in the end. Rage burns evidence. Policy preserves it. Whoever destroyed that face wanted the photograph to survive — wanted the record intact, complete, *except* for one person. That's not concealment. That's editing.

And editors, in my experience, always keep a copy of the original.

I was still staring at the ceiling around two in the morning when the thought arrived, fully formed and unwelcome: the Marchand estate had a basement full of reel-to-reel tapes I'd only begun to catalog. Elise's voice, cataloguing sittings, clients, commissions. If there had been a second woman — a guest at the gala, a hand on a shoulder — then somewhere in those recordings, Elise might have said her name.

Or, worse, Elise might have said her own name twice, in two different voices, and I wouldn't be able to tell them apart.

I got up, pulled on my coat, and emailed Delia a single line: *Can you do the same restoration on audio?*

Then I opened my case file and wrote, in the space I reserve for the sentence that changes everything:

*The victim may not be the one who died.*

Outside, the rain started again, soft against the window, patient as an archivist. Somewhere across town, in a hospice bed, a dying man was keeping a secret he'd already paid me to find — and in a decaying house on the hill, a blank canvas hung waiting in its frame like a held breath.

I just needed to know how many women had been holding it.
