## 10. The Alibi Woven Twice

There's a particular kind of silence that hospices cultivate — not the silence of death, which is loud in its own way, all machines and grief, but the silence of waiting. A building full of people who have run out of future and are learning to sit with that. The Marchand Regional Care Facility had it down to a science: beige walls, the smell of lavender and floor wax, a receptionist who looked at me like I was already a memory she'd rather not revisit.

"I'm here for Aldous Wren," I said. "Family."

"Are you family?"

"Close enough to be asked to leave by name."

She didn't laugh. Hospice staff never do; they've heard every joke the dying make and none of them are funny twice.

* * *

Aldous Wren looked smaller than he had in the photograph I'd found from 1994 — a broad-shouldered man in an estate manager's blazer, standing at the edge of a garden party with the expression of someone counting exits. Thirty years later the shoulders were gone, folded into a hospital bed like a tent taken down for winter. His skin had gone the color of old paper. But his eyes were still doing arithmetic when they landed on me.

"You're the podcaster," he said. Voice like gravel poured slow.

"Vesper Joubert."

"I know who you are. I sent you the money." He coughed, wet and long, and I waited through it because there was nowhere else to be. "I told you on the phone. Don't come back."

"You said don't call again. Coming is different." I pulled the plastic chair closer to the bed. It shrieked against the linoleum, and somewhere down the hall a nurse's voice said something soothing to no one. "Mr. Wren, I've spent three weeks reconstructing the night Elise Marchand disappeared. And your daughter's alibi is sitting in the middle of it like a piano nobody admits to owning."

His eyes closed. Not sleep. The look of a man listening for something behind a door.

"Celeste was at the Harrow & Vine," he said finally. "The whole evening. Forty people saw her."

"Forty people saw her arrive at seven-fifteen and leave at eleven-thirty. That's what the statement says. What it doesn't say is how anyone knows those times so precisely. No one checks their watch forty times, Mr. Wren. Someone did the math for them."

"The restaurant kept reservations."

"It kept one reservation. Under your name. Table for two, seven o'clock, canceled at four that afternoon." I let that sit. "You canceled it yourself. Then you drove her to the Harrow & Vine, walked her past the maître d' so he'd remember her face, and left. She stayed, as far as I can tell. She ate dinner alone and drank exactly enough wine to be memorable but not enough to be sloppy. Somebody taught her that."

Aldous Wren turned his head toward the window. Rain had started an hour ago, the kind of gray drizzle that makes even daylight feel like a rumor. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into a register I had to lean toward.

"You've been to the house."

"The estate, yes."

"The portrait. Did you see it?"

"That depends on what you mean by *see*."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't been surgically removed from him somewhere along the way. "Thirty years I dusted that frame. Every week. Even after—" He stopped. Started over. "Even after there was nothing in it worth dusting."

"And you kept paying me to prove nothing happened."

"I kept paying you to ask questions." His hand came out from under the blanket, trembling, and gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both. "Listen to me. There are two kinds of lies. The kind you tell to hurt someone, and the kind you tell because the truth would hurt worse. I only ever learned the second kind. I want you to understand that before you decide what I am."

"What are you, Mr. Wren?"

He held my wrist a moment longer, then released it, and the trembling came back like a tide reclaiming sand.

"I'm the man who corroborated an alibi," he whispered. "That's all. That's all I ever was."

* * *

Here's what I had, laid out across the desk in my rented room above the inn, index cards and red string like a conspiracy theorist's mood board:

At 6:40 p.m. on October 14th, 1994, Elise Marchand telephoned her husband Édouard from the studio above the carriage house. The call lasted ninety seconds. Neighbors — the Delacroixes, whose testimony appears exactly once in the file and then vanishes like a magician's coin — reported hearing raised voices at the estate around eight. At nine-twelve, rain began. By morning, Elise's shoes were found at the edge of the mill pond, placed side by side, toes pointing at the water.

Placed. Nobody drowns themselves out of their shoes and leaves them arranged like exhibits. But the coroner in 1994 was a man named Ferrand who signed off on more accidental drownings than any three other coroners in the province combined, and the file closed itself with the soft efficiency of a door oiled in advance.

Celeste Wren, twenty-six years old, estate manager's daughter, married to Édouard Marchand's nephew Henri for eleven months at that point, was at the Harrow & Vine from roughly seven until eleven-thirty. Her alibi was corroborated by her father, who told investigators he'd seen her there himself, dining with friends.

Except the reservation was under Aldous's name and canceled at four.

And except for this, which I'd pulled from the provincial archive two days earlier and read maybe forty times since: the original investigator's notes, never entered into evidence, contain a single line about Aldous Wren. *Estate manager states he was home all evening. States he heard nothing. States this three times without being asked twice.*

Three times without being asked twice. In my experience, that's not a man telling the truth. That's a man reciting.

* * *

I went back to the hospice on Thursday. The rain had settled in for a long stay, and the corridors smelled like wet wool and endgames. A different nurse led me to his room — word had apparently gotten around that I'd be back, and nobody had the energy to stop me.

Aldous was propped up higher this time, and there were morphine clouds behind his eyes, but underneath them the arithmetic was still running.

"My daughter called," he said, instead of hello. "Last night. After you left. She asked if I'd been talking to anyone."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her I was dying and didn't have the strength to talk to anyone." He smiled, and it was terrible, because it was fond. "Which is the first lie I've ever told her. Did you know that? Thirty years. Celeste has never once caught me in a lie. I used to think that was love. Now I think it was just very good bookkeeping on both sides."

"Mr. Wren—"

"Aldous." He waved a hand. "We're past the courtesy stage. You've got my reservation records on your little cards. Call me Aldous or don't come back."

"Aldous. The night Elise Marchand died. Where were you?"

The morphine clouds parted, briefly, and what was underneath made me glad the room was small and there wasn't far to fall.

"Home," he said. "In the gatehouse. Listening to the rain."

"Doing anything else?"

"Yes." He looked at his hands. They lay on the blanket like things that had been borrowed from a bigger man. "I was waiting for my daughter to come home from dinner. And she was late. And then she wasn't late anymore, she was early, hours early, and her dress was wet at the hem, and she stood in my kitchen and she said, Papa, whatever anyone asks you, I was at the restaurant all night. And I said, yes. Because that's what fathers say."

The rain ticked against the window. Down the hall, someone's television murmured a game show to an audience of no one.

"She was wet at the hem," I repeated. "It rained starting at nine-twelve."

"I know when it rained." His voice cracked open. "I know exactly when it rained. I've known for thirty years, son. I'm the one who checked the weather report the next morning and did the arithmetic you're doing right now, and then I went to the police and I lied anyway, because she was my daughter, and she was crying, and she said — God help me, she said — *Papa, I was protecting the one who stayed.*"

The room went very quiet. Even the game show seemed to hold its breath.

"The one who stayed," I said carefully. "Not the one who left."

Aldous Wren looked at me with eyes that had been carrying a coffin for three decades, and I watched him decide — watch the decision happen, the way you watch a man step off a ledge in slow motion — whether to keep carrying it or finally set it down.

"There were two of them," he whispered. "Everyone in this town will tell you Elise Marchand was one woman. One face, one voice, one life. And everyone will describe her differently, because they weren't describing a woman. They were describing a story they'd agreed on." His breath hitched. "But there were two. And on the night the pond took its lady, only one of them ever left this town again."

"Left town," I said. "Or left the world?"

He didn't answer. He turned his face to the window, and the rain wrote its gray sentences down the glass, and I understood that I had just been handed the center of the maze — and that the man who handed it to me was going to die before he could be made to say it plainly, because some doors only open onto darker rooms, and he'd been standing in the dark for thirty years keeping the light off on purpose.

"One more thing," I said, standing. "Your daughter. Celeste. Is she protecting herself, or protecting someone else?"

For the first time, Aldous Wren laughed — a real one, brief and broken, startling the nurse who poked her head in and retreated.

"Son," he said, "when you figure out the answer to that question, you'll wish you hadn't. Now go. She calls every night at eight, and I can't lie to her twice in one lifetime."

* * *

I walked back to the inn through the rain without hurrying. Some part of me wanted to feel triumphant — the alibi was woven twice, father and daughter, thread over thread, and I'd found the loose end. But triumph requires believing you know what the tapestry shows, and all I had now was a shape I couldn't look at directly.

*The one who stayed.*

Elise Marchand had a sister. I didn't know that yet — wouldn't know it for another two chapters of my own life, buried in institutional records and a scratched-out photograph. What I knew was smaller and stranger: that a dying man had lied for thirty years out of loyalty, and that his loyalty had a direction. Not toward his daughter. Toward whoever *stayed*.

And that the woman living in the Marchand estate today — the one whose signature still moved money through trusts, whose face still appeared, rarely and gracefully, at regional charity functions — had once stood in her father's kitchen with a wet hem and a rehearsed sentence, and had never once, in thirty years, been caught in a lie.

Good bookkeeping on both sides, Aldous had said.

I climbed the stairs to my room, spread the index cards back out on the desk, and pinned a new card in the center of the web, where the piano used to sit. On it I wrote four words:

*TWO WOMEN. ONE GRAVE.*

Then I sat staring at them until the rain stopped, sometime after midnight, and the silence that followed felt less like peace than like a held breath — the whole town, the whole thirty years, waiting to see whether I'd exhume what it had cost so much loyalty to bury.
