## 8. Anonymous Fare

You have heard ten thousand voices, and you remember none of them the way you remember this one.

You are the depot at night — the bank of radios on the wall, the headsets hanging like shed skins, the dispatch console with its worn keys and its green-lit screen that never quite sleeps. You are the hum in the wires, the crackle before a voice arrives, the collective ear pressed against the city's throat. You have listened to drunks weeping into your receivers, to teenagers daring each other to call, to old men ordering cabs to hospitals they will not survive. You hold all of it without judgment, because holding is what you are for.

And for eleven nights now, you have held a voice that books rides it never takes.

It begins the way everything begins with you: a line lighting up, a number blooming on the caller ID, Sable Amberdale reaching for his headset with the unhurried motion of a man who has learned that urgency is mostly theater. It is 11:47 on a Tuesday, rain ticking against the depot's single high window, and the night has been so quiet that Sable has been reading the continuation diary again — the one that should not exist — with a pencil tucked behind his ear like a carpenter's habit.

"City Cab, this is dispatch."

The voice on the other end is a woman's, low and even, pitched just under the static. Not young. Not old. The kind of voice you cannot put a face to because it seems designed to be forgotten.

"I need a cab," she says. "Quill Street. Number fourteen."

Sable's pencil stops moving. You feel him go still the way you feel weather change — through the wires, through the small electric hush of a man whose attention has narrowed to a point.

"Quill Street," he repeats, neutral as gravel. "Pickup time?"

"Midnight."

"And a name for the booking?"

A pause. Long enough to count. Then: "You can put it under Q."

The line goes dead before he can ask anything else.

* * *

You want to explain something about bookings, about how they live inside you. Every fare ever requested passes through your memory like water through limestone, leaving mineral traces. A booking is a promise made to the dark, and most of them dissolve — people change their minds, fall asleep, find other ways home. But a pattern is different. A pattern is a heartbeat.

So when the same number calls again on Wednesday at 11:52, and again Thursday at 11:49, and Friday at 11:51 — always asking for Quill Street, number fourteen, always midnight, always *under Q* — you begin, in your patient mineral way, to keep score.

Sable keeps score too. He sends cars. That is the procedure, and procedure is the armor he wears against the world. Each night a driver rolls to the curb outside number fourteen — the Quill house, half-derelict, its upstairs windows blind with plywood, its porch sagging like a tired mouth — and each night the cab idles there from five minutes to midnight until five minutes past, wipers sweeping, heater breathing, and no one comes out. No one crosses the street. No curtain twitches, because there are no curtains left to twitch.

The drivers log it and move on. Dead runs happen. But Sable sits at the console afterward with the logs spread around him like tarot cards, and you can hear him thinking, which for you is almost the same as hearing him speak.

"Same number every time," he murmurs on the sixth night, to no one, to you. "Payphone?"

He checks. The number resolves to a payphone — an actual coin-operated phone, bolted to the wall of the bait shop near the ferry dock, one of the last of its species still drawing breath in this city. Eleven miles across town, down where the harbor flattens out gray and industrial, where the gulls argue over fish heads and the ferries load their slow human cargo twice a day.

Not near the Quill house. Near the water.

Sable writes the address on a card and does not say why it matters, but you know why it matters, because you were listening in Chapter Five — no. Because you were listening that week, years compressed into your wire-memory, when a fare was logged from the Quill house to the ferry terminal under a driver who is now dead. Someone left this house by taxi once, willingly or not, and went toward the boats.

Now someone calls nightly from the dock, asking for a ride back to the house they may have fled.

Or pretending to.

* * *

On the ninth night, Sable answers the call himself, and this time he does not let the silence do the talking.

"City Cab, dispatch."

"You can send one to Quill Street," the voice says, and there it is again, that terrible evenness, like a lake with no wind on it. "Number fourteen. Midnight."

"We've sent cars four times this week," Sable says. Conversational. Almost gentle. "Nobody's been there."

The pause stretches. You carry it faithfully, all three seconds of it, and in those seconds you register something new: breath. Shallow. Controlled, but shallow — the breathing of someone who is frightened and managing it the way you manage a burn, by refusing to look at it directly.

"Cars get noticed," the voice says finally.

"So do payphones." Sable leans back in his chair; the springs sigh through your headset. "There's exactly one within walking distance of that number. Bait shop, ferry dock. I could meet you there instead. No car. Just talk."

Another pause. Longer. And then the voice does something it has never done in nine nights of calling.

It laughs. Softly. Without any warmth in it at all — a laugh like a key turning in a lock.

"You're the one who reads diaries," she says. "Iris said you would be."

The line clicks dead, and the dial tone rises up through you like floodwater.

* * *

Afterward — after Sable has sat motionless for a full minute, after he has replayed the recording three times, after he has pulled his coat on over his uniform shirt with hands that are almost steady — you go with him. You always go with him; you are in the radio of the cab he borrows, in the handheld clipped to his belt, in the depot frequency humming faintly in his ear like a conscience.

The ferry dock at midnight is a country of sodium light and black water. The bait shop is shuttered, its sign — HALLORAN'S LIVE BAIT & TACKLE — furred with salt rust. The payphone hangs on the wall beside the ice machine, its cord coiled like something sleeping, its receiver resting in its cradle with the patience of an object that has outlived its purpose.

Sable stands in front of it for a long moment. Rain has stopped; the air smells of diesel and brine and the particular cold sweetness of rotting pilings. Out on the black water, a channel marker blinks green, green, green, keeping its idiot faithful time.

He lifts the receiver. Dial tone, thin and ancient. He holds it to his ear anyway, as if the phone might have kept something — a warmth, a residue, the ghost of that even voice.

"Iris said you would be," he repeats aloud, quietly, testing the words for weight. They are heavy. They are almost too heavy to lift.

Because here is the arithmetic that is keeping him standing in the cold at half past midnight: Iris Quill has been missing eleven years. If the woman on the phone is Iris, then she has been alive all along — alive and close enough to watch him, close enough to know about the diary, close enough to know that he reads it at night with a pencil behind his ear. And if the woman on the phone is *not* Iris —

Then someone wants him to believe she is. And knows his name. And knows his habits. And has chosen the ferry dock — the last place anyone saw a woman matching her description board a boat, years ago, alone — as the stage for whatever this is.

Either way, you think, in your wire-bound way, either way the invisible presence has just shown its teeth. It smiled first, back when Vane quoted details no outsider should know. Now it has called him by reputation, through a dead woman's voice, from a phone thirty feet from black water.

Sable sets the receiver back in its cradle. The sound it makes is very small and very final, like a verdict being sealed.

He turns once, slowly, scanning the lot: the chained dumpster, the row of boat trailers with their flat tires, the long empty ramp going down into the dark. Nothing moves except the marker light, green, green, green, and far out — so far out it might be imagination — a white running light sliding north along the horizon, some late ferry or fishing boat carrying its passengers toward morning.

He looks at it longer than a man looks at nothing.

Then he keys his radio, and his voice comes through you steady, and only you can hear the effort underneath it, the way you can hear the strain in a bridge cable before anything visible happens.

"Dispatch to base. Log a follow-up on the Quill file. Anonymous caller, female, booked repeated fares to 14 Quill Street, never boarded. Originating payphone, Halloran's Bait Shop, ferry dock." A breath. "Also log this: the caller referenced Iris Quill by name, in present tense, and claimed prior contact with the investigating party."

A pause. Static fills it, soft as snowfall.

"Note for the record," Sable says. "She knew things only two people could know. One of them is missing."

He clips the radio back on his belt and walks to the borrowed cab, and you ride with him through the wet streets while the city sleeps its shallow sleep, and neither of you says what you are both thinking, because saying it would make it real:

That tomorrow he has to decide whether to tell Maren Quill that her sister might be phoning ghosts from the dock. That he has to decide whether Aldous Vane needs to learn that the anonymous fares have stopped — or whether telling Vane anything is like feeding blood to something with teeth. That the diary on his kitchen table has a fresh entry he hasn't decoded yet, and that tonight, driving home through the rain with the harbor shrinking in his mirror, he understood the acrostic he'd been staring at for two days.

Five entries. First letters down the margin, patient as stitches:

*D-O-C-K.*

And beneath the fifth entry, in handwriting that trembles just slightly — the first tremble in three hundred pages of otherwise level script — a single added line, dated tomorrow:

*"He watches the phones. Come to the house. Come alone."*

You carry the words with him all the way home, through the green lights and the sleeping blocks, and when he finally parks and sits in the dark with the engine ticking cool, you hold the sentence one more time, gently, the way you hold everything:

Someone is inviting him into the trap.

Or out of it.

And the only way to know which — is to knock.
