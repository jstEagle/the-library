## 18. Friday Night Shift

The sting was supposed to be simple. Sable had built enough of them in his head over eleven years of listening to other people's emergencies to know that simple was the first thing that died on contact with a Friday night.

He'd spent the week laying the trail with a patience that felt almost tender. A word in the ear of Vane's clerk — a nervous, cologne-drenched young man named Petrie who filed papers with the reverence of an acolyte — that the diaries, both of them, the original and the continuation, would change hands Friday night at the taxi depot. That a private investigator Sable had hired with three months of overtime savings would collect them at eleven and deliver them to the police by morning. That after that, no one on earth would be able to make them disappear.

He'd said it loudly enough to be overheard. He'd let Petrie see the manila envelope. He'd even, in a moment of theatrical flourish he wasn't entirely proud of, written FRIDAY — 11 PM on a whiteboard in the dispatch office where the window gave onto the street, knowing that Vane's kind of man noticed everything and assumed everyone else noticed nothing.

The depot at night was a cathedral of small lights. The board glowed amber above the dispatch desk, each lit square a cab alive somewhere in the city's dark capillaries. Sable sat with his coffee going cold and his hand resting on the radio the way other men rested a hand on a dog. The envelope sat in the open tray of his desk, fat with the diaries, exactly where a watcher would expect it to be.

At 10:40, the first cab came in. At 10:52, the second. By 10:58 the lot was quiet, drivers drifting off in their own directions, and the street outside was doing its Friday impression — laughter from the bar two doors down, a siren threading somewhere north, the wet shine of recent rain on asphalt.

At 11:00 exactly, nothing happened.

At 11:15, still nothing.

Sable told himself this was fine. Stings were mostly nothing; that was the nature of them. You assembled a trap out of hope and waited for an animal that had survived this long by never stepping in anything. Vane had been a lawyer for thirty years. He didn't walk into rooms; he sent the rooms ahead of himself to check.

But by 11:40, the nothing had developed a texture. It had stopped feeling like patience and started feeling like being watched while nothing happened, which was worse. Sable got up, walked the length of the office, checked the lot, checked the street. The envelope sat where it had sat all night. The rain had stopped. The bar had gone quiet.

At 11:47, the phone rang.

The depot line. Not his cell. Not the radio. The old landline with its beige plastic body and its cord frayed down to copper at the base — the phone that had taken ten thousand calls from drunks and lost tourists and women whispering addresses they didn't want their husbands to hear. Sable knew its voice the way he knew the board's glow, and when it rang at 11:47 on a night when the whole city should have been asleep on this line, his body understood before his mind did that something had changed shape.

He picked up. "Depot."

For three seconds, nothing. Then a breath, and then a voice.

"Sable Amberdale."

It was a woman's voice. Quiet, level, worn thin at the edges like paper kept too long folded. It said his name the way you'd read it off a file — but underneath the flatness there was something else, something that made the hair on his forearms lift: the voice sounded *rehearsed*. Not in the way of a liar. In the way of someone who hadn't spoken to a stranger in a very long time and had practiced this sentence in the dark.

"I need you to come to the house," the voice said. "Come alone. Come now."

Sable's hand found the edge of the desk. "Who is this?"

"You know who this is." A pause, and in the pause he could hear a room — not the depot's room, something smaller, with a different silence, a silence with weight in it, furniture and dust and a body breathing. "You've been reading my diary for three weeks. You know how I write. Now you know how I sound."

"Iris." Saying it out loud made the room tilt slightly. Eleven years of a name, and it had never had a voice attached until now. "If that's you—"

"It's me." Flat. Certain. Then, softer, almost an apology: "It's what's left of me."

"Where's Vane?"

A silence with a different quality this time — a flinch he could hear. "You'll want to ask him that in person," she said. "Come alone. If you bring anyone, if you call anyone, if the radios say anything tonight that isn't ordinary — he'll know. He always knows. He's known about you since Tuesday."

The cold that went through Sable then wasn't fear exactly. It was the specific chill of a man realizing his trap had been a stage set, and the play had been running around him the whole time. Petrie. The whiteboard. The envelope. Vane had let the whole week happen because the week had been useful — because a dispatcher who thought he was hunting was a dispatcher who wasn't looking over his shoulder.

"The diaries," Sable said. "You know where they are."

"I know they're in the envelope on your desk, and I know the envelope is empty except for the newspaper you put in this afternoon, because you moved the real ones at four o'clock, and I know where you moved them." The voice didn't gloat. It stated, tiredly, like a woman reading a grocery list of her own captivity. "That's why it has to be tonight. That's why it has to be you. He's coming for the house at dawn. After that there's no more house and no more me and no more anything. You're the last person who can still be reached before the last door closes."

"Why me? Why not the police?"

"Because the police come with lights and radios and a man named Vane knows every one of their first names." A breath. "And because you're the only person in eleven years who read what I wrote and understood it. Come alone, Sable Amberdale. The house will be open."

The line went dead. Not hung up — dead, the way a line goes dead when someone lifts a receiver somewhere else in the house and then sets it down. Sable stared at the phone for a long moment, then at the board, then at the envelope with its newspaper heart.

*The house will be open.*

* * *

He should have called someone. He knew that with the whole of his trained, methodical mind. Maren, who would come with her grief and her guilt and her car. Detective Okafor, who had half-believed him since the ferry manifest and owed him one favor she hadn't yet regretted. Anyone. The entire architecture of every careful thing he'd ever done said *call someone*.

But the voice had known about the four o'clock switch. It had known the envelope was hollow. And if it was Vane's voice — if this was the execution dressed as a rescue, the bait dressed as the girl — then it was a bait designed specifically for him, which meant Vane wanted him at the house, which meant the house was where the truth was kept, and Sable had spent his whole adult life unable to leave a truth sitting in the dark without going to stand in front of it.

And if it was her — if it was really her, eleven years of static finally resolving into a voice — then every minute he spent being careful was a minute she spent alone in a house with a man who had kept her there.

He took the diaries out of the floor safe under the filing cabinet — Vane's voice had known that too, which should have terrified him more than it did — and put the original in his coat, inside pocket, over his heart like a letter. The continuation diary he left behind, in the safe, where a man searching in a hurry might find it and believe he'd found everything. If Sable didn't come back, it would still be evidence. If he did come back, he'd want it anyway.

He wrote a note. It felt absurd, writing a note like a man going out for cigarettes in a noir film, but he wrote it anyway: *If I'm not back by 3, call Okafor. Quill house. Don't come yourself — send her.* He left it under the coffee cup on the desk, weighted so it couldn't be missed, timed so it couldn't be found too soon.

Then he stood in the dispatch office for one last moment, in the amber light of the board, and understood that he was looking at the only room in the world that had ever felt like it was waiting for him. Eleven years of night shifts, of voices in the static, of solving other people's vanishings from a chair. If he walked out now, he might not have this room again. Vane would see to that.

He turned off the board. The squares went dark one by one, like a city going to sleep, and Sable Amberdale walked out into the wet street to go and meet the missing.

* * *

The drive across town took nineteen minutes at that hour and felt like nineteen years. The city slid past the cab's windows — closed bakeries, a laundromat glowing like an aquarium, a fox trotting along a median with something dead in its mouth — and Sable held the wheel at ten and two and made himself think like a man walking into a trap on purpose, which required knowing which parts of the trap were the trap and which parts were the door.

Vane wanted the diaries. That was bedrock. Everything else — the clerk, the leak, the patience — was built on that foundation. So if the call was Vane's, the house was where Vane would trade the girl for the paper, or kill them both and search the depot at leisure. Either way, the diaries in Sable's coat were the only currency in the transaction, and he was the only bank.

But if the call was hers — and God, some stubborn cellar of him believed it was, believed it the way you believe in weather — then the call had been made from inside the house, on a line Vane monitored, which meant she had made it in the one window she'd ever had, and had spent whatever trust or cunning it cost. You didn't spend that on a wrong man.

*The only person in eleven years who read what I wrote and understood it.*

He thought about the layout errors as he drove. The little wrong turns in her descriptions — the staircase she always placed on the wrong side of the hall, the kitchen window she always faced east when it faced north. Her secret game, her signature, the thing no captor could fake because no captor would think an error was worth preserving. He'd recite it to her, he decided. If she was real, it would be a handshake. If she wasn't, it would cost him nothing.

The Quill street was exactly as wrong as he remembered it. Houses like held breath. The Quill house at the end, black against a sky the color of wet slate, its windows giving back nothing.

One window gave back something.

Upstairs, in the sealed wing — the wing he'd searched in chapter after chapter of this case, the wing with the kept room, the made bed, the working lamp — a light was on. Not the lamp. A candle, maybe; the light moved the way flame moves, unsteady, alive.

Sable parked two houses down, out of habit, then made himself get out and stand in the open street where anyone could see him, because a man sneaking was a man who could be picked off, and a man walking up the path in plain sight was a man with a reason. His heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing. His hand, in his coat pocket, closed around the diary like a talisman, and he noticed that his thumb had found the worn spot on the cover where Iris's own thumb had rested for years, and that this felt less like evidence and more like holding hands with a ghost.

The front door stood open. Not ajar — open, wide, the hall inside lit by a single bulb that had no business working in a house with the power cut for a decade. Someone had restored a circuit. Someone had been living around the dead wiring the way a spider lives around a swept corner.

He stepped inside. The hall smelled of dust and candle wax and, underneath, faintly, of soup — someone had cooked here, recently, in secret, in a house that was supposed to be empty. The staircase rose on the *right* side of the hall.

He stopped.

In every entry she'd ever written — the childhood letters, the diary, all of it — Iris Quill placed that staircase on the left. It was the oldest error, the first one, the game's founding lie. Sable stood in the hall of the real house and looked at the real staircase on the real right-hand side, and felt the ground shift under the whole case, because the voice on the phone had said *the house will be open*, and only two kinds of people knew this hall: the woman who always misremembered it, and the man who had lived in its truth for eleven years.

From upstairs, faintly, came a sound. Not a voice. A tapping. Deliberate, spaced, patient — three taps, pause, three taps, pause — the rhythm of someone signaling through a wall, or a pipe, or the floor of a room that had been kept dusted for a guest who never came.

Then the candlelight upstairs guttered, and a shadow crossed it — broad-shouldered, unhurried, moving with the ease of ownership — and Sable heard, from the top of the staircase on the right side of the hall, the front door clicking shut behind him, the voice he now recognized from a courtroom clip he'd watched a dozen times say pleasantly, from the dark above:

"Mr. Amberdale. You're early. She does that — underestimates how fast you'd come." A pause, and the smile was audible. "Come up. She's been waiting eleven years to be found. It would be rude to keep her waiting longer."

Sable's hand tightened on the diary over his heart, and he started up the stairs.
