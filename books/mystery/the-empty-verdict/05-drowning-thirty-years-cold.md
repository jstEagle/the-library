## 5. Drowning, Thirty Years Cold

The basement of Hartwell's Books smelled like every basement in Ashcombe: damp stone, old paper, and the faint ghost of somebody's grandfather's pipe. Mine had the added perfume of cardboard boxes I'd been promising myself I'd organize since the Obama administration.

I put the kettle on first. A man should never go looking for his own guilt on an empty stomach, or an uncaffeinated one. Then I carried my mug down the stairs, past the cookbooks nobody bought, past the local history section that was mostly pamphlets about the Ashcombe Founders' Day Parade, and stopped at the shelf in the far corner where I kept the boxes that weren't books at all.

Four of them. Banker's boxes, the cheap kind, each one labeled in my own handwriting from a life ago: *HARTWELL — CASES 1987–1991*, *1991–1994*, *1994–1999*, and one with no label at all that I'd been avoiding for a decade the way you avoid a neighbor who owes you money, or who you owe money to, depending on which side of that particular shame you live on.

I pulled down *1991–1994* and set it on the worktable under the bare bulb.

The kettle whistled upstairs like it disapproved. Fair enough. So did I.

Here's what I knew going in, and I want to be honest about how little of it I'd let myself know until last night, when Elise Vann walked out of my shop and left three hundred dollars and a name behind her like a wake. Tomas Merrick drowned in the old quarry on the night of August 19th, 1994. He was thirty-one years old. His brother Aldous was there. So were Denny Cole, Sheriff-to-be Marcus Rook, and Judge-to-be Wendell Harlan — though back then Harlan was just a lawyer with a boat and Rook was a deputy with a mustache he hadn't yet grown into. Lena Corvane, who cleaned houses for half the founding families of this town, was convicted of his murder two years later on evidence I helped gather, testimony I helped shape, and a narrative so tidy it could have come gift-wrapped.

Tidy stories are how towns survive themselves. I'd known that for thirty years the way you know your knee predicts rain — abstractly, and then all at once when it starts to ache.

I opened the box.

The smell hit me first: dust and manila and something underneath it, something like fear that had dried into the folders. My hands, which had been steady enough pouring coffee for Elise Vann yesterday, were not steady now. I noticed that. I noticed everything now, and that was the strange part — ten years of selling paperbacks to tourists and recommending mysteries to people who wanted their murders cozy, and here was the old machinery waking up one gear at a time, clicking into place like it had only been sleeping, not rusting.

Folder by folder. I made myself go in order, because order is a kindness you do your future self.

*Hartwell, B. — Field Notes, Aug 1994.* My younger handwriting, all slant and confidence. I read my own observations like letters from a stranger who happened to share my name:

*8/20/94 — Called to quarry rd. Body recovered 0630. T. MERRICK. Brother (Aldous) on scene, composed. Too composed? Note: revisit.*

I had written *too composed*. Thirty-year-old Bram had noticed. And then — I turned the pages — thirty-year-old Bram had apparently decided not to follow up, because the next entry jumped straight to witness statements, four statements that matched each other like four copies of the same letter.

Aldous Merrick: *We were on the water together all evening. Tomas went to the edge to relieve himself around eleven. When he didn't come back we searched, called for him, found nothing. Assumed he'd wandered home drunk.*

Denny Cole: same story, different words, plus a joke about Tomas's drinking that I remembered Denny telling at least six times over the years, the way men keep a dead friend alive by making him the punchline.

Marcus Rook: same story, with the added weight of a deputy's oath that he'd personally driven the quarry road at midnight and seen nothing.

Wendell Harlan: same story, delivered, I noted, *in writing, notarized* — which struck me even then as odd, because innocent men tell you things; guilty ones have them certified.

Four statements. One story. I sat back and drank my coffee, which had gone cold, and felt the thing I'd spent thirty years not feeling start to move in my chest like water finding a crack.

Because here's what I remembered that wasn't in any statement: I remembered being twenty-nine years old and hungry for a detective's shield, and I remembered Sheriff Amos Pell — Rook's predecessor, a decent man dying slowly of emphysema — handing me the Corvane file and saying, "Bram, the town needs this one closed. You understand me? Closed, not solved." And I remembered understanding him perfectly, because I was young and Ashcombe had been good to me, and because Lena Corvane was a cleaner with a temper and no alibi and a prior — she'd once slapped Vivian Merrick across the face at a church picnic, and half the town had seen it, and half the town was willing to swear to worse.

I had told myself for thirty years that the evidence against her was real. That I'd merely declined to look too hard at its edges.

Now I opened the coroner's report, and the edges looked back.

* * *

Dr. Yusuf Okafor had been county coroner for forty years, a gentle, meticulous Nigerian immigrant who played cello at St. Brigid's and terrified generations of Ashcombe teenagers by showing up at their DUIs in person rather than sending a deputy. He had also, I realized as I read, written the single most inconvenient document in the history of this town, and everyone had simply agreed to pretend he hadn't.

Cause of death: asphyxiation consistent with drowning. Fine. Expected.

But Okafor had been thorough, because Okafor was always thorough. And buried in his findings, in the flat clinical language of a man who believed facts would defend themselves, were three sentences that should have ended the trial before it began:

*Deceased exhibits bilateral contusions to the parietal region, antemortem, consistent with blunt force impact. Deceased exhibits defensive-type abrasions to both forearms. Blood alcohol content 0.11 — deceased impaired but ambulatory.*

Blunt force. Before death. Defensive wounds. On both arms.

A man who stumbles off a boat to relieve himself does not get hit on the head twice before he drowns. A man who slips into black water alone does not raise his arms to fend anything off. Tomas Merrick had been struck — struck more than once — by someone standing over him, and he had put his hands up, and then he had gone into the quarry.

And the prosecution's theory — the theory I helped carry into that courtroom, God help me — was that Lena Corvane, a hundred and thirty pounds of grieving housekeeper, had somehow overpowered a hundred and ninety-pound man, beaten him about the head hard enough to bruise bone-deep without fracturing skull, held him under, and left no trace of herself on him, in him, or near him. No fibers. No skin under the nails. Nothing but opportunity, temper, and a town's appetite for a tidy ending.

I set the report down. My hands were shaking now, properly shaking, and I found — this is the part I'm ashamed of, so I'll say it plainly — I found I was smiling.

Not because of the horror. The horror sat in my stomach like swallowed stone. But underneath the stone, in some cellar of myself I'd boarded up a decade ago, something had lit a lamp. The hunt. The terrible, beautiful, unwelcome thrill of it — the sense that the world was a locked room and I still remembered how locks worked. Ten years of retirement had told me that feeling was dead. It wasn't dead. It had just been waiting, patient as a spider, for someone to pay a retainer and speak a date aloud.

"August nineteenth," I said to the empty basement. "She said August nineteenth."

Elise Vann hadn't mentioned the anniversary. She'd said the murder would happen "in three weeks," and I'd done the arithmetic last night at two in the morning, staring at my ceiling: three weeks from Tuesday was August nineteenth. The anniversary of Tomas Merrick's drowning, to the day.

Somebody was planning a murder as a commemoration. Or somebody wanted me to believe they were.

I got up, stretched until my back cracked like green wood, and went back to the box. There was one more thing I needed, and I found it in the last folder, misfiled — deliberately, I suspected, by my younger self, tucked behind a receipt for gasoline like a sin hidden behind a virtue.

It was a photograph. Crime scene, morning of August 20th, 1994. The quarry shore, gray and wet, the police tape sagging between two pines. And at the waterline, half in frame because whoever took the picture had been careless or hurried: a rowboat. The Merricks' boat, hauled up on the shingle, and —

I got my reading glasses. I looked until my eyes watered.

There was a smear on the gunwale. Dark, long, low — the height of a dragged body, if a body had been hauled out of the water and over the side. And the boat was beached *bow-first*, nose dug into the gravel, the way a boat sits when it's been pushed ashore hard and fast by someone in a hurry.

But all four witnesses swore the boat stayed moored at the dock all night. Swore none of them went back onto the water after Tomas vanished. Swore they found the boat exactly where they'd left it in the morning.

The photograph said otherwise. The photograph said somebody had taken that boat out — or brought it in — in the dark, dragging something heavy, and then lied about it in four-part harmony.

I sat down again. The bulb buzzed overhead. Somewhere above me, through the floorboards, I could hear the shop's front door chime — a customer, browsing, oblivious, maybe pulling a cozy mystery off the rack and thinking murder was a thing that happened between pages.

Thirty years ago, four men told one story, and the story fit the town's needs, and a woman went to prison for it, and I carried the file upstairs and closed it and built a life on top of the lid. Now the lid was open, and the story had a hole in it shaped like a boat, and the coroner — bless his meticulous, inconvenient heart — had left the receipts.

One question remained, and it was the question that mattered, and I wrote it on the back of a gas receipt in fresh ink so I wouldn't lose it to the night:

*Who benefits from a dead Tomas Merrick — and who benefits now, thirty years later, from digging him up?*

Because here was the thing that kept circling back, patient as the tide: Elise Vann hadn't asked me to solve an old murder. She'd asked me to prevent a new one. And the new one had Aldous Merrick's name on it — the brother, the survivor, the most composed man at the quarry shore.

Either Aldous Merrick was about to pay for what happened in 1994.

Or somebody wanted me to *think* he was — wanted a retired detective with a guilty conscience and a talent for opening locked rooms to do their digging for them, free of charge, and hand them the shovel when I was done.

I climbed the stairs, turned off the basement light, and stood a moment in the dark at the top, listening to the customer's footsteps overhead drift toward the register.

Tomorrow, I decided, I'd go see Dr. Okafor. If he was still alive — and I prayed he was, because the man owed me nothing and I owed him an apology thirty years overdue — I wanted to ask him one question face to face: *Yusuf, when you wrote those findings, did anyone ever ask you to soften them?*

And if he said yes, I wanted the name.

The bell chimed. The customer left. I flipped the sign to CLOSED and stood in the empty shop with the whole drowned past rising around me like black water, and for the first time in ten years, Bram Hartwell was not bored, was not broke in spirit, and was very much afraid — of the town, of the truth, and most of all of how good it felt to be afraid again.
