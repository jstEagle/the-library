## 13. Lena's Letters

Elise Vann had rehearsed this conversation in the shower, on the bus, and once, memorably, in line at the pharmacy while a man behind her loudly debated cough syrup brands with himself. In none of those rehearsals did it begin with Bram Hartwell burning his thumb on a kettle and swearing with the fluency of a man who had clearly spent thirty years around sailors.

"Sit," he said, shaking his hand like a cat that had stepped in water. "Tea's ruined anyway. I'll make more."

"You don't have to—"

"I burned myself making tea for you. The tea will be made." He said it with the gravity of a judge sentencing himself, and she found, to her irritation, that she liked him for it.

She sat at the little table wedged between the mystery section and the window of Hartwell's Books. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere in the stacks, the shop's ancient radiator knocked out a rhythm like a polite ghost asking to be let in. She had been coming here for weeks now under the pretense of journalism, and the pretense had worn through in places, like the elbows of her coat. Bram had stopped pretending not to notice around the time he'd started leaving two cups out instead of one.

He set down fresh tea and then, instead of sitting, stood there with his hands in his pockets, looking at her with an expression she was beginning to catalog the way she'd once cataloged her mother's prison moods. This one was patience wearing its work clothes.

"You've been circling something for three weeks," he said. "You talk around it the way my mother talked around her second marriage. Eventually you either say it or I die of old age, and frankly I'd rather know first."

Elise looked at her tea. Then she reached into her bag and brought out the bundle she had carried every day for eleven years and never once shown another living soul.

It was wrapped in a flour-sack dish towel, faded blue, tied with kitchen twine. Her hands were not quite steady as she untied it, and she hated that they weren't steady, because Bram noticed everything, and noticing things was apparently the only hobby he'd kept from his detective days.

"What is that?" he asked quietly.

"My mother's letters." She laid them on the table between them. Forty-one envelopes, soft as cloth from handling, addressed in a slanted hand that leaned hard to the right, as if the writer were always about to run somewhere. "Lena Corvane wrote to me every month for nineteen years. From Halloway Women's Facility. One page, mostly. Sometimes less. Paper was rationed by good behavior, and she was not good at behaving."

Bram didn't touch them. He looked at them the way other men looked at altars.

"She wrote to you," he said. "All that time."

"Every month until her hands got too bad. Then the last year, a nurse wrote them for her and Lena signed. The signature got smaller each time." Elise turned the top envelope over. "I've read these so many times I could recite them. I used to think that made me their keeper. Now I think maybe I'm just the only person who ever bothered to read them twice."

"Why show me?"

Because you're the only one who might believe them, she thought. Because you owe her, even if you don't know it yet. Because I'm tired of carrying this alone and you have kind eyes and a suspicious mind, which is the exact combination I need.

What she said was: "Because you asked me why I care about Aldous Merrick. And I lied to you. Twice. And you knew both times, and you let me keep lying, which I've decided is either very rude or very kind, and I can't tell which."

"It's usually both," Bram said. He finally sat down across from her, and the chair complained the way all furniture in Ashcombe complained, as though the whole town were tired but too polite to say so. "Go ahead. I'm listening. Actually listening. It's a service I provide free of charge, unlike the tea."

* * *

She told him the shape of it first, before the letters. Born in Halloway when her mother was twenty-six. Raised by an aunt in Corvane Hollow who spoke of Lena only in past tense, as if conviction were death and the state simply efficient about it. Visits twice a year until she was twelve, then monthly letters, then visits again as a teenager, sitting across scratched plexiglass from a woman who smiled too hard and asked all the wrong questions — *are you eating, are you happy, do you still play piano* — and never, ever, talked about Tomas Merrick or the quarry or the night of June 9th, 1994.

"Except once," Elise said. "Once, near the end. She was sick. They'd moved her to the infirmary ward and the rules were looser, or the nurse just didn't care. And I asked her — I was twenty-eight, I'd waited my whole life to ask — I said, *Mom, did you do it?*"

Bram went very still.

"And she laughed." Elise heard her own voice crack on the word and pressed on before it could betray her further. "She laughed like it was the funniest thing anyone had said in nineteen years. And then she cried, and she held my hand through the plexiglass gap — they let you do that in the infirmary, nobody's watching — and she said, *Elise, sweetheart, I wasn't even there.*"

"The trial said otherwise."

"The trial said a lot of things." Elise pulled the bundle toward her. "The trial said she followed Tomas Merrick to the quarry in a jealous rage over money he owed her. The trial said she struck him and put him in the water. The trial had a witness who saw her truck near the quarry road. The trial had a prosecutor who cried real tears during closing arguments, which everyone in Ashcombe still talks about like it was a performance of *Hamlet*."

"And now?" Bram asked. "What do you say?"

"Now I say she told the truth, and the truth needed help getting convicted, so people bought it some assistance." She untied the twine. "But don't take my word for it. Take hers. She was a better witness than I'll ever be. She just had the misfortune of testifying thirty years too late."

* * *

The letters themselves were small miracles of compression. Nineteen years of a life, one page at a time.

Bram read slowly, and Elise watched him read, and it occurred to her that this was the most intimate thing she had ever done with a stranger: handing him her mother's voice and waiting to see if he flinched.

*March 2001. Dear Elise. The new chaplain lets us have real coffee on Sundays, which is the closest thing to freedom I've felt since the trial. I have taken up embroidery. My flowers look like accidents. Write soon. Love, Mom.*

*September 2004. Dear Elise. A woman here swears she knew your grandmother. Small world, even the parts of it with fences. Tell me about the ocean. You always promised we'd see it together. Love, Mom.*

*January 2010. Dear Elise. Hands bad today. Short letter. Proud of you. Love, Mom.*

And then, buried in the middle of the stack, dated October 2011 — seventeen years after the drowning, four years after the trial, written in a hand that pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through the paper:

*Dear Elise. Something happened today and I have to write it down before I lose my nerve, because paper keeps what courage cannot. There was a fourth person on that boat the night Tomas died. Not Aldous, not the ones they said. Someone else entirely. I have known it since before my arrest and I have said nothing because saying it got a look in my direction I did not survive the first time. But I am older now and meaner and I find I care less what happens to me. The initials were W.K. Remember that. Whatever else they took from me, remember that. Love, Mom.*

Bram read it three times. Elise counted, because she couldn't help herself.

"W.K.," he said finally.

"You see the problem."

"I see several problems." He set the letter down with exaggerated care, as though it might bruise. "The alibis that night — the ones the county accepted, the ones that closed the case — belonged to Aldous Merrick, Denny Cole, Sheriff Rook, and Judge Harlan. Four names. Four men who swore they were together on the water until midnight."

"And none of them are W.K."

"None of them are W.K." Bram rubbed his jaw. "Which means either your mother invented a phantom—"

"She didn't invent anything. She was many things after that trial — careful, frightened, stubborn as a fence post — but she never once lied to me. Not in forty-one letters. I'd stake my life on it, and in a way I already have."

"—or," Bram continued, undeterred, in the tone of a man who enjoyed finishing his own sentences, "there was a fifth person involved that night whom nobody has ever named. Somebody who was on the boat, who isn't in any official account, and whose initials your mother learned somehow, from somewhere, at great personal risk."

"That's what I've thought for eleven years."

"And you came to *me*." He said it without accusation, but the weight of it settled on the table between them like a third guest. "A retired bookseller with a bad knee and a kettle I can't operate."

"I came to the man whose testimony helped convict her." Elise said it plainly, because she had decided, somewhere between the pharmacy and this moment, that the era of lying to Bram Hartwell was over. His face didn't change, but something behind it did — a door opening onto a long dark hallway. "You were the young detective on the case. Your statement corroborated the timeline. I've read the transcripts, Mr. Hartwell. All of them. You said you saw Lena's truck on the quarry road at 11:40."

"I said what I believed I saw."

"Did you?" She held his gaze. "Or did you see what four important men needed you to have seen?"

The radiator knocked. Rain ran down the window in long silver threads. Bram looked at the letter again, and Elise watched thirty years move across his face like weather.

"I've asked myself that question," he said at last, "every day for longer than you've been alive, and I still don't have an answer I trust. Which is why—" He stopped. Started again. "Which is why I'm going to ask you for something I have no right to ask."

"Ask anyway."

"Leave the letters with me. Tonight. Just tonight."

"No." She said it instantly, and watched surprise flicker across his face — he'd expected negotiation. "They don't leave my sight. Ever. That's not distrust, Mr. Hartwell. That's arithmetic. These are the only physical proof my mother left on earth, and the last person who tried to introduce inconvenient evidence in this town ended up convicted. If someone ransacks your shop looking for them, I want them to fail."

Something that might have been respect crossed his features. "Fair. Unflattering, but fair."

"Then we read them here. Together. Every page. You tell me what a detective sees, and I'll tell you what a daughter knows, and maybe between us we can figure out who W.K. is."

Bram was quiet for a moment. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the awning over the window seat where, three weeks ago, she had first sat across from him and lied about being a journalist.

"There's a problem with your plan," he said.

"Name it."

"If W.K. is who I'm starting to think W.K. might be, then finding him doesn't just reopen your mother's case." He tapped the October letter once, gently, like knocking on a door he wasn't sure he wanted opened. "It means somebody in this town has been hiding inside another man's alibi for thirty years. And people who hide that well, Elise — they don't come out politely. They come out fighting. Your mother's letter says there was a fourth person on that boat. What it doesn't say is whether that fourth person was a witness..."

He trailed off, and she finished it for him, because she'd had eleven years to finish it in the dark:

"...or the killer."

Bram nodded slowly. Then he reached across the table, turned the letter back toward himself, and picked up his pen.

"All right," he said. "Start at the beginning. March 2001. Tell me about the coffee."

And for the first time since her mother died, Elise Vann began to read the letters aloud to someone who was listening — not out of pity, not out of duty, but out of the dangerous, rekindled, ridiculous hope that the truth, thirty years late, might still be admitted into evidence.
