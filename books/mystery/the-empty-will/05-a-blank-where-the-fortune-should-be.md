## 5. A Blank Where the Fortune Should Be

The will existed. That much Ines established by the second morning, and it cost him less effort than he'd expected — which was itself a kind of information.

He had come to Vane House under the fiction of a routine policy audit: the estate's coverage was due for renewal, the insurer wanted an updated inventory of the art, the silver, the boats. It was the sort of errand that made him furniture. People spoke in front of furniture. They apologized to it when they bumped into it in hallways, and then they kept talking, because furniture did not listen — or so they believed. Ines Halloran had made a twenty-year career out of being believed to be furniture.

By nine o'clock he had confirmed the existence of the document through Mrs. Adeyemi, who mentioned it with the flat efficiency she applied to everything — linens, menus, funerals.

"Mr. Sloane holds it," she said, polishing a serving spoon that already gleamed. "Sealed until the anniversary dinner. Mr. Edmund's instruction. He was very particular about it."

"Particular how?"

She set the spoon down with a small click. "Everything at the right time, Mr. Halloran. That was his way. He used to say a house runs on timing the way a clock runs on teeth." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion exactly, but the wariness of a woman who had learned which questions were safe. "You'll want the dining room inventory next. The good china comes out only twice a year."

* * *

The dining room was long enough to seat thirty and currently being prepared for eleven. Wren directed two footmen with the crisp authority of someone who had grown up watching her grandmother do it, though Ines had gathered that Wren had spent most of the last six years away — London, then Lisbon, then nowhere in particular. She caught him counting place settings and raised an eyebrow.

"Auditing the tableware now?"

"Insurance covers breakage," Ines said. "Even emotional breakage, if you itemize it correctly."

That earned him half a smile, quickly banked. "Eleven settings. Family, plus Sloane, plus you, apparently, if Marguerite has her way. She's decided you're amusing, which is more than anyone else in this house has managed in seven years."

"And the eleventh?"

Wren's hands stilled on the crystal she was aligning. "Corwin's place," she said, too lightly. "Habit. He was groundskeeper here for years. Mother kept setting his place after he left, and I suppose none of us had the heart to stop her." She resumed aligning glasses with a precision that suggested the subject was closed. Ines filed it away: *left*, not *died*. And a widow who set a table for a servant who had gone.

He was still turning that over when Tobias drifted in, dressed at eleven in the morning in the manner of a man who had dressed at midnight and simply never gone to bed. He was handsome the way old money is handsome — softened, slightly blurred, like a photograph left in a window. He poured himself a sherry from the decanter on the sideboard without asking, which told Ines everything about ownership in this house.

"The auditor," Tobias said pleasantly. "Tell me honestly — do I own this decanter or does the insurance company?"

"It depends who breaks it."

Tobias laughed, a real laugh, and for a moment looked ten years younger and considerably more frightened. "I like you. Sloane sent someone dull last time. A man with a briefcase full of adjectives." He drank. "Speaking of Sloane — you'll meet him soon enough. The great Garrick. Forty years of telling this family what its own paper says. He'll be here Thursday to go over the reading with us. Etiquette lessons, can you imagine? We're to be coached in how to inherit."

"How *do* you inherit?" Ines asked. "Properly, I mean."

"Graciously," Tobias said, "and in order of age. Wren first, since she's Edmund's blood and the favorite. Then me, since I'm the name. Then Marguerite gets the house for her lifetime, presumably, and the rest goes into trust, because God forbid any of us be trusted with actual money." He said it glibly, but Ines watched his fingers around the glass, and the fingers were not glib at all. They were white-knuckled, and they trembled once before he steadied them against the sideboard.

"You sound certain of the contents," Ines observed.

"I'm certain of what Sloane told me." Tobias smiled, and beneath the charm lay something raw. "Which is that I'm provided for. Generously. Enough to clear up certain... administrative difficulties." He set down the glass. "So you see, auditor, I have every reason to hope Thursday goes smoothly. Whatever happens at that dinner, whatever is read from those pages — remember that some of us are betting our lives on it."

He left the way he'd arrived, like weather. Ines stood alone in the dining room among eleven place settings and thought about the phrase *betting our lives*, and about a pale stranger in a rented office who had paid triple to prevent a drowning that hadn't happened yet.

* * *

Garrick Sloane arrived Thursday in a gray car driven by a gray man, and Ines understood within four minutes of meeting him why three generations of Vanes had handed this man their lives to manage. Sloane was seventy if he was a day, tall without stooping, with white hair combed back from a face built for gravitas — deep lines arranged just so, eyes the color of wet slate. He shook Ines's hand with a grip calibrated to convey respect between professionals.

"Halloran. The auditor." Sloane's voice was a cello played low. "Marguerite speaks well of you. High praise; she speaks well of almost no one these days."

"She's kind."

"She's ill," Sloane corrected gently, "and we must all learn to translate kindness out of confusion. You'll find the estate's paperwork in order. It always is. I've seen to this family's documents since before young Tobias was born." He said it without pride, the way a lighthouse might mention the sea.

They walked the gallery together while Sloane conducted his pre-dinner inspection, and Ines let him talk, because Sloane talked beautifully and beautiful talkers reveal themselves. The portraits watched them pass — Edmund Vane stern in oils, Eleanor Vane luminous and unfinished-looking, as though the painter had been called away mid-stroke and no one had dared finish her.

"The reading itself is simple," Sloane said. "The will is sealed — my office holds it, witnessed, notarized. At dinner, after the toast to Edmund's memory, I unseal it and read it aloud. There are formalities. The family will want guidance."

"Guidance?"

"Some inheritances bruise." Sloane paused before Eleanor's portrait, and Ines watched his face do something subtle — a tightening around the mouth, there and gone, like a fish under ice. "Edmund was a private man. Deliberate. He rewrote this will four times in his final decade, you know. Four times. Each revision more particular than the last. He wanted certainty that his wishes would land exactly as intended."

"Four revisions," Ines said. "That's unusual?"

"For a man with one granddaughter, one son-in-law, and a widow? Extremely." Sloane turned from the portrait and smiled, and the smile was warm and professional and absolutely opaque. "But then, Edmund was an unusual man. Now — the boathouse. The policy on the boats needs updating. Shall we?"

* * *

It was Wren who gave him the first crack in the wall, later that afternoon, deliberately, he was almost sure. She found him in the library pretending to inventory first editions and dropped into the chair across from him without invitation.

"Sloane told you the reading order," she said. "Who inherits first."

"He described the structure. Age and bloodline."

"Mm. And what did he tell Tobias?"

Ines weighed it. "That he's provided for generously. Enough to settle debts."

Something flickered across Wren's face — not surprise. Confirmation. "And Marguerite?"

"That the house is hers for life."

Wren leaned back and studied him the way one studies a chessboard after an opponent's surprising move. "Here's the thing, Mr. Halloran. Sloane told me I inherit nearly everything. The bulk of the estate, the trusts, the art — 'your grandfather's fondest wish,' he said, verbatim, three weeks ago, in his office, with tea." Her voice stayed level, but Ines heard the wire underneath it. "And I know for a fact he told Tobias he's provided for *generously*. Generously. Tobias has debts you could sail a ferry through. Generous means Tobias walks away whole." She ticked points off on her fingers. "And Marguerite keeps the house. And I get nearly everything. Add it up, auditor. Everyone's been promised the lion's share. Everyone's been told, in Sloane's exact careful language, that their hopes will be honored."

"People misremember."

"My memory is fine," Wren said. "Better than fine. I've had six years away from this family to practice remembering things clearly." She leaned forward. "Four revisions, Sloane says. Four. A man doesn't revise a will four times and leave everyone's share ambiguous. Unless—" she stopped herself, jaw tightening.

"Unless what?"

"Unless the promises aren't in the will at all." She said it quietly, testing the shape of it in her own mouth. "Unless what's in the will is something else entirely, and the promises were just... lubricant. To make sure everyone shows up to dinner smiling."

The library clock ticked through several seconds. Ines felt the familiar cold click behind his sternum, the one that meant a case had shifted its weight — the sensation of a locked door revealing that it had never been locked at all, merely painted shut.

"When did Sloane tell you these things?" he asked. "Exactly. Dates, if you have them."

"Three weeks ago, mine. Tobias's was — he mentioned it at the wake for Edmund, actually. Over brandy. Marguerite's was earlier, months ago, she told me herself, though she tells me lots of things lately and half of them arrive sideways." Wren frowned suddenly. "Why does the timing matter?"

Because, Ines thought, a lawyer of forty years' standing — a man whose entire profession was the sanctity of sealed documents — did not hint at contents. Not once, not carelessly, not to three separate heirs. Either Sloane was the sloppiest solicitor in the country's history, or—

Or the hints cost him nothing, because there was nothing in the will to contradict them.

"Miss Vane," Ines said carefully, "has it occurred to you that a promise can be true even if the paper it's written on is blank?"

Wren went very still. Outside, wind moved through the cedars, and somewhere in the house a door closed — softly, the way doors close in houses where people are listening.

"What do you mean, blank?"

"I mean," Ines said, "that I'd like to see the seal on that will. And I'd like to know who witnessed it. And I would very much like to know why a man who revised his will four times chose to lock it away from the one person — his lawyer — who'd have to fill in the blanks if anything were missing."

Wren stared at him. "Fill in the— Sloane drafted it. Sloane always drafts everything. He's drafted every Vane document since before I was born. Birth certificates, marriage settlements, death certificates, wills." She recited it like a litany, and then, mid-litany, heard it. Ines watched the color change in her face. "*Death certificates.*"

"Your grandmother's among them."

The silence stretched. When Wren spoke again, her voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "Seven years ago, when Eleanor died, Sloane handled everything. The ruling, the insurance, the burial arrangements. My grandfather signed whatever was put in front of him — he wasn't well, near the end, his hands shook, he'd sign anything Sloane told him was routine." Her eyes came up, sharp and frightened and alive. "Mr. Halloran. What did my grandfather actually sign?"

Ines didn't answer, because he didn't yet know — but his memory, that merciless archive, had already begun arranging the pieces on its dark table: four revisions of a will that might contain nothing; three heirs promised three fortunes; a lawyer who coached families in how to inherit; a pale man named Corwin paying triple his fee to stop a drowning scheduled for the same night as a reading; and a housekeeper who set a twelfth place at the table for a groundskeeper who had *left*.

One document tied them all together. One sealed envelope in a gray office, waiting for Thursday.

"I don't know yet," Ines said truthfully. "But I intend to find out before anyone reads a single word aloud."

Wren nodded slowly. Then, rising, she paused at the door and looked back at him with an expression he would remember verbatim for years afterward — the look of a woman deciding to trust someone with no rational grounds whatsoever.

"There's one more thing," she said. "Sloane told me my grandfather's fondest wish was for me to inherit. But my grandfather told *me* his fondest wish, the last time I saw him alive. He held my hand and he said: *Whatever they tell you is in that will, Wren — don't believe it until you've seen the pages yourself.*"

She left him alone with that, and with the portraits, and with the growing, glacial understanding that Edmund Vane had known exactly what he was doing — that the blankness, if blankness there was, was not an absence at all.

It was bait.
