## 11. Tobias's Debts

The county records office smelled of dust and old paper and the particular despair of men who had spent their lives filing other men's failures. Ines Halloran signed the visitor's ledger with a hand that did not tremble, though everything in him was trembling quietly, the way a struck bell trembles after the sound has died.

He had come for Tobias Vane's debts.

It had taken him two days to get here, two days of careful questions dressed as insurance due diligence. A routine audit of the Vane estate's liability exposure — that was his cover, and it held well enough. Insurance companies were vultures by trade; nobody questioned one circling. The clerk, a gray woman named Pell whose cardigan had been mended at both elbows, fetched the lien filings without suspicion. She had seen a hundred auditors. She would not remember him by tomorrow, which suited him fine.

What she brought him was a box.

Not a metaphorical box — an actual one, cardboard, softening at the corners, tied with string that someone had knotted and reknotted so many times the fibers had gone furry. Inside: seven years of financial filings against Tobias Edmund Vane, junior of that name, heir to nothing yet and debtor to nearly everyone.

Ines untied the string and began to read.

His memory worked like this: he read a page once, and it stayed. Not as words on a page but as the page itself — the coffee ring on the second filing, the clerk's hasty initial in the margin of the fourth, the way the ink of one signature had bled because the signer's hand had been wet or shaking. He could close his eyes later and see them all again, laid out like photographs on a darkroom table. It was a gift, people said. They said it the way you'd call a scar a gift. It meant he never got to forget anything, including the things he wanted to.

The first lien was dated eleven years ago. Eight hundred pounds, secured against a motorcar. Small. Almost charming. A young man's debt, the kind you incur on a dare or a woman or a bad night at cards. The lender was a firm called Meridian Advance, address on Cordwainer Street, a name Ines didn't know but wrote down anyway, though he didn't need to write anything down. He wrote things down sometimes just to feel normal.

* * *

By the third hour, the shape of it had emerged, and the shape was ugly.

Tobias's debts did not behave the way debts usually behaved. Ordinary debtors zigzagged — they borrowed from one source, defaulted, borrowed from another, spiraled messily outward like water finding every crack. Tobias's debts descended. They went down in a straight line, year over year, each loan larger than the last, each secured against something closer to the bone. Motorcar. Watch. A share of an annuity from his grandmother's trust. Then, five years ago, the first lien against his expected inheritance — his expected inheritance, which was to say, against a will no one had read, sealed these many years, its contents known to no living soul except perhaps the man who drafted it.

Ines sat back in the hard chair and looked at that document for a long time.

How do you secure a loan against money you have not received, from a will you have not seen? You don't — not unless the lender knows what the will says. Or believes it does. Or has been told, confidently, privately, by someone in a position to know.

Meridian Advance. Cordwainer Street. He turned the name over. It sounded like a lending firm the way a stage set sounds like a room — correct from the front, hollow if you walked around behind it.

He asked Miss Pell for the corporate registry volumes, and she brought them, and he found Meridian Advance listed, incorporated nineteen years past, directors: three names he'd never heard of, all sharing a single registered agent.

The registered agent was Sloane & Verrity.

Garrick Sloane's firm. The family's lawyers. Executors of every Vane death, drafters of every Vane document, keepers of the sealed will — and the registered agent of the company that had been tightening a leash around Tobias Vane's throat for eleven years, link by link, each loan a little longer than the last, until the leash reached all the way back to the estate itself.

Ines closed the registry volume and sat very still.

A man walked past the reading table, and his footsteps sounded very loud.

* * *

He spent the afternoon pulling thread after thread, and every thread led back to the same spool.

Meridian Advance had bought Tobias's paper from two smaller lenders — standard practice, debt consolidation, nothing sinister in itself. But the purchase prices were wrong. Too generous. Nobody paid face value for a wastrel's unsecured paper unless they wanted the paper more than the money. And why would a lender want a debtor? Because a debtor wasn't a liability. A debtor was an asset. A debtor was a man who did what he was told.

The interest terms were stranger still. Twice, across eleven years, Meridian had renegotiated Tobias's obligations downward — forgiven principal, deferred payments — at moments that mapped, with uncomfortable precision, onto events at Vane House. The first forgiveness came six months after Eleanor Vane drowned. The second came fourteen months ago, within weeks of Edmund Vane's final revision of his will — the revision that produced the sealed document now waiting in Sloane's office safe like a loaded gun.

Forgive a man's debts twice, and he is yours forever. Not his gratitude — his fear. Because a man whose debts are mysteriously lightened knows, in the marrow of him, that the lightness isn't kindness. It's payment. Payment for something he did, or something he saw, or something he agreed not to see.

Ines thought about the boathouse. Seven years ago. The night Eleanor Vane went into the water. Tobias had been seen near it — Wren had hinted as much, in her sharp, sideways way, and the old police file had a statement from him that read like a boy reciting lines he'd been made to memorize. *I was walking. I heard nothing. I went home.*

And six months later, eight hundred pounds of debt became four hundred, and then nothing, and Tobias Vane had gone on drinking and gambling and failing ever since, always broke, always bailed, always exactly as free as a dog on a chain that is long enough to let him pretend he isn't chained.

Motive bloomed everywhere. That was the trouble.

If Ines needed a murderer, he now had one with textbook perfection: a wastrel heir, drowning in debt, controlled by the family lawyer, positioned at the scene of the first death, silent ever since. Any magistrate in the county would look at this file and nod. Case shaped. Motive, means, opportunity, history. Neat as a place setting.

Too neat.

That was the thing his memory kept flagging, the way it flagged the tide chart that contradicted the timeline of Eleanor's death. Real guilt was messy. Real murderers left ragged edges, contradictions, stupid mistakes. This file had no edges. Every document dovetailed into the next. Eleven years of paperwork, and not one irregularity, not one human stumble — as if the whole record had been composed rather than accumulated. As if somewhere, years ago, a careful hand had sat down and designed a debtor the way an architect designs a foundation: not to hold a building up, but to control where the building could stand.

You don't create evidence of motive before the crime it explains. Unless the crime is part of the design.

Ines packed the files back into their box, retied the string with the same fussy double knot, and thanked Miss Pell, who had already forgotten his face. Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of pewter, and the wind coming off the estuary carried the smell of rain and salt and, faintly, of the sea that had taken Eleanor Vane and given nothing back.

* * *

He walked the long way back toward Vane House, along the cliff road, because he wanted to think and the house was the worst place he knew for thinking. The portraits watched you there. The staff flinched at the word *dinner*. Grief hung in its corridors like a coat left on a hook seven years too long.

Money as a leash. He turned the phrase over as he walked. He had seen it before, in other houses, other families — wealth used not as a possession but as an instrument, a means of holding people in position. But he had never seen it done with such patience. Eleven years. Most men couldn't sustain a lie for eleven days. Whoever built Tobias's debt structure had sustained it across a decade, adjusting, forgiving, tightening, always with the long game in view: a will reading, someday, when the family gathered and the documents were opened and the inheritance flowed — into whose hands?

Into the hands of a man who owed everything. An heir who inherited nothing he could keep. Money that passed through Tobias like water through a sieve, straight into the accounts of Meridian Advance, straight back through the registered agent, straight home to Cordwainer Street, to Sloane & Verrity.

Except—

Except that the will was sealed. Except that nobody knew what it said. Except that Corwin, whoever he was, had paid triple to prove a murder hadn't happened yet, naming Lucinda Vane, drowned like her mother. Except that the tide chart lied, and the boathouse held secrets, and Mrs. Adeyemi flinched when she thought no one watched her watching.

The pieces refused to sit still. Every time he arranged them into a picture, one of them slid.

Unless the will was empty.

The thought arrived unbidden, the way his best thoughts always did — not reasoned to but recognized, the way you recognize a face in a crowd you weren't searching. A will signed but blank. A fortune that existed only in the telling. If Sloane had spent thirty years building his web on the assumption of what the will contained — if he had lent against an inheritance he himself had drafted, secured debts against money he himself would distribute — then the empty pages wouldn't ruin him. They'd be the trap sprung on *him*. Everything he'd built, every leash, every forgiveness, every carefully managed heir, would be revealed at the moment the seal was broken and the pages proved blank.

But that required Edmund Vane to have planned it. Required the old man to have known.

Ines stopped at the cliff's edge. Below, the tide was going out, exposing the black ribs of rocks that were underwater half the day. You could drown in water that looked shallow. You could drown in water that looked safe. The chart said one thing; the sea said another; and the body always told the truth too late.

He stood there until the cold worked through his coat, and then he turned back toward the house, because there was one more thing he needed, and it wasn't in any records office.

He needed to hear Tobias Vane lie to his face.

* * *

He found him in the billiard room, which was less a room for billiards than a room where Tobias went to be alone with a bottle and the comforting green silence of felt. The lamps were lit against the graying day. Tobias stood at the table, cue in hand, addressing a shot with the seriousness of a man defusing ordnance, and missed it by a full ball's width.

"Mr. Halloran." He didn't turn around. "Come to count my father's silver?"

"Come to talk about Cordwainer Street."

The cue tip clicked against the slate. For a moment — just a moment — the charming languor dropped off Tobias Vane like a coat slipping from narrow shoulders, and underneath was something quick and frightened and young. Then the charm came back up, buttoned and smiling, but Ines had seen it, and Tobias knew he'd seen it, and the knowledge sat between them like a third man at the table.

"I don't know the street," Tobias said.

"Eleven years of loans," Ines said pleasantly. "Meridian Advance. Registered agent, Sloane & Verrity. Your family's own solicitor holding the other end of your leash, and you signing your inheritance away a piece at a time against a will you've never read." He took a step into the lamplight. "I'm not the police, Mr. Vane. I'm worse. I'm a man with a perfect memory and a great deal of time."

Tobias set down the cue with exaggerated care, the way a drunk sets down a glass to prove he can. When he spoke, his voice had gone quiet and stripped of performance.

"You think I don't know what I am?" he said. "You think I haven't done the arithmetic? I've had eleven years to do the arithmetic." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as a cork pulled from an empty bottle. "Every penny I've borrowed in my life has passed through the same three hands. I sign, Garrick witnesses, Meridian pays, Garrick collects. Round and round. I'm not a debtor, Halloran. I'm a *holding pattern*."

"And the night your grandmother died—"

"Don't." The word cracked like ice underfoot. Tobias's hand had gone white on the rail of the billiard table. "Don't ask me that. Not tonight. Not—" He stopped. Swallowed. Looked at the door, then at the window, then at Ines, and his eyes were the eyes of a man standing at the edge of a confession the way Ines had stood at the edge of the cliff, feeling the pull of the drop.

"Not tonight," he said again, softer. "Ask me again when the will is read. Ask me anything you like then. Because whatever's in those pages—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the study where the sealed document waited in its safe, "—it decides whether I'm a free man or a dead one. Either way, I'll have nothing left to lose. And a man with nothing left to lose, Halloran—"

"—is dangerous," Ines finished.

"Is *honest*," said Tobias. "For the first time in his life."

He picked up the cue again, turned back to the table, and addressed the balls with his ruined concentration, and Ines understood the interview was over. At the door he paused.

"One question," Ines said. "When Meridian forgave your debts — the second time, fourteen months ago — what did they tell you it was for?"

Tobias's shoulders went rigid beneath his expensive, shabby jacket.

"They didn't tell me," he said, without turning around. "Garrick did. He came here, sat where you're sitting, poured himself the good whisky, and told me the estate had decided to *look after its own*." A pause. The lamp flickered. "And then he smiled, and he said — I've remembered the words for fourteen months, Halloran, the way you remember words when they frighten you — he said, *'Everything comes right at the anniversary dinner. You'll see.'*"

The cue struck. The balls scattered with a sound like small bones breaking.

Ines stood in the doorway a moment longer, watching the green felt, the lamplight, the man bent over his beautiful useless game. Fourteen months ago. Weeks after Edmund's final revision. *Everything comes right at the anniversary dinner.* Sloane had said it then, casually, over whisky — had promised it, promised a future only the sealed will could deliver, to a man whose debts he controlled.

Either Sloane knew what the will contained.

Or Sloane intended to make it contain it.

Outside, the rain had begun, soft and steady against the windows, and somewhere below, near the dark line of the shore, the tide was turning — coming in now, patient and certain, covering the rocks that had shown themselves all afternoon, erasing the evidence of the shallows, making the deep water look like safety all the way to the edge.

Ines pulled his coat collar up and went to find Wren.
