## 16. What Wren Carries

Wren Vane had learned to read people the way other people read weather — quickly, from a distance, with an eye toward the storm.

So when the "insurance auditor" appeared at Vane House on a Tuesday in October, asking questions no auditor had ever asked her grandmother's housekeeper about the placement of the boathouse dock, she did what she always did: she watched, she waited, and she said nothing until saying nothing stopped being useful.

Three days was enough.

She found him on the fourth morning in the east garden, standing before the stone sundial that hadn't told time since before she was born, his hands in the pockets of a coat too thin for the season. He didn't startle when she approached. That alone set him apart from every other stranger who had ever walked these grounds; the garden had a way of making people flinch, as though the hedges themselves were eavesdropping.

"You're not an auditor," she said.

He turned. Up close, his eyes were the color of wet slate, and they moved over her face with an attention that should have been unsettling but wasn't — or rather, it was unsettling precisely because it felt like being *seen*, which was a thing Wren Vane had spent thirty-one years avoiding.

"No," he agreed. "I'm not."

"Then what are you?"

"Ines Halloran." He said it plainly, without flourish, as if the name were just a name and not a key turning somewhere. "And you're Wren Vane, which means you already knew that, because you've been watching me since I arrived, and you have your grandfather's memory for faces even if you'd never admit it."

Her breath caught — a small, involuntary sound, quickly smothered. "You've been here four days."

"Five," said Ines Halloran. "But who's counting."

* * *

They walked, because walking made conversation easier and because neither of them wanted to be overheard by the house. The gardens of Vane House ran long and half-wild now, the formal beds gone to seed since Eleanor's death, the gravel paths softening into moss. Wren had loved the neglect. It felt honest, after years of rooms where everything was polished to hide the rot beneath.

"You asked Mrs. Adeyemi about the boathouse," she said. "Auditors don't care where the boathouse is."

"Auditors don't care about much of anything," Ines said. "That's what makes the cover so good. Nobody looks closely at a man whose job is to be boring."

"And your actual job?"

He considered her for a long moment. The morning light came through the bare linden branches in pale bars, striping his face, and Wren thought: *he's deciding how much truth I can carry.* She straightened her spine. Whatever else she was — the granddaughter nobody counted, the one who'd left and come back with nothing but a suitcase and a grudge — she could carry more than anyone gave her credit for.

"I investigate things," he said finally. "For money. Usually insurance fraud, sometimes worse. A week ago a man walked into my office, paid me three times my fee, and asked me to prevent a murder that hasn't happened yet."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. Wren kept walking, one step, two, three, because if she stopped moving she would stop breathing.

"A murder," she repeated.

"At this house. On the night of the anniversary dinner. The victim—" he paused, and she heard him choosing, again, how much to give her— "was named in advance."

"Lucinda," Wren said quietly. "My mother."

Ines stopped walking. So, a moment later, did she.

"You know something," he said.

"I know my mother drowned seven years ago in a pond that was barely deep enough to drown a child. I know the coroner called it an accident and everyone in that house nodded and went back to their sherry." Her voice came out flat and hard, the voice she used for difficult phone calls and hospital corridors. "I know I was seventeen and nobody thought to ask me anything, because why would they? I was just the girl who cried at the funeral."

"That's not what I meant."

"No," she admitted. "It isn't." She looked out across the lawn, toward the distant gray line of the boathouse roof, half-swallowed by ivy. "I never believed it. Not for one day. And there's something else — someone else who was there that night, or close enough to see. But that's not what I came to tell you."

She reached into her coat pocket.

Her fingers had been shaking for six days, ever since the letter arrived, and they shook now as she drew out the envelope — cheap paper, no return address, postmarked from a town forty miles away that meant nothing to her. She held it out, and Ines took it with a care that surprised her, the way one handles something that might still be alive.

* * *

They sat on the cold stone bench beneath the dead sundial, side by side, not quite touching, and Wren watched him read.

She had read the letter perhaps fifty times herself. She knew its contents the way she knew the scar on her left knee: intimately, unwillingly, forever.

*Miss Vane — Your presence at the anniversary dinner on the fourteenth is required. Failure to attend will result in forfeiture of all claims under the estate of Edmund Vane. This is your only notice.*

No signature. No warmth. Just that flat administrative menace, typed in slightly faded ribbon on paper that smelled faintly of dust.

"There was a second line," Wren said, when he'd finished. "Penciled underneath, in different ink. It said: *Come, or lose everything.* Same message, but — I don't know. Angrier. More human. Like the typist wrote the letter and then someone else leaned over their shoulder and added the threat by hand."

Ines went very still. It was a particular kind of stillness, she realized — not absence of movement but compression, a man gathering himself around something enormous.

"May I?" He produced from his own coat a folded sheet of paper, worn soft at the creases, and laid it beside hers on the bench.

Two letters. Two envelopes. Wren bent over them, and her heart began to beat in a strange, slow rhythm, because the similarity was undeniable and the difference was worse.

Same typewriter. She could see it in the letterforms — the slightly crooked lowercase *e*, the *r* that printed heavy on its left shoulder, the way the capital *V* sat a fraction too high on the line. Whoever had typed both letters had used the same battered machine, probably for years, probably daily.

But the hands behind the words were different. Hers was clipped, bureaucratic, each sentence a closed door. His — the letter Ines had received, warning him of a murder yet to come — rambled, doubled back, apologized mid-sentence, as though the writer couldn't decide whether to be afraid or ashamed.

"Different people," Wren said slowly. "Same machine."

"Same office, most likely." Ines's slate eyes had gone distant, reading something far beyond the paper. "A typewriter like this isn't a personal object. It's institutional. A firm. A practice. Somewhere there's a room with this machine in it, and more than one person uses it."

"Garrick Sloane's office has a typewriter," Wren said. The words came out of her before she could weigh them, and once they were out she understood they'd been waiting in her chest for days, looking for a listener. "I saw it when I was a girl. An old Underwood, black as a beetle. Grandfather dictated letters to him every Thursday. I used to sit in the corner and watch the keys jump."

Ines turned to look at her fully then, and whatever he saw in her face made him nod, slow and grave, as if confirming a suspicion he'd been carrying alone.

"The lawyer," he said. "Forty years with the family. Executor of every will, drafter of every document, keeper of every seal." He tapped the two letters with one finger. "And now possibly the author of anonymous threats mailed to the heirs — and to me."

"Why would Sloane threaten his own clients?"

"Maybe to gather them." Ines's voice dropped low, though the nearest living soul was a hundred yards away behind a hedge. "Think about it, Miss Vane. You were told to attend the dinner or lose everything. Tobias has debts he can't speak of. Marguerite hasn't left her rooms in weeks except when summoned. Everyone with a claim on that estate is being pulled toward the same table on the same night, by the same invisible hand. That's not a threat. That's stage management."

The word settled over Wren with a chill that had nothing to do with the October air.

"Cast," she said. "We're being cast. All of us. Placed at our marks."

"And the dinner is the performance." Ines folded both letters together, hers and his, and held them a moment between his fingers as though weighing them. "Someone has written an ending for this family, Miss Vane. Someone who knows the house, knows the will, knows exactly where every player will be sitting. The question is whether we let them play it out — or whether we rewrite it first."

* * *

They stayed on the bench as the light thickened toward noon, and something happened between them that Wren would later find difficult to name — not trust, exactly, not so soon, but the beginning architecture of it. Two load-bearing walls, erected in silence.

She told him about leaving home at nineteen and not coming back for twelve years. About the apartments in three cities, the jobs that never quite fit, the sense she'd carried all her life of being a guest in her own family's story. About the phone call from Mrs. Adeyemi last month — *your grandfather's dinner is coming, miss, and the house feels wrong, and I thought you should be here for it* — and how she'd packed a bag within the hour without understanding why.

"I don't belong here," she said. "I never have. Tobias got the charm, Mother got the beauty, and I got—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the sharp angles and the unsoftened voice— "this."

"Observation," Ines said. "You got observation. It's worth more than the other two combined, and considerably harder to spend."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse. When had anyone last made her laugh at Vane House? When had anyone last *seen* her clearly enough to try?

"And you?" she asked. "Where do you belong, Mr. Halloran?"

He was quiet for a while. A crow crossed the gray sky above the lindens, and the wind moved through the dead garden with a sound like old paper turning.

"Nowhere, particularly," he said at last. "I remember everything, Miss Vane. Every face, every page, every conversation, word for word, going back to when I was four years old. Do you know what that does to a person? It means nothing ever fades. Nothing softens. Every cruelty I've ever witnessed is filed away in perfect condition, available on demand." He smiled, and it didn't reach his eyes. "People think a memory like mine is a gift. Mostly it's a warehouse I can't leave."

Wren studied his profile — the fine lines at his temple, the careful way he held his hands, as though they might betray him.

"So you memorize everyone else's endings," she said, "and you never get one of your own."

Something flickered across his face — surprise, yes, but underneath it something rawer, almost grateful, the look of a man hearing his own secret spoken back to him in a stranger's voice.

"That's it exactly," he said quietly.

"Well." She pulled her coat tighter against the wind and found, to her own astonishment, that she was smiling. "It seems to me we're in the same predicament, Mr. Halloran. Two outsiders, hired or summoned to a house that doesn't want us, caught in a story someone else is writing. If we're going to be cast in this play regardless, I'd rather choose my fellow actors."

"Is that an offer?"

"It's an alliance. Don't make it sentimental." But her voice betrayed her, warming despite itself, and she suspected he noticed, and suspected further that he wouldn't hold it against her.

"Alliance," he agreed. Then, rising from the bench, he held out the two letters — hers and his, paired now, evidence and bond alike. "Keep yours. I'll keep mine. And tomorrow, if you're willing, I'll show you something about the tide charts from the night your mother died that the coroner either missed or ignored."

The cold that moved through Wren then was not entirely unpleasant. It was the cold of clarity, of a fog lifting off water she'd been unable to see for seven years.

"Tobias was in the boathouse that night," she said. "I'm telling you now so you're not surprised later. I don't know what he saw. But he's been drinking himself toward death ever since, and men drink like that for only two reasons — guilt, or fear. Sometimes both."

Ines Halloran looked down at her for a long moment, and in his wet-slate eyes she saw the warehouse of everything he remembered, and — newly, carefully — a place being cleared inside it, a shelf reserved, for her.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we go and ask him what he's afraid of."

He turned toward the house, coat collar up against the wind, and Wren remained a moment on the bench beneath the broken sundial, holding her letter against her ribs like a shield, or a promise.

Behind her, at the edge of the garden, half-hidden by the hedge, Mrs. Adeyemi stood watching them — and the expression on the old housekeeper's face was not disapproval.

It was relief. As if, after seven long years, someone had finally come to help her carry what she carried too.

And in her hand, Wren saw, glinting dull silver in the noon light, was a small iron key.
