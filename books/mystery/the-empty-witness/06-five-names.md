## 6. Five Names

The rain had been at the cloister glass all morning, patient as a clerk, and Jonah Fairweather had been at his desk almost as long, doing the thing he was best at in all the world: making lists.

He had three sheets of paper before him now, which for Jonah constituted chaos. The first held the text of the two letters, transcribed in his small, upright hand — the confession and the promise, *the fifth will follow the fourth*, copied out twice because he did not trust himself to look at the original without feeling the words move. The second sheet held everything he knew about Edward Lassiter's death on the north stair: the angle of the fall, the wear on the stone that said no body had ever actually tumbled there before, the curious absence of scuffing on the eleventh step where a man's heel ought to have caught.

The third sheet was blank except for a single line at the top, underlined twice:

**Who died in 1994?**

And beneath it, after thirty years of dust and one very good afternoon in the diocesan registry:

**One boy. Five survivors.**

Jonah sat back and looked at it the way a man looks at a door he has walked past every day of his life and only now noticed was standing slightly ajar.

The fire itself he had documented the previous evening, working late with the cathedral's own heating turned off because he liked the cold when he was thinking — Marguerite would have laughed at that, once; she used to say he thought better in winter the way other men drank better in company. The dormitory block of St. Aldhelm's choir school, November 1994. One boy dead — a scholarship pupil named Thomas Wren, eleven years old, asleep in the end cubicle where the fire had started or been started. Five boys pulled out alive. And Father Ambrose, young then, golden then, beloved then, standing on the wet grass in his cassock with his arm around each boy in turn, telling the police — telling everyone, for thirty years — exactly what had happened.

Five statements. Jonah had read them side by side until midnight, and the thing that had made him put down his reading glasses and press the heels of his hands into his eyes was not any discrepancy. It was the absence of one. Five boys, five separate interviews, conducted separately, and the accounts matched word for word in places where no two honest memories ever match. *We heard the bell. We went down the west stair. We stood by the lychgate.* Word for word. Not similar. Identical.

Someone had given them a story, and they had learned it well enough to keep it for thirty years.

Which meant, Jonah thought, tapping his pen against his teeth, that there were five people alive in the world right now who knew what really happened in that dormitory — plus whoever had taught them the story, though Ambrose was ten years in his grave. Five witnesses. And a letter writer who confessed to killing a verger and promised a fifth death.

*The fifth will follow the fourth.*

If you were counting deaths, Lassiter was the fourth. But if you were counting something else — if you were counting *them* —

Jonah's pen stopped moving.

"Oh," he said quietly, to the empty office, in the tone of a man who has just found a mistake in a ledger that has been balanced for years. "Oh, you wretched, clever person."

* * *

He worked through lunch, which was not unusual, and forgot tea entirely, which was. Mrs. Pargeter from the vestry office brought him a cheese sandwich at half past one and left it untouched beside him like an offering at a shrine, and he discovered it at four o'clock and ate it in two bites without tasting it, because by then he had names.

Names were not difficult, once you knew to look. That was the trouble with the great and the good of a cathedral city: they never left. They accumulated, like candle soot. Thirty years was long enough for a boy of eleven to become anything, but it was not long enough for this city to forget them, because this city forgot nothing — it simply filed things away, in registers and plaques and the back pages of the *Herald*, and waited for someone like Jonah to come along with a ladder and a good lamp.

He wrote them in pencil first. He always wrote suspects in pencil; ink felt too much like accusation.

**1. The judge.**

Marcus Halloway. He found him in fifteen minutes, which embarrassed Jonah a little; he'd expected the law to be harder to find. Recorder Halloway, as he now was, presided over criminal trials at the Crown Court on Northgate Street, lived in a Georgian house on the Close with a view of the cathedral tower that Jonah happened to know cost more per year than Jonah's entire salary, and had been photographed in the *Herald* no fewer than nine times in the last decade — opening fêtes, sentencing burglars, standing beside the bishop at the rededication of the school chapel. In the oldest photograph, from some charity dinner in the nineties, he was a young man with heavy dark eyebrows and a smile that looked practised even then. Jonah looked at the eyebrows, then at the class photograph from St. Aldhelm's choir school, autumn term 1994, borrowed that morning from the diocesan archive under the unimpeachable pretext of preparing a thirtieth-anniversary exhibition.

Second row, third from left. Heavy eyebrows, even at eleven. Marcus Halloway, survivor number one.

**2. The developer.**

This one took longer, and Jonah enjoyed it more than he cared to admit. The boy in the class photograph — round-faced, grinning, tie already askew — was called Daniel Ockham, and Daniel Ockham had grown up into the sort of man whose name appeared on hoardings. Ockham Developments. Half the new-build flats on the riverfront were his; there had been a fight in the planning committee two years ago about whether he could put a glass box on the old tannery site, and the glass box had won, because money usually does. Jonah had catalogued the planning correspondence himself, in a former life of the archive, and remembered thinking at the time that Mr. Ockham's letters had the tone of a man accustomed to being told yes.

Round face gone hard now, jaw set, hair cropped to grey velvet. But the grin in the photograph was unmistakable, and so, when Jonah checked, was the school record: D. J. Ockham, dormitory block B, cubicle four. Survivor number two.

**3. The headmistress.**

Eleanor Voss. Head of St. Hilda's Preparatory School, out past the ring road — a girls' school, small, expensive, excellent results. Her entry in *Debrett's* (the archive kept a run of *Debrett's* going back to 1962, because you never knew) gave her maiden name: Eleanor Pryce. Pryce, P., dormitory block B, cubicle six, the only girl in the photograph, sitting very straight at the end of the front row with her chin lifted as though she were already aware the camera owed her something. She had been eleven years old and she had already learned to hold still while the world lied around her. Jonah felt something twist gently in his chest looking at her face, and told himself sternly that an archivist does not sentimentalise his subjects.

Survivor number three.

**4. The policeman.**

Gerald Fenwick. This was the one that made Jonah sit very still for a long moment with his pen hovering, because Gerald Fenwick was not merely a survivor of the fire. Gerald Fenwick had been the detective constable assigned to it — no, Jonah corrected himself, checking the date of birth against the school register. Too young in '94 to be a DC. He had joined the force two years later, aged nineteen, and here was the detail that made the rain seem suddenly louder against the glass: Detective Inspector Gerald Fenwick, retired 2019, had spent twenty-three years as the cathedral city's senior investigating officer. Twenty-three years of sudden deaths, and among them — Jonah pulled the file without needing to look up its reference, because he knew these files the way other men know their children — among them the drowning of organist Silas Crane, ruled misadventure. The fall of stonemason Peter Aylward from the tower, ruled accident. The heart attack of sacristan Dennis Hobbes, ruled natural causes.

Fenwick had signed off on all three. Jonah had spent years noticing what Fenwick had declined to notice. And now Fenwick's name sat on Jonah's list of five, in pencil, and Jonah understood with a slow cold settling in his stomach that the man who had smoothed over the accidents might have been smoothing them for a reason — or might, all along, have been waiting for someone else to do the arithmetic he couldn't afford to do himself.

Survivor number four. And the only one of the five who knew about Jonah's three.

*Did he?* Jonah asked himself, and could not answer. He hoped not. Hope, he had learned, was a poor archivist but a loyal companion.

**5.**

The fifth name he did not need the registry for at all. He needed only to stand up, walk the length of the crypt passage, climb the worn steps into the north cloister, and look out of the window at the garth, where the rain was coming down silver on the old grass and Brother Anselm was moving along the herb beds with a trug on his arm, bent double over the rosemary, unhurried, gentle, humming something that was probably a psalm and might possibly have been a music-hall song, because Anselm contained multitudes.

Anselm Reeve. A. Reeve, dormitory block B, cubicle one — the cubicle nearest the door, Jonah noted, the first one out, the one who would have had the clearest view back into the burning room. He had come back to the cathedral at twenty-five, taken his vows at twenty-seven, and had lived in the cloister ever since, tending herbs and grieving pilgrims and being, by universal agreement, the kindest man in the building. Children adored him. The vergers adored him. Jonah adored him, in his reserved way.

Edward Lassiter had adored him. They had played chess on Thursday evenings for years, down in the warming room, and Jonah had catalogued the chess set among the cathedral's minor treasures with a note in his own hand: *gift of E. Lassiter to Br. Anselm, 2011.*

Jonah stood at the cloister window watching the monk in the rain and felt the dread arrive properly at last — not the sharp fright of the letters, which had been fear of a stranger, but this slower, heavier thing, fear of a familiar face. Because here was the shape of it now, undeniable, laid out on his desk in pencil: five children taught one lie by a beloved priest, and thirty years later, one of them dead on a stair, and a letter promising more, and every single one of the five still within reach of this city, this close, this stone.

A judge. A developer. A headmistress. A policeman. A monk.

They had stayed. All five of them had stayed, within the sound of the bells, for thirty years. People did not stay near the scene of an ordinary tragedy. People stayed near the scene of a secret.

* * *

Mrs. Pargeter found him there at the window when she came back for the sandwich plate, and she looked at him with the frank appraisal of a woman who had worked in cathedrals for forty years and considered herself an authority on the interior weather of clergymen, archivists, and other ecclesiastical wildlife.

"You've gone grey," she announced. "Greyer."

"I'm always grey, Mrs. Pargeter. It's my natural condition."

"Greyer than natural." She came and stood beside him, following his gaze out to the garth, where Anselm was now shaking rain off the trug and laughing at something — a robin, probably; Anselm laughed at robins. "That poor brother," she said. "Terrible news about Mr. Lassiter. They were close, those two. He's taking it very quiet, I heard. Barely said a word at the requiem." She sniffed. "Some grieve quiet, mind. My Harold went quiet as a mouse when his mother died, and then cried for a week straight over a broken biscuit tin, so you can't read owt into it."

"No," Jonah agreed. "You can't read owt into it."

She gathered the plate, paused at the door, and added, with the devastating casualness of her profession: "There's another letter come for you, by the way. Hand-delivered. No stamp, so it'll be someone local." She was gone before he could ask how she knew it mattered, though of course she'd known; Mrs. Pargeter could read a man's post like braille.

The envelope lay on his desk where she'd left it, cream-coloured, his name in that same careful hand — the hand from the two letters, the hand he now knew better than his own signature. He stood over it for a full minute, listening to the rain and to the distant sound of Anselm's humming drifting up from the garth, and he understood, with the particular clarity that comes at the end of a long day of finding things out, that the game had changed shape again. The letters had been an audience's ticket. This one, arriving the very day he finished his list, felt like something else entirely.

It felt like being watched back.

He opened it. Inside was a single line, no salutation, no signature:

*You've found all five. Now count backwards from the newest, and ask yourself why the policeman kept your secrets.*

Jonah read it three times. Then he looked at his third sheet of paper, at the pencil names in their neat descending order, and drew a small, steady circle around the fourth one — G. Fenwick, retired, cottage on the canal, twenty-three years of closed files — and beneath it, because he was an archivist before he was anything else, and archives are built on dates, he wrote the only question that mattered now:

*How much time does the fifth have?*

Outside, the rain kept falling on the herb beds, and somewhere below, gentle as ever, Brother Anselm went on humming.
