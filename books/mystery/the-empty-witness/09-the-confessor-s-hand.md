## 9. The Confessor's Hand

Jonah Fairweather had a rule about evidence: it lived in the archive, and the archive did not live in his flat. He had broken that rule twice now — once for Lassiter's stair-wear photographs, once for the letters — and both times he had slept badly, as though the documents were guests who kept wandering into his dreams and rearranging the furniture.

So on Monday morning, before the cathedral had properly woken, before Mrs. Petchey arrived to vacuum the nave and complain about the state of the candle wax, Jonah carried the two envelopes down into the crypt and laid them out on his work table under the good lamp.

The lamp was a gift from Marguerite, twenty-two years ago, when they were both young enough to believe that a lamp was a reasonable thing for a girl to give a boy she intended to keep. It still worked. He tried not to read anything into that.

"Right," he said aloud, to the envelopes, because talking to documents was one of the habits that had gotten him a reputation for oddness, and because the crypt echoed pleasantly and made him feel less like a man alone with a murderer's handwriting. "Let's find out who you are."

* * *

The first thing he noticed — had noticed already, on the train home Friday night, staring at them under the anemic light of the carriage — was that the two letters were written by the same hand but at different speeds.

The confession to Lassiter's death was quick. The pen had moved fast; you could see it in the way the ascenders leaned forward like people hurrying for a bus, in the ink pooling slightly where the nib had paused at the ends of words and then thought better of it. Whoever wrote *I killed Edward Lassiter* had written it the way you'd write a shopping list. That frightened Jonah more than any trembling hand would have. Trembling hands meant conscience. This was fluency.

The second letter — *the fifth will follow the fourth* — was slower. Deliberate. Almost ceremonial. Each letter stood upright, disciplined, like choristers waiting for the processional.

Same writer. Two registers. Which meant the writer could choose how they appeared on paper, and a person who could choose how they appeared was a person who had thought about appearing.

Jonah put on his white cotton gloves — habit, even though modern conservation wisdom said gloves did more harm than good, and even though these were hardly precious folios — and lifted the confession letter to the lamp.

The paper was laid stock, cream, with a faint watermark that only showed when the light came through at an angle. He tilted it. Tilted it again. Then he got up, went to the shelf where he kept the reference files, and pulled the folder marked **SUPPLIERS — STATIONERY**, which he maintained with the devotion other men reserved for football statistics.

Wexford & Sons, Bristol. Bond weight, 100 gsm, laid finish. The cathedral had ordered its own correspondence paper from Wexford & Sons for forty years. The chapter office used it. The dean's secretary used it. The sacristy used it. Jonah used it.

The confessor was writing on church stationery.

He sat back down rather slowly, the way you sit down when a chair has quietly become a stool.

"Well," he said to the envelopes. "That narrows it to roughly everyone I know."

* * *

The ink was next. He hadn't the equipment for proper chromatography — that was the sort of thing he'd have to explain to a forensic lab, along with why he had it, which was a conversation he was not yet ready to have — but thirty years of archiving teaches you things no laboratory bothers with. Jonah knew inks the way sommeliers knew vintages: by behavior, by temperament, by the little lies they told as they aged.

This ink was iron gall — old-fashioned, unfashionable, the ink of people who believed pens should have personalities. It had browned slightly at the densest strokes, that characteristic sepia bloom. Iron gall was beloved of traditionalists, liturgical scholars, and exactly the kind of person who kept a fountain pen in a breast pocket and considered ballpoints a symptom of civilizational decline.

It was also, Jonah noted, the ink stocked in the cathedral shop. Three pounds ninety a bottle. He had sold it himself, on the Saturday afternoons when Mrs. Okonkwo was at her granddaughter's.

He laughed — a short, unhappy sound that bounced off the stone and came back embarrassed.

The sanctuary itself was supplying the murderer. Paper from the chapter office, ink from the shop. It was almost domestic. It was almost *intimate*. Whoever this was didn't need to acquire materials; they were simply writing with what lay to hand, the way you'd write at your own desk. And that meant the letters had probably been written *here*, within these walls, perhaps within fifty yards of where he now sat.

Jonah looked around the crypt — at the tombs of bishops long past caring, at the shelves of diocesan records, at the little electric heater he wasn't supposed to have — and felt the building shift subtly beneath him, the way a house shifts when you learn something about a member of the household. St. Aldhelm's had been his refuge for thirty years. Refuges, he understood now, were also hiding places, and he had never asked himself who else was hidden here.

* * *

It was the calendar that cracked it open.

He'd been over the second letter a dozen times — *the fifth will follow the fourth* — looking for what the police would call a threat and what Jonah had begun to suspect was something else entirely. A schedule. Because the confessor, whoever they were, was precise. Precise people dated things.

There was no date. But there was a line he'd taken for flourish, tucked beneath the signature-less closing:

*Written in the season of the Dedication.*

Jonah had assumed it was piety, or theater. But precision people don't decorate. Precision people *encode*.

He went to the Ordo on the shelf — the liturgical calendar the cathedral followed, all feasts and ferias and commemorations — and found it. The Feast of the Dedication of St. Aldhelm's: the anniversary of the cathedral's consecration. A movable feast in principle, fixed in practice by tradition to the third Sunday of October.

Which was, according to the postmark he'd photographed with the mild paranoia that was becoming his default setting, six days away.

Six days until "the fifth."

But there was more, and it was Marguerite he had to thank for seeing it — or rather, it was the memory of Marguerite, aged nineteen, sitting cross-legged on the floor of this very crypt while he catalogued, saying, *You realize half your dates are just saints wearing costumes?* She had teased him about the Church's habit of layering new meanings onto old days, and he had defended it, and somewhere in the argument she had taught him the thing he now needed: that in a place like this, every date is a palimpsest.

Because the third Sunday of October was not only the Dedication. He checked the sanctoral cycle, ran his finger down the columns, and felt the hair rise on his forearms.

October, in the old calendar the school had kept — the cathedral school, the one with the fire — held the commemoration of a minor saint whose feast day fell on the fourteenth. A saint Jonah had never once heard preached upon. A saint whose name, in the martyrology, was listed among those who died in a fire.

Saint Anianus, he read. Bishop. *Burned with his clergy in their church, refusing to leave his books.*

Jonah set the Ordo down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The confessor wasn't threatening on a whim. They were keeping an anniversary. The fifth death was scheduled for the fourteenth of October — and the fourteenth of October meant something to someone who had been inside the cathedral school. Not just inside it. Formed by it. Someone who still thought in its calendar, prayed in its rhythm, dated their vengeance by its saints.

And then a second thought arrived, quieter and worse than the first: the school's calendar was not a public document. It hung in the sacristy. It was reproduced in the cathedral's own service booklets. You had to be *of* this place — staff, clergy, or a lifelong fixture — to think that way.

The circle was drawing inward. Not toward strangers. Toward the household.

* * *

He spent the afternoon doing what he was actually good at, which was making lists.

On a fresh sheet of his own (non-cathedral) paper, he built a profile of the hand itself, the way he'd build a catalogue entry: objective, exhaustive, honest.

A mature hand — the letterforms had the settled confidence of someone over fifty, no tremor, no uncertainty in the joins. Educated beyond the ordinary; the abbreviations were Latin, correctly formed (*vid.* in the confession, *scil.* in the promise), used without showing off, which meant Latin was furniture to this person, not ornament. A right-hander — the slant and the wear of the pen pressure said so. Someone accustomed to writing at length; the hand didn't fatigue across either page. A teacher's stamina, or a cleric's, or a judge's.

And one more thing, the thing he circled twice and sat looking at for a long time.

The capital letters. In both letters, the capitals were formed in a style Jonah had seen thousands of times and never once outside one particular world: the old Anglican chancery hand, the one taught in cathedral schools and theological colleges before the war and stubbornly preserved afterward by people who learned it from people who learned it from people. The *T* with the curved foot. The *H* with the high crossbar. It was a hand passed down like a recipe, teacher to pupil, and it survived now mostly in pulpits and chapter houses and the occasional elderly headmistress's report cards.

Whoever wrote the letters had learned to write *in a place like this*, from someone formed in this tradition.

Five survivors of the 1994 fire. A judge. A developer. A headmistress. A policeman. A monk.

Jonah looked at his list, and the list looked back, and he understood that he had just spent a whole day proving that the murderer was one of five people — except that the hand might belong to none of them, because there was a sixth possibility he had been circling for days without naming: that the letters were written by someone who wanted him to believe it was one of the five. That the hand itself might be a performance. That a person capable of two registers of handwriting might be capable of three.

"You are enjoying this," he told the letters. They declined to confirm it.

* * *

Mrs. Petchey's vacuum started up overhead, a distant asthmatic thunder through the vaulting, and Jonah realized with a start that it was past noon and he had eaten nothing and discovered everything. He made tea on the hotplate — the crypt's one concession to the twentieth century, let alone the twenty-first — and drank it standing at the table, looking at the two envelopes propped against the lamp base like suspects in a lineup.

Paper: cathedral stock. Ink: cathedral shop. Calendar: cathedral school. Hand: cathedral-trained.

Every thread led home. And that was the trap in it, he saw now — the elegant, horrible elegance of it. If he ever took this to the police, the first thing any competent investigator would observe was that all the materials pointed inside St. Aldhelm's, and the second thing they would observe was that the archivist who reported the letters had unreported access to all of them, and unreported knowledge of three deaths, and no alibi worth the name for any evening in the last month. The confessor had written the letters out of the cathedral's own body so that the cathedral's own body would be the suspect.

Perhaps especially so that *he* would be the suspect. The writer knew about his three solutions. Knew details. Knew him.

Unless —

Unless the point was never to frame him at all. Unless the point was the opposite: that only a man inside the church could follow a trail made entirely of church. The paper, the ink, the saints' days — it was a map drawn for someone who could read it, addressed to the one person the writer trusted to keep reading.

*You know our language,* the letters said, in everything but words. *You are the only one left who does.*

"That's not trust," Jonah said to the empty crypt. "That's a hostage situation with extra steps."

The crypt, being a crypt, agreed with him in silence.

* * *

He worked until the light through the slit window went amber and the tomb of Bishop Crewe began throwing its long afternoon shadow across the floor, the shadow it had thrown every afternoon for four hundred years and would throw long after everyone currently worrying about murders was comfortably dead. There was comfort in that. Jonah took it greedily.

Then he did the last thing on his list, the thing he'd been saving, the way you save the last piece of a puzzle because once it's placed the picture is finished and finished pictures are obligations.

He compared the hand against samples.

Not against the five — he had no samples of the five, not yet, though he was already composing plausible archival reasons to request Judge Halloway's old correspondence. No. Against the samples he *had*: sixty years of chapter minutes, deans' letters, verger reports, school records. The great slow river of institutional handwriting that flowed through his shelves.

Most of it was nothing. Typewriters, then word processors, then the sad uniformity of email. But the older volumes — and Jonah loved the older volumes the way some men love horses — held hands. Real hands. Generations of them.

He worked backward through the decades, page by page, and somewhere in the middle of the afternoon he stopped taking notes and simply stared.

Because he had found the source. Or the near-source. In the school logbooks of the late 1980s — the cathedral school, the fire years — the boys' exercise standards were recorded in a beautiful, severe, chancery-trained hand, every *T* footed and curved, every *H* crossbarred high. The hand of the man who taught them to write.

Father Ambrose.

The letters' capitals were not identical to Ambrose's — twenty years and a different soul stood between them — but they were descended from it. Child of that hand, or student of it. The confessor had learned to write from Father Ambrose, or from someone Ambrose had taught.

Five survivors. All of them Ambrose's pupils. All of them taught the same hand.

And one of them, Jonah thought, laying the logbook down with hands that were not quite steady, had kept the hand alive for thirty years, kept it warm, kept it ready — and had finally picked up the pen.

He looked at the clock. Half past four. Across the cloister garth, visible through the crypt's high slit of window if you knew where to look, the light was going gold on the east walk, where Brother Anselm could be found most evenings at this hour, sweeping leaves off stones with the unhurried tenderness of a man who had nowhere else in the world to be.

The gentlest of the five. The one who fed the birds. The one whose Latin was flawless and whose handwriting — Jonah had catalogued the refectory notices for years, it was part of the job, he had *catalogued everything*, that was the whole trouble with him — whose handwriting carried, unmistakably, the curved-footed *T* and the high-crossbarred *H*.

"Oh," Jonah said quietly, to the tombs, to the tea, to thirty years of careful silence. "Oh, I do hope I'm wrong."

He put on his coat, turned off the lamp Marguerite had given him, and went up the stairs toward the cloister, telling himself all the way that he was merely going to ask Brother Anselm about the school calendar, purely as a matter of archival routine, and noticing as he said it that lying to himself had begun to feel exactly like lying to the police.
