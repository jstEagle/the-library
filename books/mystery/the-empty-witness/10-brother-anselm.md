## 10. Brother Anselm

The cloister at St. Aldhelm's was built for men who wanted to be found slowly.

Jonah had always liked that about it — the way the covered walkway turned a simple crossing of the garth into a small pilgrimage, four sides of cold stone between you and wherever you were going, each one giving you time to rehearse what you'd say and then to abandon it. He crossed the east walk now with his hands in his coat pockets and his heart doing something unarchival, which was to say, improvising.

Brother Anselm was in the herb garden, kneeling in the wet grass with a trowel and an expression of profound contentment, thinning the row of thyme like a man defusing a bomb he hoped would go off.

"Brother Anselm," Jonah said.

The monk looked up. He was seventy-something now, Jonah guessed, though he wore age the way he wore his habit — loosely, without much conviction. His face was all soft lines and pale blue eyes, the kind of face that made strangers confess things on trains. Jonah had seen him do it. A tourist once told him her entire marital history in the space of a single candle purchase.

"Mr. Fairweather." Anselm beamed. "Come to arrest me for the rosemary? I know it's gone woody. I've been meaning to have words with it."

"I've been meaning to have words with you, actually."

"Ah." The monk set down his trowel with great care, as if it might be needed as evidence later. "Then you'd better sit down. Gardening conversations are terrible for the knees and worse for the soul."

There was a stone bench along the cloister wall, worn into two shallow hollows by centuries of other people's difficult conversations. Jonah sat on the edge of one. Anselm lowered himself onto the other with the creaking deliberation of old wood, folded his hands, and waited. It was, Jonah thought, the patience of a man who had been waiting thirty years and could certainly manage another five minutes.

"You were at the cathedral school," Jonah began. "In 1994."

"Yes."

"You were in the dormitory wing. The night of the fire."

"Yes."

"One boy died. Daniel Pryce."

Something moved behind the pale eyes — not flinching, exactly. More like a door being closed gently by someone standing behind it. "Yes," Anselm said again. "Daniel."

"The official record says electrical fault. Faulty wiring in the north wall, exacerbated by — " Jonah consulted his memory, though he didn't need to; he had read the file so many times the pages had worn smooth in his mind — "exacerbated by delayed response due to a malfunctioning alarm system. The caretaker, Marguerite Okafor's father — Elias Okafor — was held responsible for maintenance lapses. He died in the fire trying to reach the boys' corridor."

"He did die trying to reach us," Anselm said quietly. "That part is true. Whatever else is true or isn't, that part is true."

"And you and four others survived. You, Thomas Halloway, Marcus Vane, Edith Carrow, and Gerald Mowbray. And every one of you gave Father Ambrose the same account. Word for word. Same sequence of events. Same times. Same details down to which stairwell you used."

Anselm looked out over the garth, where the rain had stopped and the wet grass was steaming faintly in a thread of sun. "We rehearsed," he said. "That's not a confession of anything except that we were eleven years old and terrified, and Ambrose sat us in a row in the chapel vestry and made us say it over and over until we couldn't tell anymore what had actually happened and what we'd been taught to say. Is that a crime, Mr. Fairweather? Grief with rehearsal?"

"It is when the grief is load-bearing," Jonah said. "It's been holding up a wall for thirty years. And lately people who lean on that wall have started falling off it."

The monk turned to look at him then, fully, and Jonah felt the strange sensation of being examined by someone whose profession was gentleness. "Edward Lassiter," Anselm said.

"You knew him."

"Forty years in this building. He polished the brass on the west door so faithfully I sometimes thought he was praying to it." A pause. "They're saying he slipped."

"They're saying a great many things," Jonah said. "I've received two letters, Brother. One confessing to Edward Lassiter's death. One promising a fifth will follow the fourth. They were written by someone who knows about the fire, knows about the alibis, and knows about me — about the three deaths I've looked into privately and never reported. Which means whoever wrote them has either watched me very closely for a very long time, or was told about me by someone who did."

"And you thought: the monk," Anselm said. Not bitterly. Almost with approval. "The quiet one. The one still inside the walls. The obvious suspect."

"You're one of five survivors. Four of the letters' likely subjects are accounted for in ways I can verify — Halloway was on the bench in open court the week Lassiter died; Vane was photographed cutting a ribbon in Bristol; Miss Carrow hasn't left her school since September; Mowbray—" Jonah hesitated. "Mowbray I'm less sure of. But you live forty yards from where the letters were posted. You had access. And you are the only one of the five who never left."

"No," Anselm agreed. "I never left. That's either the most suspicious thing about me or the least, depending on how you look at it. A guilty man runs, Mr. Fairweather. Or so the detective novels say. I confess my knowledge of the genre comes mostly from the ones left in the guesthouse."

Jonah almost smiled despite himself. This was the trouble with Brother Anselm, he was beginning to understand. He was impossible to interrogate because he kept agreeing with you, warmly, helpfully, like a man helping you carry your own furniture up stairs.

"Tell me about the fire," Jonah said. "Not the rehearsed version. The one from before the vestry."

For a long moment the monk said nothing. A blackbird landed on the garth wall, considered the thyme bed, and decided against it.

"There isn't one," Anselm said finally. "That's the truth, and I know how it sounds. There's the version I remember, which is smoke and shouting and Daniel's empty bed, and there's the version Ambrose built, which is tidy and identical five times over, and somewhere between them the actual night went missing. Thirty years ago, Mr. Fairweather. I was eleven. Memory at eleven is a child carrying water — most of it arrives spilled."

"But something spilled *out*," Jonah pressed. "Something reached the ground. Because someone is killing their way through the people who were in that dormitory, and they're not doing it for nothing."

"They might be doing it for guilt," Anselm said. "Guilt keeps poorly. It ferments. Thirty years in the dark and it can come out of the bottle unrecognizable even to the person who bottled it." He rubbed his hands together, and Jonah noticed they trembled slightly — age, or something else. "You want me to say *yes, I killed Edward* or *no, I didn't*, and I understand why, because a yes or a no is a door you can close. But I won't give you one. Not because I'm protecting myself."

"Then who?"

The monk smiled, and it was the saddest smile Jonah had ever seen on a face built for joy. "Everyone. All of us. We were children, and Ambrose told us what to say, and we said it, and after enough years of saying it, every one of us became partly responsible for the shape of the lie. If one of us is killing now — and I'm not saying one of us is — then they're not killing to hide what they did. They're killing to hide what was done *to* them. Do you see the difference? No?" He shook his head gently. "Neither do I, entirely. But I feel it. Like a stone in the shoe of the soul."

Jonah sat back. The bench was cold through his coat. He had come here expecting either denial or collapse, and instead he'd gotten — theology. Pity tangled up with suspicion until he couldn't pull the threads apart, and he suspected that was precisely the point, whether Anselm intended it or not. A man who neither confirmed nor denied was more unnerving than either.

"Father Ambrose," Jonah said. "He died ten years ago."

"In 2014. In his sleep, in the presbytery at Wellow. A good death. He'd earned a difficult one, if half of what I believe about him is true."

"What do you believe about him?"

Anselm was quiet for a while. When he spoke, his voice had changed — lower, more careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice he'd avoided for decades.

"Ambrose loved us," he said. "That's the thing no one wants to hear, because it complicates everything. He wasn't a monster. He was kind in a hundred small ways — he remembered our mothers' names, he snuck us sweets during Lent and called it 'liturgical flexibility,' he sat with Daniel's mother for six hours the day after the fire and let her hit him, twice, and never raised a hand to stop her. And he also sat five frightened boys in a vestry and built them a story the way a carpenter builds a coffin. Fitted. Sealed. Made to last." He looked at Jonah. "Both of those men were real. I've spent thirty years trying to make them add up, and they don't. That's my answer, Mr. Fairweather. That's everything I have."

"It's not everything," Jonah said softly. "Because you just told me you *believe* things about him. Half of what you believe. Belief implies evidence. Monks don't traffic in impressions, Brother — you of all people know that. What do you know?"

The blackbird returned to the wall. Somewhere across the garth, the cathedral clock began striking eleven, each note falling into the wet air like a coin into a well.

When the last stroke faded, Anselm said: "Ambrose wrote things down. He was like you that way — he trusted paper more than people. And near the end, when he was ill, he became frightened of what he'd written. He burned most of it himself, in the presbytery garden, in a biscuit tin. I helped him carry it out." A pause. "But Ambrose was a pack rat under the piety, and pack rats always keep one box. He told me once — the last time I saw him alive — that the truth of 1994 was 'filed where only a fool or an archivist would ever think to look.'"

Jonah's breath went out of him slowly.

"He said that? Those words?"

"*Fool or archivist.*" Anselm nodded. "I thought it was one of his jokes. Ambrose's jokes were mostly riddles he refused to solve. But you came asking questions, Mr. Fairweather, and you came asking them in the order an archivist would ask them, and I've been watching you circle the crypt for three weeks like a moth that's found the wrong lamp. So I'll give you this, freely, because I'm tired, and because if one of my brothers or sisters is dying for that night, then the lie has already cost more than the truth ever will."

He leaned close, and up close his eyes weren't soft at all; they were old and clear and terribly awake.

"The sealed collection in your crypt. The anonymous donation, ten years ago, catalogued under the name 'A. Penitent.' Ambrose died in 2014. Your anonymous donor signed the deposit book in March of 2015. Ask yourself, Mr. Fairweather — who had access to Ambrose's papers after he died? Who had a key to the presbytery? Who was trusted enough that no one ever thought to ask?" He straightened, wincing, one hand on the small of his back. "I'm not telling you it was me. I'm telling you that you should stop asking monks and start asking who loved Ambrose enough to keep his secrets — and who hated him enough to want them read."

"That's not an answer, Brother."

"No," Anselm agreed pleasantly, gathering up his trowel. "It's a better thing than an answer. It's a shelf mark."

He rose, bowed — actually bowed, the old courtly gesture — and shuffled off along the east walk toward the chapel, leaving Jonah alone on the bench with the smell of thyme and rain and the sound of his own pulse.

*A. Penitent.* He had catalogued that donation himself, three years into his tenure, one of hundreds, a crate of mildewed theological notebooks he'd skimmed and shelved and never opened properly because the finding aid was a single index card in handwriting he hadn't recognized. He hadn't recognized it because he hadn't known whose it was.

He recognized it now. He'd spent the last month staring at those same slanted, self-satisfied capitals in two envelopes on his desk.

The letters and the papers were in the same hand.

Which meant the confessor had written Ambrose's archive. Which meant the confessor had been close enough to a dying man to inherit his secrets — and had sat on them for ten years before starting to kill.

Or — Jonah stood, his knees protesting, his mind moving faster than his body wanted to go —

Or the hand wasn't the confessor's at all. Or the papers weren't what the finding aid promised. Or any of a dozen things, because the box marked *A. Penitent* had been sitting twenty feet from his desk for a decade, patient as a tomb, waiting for a fool or an archivist.

Jonah walked back toward the crypt as fast as dignity allowed, and behind him, unnoticed, Brother Anselm paused at the cloister door and watched him go — and knelt, briefly, in the doorway, as if praying.

Or as if closing a gate behind him.

* * *

The crypt was cold and smelled of stone and candle wax, and Jonah took the stairs two at a time anyway, which at his age was less exercise than prayer.

Shelf 14, bay C, third row. The grey archival box sat where he had put it ten years ago, its label curling at the edges: **A. PENITENT — THEOLOGICAL NOTEBOOKS, SERMON DRAFTS, CORRESPONDENCE. 1978–2013.**

He set it on his desk beside the two letters. He laid the first envelope flat. He compared the capitals — the *P*, the *T*, the self-important little flourish on the final *t* — and felt the floor of the last thirty years tilt gently beneath him.

The handwriting matched.

And pinned inside the box lid, where he had somehow never looked in ten years of dusting around it, was a second index card, older than the first, in a different hand — smaller, hurried, pressed hard enough into the card to emboss the paper:

**If these are ever read, read them in order. And God forgive the one who filed them.**

Jonah reached for the string tie with hands that were not quite steady, and began, at last, to open the box.
