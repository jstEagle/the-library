## 2. The Girl Who Wasn't Missed

The restricted stacks of Ely Cathedral lived beneath the north transept, down a spiral stair that smelled of cold stone and older paper, and August Whitlock had always maintained that it was the best-smelling room in Cambridgeshire. Tom Beddoe said it smelled like a tomb that had given up on fresh air, but Tom had no romance in him whatsoever, which was remarkable for a man who spent his days polishing brass.

She let herself in with the heavy iron key, hung her coat on the hook that had hung coats since Victoria, and stood a moment in the quiet, letting the building settle around her. Above her head, eight hundred feet of Norman masonry went about its business of being eternal. Down here, the business was more modest: boxes, ledgers, the sediment of parishes.

The bequest boxes from the Hargreaves estate sat where she'd left them yesterday, and beside them, in a folder of her own, the two letters. She hadn't slept especially well. Letters like that have a way of following one upstairs, into the lamplight, into the small hours. *I drowned her in the drain at Roswell Drove and God has kept the receipt.* And then, in the same hand, the same ink, the same terrible evenness: *The second will follow.*

She had catalogued the 1994 file herself. Eleven years ago, when she was new to the post and eager, she'd processed the transfer from the police archive — a courtesy deposit, the diocese wanting its records of the parish in order after the drowning inquest. She remembered the file because of the handwriting, which was beautiful. A copperplate so disciplined it looked printed. She'd noted it in her catalogue with the small vanity of a young archivist: *correspondence in a fine, regular hand.*

And now that hand had written to her, twice, confessing to a murder and promising another.

"Right," she said aloud, to the shelves, because the shelves were good listeners. "Let's have a look at you, then."

* * *

The 1994 file came up from the deepest run of shelving on a trolley whose left wheel had a limp it had carried since the 1970s. August set it on the reading table under the lamp, put on her white cotton gloves, and untied the legal tape with the care other people reserved for newborns.

Inside: the inquest correspondence, the parish burial register extracts, the police request for records, and — the thing she'd come for — the original incident documentation, deposited by the investigating officer with a covering letter she now read three times.

*Dear Sir, in connection with the above matter, I enclose copies of the relevant parish registers as verified against the civil record. The deceased has been identified as MARGARET ELLEN COOPER of Little Downham, aged 17. All records are consistent. Yours faithfully, E. Holloway, DI.*

*All records are consistent.* She said it aloud, tasting it. It had the ring of a man who wanted the sentence to be true so badly he'd made it true.

She turned to the register extract. *Margaret Ellen Cooper, of Little Downham, drowned 14 April 1994, recovered from the Roswell Drove drainage cut. Interred St. Wendreda's, March, 21 April 1994.*

And then, because she was an archivist and couldn't help herself, she did the thing she did with every name that crossed her desk: she cross-checked. The diocesan index of confirmations, the parish rolls, the marriage registers. It was a habit, a small devotional act — every name a life, every life owed its record.

Margaret Ellen Cooper of Little Downham, aged seventeen in 1994.

Margaret Ellen Cooper of Little Downham had married a farm mechanic in 1998. August found the banns. Found the marriage register, the entry in a wobbling, joyful hand. Found, three entries down the same page, the baptism of a child in 2001. Margaret Ellen Cooper, née Cooper — no, wait. She went back. Read it again. The bride's father was listed: *Arthur Cooper, farmer.* The drowned girl's father, in the inquest papers, was *Raymond Cooper, drainage board labourer.*

Different fathers. Same name, same town, same age.

August sat very still, in the way she did when her heart began to run ahead of her hands. There were, she supposed, coincidences. People shared names. But not — she pulled the electoral roll extracts, the diocesan census returns — not like this. The Margaret Cooper who married in 1998 was alive. Had been alive every year since, a solid, unremarkable, gloriously ordinary life unfolding down the page: addresses in Norfolk, a second child, a golden wedding anniversary notice in the *Eastern Daily Press* that August found in the newspaper deposit, dated only two years ago, with a photograph of a broad, laughing woman of about forty-seven holding a bouquet.

The drowned girl had been buried under a living woman's name.

Which meant the girl in the grave — the girl in the drain — was someone else. Someone whose real name appeared nowhere in this file at all. Someone who had been drowned, buried, mourned by no record, and closed away by a fine, regular hand that wrote *all records are consistent.*

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere above, the cathedral bells began the quarter-hour, and the vibration came down through the stone into August's teeth.

* * *

She should have stopped there. She knew that even then, some sensible August standing at her own shoulder saying, *this is a matter for the police, telephone them, telephone them now.* But there was a fragment, a loose thread, and she had never in her life left a loose thread.

The burial register for St. Wendreda's, March, April 1994. The original, not the extract. She had it on deposit from a closures review in 2009. She fetched it, and there was the entry — *Margaret Ellen Cooper* — in the vicar's tidy hand, and beneath it the officiating minister's signature, and beneath that, in the column for *undertaker,* a name she didn't recognize: *J. Beddoe & Son, Ely.*

Tom. Tom's father's firm. She filed that away in the small locked drawer of things she wasn't ready to think about.

But it was the margin she cared about. Because the vicar of St. Wendreda's in 1994 had been a conscientious man, and conscientious vicars make marginal notes. Beside the Cooper entry, in faded pencil, almost erased by a thumb's rubbing across thirty years:

*Body unclaimed by family. Young woman known locally only as "Lark." No Cooper family in attendance. Interred at parish expense.*

*Lark.*

August closed her eyes, and the archive fell away, and she was — this made no sense, she was nine years old, she had never been to the Fens in her life, and yet the memory arrived with the authority of something genuinely remembered, the way old stories do when they've been told to you by someone who loved them —

— a summer evening, water lying flat and bright as hammered pewter between the reed beds, and a girl walking the towpath ahead of her, barefoot, carrying her shoes by their laces. The girl was laughing at something, turning to say something over her shoulder, and her hair was coming loose from its plait, and she had a smudge of mud on one cheekbone that she wore like a medal. *They call me Lark,* she was saying, *because I'm up before the whole blessed world singing, and my mum says it's the only true thing about me.* And the light was going long and gold on the water, and the girl's voice was the happiest sound —

August opened her eyes. Her heart was going like a bird in a box. She had never met that girl. She was certain she had never met that girl. And she could still hear her.

She wrote in her notebook, in her own hand, which was not beautiful but was honest: *Victim buried as M.E. Cooper. Real name unknown. Known as "Lark." Register altered or never corrected. Who benefits from a dead girl with no name?*

And beneath it, because her hand seemed to know before she did: *Who wrote "all records are consistent"?*

* * *

The dread arrived an hour later, quietly, the way weather comes off the Wash.

She had gone back up into the body of the cathedral for air and light, and stood in the crossing while tourists drifted and a verger's hoover whined somewhere in the side aisles. And she thought: the letters. The confession named Roswell Drove. The threat promised *a second.* And now she knew there was a first death that had been misfiled, misnamed, misburied — a girl erased so thoroughly that the only trace of her true self was a pencil ghost in a margin and a name like a birdsong.

The letters hadn't come to her by accident. Both in that same fine hand — the hand of the file, the hand of the covering letter, the hand of DI E. Holloway, who had verified *all records* and found them *consistent.* Someone wanted her at this file. Someone had used her catalogue, her archive, her address, like a map with a great red arrow drawn on it.

Or — and this was the thought that made her cold in a warm building — someone wanted her to authenticate something. Archives authenticated things. It was, professionally speaking, what she was *for.* And you did not bait an archivist with two letters unless you needed an archivist's word on paper.

The hoover stopped. Footsteps. Tom appeared at the crossing with his dust cloth over his shoulder and his face full of the particular worry he wore when he'd been watching her work too long.

"You've been down in the stacks four hours," he said. "I brought tea. It's stewed. It's always stewed, you never drink it while it's drinkable." He set the mug on the parapet and peered at her. "August. What have you found?"

She looked at her oldest friend — Tom, whose father's firm had buried the nameless girl at parish expense, thirty years ago this April — and heard herself say, lightly, lying for no reason she could yet name:

"Nothing. A clerical muddle. A woman buried under the wrong name, that's all. I'll write to the diocese."

Tom went very still. Not shocked-still. *Careful*-still. The stillness of a man deciding, in half a second, what not to say.

"Well," he said, and picked up his dust cloth. "Those happen. Muddles." He smiled, and it was Tom's smile, warm and crooked and known to her since she was twenty-two. "You'll leave it alone, then."

"Of course," said August, who had absolutely no intention of leaving it alone, and who watched something flicker behind her friend's eyes that she had never seen there before, and could not yet name.

The bells rang the hour. Down in the restricted stacks, under the lamp, the file lay open to the covering letter, and the fine regular hand, and the words that were a lie thirty years old and still, somehow, waiting.
