## 6. Drowned Twice

They let August see him at half past nine in the morning, which was a cruelty dressed up as procedure. The crypt had been pumped out overnight, but the stone still wept, and the portable lamps the police had rigged threw long amber shadows that made the twelfth-century pillars look like they were leaning in to listen.

Tom Beddoe lay where the water had left him — or where someone had arranged for the water to find him. His verger's cassock was buttoned to the throat. His hands were folded on his chest. Someone had combed his hair.

August stood at the foot of the bier-like slab and did not cry, because crying would have been a kind of agreement, and she was not agreeing to anything this morning.

"Drowned in perhaps eight inches of water," said DS Nkem Okafor, who had drawn the short straw of managing her. She liked Okafor; he was patient with archivists in a way most officers were not. "There's a contusion at the back of the skull, consistent with a fall against the step there. Toxicology will take days, but the paramedic noted constricted pupils. We think he may have taken something before he came down."

"Tom didn't take things," August said. "Tom took paracetamol and an occasional sherry at Christmas."

"Miss Whitlock—"

"He was seventy-one years old and he could still carry a full tray of service books up forty steps without stopping to breathe. He rang the bells until last year. He was afraid of deep water — *afraid*, Detective Sergeant, since he was a boy — and you're asking me to believe he walked down into a flooded crypt of his own accord and lay down in it like a man getting into bed?"

Okafor looked at his notebook rather than at her. "The note," he said gently, "is in his handwriting. We've shown it to three members of staff."

August had seen it already, photographed on Okafor's phone while she'd been waiting in the north transept with tea she hadn't touched. *I did wrong in 1994 and I can't carry it any longer. Forgive me.* Eleven words. Tom's careful, slightly old-fashioned script, the letters upright as choirboys.

"It's his hand," she admitted. "Or it's a very good copy of it. But Detective Sergeant — Tom Beddoe wrote shopping lists in that hand. He wrote the flower rota in that hand. He would not have written his own death in it, because Tom believed — he told me this not six weeks ago — that suicide was the one sin the Fens never forgave. He said the water here keeps what it's given and asks for more."

Okafor closed his notebook. "Then I'm sorry, but you'll want to hear what we've found regardless." He led her along the crypt's eastern wall, lamplight sliding over wet stone, and stopped at the recessed niche where the parish registers were kept in their fireproof cabinet.

The cabinet door hung open. Inside, the shelf was bare except for a single item, laid flat and squared to the edges with a precision that made August's stomach turn: the 1994 burial ledger, open to the page recording the interment of *Margaret Ellen Croft* — the name that belonged to a living woman in Norfolk — with a fresh annotation beneath it in blue-black ink:

*Removed to Ely Cathedral crypt, 12 October 1994. R. Beddoe, verger.*

"His signature?" Okafor asked.

August bent close without touching. The letters were right. The loops were right. Even the little hesitation-mark where Tom's pen always caught on the double-*d* was there.

"It's a forgery," she said, "done by someone who has studied his signature very carefully indeed. And Detective Sergeant — that date is wrong. Tom became verger in 1996. In October 1994 he was a bell-ringer with no keys to anything. Whoever wrote this knew Tom's hand but not Tom's history."

Okafor wrote that down. She watched him write it, and understood that he was writing it the way one writes down the ramblings of a grieving friend — kindly, completely, and without consequence.

"The water came up early," he said instead. "Spring tide, plus the sluice failure upstream. The Environment Agency is calling it a one-in-ten-years event."

"I know. I read the tide tables." She had read them obsessively for two days, ever since the second letter. The flood had come eleven hours ahead of the published prediction. She had not yet decided what that meant, only that it meant something, and that everyone else was content to call it weather.

* * *

They released the crypt at noon, and August went back in alone, because grief in August Whitlock had always behaved less like sorrow and more like a filing system under strain.

She stood where Tom had lain and made herself look at the scene as a document. That was the trick of her trade, after all: a crime scene was only a text written in bodies and mud instead of ink, and she had spent thirty years learning to read difficult texts.

The cassock buttoned to the throat. The hands folded. The hair combed. The body positioned on the low slab nearest the eastern wall — not the nearest exit, not the deepest water, but the slab that matched, exactly, the position described in the 1994 post-mortem report she had read two nights ago with her heart hammering: *recovered supine upon a stone table, hands composed, fully clothed.*

The drowned girl had been found like that. Laid out like that. By whoever put her in the water.

And now Tom.

"Oh," August said aloud, and her voice fell strangely in the wet dark. "Oh, you vain, meticulous monster."

It wasn't a drowning staged to look like guilt. It was a *reenactment*. Page by page, entry by entry, the killer was transcribing the 1994 file into the world — the confession letter first (the opening statement), then the promise of a second death (the charge), then the altered register (the false record), and now the body, posed to match the original scene down to the fold of the hands. He wasn't killing Tom. He was *completing the paperwork*. And when the file was finished — when every page had its real-world counterpart —

He would need a final page. A closing signature. An authentication.

Her blood went cold and then, oddly, hot. Because there was only one person in Ely whose professional function was to authenticate documents. Only one person whose word could make a forgery into fact, a fiction into record. He had needed her archive from the beginning. The letters hadn't been confessions. They'd been *requests*.

* * *

She found Okafor in the cloister garth, eating a sandwich with the grim efficiency of a man who had learned to eat whenever the opportunity presented itself.

"Detective Sergeant. Two questions."

He sighed, but he turned to face her, and she gave him credit for that.

"One. Has anyone checked whether Tom's keys are all accounted for? The crypt key specifically. There are four cut. Tom's ring, the sacristy board, the Dean's safe copy, and one held by the fabric advisory committee."

"We're doing an inventory now."

"Do it fast. And two—" She hesitated, then plunged, because lying to Holloway had taught her that lies were easier the second time, and she disliked how easy. "In 1994, who was the officer who recovered the girl's body? Who described the scene? Because whoever killed Tom knew that description intimately. Not from gossip. From the file itself."

Okafor chewed, swallowed. "That case was handled by Cambridgeshire Constabulary. The lead investigator..." He flipped back through his notebook. "DI Edmund Holloway. Retired now, I believe. Lives in Ely, as it happens."

"As it happens," August repeated softly.

"You know him?"

"Everyone in Ely knows Edmund. He gives talks to the Friends of the Cathedral. Charming man. Very precise." She smiled, and was distantly surprised to feel it working properly on her face. "He visited me on Tuesday, as it happens. Asking after some letters."

Something shifted in Okafor's expression — the small tectonic movement of a policeman recalculating. "What letters?"

"That's the third question, and I'll answer it at the station, tonight, in writing, with everything else I have. But Detective Sergeant—" she stepped closer, and lowered her voice though the garth was empty, "—when your inventory turns up that fourth key, check who chairs the fabric advisory committee. Check who has signed out restricted files from the county records office in the last year. And check whether Edmund Holloway's charming, precise handwriting appears anywhere it shouldn't."

"Why Holloway?"

"Because Tom once told me that in thirty years of ringing bells together, he'd only ever seen one man stand in the tower doorway and never once offer to pull a rope. He said some men love churches the way other men love alibis." Her voice cracked on the last word, finally, treacherously, and she pressed on through it. "And because yesterday I found a page razored out of the crypt ledger — the record of a 1994 removal that never happened — and the razor-cut was clean, deliberate, done by someone calm. Tom couldn't have done it. Tom apologized to books when he dropped them."

Okafor was writing again, faster now, and this time it did not feel like charity.

"Miss Whitlock," he said, "stay available today. Don't go anywhere alone. And don't—" he glanced toward the dark mouth of the crypt stair, "—don't go down there again by yourself."

"Of course not," said August Whitlock, who had already worked out that the tide would turn again Thursday night, and that the diocesan audit was Friday morning, and that if Edmund Holloway was reenacting a file, the final page required her living hand — and she intended to be holding the pen when he reached for it.

She walked home through the rain-washed Close, past the house where the lights of Holloway's study burned steady and yellow behind lace curtains, and she did not look away.

Let him watch her pass. Let him count her steps. The file was nearly complete, and she knew now what the last entry would be — and she had two days to decide whether to be the victim of the record, or its author.
