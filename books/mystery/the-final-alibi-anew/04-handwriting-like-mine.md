## 4. Handwriting Like Mine

I did not sleep. This is not a confession of passion or insomnia-as-personality; it is a professional observation. I lay in bed at 1:14 a.m., at 2:40 a.m., at 4:03 a.m.—I know the times because I checked, because checking is what I do—and every time I closed my eyes, the diary opened itself behind my eyelids to that impossible final entry.

*Eleven days after she vanished.* Dated October 19, 1994. Marisol Reyes, missing presumed drowned, writing about the smell of rain on hot stone and a man who "counts things when he lies."

I got up at five and made coffee I didn't drink, and by six I had the diary out of its evidence sleeve again—my own copy, forty-one photographs of it on my phone, but there is a difference between a photograph and a thing. Photographs are testimony. The thing itself is a witness. And witnesses, in my experience, lie with their bodies even when their words hold.

The handwriting was Marisol's. That much was certain. I'd spent two hours the night before comparing the 1994 entries against the samples in the insurance file—the employment application from the Aldermoor estate, the signed receipt for her final paycheck—and they matched the way a key matches a lock it was cut for. Same forward slant, same narrow *m*'s like bird tracks, same habit of crossing her *t*'s with a single impatient stroke that sometimes clipped the neighboring letter. People can forge a signature. Almost no one can sustain a hand across sixty pages without the seams showing.

So either Marisol Reyes wrote every word in that book—

—or someone had thirty years, unlimited patience, and a reason to learn her hand the way a musician learns a piece: not memorized, but inhabited.

I sat at my kitchen table with the diary open under the lamp and did what I do, which is notice things nobody asked me to notice.

* * *

The early entries were a young woman's entries. Marisol came to Aldermoor in March of 1994 as a housekeeper, twenty-three years old, and her diary reads like what it was: a private ledger kept by someone learning the geography of a new world. She catalogued the household the way I catalogue crime scenes. *Mrs. Aldermoor takes her tea at four and pretends she isn't watching the clock.* *The judge visits Tuesdays. He laughs before his own jokes finish.* *Theo Brandt asked me to dance at the benefit and held my waist one song too long.*

She noticed everything and understood almost none of it, which is the condition of being twenty-three and employed by people whose money does their thinking for them. Her observations were sharp but innocent. She saw the pieces on the board and assumed they were decorative.

Then September 1994, and the entries changed temperature.

*Something is wrong in this house and everyone is smiling too hard about it.* Then: *The study was locked all day yesterday. Daniel says Mr. Aldermoor hasn't come out since Tuesday.* Then, three days before the fire: *I heard shouting through the floor. Four voices. I counted. Four.*

Four voices. Four survivors. I set my pen down and looked at that sentence for a long time, because Marisol Reyes had written, eleven days before she disappeared, the exact arithmetic that would take me three chapters of other people's lies to reconstruct.

And then—the fire. September 17, 1994. The estate burned; Elias Aldermoor and his housekeeper-cook Rosa Vidal died inside; four people walked out: Judge Corliss Hale, family friend and frequent guest; Theo Brandt, business partner; Elena Marsh, née Aldermoor, nineteen years old; and the groundskeeper, whose name I'd have to pull from the file again. Marisol Reyes was listed among the unaccounted-for until her car turned up at a ferry terminal in October, and then she became something simpler: gone. A life-insurance policy on her—taken out by the estate, oddly, as part of a staff benefits package Brandt had championed—lapsed into paperwork purgatory, which is how a dead woman's diary ended up on my desk thirty years later, delivered by a lawyer who smelled of expensive cologne and cheaper motives.

The final entry. October 19, 1994. Eleven days after the fire, eight days after her car was found.

*I am not going to tell you where I am going because paper can be read by anyone and I have learned this month what these people do to loose ends. If anyone ever finds this: the fire was not an accident. I know because I saw who left the study before the flames. I will send proof when it is safe. The man counts things when he lies—he counted the silverware, he counted the steps, he counted the money out loud to himself in the dark—and if you meet him, count along.*

That was where the book was supposed to end. A dead woman's last testament, dramatic and useless, the kind of thing that makes lawyers rich and investigators tired.

Except.

Except that when I read it again this morning, coffee going cold beside me, I noticed something I had filed away overnight without examining, the way my mind files everything whether I consent to it or not. There were more pages. Not many. Seven, tucked between the back cover and the final entry, unnumbered, undated in any conventional sense—but written in the same hand, in the same ink, and I had read them last night as an appendix, a curiosity.

This morning they were not a curiosity. This morning they were a message, and the message was addressed to me.

* * *

The first of the seven pages read like a continuation. Marisol's voice, Marisol's slant, describing a bus station and a woman who sold her a sandwich and didn't ask questions. Fine. Plausible. A fugitive's fragment.

The second page described the view from a motel window: *a parking lot shaped like a horseshoe, and every morning the same blue truck parks nose-in by the ice machine, and the man who drives it wears his cap even indoors, and I think he is watching me, or I am becoming the kind of person who thinks everyone is watching her, which might be worse.*

Also plausible. Also sad in the specific way of the permanently displaced.

The third page is where the floor began to tilt.

*A detective came asking questions today. He had a way of looking at rooms like he was taking them apart and putting them back in order, and he remembered everything I told him, I could see him filing it somewhere behind his eyes. He said memory is the only honest witness. I liked him. I hope he never has to look for me.*

I read that sentence four times. *Memory is the only honest witness.*

It's a good line. It's a true line. It is also, verbatim, a line I have said—out loud, in depositions, in interviews, twice on the record—in every one of the three cases I worked before this one. I say it the way some people say grace. My last supervisor used to imitate me doing it, badly, at office parties: *memory is the only honest witness, Vasquez, God, give it a rest.*

Marisol Reyes vanished in 1994. I was fourteen years old in 1994, living nowhere near Aldermoor, having never heard of it. She could not have met a detective who said that line, because the detective who says that line did not exist yet in any professional capacity, and when he did exist, he existed in three jurisdictions over twenty years, and none of those cases involved a missing housekeeper.

Unless the page wasn't written in 1994.

Unless the page was written recently, by someone who knew my cases, knew my habits, knew my sentences—and wanted me to find it.

I photographed the page. Then I photographed it again from a different angle, because photographing things is the only prayer I know. The flash whitened the paper; the ink stayed put, patient, smug. Somewhere in the grain of that page was a pen, a purchase, a hand. Hands age. Hands tremble. Thirty years of living changes pressure and speed and the arthritis in the second knuckle. If these pages were recent, the hand would know it, even if the hand thought it was hiding.

I looked closer.

The slant was right. The *m*'s were right. But the *t*-crossings—Marisol's impatient stroke, the one that clipped its neighbors—were careful here. Deliberate. Performed. Whoever wrote these seven pages had studied the crossings and gotten them ninety percent correct, and the missing ten percent was exactly the part a forger can't fake: the unconscious haste of a person who isn't thinking about her own handwriting.

Someone had learned to be Marisol the way you learn a language from books—fluent, correct, and native to no one.

* * *

Pages four through six escalated, and I use that word deliberately, because by page six I was standing up, pacing my kitchen at seven in the morning, talking to a dead woman's ghost-writer out loud like a lunatic.

Page four: *They settled the claims quietly. Four envelopes. I know because I watched the lawyer count them.* Page five: *Elena was only a girl. Whatever she knows, they taught it to her afterward, the way you teach a child a family story until she believes she remembers it.* Page six was half water-stained or deliberately stained, and what remained legible said: *If you are reading this, then it finally reached the right eyes. Check the depositions. Alibis agree too perfectly when they are built together. One of them changed his story between '94 and '97 and thought no one kept the older copy.*

Check the depositions. That was chapter nine of an investigation I hadn't started yet, handed to me by a woman who died before I finished high school—or by whoever was wearing her.

But page seven. Page seven is why I'm writing this down instead of keeping it in my head, and I keep almost nothing on paper, because paper can be searched and my head cannot. Page seven, in Marisol's stolen hand, in ink that smelled faintly of a fountain pen rather than the ballpoint of the earlier entries:

*You photograph every page. You photograph everything. You did it in the Hollis case, and the Deveraux case, and the one in Vermont with the teacup. You think your memory protects you. It does. But it also means you cannot unknow what I show you, and I need you to know it, all of it, exactly, because you are the only one who will hold it perfectly enough to matter.*

*Count along.*

I sat down. My hands were steady, which annoyed me; some part of me felt the occasion called for trembling and my body declined to perform it.

Whoever wrote this knew the teacup. The Vermont case—Edith Deveraux, seventy-one, "accidental" fall down her own stairs, except the teacup by her hand had been placed by a left-handed person and Edith was right-handed, and the scuff mark on the third stair ran the wrong direction for a falling body, and I had stood in that kitchen and said, quietly, to no one, *the cup is wrong,* and no one heard me but the killer, presumably, and the county medical examiner, who eventually agreed with me in writing.

Three solved cases. Three quiet corrections of the official record. None of them front-page news. All of them in files that a sufficiently motivated person with legal access could read—and in the details of which only two parties were ever fully versed: me, and the people who arranged the deaths.

The diary writer knew the teacup. Knew it the way I knew it—not as a file summary but as a detail, a left hand, a wrong-direction scuff. Which meant my audience was not a journalist, not a curious lawyer, not a true-crime hobbyist who'd read the summaries.

My audience was on the inside of one of those deaths. Or all of them.

Or—my mind offered this generously, the way it offers everything—it was Marisol herself, alive, thirty years older, writing to me from wherever she'd been hiding, having followed my career the way you follow weather in a place you once fled. Possible. I held onto it for an hour like a raft. But the raft leaked: the phrasing was mine, not hers. *Hold it perfectly enough to matter.* That's how I talk. That's how I think. Even her sentence rhythms had been colonized.

Someone was writing to me through a dead woman's voice, using my own language, quoting my own cases, and the effect was not flattery. It was the particular horror of finding your fingerprints on a door you've never touched.

* * *

By nine I had photographed all sixty-eight pages—all of them, including the seven, including the inside covers, including a faint embossing on the back board that might be a stationer's mark and might be nothing. My phone held 214 images. My head held all of them, permanently, in the order taken, retrievable at will, which sounds like a gift and functions like one until the contents turn hostile. Most people get to forget things. They read a disturbing passage and by Thursday it has softened into a mood. I would carry page seven verbatim to my grave, punctuation intact, and I already resented it.

I made a second pot of coffee and actually drank some of it, and while it went down I laid the case out in my head the way I lay out anything: chronologically, ruthlessly, without mercy for my own comfort.

Fact: the diary's core was authentic 1994 Marisol. Fact: the final entry predated her disappearance's official confirmation and contained knowledge—four voices, the locked study—that fit no public record. Fact: the seven appended pages referenced events spanning decades, including my own unpublished investigative habits, and were written in a forged version of her hand by someone with intimate knowledge of at least one murder I'd helped expose.

Conclusion, provisional: the diary was not evidence of the past. The diary was correspondence. Someone had been mailing it, or planting it, or curating it, installment by installment, for years—waiting for it to surface in a claim, waiting for a particular kind of reader. Maybe waiting for me specifically. *You are the only one who will hold it perfectly enough to matter.*

Which raised the question I circled all afternoon while pretending to organize my files: was I the intended recipient, or merely the first competent person to open the mail?

There's a difference, and the difference matters enormously to my continued breathing. An intended recipient has been chosen—a compliment and a death sentence in the same envelope. A coincidental reader is just collateral with good eyesight.

I thought about the lawyer who delivered it. Greer, his card said. Ambrose Greer, of some firm whose name was a law school's worth of partners. He'd handled the lapsed claim's paperwork for the estate's successor entity—which traced back, I'd checked at midnight, to a holding company whose registered agent was a firm connected, two degrees out, to Theo Brandt's old operations. Everything in this case led back to those four survivors the way rivers lead to the sea.

At four o'clock I did something I almost never do: I called a colleague. Not a friend—I don't keep those in inventory—but Detective Amara Okafor, who has called me a nuisance to my face and behind my back with equal conviction, and who keeps the cold-case files for a jurisdiction that includes a certain burned estate.

"Vasquez." She answered on the second ring, sounding like a woman who'd been expecting worse news than mine. "Please tell me you're calling to apologize for 2019."

"I'm calling about a fire," I said. "September 1994. Aldermoor."

A pause long enough to pour something into. When she spoke again, the nuisance had been set aside, carefully, like a coat.

"Why does that name make my desk feel heavier?"

"Because," I said, looking at the photograph of page seven glowing on my screen, at Marisol's borrowed handwriting delivering my own sentences back to me like a ransom note composed entirely of things I'd said, "someone just mailed a dead woman's diary to my case load, and the last page knows things about me that only a killer should."

Another pause. Shorter.

"Come in tomorrow," Okafor said. "Bring the diary. Don't bring coffee opinions." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "Aldermoor. Christ. I worked a drowning out that way in '97. Nobody insured. Nobody cared."

I wrote the year down—'97, drowning, uninsured—on a sticky note, because some numbers deserve to exist outside my skull, where at least they can burn.

Then I sat with the diary one last time before bagging it, and I read page seven again, word-perfect, against my will, and I noticed—for the first time, and I will never forgive myself for the delay—what was pressed into the paper beneath the ink. A blind impression, the ghost of a previous page, written on and torn out. I angled the lamp. Three words surfaced in the dent of the fibers, in a hand that was not Marisol's at all:

*He counts still.*
