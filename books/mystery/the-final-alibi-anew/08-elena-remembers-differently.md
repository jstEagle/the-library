## 8. Elena Remembers Differently

People assume that surviving a fire means you remember it in flames. That your memory is all orange and roaring, a wall of heat you carry around inside your chest forever.

Mine isn't like that. Mine is mostly quiet. It's the sound of my father's voice through a locked door, and the particular way gravel sounds under tires when a car is leaving too fast and then not leaving at all. The fire itself is almost an afterthought, tucked into the corner of the night like an uninvited guest who arrived late and left early, taking two people with him on the way out.

I was nineteen. I want to say that up front, because everything I did afterward, I did at nineteen, and then I just... kept doing it. For thirty years. At some point the nineteen-year-old's decisions stopped being hers and started being mine, and I never noticed the exact moment the handoff happened. That's the trick of it. Nobody tells you that a lie doesn't stay young. It ages right along with you, and one morning you look in the mirror and realize the lie has crow's feet now, and a mortgage, and a vineyard with your name on the deed.

My name is Elena Marsh. I was born Elena Aldermoor, which is a name I shed the way you'd shed a coat that had been through a fire — technically salvageable, but you'd never feel clean in it again.

* * *

Let me tell you what I remember, in the order I remember it, which is not the order it happened. Memory is a terrible archivist. It files things by weight, not date.

First fragment: my father's voice, raised, coming from the study. He didn't shout often. Gerald Aldermoor was a man who believed volume was for people who'd lost the argument, so when his voice went up an octave that night — the twenty-third of October, 1994 — it frightened me more than any scream would have. Shouting was him breaking his own rules, and a man who breaks his own rules is a man approaching some kind of edge.

"You signed it," he was saying. "You sat across from me and you signed it, Marisol, don't you dare stand in this house and—"

The rest was lost under the study door. The study door was oak, and it had a lock my father installed the year I turned twelve, ostensibly for the documents. Insurance papers. Deeds. Whatever men like my father kept behind oak and brass.

Second fragment: Marisol's shoes by the back step. She always took them off at the door — she grew up in a house where you did that, she said, and old habits were load-bearing. Her flats were there, side by side, toes pointed out, the way she always left them. Which meant she hadn't left. Which meant she was still in the house, behind the oak door, being shouted at by my father about something she had signed.

Third fragment: the smell of smoke, faint, wrong. Not woodsmoke. Woodsmoke in October is a comfort; every house in the valley had a chimney going. This was different — chemical, sharp, the smell of something that wanted to burn rather than something content to. I stood in the kitchen holding a glass of water I'd poured for no one and thought: *that's not our chimney.*

Fourth fragment, and this is the one I've spent three decades trying to file somewhere it won't cut me: headlights. Marisol's car, the little blue Corolla she loved more than most people, reversing down the drive. Slowly. Too slowly, if you were fleeing a burning building. And then — here is the part I have never said aloud, not to the investigators, not to the lawyers, not to God during the years I still bothered with Him — the car stopped. At the end of the drive. Engine running. Headlights on. Just sitting there in the dark like a held breath.

And then the east wing lit up orange, and the car wasn't there anymore, and I have spent thirty years deciding whether that means she drove away or whether it means something worse, and I have never once let myself finish the arithmetic.

Because here's what the official record says: Marisol Reyes left the Aldermoor estate before the fire began. Her car was found two counties over, abandoned. No body. No witnesses to her departure. A woman who walked out of her job, out of her life, out of the path of a fire that killed the estate's accountant and my father's business partner, and was never seen again.

Here is what I know that isn't in the record: her shoes were by the back step. Her car was in the drive when the flames rose. And my father never once, in the weeks before the fire, in the investigation after, in the years until his heart gave out — never once asked anyone to look for her. Not the police. Not the insurance company. Not a private man with a discreet phone number. Gerald Aldermoor, who tracked down a stolen tractor in 1988 with the persistence of a bloodhound, did not lift a finger to find the woman who had been shouting-distance from his desk on the last night anyone saw her.

You don't have to be an investigator to notice that. You just have to be someone who was paying attention, and then spend thirty years pretending you weren't.

* * *

I nailed the door shut myself, metaphorically speaking. Though honestly, "metaphorically" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, because I also did it literally, in a sense — I bought this vineyard, planted vines over the footprint of the east wing, and turned the whole ruin into something beautiful and profitable and impossible to interrogate. You can't dig up a memory that's under four hundred acres of pinot noir. Or you can, but you'll get dirt and root systems, and everyone will agree the past is best left agricultural.

For thirty years it worked. Grief is a fine camouflage because nobody looks closely at it. They see a woman who lost her childhood home and they give you space, and wine, and sympathetic pauses, and they never ask why you flinch when someone mentions October.

Then, three days ago, a woman with gray eyes and a memory like a court stenographer walked into my tasting room and asked me about scorch patterns.

* * *

Her name is Sebastian Vasquez. She introduced herself as an insurance investigator, which is a profession I associate with clipboards and polite robbery, but she is not a clipboard person. She's the kind of person who looks at you the way you look at a document you're checking for forgery — patiently, thoroughly, with the awful courtesy of someone who already suspects.

She asked about the night of the fire. I gave her the version I've given everyone: I was in the cottage, I heard the alarms, I ran out, I watched it burn. All true. Every word true. That's the architecture of a good lie — you build it entirely out of true things, you just leave out the beams that hold it up.

But then she asked me about Marisol. Not *did you know her* — everyone knew Marisol, she was the housekeeper's daughter who'd grown up half-raised in our kitchen. Sebastian asked, "What was the last time you saw her?"

And I said, "That afternoon. In the kitchen." Which was true.

And she looked at me — not accusingly, that's the thing, she didn't do accusation, she did *attention*, which is far worse — and she said, "Her car was still in the drive that night, wasn't it."

Not a question. A statement wearing a question mark the way a wolf wears sheep's clothing. And I felt it go through me like cold water finding the crack in a dam, and I heard myself say, "Was it? I wouldn't know. I wasn't watching the drive."

Which was a lie. I was watching the drive. I watched those headlights for what felt like a full minute, maybe longer, standing at the kitchen window with my glass of untouched water, and I have replayed that minute so many times the film has worn thin in places, gone white at the edges like overexposed photograph paper.

She nodded. She thanked me for my time. She bought a bottle of the reserve cabernet, paid cash, and left, and I stood in my tasting room surrounded by the comfortable smell of oak barrels and thought: *she knows.* Not everything. But she knows the shape of the hole in my story, the way you can run your hand along a wall and feel where a door used to be, even after it's been plastered over.

* * *

Here is the thing I could not explain to her, and cannot fully explain to myself: I'm not protecting my father. My father is dead. Dead men don't need alibis; they need gravestones, and he has a very nice one.

I'm protecting — what? A nineteen-year-old girl who stood at a window and did nothing? A family name that was already rotten and just needed the fire to finish the job? Or am I protecting the only version of that night I can live inside, the one where Marisol walked away free, chose to disappear, started a new life somewhere warm, and simply never wrote?

Because the alternative versions are all unbearable. Version two: she tried to leave and something happened on that dark road, and my father's money made it un-happen. Version three: she was in the house when it burned, and the person they identified as the accountant's assistant was — no. I can't even finish that one. Thirty years and I still can't finish that one.

So I keep the door nailed shut. I water the vines. I make wine that critics call "haunted" in reviews, which they mean as praise, and I laugh at it at dinner parties, and it is the truest sentence anyone has ever written about my product.

* * *

Except now there's a diary.

Okafor — Detective Okafor, who came by yesterday with that same gray-eyed investigator, both of them drinking my coffee and asking their careful questions — mentioned it the way you'd mention weather. Recovered effects. Marisol's diary, found in a storage unit auction, of all mundane things. Evidence in a lapsed claim.

And I smiled and said how strange, how sad, and inside my chest something with claws turned over.

Because I knew Marisol kept a diary. Everyone knew; she wrote in it at the kitchen table like other people chew gum. And I know — I *know*, the way you know a face — that Marisol would never have left that diary behind. Not in a storage unit, not anywhere. She told me once, laughing, that if she ever died, I should burn it unread, because half of it was about my brother and she refused to be posthumously embarrassing.

A diary that survived its owner is either a miracle or a message. And nothing about that night has ever struck me as miraculous.

* * *

Tonight I walked the rows. It's what I do when the past gets loud — I walk between the vines in the dark, and the vines don't ask questions, and the work of staying upright on uneven ground occupies just enough of my brain to keep the memories orderly.

But tonight they wouldn't stay orderly. Tonight they came in the original order, the real order, the order I've spent thirty years shuffling like a deck I couldn't afford to have read.

Father shouting. Oak door. Shoes by the step. Water glass. Chemical smoke. Headlights. Held breath. Orange.

And underneath it all, a detail I have never told anyone, never written, never even admitted in the privacy of my own skull until this very moment, walking row fourteen with frost forming on the trellis wire: when the car stopped at the end of the drive, the driver's door opened. Just for a moment. Just long enough for someone to lean out and look back at the house.

Look back at *me*, maybe. Standing in the lit kitchen window like an idiot, holding my glass of water.

And then the door closed, and the taillights pulled onto the road, and forty seconds later the east wing caught, and I have spent thirty years telling myself those two facts are unrelated because the alternative requires me to believe that Marisol Reyes watched our house begin to burn and drove away from it — or that she never got the chance to drive away at all, and the car I watched was driven by someone else, someone who wanted us all to believe she'd left.

Either way, someone was curating that night. Someone was arranging the scene the way you arrange flowers — carefully, meaningfully, stem by stem.

I used to think that someone was my father. My father is twenty-two years dead, and the diary pages are apparently still arriving, and the arithmetic no longer works.

* * *

She'll come back. The gray-eyed one. People like her always come back; curiosity is their native weather. And when she does, she'll stand in my tasting room with her patient, forensic courtesy, and she'll ask me another question shaped like a key, and I will have to decide — again, thirty years late, with better wine and worse knees — whether to keep my hand on the nailed-shut door or finally, finally let someone pry it open.

The terrifying thing, the thing I'll admit here in the dark among the vines where no one can hear it: part of me wants her to. Part of me has been waiting thirty years for exactly this woman — someone who sees everything, remembers everything, someone whose mind is a room nothing can be hidden in. Part of me thinks: *finally, someone strong enough to hold the other end of this.*

The rest of me knows what opening that door costs. Because whatever is behind it, I helped nail it shut. Nineteen years old, glass of water going warm in my hand, watching taillights and flames and saying nothing to anyone, ever, for three decades.

Tomorrow, they say, she's walking the grounds again. Okafor called this evening, polite as a subpoena, asking permission to "survey the site."

I said yes. Of course I said yes. What was I going to say — no, you may not examine the crime scene I've been farming for thirty years?

I hung up the phone and stood in my kitchen — the new kitchen, the rebuilt one, though the window sits in the same place, faces the same drive — and I caught myself doing it again. Looking at the end of the driveway. Waiting for headlights.

Some nights, I swear, they're still there.
