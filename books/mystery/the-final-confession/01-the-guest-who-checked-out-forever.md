## 1. The Guest Who Checked Out Forever

The wake-up call system at the Grand Meridian was older than some of its guests and more reliable than most of its staff, which is why the red flag on Kestrel Ashgrove's terminal bothered her before she even understood why.

Room 714. Mr. Voss. Wake-up requested: 6:45 a.m. Status: not answered.

She stared at it for a full ten seconds, which in concierge time was a geological era.

In eleven years at the Meridian, Kestrel had learned that guests were legible the way handwriting is legible — every one of them a signature of habits, tells, small repeated marks. Mr. Voss had checked in nine days ago under a reservation made four months in advance, paid cash for the first week and card for the second, tipped in exact increments, and requested a 6:45 wake-up call every single morning without exception. He took his breakfast in the lobby at 7:20, black coffee, nothing sweet, and read the paper back to front because, he'd told her once with the ghost of a smile, "I like to know how things end before I learn how they started."

He had never missed a call. Not once.

Kestrel picked up the house phone and dialed 714. It rang six times. She let it ring eight, then hung up and dialed again, listening past the ringing for anything on the other end — breathing, a receiver knocked from a cradle, the wet mechanical sound of something wrong. Nothing. Just the empty arithmetic of rings.

"Morning, Kestrel." Dev appeared at her elbow with two coffees, setting one on the desk beside her keyboard, the lid already steaming. He was rumpled the way he always was at shift change, tie knotted loosely, glasses fogged from the cold outside. Twenty-six years old, night auditor, keeper of the hotel's sleeping numbers, and the closest thing she had to a brother. "You look like you're doing math."

"I am." She nodded at the screen. "Voss missed his call."

Dev peered over her shoulder. "Maybe he finally slept through one. Guy's what, seventy?"

"Sixty-eight. And he sleeps like a man keeping watch." She turned the coffee slowly on the desk, not drinking it. Something in her chest had begun to tick, quiet and insistent, like a clock in another room. "He told me once he hadn't overslept since 1994."

"You remember everything people say to you?"

"It's the job."

It wasn't just the job. Kestrel had come to the Meridian at twenty-two with nothing but an ability to read faces the way other people read street signs, and she had built a career out of it. She could tell a marriage ending by how a couple carried their luggage. She could tell a lie by where a man put his hands. The general manager called her the best concierge the hotel had ever employed; what he meant, though he didn't know it, was that she noticed things, and noticing was the whole profession. The desk was just furniture.

"Dial him again," Dev said, "and if no answer, do the wellness check?"

"Wellness check," she confirmed. Standard procedure. Guest unresponsive after missed commitments, security escorts staff to the door, nobody enters alone. She'd done it maybe a dozen times in eleven years. Nine times out of ten the guest was fine — dead battery, deep sleep, a phone left in a gym bag. Once it had been a heart attack, survived. The odds were overwhelmingly on her side.

The ticking in her chest said otherwise.

* * *

Tomás from security met her at the service elevator, keys jangling, yawning into his fist. "Which room?"

"714. Voss. Missed his wake-up, not answering."

"Probably got drunk last night. There was a wedding."

"Mr. Voss doesn't drink." She pressed the elevator button. "He drinks seltzer water with lime and goes to bed at ten."

Tomás gave her the look all the security staff eventually gave her — half amusement, half unease, the look of a man who suspected she knew what he'd had for lunch before he'd eaten it. They rode up in silence. Seventh floor. The corridor smelled of lemon polish and cut lilies from the conference florist, and the carpet swallowed their footsteps the way it swallowed everyone's, which was part of the Meridian's charm and, Kestrel thought idly, part of why rich people loved committing their sins here. Nothing echoed. Nothing carried. The hotel kept secrets the way it kept linens — pressed, folded, stored in rooms no guest ever saw.

She knocked on 714. Three knocks, polite. "Mr. Voss? Concierge. Your wake-up call went unanswered — I just wanted to be sure you're all right."

Nothing.

"Mr. Voss?" Harder this time. Tomás shifted behind her, master key already out.

She stepped aside and let him open the door, because procedure said security opened, and because some animal part of her brain had already begun whispering that she did not want to be the first person through it.

The room was dim. Curtains drawn — and Voss never drew the curtains; he liked the morning light, said it was the only honest thing in a hotel. The bed was made, corners sharp, unslept in. His suitcase stood closed by the closet, shoes aligned beneath it. And in the armchair by the window, facing the door as if he'd been waiting for whoever came through it, sat Mr. Voss.

Still. Too still. The stillness of furniture, of stone.

Kestrel's mind did the terrible fast work it always did, cataloguing before feeling could arrive: gray coat, wool, belted. Gray scarf wound at the throat. Hands in lap. Head bowed slightly forward, chin near chest, the posture of a man who had fallen asleep reading — except there was no book, and the angle of the head was wrong, tilted too far, and the color of his face beneath the pallor was the color of candle wax, and there was a smell underneath the lemon polish and lilies that she knew, distantly, from her grandmother's funeral, from the one time she'd walked into a room like this before.

"Oh," Tomás said, stupidly. "Oh, God. Sir? Sir!"

He moved forward; Kestrel's hand shot out and caught his sleeve. "Don't touch him."

"He's—"

"I know what he is. Don't touch him. Don't touch anything." Her voice came out level, clipped, professional, and she marveled distantly at it, at the composure operating her mouth while the rest of her stood in the doorway of a nightmare. "Call it in. Call Okafor's division — homicide will want it, but get the manager and medical first. Go."

Tomás went. His footsteps ran loud down the corridor and then were gone, and Kestrel stood alone in the open doorway of Room 714, and that was when the cataloguing caught up with the rest of her, item by item, like a folio posting charges to an account.

The gray coat.

Wool. Belted. Two buttons, mother-of-pearl, one slightly loose on the left cuff — she'd been meaning to sew it for three weeks. A small burn scar on the inside of the right pocket lining, from the time she'd leaned too close to a lobby candle while retrieving a dropped glove.

Her coat.

And the scarf — charcoal cashmere, fraying at one fringe where her cat, back when she'd still allowed herself a cat, had decided it was prey. She'd noticed both items missing from her locker eleven days ago. The day Voss checked in. She'd torn the staff room apart, filed a report with housekeeping, assumed theft by some transient agency cleaner, mourned the coat quietly because it had been expensive and because it had been hers, and then let it go, because letting go was also part of the job.

Now here it was. Here they both were. Wrapped around a dead man in Room 714.

Kestrel's legs felt strange, distant, like they belonged to someone standing in a different corridor. Violation was a word she'd used before, casually, about break-ins and identity theft, about the way hotels commodified privacy. This was different. This was someone reaching into her life, taking the cloth off her body, and dressing a corpse in it — deliberately, carefully, with the belt tied in a neat bow. Whoever had done this wanted her to see it. Wanted the police to see it. Wanted the first thought in every mind in the building to be *Kestrel Ashgrove's clothes, Kestrel Ashgrove's guest, Kestrel Ashgrove.*

They had known she would be the one to find him. Of course they had. She always found them; she was the one who noticed.

*They used my noticing against me,* she thought. *They dressed him in my absence and waited for me to come looking.*

The ticking in her chest had become a bell.

She forced herself backward into the hallway, out of the room, and pulled the door shut until it clicked, careful not to lock it, careful to touch only the handle with her sleeve — because she'd watched enough, listened enough, absorbed enough over eleven years to know that the person who finds the body is the person the body belongs to until proven otherwise. And right now, by every thread of wool and cashmere in that room, the body belonged to her.

She stood with her back against the opposite wall, arms folded tightly across herself, holding her own elbows as if she might otherwise come apart at the seams the way the coat's cuff had been coming apart for weeks. The lilies down the hall breathed their sweetness into the silence. Somewhere below, the lobby was waking up — luggage carts, espresso machine, the morning tide of arrivals — and none of them knew yet. In forty minutes the corridors would fill with gawkers and cameras and men in windbreakers, and the Grand Meridian would become the kind of hotel that appeared in headlines instead of travel guides.

Her phone buzzed. Dev: *Everything ok up there?? Tomás came down white as a sheet.*

She looked at the message for a long moment. Then, because she was who she was — because the composure had roots deeper than the shock — she typed:

*Guest in 714 deceased. Lock down the floor access log. Nobody pulls it before the detectives arrive. And Dev — don't say anything to anyone. Not yet.*

Then she added, because her hands were shaking now and honesty was the only thing keeping them attached to her:

*He was wearing my coat.*

She hit send, slid the phone into her pocket, and stayed there against the wall, watching the door of Room 714, guarding it the way she guarded everything in this building — until the elevator chimed and the world came up to meet her.

The doors opened onto a woman in a dark coat, badge already in hand, eyes already working the corridor — the detective whose name Kestrel knew from a charity event two winters ago, whose gaze she remembered because it had felt less like being seen than being *read*, and she had wondered then, idly, what it would be like to be on the other side of those eyes.

Now she knew.

"You found him?" the detective asked.

"I did." Kestrel heard her own voice, steady and clear, and understood that from this moment forward, everything she said would be evidence. "Detective, before you go in there — there's something you need to know about what he's wearing."

The detective's eyes narrowed, patient and cold. "Then let's start there."
