## 12. Dead Air

The train to Greywater Bay took four hours and cost Silas Brightwater the last of her composure somewhere around the second hour, when the carriage emptied of commuters and she realized she had been rehearsing a conversation with a stranger for the entire journey.

*Good afternoon. I'm looking for Rosa Ibarra. I have questions about a ledger. Please don't be alarmed.*

She revised it. *Good afternoon. My name is Silas Brightwater, and I believe you may be in danger—*

Worse. She tried again. *Hello. Do you like ledgers?*

She gave up and watched the sea arrive sideways through the window, grey at first, then suddenly, shockingly blue, as though the coast had been keeping it hidden from inland eyes on principle. The carriage smelled of salt and someone's abandoned mackerel sandwich. Silas found this oddly comforting. Hotels never smelled of anything true.

In her handbag, wrapped in a scarf, sat a photograph: a woman of perhaps sixty, taken at some office party a decade ago, laughing at something off-camera. Thomas had given it to her three nights ago, along with the ledger and a look she was still learning how to carry.

"She'll run when she sees you coming," he'd said. "So don't come like you're coming. Come like a tourist."

"I've never been a tourist in my life."

"I know," Thomas said, with that terrible gentleness of his. "That's rather the point, isn't it?"

So Silas had packed a suitcase she didn't need — two dresses, a paperback novel she'd bought specifically to be seen reading, sunglasses despite the forecast — and told the front desk she was taking annual leave, her first in eleven years. Mr. Okafor had looked at her the way one looks at a clock that has begun ticking backwards.

"Annual leave," he repeated.

"There's a clause in my contract," Silas said. "I checked."

And now here she was, stepping onto a platform so small it barely qualified as one, breathing air that tasted of brine and chips, wearing sunglasses that made the world feel like a lie she was telling herself.

Greywater Bay consisted of one high street, two churches, a fish-and-chip shop with a queue that suggested either excellence or a local emergency, and a promenade where the wind had clearly won several long arguments with the railings. It was, Silas thought, exactly the sort of place a person went to stop existing.

Rosa Ibarra's cottage stood at the end of Harbour Lane, the last house before the lane gave up and became shingle. It was white once; now it was the colour of old teeth. A garden fought bravely against the salt wind — rosemary, mostly, which survives everything — and a blue door with a brass knocker shaped like an anchor.

Silas stopped twenty feet away, because something was wrong, and she had spent nineteen years training herself to notice wrongness before she could name it.

The door was open. Not ajar — *open*, swung inward, and the light inside was the wrong colour for mid-afternoon, because there were things lying across the floor that shouldn't be lying across floors.

She approached the way she'd approach a guest who might be about to faint: smoothly, without hurry, hands visible.

Inside, the cottage had been turned inside out. Drawers pulled and emptied onto beds. Mattresses slashed with a violence that felt personal. Books shaken spine-up until their bindings cracked, then left in drifts. In the kitchen, every tin had been opened — every single one, including the golden syrup, which had achieved a kind of tragic art across the ceiling. Someone had been looking for paper. Someone had been very thorough and slightly unhinged about it.

On the wall above the cold fireplace hung a single framed item: a certificate of appreciation from the Grand Meridian Hotel, awarded to R. Ibarra, Accounts Department, fifteen years of service. It was the only thing left hanging. Either they hadn't wanted it, or they'd wanted her to see it.

Silas stood very still in the wreckage and felt the sea sound through the open door behind her, patient and enormous.

"No," she said quietly, to nobody. "No, no, no."

* * *

The neighbour was called Mrs. Tredinnick, and she materialized within ninety seconds of Silas stepping back outside, drawn by the gravitational pull of an unfamiliar face on Harbour Lane. She was seventy-something, built like a lifeboat, and wearing a cardigan that had survived more weather than most ships.

"You're not from the insurance lot," Mrs. Tredinnick said. It wasn't a question. It was a filter.

"No," Silas said. "I'm — a friend of Rosa's. From London." She let the accent do its work. It usually did.

Mrs. Tredinnick's face did something complicated. "Friend of our Rosa's, are you." She looked at the open door, and her jaw set. "Then you'd best come and sit down before you hear it standing up."

There was no invitation involved. Silas simply found herself steered next door into a front room smelling of lavender and coal fire, deposited into an armchair, and presented with tea she had not asked for and knew better than to refuse.

"It was Tuesday week," Mrs. Tredinnick began, sitting opposite with her own cup held in both hands like a hot water bottle. "I heard the coast road ambulance go past — you can tell the difference, the coast road ones take the bend slower — and later on the police came knocking. Said there'd been an accident. Down at Miller's Point."

"An accident," Silas said.

"A car accident." Mrs. Tredinnick's eyes were steady on hers, and there was something in them — something careful, something that had been thinking hard for days. "Car went off the road at the point. Into the water. They said—" She paused. Set down her cup. "They said the driver was a visitor. Nobody local. They wouldn't give a name, not to us, but the cottage has been empty since, and then yesterday these men came — said they were clearing the property for the family. Two of them. Big fellows. Didn't say please or thank you, and they had a van."

Silas's tea sat untouched, going cold with a kind of symbolic appropriateness.

"Did you see Rosa that week?" she asked. "Before the accident?"

"Tuesday morning, early. She was taking her bins round." Mrs. Tredinnick smiled briefly, and it transformed her whole face. "Always took her bins round at six sharp, even in winter. We used to say she was the only woman in England who enjoyed it." The smile faded. "She had a case with her. A little wheeled case. Going somewhere, I thought. But she didn't wave. And Rosa always waved."

The room was very quiet except for the fire and, beneath it, the sea.

"Mrs. Tredinnick," Silas said carefully, "when the police came — did you speak to them yourself? Or did someone speak *for* you?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she leaned forward, and her voice dropped as though the walls had relatives.

"Now that's an interesting question, miss, because yes, I did speak to them. Young constable, polite enough. And while we were talking, his superior — inspector type, city coat, too clean for this town — came and stood just close enough to listen. And every answer I gave, the young one wrote down different from what I said." She sat back. "I said the car went over at dusk, because that's when I heard the gulls scatter. He wrote *early morning*. I said I saw one man by the van. He wrote *no witnesses present*. I'm seventy-three years old, miss, not stupid. So after they'd gone, I walked down to Miller's Point myself, and I'll tell you what I found."

"What did you find?"

"Tyre marks," Mrs. Tredinnick said, "going *off* the road in a straight line. No skid. No swerve. Cars that go off roads by accident argue about it first. That car went over like it was being shown the way." She picked up her tea again, hands perfectly steady. "I haven't said that to anyone but you. And I'll deny it to my grave if you repeat it, because I've got a daughter in Plymouth and no wish to make her an orphan at forty-eight."

Silas felt something crack open in her chest — part grief, part fury, part a horrible, vertiginous recognition.

"They're saying she's dead," Silas said. "Officially. There'll be paperwork. A coroner's report."

"There'll be an urn, too, if they get their way. Some cousin from upcountry rang the chapel asking about services." Mrs. Tredinnick sniffed. "I told them Rosa wasn't a chapel sort, which is true, and also bought her time, which was the point."

Silas stared at her. "You know she's alive."

"I know our Rosa," Mrs. Tredinnick said simply. "Forty years I've known her. She takes her bins out at six, she waters my begonias when I'm poorly, and she once drove me to Exeter hospital at three in the morning with my hip gone and sang hymns to keep my mind off it — *out of tune*, mind, which is how I knew she meant well. That woman does not die in a car she knows how to drive, on a road she's driven a thousand times, without leaving so much as a note under my door." Her chin lifted. "Whatever game she's playing, she's playing it alive. You can smell the difference."

Silas laughed — a short, wet, unexpected sound — and pressed her handkerchief to her eyes before it could become anything more.

"I'm sorry," she managed. "It's only — I've spent three weeks believing a man was dead who wasn't, and now everyone else believes a woman is dead who isn't, and I appear to be the only person in England keeping both sets of books."

"That sounds exhausting, dear."

"It is," Silas said, with feeling. "It is genuinely the most exhausting thing I have ever done, and I once coordinated a wedding for four hundred guests during a tube strike."

* * *

She went back to the cottage as the light failed, because she couldn't help it. Grief, she was learning, made her tidy. She righted the fallen chairs. She stacked the books, spine-up casualties though they were. She wiped syrup from a kitchen shelf and found, beneath the tin that had held it, a hollow — the kind of hiding place every old house keeps and every searcher checks twice.

It was empty. Of course it was empty. Rosa Ibarra had not survived decades inside Edmund Marsh's accounts department by leaving things in obvious hollows.

But tucked into the back of the hollow, flat and deliberate, was a postcard. A picture of the harbour at sunset, aggressively picturesque. On the reverse, in handwriting so ordinary it could belong to anyone:

*M — If you're reading this, I'm sorry about the mess. Look where the mourners look least. — R*

Silas read it four times. *M.* Not her initial. Which meant the card wasn't for her — it was for whoever Rosa expected to come looking. Thomas? Or...

Or Evelyn. *M* for Marsh. Rosa had left a message for the family — or for whichever Marsh she trusted. And Silas had simply arrived first, holding the same key to the same locked door.

*Look where the mourners look least.*

A funeral. There would be a funeral — the cousin from upcountry had already rung the chapel. And nothing on earth was less examined than the edges of a mourning crowd: the people at the back, the ones by the gate, the ones whose grief doesn't quite fit their faces.

Silas stood in the ruined kitchen of a dead-alive woman's house, holding a postcard addressed to someone else, and understood two things with total clarity.

First: Rosa Ibarra was alive, and cleverer than all of them, and currently the most important person in England.

Second: whoever had driven that car off Miller's Point would be at the funeral. Watching. Counting heads. Making sure the guest of honour stayed absent.

And if Silas could spot them first —

She looked down at the postcard, then at the certificate on the wall — *fifteen years of service* — and felt the old hotel instinct rise in her like a tide: the instinct to manage an event, to seat the difficult guests, to ensure that everything, absolutely everything, went according to plan.

Except this time the plan required a funeral to go slightly, deliberately wrong.

"Well," Silas said aloud to the empty cottage, to the sea beyond it, to Rosa wherever she was hiding. "I suppose I'd better buy a black dress."

Outside, the wind changed, and carried with it, faintly, the sound of the chapel bell practising for Sunday.
