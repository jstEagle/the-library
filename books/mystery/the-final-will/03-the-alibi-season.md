## 3. The Alibi Season

Casper Okafor had learned that old men lie best in kitchens.

Not in living rooms, where the furniture was formal and the light came from lamps arranged for photographs. Not on porches, where the wind could carry a voice to a neighbor's ear. Kitchens. The table was scarred and familiar, the coffee was hot, and the chair across from you had held thirty years of visitors who asked nothing harder than how the garden was doing.

He sat in four of those kitchens over the course of one long October week, and he brought with him a folder he never opened, because the folder was not for them. It was for him—a prop of authority, a way of making his silences feel official. He'd learned that too. In Hollow Creek, people filled silence the way water fills a crack, and what poured out of them was usually the thing they'd sworn never to say.

The first kitchen belonged to Dale Pruitt, retired fire warden, who in October 1995 had been thirty-four years old and certain of everything. His alibi was the simplest: he'd been at the Calloway house until nearly midnight, playing cards with Ansel Calloway and the bank's attorney, Whit Harrow.

"Left around eleven," Dale said, pouring coffee with a hand that trembled only slightly. "Maybe eleven-fifteen. Ansel walked me out."

"You're sure about the time?"

Dale smiled, and the smile was warm and easy and arrived perhaps half a second too quickly. "Sure as I can be after thirty years, Casper. It was a Friday. I remember because I had to be up early Saturday for the burn inspection." He sat down across the table. "You know, folks around here were real glad when Eli Brandt confessed. Even if it come late. Poor Maren deserved somebody to answer for it."

*Even if it come late.* Casper turned the phrase over gently, like a stone in a creek bed. "Eli died before anyone could arrest him," he said. "Confession showed up in my mail last month."

"That so." Dale looked at his coffee. "Well. Dead men tell the truth eventually, I suppose."

It was an odd sentence. Casper wrote it down word for word in the car afterward, sitting outside the Pruitt place with the engine off, listening to the wind move through the dead soybean field. *Dead men tell the truth eventually.* Most people would have said the opposite—that dead men couldn't confirm anything, that a confession from beyond the grave was worth exactly nothing in a court of law. But Dale Pruitt hadn't said that. Dale Pruitt had sounded relieved.

* * *

Ruth Calloway knew Casper Okafor was back in town before her father's hospice nurse mentioned it, before she saw his dented Subaru parked outside Dale Pruitt's place on Route 9. She knew because of the way the town moved—how conversations stopped when she entered the pharmacy, how Marlene Dutton at the counter suddenly found the lottery tickets fascinating. Hollow Creek communicated by silence. It always had.

She drove out to the cemetery on the hill above the creek, where her mother lay under a granite stone worn smooth by weather, and she sat in the car with the heater running and let herself think the thoughts she did not allow herself anywhere else.

Her father was dying. That was the plain fact of it, the mercy of it, some days. Ansel Calloway, who had signed her school permission slips and taught her to fish and once, memorably, driven through an ice storm to retrieve her from a college dorm because she was homesick—that man was now ninety pounds of failing machinery in a room that smelled of lavender and antiseptic, mistaking the nurse for her mother, sometimes mistaking Ruth for her mother too.

And yet there were other days, lucid days, when his eyes cleared and fixed on her with something that was not confusion at all. On those days he said things like *Has anyone been asking about the trust?* and *If a man comes asking, you tell him nothing, Ruthie, you hear me, nothing*—and then the fog would roll back in and he'd call her Margaret, who had been dead eleven years.

Ruth had spent sixty-one years being her father's daughter. She knew what loyalty cost. She also knew, now, what it bought, and the ledger had stopped balancing somewhere around the time two letters landed in a podcaster's mailbox and the whole rotten season of 1995 began thawing out of the ground.

Because Ruth had known about the letters before Casper did. She'd seen her father's attorney, Whit Harrow—eighty-two years old himself now, hands like dried leaves—carrying a manila envelope into the hospice room three weeks ago, and she'd heard her father's voice, thin but clear, say: *"Is it done? Is it in his hand?"*

In whose hand? She hadn't asked. Asking questions in this family had never gone well. Her brother Thomas had asked questions in 1995—he'd been nineteen, home from college, full of righteousness—and by Christmas he'd transferred to a university in Oregon and never come home again except for funerals.

She got out of the car and stood at her mother's grave, arms crossed against the cold.

"I don't know what to do, Mama," she said, which was true, and which was also the most honest conversation she'd had in weeks.

* * *

The second kitchen was Whit Harrow's, and it was different in every particular. Harrow had been the bank's attorney for forty years, and his kitchen looked like a law office that had gotten lost—a wall of bound volumes, a desk instead of a table, a clock that ticked with the patience of a man billing by the hour.

"Maren Voss," Casper said, when the pleasantries had run their course. "Friday, October thirteenth, 1995. Where were you?"

Harrow didn't reach for coffee. He folded his hands. "At the Calloways'. Card game. Ansel, Dale Pruitt, myself, and—" he paused, and the pause was surgical, "—Raymond Okafor."

His father's name, laid on the table like a card turned face-up. Casper kept his breathing even. "My dad played cards?"

"Occasionally. Your father and Ansel went back a long way. Deer camp, church committee, all of it." Harrow's eyes were pale and unhurried. "We broke up around eleven. Eleven-thirty at the latest. I remember because I had a filing deadline Monday and I wanted a clear morning."

Eleven. Eleven-fifteen. Eleven-thirty. Three men, three slightly different times, all clustered within half an hour of each other—all safely before midnight, all placing each other in a warm lit room while Maren Voss drove home alone on a road that had no streetlights then and none now.

But here was the thing Casper had noticed, the thing that had kept him awake since Tuesday: the original statements, which he'd obtained through a records request two years ago, said something different. Dale Pruitt's 1995 statement put him leaving at *ten o'clock*. Harrow's said *around ten-thirty*. Only Ansel Calloway's statement—the one taken by Deputy Raymond Okafor himself—said eleven.

Sometime in the past thirty years, the story had been adjusted. Rehearsed. Synchronized. And nobody adjusts a memory toward greater precision unless someone has told them what precision is required.

"You've told this story before," Casper said mildly. "Recently?"

"I've told it whenever asked." Harrow smiled without warmth. "That's what witnesses do, Casper. You'd know—you've made a profession of asking." He stood, signaling the interview's end. "Your father was a good man. Whatever you think you're digging up, I'd ask you to consider that some things get buried because the ground needs rest."

* * *

The third and fourth kitchens blurred together, or rather they rhymed. Ray Tuller, retired county road supervisor, and Edna Marsh, widow of the trust's bookkeeper—both at the card game, both with times that had drifted upward over three decades, both with the same too-quick warmth, the same rehearsed cadence. Edna even used the same phrase Dale had used, almost verbatim: *poor Maren deserved closure*. When Casper asked Edna what she remembered about Maren herself, the woman's face did something complicated—grief trying to surface through a crust of discipline—and failed.

"She taught my Bobby in fourth grade," Edna said. "Sweet girl. Asked a lot of questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"Oh—" Edna waved a hand at the window, the yard, the world. "Teacher questions."

Casper thanked her and left, and in the car he sat for a long moment watching a hawk circle the bare fields. Four witnesses. Four stories that had been sanded and fitted together like floorboards. And at the center of every version, holding the room together like a nail: Ansel Calloway's house, Ansel Calloway's game, Ansel Calloway's name spoken by everyone first, as if the evening itself belonged to him.

Maren Voss had scheduled a meeting with the Hollow Creek Community Trust board for October sixteenth, 1995. Casper had the appointment card now—it had come with the journals, tucked between pages like a pressed flower. She had found something in the books. She had wanted to talk about it. And the entire founding generation of Hollow Creek had spent the same Friday night manufacturing a reason why none of them could possibly be disturbed by whatever she wanted to say.

* * *

On Thursday evening, Ruth Calloway did something she had never done in seven years of Casper's podcasting: she called him.

"Casper. It's Ruth. Ruth Calloway." As if he wouldn't know. As if her voice hadn't been the sound of Sunday school and summer picnics for his entire childhood.

"Ruth. I've been meaning to—"

"I know what you've been doing. Driving around with your little folder, sitting in everyone's kitchens." A breath. Outside her window the hospice parking lot lights buzzed against the dark. She could see her father's room from here, third floor, fourth window, the lamp always on because he was afraid of the dark now, which was its own kind of joke. "I'm asking you as a favor. One favor. Stop."

"Ruth—"

"My father is dying, Casper. He has maybe weeks. Maybe less. And you're going to drag his name through—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the crack, hated that grief and guilt sounded identical coming out of her mouth. "Whatever you think happened, whatever those letters say, it won't bring her back. It'll just burn down what's left."

The silence on the line stretched long enough that she thought he'd hung up.

"I liked your dad," Casper finally said quietly. "He coached my Little League team. He gave me five dollars for every A on my report card. You know I don't want it to be him."

"But you think it might be."

He didn't answer, and the not-answer was worse than any accusation.

Ruth closed her eyes. Third floor, fourth window, the lamp burning. Somewhere below her, in a drawer in her father's old study that she had never been able to bring herself to clean out, there was a box of bank stationery she'd found last spring—cream-colored, heavy stock, the Calloway First Trust letterhead—and she had told herself it meant nothing, that stationery was just stationery, that the missing ream meant nothing at all.

"Eli Brandt couldn't have written that confession," she heard herself say. "He had arthritis so bad he couldn't hold a pencil. Everybody in town knew that."

The words were out before she understood she'd decided to say them. There was no taking them back. There never was, in this town; that was the whole trouble with Hollow Creek. Everything you said stayed said, buried shallow, waiting for someone with a shovel.

Casper's breath came slow on the other end. "Ruth. Why are you telling me this?"

She watched the lamp in her father's window, steady and small against the dark, and she thought about Maren Voss driving home on an unlit road, thinking she was bringing help to people who had already decided what her help was worth.

"Because somebody has to stop lying," Ruth said, "and apparently it's going to be me."
