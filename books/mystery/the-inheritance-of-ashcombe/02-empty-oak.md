## 2. Empty Oak

The morning of the exhumation came in cold and clear, the kind of October light that makes everything look like it has been arranged for a photograph. Gideon Halloran stood at the edge of the cathedral close at half past six, a thermos of tea going lukewarm in one hand, and watched the workmen assemble the hoist over the crypt steps. Ropes creaked. Canvas had been laid down to protect the flagstones from the crane's outriggers, and someone—Tamsin, probably, though she'd never admit to being so eager—had already swept the leaves from the path the procession would take.

Three hundred years, Gideon thought. Three hundred years of Ashcombe telling itself the same story about Edmund Vane: founder, benefactor, saint of the harbor, dead in his bed in the winter of 1724 and buried beneath his own cathedral with all the honors a grateful town could heap on him. And today they were going to lift him out of the ground, dust him off, and put him back with new velvet and fresh brass, because tercentenaries were good for tourism and the heritage board had secured a grant.

Gideon had spent eleven years as keeper of the Ashcombe archive and had learned one thing above all others: closed cases were the only kind worth having. A case that stayed closed was a case that couldn't hurt you. The Vane interment was the most closed case in the building—sealed ledger, sealed register, sealed crypt door with its triple lock and its wax stamp renewed by every dean since Victoria. Gideon's own signature was on the last renewal, four years ago, in their small careful hand. *Verified intact.* They had written it without thinking, the way you write anything you've written a hundred times.

They thought about those two words now, watching the ropes go taut.

The unease had started three weeks ago, when the exhumation was first proposed, and Gideon had told themselves it was nothing—the ordinary reluctance of an archivist asked to let strangers handle what they kept. But it hadn't faded the way ordinary reluctance fades. It had sat in them like a stone swallowed whole, and last night it had kept them awake past two, staring at the ceiling of their flat above the archive, listening to the harbor bell count the tide.

There was no reason for dread. There was paperwork. That was all.

"Gideon." Dean Merridew's voice, behind them, pitched low and pleasant as always. He came to stand at their shoulder, hands folded over his cassock, looking up at the hoist with the expression of a man admiring a flower arrangement he had personally approved. "A fine day for it."

"A fine day," Gideon agreed.

"The mayor is bringing the schoolchildren. Aldous feels very strongly about the educational value." Merridew smiled at something private. "You'll be present throughout, of course. If there are documents required—certificates, the register—I want no delays. This is a ceremony, not an excavation. We mustn't let it become an excavation."

"No delays," Gideon said. It was not quite a promise, and Merridew either didn't notice or didn't care, which was how their relationship worked best. The dean valued Gideon for the same reason the archive valued acid-free boxes: things went in, things stayed put, nothing leaked. Eleven years of being useful and unremarkable. Gideon had built that invisibility plank by plank, and most days it felt like safety.

Most days.

* * *

By nine the close was full. Gideon stood near the crypt steps with the register open on a folding table before them, protected from the light under a canvas awning, and watched Ashcombe assemble itself. The schoolchildren in their maroon blazers, fidgeting in rows. The choir, robed, murmuring. The heritage board in their best coats, the ones who had fought for two years over whether the coffin should be displayed horizontally or at a reverent angle. And down the front, shaking hands and laughing at jokes Gideon could not hear, Mayor Aldous Crane—tall, silver-templed, wearing the chain of office like it had been cast for his neck specifically. Which, in a sense, it had. A Crane had held some office or other in this town almost continuously since the 1730s. Gideon knew that the way they knew the dew point: without effort, without ever having decided to learn it.

The service was short. Merridew spoke about foundations and legacies, about the man whose vision had raised these walls, whose charity had endowed the school whose children now sang for him. The choir sang. Then the workmen took hold of the ropes, and the crowd went quiet in that particular way crowds do when the sacred becomes physical—a hush with weight in it, hundreds of people breathing carefully.

The crypt door came open. Gideon had unlocked it themselves at dawn, all three locks, and signed the access log, and now stood with the logbook on their table like a shield. The workmen descended. There was a long interval of muffled sounds, of instructions passed up the stairwell, and then the first edge of the coffin broke the plane of the doorway and the crowd made a sound—not a gasp, something softer, a collective intake, like the sea drawing back off shingle.

The oak coffin came up into the light.

It was beautiful. That was Gideon's first thought, and their second, and it did not leave. Blackened oak, iron-bound, the wood dark as wet peat but sound—no rot, no collapse, the grain still visible where the light caught it. The iron fittings had gone to lace-thin rust but held. Someone had carved a cross into the lid, and below it, worn nearly smooth but legible if you knew to look, the letters E.V.

"Careful," Merridew said, unnecessarily. Everyone was being careful. The workmen set the coffin on the trestles that had been built for it, directly in front of the steps, and stepped back, and for a moment nobody moved at all.

Then the joiner came forward with his tools—a conservation joiner brought up from Exeter, vetted twice—and began to lift the lid.

Gideon had opened enough coffins in records to know what should happen next. The nails would shriek. The sealant would crack in long black lines. Inside: lead, probably, for a man of Vane's standing; and within the lead, after three hundred years, the honest arithmetic of death. Bone. Shroud. Perhaps hair, perhaps boot leather, perhaps the wedding ring everyone hoped would be found because it would make such a photograph.

The lid came free with a groan.

Inside was river stones.

Not bones. Not lead. Not even darkness. The coffin was lined with linen—coarse-woven, undyed, the color of old cream—and the linen was filled with stones. Rounded gray and brown stones, harbor stones, river stones, the kind a child pockets on a beach. They lay in the coffin in a neat bed, packed with care, filling the human shape of it exactly: more of them where the torso would be, fewer where the legs, a single larger stone placed where the head should lie. Someone had done this deliberately. Someone had taken the time to make the stones look like a body.

The silence lasted four seconds. Gideon counted them; counting was what they did instead of feeling.

Then the sound started. It moved through the crowd like fire through dry grass—a woman's cry cut off, a child asking a question in the bright carrying voice children use for exactly the wrong moments, the schoolmaster hushing him too late. Someone said *empty* and someone else said *God* and someone said, loudly, *that's not possible,* which was the truest thing anyone had said all morning.

Merridew's face did something complicated and settled on serenity, the way a pond settles after a stone. "Ladies and gentlemen—" he began, arms rising.

But the crowd wasn't listening to the dean anymore. The crowd was looking into the coffin, and the coffin was looking back, and three hundred years of Ashcombe's story lay in pieces among the river stones.

Gideon did not look at the crowd. Gideon looked at the linen.

Because here was the thing—the thing that turned the cold stone in their stomach into something else, something harder and hotter. The linen was not modern. Gideon had handled enough eighteenth-century textiles to know the weave at a glance: the irregularity of hand-spun flax, the selvedge finished in the old style. And the stones had been wrapped individually—each one in its own twist of cloth, tied off, the way you'd pack goods for a sea voyage or a long journey. This was not vandalism. Vandals didn't sew. This was ceremony. Whoever had emptied Edmund Vane's coffin in 1724 had done it with reverence, or with terror, or both, and had wanted whoever found it someday to understand that.

Which meant it had been done within days of the burial. Which meant—

"Mr. Halloran." Aldous Crane's voice, at their elbow, quiet enough that only Gideon heard it. The mayor had come around the trestles without anyone noticing, or rather everyone had noticed and decided not to see; that was Crane's gift. Up close he smelled of bay rum and wool, and his eyes, pale as harbor water, were fixed on the coffin with an expression Gideon could not read and did not like. "A word."

"Mayor."

"You're the archivist. You keep the records." Crane said it as if establishing facts for a later hearing. "I'm going to ask you to manage this finding."

"Manage it," Gideon repeated.

"Manage it." Crane's voice stayed level, courteous, almost warm. "This town has one story about Edmund Vane, Mr. Halloran, and that story holds up the school, the trust, the harbor commission, my family's name, and your cathedral. Whatever happened in 1724—and I expect there's an explanation, grave-robbers, Victorians, God knows what—it will be found calmly, quietly, and by the right people. Not by a crowd of schoolchildren and not by the *Herald*. Do we understand each other?"

Gideon looked at him. Eleven years of practice kept their face still. "I understand that the interment register is a public record, Mayor, and that the finding will need to be documented accurately."

"Accurately." Crane smiled. It reached his eyes the way frost reaches a window. "Accuracy is a matter of sequence, Mr. Halloran. What gets recorded first tends to be what people believe longest. Think about that." He touched the brim of an imaginary hat toward the coffin, toward the stones, as if greeting an old acquaintance, and walked back through the parting crowd to the front row, where he shook the headmistress's hand and said something that made her laugh shakily.

Gideon looked down at the register open on the table. Their own signature at the top of the page, from the dawn unlocking. *Verified intact.*

Someone had lied. Either the seal had been broken and re-stamped across three centuries of deans who signed without looking—which was possible, Gideon supposed, possible the way a shipwreck is possible—or the coffin had been empty from the very first day, and every record, every ledger entry, every solemn reinterment and centenary procession since 1724 had been performed over a box of river rocks.

And if the second thing were true, then the question was not *what happened to Edmund Vane's body.*

The question was *who needed him buried, and why.*

* * *

The crowd dispersed badly, in knots, in arguments. The heritage board clustered by the lychgate like gulls over a dropped chip. Two local journalists wrote standing up against the wall. The schoolchildren were marched away in a silence more shaken than respectful. Merridew ordered the coffin covered with a cloth and the close cleared, and then disappeared into the deanery without speaking to Gideon again, which was itself a message.

Gideon stayed. They waited until the last workman had gone, until the canvas cover was settled and the trestles stood alone in the emptying close, and then they did what they always did, the only thing they had ever been any good at.

They took out the logbook and they documented.

Time of opening: 10:14. Condition of outer oak: sound. Absence of lead lining: noted. Contents: riverine stones, approximately two hundred, individually wrapped in plain-woven linen consistent with early eighteenth-century manufacture. No osseous material. No nameplate beyond the carved initials. No evidence of recent disturbance of the outer boards—the dust and cobweb matting across the lid joints had been continuous, unbroken, older than living memory.

That last line stopped Gideon's pen.

Unbroken. The cobwebs had been unbroken. Gideon had seen them at dawn, when they'd unlocked the door and knelt with a torch to check the seals before the workmen came down. The webbing had run across the lid seam like frost fern, thick with three centuries of crypt dust, and it had been whole. Nobody had opened that coffin since it went into the ground. Which meant the stones had gone in with the coffin. Which meant the funeral itself—the grand public funeral, the one in the ledger, the one with the mourning rings and the tolling bells—had been a performance.

Gideon closed the logbook and stood for a long moment in the cold, listening to the harbor bell.

Their whole life, they had believed in closed cases. Put the paper away, lock the box, let the past stay past—that was the job, that was the discipline, that was how you survived inside an institution: by keeping things where they belonged. And here was the most closed case in England, sealed under triple lock and wax and three hundred years of civic pride, and it had never been closed at all. It had been *staged.* The difference mattered. A closed case was finished. A staged case was a door painted on a wall, and somewhere behind it, someone was still moving.

Resolve arrived the way the cold does—not suddenly, but completely. Gideon felt it settle into their chest and recognized it, because they had felt it only once or twice before, the times they'd caught a cataloguing error that unraveled a whole collection's provenance. The pleasure of it was shameful and enormous. *This case will be closed properly,* they told themselves. Not managed. Not smoothed over. Closed—meaning opened all the way to the bottom, every page turned, every lie given its proper citation.

They gathered the register, the logbook, the thermos. As they crossed the close, Tamsin fell into step beside them—young, freckled, trying very hard to look composed and failing beautifully.

"Did you see?" she whispered. "The stones were *wrapped.* Each one. I saw through the—when they lifted the corner of the cloth. Who does that?"

"Someone with time," Gideon said. "And a reason."

"And the register—the burial entry, I've pulled it twice for researchers, it's all there, the fees, the bells, everything—"

"Yes." Gideon had already thought of that. The ledger recorded the funeral as paid in full. Which meant the money had gone somewhere, and the men who'd taken it had known what they were paid for. "I'll want the original volume on my desk this afternoon, Tamsin. The 1724 interment register. And the sexton's accounts for the same year. Don't use the reading-room copies—I want the bindings checked, every leaf accounted for."

Tamsin's eyes went wide. "You think pages are missing."

"I think," Gideon said, "that a town which can bury a box of stones in front of two hundred witnesses and call it a founder can also lose a page or two." They heard their own voice and were faintly surprised by it: steady, certain, already someone slightly different than the person who had stood at the crypt steps at dawn dreading nothing in particular.

At the archive door they paused and looked back once. The covered coffin sat alone on its trestles in the great stony emptiness of the close, and beyond it the harbor lay flat and gray, keeping whatever it kept.

Somewhere in the stacks behind them, under Gideon's own careful hands, three hundred years of paper waited to say what the stones could not.

They went in, and locked the door, and began.
