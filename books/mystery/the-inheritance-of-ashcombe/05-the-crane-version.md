## 5. The Crane Version

The invitation came on cream cardstock, hand-lettered in copperplate that cost more per word than Gideon earned per week.

*Mr. Aldous Crane requests the pleasure of your company at Crane House, Thursday next, seven o'clock.*

Gideon turned it over twice, as if the reverse might contain a footnote explaining why a mayor summoned an archivist to dinner. It didn't. It didn't need to. In Ashcombe, when a Crane asked you to Crane House, you went—and you understood, before you ever crossed the threshold, that the evening would not be about dinner.

They dressed carefully. Not their best clothes; their most forgettable ones. The grey wool suit that made them look like what they were supposed to be: a functionary, a keeper of shelves, a person whose name appeared on accession forms and nowhere else. Gideon had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being overlooked in a town that ran on names, and tonight they intended to wear that invisibility like armor.

Crane House stood at the top of Quay Hill, a Georgian pile of honeyed stone that had watched over the harbor since before the harbor had its present shape. Its windows caught the last of the light and held it, gold on gold. A house built to be looked at. A family built the same way.

The door opened before Gideon knocked.

"Mr. Halloran." Aldous Crane stood in the frame with a glass of something amber already in his hand, though it was clearly meant for his guest. He was seventy if he was a day, tall without softness, silver-haired in the way of men who paid for the color rather than earned it. His handshake was dry and brief, a transaction concluded. "You'll take a whisky. You look like a man who takes a whisky."

"I'm on duty," Gideon said, which was true, and took the glass anyway, which was also true.

"Duty." Crane smiled as he led them down a corridor paneled in dark oak. "There's a word I've been wanting to discuss with you."

* * *

The study was everything the archive was not.

Where Gideon's office smelled of dust and cold stone and the particular mildew of documents stored too long against exterior walls, Crane's study breathed leather and beeswax and woodsmoke. Where the archive's light came grudgingly through slit windows, here it poured from French doors open onto a terrace overlooking the water. And where every object in Gideon's keeping was catalogued, numbered, and cross-referenced, this room's treasures sat about in confident disorder—a brass telescope, a ship's wheel mounted like a hunting trophy, portraits of men in cravats whose faces repeated down the generations like a signature practiced until it became a brand.

On the desk, squared neatly beside a decanter, lay a folder.

Gideon knew that folder. They had accessioned it themselves, three days after the coffin came up empty. *Preliminary Conservation Report — Vane Interment Materials.* River stones. Linen wrapping, 1720s weave. No remains. Their own handwriting on the cover sheet.

"You've been reading my paperwork," Gideon said.

"I've been reading *your* paperwork for thirty years, Mr. Halloran. Or rather, my family has." Crane settled behind the desk and did not offer a chair, so Gideon remained standing, glass in hand, positioned—deliberately—between the desk and the French doors. Old habit. Never let them get between you and the exit. "Sit, sit. You make me feel like a headmaster."

Gideon sat. The chair was too low by design; they'd seen the trick before in interviews, depositions, the small theatrics of power. They crossed their legs and let their spine do the work their dignity couldn't.

"The report is thorough," Crane said, tapping the folder once. "I'll grant you that. Stones in place of bones. Victorian linen, or linen that could pass for Victorian. A substitution made sometime in the last two hundred years, undetected, unrecorded." He leaned back. "Grave-robbers."

"Grave-robbers don't usually leave the valuables and steal the body."

"No. But Victorians were strange people. Resurrection men, body-snatchers, all that morbid trade. Who's to say what they wanted with a founder's corpse? Relics. Souvenirs. Some ghoul's private museum." Crane spread his hands, and the gesture was almost convincing—the shrug of a reasonable man confronted with an unreasonable world. "It happens. It happened. It's sad, and it's sordid, and it has nothing whatever to do with anyone living."

"And?"

"And I'd like you to write exactly that." He said it the way other men offered canapés. "A finding. Your finding, in your professional capacity, signed under cathedral seal. Substitution by unknown persons, circa eighteen-forties. Case closed."

Gideon looked at him for a long moment. Then they set down the whisky untasted and stood.

"Mr. Crane—"

"Before you refuse me, hear the rest." Something moved behind his face—not anger, nothing so legible. Patience. The patience of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice because the building itself raised it for him. "The tercentenary fund has an endowment line for archival preservation. Two hundred thousand pounds, released at the trust's discretion. There's a senior archivist post opening in the spring—your superior retires, and the dean will want a recommendation. And there's the matter of the crypt itself." He smiled thinly. "Access. Continued access. Which I understand matters to you more than any of the rest."

The room was very quiet. Out on the water, a gull cried once and gave up.

"You're offering me money to falsify a record," Gideon said.

"I'm offering you funding to publish a *finding*. Findings are interpretations, Mr. Halloran. You of all people know that. Every ledger entry is an interpretation. Every catalogue card. You choose what to emphasize, what to footnote, what to leave in the box." Crane rose and came around the desk, and up close he smelled of sandalwood and something older, something like a church that hadn't been aired in centuries. "I'm not asking you to lie. I'm asking you to stop digging where the ground belongs to other people."

"Whose ground is it?"

The question landed harder than Gideon had intended, and they watched it land—watched something flicker across Crane's face that was not quite surprise. He recovered in half a breath.

"Ashcombe's ground," he said. "Which is to say, the town's. Which is to say—" and here he gestured, one smooth sweep taking in the study, the terrace, the harbor beyond, the whole glittering arrangement of roofs and masts and ancient stone, "—ours. My family has kept this town for three hundred years, Mr. Halloran. Kept its accounts, kept its peace, kept its dead. That's not a boast. It's a burden. And burdens are easier to carry when everyone agrees to hold them together."

* * *

Later—much later, walking home along the quay with the tide out and the mud flats breathing their cold salt breath—Gideon would replay the evening and understand that the real conversation had happened somewhere else entirely. Not in the offer. Not in the refusal, which they had delivered carefully, professionally, in the language of procedure: *the matter requires further investigation; no finding can responsibly be issued at this time.*

No. The real conversation had happened in the forty seconds when Aldous Crane, having heard the refusal, simply nodded—nodded as if he'd expected it, perhaps even wanted it—and walked Gideon to the French doors and paused there, looking out at the darkening harbor.

"Do you know what my family did before we were mayors?" he'd asked.

"Shipping," Gideon said. "And land."

"Land." He tasted the word. "After 1724, mostly land. Do you know why 1724?"

"Edmund Vane died in the winter of '23–'24. His estate was settled the following year."

"His estate." Crane had laughed then, softly, a sound with no humor in it anywhere. "Yes. His estate. Everything changed hands that year, Mr. Halloran. Everything. And my ancestors were standing there with their hands open when it fell." He'd turned from the window, and for just a moment the mayor's polish had dropped away and Gideon had seen the thing underneath, the thing that had been waiting in that study for three hundred years. "Think about that. Think about what kind of man stands with his hands open while a founder's fortune falls into them. And then think about whether you really want to know what else those hands were doing that winter."

Then he'd shaken Gideon's hand again, dry and brief, and wished them good evening as if they'd discussed the weather.

* * *

The archive was cold when Gideon let themselves in at nine o'clock, but they didn't notice. They went straight past their own desk, past the sealed evidence boxes from the exhumation, past Wren Calloway's harbor maps still spread where she'd left them two days ago, to the deed room at the back—the oldest holdings, the ones nobody requested anymore because nobody alive cared who owned what in 1720.

They pulled the land registers for the parish and carried them to the reading table under the one good lamp.

1720. Gideon turned pages. The handwriting varied, clerk to clerk, but the names repeated in patterns even a stranger could read: Vane, Marlowe, Pennell-Quay, Ashcombe freeholders, plots measured in rods and furlongs along the harbor's edge. Edmund Vane's name appeared everywhere—wharf leases, warehouse deeds, the tide mill, the customs bond. A man who owned the town the way a spider owns a web.

Josiah Marlowe's name appeared nearly as often. Partner, neighbor, co-signer. The two men's hands appeared side by side on document after document, inked within days of each other, sometimes hours. Whatever else history had buried, these two had been close. Close enough to build a fortune together.

Gideon turned to 1724.

The register thinned. Winter entries were sparse—few transactions in the dead months—but the ones that existed told a story with terrible clarity. January: the estate of Josiah Marlowe, deceased, probate granted. February: the estate of Edmund Vane, deceased, probate granted. March through June: transfer after transfer after transfer. Marlowe holdings sold at auction. Vane holdings sold at auction. Wharfage, warehouses, the mill, the customs interest, parcels of the quayside that would be worth millions in three hundred years—all of it moving, all of it flowing toward a handful of buyers whose names Gideon read with a coldness gathering in their chest.

Pennell. Quayle. And Crane. Crane, Crane, Crane. On the wharf leases. On the warehouse deeds. On the tide mill. On the customs bond. On the parcel of marshland that would become Crane House's gardens. On the burial ground easement for the cathedral crypt itself—*one shilling per annum, in perpetuity, to the family of Nathaniel Crane, for maintenance of the founders' vault.*

The Cranes owned the grave. Had always owned the grave. Had collected rent on Edmund Vane's resting place for three hundred years, one shilling at a time, a quiet annuity paid by the cathedral into the pocket of the family now asking Gideon to declare that resting place empty and blame the Victorians.

Gideon sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere overhead, a pipe ticked as the heating cycled.

Every land deed after 1724. That was what Crane had said, and Crane had undersold it. It wasn't just that the Cranes' name appeared on every deed—it was that the deeds only *began* in 1724. Before that winter, the harbor belonged to Vane and Marlowe. After it, it belonged to everyone else. Two men die—one of them the revered founder, one of them barely a footnote—and in six months the entire economic geography of Ashcombe changes hands.

Fortunes didn't fall into open hands that fast unless someone was pushing them.

Gideon thought of the empty coffin. Of river stones wrapped in careful linen, placed by hands that had known the tomb would be visited, venerated, checked—placed well enough to fool three centuries of mourners. Whoever staged that death had been intelligent, patient, and close. Close enough to dress a coffin. Close enough to control the burial.

Close enough to inherit.

They thought, too, of Aldous Crane's voice: *stop digging where the ground belongs to other people.* They had taken it as a threat. Now, alone in the lamplight with the registers open around them like witnesses, Gideon understood it was something worse than a threat.

It was a confession of motive, three hundred years old, delivered by a man who didn't know he was making it.

Gideon reached for a fresh card and began, in their own tight archival shorthand, to write. *Vane estate dispersed Mar–Jun 1724. Primary acquirer: N. Crane et al. Crypt easement held by Crane family, 1724–present. Query: who certified deaths? Who prepared bodies? Who profited by sequence?*

The pen paused over that last word. *Sequence.* Josiah Marlowe died first—in January, according to the probate. Edmund Vane died second, weeks later. But the letters—if Wren was right, if the woman she was chasing through the harbor mud was right—told a different order entirely. A flight. A pursuit. Only one of the three coming back.

One of the three. Gideon wrote the phrase and stared at it. Three people had gone into that winter. Three coffins' worth of story. And the town had buried two of them with full honors while the third—

The third was the woman. Mercy Marlowe. Vanished, unmourned, unmarked, remembered only as a witch.

Gideon closed the register gently, the way they closed all books, even the ones that frightened them. But their hands weren't steady, and they noticed, and they hated noticing.

Across the archive, on the long table, Wren Calloway's survey maps lay where she'd left them. The anomaly beneath the old quay stones was marked in red pencil, circled twice, annotated in her quick slanted hand: *deliberate deposit — depth consistent with pre-1730 fill — needs diving permit — NEEDS EYES.*

Gideon had resisted her. Duty said report; instinct said dig; and Gideon had spent eleven years letting duty win, because duty was safe and instinct got archivists fired.

But duty, as of tonight, had a price list. Duty had an endowment figure and a promotion and a dean's ear attached to it. Duty had been in Aldous Crane's study drinking his whisky and admiring his view.

Gideon stood, took the lamp off the reading table, and carried it across the room to Wren's maps.

Outside, far below the cliff, the tide was turning. It would come back up the harbor in the dark, patient as it had always been, lifting the boats, pressing at the old quay stones, keeping what it kept.

Three hundred years was a long time to hold a secret. But tides, Gideon thought, switching off the lamp at last, were patient in the other direction too.

Tomorrow they would find Wren Calloway.
