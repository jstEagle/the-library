## 7. M Is for Marlowe

Wren Calloway had grown up on stories of the witch of Ashcombe.

Not the sanitized kind they told at the heritage festival—no pointed hat, no broomstick—but the kind her grandmother told in a low voice over mending, when the fire was down to embers and the wind came off the harbor hard enough to rattle the shutters. *Mercy Marlowe*, Gran would say, threading a needle without looking at it. *They said she could call the tide. They said she drowned three fishermen with a word because they'd seen something they shouldn't have.* A pause, the needle flashing. *And then they burned her name off every record in town and called it mercy.*

Wren had thought, for most of her life, that it was just that—a story. The kind coastal towns grow like barnacles, layering themselves over whatever's inconvenient underneath.

She was standing in Gideon Halloran's archive now, holding a letter signed *E.V.*, and the story was looking back at her from two hundred and eighty years away.

"Read it again," she said. "The last line."

Gideon didn't move. They stood by the false-backed panel with their arms crossed, wearing the particular stillness Wren had learned to read as discomfort—the archivist equivalent of a cat presenting its belly and daring you to touch it. But after a moment they lifted the letter, angling it toward the lamp, and read aloud.

"'If this reaches you, M, know that I have sold the Harrow shares and booked passage under a name that is not mine. Tell no one—not your maid, not your confessor, not the sea itself. I will wait through the dark of the moon. If you do not come, I will go alone, and I will spend what remains of my life being sorry.'" A pause. "'Yours entirely, against all sense and all salvation, E.V.'"

*The dark of the moon.* Wren felt the words settle into her like cold water into ballast. "That's not a metaphor. That's navigation. You don't run a boat out of a harbor past customs eyes except on the darkest night of the month." She pulled the letter gently from Gideon's fingers—Gideon let go with the reluctance of someone surrendering an organ—and held it closer to the lamp. The paper was good stock, foxed at the edges, folded into eighths and refolded so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth. "He wasn't writing poetry. He was writing a schedule."

"You sound very certain."

"I grew up on tide tables instead of bedtime stories." She set the letter down on the table between them, next to its seven siblings, arranged in the careful order Gideon had imposed: earliest to latest, each in its mylar sleeve, each one another degree of impossible. "Whoever M was, she knew boats. Or she knew someone who did."

"And you think you know who M was."

It wasn't quite a question. Gideon had spent the evening circling it the way they circled everything—obliquely, hands in pockets, never landing. Wren had watched them do it all week: with the mayor, with the dean, with her. Ask nothing directly, commit to nothing, keep every door on its latch. It should have been infuriating. Instead she found herself wanting to pry the latch open just to see what they kept behind it.

"I think," Wren said, "you already suspect, and you want me to say it first so it isn't yours."

Something flickered across Gideon's face—annoyance, or maybe respect, possibly both. "Say it, then."

"Mercy Marlowe."

The name dropped into the room and sat there. Outside the archive window, the cathedral close was quiet, lamps burning amber along the gravel paths, the great tower cutting a black wedge out of the stars. Somewhere a door slammed; the verger locking up, probably. Wren realized her heart was going faster than the moment strictly required.

"Josiah Marlowe's wife," Gideon said. "Vane's business partner."

"Vane's *young* business partner's wife. Mercy Hale before she married him—she was nineteen, he was thirty-four. There's a marriage settlement in the county records; I pulled it last spring when I was doing provenance work on the harbor sediments and got distracted." Wren had gotten distracted a lot, that spring. The archive's land records were a swamp and she'd waded in happily. "And here's the part that matters, Halloran. Josiah Marlowe disappears from the shipping ledgers in the winter of 1724. Not dies—disappears. No burial fee, no funeral sermon in the parish book, nothing. And Edmund Vane, who was hale as an ox according to every account, is suddenly dead and in the ground within a fortnight. And Mercy Marlowe—" Wren spread her hands. "Mercy Marlowe vanishes the same season. No death record. No departure record. No remarriage. She's twenty-three years old and one of the wealthiest women on this coast, and she simply stops existing."

Gideon had gone very still. It was their gift, Wren was learning—that stillness. Most people fidgeted when they were thinking; Gideon seemed to pour all their motion inward until they were practically a monument. "The register page," they said quietly. "The razored one. From the interment volume."

"Which would be Vane's burial."

"Which would be Vane's burial." Gideon turned toward the shelf where the interment registers lived, but didn't reach for them. "You've been in these records before. Before the coffin was opened."

"I told you. Sediment cores. Provenance chains. The harbor authority wanted a baseline survey and half the deeds along the quay trace back to the Vane trust, so." She shrugged. "I read everything. It's a disease."

"It's a useful disease." Gideon finally took down the register—the big folio with the broken clasp, the one whose December pages ended in a ragged stub where a blade had done its work three centuries ago. They laid it open on the table beside the letters, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Two documents, side by side: the public lie with its wound, and the private truth folded into eighths.

"So we have a founder who staged his own death," Wren said slowly, working it through, feeling it take shape the way a wreck takes shape out of murk as you descend. "Letters planning a flight. A woman who vanishes the same winter. A partner who also vanishes. And a town that has spent three hundred years telling everyone the founder died beloved in his bed and his partner died—what? At sea? Where does the legend put Josiah?"

"Nowhere. That's the thing." Gideon's voice had dropped into the register Wren privately called the ledger voice—flat, precise, each fact laid down like a coin. "I went back through the sermons this afternoon. Josiah Marlowe gets one memorial mention, in 1725, a line about 'our brother lost upon the waters.' No date. No grave. And Mercy Marlowe gets nothing at all. Except—"

"Except the stories."

Gideon looked at her.

"The witch stories." Wren laughed, but there was no humor in it; it came out more like a breath knocked loose. "God. It's been under our feet the whole time. She doesn't vanish, so the town gives her somewhere else to go. She becomes the woman who called the tide. The woman who drowned the fishermen. Every town needs somewhere to put the women it can't explain, and Ashcombe put hers in the water." She pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eyes. "My grandmother used to say Mercy could call the tide. I thought it was nonsense. It's not nonsense—it's a *record*. It's the only record anyone was allowed to keep."

*

Her grandmother's house sat at the top of Fore Street, the last one before the road gave up and became the coast path, and it had been leaning into the southwest wind for so long that the whole structure seemed permanently braced, shoulders hunched, waiting for the blow to come. Wren had grown up in that house in the summers, in the attic room with the salt-clouded window, sleeping under a quilt stitched from sailcloth scraps while the gale walked the ridge tiles like something restless.

Gran was ninety-one now and mostly confined to the front room, but her mind was a harbor chart with every sounding marked, and when Wren came up the path that evening, letting herself in with the key she'd had since she was twelve, the old woman was awake and waiting as if she'd known.

"You've the look," Gran said, before Wren had her coat fully off. She was in her chair by the window, blanket over her knees, hands folded on top like a closed book. "Your mother had it too, when she was digging into things. The look of someone carrying news in wet paper."

"Hello to you too."

"Don't hello me. Sit down. Kettle's not long boiled." Gran's eyes followed her across the room, sharp and pale as gull's eggs. "Is it the coffin? Everyone in town's talking of nothing else. Empty as a Sunday collection plate, they say."

"That's part of it." Wren sat on the sofa opposite, leaning forward, elbows on knees. This was the conversation she'd been rehearsing in the car and abandoning in equal measure. "Gran. What did you actually know about Mercy Marlowe? Not the festival version. The real one."

The change in her grandmother's face was small and total. The sharpness didn't leave, but it went underground, the way light goes under water—still present, refracted, dangerous to navigate by. For a long moment the only sound was the clock on the mantel and the wind worrying the chimney.

"Who's been asking?" Gran said at last.

"Gideon Halloran. The archivist. We're—we're working together on the exhumation question."

"Halloran." Gran tested the name like a coin bitten for gold. "Old Missus Calloway worked for them, you know. In the crypt. Back in the fifties, cleaning brasses, dusting the tombs. Told me once she found a door in the crypt wall that wasn't on any plan, and when she asked the verger about it, she was told she'd dreamed it, and a fortnight later she was let go. 'Dreamed it,'" Gran repeated softly. "That family could make you doubt your own eyes, and they called it keeping the peace."

"Whose family?"

"The same one that's always kept the peace here." Gran unfolded her hands, refolded them. "But that's not what you came to ask. You came to ask about Mercy."

"Yes."

Another silence. Outside, the light was failing; the harbor below had gone pewter-colored, the moored boats knocking gently at their moorings like patient animals. When Gran spoke again, her voice had shifted into the low storytelling register Wren had heard perhaps five times in her life—the voice she saved for the dead.

"My grandmother's grandmother was housekeeper to the Marlowe household. Mercy's household. This would have been 1723, 1724—the last year of it. The girl's name was Prudence Calloway, and she was fifteen when she went into service there, and she lived to be eighty, and she talked, near the end, when her mind was going soft and nobody bothered to silence her anymore." Gran's mouth curved slightly. "Mind goes soft and the truth comes out, in my experience. All the locks fail at once."

"What did Prudence say?"

"She said Mrs. Marlowe was the kindest mistress on the coast and the loneliest. Said her husband was a hard man with a loud voice and a temper he wore like a coat. Said—" Gran paused, choosing, and Wren understood she was choosing carefully, deciding how much of three centuries of guarded inheritance to hand over in a single evening. "Said that in the autumn of 1724, Mrs. Marlowe gave her a bundle to carry down to the quayside at night, wrapped in oilcloth, and told her if anyone asked, she'd dreamed it. And Prudence carried it, and saw a man waiting by a boat—one man, tall, hooded—and she went home and said nothing, because saying nothing was how you stayed alive in Ashcombe even then."

Wren's skin had gone tight. "A bundle. What kind of bundle?"

"Papers, likely. Keepsakes. Things you can't bear to burn and can't bear to leave." Gran's eyes fixed on her, unblinking. "And here is the rest of it, child, and you'll mind how you use it. Prudence said Mrs. Marlowe left on the dark of the moon that November. Walked out of the house with one small case while her husband was at his warehouse, and was never seen in Ashcombe again. And the town said she'd been taken by the devil, and then they said she'd been a witch, and then they stopped saying anything at all, which is what this town does best." The old woman's hands tightened on each other. "But Prudence said—Prudence, who was there—said Mrs. Marlowe was smiling when she went. Smiling like a woman walking out of a burning house. And Prudence said she'd made the mistress promise one thing before she left."

Wren realized she was barely breathing. "What promise?"

"That if she ever could come back safely, she would. That Ashcombe was hers too—born and bred—and she shouldn't have to be a ghost in her own town." Gran looked out the window at the darkening harbor. "She never came back. Whatever happened to her out there, it happened fast, and it happened final. But somebody in this family has kept that promise alive for three hundred years, Wren. Kept the story alive. Kept the *name* alive, when every record in this town was cut clean. My grandmother told me. Her grandmother told her. Right down the line, woman to woman, quiet as breathing: *remember Mercy Marlowe, and remember she was no witch. Remember she was running.*"

The clock ticked. A boat knocked against its mooring. Wren found that her eyes were hot and her throat was tight, and she couldn't have said exactly why—except that she could. Because history had just reached up out of the archive and taken hold of her by the wrist. Because the story she'd been raised on as superstition was testimony, smuggled down through eight generations of women who'd understood exactly what it cost to remember out loud.

"Gran," she said. "There are letters. Vane's letters. Hidden in the archive—they were planning to run together, him and Mercy. He wrote to her about tides and passages and selling his shares. He signed himself *yours entirely, against all sense and all salvation.*"

For the first time that evening, Gran smiled—a real smile, brief and fierce, transforming her whole face into the woman she must have been at twenty. "Against all sense and all salvation," she repeated. "Well. That's honest, at least. Most men write prettier and mean less." Then the smile faded into something graver. "You be careful with this, Wren. You and your Halloran both. The people who razed those pages weren't fools, and their grandchildren are still sitting in the front pews. Prudence Calloway kept silent sixty years, and she only spoke when her mind was going, because she knew what speaking cost. You understand me?"

"I understand you."

"Do you? Because understanding and agreeing aren't the same animal, and you always were your mother's girl." Gran reached out and caught Wren's hand, her grip still shockingly strong, dry and warm as old rope. "Three hundred years this town has slept easy on a lie. Some sleeps are worth breaking. But you want to be sure, before you start shaking the bed, that you're ready for what wakes up."

*

Wren walked back down Fore Street in full dark, the harbor lights strung out below her like a reflection of the sky, and her mind was moving faster than her feet.

It fit. God, how it fit. The letters' timetable matched Prudence's testimony to the week—the dark of the moon, November 1724. The bundle of papers Prudence carried to the quay. The man in the hood, waiting by the boat: Edmund Vane, alive, three weeks before the town buried him. And Mercy Marlowe, born-and-bred daughter of Ashcombe, walking out of her own life smiling, promised she could someday come home.

She never came home. Which meant something had gone wrong between the quay and wherever the boat was bound. Something that swallowed a founder, a wife, and a business partner, and left a town to invent witches to cover the hole.

And something, Wren thought, stopping at the rail above the old quay, looking down at the black water slapping against stones that had stood there since before any of it happened—

Something might still be down there.

She thought of the survey anomaly beneath the quay foundation. The mass that didn't belong. She'd flagged it months ago as probable ballast, probable rubble, probable nothing, and filed it, because filing things was how you stayed safe in Ashcombe too, apparently, and the habit ran deeper than she'd known.

Tomorrow she'd pull the survey charts. Tomorrow she'd show Gideon.

Tonight she stood at the rail a moment longer, over the water that had taken—or been given—three lives, and said, quietly, to the dark and the tide and the woman who'd once been promised she could return:

"We're coming. Hold on."

The tide slid in against the stones, soft and ceaseless, like something answering.
