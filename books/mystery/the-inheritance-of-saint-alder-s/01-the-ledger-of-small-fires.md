## 1. The Ledger of Small Fires

The taxi would go no farther than the gate.

"Road's washed out past here," the driver said, not looking at Emrys so much as at the wall of fog beyond it, as though the fog might be listening. "You want I should come back Thursday?"

"Friday," Emrys said. "I'll need two days minimum."

The driver took the cash without counting it — a small courtesy that told Emrys he was being paid to forget this fare — and reversed down the gravel lane until the taillights dissolved into the grey. Then there was only Emrys Almeida, a leather satchel of claim forms, a camera he wouldn't strictly need, and the long walk up to Saint Alder's.

He had read the file twice on the train north and once more in the motel parking lot at dawn, which meant he had read it three times, which meant he had it. Meridian Coastal Insurance, policy number 44-7719-C, insured property: Saint Alder's Sanatorium and grounds, forty-one acres, structure erected 1911, decommissioned 1998, held in trust by the Voss family estate since. Nature of loss: storm damage, February 14th — wind gusts to ninety miles an hour, roof failure over the east wing, flooding in the lower corridors. Estimated claim: substantial. Assigned adjuster: E. Almeida, senior investigator, Portland office, with a note from his supervisor that Emrys could hear in the man's voice even in ink: *Take your time with this one. The family has friends.*

Everyone had friends. That was usually the first thing Emrys wrote in the margin of a suspicious file, in his own private shorthand: *F = ∞.* Friends equaled infinity. It was never a good sign.

The road climbed through black pines whose branches had been combed flat by thirty years of sea wind, and then the trees stopped all at once, as if cut by a blade, and Saint Alder's stood against the sky.

It was larger than photographs suggested. Photographs always lied about size; they flattened grandeur into quaintness. But standing before it, Emrys felt the building the way you feel a large animal nearby — not seeing so much as sensing the mass of it, the four stories of granite and slate rising out of the fog like something surfacing. The central block was almost handsome still, its arched windows tall and regular, its stone the color of old bone. The wings were another matter. The east wing wore its wound openly: a gash of torn roofing, tar paper flapping like skin, rain-stained brick beneath. The west wing had simply been left to die decades ago, its windows boarded or broken, ivy pulling at the mortar with the patience of something that intended to win.

A bronze plaque beside the double doors read:

**SAINT ALDER'S SANATORIUM FOR THE RECOVERY OF THE LUNGS AND SPIRIT**
*Founded 1911 by the families of Alder Bay*
*"The Sea Gives Back What It Takes"*

Emrys stood before the plaque for a full minute, reading it again, because his memory did not work the way other people's did and he had learned young that the only way to manage it was to feed it deliberately, cleanly, once, rather than let it feed on everything indiscriminately. He looked at the motto. *The sea gives back what it takes.* He filed it away and immediately disliked it. Mottos were promises, and promises made in granite by founding families were the kind of promises that came due with interest.

He tried the front doors. Locked, as expected. He walked the perimeter instead, because the perimeter always told him more than the parlor.

The building measured roughly three hundred feet along its seaward face. He counted window bays as he went — twenty-two on the ground floor of the main block, fourteen in the east wing, eleven in the west, three of those bricked over entirely. There had been an addition at some point, a low-slung annex of 1950s cinderblock tacked onto the north side, connected by a glass corridor that had shattered in the storm and now lay across the lawn in a spill of green diamonds. A service entrance on the landward side stood ajar, its padlock hanging open on a hasp, and Emrys noted that the rust around the lock was bright and fresh where the shackle had recently scraped it. Someone had opened this door within the last few weeks. Possibly the storm. Possibly not.

He photographed what needed photographing — the roof breach from three angles, the flooded lower corridor through a cellar grate, the collapsed chimney stack — and then he went inside through the service door, because the claim could not be assessed from the lawn, and because he wanted to see how the building smelled.

Buildings remembered things their owners forgot. Smoke especially. Fire especially. Emrys had spent nineteen years walking into rooms where bad things had happened, and in nineteen years he had never once been wrong about the smell of a fire that had been cleaned up too well. Paint and plaster could cover scorch; insurance settlements could cover arson; whole towns could cover murder. But the smell lived in the wood, in the mortar, in the grain of the floorboards, patient as sediment.

Saint Alder's smelled of salt rot and pigeon droppings and the sweet chemical ghost of old disinfectant — carbolic, he thought, or Lysol of some pre-1980 formulation — and beneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, in the corridor leading toward the east wing: smoke. Old smoke. Decades old. Scrubbed and painted over and officially forgotten, but present, the way a scar is present under a sleeve.

Emrys stood in the dim corridor and breathed it in and said nothing, to no one, which was his habit when the facts began arranging themselves into shapes he didn't yet have permission to see.

*Professional detachment,* he reminded himself. *You are here for a roof.*

* * *

The interior was a museum of interrupted time.

He worked methodically, room by room, wing by wing, because the claim required a full inventory of damaged contents and because method was how he kept the other thing — the memory, the total, merciless recall — useful instead of drowning him. Every room he entered, he entered once. He did not take notes beyond the barest checklist; notes were for men who could forget. What he saw, he saw permanently: the wheelchairs stacked in the hydrotherapy room like a sculpture called *Abandonment*, seven of them, chrome gone brown. The mural in the children's ward, a painted seascape with smiling dolphins, water-stained now so that the dolphins seemed to be dissolving upward into the ceiling. The dispensary, its shelves still holding amber bottles with paper labels gone soft as cloth. The records room.

The records room stopped him longer than the rest.

It occupied the ground floor of the east wing — the wounded wing, the wing under the breached roof — and it had taken water. Not catastrophically; the flood line ran six inches up the shelving, and someone, years ago, had moved the most important-looking boxes to higher shelves, leaving the lower rows to soak and swell and grow a fine grey bloom of mold. Patient files, presumably. Admissions ledgers. The administrative sediment of a place that had housed, by his rough arithmetic from bed counts and occupancy eras, somewhere between four and six thousand human convalescences over eighty-seven years.

Four to six thousand people had come here to be healed by the sea air, and the sea had eventually come in after them, patient, thorough, keeping its own records.

Emrys catalogued the damage for the report — approximately one hundred and forty linear feet of archival material, water- and mold-compromised, est. replacement value negligible, est. evidentiary value — and there his pen paused above the form, because *evidentiary value* was not a field on a storm damage claim, and he had no idea why his hand had reached for it.

He finished the room. He photographed the flood line. He checked the far corner behind the last bank of shelving, where the water damage deepened and the light from his flashlight gave out into shadow, and that was where he found the filing cabinet.

It was a standard four-drawer steel cabinet, government surplus by the look of it, and it had been pulled a foot from the wall — probably during whatever hasty evacuation of records had happened years ago — and never pushed back. In the gap between cabinet and wall, wedged vertically into the dark, was a book.

Not a ledger. Not a box of files. A book with a soft black cover, the kind sold in stationery shops to adolescents who intended to keep secrets.

Emrys crouched, worked it free with two fingers, and held it up to the flashlight.

The cover was furred with damp and dust, but the spine had been protected by the angle of its hiding, and on the first page, in blue ballpoint, in handwriting that slanted forward like someone always slightly late for something, was written:

*Property of Lena Voss. If found, yes, you may read it. I hide things where no one looks because everyone is looking everywhere else.*

Emrys Almeida had processed eleven thousand claims in nineteen years. He knew every species of object that turned up in dead buildings: love letters, ledgers, liquor bottles, once a human femur, once a shoebox of ninety-four milk teeth. He knew the physics of abandoned places, the way objects migrated and settled, the way anything left loose ended up either destroyed or hidden by someone who meant to come back.

This book had not migrated. It had been placed — wedged carefully, deliberately, into a gap exactly the width of its spine, in a position where anyone doing a routine inventory would have to kneel and reach into the dark to find it. Someone had hidden it well enough to survive casual discovery and findable enough to survive forever.

And the name on the flyleaf was Voss.

He should have logged it as found property, photographed it, bagged it, added it to the schedule of recovered items, and turned it over to the executor of the estate. That was procedure. Procedure was the load-bearing wall of his entire career, the thing that kept a man with a perfect memory honest when honesty got expensive.

Emrys put the diary inside his coat, against his chest, and told himself he would examine it first — just to determine whether it was relevant to the claim — and turn it over afterward.

He was already lying to himself, and he noticed that he noticed, and he did it anyway.

* * *

By late afternoon the fog had thickened into something nearly solid, and Emrys worked the upper floors by flashlight, his footsteps raising small ghosts of dust in the wards. The east wing's third floor was directly beneath the roof breach, and here the storm had done its real work: rain had poured through the gash for two days before anyone came, and the ceiling of the old solarium had come down in slabs of wet plaster, burying wicker chaise longues under white drifts. Through the broken roof he could see the sky itself, low and moving, and beyond it the sound of the sea working at the rocks below the bluff, tireless, indifferent, giving back nothing.

He stood in the ruined solarium and let the building talk.

Sanatoriums had a grammar. He'd assessed two others in his career, both converted, both gentrified, and both had retained the same bones: the wide corridors built for gurneys, the south-facing sun rooms, the small sealed rooms at the ends of the wings that were never labeled on any floor plan and were never, ever discussed. Isolation rooms. Restraint rooms. Rooms whose names changed with each decade — *the quiet ward*, *the observation suite* — but whose function stayed constant, which was to contain the patients the institution could not otherwise contain.

At the end of the east wing's third-floor corridor, behind a door with a rusted iron hasp and no handle, was such a room. And set into that door, at the height of a seated person's shoulder, was a hatch — a feeding slot, a pass-through, twelve inches by eight, with a sliding steel cover.

Emrys looked at the hatch for a long time.

Then he looked at the floor outside the door, where the old linoleum had rotted through in patches, exposing the boards beneath. And on those boards, in the wood itself, he saw them: four shallow rectangles, darker than the surrounding grain, evenly spaced, where something heavy had stood for a very long time and been removed. Bed bolts. The ghosts of four beds, arranged outside the locked room, or — he corrected himself, measuring the spacing with his eyes, memorizing the measurements whether he wished to or not — arranged *inside* it, visible now only because the threshold board had warped and lifted.

Four beds. One room. A room with a feeding hatch and no interior handle.

He wrote nothing down. He took no photograph. Some part of him understood, with the cold clarity that nineteen years of this work had installed like a pacemaker, that certain observations were safer as memory than as evidence — safer for now, at least, until he understood what kind of town he was standing in.

The fog pressed against the broken roof. Somewhere below, in a building that should have been empty, a door moved — a slow, groaning swing and click, wind through some unlatched frame, almost certainly, certainly, almost certainly — and Emrys Almeida stood alone in the solarium of Saint Alder's with a stolen diary against his ribs and the taste of old smoke at the back of his throat, and waited, counting seconds, until the building settled back into silence.

* * *

He walked down in the last of the light.

The diary rode inside his coat the whole way, and he was aware of it the way you're aware of a heartbeat — constantly, faintly, increasingly. At the ground floor he passed the records room again and made himself stop at the doorway, made himself look at the filing cabinet and the gap behind it, made himself hold the image steady: the cabinet's position relative to the shelving, the water stain on the wall, the depth of the gap, the absence of any other loose object in the vicinity. If the diary's owner ever came looking for it, he wanted to know exactly what scene she would find altered.

*If.* The word sat strangely. He didn't know yet why the diary had been hidden rather than carried out, why a woman would entrust her secrets to a gap behind a filing cabinet in a drowned building unless she expected to come back for it — or unless she hadn't expected anything at all.

Outside, the fog had eaten the world down to fifty feet. His headlights carved a tunnel through it as the rental car wound down toward the gate, and at the bottom of the drive, where the road met the coast highway, he passed a man walking up — an older gentleman in a waxed coat and a flat cap, silver-haired, upright, taking the evening air with the unhurried confidence of someone who owned the weather. The man raised a hand in courteous greeting as they passed, and Emrys, meeting his eyes for half a second in the wash of the headlights, thought: *That face will matter later.* He didn't know why. He filed it anyway, next to the four bed-bolt shadows and the smell of smoke and the motto in bronze, in the growing ledger of small fires that Saint Alder's was quietly keeping inside him.

Back at the motel, he hung his wet coat in the bathroom, boiled the kettle, and set the diary on the desk beneath the lamp.

Black cover. Blue ink. Slanted, hurried, intelligent handwriting. *Lena Voss.*

He washed his hands first — an odd ritual, but he'd learned it from a coroner in Bellingham who claimed you handled the dead differently with clean hands, and Emrys had come to believe that documents could be dead too — and then he sat down, opened the diary to the first entry, and began to read.

The early pages were ordinary and alive: complaints about the damp, jokes about the pigeons, careful professional notes about mislabeled boxes in the archive, a running argument with her aunt Marguerite about funding and the future of the collection. She had a sharp eye and a sharper pen. She catalogued the sanatorium the way he did — bay by bay, room by room — and reading her was like reading a letter from a colleague he'd never met, one who noticed the feeding hatch on the chapel ward door on March 3rd and wrote, in the margin, in smaller letters: *Why does a chapel need a lock like that?*

He read for two hours. The entries marched forward through spring, through summer, growing denser, the margins filling with names and dates and small arrows connecting them — patient files, death certificates, discrepancies. On the final dated entry, September 9th, her handwriting was faster, the sentences shorter, the arrows thicker:

*Edwin knows I've pulled the '94 files. I can feel it. Tomorrow I'm going to copy everything and hide the originals where the water can't reach them. If anything happens to me, it wasn't the sea.*

Emrys turned the page.

The next page was blank. So was the one after. He thumbed forward through the remaining leaves — blank, blank, blank — and closed the book, and sat for a while with his hands flat on the cover, listening to the motel heater tick.

September 9th. He knew the date the way he knew all dates, instantly and irreversibly, cross-referenced against the file he'd read three times on the train: Lena Voss, estate archivist, reported missing by her aunt on September 11th, two days after that final entry. Missing now for over a year. Presumed drowned; no body recovered. The sea gives back what it takes.

Except.

Except that as he'd skimmed the last pages, his eyes — his cursed, flawless, unasked-for eyes — had snagged on something at the bottom of the September 9th entry, something he'd registered without registering, the way you register a wrong note in a familiar song. A detail. A physical detail. Something about the light, or the weather, or the state of the building, that did not belong to September of last year at all.

Something that belonged, if he was not mistaken — and he was never mistaken about details, that was the whole tragedy of him — to the afternoon he had just spent walking through those halls himself.

Emrys opened the diary again, turned to the final entry, and read the last line slowly, letting each word fix itself into permanent place like a nail driven home:

*The adjuster arrived today. Young, polite, remembers everything. I watched him from the west window. He'll do.*
