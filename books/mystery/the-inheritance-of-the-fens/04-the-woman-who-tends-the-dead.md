## 4. The Woman Who Tends the Dead

The cemetery at Marlowe Fen had two kinds of silence. There was the ordinary kind—the hush of a place where people were polite enough to keep their voices down even when no one was left to hear—and then there was the other kind, the fen's own silence, which was less an absence of sound than a presence of listening. Quentin Halloran had spent three nights learning the difference, wedged into the lee of a leaning headstone marked *BELOVED HUSBAND AND FATHER, GONE TO HIS REWARD*, which he had privately begun to think of as his landlord.

He'd brought a thermos of coffee that had gone the way of all thermos coffee—lukewarm and faintly bitter, like an apology from someone who didn't mean it—and a pair of binoculars he'd bought secondhand from a man in Ely who swore they'd once belonged to a birdwatcher of some renown. "He tracked a bittern for eleven days," the man said. "Never saw it. Best fortnight of his life." Quentin felt this boded well.

Three nights, and the woman in the green coat had not come.

But the flowers had.

That was the thing that kept him coming back, folding his long frame into the cold gap between the stones with a patience that would have astonished his colleagues at dispatch, if Quentin had colleagues in any sense that involved them knowing he existed. Every morning at some hour before dawn, fresh flowers appeared on the grave of Ada Voss. Not shop flowers—never shop flowers. These were cut with scissors, you could see it in the stems, snipped at an angle by someone who knew what they were doing. Snowdrops first, then the last stubborn chrysanthemums of the season, and this morning a single stem of winter jasmine, yellow as a struck match against the grey sky.

Eleven years dead, and somebody still cut her flowers by hand.

Quentin had spent his adult life making himself unnoticeable—a talent so complete that his own supervisor once walked past him in the corridor and asked the air whether anyone had seen Halloran—and he knew better than most that being unseen was not the same as being unseeing. Whoever tended Ada Voss's grave moved through this churchyard the way he moved through the world: carefully, habitually, with the practiced economy of someone who had long ago stopped expecting to be found.

Which meant she—if it was a she; the jasmine suggested a she, though Quentin admitted this was hardly forensic—knew how to vanish.

And yet she came back. Night after night, or rather dawn after dawn, to a grave that should have held nothing but her grief. That was the part that snagged in him like a fishhook in a sweater. You did not keep visiting a grave out of guilt. Guilt burned hot and fast and then went looking for easier company. You kept visiting out of love, or out of hope, or—

Or because the grave was empty, said a voice in his head that sounded uncomfortably like the letter-writer.

*You watch everything, Mr. Halloran. It's your one virtue.*

"Shut up," Quentin told the voice, quietly, because even alone in a cemetery at four in the morning he was a man who kept his voice down.

The cold crept up through the ground and into his knees. He shifted, and his landlord stone—Gone To His Reward—shed a crust of frost onto his shoulder like a disapproving elder. Somewhere out across the fens, a curlew called, that rising, falling note that sounded less like birdsong than like a question nobody wanted answered.

And then, between one curlew call and the next, the churchyard gate clicked.

Quentin went very still. Stillness was his native language; he'd been fluent since childhood, since the night on the fen road he did not let himself think about, the night that lived in him like a splinter too deep to grip. He eased one eye past the edge of his stone.

A woman stood at the gate.

She wore a green coat—dark green, the green of deep water, of bottle glass—and she carried something in one hand: a paper-wrapped bundle, flowers no doubt, and in the other a small trowel. The moon was down but the sky had that pre-dawn translucence, the world rendered in charcoal and silver, and she moved through it without hurrying, without looking around, straight down the path toward the grave he'd been watching for three nights, as though the path were her own hallway and the dead her neighbors.

Quentin's heart performed a maneuver he could only describe as unprofessional.

She knelt at the grave. Even at this distance he could see the care in it—the way she turned back the soil around the headstone with the trowel, light and quick, the way she laid the new flowers over the old ones and gathered the spent stems into her apron pocket, leaving nothing behind but freshness. Tidying. Tending. A ritual worn smooth as a river stone by eleven years of repetition.

Then she paused. Her head lifted—not toward him, he was sure of it, *sure*, but toward the fen itself, east, where the sky was beginning to think about grey. She stayed that way a moment, listening to whatever the fens say to people who listen back, and then she rose, brushed her knees, and walked on.

Not back toward the gate. On. Deeper into the churchyard, past row after row of the Marlowe dead, toward the yew tree at the far corner where the ground dipped toward the drainage ditch and the old lych-gate nobody used anymore.

Quentin gave her ten seconds. Fifteen. His whole body was a held breath. Then he unfolded himself from behind Gone To His Reward—apologizing silently to the man, whose reward had apparently included hosting a stakeout—and followed.

He was not built for pursuit. He knew this. He was built for booths and ledgers and the patient arithmetic of other people's mistakes. But he moved as quietly as a man of his dimensions could, along the path, past the Hollis family plot, past a stone so weathered its name had surrendered entirely to moss, around the flank of the yew—

—and the churchyard was empty.

Not empty-empty. The dead remained, as they do. But the woman in the green coat had gone the way of the bittern: completely, professionally, insultingly. The lych-gate hung open on its one surviving hinge, creaking gently, and beyond it the ditch path ran off into the reeds in both directions, and there was no sign of her anywhere except, absurdly, a single pressed footprint in the soft ground by the gate. Small. Neat. Pointing away.

Quentin stood in the cold and felt something he hadn't felt in years and couldn't immediately name. It took him a moment to recognize it, because it was so unfamiliar: hope. Grief-tinged, badly bruised hope, the kind that hurts more than despair because despair at least has the decency to be final.

Ada Voss might be alive.

* * *

The vicar of Marlowe Fen was named Reverend Osgood, and he was the sort of clergyman who seemed to have been assembled from spare parts of several kinder centuries. He was seventy if he was a day, with white hair that stood up as though permanently startled by his own sermons, and when Quentin presented himself at the vicarage door at nine o'clock that morning—having slept two hours in his car and eaten a service-station sandwich whose ingredients read like a legal disclaimer—Osgood looked at him with mild blue eyes and said:

"You're the one who's been sleeping in my churchyard."

"I—" said Quentin.

"Don't trouble yourself," the vicar said, stepping aside to admit him. "I've buried forty years of parishioners. I know a man keeping vigil when I see one. Though most vigils are shorter than yours, and most come with more crying." He paused. "Tea?"

"Yes," said Quentin, with feeling.

The vicarage kitchen smelled of toast and beeswax. Osgood poured tea from a pot that had clearly seen active service since the war, and Quentin, wrapping his hands around the cup, tried to find a version of the truth that wouldn't end with the vicar calling the police. He settled on: "I'm looking into the death of Iris Calloway. The drowning. Eleven years ago."

Something happened to the vicar's face. It didn't close exactly—it was too kind a face for that—but it settled, the way water settles after a stone.

"That was a hard year," Osgood said. "For this parish. For one family especially." He sat down across from Quentin, and his hands, wrapped around his own cup, were spotted and steady. "You're not police."

"No."

"A journalist, then."

"No."

Osgood studied him over the rim of his cup. "You have the look," he said at last, "of a man who is very good at finding things out and very bad at telling anyone. Am I close?"

Quentin blinked. In thirty-eight years, precisely no one had described him that accurately, including himself. "Something like that."

"Mm." The vicar drank his tea. Outside the window, a robin was bullying the bird table with the cheerful tyranny of its kind. "Iris Calloway drowned in the Marlowe channel. The inquest said accident. The village said nothing, which in a village is a great deal louder than saying something. And Ada Voss—" he set down his cup, "—Ada Voss was the one who saw it. Or said she did. She was sixteen."

"And then she died," Quentin said. "Two years later. There's a grave."

"There is a grave," Osgood agreed, and the way he said it made Quentin's skin prickle.

"But?"

The vicar looked out the window for a while. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into the register men use for things they've carried a long time. "Ada had a sister. Maren. Older by four years. After the drowning, Maren left—London, we assumed. After Ada died, Maren came back for the funeral, and then she left again, and she never wrote, never telephoned, never sent so much as a Christmas card. Which I confess I judged her for, in my weaker moments. A sister ought to grieve." He turned his mild eyes on Quentin. "But here is the thing I have learned about grief, Mr.—?"

"Halloran."

"Mr. Halloran. Grief does not always look like grieving. Sometimes it looks like leaving. Sometimes it looks like staying. And sometimes—" he smiled, and there was something almost mischievous in it, "—sometimes it looks like a person walking through my churchyard at half five every morning for eleven years, cutting flowers with scissors, because she cannot bear to bring shop flowers to a grave that isn't hers."

The kitchen went very quiet. The robin shouted at something.

"The grave," Quentin said carefully. "Whose name is on it?"

"Ada Voss," said the vicar. "Born March the third. Died November the ninth, eleven years ago. Buried on the twelfth, in the rain, with six mourners." He refilled Quentin's cup without asking. "I conducted the service myself. And I will tell you something else, Mr. Halloran, because you strike me as a man who collects true things the way other men collect stamps. I never saw the body. The doctor signed the certificate—he was new, and hurried, and Ada had been ill that autumn, and everyone said *she's gone, let her rest*. Her sister arranged everything. Paid for the stone. Chose the words." He paused. "*Resting where the water runs clear.* Do you know, in forty years I have never once chosen a gravestone inscription that a family didn't hand me ready-made? They always know. They always arrive with the words already in their pockets, as if they've been carrying them for years."

Quentin's tea had gone cold in his hands and he didn't care. His mind was moving now with the old cold-case click, the beautiful terrible machinery engaging gear by gear. A sister who left. A sister who came back. A grave arranged in a hurry by the only person who loved the dead woman enough to lie for her. Fresh flowers, cut by hand, laid by a woman in a green coat who vanished through a lych-gate like smoke through fingers.

"Maren," Quentin said. "Do you have a photograph? Of either sister?"

"I have the parish photographs," Osgood said, rising. "Confirmation classes, choir, the harvest festival of shamefully many years ago. I'll warn you—they're mostly hats."

He shuffled out, and Quentin sat alone in the warm kitchen, heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't permitted it in years. If Ada Voss was alive—if she had faked her death to escape something, to survive something—then the letters made a new and dreadful sense. *Someone keeps leaving fresh flowers on a grave that shouldn't be occupied.* Someone who knew the truth of Iris Calloway. Someone testing him, drawing him, letter by letter, toward the fen.

The vicar returned with a shoebox, blowing dust off the lid. "Now," he said, settling beside Quentin with the box between them like a shared meal, "the harvest festival of 1998. Prepare yourself. There is a marrow involved."

Quentin laughed—a real laugh, rusty, surprising them both—and reached into the box.

And there, at the bottom, beneath the hats and the marrows and the grinning children of a vanished decade, he found what he was looking for: two girls by the churchyard wall, arms linked, squinting into the sun. The younger one thin and watchful, dark-haired, half-turned as if she'd heard something behind the camera.

The older one taller, fairer, wearing a coat.

Dark green. The green of deep water.

Quentin's finger stopped on the photograph, and the warmth drained out of the kitchen, and somewhere out across the fens the tide was beginning to turn.
