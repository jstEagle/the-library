## 1. The Guest Who Never Checked Out

The fog came off the creek before dawn, the way it always did in October, and by seven it had swallowed the Marlowe Estate whole — the sagging veranda, the widow's walk, the iron gates that hadn't opened for anyone but Daniel Marlowe in eleven years. Marguerite Iversen was halfway up the drive with a tray of coffee when she heard the shouting.

She knew every voice on this property. She'd catalogued them the way she once catalogued guests at the Hotel Aurelia in Geneva: arrival times, allergies, lies. Mrs. Petrossian's wheedle. Old Hollis's gravel. The high, cracked alarm of a boy named Toby Finch who cut across the estate grounds to save ten minutes walking to school.

Toby's voice was the one screaming now.

Marguerite set the tray down on the gatepost — she would remember, later, that she'd done that, that her first instinct in the face of death had been hospitality, order, service — and walked toward the sound. Past the collapsed greenhouse. Past the reflecting pool where the water lilies had gone black years ago and no one had ever drained them.

The man lay at the pool's edge, half in the reeds, one arm flung out as if he'd been reaching for something just beyond his fingers.

He was dressed in her clothes.

Not similar clothes. *Her* clothes. The charcoal wool trousers she'd bought in Lyon three autumns ago, tailored, monogrammed inside the waistband with an *I* so small only she would know to look. The oxford shirt she'd been missing since Tuesday, the one with the frayed left cuff she kept meaning to mend. And around his neck, knotted with a carelessness that made her stomach turn, was her silk scarf — the dove-grey one with the hand-rolled edges, the last gift from a woman in Lucerne whose name Marguerite still said to herself sometimes like a prayer.

"Ma'am." Deputy Ruiz stepped into her path, palm out. "You shouldn't—"

"That's my scarf," Marguerite said. Her voice came out level. It always did. Twenty-two years in concierge work taught you that the guest in crisis is watching your face, and you do not give them anything to fear. "Those are my trousers. That shirt has been missing from my closet since Tuesday."

Ruiz looked at her the way people look at a clock that has begun running backward.

* * *

Sheriff Dale Boone arrived forty minutes later, in time for the body bag but not the fog. He was a heavy man with a heavy way of moving through the world, as if doors opened for him out of exhaustion rather than respect. He crouched over the dead man, grunted, and delivered his verdict before the medical examiner had finished unzipping her kit.

"Vagrant. Drifter come up from the highway, got turned around in the fog, fell in the pool, drowned." He said it the way he read weather reports. "Happens every few years."

"He's wearing stolen clothing," Marguerite said.

Boone glanced at her without rising. "Clothes get left around old houses. Squatters take what they find."

"Not from a locked cottage," Marguerite said. "And not a scarf knotted like that."

It wasn't. She could see it clearly even from where Ruiz had stationed her — twenty feet back, behind an imaginary line drawn in the wet grass. A drifter freezing in October fog might steal a scarf; he wouldn't knot it in a loose, deliberate loop at the throat, the kind of knot a man ties when someone else has shown him how. The kind of knot you tie when you want to look *finished*. Presentable. Like a man dressing for someone.

"And there's this," she went on, because Boone had already turned away. "His hands."

"What about his hands?"

"They're soft." She said it quietly, and Boone finally turned all the way around. "No calluses. No dirt under the nails. I've worked hotels for two decades, Sheriff. I can tell a laborer's hands from a desk clerk's from a surgeon's at thirty paces. That man hasn't done manual work in years — maybe never. Vagrants don't have hands like that. Accountants do. Clerks. Men who shake hands for a living."

Boone's jaw worked. "Ma'am, I appreciate your concern—"

"Do you?" Marguerite asked pleasantly. "Because it seems to me you decided what happened here before you got out of your truck."

The medical examiner, a tired woman named Okafor who'd driven over from Cedar Falls, shot Marguerite a look that was either gratitude or warning. Hard to tell, in Hollow Creek, where the two had been folded together for generations.

They carried the body out at nine-forty. The fog burned off at ten, revealing the estate in its full decay — the peeling shutters, the gutters sprouting saplings, the windows like eyes that had seen too much and closed. Marguerite stood on the drive long after the trucks left, holding her cold coffee, looking at the flattened grass where the man had lain.

Someone had dressed him. Someone had knotted that scarf. Someone had put him down at the edge of the pool like a guest placed carefully in the right room.

And then she saw it — small, half-buried in the mud of the bank where the deputies had trampled. She crouched, checked that Ruiz was occupied with his clipboard, and picked it up with her sleeve.

A key. Old brass, heavy, with a tag of yellowed masking tape on which someone had written, in faded pencil: *CELLAR — M.*

Marguerite had lived and worked at the Marlowe Estate for three years. She knew its keys the way she knew its creaking floors. There was no cellar key on any ring in the house. The wine cellar below the east wing had been sealed since the night Eleanor Marlowe drowned — sealed by Vivian Marlowe herself, padlocked, the door painted over twice.

She closed her fist around the key until the teeth bit her palm.

* * *

By noon the town had its story, and the story was Boone's: drifter, fog, accident. Marguerite heard it three times before lunch — at the bakery, at the hardware store, from the florist who delivered weekly arrangements to the estate and had opinions on everything except, apparently, the dead man in the reeds.

That was the thing about Hollow Creek that Marguerite had learned slowly, the way you learn the current under calm water. The town didn't lie, exactly. It simply agreed. It agreed on versions of events the way a hotel agrees on which rooms are haunted and which guests are never spoken of. Everyone nodded along, and everyone kept their own ledger underneath.

She spent the afternoon doing what she did best: noticing.

The victim's shoes — she'd gotten a look at them before the bag closed — were leather loafers, resoled twice, expensive once. His hair had been recently cut, unevenly, perhaps by himself in a mirror. He'd carried nothing: no wallet, no identification, no coins. But his fingernails were trimmed, his teeth — she'd glimpsed them when the ME tilted his head — were cared for. Somebody had fed this man recently. Somebody had housed him.

And somebody had taken her clothes from a locked cottage while she slept, dressed him in them, and laid him at the water's edge like an offering.

Or a message.

Marguerite sat at the kitchen table of her cottage as the light went amber, the cellar key lying in the center of the table where she'd placed it like evidence at a trial. Three years she'd kept this house. Three years of linens and grocery orders and quiet discretion — because discretion was the entire job. She knew things about this family. Small things, mostly. Which heir drank before noon. Which letters arrived from lawyers and were burned unread. Which room Vivian Marlowe locked when company came.

She had told herself, for three years, that keeping those secrets was professionalism. Now a stranger lay dead in her own clothes, and the word for what she'd been doing felt less like *professionalism* and more like *complicity*, and the difference between them was exactly the width of a silk scarf.

There was a knock at the cottage door. Two knocks, apologetic, spaced like a man rehearsing.

Daniel Marlowe stood on her step with his sleeves rolled and sawdust on his forearms, the reluctant caretaker of a house that was eating him alive. His eyes went past her shoulder to the table, to the key, and something moved through his face — fast, controlled, gone.

"You heard," he said.

"The whole town heard. The whole town agreed."

"Yeah." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I wanted to say — if you need anything. After today. People handle it differently."

"I don't need anything, Daniel." She held his gaze, and she watched him hold hers, and beneath both their courtesies something else passed between them, quiet as water finding a crack. "But I do have a question."

"Ask."

"When is the last time anyone opened the wine cellar?"

The sawdust, the rolled sleeves, the easy caretaker slouch — all of it stopped, just for a second, like a clock hesitating between ticks.

"It's been sealed for years," Daniel said. "Since before I took over. Why?"

"No reason." Marguerite smiled the smile she'd given ten thousand guests, the one that gave away nothing and promised everything. "Thank you for checking on me."

He walked back toward the main house through the blue dusk, and Marguerite closed the door and stood with her hand flat against the wood.

He'd lied. She'd spent twenty-two years reading faces across front desks, and Daniel Marlowe had lied to her the way guests lie about smoking in their rooms — smoothly, kindly, and for a reason.

She looked at the key on the table. *CELLAR — M.* M for Marlowe. Or M for something else entirely.

Tomorrow, she thought, she would go to the town archive. Tomorrow she would find out who this man had been before the fog took his name.

Tonight she did the only thing her ordered world still allowed. She boiled water, brewed tea, and set out two cups out of habit — then stood looking at the second cup for a long moment before putting it away.

Her missing scarf was in the county morgue, knotted around a stranger's neck. Her clothes had walked out of her closet and died at the edge of a black pool. And somewhere in Hollow Creek, someone knew exactly why.

Marguerite washed the single cup, dried it, and hung it precisely on its hook.

Then she began, very quietly, to make a list.
