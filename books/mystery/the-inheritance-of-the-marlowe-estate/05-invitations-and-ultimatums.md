## 5. Invitations and Ultimatums

Vivian Marlowe had learned long ago that a dinner party was never about the food. It was about seating. About who sat where, who poured for whom, who arrived last and who left first. A table was an argument you could win before anyone spoke.

She stood in the dining room of Marlowe Estate with the good china laid out before her — the Limoges, the one with the gilt edge her mother-in-law had guarded like a relic — and considered the place cards. Eleven years since Eleanor drowned in the reflecting pool behind the rose garden, and the town still called it an accident with the comfortable ease of people who had never once been asked to prove it. This dinner would be the anniversary's final word. She intended to write it herself.

The invitations had gone out two weeks ago: heavy cream stock, black-edged, the kind of card that announced *commemoration* rather than *celebration*. Sheriff Boone would come, of course. He always came. The Hollands from the bank. Pastor Wren, who owed her three years of quiet on a matter she preferred not to name even internally. And Daniel, who would sit at her right hand and say what she needed him to say when she needed him to say it.

That was the arrangement. It had been the arrangement since he was seventeen years old, standing white-faced at the top of the cellar stairs while she explained — gently, patiently, the way one explains anything to a frightened child — that families protect their own.

* * *

She found him in the east garden, on his knees in the dirt with his sleeves rolled up, pruning the roses Eleanor had planted. He did this every October, though Vivian had told him a dozen times to let them go wild. Sentimentality. The boy was full of it, and it was going to cost them both if she didn't cut it out of him the way he cut back those canes.

"Daniel."

He didn't turn around. "Aunt Vivian."

"The caterers confirmed for Saturday. Forty covers." She watched him work. His hands were steady, careful, the shears closing with small precise sounds. "I want to talk to you about the toast."

"I'm not giving a toast, Vivian."

"You're giving a toast." She stepped closer, her heels clicking on the flagstone, and crouched beside him despite the damp soaking into her skirt. "You're going to stand up, and you're going to tell these people that it's time. That keeping this house is keeping a wound open. That selling it lets everyone heal."

He set down the shears then, slowly, and looked at her. He had his father's eyes and Eleanor's jaw, and neither of those facts had ever done him any favors. "And why would I say that?"

"Because it's true."

"It isn't true. You don't want to sell the house. You've fought every offer for eleven years." He wiped his hands on a rag, unhurried. "You want to sell it *now*, all of a sudden, weeks after a stranger turns up dead on our grounds wearing another woman's clothes. You want it gone before someone looks too closely at something."

The words landed harder than he could have known. Vivian kept her face smooth — forty years of practice — but inside, something shifted like furniture settling in an empty room.

"A stranger," she said. "A vagrant. Sheriff Boone closed the file in four days."

"And Marguerite Iversen hasn't closed hers."

Ah. There it was. Vivian had wondered how long it would take the concierge woman to get under his skin. She'd watched it happen over three years — the little kindnesses, the way Daniel lingered at the gatehouse when Marguerite brought her deliveries, the way he'd started shaving more regularly, as if a man might be seen. Love was a leak in a hull. You patched it or you sank.

"That woman," Vivian said, rising, brushing off her skirt, "is a hotel employee who has decided she's a detective. She knows nothing about this family."

"She knows the dead man wore her scarf."

"She knows what Boone tells her, which is nothing." Vivian turned toward the house, then paused. "Saturday. The toast. You'll give it, Daniel, because you gave me your word once, and I have kept my side of that bargain for eleven years without ever once asking you to thank me for it."

He said nothing. But she saw his hands close around the rag, and she knew she'd won — the way she always won, which was to say ugly, which was to say at a price she'd stop counting because counting was unbearable.

* * *

That night, alone in her study, Vivian built a small fire.

It wasn't cold enough for a fire. It hadn't been cold enough for a fire in years, not since the oil furnace was installed, but she kept the study grate clean and the kindling dry because there were things in this house that should only ever meet flame, and one wanted to be prepared.

From the false bottom of her writing desk she took a bundle of letters tied with ribbon, and from the bundle she took one letter in particular — cream stock, a woman's slanted hand, dated June 2013. Three months before Eleanor died.

*Vivian — I know what you've been doing with the trust accounts. I've spoken to Aldous at the bank. I am giving you until the first of September to restore every dollar, or I will take this to the attorneys and see you removed from the estate entirely. I have loved you like a sister, but I will not let you steal from the foundation. Consider this my only warning.*

Eleanor. Even her threats had been elegant. No shouting, no recrimination — just that cool, wounded disappointment, the tone of a woman who had already grieved the relationship and moved on to the practicalities. Vivian had read the letter perhaps five hundred times. She could recite it. Some nights she woke with it running through her head like a hymn.

*I will not let you steal from the foundation.*

As if the money belonged to some abstraction. As if Vivian hadn't bled for this house while Eleanor played patroness with the interest, funding her little charities, cultivating her little gardener — yes, Vivian had known about that too, known how Eleanor confided in the hired help, trusted him with documents, with errands, with secrets. Trusting servants with secrets. That was the kind of naivety that got a woman killed.

Not murdered. Vivian was very precise about this, even alone, even now. *Killed.* There was a difference, and the difference mattered, and the difference was that on the night of October 14th, 2013, she had gone down to the cellar to destroy the trust ledgers before Eleanor's deadline, and Eleanor had followed her, and they had quarreled, and Vivian had pushed her — shoved her, really, a two-handed shove born of pure animal panic — and Eleanor had fallen against the wine rack and struck her head, and for a long terrible moment she had lain there breathing, actually breathing, and Vivian had knelt beside her and held her hand and begged her to wake up.

And then she stopped breathing. And that was not murder. That was an accident that Vivian made into something worse by calling Harold Boone at midnight instead of calling an ambulance, because Harold understood about families, and because the ledgers were still scattered across the cellar floor, and because a drowning in the reflecting pool was a cleaner story than a fall in a locked cellar.

Cleaner. God, the vocabulary of guilt. She'd had eleven years to perfect it.

The letter curled at the edges and caught. Vivian watched Eleanor's handwriting blacken and lift, the words *foundation* and *attorneys* and *sister* glowing briefly before collapsing into ash. When it was done she stirred the embers with a poker until nothing remained but a fine grey dust that could have been anything.

There was one copy of everything else — the ledger pages, the sheriff's report with its suspiciously thin margins, the receipt for the gardener's silence — locked in a place no one would ever think to look. She was not a careless woman. Careless women ended up in newspaper photographs.

But lately, carelessness seemed to be contagious. A drifter dies on her lawn in stolen clothes, and the town shrugs. A hotel concierge starts asking questions in the archive, and the town shrugs. Everyone shrugged, and yet Vivian felt the ground shifting under the estate like water undermining a foundation — invisible, patient, certain.

* * *

Marguerite Iversen came to the house on Thursday, ostensibly to discuss catering logistics for the dinner. Vivian received her in the front parlor, poured her tea from the Limoges teapot, and smiled the smile she reserved for people she intended to dismantle.

"You've been busy," Vivian said pleasantly. "I hear you've been at the county archive. And asking questions at the Anchor Inn. And visiting the florist — poor Delia, she does love to talk."

Marguerite set down her cup with a soft click. She was younger than Vivian by twenty years, composed, watchful, with the particular stillness of a woman who noticed everything and revealed nothing. "Mrs. Marlowe. I'm coordinating the anniversary dinner. Questions about the guest list are part of the job."

"Of course they are." Vivian folded her hands in her lap. "Let me tell you a story, Miss Iversen. When I was a girl, we had a housekeeper who liked to snoop. Read letters, went through drawers. My mother didn't dismiss her. She simply began leaving things out for her to find — little traps, little humiliations — until the woman's reputation was so ruined no one would hire her. She left town in disgrace. She'd done nothing wrong, technically. But she'd made herself into a person nobody defended."

The parlor was very quiet. Outside, wind moved through the dead garden.

"Are you threatening me?" Marguerite asked. Her voice was level. Almost admiring, almost — as if she were taking notes on the technique.

"Heavens, no. I'm offering hospitality advice." Vivian leaned forward, and let the smile drop away for just one second — long enough for the other woman to see what lived underneath it. "This dinner on Saturday will honor my sister-in-law's memory, and it will be dignified, and it will be the end of eleven years of gossip about this family. Whatever you think you're doing with your little inquiries, understand that I have buried more than grief in this house. Do not make me bury anything else."

For a moment, Marguerite simply looked at her. Then she rose, smoothed her skirt, and said, "Thank you for the tea, Mrs. Marlowe. I'll see you Saturday."

At the door she paused. "One thing. The wine for the toast — Daniel mentioned the cellar's been sealed for years. Shall I order bottles from town?"

Vivian felt the floor tilt, very slightly, beneath eleven years of careful masonry.

"Town will be fine," she said.

When the door closed, she stood in the empty parlor listening to the wind, and thought about the cellar key that had vanished from her husband's desk drawer three years ago — the same month Marguerite Iversen arrived in Hollow Creek — and wondered, with a cold creeping certainty, exactly whose pocket it had turned up in.
