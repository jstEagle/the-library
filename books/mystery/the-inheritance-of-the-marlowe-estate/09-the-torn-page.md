## 9. The Torn Page

The effects of the dead man arrived at the cottage in a banker's box, hand-delivered by a deputy who wouldn't meet my eyes.

"Sheriff says inventory's done," he mumbled. "No next of kin. You found the key, so—" He set the box on my kitchen table like it might detonate and left before I could thank him.

His name, according to the property slip, had been Tomas Reyes. Forty-six. Formerly of Hollow Creek. The town that had forgotten him had at least remembered to spell his name right on the form.

I made tea I didn't drink and went through his life the way I'd once gone through guests' lost luggage at the Alpina: methodically, respectfully, looking for what mattered. A watch, stopped. A photograph of a woman I didn't recognize, soft with age. A rosary with two beads missing. A folding knife, a wallet with sixty dollars, a bus ticket dated the day before he died — he'd been leaving. Whatever he'd come back to Hollow Creek to do, he'd meant to do it and go.

And at the bottom of the box, wrapped in a plastic bag from a hardware store that had closed a decade ago: a manila envelope, soft as cloth from handling, with a single word typed on a label that had yellowed to the color of weak tea.

*IF I DROWN.*

Inside was one page, torn along its left edge, the ragged remnant matching — I knew it would, my hands were shaking before I confirmed it — the torn stub at the back of Eleanor Marlowe's journal.

Tomas Reyes had kept his insurance policy for eleven years. And then he'd come home to collect.

I read it standing up, because I knew if I sat down I might never get up again.

Eleanor's handwriting, dated October 12, 2013 — three days before she drowned:

*Vivian came tonight. I told her about the trust, the charity, all of it. She begged. Then she stopped begging. She struck me with the decanter — I still have the lump above my ear to prove it — and when she saw what she'd done she went white as milk and said, "You'll ruin us all, you understand that? Everything Father built." I said the estate was never his to build. She left without a word. I am writing this down so that if anything happens to me, someone knows it was not an accident. Tomas is hiding these pages for me in the cellar. He is the only one I trust in this whole rotten house.*

And beneath that, in Tomas's careful gardener's hand, a postscript added years later:

*She didn't drown in the pond. I saw Vivian's car at the boathouse that night. And I saw Sheriff Hollis's truck behind it. I took the last page out because Eleanor asked me to hold something back — she said, "If they kill me, don't give them everything at once." I've been holding it ever since. I'm tired of holding it.*

The last page itself was there, folded inside the envelope, and I almost couldn't make myself unfold it. But I did. Of course I did. Curiosity has always been the flaw at the center of my character, the crack the whole vessel was built around.

Eleanor's final entry, October 15, 2013:

*Vivian came again. She said if I signed the estate over to her she'd let the charity have the endowment and we'd never speak of it. I refused. She struck me again — harder. I fell. I don't know how long I was on the floor. When I came to, Sheriff Hollis was in the room, and Vivian was crying, and they were talking about the pond, about how much easier it would be for everyone, about how Daniel was asleep and would never know. Hollis said, "People drown in storms, Eleanor. It's practically tradition here." I am writing this in the boathouse. I can hear them on the path. If this is the last thing I ever write—*

It ended there. Mid-sentence. The ink smeared, as if the page had been closed in a hurry, or as if water had touched it.

Eleven years. A boy asleep upstairs. A sheriff's father with a truck and a theory everyone found convenient. A town that wanted its prettiest family left alone.

I sat down after all. The tea had gone cold. I thought about Sheriff Boone, who'd looked at a dead man in a woman's stolen clothes and seen nothing but a vagrant's misfortune, the way his father had looked at a drowned woman and seen tradition. I thought about the florist, the chambermaids, the bartender — all of them handing me pieces of that night like people returning stolen goods they'd kept out of fear. The town hadn't conspired, exactly. It had *deferred*. Year after year, it had deferred, the way you defer a hard conversation, a doctor's call, a grief. And deferral, I knew better than anyone, is just a polite word for complicity.

I had deferred too. Three years of small secrets kept for the Marlowes — the locked drawers, the unexplained deliveries, the nights Vivian's car came back at dawn. I'd told myself I was protecting Daniel. I'd actually been protecting my place in the house, my belonging, purchased with silence.

No more.

* * *

I called Daniel first, because he deserved to hear it from me and not from a courtroom.

"I have the page," I said. "The one from the journal. Tomas kept it."

The silence on the line went on so long I checked whether the call had dropped. When he spoke, his voice was stripped down to studs and beams. "It says what I think it says."

"It says your mother struck her. It says Sheriff Hollis helped stage it. It says Eleanor walked to the boathouse alive and scared."

A sound came out of him that was half breath, half something older. "She was alive. She was *alive*, and I was fourteen, asleep, and they let me grow up thinking the pond just — took her." A long pause. "Where are you?"

"The cottage."

"Don't move. Don't call anyone. I'm coming, and then we're going to do this properly — Boone, a lawyer, all of it, in daylight, on the record."

"Daniel—"

"I know." He knew what I was going to say: that Vivian was coming for dinner, that the estate was full of Marlowes, that daylight was twelve hours away and Vivian had spent eleven years proving she didn't wait for daylight. "I know. But we do it right, Marguerite. If we do it wrong, it becomes another thing this town swallows."

He arrived in twenty minutes with the page photographed in triplicate and a look on his face I'd never seen: not the caretaker's patient endurance, not the heir's reluctance. Something harder. His mother's jaw, repurposed.

We were still at my kitchen table, the page between us like a third guest, when the headlights swept the window.

Vivian's car.

"She was supposed to come at seven," Daniel said. "For the dinner. She's early." He stood, and I saw his hands had gone flat and white on the table's edge. "She knows. Somehow she already knows."

"Then we stop hiding it." I picked up the page. "We were going to the cellar anyway. Let's not make her climb the hill."

* * *

The wine cellar had not improved with familiarity. The cold down there had a texture, a memory of the night it had last been this cold. Daniel's flashlight found the racks, the dust, the corner where Eleanor's journal had waited out a decade behind a false brick.

Vivian descended the stairs without hurrying. That was the thing about her — she never hurried, because hurrying was for people who could still be surprised. She wore black, of course. She wore black the way other women wore armor, and tonight the armor had a hairline crack running through it that only someone trained to read guests could see: her lipstick had been applied twice, the second time shakily.

She looked at the page in my hand the way you look at an X-ray of your own chest.

"Where," she said.

"Tomas Reyes kept it," I said. "For eleven years. Until someone arranged for him to die in my clothes on your lawn."

"I didn't—"

"I know you didn't," Daniel said, and something in his voice made her flinch worse than an accusation would have. "I've known you my whole life, Mother. You don't have the stomach. But you know who does, and you've spent eleven years protecting them, and that's over."

Vivian looked at her son. And then, slowly, at me — and what I saw in her face was not hatred. It was relief, terrible and bottomless, like a drowning woman finally being permitted to put her feet down.

"He was going to come to the dinner," she said. "Tomas. He wrote to me in September. He said he wanted to *talk*, in front of everyone, at the anniversary. He said the town deserved to hear it together." Her laugh came out wrong, a dry little cough. "I thought he was bluffing. I told Hollis's boy. I told him, and I told myself it was just to keep an eye on him, and then the man turned up dead in—" She stopped. Her hand went to her mouth. "In your scarf. I saw the photograph in the paper and I thought, *God has a cruel sense of staging.*"

"Mother." Daniel's voice cracked on the word. "Tell it. All of it. Not to me — to the record. Marguerite, the recorder. Please."

I set the phone on a wine barrel, screen up, the red dot burning.

Vivian Marlowe stood in her dead sister-in-law's cellar and confessed. Not to murder. She was very clear about that, and the terrible thing was that I believed her, because the truth she told was worse than murder — it was the ordinary, cowardly, human truth.

"I struck her," she said. "Twice. The second time she fell and didn't get up right away, and I thought — I thought I'd killed her, and Hollis was there, and he said the only way to save me was to make it look like the pond, because who questions a drowning at the Marlowe estate, and I was so frightened, and I let him. I let him walk her down to the boathouse. I heard the splash. I heard it, and I told myself it was the storm." Her voice didn't rise. It got quieter, which was worse. "And then she was dead, and I had a son to protect, and the estate, and eleven years went by the way eleven years do — one dinner, one funeral, one lie at a time — until the lie was load-bearing. You understand? Pull it out and the whole house comes down."

"The house was already down," Daniel said. "You just kept paying rent on the ruin."

She reached out and touched his cheek, and he let her, and I stood in the cold with a phone recording and understood that I was watching a woman lose everything she'd committed her crimes to keep — and that what she'd actually wanted, all along, was what every guest I'd ever checked in had wanted: to be forgiven without having to confess.

"Mrs. Marlowe," I said quietly, when it was done. "The deputy who delivered the effects box. Who told him to bring it to me?"

Vivian frowned. "No one. The sheriff's office inventoried it. Boone said—"

"Boone said the inventory was done," I said slowly. "But Boone never ordered it released. I asked him yesterday. He told me it was still evidence."

The flashlight beam wavered in Daniel's hand.

"Then who sent it?" he asked.

And from the top of the cellar stairs, in the dark above us, unhurried, a door clicked shut.
