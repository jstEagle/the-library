## 2. Entries in Red Ink

The account book arrived on a Tuesday, which Wren would later decide was the universe's idea of a joke. Tuesdays were her filing days. Filing days were for the mundane: deed transfers, tax receipts, the slow sediment of other people's money settling into order. Nothing about a leather-bound ledger with a cracked spine and a coffee ring shaped like Portugal belonged in that category, but here it was, sitting on her desk between a probate checklist and a cold cup of tea.

The courier had no return address. The package had been postmarked from Millbrook — her town, her zip code, four blocks from her office — which meant whoever sent it wanted her to know they were close. Or wanted her to think so.

She put on gloves. Not because she expected fingerprints, but because the ritual steadied her. Probate had taught her that objects outlived their owners with an almost vindictive patience, and she preferred to meet them formally.

*Household Accounts, 2009–2024*, said the flyleaf, in careful cursive. Below it, smaller: *Every house keeps its own conscience.*

Wren turned to the first page expecting grocery lists.

She found them, technically. Flour, eggs, lamp oil, a repair to the garden gate. Ordinary purchases in ordinary amounts. But something about the columns itched at her the way a misfiled document does — that low-grade wrongness a lawyer's brain registers before the conscious mind catches up.

She pulled a legal pad toward her and began copying figures.

Flour, 4.50. Eggs, 3.20. Lamp oil, 12.75.

Then, three lines down: *Candles (memorial), 4/11, R.M., 2,400.*

Her pen stopped.

No one spent two thousand four hundred dollars on memorial candles. No one recorded the date of a candle purchase in a household ledger, let alone initials beside it. And yet there it was again, six pages later: *Garden stones, replacement, 9/02, D.V., 1,800.* And again: *Roof patching (north face), 6/17, T.H., 5,000.*

She worked through the afternoon, the light going amber in her office windows, the legal pad filling with dates and initials and sums that climbed from hundreds into thousands. The pattern assembled itself the way patterns do — reluctantly, then all at once. The dates clustered around anniversaries. April eleventh. September second. June seventeenth. And every entry was dated within a week of one of those three days, year after year, for fifteen years.

Payments made on the anniversary of something. To people identified only by initials.

R.M. D.V. T.H. L.C. J.F.

Hush money didn't usually announce itself as hush money. It dressed itself in flour and lamp oil and roof repairs. Whoever kept this book had been paying five people, on rotation, since 2009 — and had disguised each payment as the most boring line item imaginable.

"Who *are* you," Wren murmured to the ledger, "and why do you write like—"

She stopped.

She knew that handwriting. She'd know it anywhere, the way you know a voice through a wall. The looping g's. The way the t's crossed with a slight upward flick, like the pen was always about to ask a question. She had watched that hand write shopping lists on the refrigerator pad. She had received birthday cards in it, every year, even after she'd left home at eighteen and the cards became the only thread between them.

*Marisol.*

Marisol Vega, who had taken in a sullen fourteen-year-old Wren when the state ran out of options and nobody else volunteered. Marisol, who taught her to make arepas and balance a checkbook and never once asked about the father who'd signed away his rights with the enthusiasm of a man clearing clutter. Marisol, whose kitchen had been the first room Wren had ever felt safe falling asleep in.

Marisol, whose handwriting was all over fifteen years of payments to strangers.

Wren set down the pen very carefully, as if sudden movements might detonate something.

*It could be coincidence,* said the reasonable part of her brain, the part that drafted contracts and believed in due process. *Lots of people cross their t's like that.*

*Sure,* said the rest of her. *And lots of people pay five individuals annual stipends disguised as gardening expenses.*

She flipped back to the flyleaf. *Every house keeps its own conscience.* She'd slept under that roof for four years. She'd eaten dinner at that table. Had she been living inside the conscience this book was keeping?

The confession letter from the estate file sat in her bottom drawer, and she took it out now, laying it beside the open ledger. Same ink color — a deep red-brown, the kind of pen that bled slightly into cheap paper. Same hand.

One letter confessed to a murder. One ledger paid for its silence. Both written by the woman who had taught Wren to ride a bike in a church parking lot, wobbling and laughing, both of them certain the world was fundamentally kind.

Wren put her head down on the desk, briefly, next to the cold tea.

* * *

The phone call came at 9:47 that night, while she was still at the office, surrounded by photocopies of ledger pages fanned out like evidence at a trial she hadn't agreed to prosecute.

She almost didn't answer an unknown number. Then she remembered that she was, professionally speaking, a woman who answered unknown numbers.

"Ms. Reyes." A man's voice, smooth and unhurried, with the particular polish of someone accustomed to being listened to. "I understand you've received some correspondence regarding the Hollis estate."

The name landed strangely. Aldous Hollis — the dead man whose will named her executor, the man she'd never met, the man whose murder confession sat in her drawer. But the caller pronounced *Hollis* the way families pronounce names, with ownership.

"I'm not able to discuss active matters," Wren said, which was true and also a stalling tactic while her heart rearranged itself.

"Of course not. I'm calling as a courtesy. The family is holding a small gathering soon — an anniversary, of sorts. A remembrance." A pause with weight in it. "Grief makes people say careless things. Read careless things aloud, even. It would be a kindness to everyone — yourself included — if the evening stayed a remembrance."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then the books get audited, Ms. Reyes. All the books. Including whatever you keep." He let that sit. "You were a ward of the state, weren't you? Before the Vegas took you in. Records from those years are so easily... reopened."

The line went quiet. Not hung up — held open, deliberately, so she could hear him breathing, unhurried, a man with all the time in the world.

"The dinner is Saturday," he said. "You'll be receiving an invitation. Do come. Families so rarely get everyone together anymore."

He hung up.

Wren sat in the dark of her office for a long moment, the city humming below, the ledger open on her desk like a mouth.

They knew she had it. Which meant either the sender had told them — or the sender *was* one of them, and the whole delivery had been a fuse lit on purpose.

And the threat — *whatever you keep* — wasn't really about her finances. It was about the years before Marisol. The files, the group homes, the father who'd erased himself. Someone had looked into her past and found the soft place to press.

She should call the police. She knew the number by heart; she'd dialed it for clients often enough, walking them through the terror of it. She picked up her phone. Set it down. Picked it up again.

Because here was the thing she couldn't explain to a duty officer: the ledger's final entries, the ones from the last two years, had changed. The amounts had grown. The initials had shifted — new letters appearing, old ones dropping away. And the last entry, dated eleven days ago, read:

*Candles (memorial), 10/03, W.R., 0.00.*

W.R. Wren Reyes. An entry in her own initials, amount zero, dated ten days before the anniversary dinner.

Either someone was planning to pay her.

Or someone was planning to close her account.

She looked at the invitation that had, in fact, arrived with the evening mail — heavy cream cardstock, gold lettering, *In Loving Remembrance of Aldous Hollis, Saturday at seven*. At the bottom, in that same looping hand, that same upward-flicked question mark of a signature line:

*We do hope you'll join us. — M.*

Marisol wanted her there. Or Marisol's hand had been borrowed. Wren honestly no longer knew which possibility frightened her more.

She closed the ledger, tucked it into her bag — because leaving it in the office felt suddenly like leaving a child unattended — and stood in the doorway a moment, looking back at the dark room full of other people's orderly paper lives.

Saturday, then. She'd go to the dinner. She'd bring the book.

And somewhere between the toast and the dessert course, she intended to find out what her family had buried — and whether she'd been sleeping on top of it all along.
