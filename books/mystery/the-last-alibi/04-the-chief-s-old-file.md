## 4. The Chief's Old File

Amos Greer lived at the end of Dill Road, in a house that had once been white and was now the color of old teeth. Sable knew the way without thinking about it—everybody in Harrow Bend knew where Amos Greer lived, the way they knew where the water tower stood and which pews were reserved at First Methodist. Thirty-one years as chief of police. He'd retired the same autumn Maren Voss disappeared, a coincidence the town had never thought to examine because the town had never been taught to examine anything.

She parked on the gravel shoulder rather than the cracked driveway, giving herself a moment to look at the place. A bass boat sat under a tarp that had given up its argument with the wind years ago. The flagpole was bare. In the window, behind a curtain the color of weak tea, a lamp burned even though it was barely past four in the afternoon.

People who drank alone in daylight kept lamps burning early. It wasn't shame exactly. It was more like a refusal to let the dark arrive all at once.

Sable got out of her car with the box of books under one arm—Maren Voss's books, bought at an estate sale for eleven dollars, though she'd left the diary itself locked in the cold drawer back at the mortuary between a sixty-eight-year-old man who'd died of his heart and a woman who'd outlasted her own kidneys by a decade. She hadn't told anyone what she'd found in it. Not yet. But there was a name signed at the bottom of the case file that closed Maren Voss's disappearance, and that name belonged to the man whose porch she was now crossing.

The screen door had a spring that groaned like something dying slowly. She knocked anyway, out of courtesy.

"Chief Greer," she called. "It's Sable Oyelaran."

A long silence. Then, from somewhere deep in the house, a voice like gravel poured over gravel: "Door's not locked."

It was, actually. She opened it with the shoulder of experience.

* * *

The living room smelled of bourbon and dust and, underneath both, the particular staleness of a house where the windows haven't been opened since someone stopped expecting company. Amos Greer sat in a recliner angled toward a television that wasn't on, wearing a flannel shirt buttoned wrong by one hole, so that the whole garment pulled slightly to the left, like a man leaning away from something. He was seventy if he was a day, big through the shoulders still, the kind of frame that had once filled a uniform and now just filled furniture. His hands, wrapped around a glass that held more ice than whiskey, were steady.

That surprised her. She'd expected the shake.

"Sable Oyelaran." He said her name the way you'd read a headline you didn't like. "They give you the Pruitt drowning yet?"

"I gave myself the Pruitt drowning." She took the chair opposite him without being invited—another courtesy observed in the breach. "Ligature furrow under the mandible, inconsistent with the water line. Petechial hemorrhaging in both eyes. Somebody held Dale Pruitt under, Chief, and then somebody wrote a report saying he fell off the pier drunk."

"That's the coroner's opinion."

"That's the coroner's *finding*. Opinion is what the sheriff's office filed instead."

Greer drank. The ice clicked against his teeth. On the wall behind him hung a framed photograph of a younger Amos in dress uniform, squinting into a sun that no longer existed, standing beside a plaque commemorating his thirty-one years of service. Beside it, smaller, was another photograph: Greer shaking hands with Everett Aldous at some ribbon-cutting, both men smiling the smiles of men who have never once doubted which side of a rope they're holding.

"You came about the Voss girl," he said.

"The Voss woman. She was twenty-six, not a girl. And yes."

"There's nothing to come about. She ran off. Left a note."

"She left a note that said she needed time to think, written in a hand I've now spent three nights comparing against her diary, and the two don't agree on how she forms her lowercase g." Sable set the box of books on the coffee table between them, deliberately, the way you'd set down a verdict. "You closed that case in nine days, Chief. Nine days. You've worked fender-benders longer than that."

Something moved behind Greer's eyes—not anger, not quite fear, but the territory where those two share a border. He looked at the television that wasn't on as if it might rescue him.

"You want a drink?" he said.

"No."

"Suit yourself." He poured one anyway, from a bottle that should have been fuller than it was, and she understood that the glass in his hand was less a drink than a prop—the thing he did with his hands so they wouldn't do something else. "Maren Voss ran off. Happens all the time. Teachers burn out. Boyfriends disappoint them. They go to Asheville, they go to Savannah, they start over."

"With no bank account activity. No phone pings. No car—her car stayed in the school lot for two weeks until somebody finally towed it." Sable leaned forward. "You know what I do, Chief? I read the dead. And the dead are honest, because they can't afford to be anything else. It's the living I have trouble with. The living lie with their whole faces."

"Then you know how it is," Greer said quietly, "when the living won't tell you the truth even when they want to."

The room went very still. Outside, a crow said something rude and flew off.

"What does that mean?"

"It means nothing. It means an old man talking." He set the glass down and pressed both palms flat against his thighs, and she saw it then—the tremor he'd been holding in check, surfacing now that his hands had nothing to hold. "Why are you here, Sable? Really."

"Because your signature is on the bottom of a closed file that shouldn't be closed. Because Dale Pruitt drowned with a rope mark on his neck and nobody asked why. Because two years ago Hollis Deene rolled a tractor on flat ground going four miles an hour, and the county accepted it because the county accepts whatever it's handed." She paused. "And because every one of those cases ends at the same fence line, and the fence line says ALDOUS DEVELOPMENT, and you shook that man's hand in a photograph on your wall."

Greer looked at the photograph. For a long moment she thought he might actually answer. His mouth opened. His jaw worked around words like a man chewing something too tough to swallow.

Then he laughed—a short, terrible sound with no humor anywhere in it.

"You think I don't know what you found on Dale Pruitt?" he said. "I've known for six weeks. I've known since before you did."

* * *

He got up then, unsteadily, and went to the window, and stood looking out at the yard where the tarp fought its losing battle with the wind. Sable stayed in her chair. Twenty years of sitting with the bereaved had taught her that silence pulls truth out of people faster than questions ever will—if you can stand to wait inside it.

Greer waited longer than most. When he finally spoke, his back was still to her.

"When I retired," he said, "there was a pension review. Standard. Except it wasn't standard, because a week after I signed my closing report on the Voss case, a man from the county came out here and sat in that chair you're sitting in, and he laid out some photographs of my daughter's house in Knoxville. Photographs taken from across the street. My granddaughter's school schedule. He never threatened anything. Never raised his voice. He just told me what a blessing retirement was going to be, and how nice it would be if I could enjoy it in peace, and then he thanked me for my service and left."

"Who was the man?"

"He didn't say. Didn't need to. He had a lawyer's hands." Greer turned around. His face had gone gray. "You want to know what happened to Maren Voss? Nothing happened to Maren Voss. That's the whole trick of it. She wrote me a letter herself, after she left. Said she was fine, said she didn't want to be found, said please close the file. Handwriting matched. Everything matched. I closed it because everything matched, and because my granddaughter has a birthday in March, and I would like very much to keep attending it."

"Letters can be forged."

"Sure. And autopsies can be misfiled. And tractors can roll uphill if the right people say they did." He came back to his chair but didn't sit in it; he stood over it, gripping the backrest, knuckles pale. "Listen to me, Sable Oyelaran, because I'm only going to say this once, and then you're going to leave, and we're both going to pretend this afternoon never happened. Whatever you think you're doing—stop doing it. You've got a good reputation. People trust you. Don't spend that on this."

"On the truth?"

"On *this*." His voice cracked, and he hated it—she could see him hating it. "There are accidents in this county that aren't accidents, and there are men who make sure they stay accidents, and they have been doing it longer than you've had a license. You found a rope mark on Dale Pruitt. Congratulations. Now ask yourself why the sheriff's report doesn't mention it, and ask yourself who reads those reports before they're filed, and then ask yourself what happens to a small-town coroner who keeps finding things other people have decided not to find."

"I'm asking you, Chief. Tell me who."

Greer's grip tightened on the chairback. And then, in a voice dropped so low it was almost a prayer, almost a surrender, he gave her the only thing he had left to give:

"Kestrel."

One word. He said it the way you'd say the name of a disease.

"Kestrel Reservoir," Sable said. "The Aldous resort project."

"Don't." His head came up sharply. "Don't say it out loud. Not in this house, not on this road, not anywhere there's a wall thin enough to hear through. You go out to that water, you'll find what Maren Voss found, and then you'll understand why she's gone, and God help you, you'll wish you'd stayed home counting your corpses."

"They'll kill me?"

"They don't kill people like you." He smiled, and it was the worst expression she'd seen all day, worse than any face she'd ever worked on under fluorescent light. "They don't have to. They'll make you a hero. They'll put your name in the paper. They'll give you a plaque like that one—" he jerked his chin at the photograph of his younger self, "—and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what you traded for it. That's how it works in Harrow Bend. Nobody gets murdered. Things just... happen. Tractors roll. Men drown. Women run off. And everybody nods along, because nodding along is easier than digging."

Sable rose. She picked up the box of books, then set it back down—an impulse she didn't fully understand, leaving a piece of Maren Voss in the room with him, a hostage or a gift.

"Your granddaughter's birthday," she said quietly. "March. What day?"

"Why?"

"Because if anything happens to you between now and then, Chief—if your boat turns up empty, if your truck goes off Dill Road—I want to know exactly what I'm supposed to feel responsible for."

The color drained out of his face so completely that for a moment he looked embalmed already, a preview of the work she might someday do on him. He sat down heavily in the recliner, all the fight gone out of him, and reached for the bottle with hands that were finally, openly shaking.

"Get out," he whispered. "Please. Get out of my house."

She went. At the door she paused, one hand on the frame.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I talk to the dead because the living stopped listening. But somebody's still listening to you, Chief. Whoever sent that man with the lawyer's hands—they listen very carefully. Ask yourself who benefits from your silence, and then ask yourself whether silence is really keeping your granddaughter safe, or whether it's just keeping *them* comfortable."

The screen door groaned shut behind her.

* * *

Dusk was coming down over Dill Road as she walked to her car, and the first star had appeared above the tree line, hard and white and indifferent. Sable stood in the gravel a moment, breathing the cold air, letting the visit settle into her bones the way evidence settles into a case—one detail at a time, each one clicking into place with the sound of a lock turning.

Kestrel. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She thought about Maren Voss's diary entries, the bright observant ones, the ones where a young teacher watched surveyors at the reservoir and wrote about dead fish and oily sheen on the water and men in unmarked trucks who told her to mind her own business. She thought about the forged entries, flat and rehearsed, describing days that never happened. She thought about Dale Pruitt's neck, and Hollis Deene's tractor, and a retired chief drinking bourbon at four in the afternoon with his shirt buttoned wrong, terrified of a word.

The cover-up had a badge on it. She'd suspected it for weeks. Now she'd heard it confess.

Sable got into her car but didn't start it. Instead she took out her notebook—the small black one she kept for things that weren't official—and wrote a single line beneath the notes she'd made on the Pruitt ligature:

*Kestrel. Follow the water.*

As she turned the key, headlights swept across her rearview mirror—a truck slowing on Dill Road, unhurried, ordinary, passing her by without a glance. County plates. She watched it recede until it turned off toward the reservoir road, and she told herself it meant nothing, and she did not believe herself.

Three days later, Amos Greer failed to show up for his weekly fishing trip.

His boat was found adrift on Kestrel Reservoir, moored neatly—from the outside.
