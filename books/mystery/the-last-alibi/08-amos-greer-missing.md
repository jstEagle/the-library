## 8. Amos Greer, Missing

The call came at 6:14 on a Thursday morning, which was wrong before Sable even answered it.

Amos Greer fished on Saturdays. He had fished on Saturdays for the eleven years Sable had lived in Harrow Bend and, according to Rhea Calloway, for the twenty-two years before that—a devotion so fixed that the man's calendar might as well have been carved into stone beside his wife's name in the cemetery on Miller Hill. Saturday mornings he launched his aluminum skiff off the gravel ramp at Kestrel Reservoir with a thermos of black coffee and a radio tuned to the gospel station out of Cane County, and he came home by two o'clock smelling of sunscreen and failure, because Amos Greer could not catch a fish to save his life and everyone in town knew it and loved him for it.

Nobody called the coroner on a Thursday.

"Dr. Oyelaran." Deputy Rhea Calloway's voice, pitched low and careful, the way you speak when someone else is standing within earshot. "We've got a boat adrift off the east cove. It's Greer's."

Sable sat up in the dark of her kitchen, phone pressed to her ear, and felt the cold come into her hands before her mind caught up to it. "Is he in it?"

"No." A pause, long enough to hold a whole conversation. "That's why I'm calling you and not dispatch."

* * *

The east cove of Kestrel Reservoir was the prettiest lie the county owned. In summer the water went glass-green between the bluffs, and the pines leaned down to admire themselves, and the Aldous Development billboards—KESTREL LANDING: WHERE THE WATER REMEMBERS YOU—rose above the treeline like headstones waiting for their names. This morning the prettiness was stripped away. Fog lay on the reservoir in flat gray sheets, and the boat sat in the middle of it, alone, riding so lightly on the water that it looked less abandoned than patient.

Rhea met her at the ramp with two cups of gas-station coffee and an expression she was trying very hard to make professional. She was twenty-six, honest in a way that Harrow Bend punished rather than rewarded, and over the past weeks she had become the only living person Sable trusted further than she could throw.

"State game warden spotted it at five-forty," Rhea said as they walked the gravel shore. "Called it in. Dispatch ran the registration before I got here, which means everyone at the sheriff's office knows by now."

"Which means Everett Aldous knows by now."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to."

The skiff bumped gently against the rocks where the warden had beached it. Sable crouched at the gunwale and did not touch anything yet. She looked first, always looked first; hands were for later, when looking had told them everything they were permitted to know.

The boat was clean. That was the first thing. Not fish-clean, not weekend-clean—*clean*, scrubbed in the way a thing gets scrubbed by someone with time and purpose. The bait bucket was stowed. The anchor line was coiled in its figure-eights, dry on top, which meant it hadn't been wet recently or had been dried deliberately. The two folding chairs sat facing forward, side by side, like passengers. On the stern bench, squared to the edge with a precision no fisherman ever managed, sat Amos Greer's tackle box, latched, and on top of the tackle box, weighted under a small river stone, a folded piece of paper in a plastic sandwich bag.

Sable put on nitrile gloves before she lifted it.

Inside the bag, on a page torn from a legal pad, in a hand she recognized from thirty years of county paperwork—the blocky, unhurried capitals of a man who'd learned to print in a police academy—was a single line:

**GONE FISHING. DON'T WAIT UP.**

She read it three times. Around her the fog moved against the bluffs, and somewhere across the water, faint and obscene, a construction crew was already starting its day at the Kestrel Landing site, the beep-beep-beep of a reversing loader carrying flatly over the still air.

"He left a note," Rhea said quietly. She had come up beside her and was reading over Sable's shoulder. "In a bag. Weighted under a rock."

"So it wouldn't blow away."

"So we'd find it."

"Yes." Sable turned the page toward the gray light. "That's what I said."

* * *

They worked the boat for two hours, and the boat gave them nothing, which was itself something.

No blood. No scuffing on the gunwales. No drag marks on the aluminum where a body might have been hauled over the side. The drain plug was in place and the bilge was dry, which meant the boat had not taken on water, which meant whatever had happened to Amos Greer had happened without the reservoir's participation. There was no fishing rod rigged—his rods were racked at home, his daughter confirmed later, all four of them, oiled and ready for Saturday. He had come to this cove on a Thursday morning, apparently, without equipment, without bait, without the thermos that lived permanently in the skiff's storage locker and was not there now.

He had come, if he had come at all, to leave a note.

Sable stood on the shore while the warden winched the boat onto a trailer, and she let the pattern assemble itself in her head the way she let a body tell her its story—one detail, then another, then the terrible moment when the details stopped being details and became a sentence.

Dale Pruitt, eight months ago. Found floating in the millrace below the old trestle, ruled a drowning, blood alcohol twice the legal limit. Except the ligature furrow under the back of his jaw that the sheriff's report never mentioned, and the absence of water in his stomach beyond trace amounts, and the way his boots were laced too neatly for a drunk man climbing a trestle in the dark. Staged. Staged by someone who understood drowning well enough to fake one and not well enough—or not carefully enough—to fool a coroner who talked to her dead.

And now this. A boat adrift. A note in a bag. A missing man and no body, and every element arranged to say *he walked away, he chose this, don't look, don't wait up.* The same hand. The same grammar of staging—the tidy detail placed exactly where a searcher would find it, the story assembled out of props instead of truth. Whoever had done Dale Pruitt had done Amos Greer, and they had learned from the first time. No body this time. Nothing to autopsy. Nothing for a woman who reads the dead better than the living to read.

Because there was no dead. Yet.

Sable stood at the water's edge and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the absence of a body was not mercy. It was arithmetic. Dale Pruitt had been killed quickly because he had to be. Amos Greer—an old man, a retired chief, a man whose disappearance would raise questions—had been made to *vanish*, and vanishing took longer, and longer meant he might still be somewhere, breathing, waiting for someone to find him before the reservoir or the quarry or whatever hole they had chosen finished the sentence his note had started.

Or he was already gone, and the note was simply the last lie anyone would ever tell about him.

She found she was gripping the river stone from the tackle box, cold through her glove, and she set it down gently on the gravel, exactly where it had been, because evidence was evidence even when it broke your heart.

* * *

Greer's house was a white clapboard bungalow on Delaney Street with a flagpole out front and a wheelchair ramp built by volunteer firemen after his hip surgery, and his daughter Carol met Sable on the porch with her arms wrapped around herself against a cold that wasn't in the air.

"He wouldn't run off." Carol said it before Sable was up the steps, said it the way people say things they've been saying to themselves since dawn, over and over, trying to make it true by repetition. "Everybody keeps saying maybe he just—went somewhere. Dad doesn't go places. Dad fishes on Saturdays and watches the Braves and complains about the price of minnows. He's seventy-one years old."

"May I see his study?"

The study smelled of pipe tobacco and machine oil. Trophies on the shelf—not fishing trophies, shooting trophies, from a career that had ended twelve years ago in a retirement nobody in town had ever quite explained. Sable had gone to see him once, early in her investigation, and had been shown the door by a man whose fear had a smell she recognized, because she'd smelled it on witnesses before, on people who knew exactly which door in a house you didn't open. *One word,* he'd told her, pouring whiskey at ten in the morning with a shaking hand. *Kestrel. You want to stay healthy, Doctor, you'll drive past Kestrel and keep driving.*

She had not kept driving. And now his boat was adrift in the east cove with a note in a sandwich bag.

On the desk, under a blotter worn soft at the edges, she found what she was looking for without knowing she'd been looking for it: a desk calendar, the old paper kind, one month to a page. Every Saturday for the past year carried the same notation in those blocky capitals—*FISH, 6 AM*—and on last Saturday, three days ago, a second line beneath it, added in a different pen, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

**IF ANYTHING HAPPENS — CALLOWAY GIRL. TRUST HER. NOT THE OFFICE.**

Sable stood very still in the little room, listening to the clock on the mantel and to Carol moving restlessly in the kitchen, and she understood that Amos Greer had known. Had known for days, maybe weeks—since she'd come to his door asking questions he couldn't answer, since the word *Kestrel* had come out of him like a splinter working loose. He had known what knowing cost in Harrow Bend. He had written it down anyway, in hard pen, and entrusted it to the one honest deputy, and then he had gotten in his boat on a Thursday morning and driven to the east cove, either at gunpoint or under promise, and left behind a note written in his own hand that said everything except the truth.

He had left a message for whoever found the boat, and a message for whoever found the desk, and both messages were for other people. The real message—the one that mattered—he had tried to give to Rhea Calloway, and Rhea hadn't known to ask, and now a seventy-one-year-old man was somewhere in the dark with his calendar open on his desk saying *if anything happens.*

Her fault. The thought arrived without drama, the way the worst thoughts do, plain as a ledger entry. She had knocked on his door. She had asked her questions and let him see her face and let him understand that someone, finally, was pulling the thread—and a man who had spent twelve years keeping silent had felt the silence crack, and the family whose alibi he had once protected had heard it crack too. She had lit the fuse by existing in his doorway. Her investigation may have killed him, and the only comfort the thought offered was none at all: that if she stopped now, he would have vanished for nothing, and so would Maren Voss, and so would Dale Pruitt, and the machinery would grind on grinding, smooth and beloved and applauded.

"Dr. Oyelaran?" Carol stood in the doorway, eyes red, holding a photograph she hadn't yet decided to show anyone. "There's something else. His truck's still at the ramp. But his other car—the old Buick he kept in the shed—it's gone. And the shed door was padlocked from the outside. Somebody locked it after."

After. Sable looked at the calendar, at the embossed letters, at the trophies of a man who had once sworn an oath to protect a town and had spent his retirement learning what the town actually belonged to.

"Not the office," she said quietly, mostly to herself. "Calloway girl."

* * *

She found Rhea at the end of her shift, sitting in her personal car in the far corner of the Foodway lot, engine off, hands at ten and two on the wheel of a vehicle going nowhere. Sable tapped on the window, and Rhea unlocked the doors, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Through the windshield the town of Harrow Bend went about its evening: porch lights coming on, the diner sign buzzing pink, somebody's dog trotting home along the curb. A town that believed its retired police chief had gone fishing and forgotten to come back.

"He wrote your name down," Sable said. "Before it happened. 'If anything happens—Calloway girl. Trust her. Not the office.'"

Rhea stared straight ahead. Sable watched her jaw work once, twice, the way it did when she was keeping herself level by force. "I keep thinking," Rhea said finally, "that he tried to warn me and I didn't even know enough to check on him. Three days. He's been gone three days and we're finding the note *now* because a game warden saw a boat."

"They wanted the note found late. Late makes it look like wandering. Like age. Like a man who drove off confused and will turn up embarrassed in Chattanooga." Sable looked out at the pink sign, the porch lights, the ordinary blessed surface of the town. "It's the same hand as Pruitt, Rhea. Same staging. Same neat little story left out where we'd trip over it. They're telling us a story again."

"What story?"

"That there's nothing to find." She turned to look at the young deputy, and her voice came out lower than she intended, stripped of everything but the fact underneath it. "But here's what the story can't hide. There's no body. And a man like Amos Greer doesn't get a clean disappearance unless someone needs time—time to decide what to do with him, or time to make sure of something. Which means one of two things. Either he's already dead and hidden somewhere that hasn't given him up yet—"

"Or he's alive," Rhea said, "and they're keeping him the way they kept Maren Voss."

The words hung in the closed car, in the smell of cold coffee and vinyl, and Sable felt the case turn over inside her like a body turning on a table—murder becoming rescue becoming something larger, a pattern with a shape she could finally see the edges of. Accidents, plural. Years of them. A tractor rollover two summers ago that she had quietly proven and quietly buried in her files because no one would listen. Dale Pruitt. Now Greer. All of it feeding the same mouth, the same beloved family, the same reservoir with its billboards rising over the pines.

"Rhea," she said. "The sealed file. The surveyors' complaints from Kestrel. I need them tonight, and I need them in a way that doesn't have your name on it."

Rhea Calloway looked at her for a long moment. Then she reached into the console, took out a slim manila envelope that had clearly been waiting there for hours—for this exact conversation, for this exact moment when the boat and the note and the calendar all arrived at her door at once—and laid it on the seat between them.

"I pulled it this morning," she said. "Soon as dispatch ran the registration. Because I remembered something, Dr. Oyelaran. Something Greer told me my first year, when I asked him why he really retired." Her voice was steady now, steady the way ice is steady. "He said, *'Some cases you close. Some cases you just stop opening.'*"

Outside, the pink diner sign buzzed and flickered, and somewhere across the dark water of Kestrel Reservoir, in a cove or a cabin or a flooded quarry, an old man was waiting for someone to stop closing cases and start opening them.
