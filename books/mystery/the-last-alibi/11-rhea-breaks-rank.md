## 11. Rhea Breaks Rank

The evidence locker at the Harrow Bend Sheriff's Department smelled of cold coffee and old paper, and Rhea Calloway had learned to love it the way other people loved the smell of rain. It was the smell of things that couldn't lie. A blood vial sealed in 2019 didn't care who signed its chain of custody. A swab in a cardboard box didn't flatter anyone. She stood in the narrow aisle between the shelving units at six-forty on a Tuesday morning, an hour before the day shift trickled in, holding Dale Pruitt's kit against her chest like something that might still be warm.

She had told herself she wasn't going to do this. She had told herself for eleven days.

The problem was the report. Sable Oyelaran's autopsy notes had come through channels the way all coroner's paperwork came through channels—photocopied, stapled, filed—and Rhea had read them three times before she caught herself reading them a fourth time with a highlighter in her hand. Ligature furrow, posterior hairline. Petechial hemorrhaging inconsistent with simple asphyxial drowning. The sheriff's incident summary said accidental drowning, alcohol a contributing factor, case closed pending toxicology. The tox screen came back clean. Not low. Clean. Dale Pruitt, who'd had two DUIs and a beer can collection his ex-wife used to complain about, had died sober in Kestrel Reservoir with a rope burn on the back of his neck, and nobody upstairs wanted to know why.

Rhea was twenty-six years old and had been a deputy for four years, and in those four years she had learned that most corruption wasn't dramatic. It was administrative. It was a report that never got written. It was a supervisor saying *let's wait on that* until waiting became forgetting. She had grown up two counties over, in a town smaller than Harrow Bend, where her father had fixed tractors for forty years and taught her that a machine tells you the truth if you're willing to get your hands dirty looking for it.

So she got her hands dirty.

Pruitt's reference sample had been pulled during a 2021 domestic call—standard practice, sealed, forgotten. She logged it out under the open vandalism case from the Fillmore Road bridge, which technically required DNA exclusion testing, which technically made her request legitimate. The paperwork would survive an audit. Whether she would survive what came back was another question entirely.

The county lab contract went through a facility in Danvers, forty minutes east, run by a harried woman named Dr. Okonkwo who owed Rhea a favor dating back to a domestic violence call where Rhea had waited three hours in a parking lot so the woman could collect her documents without her husband seeing. Favors like that were the only currency that held value in Harrow Bend.

Rhea drove out herself, on her lunch break, in her personal car, with the sample in a cooler bag beside a sandwich she never ate. She didn't put anything in writing beyond the vandalism file. She paid the rush fee in cash.

"Exclusionary only," Dr. Okonkwo said, sliding the intake form across. "You're not asking me to run it against anything."

"Nope," Rhea said. "Just Pruitt. Confirm it's his blood at the scene or it isn't."

It was a lie by omission, and she felt it sit in her stomach the whole drive back, heavier than the sandwich.

* * *

The call came Thursday morning, while Rhea was refilling the department's coffee urn because Deputy Loomis never did it and complaining about it was easier than doing it herself. Her cell buzzed in her pocket. Danvers area code.

"Ms. Calloway." Dr. Okonkwo's voice was careful, flattened, the voice of someone aware the line might have ears. "Your exclusionary sample. It's Pruitt's. That part's confirmed."

"Okay," Rhea said slowly. "And?"

"And the extraction batch it ran with included a quality-control pass against our offender index—a routine contamination check we do quarterly. Your sample produced a secondary association. A familial-adjacent match to a profile already in the system." A pause, long enough for the coffee urn to gurgle behind Rhea. "I shouldn't be telling you this over the phone. I definitely shouldn't be telling you this at all. But you drove out here with cash and a sad turkey sandwich, so I'm going to trust you're not the problem."

"Who's the profile?" Rhea had moved into the supply closet without deciding to. She closed the door. The darkness smelled of bleach and toner cartridges.

"The secondary match belongs to a county employee sample collected under the municipal screening program—road maintenance division, I believe. The name attached is Hollis Vann." Another pause. "Rhea. The match strength suggests first-degree relation. Father, son, brother. And the collection date on Vann's sample predates your drowning victim's death by eight months."

Hollis Vann. Rhea knew the name the way everyone in Harrow Bend knew it—not as a man but as a fixture. Hollis Vann drove the county grader. Hollis Vann had been photographed shaking hands with Everett Aldous at the last three ribbon-cuttings. Hollis Vann's brother-in-law managed the Aldous Development equipment yard. And Hollis Vann had been one of the first responders quoted in the *Courier* about poor Dale Pruitt, drowned drunk, such a shame, good man though, drank too much.

A brother. Or a father. Someone whose blood sat close enough to Hollis Vann's to leave skin cells under Dale Pruitt's fingernails or fibers on his collar—she wouldn't know exactly what until she read the full report, and she wasn't sure she'd ever be allowed to read the full report.

"You need to come pick up the physical copy today," Dr. Okonkwo said. "Before I'm required to notify the requesting agency of the ancillary finding. Which, per contract, will be—" she said the next words with the weariness of a woman reciting her own funeral, "—the office of Sheriff Dale McCready. Who, I note for the record, is the requesting agency."

McCready. Whose campaign signs in the last election had been printed free of charge by Aldous Development's sign shop. Whose daughter worked in Aldous Development's front office.

"I'll be there within the hour," Rhea said.

"Bring the sandwich this time," Okonkwo said, and hung up.

* * *

She made it back to the department at 3:40 p.m. with the report in a manila envelope inside her jacket, and she had exactly enough time to lock it in her personal locker, stand there with her forehead against the cool metal, and think, *now what*, before everything happened at once.

Sheriff McCready's door opened. He filled the frame the way he always did—big, gray-templed, hands loose at his sides, a man who had spent thirty years perfecting the appearance of patience. Behind him, in the bullpen, sat a man Rhea didn't recognize: suit, soft jaw, a laptop bag, the particular stillness of someone being paid by the hour to watch.

"Calloway," McCready said. "My office."

She went. She kept her face doing nothing, which was the hardest work she'd done all week.

"Where were you Tuesday?" McCready asked, settling behind his desk. He didn't sit so much as lower himself, a bear arranging itself.

"Lunch. Personal errand."

"Personal errand." He turned his monitor toward her. On it was a log she recognized with a lurch—the county lab's intake portal, the request line bearing her name and badge number, timestamped Tuesday, 12:17 p.m. "You ran a DNA comparison outside of any active case assignment. You used the Fillmore Road vandalism file as cover. You paid rush fees in cash." He let each item land like a stone dropped in a well. "That's falsification of official records, misuse of county resources, and circumvention of chain-of-command review. Three separate policy violations. Possibly criminal ones."

The room was very quiet. Through the blinds, Rhea could see the suited man in the bullpen, watching her reflection in the dark window of the trophy case.

"Sheriff, the Pruitt case has inconsistencies that—"

"The Pruitt case is closed." McCready's voice didn't rise. That was the frightening thing about him; it never rose. "Closed by the coroner's initial findings, closed by the state's concurrence, closed. What it isn't is yours to reopen on a lunch break with cash out of your own pocket like some kind of—" he searched for the word and found it with visible satisfaction, "—vigilante."

"I followed procedure. The vandalism file justified exclusionary testing."

"And the familial hit?" McCready leaned forward. "Did procedure justify that too? Because I've got a call from the county attorney's office asking why one of my deputies is fishing around in genetic databases connected to a closed drowning. Asking why she drove to Danvers in her personal vehicle. Asking what else she's been driving around to." He folded his hands. "You're suspended pending internal investigation. Thirty days minimum. Badge and weapon on my desk. You'll want to think carefully about cooperation when the investigator meets with you Monday."

The suspension order was already typed. Already printed. Signed with a date of *today*. Rhea looked at it and understood, with a clarity that felt almost peaceful, that this document had existed before she walked into the building. Maybe before she drove back from Danvers. The machinery hadn't been reacting to her. It had been waiting for her.

She set her badge down. Then her weapon. Then, after a moment's consideration, she left the manila envelope where it was, flat against her ribs, inside the jacket McCready's eyes flicked toward and dismissed.

"Any personal effects in your locker need clearing out?" McCready asked.

"Just a granola bar and a photo of my dad's tractor," Rhea said. "Leave 'em. I'll be back."

At the door, McCready said, quietly, almost kindly: "Rhea. Whatever Oyelaran's been feeding you—whatever stories that woman tells herself about the dead—you're young. You've got a career ahead of you. Don't spend it on a drowning."

He said *drowning* the way a man says a password he doesn't realize is a confession.

* * *

She sat in her car in the department lot for twenty minutes, engine off, watching the building. The suited man left at 4:15, got into a charcoal Audi with government plates that weren't quite government plates, and drove west—toward the reservoir, toward the Aldous corridor. Rhea wrote down the plate number on the back of a receipt with fingers that wouldn't quite steady.

Her phone. They'd want her phone. They'd take it Monday, or try to, and everything on it would become theirs. She powered it off, pulled the battery on the old model she'd never upgraded because her father said planned obsolescence was a scam, and wrapped both parts in a gas station napkin in the glove box.

Then she thought about who she could call. Her sister in Columbus—no, too far, too visible. Loomis—Loomis was decent but decent men folded; she'd seen Loomis look away from things twice this year alone. Her landlord—no.

There was one person in Harrow Bend who dealt in secrets professionally, who had a building with steel doors and refrigeration units and a back entrance nobody watched because nobody liked going there. One person who had looked at Dale Pruitt's neck and written down the truth knowing no one upstairs would read it.

Sable Oyelaran answered on the second ring, from a number Rhea had gotten off the coroner's public contact page and never expected to use.

"It's Calloway. Deputy Calloway."

"I know who it is," Sable said. Her voice was low, unhurried, the voice of a woman who talked to the dead and had stopped expecting much from the living. "You sound like you're calling from inside a car."

"I am."

"You sound like you're calling from inside a car you don't plan to go home in."

Rhea laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "They suspended me. Fabricated charges—well, not even fabricated, just *weaponized*. Everything I did was technically real, they just decided it was a crime the moment it pointed somewhere they didn't like. There's a hit, Ms. Oyelaran. A DNA hit tying Hollis Vann's family to Dale Pruitt's scene, collected eight months before he died, and McCready knew about it before I was back in the building, and there's a man in a suit sitting in our bullpen right now who drives a car with fake government plates, and I have a paper report in my jacket that I am probably going to get killed for carrying."

Silence on the line. Long enough that Rhea checked the call hadn't dropped.

"Drive to the funeral home," Sable said finally. "Not the front. Circle around to the service alley on the north side, past the dumpsters. The door with the gray keypad. Code is 4-4-1-9. Come inside, lock it behind you, and sit down in the arrangement room. I'll be there in twenty minutes."

"Why the funeral home?"

"Because," Sable said, and Rhea heard something in her voice that wasn't quite humor and wasn't quite grief, "it's the only place in this town where everybody's already told the truth. And nobody in Harrow Bend comes here unless they have to. Even the people hunting you won't want to walk past three bodies in cold storage to ask their questions. Among the dead is the safest place a living witness can be."

The line went dead. Rhea sat in the cooling car as the light went amber over the water tower, then started the engine and drove, checking her mirror more often than the road.

* * *

The arrangement room of the Hollowell & Oyelaran Funeral Home was designed to comfort the bereaved and instead felt like a hotel lobby designed by someone who hated hotels—burgundy carpet, silk ficus trees, a couch no one had ever napped on. Rhea sat on it with the manila envelope on her knees and listened to the building tick and hum around her: the compressor in the prep area, the ancient furnace, the wind working at the loading dock door. Somewhere deeper in the building, behind a steel door, the town's dead lay in their drawers, keeping better order than the living ever had.

Sable arrived in nineteen minutes, letting herself in with a key, wearing a wool coat over her autopsy scrubs like a woman who'd come straight from work and saw no reason to pretend otherwise. She was fifty-something, silver-streaked, with the kind of face that gave away nothing except tiredness, and she looked at Rhea the way Rhea imagined she looked at the newly arrived dead: thoroughly, kindly, cataloguing damage.

"Show me," she said.

Rhea handed over the report. Watched her read it. Sable read the way she presumably autopsied—slowly, twice, lips moving slightly over the numbers.

"Familial match to Hollis Vann," Sable said at last. "First-degree. Sample predating the death." She lowered the pages. "Do you understand what this means?"

"That whoever killed Dale Pruitt is close enough to Hollis Vann to share half his genes. A brother, maybe. Or—" Rhea hesitated. "Or the sample's mislabeled. Or Vann himself handled the body and the lab picked up transferred cells."

"Or," Sable said quietly, "the county employee screening program collected samples from a lot of men who work around Aldous equipment yards, and one of them wore gloves that had belonged to somebody else. It doesn't matter which way it cuts yet. What matters is that it exists, it's documented, and now they know you have it." She set the report on the coffee table between them, deliberately, like laying a card face-up. "They'll come for this. Tonight, tomorrow. They'll come politely first—an investigator wanting to 'review' the chain of custody. Then less politely."

"Then I should give it to someone. State police. The attorney general—"

"With what standing? You're a suspended deputy under investigation for evidence tampering. Anything you hand over gets quarantined, and the quarantine gets leaked, and by Friday the story is *rogue deputy plants DNA to frame county worker*." Sable sat down across from her, folding her hands. "No. This stays where they can't reach it. We make copies—one set goes somewhere they'll never think to look, one set stays in play. And you stay here tonight."

"Here." Rhea looked around the burgundy room. "In a funeral home."

"There's a cot in the staff room. The coffee's terrible. Mrs. Adeyemi next door brings pie on Thursdays, and today is Thursday." Sable's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Deputy. In eleven days you've lost your badge, your weapon, and your career, and you handed me the only piece of hard evidence this case has ever had. So let me tell you the thing I've learned from ten years of listening to the dead in this town."

"What's that?"

"The truth doesn't die in Harrow Bend. It just waits for somebody honest enough to carry it." She stood, and nodded toward the hallway. "Come on. I'll show you where the extra blankets are. And then you're going to tell me everything, from the beginning, in order, and leave nothing out—including the license plate."

Rhea followed her down the corridor, past the framed sympathy cards and the price list for caskets, and it occurred to her, walking deeper into the house of the dead, that she had never in her life felt safer than she did right now.

Behind them, out on Route 9, headlights swept slow past the funeral home's dark windows once, twice—and did not turn in. Not yet.

But they'd be back.
