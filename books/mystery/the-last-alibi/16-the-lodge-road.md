## 16. The Lodge Road

The snow came in off the reservoir like something with intent.

Sable had watched weather her whole life the way other people watched television—casually, half-attentively—but this storm had a direction to it, a purpose, rolling down from the high country in gray sheets that erased the treeline and then the road and then, eventually, the hood of Rhea Calloway's truck. The wipers fought a losing war. The heater rattled. And Sable sat in the passenger seat with a thermos of coffee going cold between her knees, watching the world disappear one white inch at a time, and thought: *good.*

Storms were cover. Storms meant the lodge's cameras saw nothing but static, meant any patrol car from the county sat warm at its station, meant that if a woman was up there behind glass, the men who kept her had no reason to look twice at two sets of headlights crawling the back way through Aldous timber land.

"You're sure about this road," Rhea said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Amos fished that lake forty years," Sable said. "He used to complain about the logging spur every summer. Said it washed out every spring and the county repaved it every fall and nobody ever asked why a private hunting lodge needed county asphalt." She turned the thermos lid into a cup and poured, steam curling up into the cold cab. "Nobody asks why about the Aldouses. That's the whole architecture of the place."

Rhea drove with both hands on the wheel, jaw set, eyes moving between the white road and the mirror more often than the road warranted. She'd been suspended for six days now. Six days of sitting in Sable's guest room above the funeral home, sleeping badly, helping Sable pin photographs of documents to corkboard in the embalming prep room because it was the only room in Harrow Bend with a lock Rhea trusted. The suspension letter had come down on fabricated charges—evidence mishandling, unauthorized database access—and everyone in the sheriff's department understood exactly what it meant, which was that Sheriff Dunmore's office had finally decided Rhea Calloway was a problem rather than a person.

"They'll have fired me by Friday," Rhea had said last night, not looking up from the corkboard. "Formal termination pending review. Which means I'm not a cop anymore when we do this."

"Which means," Sable had said, "that whatever happens on that mountain is done by two private citizens, one of whom has no legal authority whatsoever."

"And you?"

"I've never had any legal authority worth mentioning," Sable said. "I weigh organs and write reports. The state does the arresting." She'd said it lightly, and Rhea had almost smiled, and neither of them had said the true thing, which was that if it went wrong on that mountain there would be no report, no arrest, no weighing of anything except what the snow gave back in April.

Now Rhea took the turnoff onto the logging spur without slowing, and the truck's tires found ruts frozen hard as iron, and the trees closed overhead until the headlights showed nothing but falling snow and black trunks sliding past like the ribs of some enormous drowned animal.

"Half a mile," Sable said. "There's a pull-off where the loggers used to stage. Amos called it the wide spot. We walk from there."

"Walk. In this."

"In this," Sable agreed. "Because in this, nobody's watching the road. And because I want to see the generator before they hear us coming."

* * *

They left the truck at the wide spot under a stand of hemlocks that held most of the snow aloft, and walked the last stretch with the wind at their backs, pushing them uphill toward the thing they'd come to see. Sable wore her winter autopsy coat—thick wool, dark green, the one garment she owned built for standing motionless for hours—and beneath it layers she hadn't worn since medical school in Chicago, where she'd learned that cold was just another kind of evidence: it recorded everything, it preserved everything, it told the truth about timelines better than any witness.

Rhea carried a pair of binoculars and, against Sable's advice, her service weapon.

"If someone finds us," Rhea said, "I'd rather be an armed trespasser than a missing one."

Sable didn't argue. There was no arguing with a woman who'd spent eleven years believing in the badge and then watched the badge get used to hang her. You didn't take a person's last piece of armor away three days before you asked them to walk up a mountain.

The spur climbed through second-growth pine, and the snow deepened, and the sound of the world narrowed to wind and their own breathing and the crunch-crunch-crunch of boots that Sable found herself counting the way she counted sutures, automatic, steadying. Four hundred steps. Five hundred. At six hundred the trees opened and there it was.

The Aldous hunting lodge sat on a shelf of land above Kestrel Reservoir like a statement of ownership. It was big—not resort-big, but the kind of big that announced itself: timber and stone, three stories at the gable end, a wraparound porch built for people who never actually sat on porches, windows glowing amber against the storm. A gravel motor court, mostly buried. A garage with two bay doors. And beside the garage, humming through the snowfall with a low diesel note Sable felt in her molars before she heard it properly—

A generator. Big industrial unit, the size of a chest freezer, exhaust plume rising straight up and then shredding sideways in the wind.

"It's February," Rhea whispered. They'd stopped at the treeline, crouched behind a deadfall crusted in ice. "Who runs a generator in February? They're on the grid. Look—the power line comes up the spur."

"Look again," Sable said. "Where's the line connected?"

Rhea raised the binoculars, studied the lodge's near corner, and went very still.

"There's no meter," she said slowly. "No drop line to the building. The line just... ends. It's decorative. It's been decorative for years." She lowered the binoculars. "They run on generator all the time. Why would anyone do that?"

"Because generators don't leave records," Sable said. "Grid power tells the co-op when you're home, when you're not, how many lights are burning at three in the morning. A generator tells you nothing. It's a house that doesn't testify." She pulled her own binoculars up and began the slow, patient work of reading the building the way she read a body—from the outside in, every anomaly noted, every explanation tested against the alternatives.

Two vehicles under the carport: Julian Aldous's black Escalade, which she'd seen at the gala, and a gray F-250 with a snowplow mount. One man moved between the porch and the garage, big, deliberate, carrying firewood in loads too small to be efficient—he wasn't hauling wood, he was making rounds. Checking sightlines. Every third trip he paused at the corner of the porch and looked out at the reservoir, unhurried, thorough. A man being paid to be bored.

"Two more inside, minimum," Rhea murmured. "You don't post one man on a hostage."

"Agreed. Watch the upstairs."

The lodge's second floor had a row of dormer windows under the steep roofline, and most were dark. But the fourth dormer from the left—the one facing the reservoir, the one whose light would be invisible from the main road but plain as a struck match from anywhere on the water—glowed soft and yellow behind what looked like a heavy curtain drawn to a hand's width.

And then the silhouette crossed it.

Sable had prepared herself for it. She'd told Rhea on the drive up: *we may see nothing, we may see a shadow, do not react, do not move.* But preparation is a story you tell yourself, and the body doesn't listen. When the shape moved behind the glass—a woman's shape, slight, unhurried, pausing at the gap in the curtain as if looking out at the storm—something in Sable's chest clenched so hard it hurt, and beside her she heard Rhea's breath catch and hold.

The figure stood at the window for perhaps ten seconds. Long enough. Long enough to see that she moved like a living woman moves, weight shifting, one hand rising to touch the glass or the curtain. Then she stepped back and the glow dimmed and the dormer went quiet.

"That's her," Rhea said. Her voice came out wrecked. She cleared her throat. "That's Maren Voss."

"That's a woman," Sable said carefully. "In a room she can't leave, in a house owned by people who need her invisible. That's enough to plan on. It has to be enough."

But it wasn't, and they both knew it, and they lay in the snow another hour while the storm thickened and the wood-hauling man made his rounds and the dormer light burned steady, and Sable catalogued everything: the rounds came every eleven minutes; the generator's exhaust plume bent east; the garage's third bay door, half-hidden by drift, had a padlock hasp that hung open—someone using that door often, carelessly, because no one was expected. The cellar doors on the north side, old timber storm cellar, boards silvered with age, snow drifted against them but not *over* them—cleared recently, or used recently. One or the other.

At 9:40 by Sable's watch, the dormer light went out.

"She sleeps early," Rhea whispered. "Or they make her sleep early."

"Or," Sable said, "she keeps her own hours, in her own head, in the dark. Maren Voss wrote in a diary every night for years. Women like that don't stop writing because someone locks a door. They find pencil stubs. They find margins." She got stiffly to her knees. "Come on. We've seen what we came to see. Anything longer and we start leaving sign."

* * *

They didn't speak until the truck, until the heater had fought the cab back to something like warmth and Rhea had the wheels pointed down the spur with the storm erasing their tracks behind them faster than they could be laid down. Then Rhea said, "Groundbreaking's the twenty-eighth. Three weeks."

"Three weeks," Sable agreed. "Everett Aldous is going to stand up in front of two hundred people and the lieutenant governor and break ground on his resort, and every deputy in the county will be working security detail or traffic, and Julian will be at his father's side being photographed being dutiful." She turned the cold cup in her hands. "The night of the groundbreaking, that lodge will have one man on it. Maybe two. The help gets invited down to watch the party on somebody's phone. It's the thinnest that door will ever be."

"And we go through the cellar doors."

"We go through the cellar doors," Sable said. "North side, below the grade of the porch, out of the wind. Rhea holds the road at the wide spot—if anything comes up that spur, she sees it ten minutes before it sees the lodge. I go in alone."

"The hell you do alone."

"Alone," Sable said, and her voice had the flatness she saved for families in viewing rooms, the tone that ended discussion without cruelty. "One person moves quiet. Two people move like a search party. And if I'm caught, I'm a coroner out walking in a storm who got lost—which is thin, but it's breathing. If you're caught, you're a suspended deputy trespassing on Aldous property with a firearm the night their patriarch breaks ground on his empire. They won't call that trespassing, Rhea. They'll call it exactly what it looks like, and they'll be right."

Rhea stared out at the snow-lashed windshield, hands strangling the wheel, and Sable watched her swallow the argument whole and hate the taste.

"Eleven minutes," Rhea said finally. "His rounds are eleven minutes. That's your window per circuit."

"That's my window," Sable said. "And I'll need four of them. Cellar, stairs, the room, and back. If she's willing to come."

Rhea's head turned. "*If?*"

Sable looked at her own reflection ghosted in the dark glass, and beyond it the white roaring dark, and chose her words the way she chose them for the bereaved—with precision, because precision was the only mercy she had.

"Maren Voss has been a prisoner for over a year. Prisoners learn things about their keepers. Sometimes they learn where the bodies are buried—sometimes literally. If she comes out of that lodge, she comes out as the only living witness to everything, and the moment she testifies, her life becomes worth killing for, permanently, publicly, forever. She knows that better than we do. She's had a year to think about it." Sable set the cup down. "So I'm not going to drag her out. I'm going to show her the diary pages Pryce described—the forged ones, the ones written in her name—and I'm going to ask her what she wants to do. Because the worst thing we could do to that woman is rescue her without her consent. She's had enough of other people writing her story."

The truck descended through the storm. The reservoir appeared and vanished between the trees below them, black and vast, holding its secrets the way it always had.

"Three weeks," Rhea said again, quieter.

"Three weeks of patience," Sable said. "We scout once more, from the water side, when the ice is safer. We map the rounds. We write it all down and lock it in cold storage with the diary. And we tell no one—not Greer's memory, not the journalist yet, no one—until Maren is out and talking."

She paused. Something had been sitting at the edge of her mind since the window, since the silhouette, since the hand that rose toward the glass, and she let it arrive now, fully formed, unwelcome:

"Rhea. The curtain was drawn to a hand's width. From inside."

"So?"

"So prisoners don't get to choose their curtains. Someone left that gap so she could see out—or so someone could see in. Either way, it means her keeper wants her able to look at the reservoir." Sable watched the dark water slide past below the road. "Julian Aldous keeps that woman alive and visible to the lake he poisoned. That's not mercy. That's a man keeping a receipt."

The snow fell harder. Somewhere behind them, up on its shelf above the water, the lodge burned its amber windows into the storm, and behind the fourth dormer, in the dark, a woman who had been declared dead was waiting—patient or broken or planning, they wouldn't know for three weeks—and Sable Oyelaran rode down the mountain with her heart beating a slow, cold rhythm that sounded, to her own ears, like a countdown.

Three weeks later, on the morning of the groundbreaking, Sable would wake before dawn to find a message waiting that changed everything: a photograph, sent from an untraceable account, showing the fourth dormer window of the lodge—dark, boarded over from the inside.

And scrawled across the image, in handwriting she knew from a hundred pages of forged diary entries, five words:

*SHE KNOWS YOU'RE COMING.*
