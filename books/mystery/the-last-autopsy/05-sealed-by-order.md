## 5. Sealed by Order

Mara Voss had been a deputy for exactly eleven days when she discovered that the filing cabinet in the back of the Hollow Creek sheriff's office had a personality.

It was an ugly green thing, older than she was, with one drawer that stuck and another that opened too easily, spilling its contents like a drunk telling secrets. She'd been assigned to "familiarize herself with county records," which Sheriff Dunmore had said the way a man says *please stop asking me questions*, and so she familiarized. Methodically. Alphabetically. The way her mother had taught her to clean a kitchen — top shelf down, everything touched at least once.

She was in the P's — property disputes, mostly, decades of neighbors feuding over fence lines with a passion usually reserved for blood feuds — when she noticed the gap.

The drawer didn't have gaps. It was the kind of archive where every case got a folder whether it deserved one or not. She'd found files on a dog bite from 1987. A file on a stolen bicycle that turned out to have been borrowed by the owner's own son, who confessed in a handwritten note still paper-clipped inside. A file consisting entirely of one page: *Complaint re: rooster noise. Resolved. Rooster relocated.* To where, the file did not say.

But between *Pruitt, Dale* and *Pruitt, Dale (duplicate)* there should have been more. She'd seen the Pruitt name before — in Orsen Galloway's morgue, on a shelf of old boxes he kept like a man keeping ashes. Drowned fisherman. 2003. Ruled accidental.

Mara pulled the duplicate folder. Inside: a single sheet of county letterhead.

*Case No. 03-114. Records sealed by order of the Honorable A. Pell, Circuit Court. Pursuant to statute. All originals remanded to judicial custody.*

No reason given. No statute cited by number. Just the seal — a faded purple stamp, slightly crooked, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper — and a signature that swept across the bottom like a man signing his own name in his sleep.

*A. Pell.*

She knew the name the way everyone in Hollow Creek knew the name. Judge Ambrose Pell had presided over this circuit for thirty-one years, retired six years ago to a white house on Calloway Hill with a garden that won ribbons, and was now the closest thing the town had to royalty. There was a plaque with his face on it in the courthouse lobby. Mara walked past it every morning. He always looked mildly disappointed in her, even in bronze.

Sealed records weren't unheard of. Juvenile cases, adoption files, the occasional witness-protection situation. But those came with paperwork. Petitions, justifications, a paper trail a deputy could follow if she were inclined to follow things, which she was, professionally speaking, extremely inclined.

This was just a stamp. One stamp, on one case.

Mara sat back on the rolling chair — which squeaked, loudly, every time, as though the building itself wanted to know what she was doing — and did the thing she always did when a fact didn't fit: she made a list. In her notebook, small neat print, because lists were how she thought and her mother had also taught her that.

*1. Pruitt drowning, 2003 — sealed, A. Pell.*
*2. ???*

Item two arrived forty minutes later, after she'd abandoned the P's entirely and gone hunting through the index cards — actual index cards, in a wooden box, because Hollow Creek hadn't fully committed to computers until roughly the Obama administration. She cross-referenced accidental deaths, 1998 through 2010, and pulled every folder that looked thin.

Two more had the same stamp.

Harlan Beckett, 2006. Fell from a grain elevator. Sealed.

Wendell Marsh, 2009. Farm machinery. Sealed.

Three cases. Three stamps. Same crooked purple seal, same sleeping signature, spread across six years like a man checking in on something periodically, the way you water a plant.

Mara closed the drawer very gently, the way you close a door when you're not sure what's behind it, and became aware — in the way you become aware of weather — that the office had gone quiet around her. Deputy Kowalski was at the front desk doing the crossword. Sheriff Dunmore was in his office with the door open, which he never bothered closing unless something mattered, and he was looking at her.

Not staring. Just looking. The way a man looks at a clock he isn't sure is running fast or slow.

"Finding everything all right, Voss?" he called.

"Learning the geography," she said, and smiled, and held up the rooster file. "Did we ever find out where they took the rooster?"

Dunmore laughed, easy and warm, and went back to whatever he was reading, and Mara felt the chill anyway — small, irrational, sitting at the base of her neck like a draft. Because she had not mentioned the sealed files. Had not asked about them. And yet for half a second there, the sheriff had watched her the way you watch someone who might be about to open a drawer.

*Paranoia,* she told herself. *You've been here eleven days. Nobody seals a murder case and then surveils the new hire.*

But she wrote it down anyway. Item three.

*3. Dunmore looked.*

* * *

The problem with an off-the-books inquiry, Mara reflected that evening over gas-station chili in her rented room above the hardware store, was that "off the books" meant no books. No official requests, no database pulls that left timestamps, nothing she couldn't plausibly deny having done. Which meant legwork. Old-fashioned, unglamorous, boot-leather legwork, which happened to be her favorite kind, though she'd have denied that under oath too.

Legwork item one: the courthouse itself. If the originals had been "remanded to judicial custody," they lived somewhere — a judge's chambers, a records vault, some climate-controlled room where inconvenient truths went to be preserved for posterity. She'd mapped the courthouse layout during her first week out of pure habit, and she knew there was a vault in the basement, behind the probate office, with a door that looked like it had opinions.

Legwork item two: the seal itself. Stamps could be traced. Court clerks logged them — or used to. And court clerks, in Mara's experience, remembered everything, because remembering was most of the job.

Which was how, the next morning, she found herself buying two coffees and carrying them into the probate office with the studied casualness of a woman who definitely had not rehearsed this in the car.

Ruth Ann Deverell presided over the clerk's desk like a lighthouse — solid, immovable, radiating a beam of attention that made people confess things unprompted. She was maybe sixty, silver hair pinned with military precision, reading glasses on a chain, and she looked up at Mara with the expression of someone who had already catalogued the coffee, the smile, and the ulterior motive, and found all three acceptable.

"Deputy Voss." Ruth Ann took the coffee. "Cream, two sugars. You asked Kowalski."

"I did my research."

"Kowalski tells everyone his coffee order within four minutes of meeting them. It's not research, it's survival." She sipped. "What do you need?"

Straight to it, then. Mara liked her enormously already.

"Procedure question," Mara said. "Hypothetical. Say an old case file gets sealed by judicial order. Where do the originals physically go?"

"The vault. Basement level, east wall. Everything sealed since 1975 is down there."

"And who has access?"

"Judges. Active judges, I mean. The sealing judge, for life — courtesy, mostly. And me." Ruth Ann set down the cup. "And whoever a judge sends, with written authorization. Why? You working a case?"

"Records familiarization," Mara said, deploying the phrase Dunmore had given her like a shield.

"Mm." Ruth Ann regarded her over the glasses with the patience of a woman who had heard every lie a government building could produce. "Familiarization. Well. Since you're familiarizing, you should know the vault log is public record. Who went in, when, how long. Requests to view sealed material are logged too, and those require the requester's name in writing." She picked up her pen. "So think carefully before you familiarize yourself with anything down there, Deputy. The vault doesn't just keep secrets. It keeps receipts."

"Understood," Mara said.

"And Deputy?" Ruth Ann smiled then, and it transformed her whole face into something almost mischievous. "The Pruitt file. 2003. That seal was entered by hand — old system, before we digitized. I remember because it was my third week on the job, same as you've had. Judge Pell brought the order down himself rather than sending it through channels. Unusual. Judges don't carry their own paperwork; that's what clerks are for." She turned back to her ledger. "I always wondered about that. Twenty years, off and on. Wondering's free. Asking questions downstairs isn't."

Mara stood very still. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you bought me coffee," Ruth Ann said, "and because in twenty years, nobody else ever asked." She waved her pen toward the door. "Go on. Some of us have probate to do. The dead of this county are very impatient about their estates."

* * *

That afternoon, Mara did the thing she'd been circling since the night before: she drove out to the cemetery.

Not for atmosphere, though Hollow Creek's cemetery had plenty — a hillside of leaning stones above the river bend, oaks throwing long shadows across the rows. She went for data. Harlan Beckett was buried there. So was Wendell Marsh. Dale Pruitt had a stone near the fence, facing the water, which struck her as either tender or cruel depending on the family.

She walked the rows with her notebook, writing down dates, cross-checking against what she knew. Beckett, 2006. Marsh, 2009. Pruitt, 2003. And then, because her feet apparently had their own agenda, she drifted toward the older section, the part where the stones went soft-edged with lichen, and found the Pell plot without trying.

It was immaculate. Of course it was. Granite polished to a mirror, a low iron fence, fresh plantings. And there, among the family names, a smaller stone, newer than the rest:

*Thomas Ambrose Pell. 1979–2002. Beloved son.*

Mara stood in the cold a long moment, breath fogging, notebook forgotten in her hand.

Twenty-two years ago. Before any of the sealed files. Before Pruitt, before Beckett, before Marsh. A boy who died young, in a town that remembered everything, and whose death — she'd stake her badge on it once she confirmed it — had been ruled accidental by a brand-new coroner named Orsen Galloway, in what everyone agreed was the first ruling of his career.

Four deaths. One family name connecting the oldest to the newest. And in between, three accidents that a judge had personally carried down to a basement vault and locked away, one at a time, across six years, like a man feeding a fire.

The thrill hit her then — the real one, the one she'd chased since she was nineteen and watching her mother read case files at the kitchen table like other mothers read novels. The click of pattern becoming story. The hunt opening in front of her like a road. Mara grinned at a headstone, alone on a hillside, feeling briefly, guiltily alive in a way that paperwork had never made her feel.

Then the chill arrived, right on schedule, because thrill and chill were conjoined twins in this business.

Because if she could see the pattern standing in a cemetery in one afternoon — a new deputy, eleven days on the job, working alone with a notebook — then other people could see it too. People who'd had twenty years instead of eleven days. People with keys to the vault. People whose faces hung in courthouse lobbies, mildly disappointed in bronze.

And one of those people had spent yesterday evening being charming at a garden party while quoting autopsy details that had never been public, according to a certain coroner who'd gone pale as his own table linen and pretended otherwise.

Mara zipped her jacket against the wind coming off the river and started down the hill. Her phone buzzed in her pocket. A text from Orsen Galloway, four words, no greeting:

*Can you come tonight. Please.*

She read it twice, thumb hovering, heart doing something unprofessional.

Below her, the river moved dark and patient around the bend, keeping whatever it kept.

Mara typed back: *On my way.*
