## 2. The Crypt That Isn't Empty

There is a particular kind of silence in a sealed crypt. I want to say it's the silence of the dead, but that would be sentimental and, worse, inaccurate. The dead are not quiet; they are simply finished. The silence of a sealed crypt is the silence of a room where no one has bothered to lie for forty years — no sermons, no apologies, no polished brass. Just stone telling the truth at last, which is that stone does not care about any of us.

I was thinking something along these lines as I stood at the top of the crypt stair with my hand on a door I had no business opening.

Let me be precise about the protocol violation, because precision is the only virtue I have never been accused of lacking. The undercroft and crypt of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral have been closed to all staff except the works department since 2019, when a survey found the vaulting "structurally ambiguous." The key lives in the safe in the vestry clerk's office. The logbook requires two signatures, one of which must be a member of the Chapter. I had neither signature nor sanction. What I had was a confession letter sitting in my coat pocket like a stone of my own, describing a murder committed in that crypt in March 1984, with details — the coffin on the north side, third bay, the one with the worn crusader effigy; the iron ring in the wall used to lower the bier; the smell of pigeon lime and old incense — that had never appeared in any public record.

I know this because I spent four hours after lunch verifying it. I pulled every press clipping, every parish magazine, every file the diocese deigned to keep. The 1984 death — because officially it was a death, an accident, a tragic fall on the crypt stair — was reported in exactly three places, and none of them mentioned the coffin, or the ring, or the smell. Those details belonged to someone who had been there. Someone who had perhaps been the only one there besides the dying.

So: either the letter was written by the killer, or by a witness with an inconvenient memory, or by someone who had recently stood in a sealed crypt and wanted me to know it.

The first two options were terrible. The third was worse, because it meant the crypt wasn't sealed at all, and everything else I believed about this building — that its silences held, that its locks worked, that its past stayed past — was a fiction I'd been paid eleven years to maintain.

I am not brave. Let's establish that now, so we can move past it. I am a person who alphabetizes for comfort and keeps a cardigan in the archive loft against the specific chill of microfilm rooms. But I have also spent eleven years learning that archives are not repositories of the past. They are truces. Somebody decided what got kept, and somebody else agreed to stop asking, and the whole arrangement holds only as long as nobody opens the wrong drawer. This morning, somebody had opened the wrong drawer and posted it to me.

I signed nothing. I took the spare key from the works cupboard — the one everyone pretends doesn't exist, taped behind the fire extinguisher inspection card since before my time — and I let myself into the dark.

* * *

The stair down is spiral, narrow, worn into shallow scoops by centuries of feet, and lit — normally — by nothing. I brought the good torch, the one with the strap, because if I was going to commit a firing offense I intended to do it with proper equipment. My breath made small ghosts ahead of me. The air changed temperature the way it always does going down into old buildings, dropping through decades with each turn of the stair: 1990s boiler-warmth, then the cool of the postwar repairs, then something older and damper that had no decade at all.

At the bottom, the crypt opened out into its low-vaulted length, pillars marching away into blackness, coffins stacked along the walls in their lead and elm shrouds like a congregation that had given up on standing. The effigies lay on top of the older tombs, hands folded, faces eroded to a kind of patient blankness. Forty years of dust lay over everything in a smooth grey skin.

Except.

You develop an eye for disturbance in my line of work. A misfiled document shows up like a scream. And the floor of the third bay on the north side was screaming.

The dust there was broken. Not scuffed — marked. Two shallow parallel grooves ran from the base of the coffin platform toward the pillar, as though something heavy had been dragged, or set down carefully and taken away again. And beside the coffin itself, on the ledge where the effigy's stone hand rested, there was wax.

Fresh candle wax. White, unaged, still slightly tacky when I touched it with a gloved fingertip, pooled and dripped in the unmistakable pattern of a candle left burning until it drowned in its own melt. Beneath it, a ring of soot on the stone. Under the soot, when I angled the torch low across the surface, faint scratch marks — deliberate, small, repeated. Letters.

I crouched there for a long time, longer than I should have, listening to my own pulse and the cathedral's enormous silence pressing down through the floors above. Then I photographed everything — the wax, the drag marks, the scratches, wide shot and detail, scale reference included, because even in extremis I am still myself — and only then did I look properly at the scratches themselves.

They were initials. Cut shallow, cut recently, in the stone beside the effigy's folded hands:

**E.M.**

Elspeth Marr. The name from the confession letter. The name that appears nowhere in the cathedral's records — I'd checked, exhaustively, and found her erased with the thoroughness of an institution that had once been very good at this sort of thing. Programmes skip from one organist to another between February and September 1984. The choir lists have a gap. Even the photograph boards in the music school corridor have a rectangle of unfaded paint where a frame once hung.

Someone had scratched her initials here, in the dark, beside her coffin — because I was increasingly sure it was her coffin, whatever the brass plate said about some medieval prior — and left a candle burning over them. A vigil. In a sealed crypt. Recently enough that the wax hadn't fully hardened.

Which meant the person who wrote to me this morning had come back. Or had never stopped coming.

I stood up too fast and my knee cracked in the dark like a gunshot, and I confess I said a word that would have gotten me a look from the Dean. The dark took it and gave nothing back. Somewhere above me, the great building settled and ticked, doing its nightly work of being enormous and indifferent, and I understood suddenly and completely that I could walk back up those stairs, lock the door, tape the key behind the fire extinguisher, and spend the rest of my career cataloguing bequest ledgers until retirement carried me out feet first. Nobody would ever know. That was precisely the problem. The letter knew. The wax knew. Whatever was happening in my cathedral had already decided I was part of it, and my choices now were only about which side of the record I'd end up on.

I'm an archivist. I believe in records. I climbed the stairs with my heart going like a novice bell-ringer and called the police.

* * *

Detective Inspector Sera Okonkwo arrived at 7:40 the next morning, which told me she hadn't slept on it, which told me the letter had rattled her more than her face admitted. Her face admitted very little. She was compact and unhurried and looked at my cathedral the way auditors look at accounts — politely, thoroughly, and without a single assumption of innocence.

"You're the archivist." She said it like a diagnosis.

"Bram Voss. Records and manuscripts." We shook hands in the cloister doorway, and hers was dry and firm and brief. "Thank you for coming quickly."

"You thanked me in your email four times. I counted." She held up a folder. "Your letter. The confession. Walk me through how you received it."

"Ordinary morning post. With the parish circulars and a conservation supplier's invoice."

"No courier, no hand delivery?"

"Through the letter slot like everyone else's junk mail."

She nodded slowly, making a note. "Whoever sent it knows the building's rhythms. Knows your post comes early, knows you open it yourself." She looked up. "How long have you worked here?"

"Eleven years."

"And in eleven years, has anyone ever confessed a murder to you by post?"

"Not until yesterday. I get the occasional request to prove someone's grandfather didn't steal a silver chalice, but that's the usual calibre."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Show me the crypt."

We went down together, and I watched her read the space the way I read a folio — systematically, top to bottom, letting the object speak first. She stood in the third bay for a long moment without touching anything, torch beam moving across the dust, the drag marks, the wax, the scratched initials.

"When was this last officially accessed?" she asked.

"The survey in 2019. Works department, structural inspection. Before that—" I'd checked the logbook until midnight. "Before that, 1986. Repairs to the stair after the accident."

"The accident." She turned. "The fall that killed Elspeth Marr."

"That's what the file calls it."

"And what do you call it?"

"I call it a file," I said, "with a hole in it shaped like a person."

Okonkwo looked at me for a beat longer than the question required, and I had the sense of being quietly reclassified in her mental index — from civilian nuisance to possibly useful. "Your letter describes the coffin in this bay," she said. "The iron ring. The smell. Correct?"

"Correct."

"None of that is public record. I've pulled what exists on the 1984 case — it's thin, closed within nine days, one detective inspector named Hale, cause of death accidental, case noted 'no suspicious circumstances' in handwriting that gave up halfway through the word 'circumstances.'" She crouched by the wax, examined it without touching. "But the letter also says something I haven't told you yet. It gives a date. The seventeenth of March. And it says, quote, 'she sang while she fell.'"

The words went through me coldly. "That's—"

"That's a detail that would require either the killer or a witness. It was never in any report. I've checked." She straightened. "And here's my problem, Mr. Voss. Either this letter is genuine, in which case a murderer has been walking around your cathedral for forty years and has just decided to start talking — or it's a fabrication by someone with access to this crypt, which means someone has been accessing this crypt." She gestured at the wax. "Either way, your sealed door isn't sealed, and I'd very much like to know who has keys."

"The official key is in the vestry clerk's safe. Signed access only. There's a works spare that's—"

"Is what?"

"Taped behind a fire extinguisher inspection card," I admitted, "and has been since approximately the reign of the previous bishop."

For the first time, Okonkwo closed her notebook and just looked at me. "You broke in here."

"I let myself in. Technically the door was locked."

"Mr. Voss. If this becomes a case, you're either a witness or a suspect, and the difference will largely depend on whether you tell me things before I find them. So." She reopened the notebook. "What else haven't you told me?"

I thought about lying. Genuinely, for a full three seconds, I considered it — considered protecting the ledger-shaped instinct already forming in the back of my mind, the sense that this building kept its secrets in ink somewhere and that whoever controlled the paper controlled the truth. But Okonkwo had counted my thank-yous. She was the kind of person who counted.

"There's a donation ledger," I said. "Churchwardens' accounts, continuous from 1740. It's in my catalogue queue. I haven't started it yet."

"Why are you telling me about a donation ledger?"

"Because last night I noticed the watermark on the confession letter. It's a private stock — a stationer in Bath, used by maybe a dozen institutions. One of them is us. The chapter office buys it for formal correspondence." I paused. "And the letter's handwriting uses a peculiar ampersand. Reversed loop. I've seen it before, in the margins of old chapter documents. I just don't know whose hand it is yet."

Okonkwo wrote for a while without speaking. Above us, faintly, the organ began its morning practice, Bach filtering down through forty feet of stone, beautiful and completely oblivious.

"One more question," she said finally. "The scratches. E.M. Who do you think cut them?"

"Someone who wanted her remembered," I said. "In the one place the institution couldn't reach with an eraser."

She snapped the notebook shut. "I'm putting a uniform on the crypt door. Discreet. And Mr. Voss — if another letter comes, if anyone approaches you, if you notice so much as a candle missing from the sacristy stock, you call me directly. Not through the switchboard. Directly." She handed me a card, plain white, mobile number in ballpoint beneath the printed ones. "Whoever wrote that letter chose you specifically. Eleven years of service, trusted, thorough, no family ties locally — I checked, forgive me — someone decided you were the person who'd take the past seriously." She glanced around the crypt, at the coffins in their patient rows. "They were right. That's the part that worries me."

She climbed the stair ahead of me, torchlight swinging shadows up the walls, and I stood a moment longer in the third bay, looking at the scratched letters and the drowned candle and the smooth grey dust holding its two grooves like a sentence waiting for its verb.

Forty years ago, someone had died down here singing. Yesterday, someone had posted me the truth in a careful hand. And this morning, standing among the coffins of St. Aldhelm's, I felt the obligation settle onto me the way the dust had settled onto everything else — evenly, silently, and with no intention whatsoever of being brushed off.

Whatever came next, I would not be filing it under *accident*.
