## 11. The Second Letter Arrives

The third letter did not come by post.

That was the first thing I noticed, and I want it on the record that I noticed it before I noticed anything else — before the handwriting, before the message, before the cold that started at the base of my skull and worked its way down like water finding a crack. It came the way a knife comes to a table: already there when you look down. Wednesday morning, I unlocked the archive loft at half past seven as I had done every working morning for ten years, and there it was, propped against my monitor, squared to the edge of the desk with a precision that made my teeth ache.

No stamp. No postmark. No envelope, even — just a single sheet of heavy cream paper folded once, waiting for me like a cat that has decided your lap is its property and is simply awaiting acknowledgment.

I stood in the doorway for a long moment, one hand still on the light switch, and considered the various lives I might have lived in which this would not be happening. The life where I'd stayed in Aberystwyth cataloguing county council minutes. The life where I'd taken Fenn's promotion and spent my days on budgets and benefactors and never touched the churchwardens' ledger again. In all of those lives, nobody left me messages on my own desk. In all of those lives, my desk held only dust, coffee rings, and the gentle administrative sediment of a cathedral that had been quietly dying of respectability since 1843.

I crossed the room. My shoes sounded very loud.

The paper was the same stock as the confession letter — I knew this without testing it, the way you know a voice through a wall. And the hand was the same: that careful, upright, almost loving script, each letter formed with the patience of a man copying out scripture. *You read faster than the others.*

I set it down. Picked it up again. Read it three times, because the first two readings I was mostly reading my own pulse.

*You read faster than the others. One more before the gavel.*

*The others.* Plural. Casual, almost companionable — the way you might compliment a colleague's efficiency at a meeting. You read faster than the others, Bram; do keep it up; there's a biscuit tin somewhere for the ambitious. Except the others were dead. Three names in the decoded ledger, three recipients of small regular sums across forty years, three people who had been paid to forget Elspeth Marr and had, one by one, stopped being able to remember anything at all. And now the writer was complimenting me on my speed, the way you'd praise a horse you intended to ride hard.

*One more before the gavel.* The trial opened in eleven days. Tomas Reyes sat in remand, eating food he didn't recognize and rehearsing, Lucía told me, the same sentence over and over — *I saw nothing, I only carried the money* — as if repetition could make it true retroactively. One more before the gavel. One more what? One more death? One more witness erased? Or — and this was the thought that made me sit down slowly in my chair, because my knees had filed notice — one more reader?

Because that was the pattern, wasn't it. Fenn — no. Not Fenn. *Whoever.* Whoever had written these letters wasn't killing witnesses, exactly. He was killing readers. People who could look at a column of figures and see a second book inside the first. Elspeth Marr had been the fastest reader of all, and she'd lasted, what, a fortnight? The watchman couldn't read anything but a door lock, and he was still alive — terrified, vanished, but alive. The arithmetic was elegant and horrible: literacy was the capital crime.

And I had just spent three nights photographing every page of the donation ledger with the methodical devotion of a man documenting his own autopsy.

* * *

I did what any sensible archivist does when threatened by an anonymous correspondent: I made tea, locked the loft door, put on gloves, and examined the letter as an artifact rather than a threat. This is either a professional strength or a coping mechanism; after ten years among the dead paperwork of St. Aldhelm's, I've stopped distinguishing.

The paper was Conqueror wove, 120gsm, the kind sold in boxes of five hundred to institutions that wanted their correspondence to feel like a benediction. No watermark visible under the loupe beyond the manufacturer's ghost. The ink was iron gall — actual iron gall, which no stationer under seventy has sold since the millennium, which meant either an enthusiast or someone with access to a very old desk drawer. The folds were sharp, made by a firm hand on a hard surface. There were no fingerprints worth lifting, or at least none that Okonkwo's lot would find, though I confess I didn't invite them to try. Not yet. That decision — the not-yet — sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, and I'll come back to it, because it deserves honest accounting.

I compared the hand against my photographs of the first two letters. The confession. The promise. Now this. Same slant — barely any, a schoolroom uprightness that had been drilled so deep it survived everything, including, presumably, conscience. Same pressure, heavy and even, the pen never hurrying even when the words must have cost something. Same peculiar *g* with its flat descender loop, same *k* whose arms met the stem slightly below the waist like a man shaking hands too low.

Three letters. One hand. One person who had written a confession to a murder he may or may not have committed, promised a second death, and now sent me a progress report on my own reading speed, addressed to no one, delivered by no one, simply *present* on my desk between the photocopier maintenance notice and a reminder about the Advent wreath budget.

Which raised a question I didn't want to ask and asked anyway: how had it gotten here? The loft door locks. The stairwell has a camera since the fire — a cheap one, installed hastily, angled badly, but a camera. The night watchman position had been vacant since our last watchman evaporated like breath off glass, which meant the building at night belonged to whoever had keys, and the list of keyholders was short, clerical, and wore a pectoral cross to meetings.

I put the thought down carefully, the way you put down something hot.

* * *

By ten I had achieved nothing whatsoever, unless you count re-shelving the same box of parish magazines four times. At ten-fifteen my phone buzzed: DI Sera Okonkwo, asking if she could "pop by." Sera did not pop. Sera arrived the way weather arrives, with intent.

She came up the loft stairs two at a time and stood surveying my domain with the expression of a woman who found archives personally offensive — all that paper, all those places for truth to hide in plain sight.

"You've gone quiet on me, Voss." She said it lightly, but Sera's lightness is a crowbar with a ribbon on it. "Two weeks ago you were ringing me about candle wax. Now nothing. Either you've solved it, lost interest, or found something you don't want to hand over."

"Good morning to you too."

"Bram." She pulled out the chair opposite mine uninvited and sat, forearms on the desk, close enough that I could see the tiredness under her eyes. She'd been carrying Reyes's case like a sack of wet cement and everyone above her kept telling her to put it down. "Talk to me."

And here is where I have to be honest, because this chapter of my life doesn't work otherwise. I looked at Sera Okonkwo — competent, decent, genuinely trying, and embedded like a splinter in an institution whose senior officers had closed this exact file once before under pressure from men in cassocks — and I weighed her against a photograph of a ledger.

If I told her everything, the ledger went into an evidence bag. Chain of custody. Disclosure schedules. A case file that would pass through a dozen hands, any one of which might mention, over lunch, over a pint, in the weary shorthand of coppers talking shop, that the archivist had cracked some old code and the ledger was the crown jewels. Forty years of hush-money records naming living people, including — I was now nearly certain, though I still flinched from the certainty — initials that resolved to *A.F.* One leak, one careless photocopy, one well-meaning officer showing a colleague, and those pages would burn twice: once in a fire brigade report and once in fact. I had already lost the loan register to a "faulty wiring" incident that occurred, coincidentally, the night after I photographed the ledger. I did not intend to lose the ledger itself to good intentions.

So I lied to her face. Not elaborately. Just a small, neat lie, the kind that fits in a pocket.

"There's nothing new," I said. "The wax, the letters — I've handed you copies of everything. I've been going back through the churchwardens' accounts for background, but it's just accounts. Tithes, roof repairs, the usual centuries of grumbling."

Sera looked at me for a long moment. She has a way of looking that makes you feel itemized.

"Background," she repeated.

"Archivists love background. It's our football."

"Mm." She stood, straightened her coat, and paused at the top of the stairs. "For what it's worth — and I know you have reasons, everyone in this case has reasons — the moment you decide I'm part of the problem instead of part of the answer, tell me to my face. Don't make me find out the way Hale did."

Then she was gone, and I sat in the ringing quiet of the loft feeling like the worst sort of traitor: the kind who betrays the right side for defensible reasons and knows the defense won't hold.

* * *

Lucía came at noon, letting herself in with the ease of someone who had stopped being a visitor sometime in the past week without either of us marking the date. She brought pasties from the bakery on Cordwainer Street and dropped into the chair Sera had vacated, and for a few minutes we ate in the companionable silence of two people who have run out of small talk because the large talk has eaten it all.

"He's stopped rehearsing," she said finally. "My dad. He just sits now. The solicitor says presenting calm, but I know him. That's not calm. That's a man who's decided the story's already been written and he's just the bloke holding the book." She brushed flakes of pastry from her jeans. "Did you get another one?"

I hadn't told her about the letter. It had been in my possession for four hours. I got up, unlocked the drawer, and laid it on the desk between us, and watched her read it, and watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"*One more,*" she said. "Before the gavel. That's Dad. That's — they're saying they'll kill him before the trial even starts?"

"Or me," I said. "Or you. 'One more' is doing a lot of work in that sentence. It's the least specific death threat I've ever received, and I once found a note in a bequest file that said simply 'he knows.'"

Lucía stared at the page. Then she did something I didn't expect: she laughed, short and harsh, with no humor in it anywhere. "You read faster than the others. God. He's *proud* of you. Do you understand what that means? Whoever this is, they like you. They're watching you work and they're pleased." She pushed the letter away as if it had a temperature. "That's worse than hate. Hate you can predict."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at me directly, and I saw Tomas in her face suddenly — the same stubborn architecture around the eyes. "Because I don't think you do, Bram. I think you're treating this like a puzzle with a body count. You photograph things. You decode things. You make tea. Meanwhile there's a person out there who walks into a locked building and leaves letters on desks, and every day we spend being careful, he's spending being *busy*."

"I gave the police copies of the first two letters—"

"And you're going to give them this one, yes? Today? Right now?" When I didn't answer fast enough, she nodded slowly, and something in her expression closed, a door drawn to but not locked. "Right. The ledger. You're worried about the ledger."

"The ledger is the only thing that can save your father. It goes into an evidence bag, it becomes a rumor, and then it becomes ash. I've seen how this institution handles inconvenient paper. I *am* inconvenient paper."

"So you're going to sit on it. Alone. With a killer who compliments your filing speed." She stood, shoved her chair in with unnecessary force. "My dad used to say the cathedral would bury anyone who dug too deep, and I thought he was being dramatic. Dramatic and guilty, apparently. But he was right about the first bit, wasn't he? It buries people. Politely. With lovely music."

"Lucía—"

"Eleven days," she said at the door. "Whatever you're planning to be ready for, be ready sooner."

* * *

The afternoon went long. I worked with the letter locked away and the ledger photographs open on my monitor, telling myself I was reviewing, knowing I was hiding. Around four, Dean Fenn came up the stairs.

He does that sometimes — appears in the archive unannounced, bringing warmth like a covered dish. Ten years of kindnesses, and I could catalogue them the way I catalogue anything: the time he'd defended my budget against the chapter's axe; the Christmas he'd noticed I had nowhere to go and simply assumed, warmly, publicly, that I'd join his table; the way he remembered my mother's name, two years after I'd mentioned her once. Ambrose Fenn remembered everything about everyone. I understood now that this was not tenderness. It was inventory.

"Bram." He set a folder on my desk — the promotion paperwork, still unsigned, patient as a spider. "I saw young Okonkwo leaving earlier. She seemed frustrated."

"She's a detective. Frustration is load-bearing."

He smiled. His smiles are excellent; I mean that literally — they are engineered, and the engineering is superb. "You know, my door is always open. Whatever weight you're carrying up here among the dust, you needn't carry it alone. That's what the church *is*, Bram. A structure for shared burdens." He tapped the folder once, gently. "Think about the future. The past keeps poorly. Believe me. I've tried to preserve it my whole career, and it rots in the hands no matter what you do."

He left. I sat looking at the folder, and at the space where the third letter lay locked in my drawer, and I understood that I had just been warned, offered, and measured, all in ninety seconds, by a man who would deny none of it under oath because none of it was deniable. That was his genius. Everything he did was innocent until you assembled it, and assembly required exactly the literacy he was hunting.

* * *

Night. The loft dark except for the desk lamp. I took out the letter again and read it a fourth time, and this time I read it as an archivist reads — not the words but the object, the material testimony.

*You read faster than the others.* Present tense. He was watching me *now*. Which meant the camera in the stairwell — badly angled, cheaply installed — was either useless or useful, depending on who else had access to the system. Which meant the fire alarm panel log, which recorded every door sensor trigger in the building, might hold a signature: the same route walked at the same hours, night after night. I couldn't pull that log myself without IT, and IT answered to the chapter, and the chapter answered to—

Stop. Breathe. One thread at a time.

I spread the three letters' photographs side by side and forced myself past the handwriting to the *dates*. The confession: posted the morning after I'd found the candle wax — no. Before. Posted the day *before* the wax appeared, meaning the writer had known I'd go down to the crypt before I knew it myself. The second letter: the day after I photographed the ledger. This one: the morning after I'd spent Tuesday evening cross-referencing the 1984 entries against the burial registers — work I'd done alone, at night, in a locked building.

Every letter had arrived within twenty-four hours of a specific act of mine. Not general surveillance. *Responses.* He wasn't watching me constantly; he was watching the records. Something — a register consulted, a database queried, a finding aid touched — was reporting back to him, and it reported faster than the post.

I sat back, heart going hard, and looked at my own catalogue system glowing patiently on the screen. Every query logged. Every search timestamped. An audit trail so thorough it had won me a small internal award in 2019.

Someone was reading my reading. And they could only do that from inside the system — or from inside the chapter that governed it.

I picked up my phone. Sera's number was on the screen, thumb hovering. Tell her everything, hand over the ledger, trust the machinery, sleep like a civil servant. Or protect it, stay alone, stay fast — and become exactly the kind of isolated, paranoid, indispensable witness who ends up as a footnote in someone else's tidy resolution.

The phone buzzed in my hand before I chose. A text. From Lucía:

*Just tried calling the watchman's mobile again. Number's been disconnected. Bram — he signed nothing. If anything happens to him now, there's no statement, no record, nothing. We're running out of people.*

I looked at the letter on my desk, at its patient, loving script, and I understood at last that I was not choosing between telling the truth and keeping it safe.

I was choosing which one to do first — and the gavel was counting down.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would copy the ledger photographs onto three drives and hide them in three places, and then I would walk into Okonkwo's office and give her everything, chain of custody be damned — because Lucía was right, and lone archivists die in footnotes.

But tomorrow was eleven hours away, and as I reached to switch off the lamp, my eye caught the stairwell camera's little red eye, steady in the dark, pointed — I was almost sure it hadn't been before — directly at my desk.
