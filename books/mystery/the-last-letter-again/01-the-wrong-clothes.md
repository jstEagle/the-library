## 1. The Wrong Clothes

The river gave him up at dawn, the way it gave up everything eventually — cats, crates, the occasional confession. Zephyrine Whitlock stood at the mudflat below the mill race with her coat still unbuttoned and watched two of Hollowmere's volunteer firemen drag the body onto the shingle, and the first thing she felt was not horror.

It was recognition.

The coat was hers.

Not like hers. Not similar to hers. It was the charcoal wool coat with the horn buttons and the torn left pocket lining, the one she'd reported missing from the coroner's office in October, the one she'd assumed a vagrant had lifted off the hook by the door. She had replaced the hook with a lock. She had told herself it was nothing.

The dead man wore it buttoned wrong, the top button pushed through the third hole, as if someone had dressed him in a hurry or dressed him without caring — and that distinction, she would learn, mattered more than anything else the river told her that morning.

"Coroner." Deputy Aldous Crane handed her a pair of gloves she didn't need. She'd brought her own. She always brought her own. "Fisher boys found him at first light. No identification."

"Then we'll let the body introduce itself," she said, and knelt.

The man was perhaps fifty, broad through the shoulders, hands soft in the way of men who had not worked with them in years. Waterlogged, bloated, dead — by her first estimate — somewhere between thirty-six and forty-eight hours. There was no trauma. There was water in the lungs, the gray froth at the nostrils, the whole unglamorous signature of drowning. She said as much to Crane, who wrote it down, and then she went through the pockets because that was procedure, and procedure was the only religion she had ever kept faithfully.

In the inside pocket of her stolen coat, folded twice, dry — *dry*, which meant it had been placed there after the man went into the water, or the coat had been placed on him after, and either way the arithmetic of it made her hands go still — was a letter.

Cream paper. Blue ink. The handwriting slanted left, letters leaning like reeds in wind, and Zephyrine had seen that handwriting every day for eleven years of her childhood, on passed notes in chapel, on the inside cover of a shared copy of *Wuthering Heights*, on the last letter Marisol Vane had ever written her before the fever took her, three years ago this month, three years during which Zephyrine had signed the death certificate herself and entered her into the burial ledger in her own careful coroner's hand.

*Dearest Z,* the letter began.

She did not read the rest standing up. She did not read the rest at all, not there, not with Crane watching and the firemen smoking and the river muttering against the stones. She folded it back along its creases and put it in her own coat, the one with all its buttons accounted for, and she said, "Get him to the slab. I'll want photographs before we clean him."

Her voice did not shake. She noted that the way she noted everything — clinically, at arm's length — and understood that the steadiness was temporary, a loan, and that interest would come due.

* * *

The autopsy room at Hollowmere's municipal building had been a butcher's cold storage once, and it still smelled faintly of sawdust and old iron, which Zephyrine had long ago decided was preferable to the chemical pine of proper facilities. Proper facilities were for towns that wanted the truth handled elsewhere.

She worked the body alone. That was also procedure — her procedure, the one she'd written for herself after Marisol died, when the town had watched her too closely, waiting for the coroner to crack the way the coroner's dead friend had cracked.

She photographed the coat. She photographed the wrong-buttoned closure. She photographed the dry letter's absence in the record, because she had already decided that letter was not evidence. Evidence belonged to the town. The letter belonged to a dead woman, and Zephyrine had learned the hard way what the town did with things that belonged to Marisol Vane.

The man's effects, laid out on the steel tray in their little evidence rows: no wallet. No watch. Boots, good ones, resoled twice. A brass hotel key, unmarked. And in the right trouser pocket, a slip of paper with a number written on it in pencil — *not* Marisol's hand, a heavier, more deliberate script — and beneath the number, one word:

**HOLLOWMERE.**

She stood over the tray for a long time, steam from her untouched tea rising past her chin.

A drowned man with no name, wearing her coat, carrying a dry letter in a dead woman's hand, and a note directing him — or directing whoever found him — to this town. Bodies did not dress themselves. Rivers did not deliver mail. Someone had composed this scene the way she composed a report: every element placed, every detail meant to be read.

She was not the finder.

She was the recipient.

Zephyrine sat down on her stool, and her hands, the loan finally called in, began to shake.

She took out the letter.

*Dearest Z. If you are reading this, then the coat worked, and you are the one who found him, and I am sorry — for the coat, for the man, for all of it. You will want to burn this. Don't. You will want to believe I'm dead, because dead is simpler and you loved simple things once, before Hollowmere taught you better. Check the ledgers you keep. Check who signed. Three names, three graves, no bodies. I was the fourth, but I got up. Count the notary. Count the ink. You taught me that ink remembers, Z. You said it when we were girls, over your father's files — ink remembers what people wish it didn't. I am not dead. I was never dead. And I am coming home, which means someone is dying to keep me gone. Trust the ledger. Trust no one who smiles. Trust yourself — you always could, even when you pretended otherwise, even about me. — M.V.*

Zephyrine read it twice. Three times.

Then she did what she had trained herself to do with every unbearable thing: she made it procedural. She locked the letter in the drawer with her father's old files, washed her hands, and pulled the burial ledger for the last six years off the shelf, because Marisol had known her — had always known her — and had known that grief would not move her, but a discrepancy would.

She started with the V page. *VANE, MARISOL E. — interred 14 March, plot 118, witnessed: A. Fell, notary.*

Ambrose Fell had notarized her best friend's burial. Of course he had. Ambrose Fell notarized everything in Hollowmere. He had notarized Zephyrine's appointment to coroner. He had notarized her father's will. He had stood at Marisol's funeral with his hand on the widow Okafor's shoulder, his voice like warm bread, saying *some losses the town carries together.*

Three names, three graves, no bodies. Marisol had written it like a riddle. Zephyrine opened the ledger and began, page by page, to solve it.

Outside, the light was failing over the cemetery on the hill, where plot 118 held a stone with Marisol's name and — as far as Zephyrine had ever been permitted to know — nothing underneath it but the town's good opinion.

She found the first false grave before midnight.
