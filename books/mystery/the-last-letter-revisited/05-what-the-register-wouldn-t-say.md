## 5. What the Register Wouldn't Say

The baptismal registers lived in the deepest drawer of the archive's oldest cabinet, a piece of furniture older than the cathedral itself, its oak gone black with a century of candle smoke and handling. Jonah had catalogued them twice in her eleven years as archivist—once by year, once by condition—and she knew their weight the way a postman knows his route. Volume 1948 through 1977 was bound in cracked green leather, its spine stitched with thread that had once been white.

She had never had reason to open it before. That was the lie she told herself as she carried it to the reading table and set it down beneath the lamp. She had had every reason. She simply hadn't known what she was looking for—or perhaps, some small and stubborn part of her had always known, and had kept the drawer shut the way one keeps a door shut on a room where something is crying.

The letter lay beside the register, wrapped now in acid-free tissue because Mara Quist had insisted, but Jonah could still see the handwriting through the folds if she let herself. *The girl who keeps the files.* Her own loops, her own slant, the particular way she crossed her t's with a single impatient stroke. A stranger's voice wearing her hand like a glove turned inside out.

*1976,* Mara had said, tapping the register's date range. *If someone wanted a child erased from this town's memory, they'd start where children are first written down.*

Jonah put on her white cotton gloves and opened the volume to 1975.

The pages smelled of iron gall ink and time. Names marched down each entry in the tidy hand of Canon Ellery Pike, who had served forty years and whose script never varied: date of birth, date of baptism, parents' names, godparents, a small cross for those who died young. March. April. May. June.

July began normally enough. Two entries, three. Then—

Nothing.

Not a blank page. Not an error struck through. The stub of paper remained at the gutter of the binding, a pale ribbon of ragged fiber no wider than her thumbnail, and above it the faint ghost of a razor's passage: a hairline score in the page beneath, running clean and straight from top margin to bottom. Someone had opened this book in 1976 or thereabouts and cut out a single leaf with the patience of a surgeon and the nerve of a thief.

Jonah sat very still. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above her, the cathedral bells practiced the quarter-hour, and the sound came down through the stone like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

She turned back through the volume slowly, checking every leaf, and found the second wound almost immediately—a second stub, this one from August of 1975, cut just as cleanly. Two baptisms removed. Two children unwritten.

Her own birthday, she realized with a cold that started in her wrists and moved inward, fell in the last week of July. Her adoptive mother had told her so every year of her childhood, usually while icing a cake: *the last week of July, when the heat breaks.* There would have been a baptism. There should have been a record. Instead there was a scar in the paper shaped exactly like the absence of her.

She reached for the magnifying glass without thinking about it, archivist's reflex, and brought it close to the stub itself. And there—in the margin, in the narrow strip of paper the razor had spared, squeezed into a space barely wide enough for three words—she found writing.

The ink was faded to the brown of old tea, but the hand was unmistakable. She had spent three nights with Adelaide Voss's letter; she knew the slight forward lean of it, the way the descenders plunged deeper than any pen needed to go, as though the writer were pressing her thoughts into the page hard enough to leave them on the next sheet too.

*Borrowed daughter — forgive me.*

Four words. Jonah read them until they stopped being words and became only shapes, then read them again until they returned. The lamp hummed. The bells finished their practice. And Jonah Dravenmoor, who had alphabetized the town's griefs for eleven years and called it order, put her gloved hand flat against the mutilated page and felt the full weight of what she did not know settle onto her chest like a stone laid reverently on a grave.

Someone had borrowed her. As one borrows a book. As one borrows a life.

And Adelaide Voss—the woman in the empty coffin, the woman who wrote letters in Jonah's own handwriting twenty years before Jonah learned to write—had asked forgiveness for it in a margin no wider than a prayer.

* * *

Edith Marsh lived in the last house before the road gave up on the town and surrendered to gorse and wind. She had been her mother's closest friend for fifty years—had stood beside her at two funerals and a dozen christenings, had taught Jonah to shell peas on this very porch when Jonah was small enough to sit on the step with her shoes off. She was eighty-one now and sharp as a new blade, and she answered the door before Jonah finished knocking, as though she'd been standing behind it all morning waiting for exactly this.

"I wondered how long you'd take," Edith said. "Come in. Mind the cat."

The parlor smelled of lavender and coal smoke. Jonah sat on the horsehair sofa that had bitten the backs of her legs when she was six and bit them still, and understood suddenly why people visited the past in person rather than in records: the records didn't bite.

"You've been in the registers," Edith said, settling into her chair. It wasn't a question.

"How did you know?"

"Because you have her look about you this morning. The look your mother got whenever she'd been anywhere near that crypt." Edith folded her hands. "I told her for thirty years to stay out of it. I'm telling you the same."

"Told my mother to stay out of what?"

Edith looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the wind worried the gorse, and the cat climbed into Jonah's lap uninvited, a warm weight she was grateful for, something to hold that couldn't be razored out of a book.

"Your mother was the gentlest liar I ever knew," Edith said at last. "She lied to you your whole life, God rest her, and she lied beautifully, and she did it because she believed—with everything in her—that the truth would take you away from her." She paused. "I helped her carry the lie. So understand that whatever I say next, I say it ashamed."

Jonah's hands went still on the cat's back. "Say it anyway."

"You arrived in this town on the fourth of August, 1975. You were maybe five weeks old. No paperwork. No birth certificate, no health visitor's card, nothing. Your mother produced you at the surgery three days later and swore to Dr. Lomax you'd been born at home, up-country, midwife attended, papers lost in a house fire." Edith's voice was steady, but her hands, Jonah noticed, were gripping the arms of her chair. "Lomax didn't believe a word of it. He signed the forms anyway. He said—" and here the old woman's mouth twisted around the memory— "he said some children arrive with more history than a file can hold, and it wasn't his place to argue with a baby."

"A house fire." Jonah heard her own voice as if from across the room. "There was no house fire."

"There was no house. There was no up-country. There was a car that came in the night, and a woman who wouldn't get out of it, and your mother going down the drive with a blanket bundle and coming back up alone and changed forever." Edith's eyes had gone bright and terrible. "I saw it from my kitchen window. I've seen it every night since, when I can't sleep. Which is most nights, these days."

"Who was in the car?"

"I don't know. It was dark, and I was younger, and I minded my business the way we all did in 1975. We were very good at minding our business in this town, Jonah. It was practically a civic duty." She leaned forward. "But I'll tell you what I do know, and then I'll tell you the thing I've kept in my throat for fifty years, and then you'll leave me be for a while, because I'm an old woman and this is costing me."

Jonah nodded. The cat purred against her ribs like a small engine carrying her somewhere.

"I know that your mother paid for that house—the one you live in now, the cottage by the grey water—in cash, the same autumn you arrived. Everyone thought it was an inheritance from a cousin. It wasn't. I know because I asked her once, gently, the way you ask, and she looked at me and said, *It was left to us both, Edie. Left to the child most of all.*" Edith drew a breath. "And I know that every year on the last week of July, your mother walked down to the water with an envelope, and posted it, and came home lighter, and never once told me to whom."

The envelope. Every year. Jonah thought of the cottage, of the sea chest she hadn't yet found the courage or the key to open, of the brass seal she'd recognized from records she'd never been allowed to touch. The threads were pulling tight now, drawing toward some knot at the center she could feel but not see.

"And the thing in your throat?" she asked quietly.

Edith rose, crossed to the mantelpiece, and took down a photograph Jonah had looked at a thousand times in this room without ever seeing it: a group shot outside the church, dated in pencil on the back—June 1975. Confirmation day. A row of girls in white, and behind them the adults, and among the adults, half-turned toward the camera as though she'd heard her name called, a young woman with dark hair and a face that made the floor tilt under Jonah's chair.

Because it was her own face. Younger, softer, unlined—but hers. The same deep-set eyes. The same mouth that went crooked at one corner when it tried not to smile.

"That's the girl who brought you," Edith said. "Or that's who everyone said she was. But I'll tell you what I've kept in my throat fifty years, child: I'd seen that girl's photograph before. On the missing posters. In the *Gazette*, the week they closed the case." She set the photograph in Jonah's hands with a gentleness that undid her. "That is Adelaide Voss's granddaughter. The girl who vanished in 1975. And whoever she was—whatever she was running from—she ran straight to your mother's door, and she left you there, and she wrote to you every July until the letters stopped."

"When did they stop?"

Edith's face, for the first time, faltered. "The summer before your mother died. And Jonah—" she hesitated, and the hesitation was worse than anything she'd said yet— "the last envelope wasn't posted. Your mother kept it. It'll be in the cottage. And I never saw what was inside it, but I saw your mother's face after she read it, and I can tell you this much for free: whatever that letter says, it's the reason she made you promise never to sell that house."

Outside, the wind had risen, driving thin rain against the windows, and far off the sea was making its low argument with the shore. Jonah held the photograph of her own face and understood that the register's missing page was not an ending at all.

It was an address. And the last letter—the one her mother had never sent—was already waiting for her, forty miles away, in a sea chest that a brass key had been waiting fifty years to open.
