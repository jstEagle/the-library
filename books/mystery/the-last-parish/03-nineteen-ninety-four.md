## 3. Nineteen Ninety-Four

The St. Veran Parish Sheriff's Office kept its records in a building that smelled like mildew and copier toner, and the woman behind the front desk looked at me the way people look at weather they've already decided to ignore.

"Public records request," I said, sliding my printed form across the counter. "Cold case files. 1994."

Her name tag said DARLENE. She read the form twice, slowly, like she was giving me time to change my mind.

"You'll want to talk to Sheriff Boudreau about that," she said.

"I'd rather just see the file."

"The file's sealed."

"It's a missing persons case from thirty years ago. Missing persons cases aren't sealed unless somebody sealed them."

Darlene's jaw worked. Behind her, through a frosted glass door, I could hear a radio playing zydeco and a man laughing at something on it. The laugh had the easy, entitled rhythm of someone who has never once been told no in his own building.

"Sheriff Marcus Boudreau," Darlene said finally, "is Auguste Boudreau's nephew. You want to make a request against this parish, you make it to him." She pushed the form back across the counter. "Or you wait for the courthouse annex to open Monday. They got duplicates. Nobody checks those."

I understood what she was doing. It took me a second — it was so far outside anything a records clerk in Chicago would ever risk — but then I had it. She was telling me where the unguarded copy lived.

"Thank you, Darlene."

"Don't thank me." She was already looking past me at the door. "I never said nothing."

**ARCHIVAL INTERLUDE — *The Last Parish*, Season One, Episode Four: "The Girl Who Drove North."**

> LENA (V.O.): In October of 1994, Odile Boudreau told her sister on the phone that she was leaving. Leaving her husband, Landry Boudreau. Leaving St. Veran Parish. Leaving — and this is a direct quote from the sister's statement, which I want you to sit with — *"that house where everything is his and nothing is mine."*
>
> Her car was found three days later at the Baton Rouge park-and-ride. Locked. Half a tank of gas. A child's car seat in the back, empty.
>
> Odile Boudreau was twenty-six years old. She was never seen again.

I recorded that episode two years ago, back when *The Last Parish* was still small enough that Remy and I split one order of pho between us and called it a wrap party. I'd covered eleven cold cases by then. I knew the shape of them the way you know the shape of your own hand.

But I hadn't known Odile's name meant anything until now.

Because Etienne Landry — the gentle, apologetic records clerk everyone in St. Veran praised within four minutes of meeting him — had married into the Boudreau family. His first wife, dead of cancer in 2009, had been a Boudreau cousin. And the man who paid for the church roof, the sheriff's budget, and the levee repairs — Auguste Boudreau — had lost a daughter-in-law to the water in 1994, or so the parish liked to say.

Lost her to the water. That was the phrase. Three different people had used it with me in two days, unprompted, the way you'd recite a catechism.

The bayou takes what it wants. Lost her to the water.

Nobody had ever found a body.

* * *

The courthouse annex opened at eight on Monday. I was there at seven-forty, drinking gas-station coffee under a ceiling fan turning slow enough to be decorative.

The duplicate files lived in a room the size of a walk-in closet, presided over by a retired deputy named Fontenot who read the paper and let me pull boxes myself as long as I didn't sneeze on anything. He didn't ask questions. Either he didn't care or he'd learned not to.

Box 1994-MP-011. I carried it to the folding table by the window and opened it, and I felt the thing I always feel — that drop in the stomach, the sense of standing at the edge of a well you can't see the bottom of.

The file was thin.

Not thin like old files get thin. Thin like *edited*. A missing persons case should have a body: canvass reports, interviews with family, statements from coworkers, phone records requests, search grid maps. This had a two-page incident report, a single interview summary with the husband — half a page, mostly boilerplate — and a property receipt for the car.

No canvass log. No interviews with the sister who'd taken the phone call. No search grid. No follow-up. Nothing dated after November 4th, 1994 — nine days after Odile disappeared.

Nine days. Then the file just stopped, like a heart monitor going flat.

I photographed every page anyway, working fast, my hands not quite steady. And it was on the last sheet — a routing slip, the kind that tracks a file between offices — that I saw it. A signature line. *Reviewed and closed: A. Boudreau.*

Auguste Boudreau wasn't law enforcement. He was a landowner. A sugar man. A man who sat in the third pew and wrote checks.

His signature was on the closure of his own daughter-in-law's case.

* * *

That night, back at the Bayou Rose Motel with its humming window unit and its bedspread the color of old mustard, I laid everything out on the second bed like a card sharp showing a losing hand.

The retainer letter. The threat letter that had come with it — the one promising that Etienne Landry would die before the month was out, typed in clean Courier font, unsigned. Printouts of the 1994 file. My notes.

I read the threat letter again. Fourth time. Fifth.

And there it was.

*"She drove north once. She won't drive north again."*

Odile's sister's statement — the one referenced in the archival episode, the one whose full text existed only in the sealed original file — contained the line: *subject stated intent to drive north to her mother's in Shreveport.* I'd never seen the full statement. It wasn't in the duplicate file. It wasn't anywhere public. I'd paraphrased it on air from a newspaper clipping.

But whoever wrote the threat letter knew Odile planned to drive north.

And there was more. *"The parish keeps what the parish is given"* — a near-verbatim echo of a phrase in Auguste's closure memo, which I was reading for the first time tonight, thirty years after it was written: *the parish has suffered enough; let the parish keep its peace.*

Two phrases. Two phrases that existed in exactly one place on earth: a sealed file in a sheriff's office that answered to the man who closed the case.

My client — my anonymous, generous, impossible client — had access to that file.

Or had access to whoever wrote it.

I sat down on the mustard bedspread and felt the thrill come up through me, hot and electric, the feeling I chase for a living, the feeling Remy says makes me insufferable at crime scenes. The pattern was forming. Not the pattern of the murder-to-come. The older one underneath it, the one nobody had ever been allowed to trace.

Somebody had killed Odile Boudreau in 1994. Somebody had sealed the file with a signature that should have been a scandal. And now, thirty years later, somebody was threatening to kill the last living witness to all of it — and quoting the sealed evidence while they did it.

The killer wasn't hiding from me.

The killer was teaching me.

A knock at the door made me go for the lamp I don't own. It was Remy, home early from the records office, holding his laptop like a shield.

"Lena." His face was gray. "You need to see where the retainer money came from. Because I traced it, and it goes through three shells, and the last one—" he swallowed, "—the last one was registered in 1994. Same week Odile disappeared."

I looked at the letters spread across the bed. At the file photos glowing on my phone. At the window, where beyond the parking lot the bayou moved slow and black and patient, keeping whatever it had been given.

"Get your charger," I said. "We're not done tonight."
