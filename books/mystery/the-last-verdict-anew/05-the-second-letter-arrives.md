## 5. The Second Letter Arrives

The second letter came on a Tuesday, which Vesper Voss would later decide was the most insulting part of the whole affair. Tuesdays were his light nights. Tuesdays were when he allowed himself the small luxury of alphabetizing his tea tins, of re-inking the labels on his filing drawers, of believing — for one soft hour between three and four in the morning — that Hollow Creek was exactly as boring as it pretended to be.

He knew it wasn't. He'd spent three years proving it wasn't, one dispatch log at a time. But he liked to let himself forget on Tuesdays.

The envelope was waiting for him in the taxi company's mail slot, wedged behind a flyer for the anniversary dinner that had been there so long its corners had gone amber. Same heavy cream stock as the first letter. Same postmark, dated the same day as its twin — the day both letters had arrived like bookends around his quiet life. But this one was addressed differently. The first had said *Vesper Voss, Night Dispatcher.* This one said:

*Marge Kettle.*

And beneath her name, in the same careful, unhurried hand: *Before the dinner. The second name.*

Vesper stood in the doorway holding it the way you hold something hot. Outside, the fog was doing its usual slow crawl up from the lake, and somewhere a dog was complaining about it, and everything looked normal, and nothing felt normal at all.

Because here was the thing that made his hands go cold: Marge Kettle hadn't received this letter. He had. Which meant whoever wrote it didn't actually want Marge warned. They wanted *him* to know she was next. They wanted him watching.

Or they wanted him running.

* * *

Marge Kettle lived eleven minutes away by cab and four by the shortcut through the rail yard that Vesper had promised Emory Hale he'd never use again after the incident with the drainage ditch. He used it anyway. He called ahead twice from the cab's radio — no answer, no answer — and by the third call he wasn't dialing anymore, just gripping the wheel and reciting street names like a rosary.

Her house sat at the end of Kettle Lane, because in Hollow Creek even the streets were filed properly. Porch light on. Door unlocked. That alone made his stomach drop; Marge Kettle had once locked her door against her own paperboy on principle.

"Marge?" His voice bounced down the hallway, past forty years of framed photographs and a coat rack wearing three coats that hadn't left the house since 1987. "It's Vesper. From the company. I'm coming in."

She was in her kitchen, sitting upright at her table with her hands folded neatly in front of her, like a woman waiting patiently for tea. Her eyes were open. Her eyes tracked him, slowly, with enormous effort, and when she tried to speak it came out as a long, syrupy vowel.

Alive. Alive but *elsewhere.*

"Okay. Okay, hey." He knelt beside her chair, checked her pulse — steady, slow, infuriatingly calm — and then saw the teacup. Half-full. Cold. A faint sweetness still clinging to the rim that wasn't sugar. "Who gave you this? Marge, who brought you tea?"

Her eyelids drooped, dragged themselves back up. One finger lifted off the table, trembling, and pointed — not at the door, not at the window, but at the hallway behind him.

Toward her office.

Vesper had been in Marge's office exactly twice, both times under supervision, both times feeling like a boy admitted briefly into a cathedral. Thirty years of dispatch logs. Carbon copies going back further than that. Taxi manifests cross-referenced in her own private notation system, which she'd once described to him as "the only honest language ever invented." It was the archive of record for half the town's buried business, and everyone who mattered knew it, and everyone who mattered pretended they didn't.

The door hung open. The file cabinets stood pulled out from the wall like teeth being drawn.

Papers everywhere. Not destroyed — *sorted*. Someone had gone through thirty years of Marge Kettle's life with care and patience, lifting out whole folders, leaving others fanned open like birds with broken wings. In the middle of the floor lay her 1994 ledger, spine cracked, pages rifled to October.

Vesper didn't touch anything. Three years of dispatcher instinct said: *scene first, panic second.* He checked the back door — unlocked, no sign of force. Checked the kitchen window — latched. Whoever came, Marge had let them in. Whoever poured that tea, she'd trusted them enough to drink it.

That was somehow worse than everything else.

He dialed Doc Ferris from the kitchen phone, keeping his voice level, giving the address twice, saying "she's breathing fine, just — under" in the tone he used for fares who'd had too much at the Legion. Then he sat down across from Marge at her own table and held her cold hand and waited, watching her fight her way up through whatever ocean they'd put her in.

It took an hour. Doc Ferris came, clucked, pronounced it "a heavy dose of something old-fashioned," and left instructions Vesper barely heard. And near dawn, with the fog pressing white against the windows, Marge Kettle's eyes finally cleared all the way, and she looked at Vesper Voss, and she said the last thing he expected.

"You got them too."

* * *

From where Marge Kettle sat, wrapped in the afghan Vesper had dug out of her hall closet, the kitchen looked like a battlefield and the boy across from her looked like a soldier who'd wandered onto it without a map. She'd known him three years. Good hands. Careful mind. Filed things the way she'd taught him to file things before she'd ever taught him, which was how she'd known, almost immediately, that he was one of hers.

Now he was looking at her with those wide dispatcher's eyes, and she was about to ruin his whole understanding of the world, and some tired, guilty corner of her thought: *well. Better now than at my funeral.*

"I got them in 1994," she said. "Both of them. Same postmark, same hand. One confessed to Aldous Pike's drowning. One promised a second name if anyone went looking." She watched the color move through his face like weather. "I never wrote those letters, Vesper. I *received* them. And I did what any sensible woman does with a live grenade."

"You filed it," he whispered.

"I filed it under K, for Kettle, because I am not a coward, thank you very much, and also under P, for Pike, because I am thorough." Her voice wobbled on the last word and she hated it. "And then I kept every log, every manifest, every carbon copy for thirty years, because someday someone would come asking, and I wanted to be ready."

"And instead someone came and drugged your tea."

"And instead," Marge agreed grimly, "someone came and drank half my good Darjeeling and went through my life's work like it was a church rummage sale." She pulled the afghan tighter. What she didn't say — what sat in her chest like a swallowed stone — was the detail she couldn't stop turning over. The tea had been made correctly. Two scoops, water just off the boil, steeped four minutes. Only two people alive made her tea that way.

One of them was dead. The other was standing in her kitchen right now, looking at her like a lost dog, and she could not — *could not* — make herself believe it of him. But belief, she'd learned over thirty years of dispatch work, was a luxury. Records were not.

"They took the 1994 files," Vesper said. "But they left the rest?"

"They took what proved the ride happened. Four passengers, one cab, cash fare." She met his eyes. "They left everything else because everything else doesn't matter to them. But here's what matters to me, boy." She leaned forward, and her hand found his wrist, and her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "Those letters were written by someone who knew my filing system. Knew it well enough to know that threatening me wouldn't work unless the threat arrived somewhere I couldn't ignore it. Somebody who learned their alphabet at my knee."

Vesper went very still.

"There are only two people I ever taught my notation system," Marge said quietly. "Emory Hale. And you."

The clock on her wall ticked into the silence. Outside, the fog was lifting off Kettle Lane, and somewhere down the road a car engine started — unhurried, patient, pulling away before either of them thought to look.

"The dinner's Saturday," Vesper said finally. "Corinne Ashe gives the toast."

"I know what Corinne Ashe gives," Marge said. "I've been listening to this town for forty years, Vesper. Now listen to me. Whoever wrote those letters wants us both at that dinner. Wants us *watching*." Her eyes, clear now and frightened and furious all at once, held his. "So the question isn't who's next. The question is — who taught *them* the alphabet?"
