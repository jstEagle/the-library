## 7. Anniversary Week

The town of Harrow's Bend began decorating for the memorial festival on a Monday, as it had every year for twenty years, and Sebastian watched them do it from his studio window with the ledger photographs spread across his desk like a spread-eagled patient.

Bunting went up along Main Street first — blue and white, the colors of the Voss family crest that nobody under forty could explain. Then the folding tables outside the church hall, then the plywood stage in the square where the mayor would read the names. Eleanor Voss. No — *Margaret* Voss. Eleanor was the daughter, the one who'd been four years old when her mother's car was found nose-down at the quarry gate with the keys still in the ignition and no Margaret anywhere inside or out.

Twenty years of bunting. Twenty years of folding tables. The town had gotten very good at mourning in a way that required nothing of anyone.

Sebastian hadn't slept much since the ransacking. He'd rebuilt what files he could from cloud backups and Dana's duplicates, but the originals were gone — twenty years of interviews, court transcripts, his own handwritten marginalia, all of it lifted clean out of the studio while he'd been drinking Judge Crane's sherry and being offered money with a smile. Whoever had taken them hadn't been sloppy. They'd been *curating*. And that word kept circling back to him, because curation was what the ledger did. It wasn't a record of crimes. It was an archive of decisions.

He picked up the photograph of the newest entry again. He'd photographed every page before the break-in, thank God, and this page was the one that had been keeping him awake.

*E. VOSS — moved.*

When he'd first decoded the cipher three weeks ago, Eleanor's name had sat in the ledger's margin column, dated next year — a future entry, penciled in the way the keeper penciled everything provisional. Now the pencil was gone. Ink stood in its place. Her entry had been promoted from the margins into the main column, and the date beside it was not next year.

It was Friday.

Four days from now. The final night of the memorial festival, when the whole town would be down at the quarry overlook holding candles, when every alibi in Harrow's Bend would be standing in plain sight of every other alibi, and nobody would notice one more car on the quarry road because everyone's car would be on the quarry road.

Sebastian set the photograph down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

She'd told him herself. She'd sat across from him in the archive stacks, surrounded by the genealogy indexes she'd helped maintain, and said, *I found my own name dated next year,* and she'd been afraid, and he had believed her, and then he had done precisely nothing fast enough. He'd spent two weeks confronting Crane, chasing provenance, building the case like a man assembling a lawsuit instead of a rescue. Methodical. Professional. The way he'd been taught to work by a podcast that, as Dana had finally confessed, was never his at all.

His phone buzzed. Dana.

"Tell me you found something," she said, without preamble. "Because I found something, and I need you to have found something too, or I'm going to lose my mind."

"The entry moved up. Friday."

A silence on the line long enough to hear her breathing change. "Then we go to the police."

"With what? A cipher I decoded myself and a hundred-year-old book that lives in a public archive?" He heard his own voice climb and forced it down. "Crane sits on the bench here, Dana. Half the department took their pensions under his administration. If I walk in there, I'm not reporting a crime. I'm announcing it."

"Then we go to Eleanor. Warn her. Get her out of town until after the anniversary."

"I've called her six times today." He looked at the window, at the bunting snapping in the wind down the length of Main Street. "Straight to voicemail. Her car's still at the archive lot — I drove past an hour ago. But she's not answering, and the archive is closed, and—" He stopped. Said the rest anyway. "And the last time someone in this town stopped answering, they found the car and never found the person."

"Don't," Dana said quietly. "Don't write the ending before you know it."

"That's the job, isn't it? That's what you told me once. That's what the show does." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "Except the show wasn't the job. Was it. The show was the net."

Dana didn't deny it. She'd confessed about the Crane trust days ago, wept through it, and he'd told her he forgave her, and he'd meant it, and none of it changed the architecture of his life: that for five years he had been a sensor array planted in Harrow's Bend, cataloguing who remembered too much, and the data had flowed home to the people who'd built him without his ever feeling the wires.

"I keep thinking about the envelope," he said. "The retainer. Ten thousand dollars in cash to prove a murder *won't* happen. Who hires an investigator to prove a negative?"

"Someone testing you."

"No. Worse." He stood, walked to the window. Down in the square, two volunteers were unrolling a banner: TWENTY YEARS OF REMEMBRANCE. "Someone paying me *in advance*. Think about the accounting, Dana. You don't pay a fee up front unless you already know the outcome. The fee wasn't for investigation. It was hush money wearing a detective's hat. I accepted payment to stand in the room while it happened and call my standing there professionalism." His throat tightened. "I cashed it. I put it in the account. I paid rent with it."

"You didn't know—"

"I suspected by chapter three. I *knew* when the ink changed." He closed his eyes. "Every keeper was once an outsider who wanted to belong. That's what the historian wrote in the oldest margin note — I decoded it last night. Every keeper was recruited exactly the way I'm being recruited. Paid, flattered, threatened, tested. And the test isn't whether you can solve the case. The test is whether, when the date arrives, you look away."

* * *

He spent Tuesday doing the thing he knew how to do, the only thing left that felt honest: working the case out loud, alone, in the studio, recording everything even though he couldn't broadcast any of it yet.

Eleanor's routine was simple enough to reconstruct from memory. Archive weekdays, coffee at the Bluebird Diner at eight, home to her mother's old house on Quarry Road by six. She had no siblings, no partner, a father who'd left the state years ago. If someone wanted to take Eleanor Voss during the busiest week of Harrow's Bend's calendar, they would hardly need to try. She was a woman defined by absence — daughter of the town's most famous missing person — and the town had spent twenty years practicing how not to see her.

He drove Quarry Road at dusk. The Voss house sat dark behind its hedge, mail fat in the box, a newspaper curled on the step going soft with dew. Two days of it. He sat in his car across the road for an hour, telling himself she might be staying with a friend, might have gotten scared and run, might be fine — and watching the dark windows the way he'd watched a hundred other dark windows for the podcast, narrating dread in a voice made smooth by editing.

This time there would be no edit. This time the dread was his.

At nine he tried her number once more, and this time, instead of voicemail, it rang through — ringing somewhere close. He got out of the car. Followed the sound around the side of the house, to the kitchen door standing ajar, and there on the flagstones lay her phone, screen lit, his own contact name glowing in the dark: *Sebastian Winterbourne*, calling, calling, calling, into an empty yard.

Beyond the kitchen door the house was untouched. Dishes drying. A genealogy index open on the table with a pencil resting in the gutter — she'd been working, right up until she wasn't. On the open page, in her small neat hand, a note in the margin: *the keeper updates the ink AFTER the festival. always after. why would this year be different—*

The sentence stopped mid-word.

Sebastian stood in Eleanor Voss's kitchen with her phone still ringing in his hand and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that he was no longer investigating the ledger. He was *in* it. The outsider who'd come asking questions, paid in advance, named in the final column — he wasn't the threat to the system. He was its next prospective employee, and Eleanor, the loose thread, the archivist who'd found her own name, had been pulled before she could unravel anything else.

They'd taken her early so that Friday would be clean. So that the town could hold its candles and read its names and go home, and the ledger would balance, and the new keeper — if he passed — would inherit a tidy book.

He set her phone down on the counter, gently, the way you'd return evidence.

Then he took out his own phone and photographed the kitchen — the open index, the pencil, the interrupted sentence — and walked out to his car, and sat there shaking, and did not drive toward the police station or the judge's house or his own bed.

He drove toward the quarry.

Because there was one more thing he'd decoded, late last night, in the oldest pages of the ledger, in the hand of the first keeper: a notation beside the very first disappearance, a century ago. Not a name. A place. Three words in the cipher that translated, plainly, without art:

*The water keeps.*
