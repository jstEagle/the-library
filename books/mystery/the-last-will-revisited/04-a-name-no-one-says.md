## 4. A Name No One Says

The Tidehaven Public Library opened at nine, which gave Vesper Galloway four hours after her shift ended to shower, change into civilian clothes, and convince herself she was doing nothing suspicious.

She was just a woman going to the library. Women went to libraries all the time. Nobody looked twice at a woman in the library, and that was precisely why she'd chosen it over the courthouse records office, where a clerk named Denny Prewitt would absolutely look twice, and then three times, and then mention it to his cousin at the bait shop by lunch.

Vesper had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being overlooked. She sat in the back of staff meetings and was never called on. She stood in line at the pharmacy and cashiers forgot she was there until she cleared her throat. At the cab office, drivers walked past her desk with their coffee and their complaints and their stories about fares who tipped in fishing lures, and only remembered she existed when they needed a fare rung through or a route untangled. For most of her life this had felt like a small tragedy. Lately it had started to feel like a superpower.

She took the stairs to the local history room on the second floor, a low-ceilinged space that smelled of dust and old glue, presided over by Mrs. Ansel, seventy-something and sharp as a tack left out in the rain too long but somehow still pointy.

"Morning, Vesper," Mrs. Ansel said without looking up from her cataloging. Of course she knew her name. Librarians were the one species of human immune to invisibility. "You're up early for a night worker."

"I couldn't sleep."

"Mm." Mrs. Ansel made a note on a card. "The probate files are in the gray boxes, third shelf, if that's what you're here for."

Vesper stopped halfway to the shelf. "I didn't say—"

"You didn't have to. Half the town's been in here since the coffin came up empty. The other half is at the Salt Line Diner arguing about it." Mrs. Ansel finally looked up, and her eyes behind the bifocals were kind and knowing. "You're the one who signed the burial log, aren't you? The dispatcher girl."

"The dispatcher woman," Vesper said, before she could stop herself.

Something flickered across Mrs. Ansel's face — surprise, maybe, or approval. "Fair enough. Gray boxes. Third shelf. Holler if you need the microfilm machine; it jams if you're rude to it."

* * *

The probate file for the Estate of Aldous Merrick ran to four thick folders, and Vesper read them the way she read dispatch logs: fast, cross-referencing, hunting for the thing that didn't fit.

It didn't take long to find.

Aldous Merrick had died — officially died, paper-died — on the fourteenth of October, six years ago. Heart failure, per the attending physician's note, though the physician's signature was a scrawl that could have belonged to anyone, including someone signing with a pen in a moving car. The funeral followed within thirty-six hours. That was the first oddity. In Tidehaven, funerals took a week minimum, because half the town traveled south for the winter and everyone wanted to wait for Aunt somebody to fly back. A thirty-six-hour turnaround for the town's founding father wasn't efficiency. It was urgency.

The second oddity was in the list of next of kin and household staff, appended for the will contest. Merrick had been a widower with no children of his body — the heirs contesting the will were a nephew, a grand-niece, and a grand-nephew by marriage, which explained why the fortune had produced such a feeding frenzy. And under *household staff, deceased*, in the same clerk's tidy hand:

**Okafor, Ruth — chauffeur (d. 2019).**

No date beyond the year. No place. No cause.

Vesper pulled the county death index, a bound volume covering 2015 through 2021, and ran her finger down the O section. Okafor, Joseph. Okafor, Margaret. Okafor, Samuel. No Ruth.

She checked the state registry next, on the library's ancient public terminal, which took four minutes to load a page and made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. Nothing. Ruth Okafor had no death certificate anywhere Vester could reach, which meant one of two things: either the probate file contained an error so careless it amounted to fiction, or someone had declared a living woman dead because it was convenient.

People didn't end up in probate files as deceased by accident. Somebody had written it down. Somebody had wanted her gone from the record — not dead, necessarily, just *unfindable*. Unmissable. The way Vesper herself had spent years making sure nobody missed her when she wasn't there.

There was a difference, Vesper thought, between hiding and being hidden. One was a choice. The other usually had a beneficiary.

She photocopied the page — the library copier charged ten cents and judged you silently — and sat back down with the folders, because there was a third oddity, and it was the one that made her hands go cold against the warm paper.

In the inventory of Merrick's estate, listed among the assets: **one (1) 1968 Humber Pullman, black, garage-kept, chauffeur-maintained.**

Chauffeur-maintained. Present tense, in a document drafted after both Merrick and his chauffeur were supposedly dead. Clerks copied language from earlier drafts all the time; Vesper knew this because she'd once spent a whole night reconstructing a disputed fare ledger from draft copies. But it meant the car had been maintained by a chauffeur recently enough that the phrase hadn't been struck. Recently enough that whoever drafted the inventory hadn't thought to change it.

Or recently enough that the draftsman knew exactly who was still keeping the Humber polished, and never expected anyone to read closely enough to notice.

Vesper sat in the humming quiet of the history room and felt something she hadn't felt since the coffin came up empty: not dread. Hope. Thin and strange and slightly frightening, like the first warmth after a long fever. Because a dead chauffeur with no death certificate wasn't a ghost. It was a thread. And threads, unlike ghosts, could be followed.

* * *

Ezra Tull had dug graves in Tidehaven for forty-one years before his knees retired him, and he now spent his retirement on the same bench outside the Salt Line Diner every day from ten to noon, weather permitting, heckling gulls. Vesper had driven him home from his niece's wedding two years ago and he'd talked the entire ride about drainage, which is how she knew he was the sort of man who would answer questions if you brought him something warm.

She bought two coffees and a bag of day-old cinnamon rolls and sat down beside him at 10:15.

"Dispatcher girl," Ezra said, accepting the coffee. "Heard your name come up at the hearing. You signed old Merrick's burial log."

"I did."

"Funny thing, that log." He tore a cinnamon roll in half and offered her the bigger piece, which she took, because refusing would have ended the conversation. "Forty-one years I dug graves in this town. You know how many times I signed a burial log?"

"How many?"

"Hundreds. And you know what I remember about every single one?" He chewed thoughtfully. "Nothing. Absolutely nothing. Logs are for lawyers and liars." He glanced sideways at her. "But you remember yours. So either you've got a memory like a church bell, or something about that day stuck in you like a splinter."

Vesper looked out at the harbor, where the morning boats were coming in with the tide. "Something stuck."

"Mm." He drank his coffee. He let the silence do some work. Old men who'd spent decades listening to grieving families were very good at silence.

"Ezra," she said finally, "who dug Merrick's grave?"

"Not me. I'd retired by then. They brought in a crew from upcounty — Hartwell Brothers, out of Corwick. Flew them in special." He snorted. "For a hole in the ground. Tidehaven's got three men who dig graves regular. But no. Upcounty crew, in and out in a day, paid cash I heard tell."

"Why would the family do that?"

"That's the question, isn't it." He set the coffee on the bench between them and leaned back, looking up at the gulls wheeling over the fish market. "Here's what I'll tell you, and you didn't hear it from me because technically I heard it from my knees, which complain louder than I do. My last year working, I got asked to quote a plot up on the north ridge. Prime spot, sea view, the works. For Mr. Merrick himself — this was years before he died, mind. Family wanted it reserved, paid the deposit, done deal. Then about eight months before the funeral, the reservation gets cancelled. Refund requested. And then the funeral happens and where do they put him?"

"Where?"

"Cedar Row. The cheap rows. Flat ground, no view, plots they sell to folks who don't care where they end up." He shook his head slowly. "Now, I've buried rich men my whole life, and rich men get the ridge. Rich men's families fight over the ridge. A family that moves its patriarch from the sea-view plot to Cedar Row inside eight months is a family that doesn't expect anybody to visit that grave. Ever."

Vesper's skin prickled. "Because they knew no one would be mourning."

"Or," Ezra said, and his voice dropped into the register he probably used for telling children the truth about Santa Claus, "because they knew there'd be nothing in it to visit." He picked his coffee back up. "Course, that's just an old man's knees talking."

"It's more than anyone official has said."

"Officials ask different questions than you do." He looked at her properly then, and there was nothing dotty in his face at all. "You want the real detail? The one that kept me up nights when I heard it? The upcounty crew finished the dig by four in the afternoon. Lowered the box, filled it, done by five. Forty minutes for the service. I've seen longer services for dogs." He paused. "And the widow's flowers — well, there's no widow. The flowers. Ordered from Corwick, delivered direct to the site, never passed through the florist here. Out-of-town flowers for an in-town man. Like the whole thing was staged from a checklist written by somebody who didn't know Tidehaven and didn't want to learn."

Vesper sat very still. A checklist. A rushed timeline, an imported crew, a downgraded plot, a chauffeur erased from the record. None of it proved anything. All of it pointed the same direction: toward a funeral arranged by people who needed it done quickly, quietly, and shallowly — people who expected the ground to stay undisturbed forever.

Except it hadn't stayed undisturbed. Someone had sent an anonymous letter, and now the whole town was staring into that hole, and whatever had been arranged so carefully was coming apart.

"Ezra," she said. "Ruth Okafor. Merrick's chauffeur. Did you know her?"

His eyebrows went up — genuine surprise, the first unguarded expression he'd shown. "Ruthie. Now that's a name nobody says anymore." He turned his coffee cup in his hands. "Quiet woman. Kept that big old Humber like it was a church. Used to park it down by the seawall on her breaks and just sit. I'd be digging nearby sometimes and we'd nod. Never much talk. But once—" he squinted at the middle distance, retrieving it, "—once I saw her pull a kid out of the water. Must be fifteen years back now. Car went off the road near Miller's Bend, went into the cove. Everybody else stood around hollering. Ruthie was in that water before I'd even got my coat off. Pulled the girl out and breathed her back, right there on the rocks. Never told a soul, never wanted thanks. That was Ruthie."

Vesper's chest did something complicated. She knew that story. She had been, in the loosest and most mercifully vague sense, that story — the details were hers alone, the crash, the cold, the hands that had hauled her into the air. She had never known the name of the woman who saved her. She'd been sixteen, half-drowned, and the police report had credited "a bystander."

A bystander named Ruth Okafor, who parked by the seawall on her breaks and asked for nothing.

The coincidence was too large to be a coincidence, and Vesper had learned — these past few days, learning fast — that in Tidehaven nothing was ever just a coincidence. The town was too small. Everything connected to everything, like roots under a sidewalk.

"Ezra," she said carefully, "when did Ruth leave town?"

"After Mr. Merrick died. Week after, maybe less. Just gone. Some said she moved to her sister's downstate, but her sister denied it at the hardware store, loud enough for the whole aisle." He shrugged. "Then the probate papers came out a couple years later and she was listed dead, and folks figured that settled it. Dead women don't need finding."

"Do you believe she's dead?"

Ezra Tull looked at her for a long moment. Somewhere behind them the diner door banged open and closed, carrying out a gust of bacon smell and somebody's laughter.

"I dug graves for forty-one years," he said. "You develop a feeling for it. Which ones are full and which ones are just... occupied." He drained his coffee and stood, joints announcing themselves one by one. "That grave on Cedar Row never sat right with me. Too quick, too flat, too far from the water old Aldous loved. And Ruthie Okafor — I never once believed she was in the ground. Woman like that doesn't die without a wave somewhere noticing."

He tucked the remaining half of the cinnamon roll into his jacket pocket, nodded to her like a man concluding business, and shuffled off toward the seawall.

Vesper stayed on the bench a while longer, the photocopy of the probate page folded in her pocket like a coal.

Ruth Okafor had pulled a drowning girl from the cove at Miller's Bend and vanished before the ambulance came. Vesper had spent fifteen years not knowing her savior's name, and now the name had surfaced twice in two days — once in a dead man's paperwork, once from a gravedigger's memory — and both times it had come attached to the word *gone*.

The case had a living thread. She could feel it now, running through everything: through the rushed funeral and the cancelled plot and the present-tense clause about a chauffeur-maintained car. Somewhere, a woman the law had declared dead was either resting in peace or refusing to cooperate with the arrangement.

Vesper intended to find out which. Not because she was brave — she wasn't, particularly. But because she owed a debt to a pair of hands she'd never stopped dreaming about, and debts, in Tidehaven, always came due.

She was halfway back to the cab office, planning how to search dispatch records for any fare associated with the Okafor name, when her radio crackled on the seat beside her — the office frequency, which shouldn't have been transmitting to her personal unit at all.

Static. Then, faint, warped, almost lost under the interference:

"...girl who signs the logs... if you're listening..."

Then nothing but the hiss of the harbor wind, and the long empty channel, waiting.
