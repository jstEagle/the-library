## 5. The Accountant

The ledger's numbers had a grammar of their own. Kestrel had spent two nights at the kitchen table with the decoded pages spread before him like a patient's chart, and by the third night he could read the illness in them. Every quarter, like a heartbeat: a payment out to something called *Meridian Adjustments*, followed within days by a payment in from the same name, shaved by ten percent. Ten percent for the trouble of making dirty money look like weather—like interest, like dividends, like nothing at all.

He'd been a detective long enough to know that laundering was a service industry. Somewhere on the other end of those quarterly pulses sat a man with a calculator and a conscience he'd sold in installments.

The name came to him the way names always did—not through brilliance but through the dull arithmetic of elimination. Meridian Adjustments had filed incorporation papers in 1991. The registered agent was a firm; the firm's founding partner was a man named Aubrey Fenn, who had once, thirty years ago, kept books for half the small businesses along Cordwainer Street—including, for six years, a certain secondhand bookshop.

Kestrel remembered him now. A narrow man with ink-stained cuffs who had come by every January to reconcile Elias's accounts, drinking tea he never finished and flinching at loud noises. Kestrel had liked him, vaguely, the way you like furniture.

Fenn still lived above his old office on Cordwainer Street, if the city directory was to be believed. Kestrel believed it. He also believed that a man who had spent three decades hiding money for Elias Vane would not want to be found—which meant Kestrel should find him before someone else did.

* * *

Cordwainer Street had gone shabby in the way streets do when the city forgets them: awnings faded to the pink of old scars, a bakery that was now a nail salon that was now boarded over entirely. Fenn's office occupied the ground floor of a narrow brick building, its window papered over except for a slit at eye level, as though the room itself were peering out.

Kestrel knocked. Nothing. He knocked again, longer, and heard—faintly, from above—the particular creak of a floorboard under a cautious weight.

"Mr. Fenn," he called up toward the windows. "My name is Kestrel Crane. I was Elias Vane's partner. In both senses."

Silence, long enough that he counted the pigeons on the wire overhead. Then, from behind the door, a voice worn down to gravel: "I don't have anything to say about Elias."

"You haven't asked me anything yet."

Another silence. Then the sound of three locks surrendering in sequence, and the door opened onto a dim hallway smelling of dust and old toner.

Aubrey Fenn had shrunk into himself the way old men do when they've spent years expecting bad news. His cardigan hung off shoulders that had once filled it. But his eyes—his eyes were quick and wet and terribly awake, the eyes of a man who had rehearsed this moment so many times that living it felt almost like relief.

"The partner," Fenn said. "He talked about you. Toward the end." He stepped back to let Kestrel in. "Toward the end he talked about very little else."

* * *

The office was a museum of defunct accounting: a green-shaded lamp, a filing cabinet with a padlock hasp, ledgers stacked in towers like a paper forest. Fenn cleared a chair of yellowing newspapers and perched on the edge of his desk instead, as if sitting properly might commit him to something.

"I saw the smoke," Kestrel said. "Your shop."

"My shop." Fenn laughed without any humor in it. "You think I didn't know what that fire was? I've known Elias forty years, Mr. Crane. When a bookshop burns and the owner sleeps in the back room and survives—that's not arson failing. That's arson *message*." He rubbed his hands together as though warming them at a fire only he could see. "So. You found the ledger."

"You knew there was a ledger."

"I *kept* the ledger. The real one. Elias kept the pretty one for the tax man, and I kept the true one, because somebody had to remember where all of it went." Fenn's voice cracked and steadied itself. "Do you know how much money passed through my hands for him? Over thirty years? Neither do I. That's the point. That's the whole *point* of what I did—it was designed so no one would ever count it all. But it was more than a lifetime of honest work. For closing one case. One."

Kestrel sat very still. "Tell me about the case."

Fenn looked at him then—really looked, the way a man looks at a mirror he's been avoiding—and whatever he saw in Kestrel's face made him stop performing fear and start telling the truth.

"You don't remember, do you? Not really. You were young. You wanted to be good at your job." He shook his head slowly. "1989. The Halloway fire. Two people dead in that row house on Verrin Street. Your inspector closed it in nine days. Nine days, Mr. Crane. Arson cases take months. Years, sometimes. But Corliss had his man inside a week and a half, and everyone got medals."

The name landed in Kestrel's chest like a dropped stone.

"Corliss," he said. "Commissioner Corliss."

"Inspector Corliss, then. Commissioner now. Same man. Same appetite." Fenn got up, crossed to the filing cabinet, and worked the padlock with hands that trembled until the key found the lock. "Elias told me once—he was drunk, the only time I ever saw him drunk—that Corliss called him in the morning after the fire, before the ash was even cold. Told him who to arrest. Gave him the evidence. A can of accelerant residue, a witness statement, the works. All of it manufactured upstairs, handed down like a delivery."

"And Elias took it."

"Elias took it because Corliss made it clear that taking it was the price of staying on the force, and refusing it was the price of everything else. And then—" Fenn pulled a folder from the cabinet, thick and soft-edged with age, "—and then Corliss discovered that a detective with a secret is a detective you own forever. The payments started the next year. Quarterly. Through me. So that no ledger anywhere would connect a commissioner's office to a bought detective."

He laid the folder on the desk between them. Inside were carbon copies—actual carbons, decades old—of disbursements dressed up as consulting fees, property settlements, insurance adjustments. And beside each one, in Fenn's cramped hand, a single notation: *V. — per C.*

Vane, per Corliss. Thirty years of it.

"Why keep proof?" Kestrel asked quietly. "Why not destroy it?"

Fenn smiled, and it was the saddest thing Kestrel had seen since the funeral. "Because men like Corliss burn loose ends, Mr. Crane. You of all people should understand that now. My records are the only reason I'm alive. A dead accountant proves nothing, but an accountant with copies is a mutual hostage." He closed the folder. "Though I'll tell you something I've only admitted to myself this week. When I heard Elias was dead—when I heard *how* he died—I understood that the hostages have stopped being useful. Corliss is retiring soon. Retiring men clean house."

"That's why you're talking to me."

"That's why I opened the door." Fenn's wet eyes held his. "But understand what you're holding, son. You think you found a crooked cop. This goes up through three chiefs, two mayors' offices, a judge or two who owed Corliss favors. Elias's money bought silence at every level, and Corliss collected a commission on every purchase. You're not carrying a stone to throw. You're carrying the keystone. Pull it and the whole arch comes down—on you, too. On everything you ever solved, because every conviction that crossed Corliss's desk is going to get looked at twice. Including yours."

Kestrel stood. His legs felt distant, borrowed. Outside the papered window, Cordwainer Street went on being forgotten, and somewhere across the city a commissioner was sleeping soundly in a house paid for, in part, by a dead man named Halloway.

"One more question," Kestrel said at the door. "The last page of the ledger. The final entry. It's missing from my copy."

Something moved behind Fenn's face—fear changing shape, becoming something older.

"Then you'd better hope," Fenn said softly, "that whoever tore it out wanted it missing. Because if Elias hid it instead—if he *kept* it—then Corliss isn't hunting a ledger anymore." He glanced past Kestrel, out the window slit, at the empty street. "He's hunting the one page that hangs him. And Mr. Crane—men like Corliss always know exactly who to ask."

Kestrel walked back to the shop through streets gone gold with late afternoon, and it was only when he reached his own door that he noticed the car parked across the way—a gray sedan, engine ticking as it cooled, empty.

It had been empty when he left that morning, too.
