## 1. The Will That Wasn't

The rain had been falling on Ashford since Tuesday, a thin gray insistence that turned the cobblestones outside my office into something out of a daguerreotype — all soft edges and old light. I watched it from the window of Sterling & Associates while my coffee went cold beside me, and I told myself, as I did most mornings, that this was enough. The town, the practice, the tidy rows of estate files in the back room, each one labeled in my own handwriting, each one a life reduced to its assets and its debts. Order. That was what I sold. Order for the living, order for the dead.

Marisol Vane arrived at four o'clock, without an umbrella, her coat beaded with rain like something furred.

I knew her the way you know people in small towns — by adjacency, by rumor. She'd been a fixture at St. Brigid's for as long as I could remember, always in the third pew, always alone. Her daughter had died years ago. The town had a way of not saying things like that out loud, of letting them sink like stones into the water table, so that everyone drank around them. I knew she was dying because everyone knew she was dying. The hospice nurse at the pharmacy had said six months, then four, and now nobody said a number at all.

"Ms. Sterling." Her voice was dry paper. She lowered herself into the chair across from my desk with the deliberateness of someone who had learned to negotiate with her own body. "Thank you for seeing me."

"Of course. How can the firm help you?"

"I need a will drafted." She set a leather envelope on my desk, and beneath it, her hand trembled — not with weakness, I would learn, but with something that looked terribly like patience. "Not for me. Mine is done. Notarized, witnessed, filed. I'm not here about my death, Ms. Sterling. I'm here about someone else's."

I waited. Clients often circled before they landed. It was part of the work — letting them find the words, watching for the ones that mattered.

"Edmund Hale," she said.

The name sat in the room like a draft. Edmund Hale was not a client, exactly, but he was a neighbor of the firm's business — the Hale family had land, and land meant probate eventually, and I had seen his name in enough ancillary documents to know the shape of it. He was sixty-one, hale in the literal sense, still rowed the river in the early mornings when the fog was on the water. I'd passed him at the farmer's market a hundred times. He bought honey. He complained about the price of honey. He was alive in the aggressive, unremarkable way of healthy men.

"Mr. Hale wants a will," I said. "And he sent you?"

"He doesn't know about it." She opened the envelope and slid the contents across my blotter: a cashier's check, and a single sheet of paper covered in her handwriting — cramped, precise, the penmanship of a woman who had once been a schoolteacher and had never quite stopped. "I want you to draft it. A full will. Testamentary capacity affirmed, witnesses arranged, everything proper. Everything *legal*, Ms. Sterling. That matters to me. It has to be unimpeachable."

I looked at the check. It was for double my standard rate, and then some. "Ms. Vane, I can't draft a will for a man who hasn't retained me. He'd have to sign it. He'd have to sit across from me and tell me his wishes."

"He will." She folded her hands. "Three days from now, he'll be in a position to sign anything you put in front of him."

I laughed. I'm not proud of it — it was the small, reflexive laugh you give to a confused old woman, the laugh that is really a door being closed gently. "Ms. Vane, I think there may be some confusion—"

"His sister gets the river property. Not the house in town — the property, with the boathouse. The house goes to a nephew in Ohio he hasn't spoken to since 2019. His collection of maritime clocks goes to the historical society, all except the Chelsea clock on the mantel, which he'll want buried with him, though of course it won't be, it'll sit in a drawer in the probate office for eleven months because no one will want to make the decision about it." She recited it without looking at the paper. She wasn't reciting at all. She was *remembering*. "His debts are small. A credit card, the boat loan. He'll have paid the boat loan by Friday, so leave that out."

The rain filled the silence. I became aware of my own heartbeat, which is not a thing a person usually notices, and of the fact that I had stopped reaching for my pen.

"Ms. Vane." I kept my voice level, the way you do around a dog you can't read. "Edmund Hale is sixty-one years old. He rowed past my window this morning. Why are you drafting his will?"

"Because on Thursday night," she said, "Edmund Hale will drown in the river behind his own house. He'll go out in the fog to check a mooring line — he always checks it before weather, it's a habit his father gave him and he never questioned it — and the bank will be slick, and he'll go in. The coroner will call it an accident. It will be an accident, in every way that matters to a coroner." She met my eyes, and hers were not cloudy, not confused, not anything but terribly, lucidly clear. "And on Friday morning, when they pull him out, you'll want to know why you already have his will in your filing cabinet. Dated Tuesday. Signed, witnessed, notarized. Perfect."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply reported for duty. "I think you should leave."

"I think you should take the check." She rose more slowly, gathering her coat, and at the door she paused with her hand on the frame, and for a moment she looked so frail that my fear transposed itself into pity, the way fear does. "You're the town's filing cabinet, Ms. Sterling. You keep everything and read nothing. I'm asking you to read." She smiled, and it was the saddest thing I'd seen in years. "Double rate. For a will that wasn't."

The door clicked shut behind her. The rain went on. I sat back down across from the check, and the handwritten page, and the cold coffee, and I did the arithmetic that lawyers do — the liability, the ethics board, the complaint she could file, the simple fact that no will could exist without a testator's signature and a man who was, at that moment, alive and buying honey.

It was senility, I told myself. It was a dying woman's mind arranging the world into stories it could bear. It was grief, ten years old and still hungry. I had seen stranger things in estate law. I had seen a man leave everything to his horse.

I picked up the phone to call Dr. Ahn, her hospice physician — to report a confused patient, to discharge myself from whatever this was. And then I looked at the page again. *The Chelsea clock on the mantel.* I had been in Edmund Hale's house once, three years ago, to witness an affidavit. I had stood in that parlor. I had seen the clock.

I had never mentioned it to anyone. Neither had he.

I didn't call Dr. Ahn.

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the light to fail and the rain to write itself against the glass in long, silver lines. Long enough to do the thing I will regret for the rest of my life, which is to fold the check into my blazer pocket — not to deposit it, not at first, but because my hands needed to hold something and it was the nearest thing — and to slide Marisol's handwritten page into a folder, and the folder into the bottom drawer of my desk, beneath a decade of files I had never read.

I told myself I was preserving evidence. I told myself I was a lawyer, and lawyers keep things.

At seven the next morning, through the rain-streaked glass, I watched Edmund Hale row out onto the river, his oars dipping through the fog, a small, steady, oblivious man in a small, steady boat. He raised a hand to my window. I did not raise mine back. I stood there with his death already folded in my drawer, dated and witnessed in my imagination, and I felt the advance in my pocket like a stone I'd swallowed.

Three days, she had said.

I had three days to decide whether Marisol Vane was a madwoman — or the only honest client I'd ever had. And somewhere under the fog on the river, under the rain, under the tidy alphabet of my files, I already knew which answer I was afraid of.

* * *

I didn't sleep Tuesday night. I told myself it was the storm, which had thickened from insistence to assault, the wind worrying at the old sash windows of my apartment like a dog at a gate. I sat up with Marisol's page under a lamp, reading it the way I'd never read anything — not for what it said, but for what it knew.

The details were the problem. Senility invents; it doesn't *inventory*. A confused mind reaches for the general — he'll die, he'll drown, the river — the way a dreamer reaches for a door that isn't there. It does not produce the Chelsea clock. It does not produce the mooring line, the habit inherited from a father, the boat loan paid by Friday. Those were not visions. They were *observations*, delivered in advance, and the only two explanations for them were the ones that had kept me awake: that Marisol Vane had somehow seen a future, or that she had arranged one.

And there was a third possibility, the one I circled until dawn and refused to land on: that she had seen this before. That Edmund Hale had drowned once already — somewhere, somehow — and I was looking at a transcript.

Wednesday I did nothing. That is not accurate. Wednesday I performed the elaborate theater of doing nothing: I drafted a genuine will for the Okafor estate, I returned two calls, I ate lunch at my desk. All the while the folder sat in the bottom drawer like a held breath, and twice I opened the drawer and twice I closed it, and at four o'clock — her hour, I noticed, her hour — I found myself at the window watching the river, where Edmund Hale's boat sat empty at its mooring, rocking in the rising wind, and I hated how much relief I felt that it was empty.

Thursday came in gray and quiet, the storm spent, the air washed and cold. I worked late. I told myself it was the Okafor filing, and then I told myself it was nothing, and at nine-forty I was standing at my apartment window with the lights off, looking down at the black ribbon of the river, at the fog beginning to gather on it the way Marisol had said it would, when my phone lit the ceiling white.

Ray Okonkwo. I hadn't spoken to him in two years — not since the retirement party nobody had wanted to call a disgrace. I stared at the screen through four rings, five, and some animal part of me already knew, the way you know the shape of bad news by the way the phone rings.

"Sterling." His voice was rough, and underneath the roughness, careful. "Sorry to call late. There's — there's been an incident out at the Hale property. The river."

The fog was on the water. The bank was slick. He always checked the mooring line before weather.

"Is he alive?" I asked, and my voice sounded like someone else's.

"They pulled him out twenty minutes ago," Ray said. "I'm sorry, Jude. He's gone."

I sat down on the floor. Not a chair — the floor, my back against the window, the fog and the river and the small lights of the emergency vehicles blooming and fading beyond the glass. In my blazer pocket, folded into quarters, was a check for double my rate, dated Tuesday. In the drawer of my office, three blocks away, was a will that did not exist yet, for a man who had just stopped existing, and a dead woman's — a dying woman's — handwriting that had known everything first.

"Jude?" Ray said. "You still there?"

I looked at the phone. I thought about the third possibility, the one I'd refused to land on. *She had seen this before.* And beneath it, rising like the river itself, the question I would spend everything trying to answer:

What had happened in 2015 that Marisol Vane was making us all live through again?

"I'm here," I said. "Ray — I think I need to show you something."
