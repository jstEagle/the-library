## 3. Everyone Swears She's Dead

Calder Bend in October smelled like woodsmoke and river rot, and Jude Nystrom had been there less than a day before deciding the town practiced memory the way other towns practiced religion — devoutly, and with an iron insistence on orthodoxy.

Jude had checked into the only motel on Route 9, a low brick L with a flickering vacancy sign that buzzed like something trapped, and spent the evening doing what Jude did best: reading. Not novels, not briefs — paper. The photocopied estate file spread across the motel's scratchy bedspread like a dissection. Edith Marlowe's reopened estate. The anonymous filing that had cracked it open after forty years. And one name, typed into the beneficiary line with the calm confidence of a fact rather than a hope: *Lena Voss.*

The problem was simple. Lena Voss did not exist.

Or rather — she had existed, briefly, and then she hadn't. The file's own attachments said so. A newspaper clipping from March 1984, yellowed to the color of weak tea: *LOCAL WOMAN DIES IN ONE-CAR ACCIDENT ON DROWN ROAD.* A grainy photograph of a young woman with dark hair and a smile that looked, even at newsprint resolution, like it had been taken without her permission. And beneath it all, the document that ended every question before it could be asked: Certificate of Death, Commonwealth Registry, signed and sealed.

Jude had closed four hundred estates in twenty-two years. Dead people were Jude's native country; their paperwork was its language. And yet something about this certificate itched the way a forged signature itches — not visibly wrong, but wrong in the wrist, wrong in the rhythm. So in the morning, Jude put on the gray suit that made people underestimate them, and went out to ask Calder Bend what it remembered.

* * *

Doris Pike received Jude on her porch at nine sharp, because Doris Pike had received everyone on that porch for forty years and saw no reason to break the habit for a lawyer.

She was the widow of Sheriff Emmett Pike — yes, *those* Pikes, Jude had already noted, the same surname as Dr. Ambrose Pike whose name sat on the fraudulent-adjacent edges of this case like a watermark — and she was small and silvered and upholstered in lavender chintz, with a teacup balanced on her knee and eyes that missed nothing.

"You're the one handling Edith Marlowe's estate," she said. It wasn't a question. "Forty years late. That's a strange kind of law."

"The strangest kinds are usually the truest," Jude said, and took the offered chair.

Doris smiled the way a drawbridge smiles before it closes. "You want to know about Lena Voss."

"I want to know about everyone connected to Edith Marlowe."

"Then I'll save you time." She sipped her tea. "Lena Voss worked for Edith, summers, when she was in school. Good girl. Quiet. The kind of quiet that made people nervous, if you follow me. Then in '84 she went off Drown Road in the rain and hit the culvert, and that was that. Emmett worked the scene himself. He came home that night soaked through and didn't say ten words for a week." Her voice did something almost tender around the memory of her husband's silence. "He said it was the worst kind of accident. The kind where you keep asking why she was driving that road at all."

"Did he ever find out?"

"He died in 1991," Doris said pleasantly, "so you'd have to ask him."

It was delivered without malice, which somehow made it worse. Jude tried another angle. "Were there family? Anyone who claimed the body?"

"A cousin downstate, I believe. There wasn't much to claim." She set down her cup with a small ceramic click. "Mr. Nystrom. May I give you some advice, since you're new here?"

"I'd be grateful."

"Calder Bend remembers everything, and it remembers it the same way every time. When a town agrees on a story for forty years, the story stops being a story. It becomes the ground you walk on." She folded her hands. "Digging is fine. But mind where you dig."

"I appreciate the concern," Jude said, standing.

"Oh, it isn't concern." Doris Pike smiled again, and this time there was nothing of the drawbridge in it at all. "It's geography."

* * *

The First Congregational Church of Calder Bend kept its archives in a basement room that smelled of dust and old glue, and kept its archivist in the person of Mr. Hollis Brandt — *those* Brandts too, Jude was compiling quite a genealogy — a man of perhaps seventy-five with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and the patient air of someone who had spent decades being the second-smartest person in every room and had made his peace with it.

"Lena Voss," he repeated, when Jude asked. He pulled off his glasses. "You're the third person to ask me about her."

Jude felt the floor tilt a degree. "Who were the first two?"

"Now, that I can't say, because I don't gossip about who asks questions. Only about what's in my records." He said it with such mildness that it took Jude a moment to understand it was a refusal, and another moment to admire its craftsmanship. "But I can tell you what's in my records. Come."

He led Jude past shelves of bound parish registers to a flat cabinet of hanging folders. "Edith Marlowe was our most faithful member until her health failed. Taught Sunday school for thirty years. Donated the roof, twice." He drew out a folder. "And in the summer of 1983, she sponsored a confirmation student. A girl who'd stopped attending after her mother passed. Edith drove out to bring her herself, every week." He opened the folder and turned it toward Jude.

A confirmation photograph. Rows of adolescents in stiff white robes, and there, second row, third from left: the girl from the newspaper clipping, except alive, except smiling — no, not smiling, Jude thought, looking closer. Squinting against the sun, mouth set in something between patience and defiance. Beneath the photo, in faded ink: *Confirmed June 12, 1983 — Lena Voss.*

"She was confirmed," Jude said slowly, "in June of 1983."

"That's what it says."

"And she died in March of 1984."

"Also what it says." Hollis replaced the glasses on his nose and studied Jude over their rims. "You have a very particular way of repeating things, Mr. Nystrom. Like a man checking a lock twice."

"Just thorough."

"Mm." He slid the folder back. "For what it's worth — and I know exactly how little that's worth — Edith paid for that girl's confirmation dress. I processed the check myself. And after the accident, Edith stopped coming to services for nearly two years. Grief, we assumed." He paused. "Though grief usually looks like sorrow. Hers looked like arithmetic. Like she was counting something."

"Counting what?"

"If I knew, Mr. Nystrom, I'd have told someone long ago." He walked Jude up the stairs, and at the door he added, quietly, without turning around: "The first person who asked me about Lena Voss came in eleven months ago. Before the estate was ever filed. You might ask yourself who opens a question before anyone knows there's a question."

* * *

The retired coroner lived ten miles out of town on a gravel lane, in a house with a flagpole and a garden gone beautifully feral. His name was Gerald Ostrander, and he'd held the county coroner's office from 1978 to 1996, and he answered the door in suspenders and immediately said, "You're here about the Voss girl. Doris called me last night."

Jude blinked. "Mrs. Pike called you?"

"Doris calls everybody about everything. It's how she prays." He waved Jude inside, poured coffee without asking, and settled into an armchair with the sigh of a man who had told this story many times and expected to tell it once more. "March ninth, 1984. Rain like the sky had a grudge. Girl comes off Drown Road doing better than fifty, hits the culvert head-on. Car folded like a lawn chair." He shook his head. "I signed the certificate myself. Accidental death. No irregularities."

"May I see your copy?"

Ostrander's eyebrows rose, but he got up without complaint and returned with a metal document box, flipping through folders with thick fingers until he found it. He handed it over.

Jude read it the way Jude read everything — twice, then again slower. Name: Lena Marie Voss. Date of death: March 9, 1984. Cause: multiple blunt force trauma consistent with motor vehicle collision. Manner: accidental. And at the bottom, the signature, looping and confident:

*Ambrose T. Pike, M.D., County Coroner.*

Jude looked at it for a long time.

"This isn't your signature," Jude said.

"It certainly is not." Ostrander sipped his coffee, unbothered. "That's Ambrose Pike's signature. He was deputy coroner back then. Signed off on maybe half the certificates those years. Perfectly normal division of labor."

"Was it?" Jude laid the certificate flat on the table between them. "Dr. Ostrander, I've spent three days in registries. Ambrose Pike completed his medical residency in June of 1985. He took his licensing exam in July of 1985. In March of 1984, he was not yet licensed to practice medicine in this commonwealth. He couldn't sign a prescription, let alone certify a death."

The silence that followed had texture. Ostrander set down his cup very carefully, the way a man does when his hands need something to do.

"Well," he said. "Now that's interesting."

"Is it? Or is it normal division of labor?"

Ostrander looked at Jude for a long moment, and whatever he saw in Jude's face made him decide something. He leaned forward, elbows on knees. "I'll tell you something I've never said out loud, because saying it would have cost me my office and probably my friends, and I liked my friends. That certificate came to me already signed. Came to my office in an envelope, two days after the accident, with a note saying the family wanted expedited processing. I never examined the body. Never saw the scene. By the time I heard about it, the car was already crushed and sold for scrap." He rubbed his jaw. "I told myself it was sloppy procedure. Towns are sloppy. But I'll tell you what I couldn't explain, all these years."

"Tell me."

"There was no next of kin at the burial. No cousin downstate — I checked, later, when it nagged at me. The plot was paid for in cash by an attorney whose name I never could run down. And the casket was closed." He met Jude's eyes. "Closed-casket burials happen, Mr. Nystrom. But a closed casket, a cash plot, a ghost attorney, and a signature from a man who wasn't a doctor yet — that's not sloppiness. That's a recipe."

Jude sat very still. Twenty-two years of estates had taught Jude the difference between a document that ends a story and a document that *is* the story. This one was the latter. This one had always been the latter, lying in a metal box on a gravel lane for forty years, waiting for someone to read it twice.

"Dr. Ostrander," Jude said. "Would you be willing to swear to all of this? On the record?"

The old coroner was quiet for a while. Outside, wind moved through the feral garden, and somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and gave up.

"I'm seventy-nine years old," he said finally. "My wife's buried in the churchyard in town, under a stone Ambrose Pike's family donated to the cemetery fund. Everything I love is inside ten miles of that man's porch." He looked at the certificate on the table, at the confident looping signature, and something in his face hardened into something older than fear. "So no. Not today. But take a photograph of it. Take two. And Mr. Nystrom—"

"Yes?"

"When you find out why they needed that girl dead on paper—" he tapped the certificate once, gently, like knocking on a door, "—you come back and tell an old man. Because I've been carrying the question for forty years, and it weighs more than any body I ever moved."

* * *

Jude drove back toward town with the photographs on the passenger seat and the light going amber and long across the fields. The town story was beautiful in its simplicity: girl, rain, culvert, grave. Four facts, load-bearing, holding up forty years of silence.

But now one of the facts had a crack running through it, hairline and clean, the kind of crack that doesn't weaken a structure so much as prove it was hollow all along. Lena Voss had died on paper, certified by a man who wasn't yet allowed to certify anything, in a casket no one saw opened, paid for by a lawyer no one could name.

And eleven months ago — before the filing, before the letter, before any of it — someone had already begun asking the town her name.

Jude's phone buzzed against the console. An email from the registry clerk, responding to yesterday's request, subject line terse: *RE: PIKE, A.T. — LICENSURE VERIFICATION.*

Jude pulled over at the overlook above the river, where the water ran brown and patient below, and opened it. Confirmation of the dates. Residency complete June 1985. License issued July 1985. And then a final line, appended almost as an afterthought:

*Note: Dr. Pike's original application file was flagged for review in 1999 and subsequently sealed by court order. Requesting party may petition the court for access.*

1999. The year was starting to recur, Jude thought, like a name whispered too often in a house. Jude stared at the river, at the far bank where the trees stood in their autumn fire, and understood with sudden, cold clarity that the question was no longer whether Lena Voss was dead.

It was who had needed her dead — and who, eleven months ago, had started digging toward the answer before Jude ever received a single piece of mail.

The phone buzzed again. Unknown number. A text, four words long:

*Stop asking. She's dead.*

Jude read it twice, then looked up at the road ahead, at the town glowing in the last of the light, and did the thing Jude always did with threats: filed them, dated them, and noted with professional interest that whoever sent it had just confirmed, in writing, that they were afraid of a dead woman.
