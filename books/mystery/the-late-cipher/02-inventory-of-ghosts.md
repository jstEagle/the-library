## 2. Inventory of Ghosts

The rain had stopped by the time Rafael Thackeray turned the key in the shop's front door, but the sky had not decided what it wanted to do with the day. It hung low and pewter-colored over Marrow Lane, promising nothing, threatening everything—the kind of morning that made a man grateful for the smell of old paper and dust, for the small rituals that kept a life stitched together.

He had not slept. That was no surprise. He had expected not to sleep, had almost planned for it the way one plans for weather, and so when three o'clock came and went without surrendering him to unconsciousness, he simply got up, put the kettle on, and sat in the dark kitchen watching steam climb the window like something trying to escape. By five he was dressed. By six he was standing across the street from his own shop, studying it from beneath the awning of the closed bakery, because thirty years of habit do not die quietly even when you strangle them twice.

Nothing had changed. The brass bell above the door. The hand-lettered sign—THACKERAY'S BOOKS, EST. 1994—in paint gone soft at the edges. The window display he'd arranged on Sunday: a row of battered hardcovers propped face-out like patients waiting to be seen. No one had come in the night. No one had left a note taped to the glass or a car idling at the curb with its engine ticking.

And yet.

He unlocked the door and stepped inside, and the bell rang its small bright note, and for one suspended second the shop felt wrong in a way he could not name. Not violated. Nothing so crude as that. Wrong the way a room is wrong when a picture has been moved half an inch—when everything is where you remember it being and yet the whole refuses to add up.

Rafael stood in the doorway with his hand still on the bell's pull-chain and let the feeling settle over him like silt in water. He did not move. He breathed. He catalogued.

The till: closed, drawer shut, key where it lived under the register tray. The reading chair by the radiator: cushion undisturbed, the folded newspaper on its arm exactly where he'd left it, folded to the crossword he hadn't finished. The back-room door: latched. The floorboards: dry, despite the rain, which meant the gutter repair had held. All of it correct. All of it, he told himself, the ordinary inventory of an ordinary Tuesday in a shop that had survived twenty years of Tuesdays by being aggressively unremarkable.

But the air tasted different. Faintly, underneath the vanilla-and-vellum sweetness of aging paper, there was something else. Cold air. Canal air. It clung to his memory of the night before—the tarpaulin, the white faces of the constables, the dead man's wrist turning slowly against the current with a cufflink catching the light of the police lamps.

*E.T.* Ellery Thackeray. His own initials, stamped into silver he had bought himself, worn now by a stranger fished out of black water.

Rafael hung his coat on the hook behind the counter, poured himself the day's first cup of coffee from the dented percolator, and began, deliberately, methodically, to take stock of his ghosts.

* * *

Inventory was a discipline he had learned twice: once as a detective, once as a bookseller, and the two trainings were more alike than anyone would guess. A crime scene and a bookshop both demanded that you notice what was present and, more importantly, what was absent. Both punished assumption. Both rewarded the patience to look at a thing until it stopped looking back.

He started at the front tables, counting. New arrivals, forty-two titles. He counted them twice, then a third time, because the number would not hold still in his mind, and only on the third pass did he understand why: there was nothing wrong with the count. There was nothing wrong anywhere. That was the wrongness. After last night—after a corpse wearing his clothes pulled from the canal four streets away—he had expected disorder. He had braced for it the way a man braces for a blow. And the absence of the blow was itself a kind of message.

*Someone wants me calm,* he thought, carrying his coffee down the center aisle. *Or someone wants me looking here, at this, instead of there, at that.*

He reached the poetry section just after nine.

It occupied two bays near the back wall, in the corner farthest from the window, because poetry did not sell and Rafael had long ago stopped pretending otherwise. He kept it stocked anyway—for Ellery, though he had never said so aloud, not even to himself in the plain words. Ellery had loved the poets. Ellery had been the one who taught him that a well-made line could hide a thing in plain sight better than any safe.

Rafael set his cup on the step stool and looked at the shelf, and the cold moved up through him slowly, the way canal water moves into cloth.

The books were wrong.

Not damaged, not missing, not defaced. *Reordered.* Someone had taken the spines—that long, patient wall of spines—and shifted them, a few centimeters here, a few there, so that the colors broke differently than they should. Where there should have been a run of drab university-press grays there was a slash of green. Where the greens ran, a single white spine interrupted like a tooth in a smile. He knew his own shelves the way a man knows his own heartbeat; he shelved alphabetically within genre, and the alphabet had been broken.

He pulled the white book out. *Selected Poems*, someone obscure, price penciled inside the cover in his own hand: 3.50. He put it back. He took a step backward into the aisle and looked at the whole bay again, letting his eyes unfocus the way he'd once been trained to let them unfocus over surveillance photographs, hunting pattern instead of detail.

And there it was—or almost there, hovering at the edge of recognition like a word on the tip of the tongue. The color sequence. Gray, gray, green, gray, white, gray. Green, green, gray. It repeated. Not perfectly—the shelf was too full, too cluttered, for perfection—but repeating, with interruptions, the way a signal repeats through static.

His mouth had gone dry. He picked up his coffee and drank, and the coffee was cold and he barely noticed.

Because he knew this game. He had played this game, decades ago, in another life with another name stitched into other cuffs. Dead drops marked by chalk lines on brick. Bookshops used as letterboxes, volumes shelved spine-outward in agreed sequences to say *message waiting* or *all clear* or *run*. The tradecraft was older than either of them; it went back to the wars, back further, and it depended on exactly this—a pattern visible only to the person who knew to look, invisible to everyone else, deniable to the point of absurdity. Who could arrest a man for shelving poetry badly?

*Ellery,* he thought, and the name arrived with grief attached, sudden and physical, like a hand closing around his throat.

Ellery Finch had been dead for twenty years. Officially. Unofficially, Ellery Finch had been dead because Rafael had needed him to be dead, had helped make him dead, had stood in the rain outside a burning car on the coast road and watched the flames eat the driver's seat and told himself, *this is the price, this is how we both live now.* He had mourned him properly, at least in private. He had mourned him every time he restocked the poetry shelf.

And now someone had rearranged that shelf in a cipher only a certain kind of man could read, and a stranger had died in Rafael's coat, and the past had found an address.

He heard the bell ring.

* * *

She came in without announcing herself, which told him she was police before he turned around—civilians announce themselves in bookshops, apologetically, as if interrupting a church service. She stood just inside the door letting her eyes do what his had done: sweep, catalogue, dismiss. A tall woman, mid-forties perhaps, in a charcoal coat that had been good once and was now merely honest, hair pinned back with the economy of someone who considered vanity a tax she refused to pay. Her shoes were wet. She had walked from the canal.

"Mr. Thackeray." She produced identification without being asked, held it up long enough to be read. "Detective Inspector Sorrel Vane. I'm leading the inquiry into yesterday's—recovery."

"Recovery," Rafael repeated. "That's a gentle word for it."

"We're gentle until we can't afford to be." She crossed to the counter, unhurried, and he noted that she noted things as she came: the till, the coat hook, the cold coffee on the step stool, the poetry aisle where he stood. She filed each observation somewhere behind her eyes, and he recognized the filing system because it was his own. "You identified some articles yesterday. I have a few questions, if you can spare the time."

"The shop opens at ten. Until then, my time belongs to whoever asks for it first."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more the acknowledgment of a move made on a board. "Then I'll be quick." She drew out a notebook, small and black, its cover soft with handling. "The deceased. We've had no identification. No missing-persons report matching him anywhere in the county, nothing in the national database. Fingerprints came back negative—which, for a grown man in this century, is nearly a profession in itself. Dental records are pending. At present, Mr. Thackeray, the man pulled from your canal does not exist."

"And yet he drowned."

"And yet he drowned." She wrote nothing; the notebook seemed to be for show, or for rhythm, a metronome to pace the conversation. "Wearing your coat. Your shirt. Your cufflinks—one of them, at least. Monogrammed E.T., which matches the name on your business registration. Can you explain that?"

"I can explain the monogram. My late partner's name was Ellery Thackeray—we registered the shop jointly. The initials were his idea. He thought it was funny, sharing my surname on paper." This much was true, and truth delivered flatly was the best camouflage he owned. "As for the clothes—they were stolen. I discovered it yesterday morning. I'd already called it in when the constable knocked about the body."

"So your statement says." She turned a page she didn't read. "Stolen from a locked premises, no signs of forced entry, nothing else taken. No cash box touched, no electronics, no stock. Just a coat, a shirt, trousers, shoes, and a pair of cufflinks. Whoever it was cleaned you out of one complete outfit and left the till sitting fat and untouched." She looked up at him then, directly, and her eyes were pale gray and entirely without mercy or malice—just appetite, the clean appetite of a mind that wanted feeding. "Does that sound like theft to you, Mr. Thackeray?"

"It sounds like someone needed clothes."

"Someone needed *your* clothes. Specifically. Down to the monogram." She closed the notebook. "Here's my problem. Either you were robbed by the most selective burglar in England, or those clothes walked out of here on a body that was always going to end up in the water, and someone arranged the costume in advance. And if it was arranged in advance, then the question isn't who stole from you." She paused, precisely, the way a surgeon pauses over the incision. "The question is who was dressing the corpse as *you*—and why."

The silence stretched. Rain began again against the front window, soft as static. Somewhere in the walls a pipe ticked. Rafael became aware of the poetry shelf at his back, of the reordered spines pressing their silent pattern into the space between his shoulder blades, and he understood suddenly that he was standing guard over it without meaning to—that his body had placed itself between Sorrel Vane and the one thing in the room that mattered.

"You think I knew him," he said. "The victim."

"I think somebody knew him. Somebody dressed him. Bodies don't pick their own wardrobes." She drifted along the counter as she spoke, fingertips trailing the wood, reading the shop the way a blind woman reads a face. "I pulled the ownership history on this place before I came. Twenty years, same proprietor, no incidents, no complaints, taxes paid on time to the week. You know what that tells me, Mr. Thackeray?"

"That I'm boring."

"That you're *careful.* There's a difference, and in my experience the difference is usually guilt." She stopped at the end of the poetry aisle, and for a moment her gaze traveled up the spines—the gray, the green, the white interruption—and Rafael felt his pulse knock once, hard, against his ribs. Then she looked away, dismissing the shelf as every customer dismissed it, and he exhaled through his nose, slow and silent. "One more question. Yesterday, at the canal—you looked at the body for less than ten seconds before you told the constable you didn't recognize him. Ten seconds is fast. Most people need longer, even when the answer's no. Especially when the answer's no."

"How long do I need to look at a stranger?"

"Longer than that." She put the notebook away in her coat pocket. "Unless you've had practice. Looking at bodies, I mean. Deciding quickly what they mean to you." She let that sit between them, unhurried, and then inclined her head toward the door. "I'll leave my card. If anything occurs to you—anything at all, however small—I'd rather hear it from you than find it myself. Finding things myself makes me irritable."

At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and glanced back at him with an expression he couldn't fully parse—part suspicion, part something almost like sympathy.

"For what it's worth," she said, "whoever your dead man was, he went into that water on purpose. The pathologist found no restraint marks, no defensive wounds. People who are thrown off bridges struggle. People who walk in don't." The bell chimed as she opened the door. "A man who dresses himself in another man's clothes and walks into a canal, Mr. Thackeray—that's not a murder. That's a message. I'd very much like to know who it was addressed to."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading into the wet sound of the lane, and Rafael stood alone among his books with his heart going like a trapped bird.

* * *

He waited until her outline had passed the window and dissolved into the gray. Then he locked the door, turned the sign to CLOSED, and went back to the poetry shelf with the deliberation of a man approaching a minefield he himself had laid.

Ten seconds. She'd clocked him at ten seconds. He turned the number over, examining it, and admitted the truth of it: he had known instantly, in the first half-second, that the dead man was no one he recognized—and he had spent the remaining nine and a half making sure his face reported nothing. Because the face had wanted to report something else. Recognition, but not of the man. Of the *costume*. Of the terrible, deliberate grammar of it—his coat, his collar, his initials—arranged on a stranger like words copied out by someone who had studied the handwriting but never learned the language.

Now he pulled the white-spined volume again, and the green one beside it, and the third green, and carried them to the counter under the lamp. He opened each cover. And there, penciled faintly inside the rear dust jacket of the second book—so faint he had to tilt it against the light—were numbers.

Not prices. Prices were his handwriting, rounded and quick. These were small, upright, ruled with a straightedge's precision: *14 — 6 — 22.* Below them, an initial. *F.*

Ellery Finch. F. The initial landed in Rafael's chest like a dropped stone landing in a well—long fall, deep water, no splash he could hear.

He stood in his empty shop with three open books spread before him and the rain writing its own ledger on the glass, and the paranoia that had ridden him since the canal did something he had not expected. It cooled. It condensed. It sharpened into a single clean point, the way fog sharpens into rain, and what remained was not fear anymore but purpose, cold and specific and almost welcome.

Someone had rebuilt Ellery's code on his shelf. Someone had sent a man to die in his clothes. Someone was speaking to him in the only dialect the two of them had ever truly shared—the dialect of hidden things—and they had killed to make sure he was listening.

Rafael took out his pen, turned to a fresh page in the shop's order ledger, and began, carefully, to copy the numbers down.

Whatever the message was, he intended to read every word of it.
