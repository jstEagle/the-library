## 8. The Man Who Wasn't Missing

The dental records came back on a Thursday, and with them the name, and with the name everything else began to tilt.

Rafael learned it the way he learned most things now—secondhand, sideways, through the small courtesies of a frightened man. The mortuary attendant, a soft-spoken fellow named Okafor who bought his crime novels from the shop and always asked for first editions he couldn't afford, slid the file across the counter between them as though it were a paperback Rafael had special-ordered. No official channel. No paperwork trail. Just a man who owed a bookseller a kindness and was too honest to know what the kindness would cost him.

"Tomas Reyes," Okafor said quietly, not looking at the name as he said it, as though the syllables might bruise. "Forty-nine. No next of kin on file. The work was thorough—the teeth had been capped, recent, within the last year. Somebody paid for good dentistry on a man nobody will miss."

Rafael's hand rested flat on the file. He did not open it. He already knew.

"Tomas Reyes," he repeated, and heard his own voice come out level, and marveled distantly at the machinery of himself, the old apparatus that flattened everything before it could reach his face. Thirty years of practice. He could have recited the shipping manifest of his own soul and made it sound like small talk.

"You knew him?" Okafor asked. Then, catching something—Rafael never learned what, some flicker, some failure of the machinery—"You didn't. Forgive me."

"No," Rafael said, and it was true, and it was a lie, and both halves of it were load-bearing. "I never knew him."

He carried the file into the back room and sat with it for a long time among the crates of unsorted stock, in the room that smelled of dust and old glue and the particular sweetness of decaying cloth bindings, and finally he opened it, and there was the face he had already memorized from the canal—waxy now, drained, but the same face, the face that had haunted the edges of his sleep for a week like a word he couldn't place in a language he used to speak fluently.

Tomas Reyes.

* * *

He had not thought of that name in twenty-six years, and when it came it did not come alone. It came with a smell—cigarette smoke and river damp and the vinyl of a car interior baking in summer—and with a voice, low and unhurried, with the faint rounded vowels of a man who had learned his English on the docks and in the courts of two countries.

*Relax, jefe. You breathe like this, they clock you from across the street. Breathe like me.*

They had used him six times. Rafael could count the operations on his fingers, and he did, sitting in the dark of the back room, touching thumb to forefinger, forefinger to middle finger, the old tally. Six times in eighteen months, in the years before Ellery died, when Rafael Thackeray had still been a detective and the Marrow family had still been a client rather than a memory. Surveillance work, they'd called it. Observation posts. When the Marrows suspected a leak in their own house—when they wanted to watch their own lieutenant, their own lawyer, their own cousin—Rafael would sit in a parked car or a rented room and watch, and Tomas Reyes would walk the route Rafael was supposed to walk, visible, deliberate, a decoy wearing Rafael's coat and Rafael's hat and Rafael's particular slouch, so that anyone counting Rafael Thackeray's movements would count them wrong.

Tomas had been good at it. That was the thing Rafael had forgotten and now remembered with the force of a blow: the man had been *good*. Not a thug, not a hired body. A craftsman of small deceptions. He had studied Rafael the way an understudy studies a lead—had learned how he held his hands, how he paused at curbs, how he glanced up at second-floor windows without seeming to. Once, over bad coffee in a car park in the rain, Tomas had laughed and said, *You know what's funny, jefe? I know your walk better than your wife does.*

Rafael hadn't had a wife. That had been the joke. That had been the whole tragedy of the joke, though neither of them had known it then.

And now Tomas Reyes was dead in a mortuary drawer, dressed in Rafael's clothes, drowned or dumped or placed in the canal a mile and a half from Rafael's shop, wearing a monogrammed cufflink with Rafael's initials on it like a signature on a confession.

* * *

He walked the route that afternoon. He told himself it was to think, and it was, but thinking had always required movement, required the body to be busy so the mind could work unwatched, and so he walked—out the shop door, past the shuttered bakery, down toward the canal with its green-scummed water and its rusted railings and the joggers and dog-walkers who passed the spot where the body had been found without a glance, because the dead are quickly absorbed into the geography of a city, another stain on the stones.

The clothes had been his. That was the fact he kept circling, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Not similar clothes. Not clothes bought to resemble his. His—pulled from his own closet, from the rail in the back of his bedroom where he kept the good coat and the two shirts he still wore from the years when he'd had a reason to dress like a man who mattered. The police had photographed the closet, catalogued the gaps, asked their careful questions. *Anyone with access to your home, Mr. Thackeray? Any break-ins you failed to report?* And Rafael had said no, no, nothing like that, and had watched Inspector Vane write it down without appearing to, her pen moving in small economical strokes, her eyes doing the real recording somewhere behind her careful, unreadable face.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not resolve: the clothes were his, the cufflink was his, the face was a face built to pass for his—and yet the man inside all of it was Tomas Reyes, a man Rafael had not seen in twenty-six years, a man who should not have known Rafael Thackeray was alive, because no one was supposed to know that. That had been the entire architecture of Rafael's survival. He had buried his name, buried his service record, buried Ellery's death and his own part in it, and built a bookshop over the grave like a man who builds a garden over a well and never draws water.

Tomas Reyes had found him anyway. Had found him, and then had died in his clothes at the edge of his water.

Unless—unless. Rafael stood at the canal railing and watched a heron stand in the shallows with the patience of something that had never once doubted itself, and he turned the problem the way you turn a cipher wheel, one notch at a time.

If Tomas had come to kill him, he would not have worn Rafael's clothes to do it. A killer does not dress as his victim; that is the logic of a frame, and a frame requires the framer to survive it. If Tomas had come to impersonate him—to be Rafael Thackeray somewhere, briefly, in front of witnesses—then he had failed spectacularly, or he had succeeded and been disposed of, and either way the question remained: impersonate him *where*? Rafael Thackeray had no accounts worth forging signatures for, no property but the shop, no life worth stealing. He had spent three decades making himself worthless to steal. That had been the point.

So the impersonation was not for gain. Which left—

The heron struck the water and came up with nothing, and Rafael felt the thought arrive the way the heron's strike must have felt to the fish: sudden, total, from below.

Which left warning.

* * *

He went back to the shop and did not turn on the lights. The gray afternoon came through the front window in a pale wash, laying long shadows from the shelves like the bars of some enormous instrument, and he moved through the dimness to the shelf that had started all of it—the reordered shelf, the shelf whose spines had formed a pattern he had almost recognized before Inspector Vane's questions had interrupted the recognition, and he stood in front of it now with his arms folded and let himself finally, fully see it.

He had been avoiding it. He could admit that now, alone in the dark of his own shop. He had found the ledger inside the dust jackets—Ellery's penciled numbers, the payments, the dates, the initials, the terrible tidy bookkeeping of a corrupt enterprise—and he had stopped there, stopped at the horror of what Ellery had recorded, because the horror was large enough to hide behind. The ledger was a wound you could look at. The shelf was something else. The shelf was a message addressed specifically to him, and messages have senders, and senders can be dead or alive, and Rafael had not been ready to learn which.

But Tomas Reyes was dead, and the dead do not need protecting from the truth, and neither, he was beginning to understand, does the living man who has spent thirty years hiding from it.

The arrangement was a substitution cipher. He saw it now without effort, the way you see a face in a crowd once you know you're looking for it. Ellery had built it decades ago, back when they were selling books together out of a storefront two neighborhoods over, back before everything—had built it as a joke, almost, a bookseller's game: the order of the spines encoding a phrase from the poem Ellery loved, the poem he'd annotated in the margins of half the stock in the old shop. Rafael had decoded the ledger's numbers using the shelf's logic, but he had never asked the question the shelf itself was asking, the question that existed one level up from the numbers:

*Who arranged the shelf?*

Not Ellery. Ellery had been dead for twenty-six years, and the shelf was recent—he'd have sworn to it, the dust disturbed, the spines handled. Someone living had walked into his shop, quietly, at some point in the recent past, and reordered his inventory according to a cipher only two people alive should have understood, one of whom was standing in the dark with his heart going like a fist against a door.

The other was dead in the canal.

Tomas Reyes had stood in this shop. Tomas Reyes had touched these books. Tomas Reyes—the understudy, the body double, the man who knew Rafael's walk better than his wife did—had arranged this shelf as a message, and then had put on Rafael's clothes and died in them at the water's edge, because a message that can be ignored is not a message, and Tomas had needed Rafael to *look*. Needed him to look at the body, at the clothes, at the cufflink, needed the past to grab Rafael by the collar and shake him until the sealed rooms of his memory broke open. A stranger's corpse would have been a news item. A corpse wearing your own face and your own coat is a summons.

*You breathe like this, they clock you from across the street.*

Tomas had clocked him. All these years, or someone had, and Tomas had been sent—or had come on his own, Rafael would never know now—to say what no note could safely say: *they know where you are, jefe, and they are coming, and here is the map of what they are coming with.*

And then Tomas had died for it. Or had been made to die for it. The clothes were the proof of intent and the method of execution both: dress the double as the original, drown him, and if the original ever surfaces—if Rafael Thackeray's heartbeat ever registers anywhere in the world again—there is already a body wearing his identity, a death pre-recorded, a grave with his name waiting. Kill the warning and the messenger both. Tie off both loose ends with one knot of canal water.

Rafael stood a long time in the dark. Somewhere outside, a delivery van idled; a customer's voice asked a question of no one and moved on. The shop breathed around him, its thousand spines holding their silence.

He thought: *I owe him a debt.* He thought: *I owed Ellery a debt, and I paid it with silence, and silence bought nothing but thirty years of not-living.* He thought of the ledger in its hiding place, the initials and the sums, the judge and the councilman, and the initial R.T. marked *owed*—owed, as though he were a line item, as though the whole machinery of corruption had carried him on its books like an asset, accruing interest.

Tomas Reyes had died in his clothes. The least Rafael could do was find out why, and finish what the man had started with his last act of craftsmanship.

He was reaching for the light switch when he saw it.

A book on the reordered shelf, third from the left, slightly proud of the row—pushed forward a centimeter, the way a bookseller flags a hold, the way Ellery had always flagged Rafael's purchases, a private habit from the old shop, a habit no one else could have known. Rafael slid it out. A cheap modern reprint, poetry, its spine uncreased. He opened it with hands that were almost steady and found, penciled faintly on the flyleaf in a hand he recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall—small, slanted, unhurried, the hand that had kept the ledger and had kept, apparently, one last account—

a single line, and beneath it a date. The date was last Tuesday. Five days before a man wearing Rafael's coat went into the canal.

The line read: *He knows about the box. Get out first.*

Rafael stood in the dark of his shop holding the book, and outside the window the streetlights were coming on one by one, small yellow witnesses, and he understood that the message on the flyleaf was not from Tomas Reyes at all—because the date was after Tomas was already dead—and that somewhere in the city, someone who knew about the box was still writing to him, still arranging his shelves, and was running out of time faster than he was.
