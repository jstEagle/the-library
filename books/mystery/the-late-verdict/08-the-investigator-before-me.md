## 8. The Investigator Before Me

The man I was looking for had a name like a weather report — Dewey Halloway — and a reputation that arrived three towns ahead of him. *Fell apart,* the clerk at my firm's regional office had said, in the tone people use for spoiled milk. *Quit mid-file. Wouldn't take calls. Last anyone heard he was up in Pelican Narrows doing what men do when they stop wanting to be found.*

Pelican Narrows was forty miles north of Marrow Bay, past the orchards and the salt marshes, a fishing town where the fog came in off a different ocean but smelled exactly the same. I drove up on a Tuesday because Tuesdays are honest days. Nobody performs on a Tuesday.

I had memorized his personnel file on the ferry crossing, which took eleven minutes and gave me time to also memorize the safety placard, the menu board, and the face of the man selling coffee who I was fairly certain had once sold me coffee in a different life entirely. Dewey Halloway, forty-nine at resignation, nineteen years with Meridian Mutual. Commendations for thoroughness. A note in the margin of his last review, written by a supervisor with a heavy hand and a heavier pen: *D.H. does not let go. Consider whether this is always an asset.*

*Does not let go.* I'd read that sentence four times before I understood it wasn't praise.

The bar he drank in was called the Anchor's Regret, which told you everything about Pelican Narrows' relationship with optimism. It sat at the end of a street that ended, literally, in water — the asphalt simply gave up one afternoon years ago and the town had decided not to argue. Inside, it was dim and warm and smelled of fryer oil and old rope, and there were perhaps nine patrons, all of whom looked up when I walked in with the synchronized wariness of men who had learned to identify trouble by its shoes.

I was wearing good shoes. That was a mistake I'd only make once.

"Dewey Halloway," I said to the bartender, a woman built like a lighthouse with the disposition of one that had stopped warning ships out of sheer exhaustion.

She jerked her chin toward the far corner booth. "He's on his third. You want to catch him before the fourth or after the sixth. In between is no good for anybody."

"What happens after the sixth?"

"Philosophy." She said it the way other people say *rabies.*

The man in the corner booth was smaller than his file photograph suggested, or perhaps the photograph had been taken in a year when he still stood up straight. He had a face that had been handsome the way an old dock is sturdy — something had happened to it, weather and weight and time, and now it was mostly function. His hands around the glass were enormous and very still, the hands of a man who had spent a career noticing things and had recently decided noticing was a young man's game.

"Mr. Halloway," I said. "My name is Marguerite Sandoval. I'm with Meridian Mutual."

He didn't look up. "No, you're not."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You're not *with* them. Nobody's *with* them. You're *against* them, temporarily, until they're against you." He turned the glass a quarter rotation on the sticky wood, precisely, like a man aligning instruments. "You're the one they sent about the Verity file."

I hadn't said Verity. I hadn't said anything except my name and my company, and the company part he'd already dismissed. I felt the small cold click inside me that I had come to think of as the sound of a lock turning — the moment when a fact seats itself permanently into memory and refuses to be evicted. *He knew before I told him.*

"How did you know that?"

"Because you're the first person from Meridian to walk into this room in three years, and because you have the look." He finally raised his eyes. They were bloodshot but underneath the bloodshot they were quick, quick as a bird's, and they went over me the way I went over documents — cover to cover, margins included. "The look is: polite, prepared, and about to ruin somebody's week. Sit down, Miss Sandoval. You're making the regulars nervous. Earl over there thinks you're from the tax board, and Earl cannot handle the tax board before noon."

I sat. Up close he smelled of salt and bourbon and, faintly, of engine grease, though nothing about him suggested he'd been near an engine in years. Some smells are memories wearing costumes.

"I'll be direct," I said.

"They're always direct. It's the indirect ones you want to worry about." He signaled the bartender with two fingers. She shook her head. He put the fingers down slowly, like a man setting down something fragile. "Direct away."

"The Aldous Verity death file. You closed it in eleven days. Nineteen-year veteran, commendations for thoroughness, closes a seven-figure claim in eleven days."

"Ten and a half."

"*Ten and a half.*"

"Half day on the Saturday. I don't work Sundays. Never have." He smiled, and the smile was the saddest thing in the room, and the room contained a painting of a drowning sailor. "You've read my reviews."

"I've memorized your reviews."

Something moved behind his eyes — recognition, maybe, or its cousin. "That's a strange thing to say."

"People tell me that a lot."

"And do you mean it? Memorized?"

"Word for word. Including the note in the margin of your last review. *D.H. does not let go. Consider whether this is always an asset.*"

He looked at me for a long moment then, and whatever he saw made him laugh — a short, cracked sound, like ice shifting. "Oh, you're one of those. The real thing. They actually sent the real thing." He rubbed his face with both hands. "God help you."

"That's what I'm here to find out. God, specifically, and whether He was involved in the paperwork."

That got a second laugh, longer. "All right. All right." He leaned forward, and I watched him make a decision the way you watch a man step off a ledge he's been standing on for three years. "Ask me your questions, Miss Sandoval. But I'll tell you how this goes first, so you can plan accordingly. You'll ask me why the file was clean. I'll tell you. You won't believe me, then you will, and believing it will be worse. Then you'll ask me who paid me, and I'll tell you I don't remember, and that answer will make you angry, and you'll think I'm lying or protecting someone or drunk enough to have lost it. And none of those things will be true. And then you'll leave, and you'll spend weeks trying to find the name I couldn't give you, and eventually you'll understand why I drink in a town where the road gives out."

The bartender brought him a coffee instead of a bourbon. He looked at it with betrayal but drank it.

"Why was the file clean?" I asked.

"Because I made it clean." He said it flatly, without flinching, a man reading his own confession off a card. "Not falsified — I want to be precise with you, because precision is going to matter to you more than it does to most people. Every document in that file was genuine. Every signature real. Every witness statement accurate to what the witness said. I checked everything twice. Widow's statement, doctor's certificate, ferry log, tide tables, the works. It was the cleanest file I ever closed, and I closed it in ten and a half days because it closed itself. Do you understand? It wanted to be clean. It was *eager.*"

"Eager how?"

"You know how." He pointed a thick finger at me. "You've felt it. Three years later, empty coffin, and you go back to the original file expecting rot, and what do you find? Fresh paint. Everything answers every question before you finish asking it. There's a word for evidence like that."

"Rehearsed," I said.

His finger stayed pointed at me for a second longer than necessary. "Yeah," he said quietly. "Rehearsed. Only I didn't know that word yet, back then. Back then I just thought I was having a good month."

* * *

He talked for two hours. I remember all of it — I remember all of everything — but some of it I remember the way you remember a fire, whole and burning, and the rest the way you remember a grocery list.

He'd caught the assignment in October, three years ago. Routine: large policy, elderly insured, fog-related drowning, the kind of claim that crosses an adjuster's desk in a coastal county every winter. He drove to Marrow Bay expecting a week of pleasant obstruction and a rubber stamp.

"First thing I noticed," he said, "was that everybody agreed."

"Agreed about what?"

"Everything. I interviewed fourteen people — the widow, the daughter, the harbor master, the ferry operator, three orchard hands, the church sexton, the pharmacist, and a fisherman whose name I wrote down and then, I swear to you, could never find again. Fourteen interviews. And Miss Sandoval, in fourteen interviews, nobody contradicted anybody. Not on times, not on weather, not on who-said-what. Do you know what the odds of that are? I've done hundreds of these. People contradict each other about *what they ate.* In Marrow Bay, fourteen witnesses produced one story, seamless, like it came off a single loom."

I said nothing. My silence has been mistaken for skepticism so often that I've stopped correcting people, but Halloway read it correctly anyway.

"You already know," he said. "You've got the same itch I had. The story's too smooth. So you start pulling threads, right? Little ones. And every thread you pull, the fabric just — reweaves. You ask the harbor master about the fog, he tells you about the fog, perfect detail, visibility under thirty feet, lamps lit early. You ask the sexton about the funeral, he mentions the fog, same detail, same phrasing almost. *Thick as wool that night.* Two different men, same words. I wrote it off as coincidence. Small towns share vocabulary. Weather's a communal property."

"But?"

"But then I asked the widow one question I probably shouldn't have. Just one. Off-script. I said, Mrs. Verity, the night your husband died — did he say anything to you before he left?" He paused. The coffee steamed between us. "And she looked at me — Celeste Verity looked at me with those lovely gray eyes — and she said, *He said he'd be home before the lamp burned low.* And it was beautiful. It was heartbreaking. It was perfect. And I wrote it down, and I got in my car, and halfway back to the motel I realized what was wrong with it."

I waited. He made me wait. He'd earned that much.

"It was too beautiful," he said. "Nobody's dying words are that well-composed. Grief doesn't quote itself. And I lay awake that night with the itch, and the next morning I went back through my notes, and I want you to hear this exactly, because I know you'll keep it." He leaned across the table. "Every witness I'd interviewed had given me at least one phrase that sounded *written.* Not spoken. Written. Rehearsed. Like they'd all been handed lines from the same play, and the play was very good, and they were all very committed to their roles, and not one of them knew they were in it."

"What did you do?"

"What any thorough man does." He laughed at himself, without humor. "I started digging. Ferry logs, tide charts, the doctor's credentials. And here's the thing — everything held. Everything. The deeper I dug, the cleaner it got. And that's when I understood, with a feeling I hope you never have, that the file wasn't clean despite my digging. It was clean *because* of it. It was designed to survive exactly the investigation I was conducting. Somebody had built it for a man like me, and I was walking through it like a tourist in a house where every door opens the way you push."

"So you quit."

"No." He looked into his coffee. "First I got paid."

* * *

The payment, he said, came the way such payments come — deniable, elegant, almost flattering. A man he'd never seen before met him at a diner on the coast road, halfway between Marrow Bay and anywhere else. Cash in a document envelope, thick enough to sit on. No name. No threat. Just a request, delivered politely, that the file be closed as it stood, and a suggestion that a man of Halloway's talents might find other opportunities more suited to his gifts.

"Nineteen years," Halloway said. "Nineteen years I did it straight. And the money wasn't even the worst part. The worst part was the relief. I signed the closure report and I felt *good.* Clean file, clean close, bonus check. I drove home whistling." His voice dropped. "It took about six weeks for the whistle to die. Six weeks, and then I started hearing that woman's line everywhere. *Home before the lamp burned low.* I'd wake up saying it. I'd say it into my drinks. Because here's what I figured out too late, Miss Sandoval — I didn't take their money to lie. I took it because the file was already lying, and my signature was just going to be one more true thing inside a false thing, and who would ever know the difference?"

"Who paid you?" I asked. "The man at the diner. Describe him."

And here Halloway did the thing I had been dreading since the bartender mentioned philosophy. He closed his eyes. He reached. I watched him reach — watched his big hands curl slightly, watched his face work — and I watched him come back empty.

"Gone," he said. "Most of him. Height, I think tall. Coat, dark. Voice, quiet. And I know — I *know* — I looked at his face for a full minute while he talked, because looking at faces is the job, it was always the job, and now there's nothing. Not blurred. Nothing. Like a page torn out." He opened his eyes and they were wet and furious. "Three years of drinking, they'll tell you. Pickling it. Maybe. Or maybe somebody paid to have a page torn out of me too, and I don't even get to know which pages."

I believed him. That was the terrible part. I believed him completely, because I recognized the shape of the hole. Someone had edited Dewey Halloway the way they'd edited the ferry log — not by adding lies but by removing truth, leaving the surrounding text intact so the absence wouldn't show. Except absences always show, if you're the kind of person who reads white space.

"Mr. Halloway," I said carefully. "Did you keep copies? Notes? Anything outside the official file?"

For the first time all afternoon, he smiled a real smile, crooked and boyish, and for a moment I saw the investigator with the commendations, the man who did not let go.

"Miss Sandoval," he said, "nineteen years. What do you think?"

* * *

He wouldn't show me the records that night. "Tomorrow," he said. "A man should sleep on a decision like this. Besides, you'll want daylight. The stuff's in my boatshed, and my boatshed at night looks like a confession." He walked me out to the street-that-ended-in-water, and we stood a moment in the wind while he gave me directions to a boarding house run by a widow named Mrs. Tull who, he warned me, charged extra for questions and answered none of them free.

"One more thing," he said, as I turned to go. "You'll figure out eventually that whoever built that file built it knowing someone would come back someday. They planned for the second investigation the way generals plan for the second war. So ask yourself — when you find the thing they missed, the seam, the flaw — ask yourself whether it's really a flaw." He tucked his chin into the wind. "Or whether it's bait."

I drove to Mrs. Tull's with his words sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone. I lay awake in a narrow bed under a quilt that smelled of cedar, running the day's testimony through my head, filing it, indexing it, unable to stop — every phrase, every pause, the exact angle of light on his glass at 4:17 p.m. This was my gift and my cell: I could not put the day down. Somewhere below, in a boatshed at the edge of a town where the road gives out, a man who did not let go was sleeping on the biggest decision of his ruined life, and I had memorized his face well enough to pick it out of a crowd of thousands, and I intended to see it again at nine o'clock sharp.

At 6:40 the next morning, before I had even finished the free coffee Mrs. Tull provided with her silence, a Pelican Narrows patrol car pulled into the yard, unhurried, its lights off.

The officer who stepped out removed his hat, which is what officers do when the news is bad, and looked at me with the particular gentleness reserved for strangers who are about to have a very bad day.

"Miss Sandoval?" he said. "There's been a fire down at the boatshed."
