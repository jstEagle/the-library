## 9. Blood and Alphabetical Order

The phone call came at 6:14 in the morning, and I remember the time precisely because I always remember times precisely, which is less a gift than a condition, like being unable to look away from a car accident you're personally standing in.

"Miss Sandoval." Deputy Elias Crane's voice, stripped of its usual skepticism, down to bare wire. "You'd better come out to Kestrel County. It's about your Mr. Ostrander."

"He was never my Mr. Ostrander," I said, already reaching for my coat.

"No," Crane said. "But he might have been the only man in three counties telling you the truth, and now he's dead in a house fire that started in exactly one room."

I want to be honest here, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life whether I consented to it or not: my first feeling was not grief. My first feeling was arithmetic. Harlan Ostrander had been dead for perhaps six hours, and somewhere between his last breath and Crane's phone call, someone had made a decision about me — whether I knew too much, whether I could prove it, whether I mattered enough to kill for. That is not a comforting thing to calculate before coffee.

My second feeling was guilt, and that one stayed.

* * *

Kestrel County is forty minutes north of Marrow Bay if you drive like a person with nothing to hide, and I drove like that, mostly because my hands were shaking too much to do otherwise. The fog thinned as I climbed out of the bay, and by the time I reached Ostrander's road the morning was almost embarrassingly clear — pale light on wet fields, a heron standing in a ditch with the patience of a creditor.

His house sat back from the road behind a windbreak of pines, or what had been a windbreak. The fire had taken the study and gotten greedy from there. Firefighters were still picking through the black skeleton of it, and the smell — wet ash, charred paper, something underneath that I refused to name until someone official did — hung over everything like weather.

Crane met me at the tape. He looked like he hadn't slept, which was new; in Marrow Bay he'd always looked like a man who slept fine and resented everyone who didn't.

"One room," he said, walking me toward the ruin. "The rest of the house smells like smoke, but it didn't burn. The study burned down to the joists. Accelerant, probably, though the county man won't say so yet. And here's the part I don't love, Miss Sandoval." He stopped and looked at me with those tired deputy's eyes. "Mr. Ostrander kept records. Boxes of them. His sister told us he never threw out a receipt in forty years. There's nothing left. Not a box, not a folder, not a single sheet of insurance paper. In a house fire that stayed in one room."

"The fire ate selectively," I said.

"The fire ate alphabetically," Crane said, and I heard it then, the thing under his voice. Not just horror. Recognition. He was a skeptic by trade, and skeptics know the difference between chaos and curation.

* * *

Harlan Ostrander had been the insurance company's original investigator on Aldous Verity's death, three years ago. He'd quit abruptly, moved two counties over, and taken up residence at the bottom of a bottle with occasional trips up for air. When I'd found him eight days ago, he'd been sitting on his porch with a coffee can of beer at ten in the morning, laughing at me before I finished introducing myself.

"You're the one they sent now," he'd said. "The one with the memory. Oh, they'll love that. They'll *love* that."

"Who's *they*, Mr. Ostrander?"

He'd waved the beer can at the horizon, which contained no one. "Whoever it was paid me to make the file clean. I don't remember their face. I want to be clear about that, because you strike me as a woman who'll ask me eleven times. I was drinking pretty steadily in those days, and somebody bought me a lot of drinks and told me a story about a heart attack, and I wrote it down, and it was clean, and I took my money and my shame and I left." He'd squinted at me. "You know what the funny thing is? I can't remember who told me. I've tried. It's like there's a hole where that ought to be. You'd think a man would remember the worst thing he ever did."

"Do you remember anything?" I'd asked.

"I remember I kept notes anyway." He'd said it quietly, suddenly sober in a way that frightened me more than the beer can had. "Old habit. Even drunk, I wrote things down. Somewhere in this house there's a ledger with every name, every date, every dollar that moved around Aldous Verity's death. Insurance companies think paper is holy. So does whoever paid me, which is why I keep it in a fireproof box under floorboards, which is why I'm still alive, probably." He'd laughed then, a dry sound like paper tearing. "Probably."

He'd shown me the ledger. That was the part I couldn't stop replaying on the drive home, and couldn't stop replaying now, standing in his yard while men in rubber boots carried out what was left of him.

He'd opened it to show off, really — an old man proud of the one discipline he'd kept. A green ledger, columns in his cramped hand, entries alphabetical by surname because, he said, "a drunk can't find chronological order but even a drunk knows his ABCs." I'd read the page he showed me. Of course I'd read it. I read everything. That is the whole tragedy of me in one sentence: I read everything, and then I keep it.

Brennan, T. — retainer, cash, $4,000, dated nine days after the death.
Calloway — ferry office — log amended, instruction received via Celeste V.'s attorney.
Dunmore — pharmacy — phenobarbital purchase, unsigned slip, filed under Verity, A.

Three lines. Maybe four; there'd been an entry half-visible at the bottom of the page that I'd leaned to see and he'd closed the book with a snap, suddenly careful again. But three full lines, in his handwriting, dated, specific — and now the ledger was ash, and Harlan Ostrander was ash-adjacent, and the only surviving copy of that page existed inside my skull.

* * *

They let me identify nothing, thank God; his sister had come from Spokane and done that grim work. But Crane walked me through what the county knew, in the low voice deputies use when they're saying things they shouldn't.

"He was alive at eleven o'clock Tuesday night. Neighbor heard him arguing with somebody on the porch — couldn't hear words, just the rhythm of it. Two voices, one angry, one calm. The calm one left around midnight. Fire started sometime after two." Crane rubbed his jaw. "You talk to him last week. Did he seem scared?"

"He seemed like a man who'd survived something by being beneath notice," I said. "And then I came along and made him noticeable."

"That's not how guilt works, Miss Sandoval."

"It's exactly how guilt works. It just isn't how blame works. I'm working on keeping them separate." I looked at the ruined study, its window now a hole opening onto the sky. "Deputy, did you find a fireproof box?"

"We found the box." He said it flatly. "Open. Empty. Sitting in the middle of the burn pattern like it was placed there. Whoever did it brought the key or the combination, took the contents, and set the fire on their way out. This wasn't rage. This was filing."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about having a memory like mine: people assume it makes you powerful. It doesn't, particularly. It makes you a library, and libraries are only useful to people who come looking, and for most of my life the people who came looking wanted either to use me or to disbelieve me, and both feel roughly the same from the inside.

But standing in that yard, I understood for the first time that I was not a library anymore. I was a witness. Worse — I was the *only* witness, the sole surviving copy of a document someone had just killed a man to destroy. Every line of that ledger page sat in my head with perfect fidelity: the ink color, the smudge on Brennan's retainer, the way Ostrander's pen had torn slightly on *phenobarbital* because he'd pressed hard writing that word, the way he'd watched me read it with an expression I had catalogued without knowing why — hope and terror in equal parts, a man handing over the thing that could save him or hang him.

Someone had decided the ledger was worth a life. Which meant someone would eventually do the same arithmetic about me.

And beneath the fear, doing its quiet corrosive work, was the guilt. Because I had gone to Harlan Ostrander for my own investigation, taken what I needed, thanked him, and driven away — and I had known, some part of me had known, that a man who talks that openly about being paid off is a man whose silence has an expiration date. I could have insisted he leave town. I could have called Crane that night instead of waiting three days to decide whether Ostrander's ledger was credible enough to share. I had treated him like a source instead of a person, because treating people like sources keeps the work clean, and God knows I have always preferred clean.

Now the cleanest thing in Kestrel County was a burn pattern.

* * *

Ostrander's sister found me before I left. Her name was Dorothy, she was seventy-something and built like a fence post, and she gripped my hand with a strength that suggested grief had burned off everything soft.

"You're the investigator," she said. "He talked about you. Called me Sunday night, after you visited." Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely level. "You know what he said? He said, 'Dot, I finally told somebody the whole thing, and she remembered it better than I did.' He sounded — I haven't heard him sound like that in years. Like a man putting down weight."

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it so plainly that it came out with no polish at all.

"He was a good man who did a bad thing and never forgave himself." She squeezed my hand once, hard. "Whatever's in your head that he gave you — you use it. Don't you dare let it die in there with everything else."

I promised. It was the easiest promise I've ever made and I suspect it will be the most expensive.

* * *

On the drive back to Marrow Bay, the fog rose up to meet me like it had been waiting, and I did what I always do when I can't sleep or breathe or stop thinking, which is recite. I ran the ledger page front to back, then back to front, then checked it against itself the way other people check a lock twice. Brennan. Calloway. Dunmore. The torn pen-stroke. The date column. The half-entry at the bottom — I closed my eyes at a red light and pulled it up, and there it was, the fragment I'd glimpsed before Ostrander snapped the cover shut:

**E—, E. — payment authorized, "C.V. personal account," memo: "for the fog."**

For the fog. Three words, and my whole understanding of the case tilted. The fog that was supposedly too thick for the ferry the night Verity died. Someone had *paid* for the fog — or paid for the record of it, or paid for the lie built on it. And the authorization came from C.V.'s personal account. Celeste Verity. Or whoever had access to Celeste Verity's money, which in Marrow Bay might be a longer list than anyone would admit.

I sat in the drifting gray with my hands at ten and two and understood, finally and completely, what I was carrying. Not information. Ammunition. And ammunition makes you a target, but worse, it makes everyone near you collateral — Crane, Iris, poor hollow-eyed witnesses, anyone who stood close enough to be mistaken for a hiding place.

My phone buzzed at the town limit. Crane.

"Miss Sandoval. Two things." His voice had changed again — harder, faster, a man running downhill. "First, the county traced the accelerant purchase. Cash, hardware store in Kestrel, Tuesday afternoon. Store camera caught the buyer from behind. Tall, coat with a fur collar."

Celeste Verity owned a coat with a fur collar. Half the women of Marrow Bay did; it was practically a uniform. That was the trouble with this town — every clue fit everybody, because everybody had been dressed by the same decade.

"Second thing." A pause, long enough that I checked the signal bars. "When I got back from Kestrel tonight, there was a message waiting from the sheriff's office. Orders from upstairs. Effective immediately, I'm off anything touching the Verity estate. Reassigned to traffic detail." A bitter little laugh. "Traffic. In a town with four roads."

"Crane—"

"And the third thing, which I wasn't supposed to call you about at all." Lower now, almost a whisper, fog pressing against the car windows like it wanted in. "Before they reassigned me, I pulled one file. One. An autopsy addendum on Aldous Verity that somebody ordered sealed three years ago. I've got it in my glovebox right now, and I don't know who I can trust, and I keep thinking about a man in Kestrel County who kept records in a fireproof box and it didn't save him." Another pause. "Can I trust you, Miss Sandoval? Honest answer. Because I'm about to do something stupid, and I'd like to do it with the right person."

I looked out at Marrow Bay materializing around me, lamplit and fog-wrapped and loyal to the point of burial, and I thought of Harlan Ostrander's torn pen-stroke, and Dorothy's grip on my hand, and the empty fireproof box sitting in the middle of a burn pattern like a signature.

"Yes," I said. "Come to my office. And Deputy — take the long way. Check your mirrors. If anyone followed you out of that station, drive past my window and keep driving, and we'll find each other tomorrow."

He said he would. He hung up.

I sat in the parked car outside my office for a full minute, memorizing the street — every lit window, every parked truck, every silhouette — because from tonight forward, I couldn't afford to forget anything, and neither, apparently, could anyone else.
