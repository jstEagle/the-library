## 17. What Celeste Buried

The conservatory smelled the way it had three days ago — wet loam and jasmine and something older underneath, something like a church that had learned to grow things instead of forgive them. Marguerite stood at the threshold of the glass house and let her memory do what it always did: lay the room out in perfect inventory. The fig tree in its copper tub, third leaf from the top newly yellowed since Tuesday. The watering can on the left bench, moved six inches to the right. The pruning shears, absent now, where they had lain open like a small silver mouth.

And Celeste Verity at the far end, in dove-gray wool, her hands folded in her lap as though she'd been sitting there since before Marguerite was born and intended to go on sitting there long after.

"You came back," Celeste said. "I did wonder. People so rarely come back after they've declined an offer. Pride usually carries them off like a fever."

"I brought my notebook," Marguerite said, holding up the leather cover. "Empty pages. I thought you'd appreciate the symbolism."

Celeste's smile arrived precisely on schedule, the way a ferry arrives on a fogless day. "Sit down, Miss Sandoval. You'll want to be comfortable for whatever speech you've rehearsed."

"That's just it," Marguerite said, taking the iron chair across from her. The metal was cold through her coat. "I didn't rehearse anything. I never have to."

* * *

She had spent the night before this one in her rented room above the chandlery, not sleeping but *reading* — reading the interior of her own head, which was the only library that had never once misplaced a book. Six witnesses. Six testimonies gathered over five weeks of canvassing the town that kept its own records. And threaded through all six, like a colored thread pulled through six different garments, the same phrases. Not similar. Identical. Word for word, pause for pause, from people who had nothing in common except Marrow Bay and forty years of breathing the same fog.

A town doesn't talk like that. A town talks in variations, in dialects of grief and grudge. A town that talks in unison has been taught.

She'd made the list at two in the morning, her handwriting getting calmer as the list got longer, because that was the terrible arithmetic of it: the more certain she became, the quieter she felt. Certainty in Marguerite Sandoval had never felt like triumph. It felt like standing at the edge of a high place and realizing the railing had been removed by someone who knew exactly how much you liked to look down.

Now she looked at Celeste Verity across four feet of cold conservatory air and began.

"Doctor Amos Pell," she said. "Retired two years before Aldous Verity died, though he signed the death certificate anyway. When I asked him about it, he told me — and I quote him exactly — '*I signed what needed signing. In a town this size, doctoring is mostly paperwork with a stethoscope.*'"

Celeste's expression did not change. Marguerite hadn't expected it to. Not yet. This was the overture; even Celeste wouldn't recognize the piece until the second instrument came in.

"Deputy Elias Crane," she went on, "who was ordered to suppress the autopsy addendum and who told me, in his kitchen, with his hat wrung in his hands like a dishrag: '*You get to a certain age in a town like this and you understand some doors are painted on. You can knock all you want. It's kinder for everyone if you stop.*'"

Nothing. A faint tilt of the head, the way one listens to weather.

"Mrs. Odette Brannigan, who kept the ferry ledger. '*Fog that thick, no boat goes out, and no man who loved this bay would ask one to.*'" Marguerite let the sentence sit in the jasmine air. "Mrs. Brannigan is seventy-eight years old, Mrs. Verity. She has lived her whole life within earshot of that dock. And yet when she said those words to me, she said them the way a schoolgirl recites — with the little hitch in the middle, the breath taken before 'no man,' the way you breathe before a line you were made to memorize and are afraid of dropping."

Still nothing. But the hands in Celeste's lap had rearranged themselves by a finger's width, right thumb crossing over left instead of under, and Marguerite's memory took a photograph of that too, filed it, labeled it.

"Tomas Reyes, who drove the hearse. '*We bury our dead proper in Marrow Bay. Whatever else a man was, the ground gets him whole.*'" She paused. "Mr. Reyes cried while he said it. Real tears. I believe he believed every word. That's what makes it remarkable, don't you think? He wasn't lying. He was *reciting*, and the recitation had become true for him, the way a prayer becomes true if you say it enough times in front of enough coffins."

The fig tree's highest branch ticked against the glass in a wind Marguerite couldn't feel from inside. Somewhere out in the fog-bound streets, the noon bell rang its two flat notes, and neither woman moved.

"The Reverend Halloway," Marguerite continued. "'*Grief is a debt this town pays in full, and we do not haggle with it.*' The Reverend, who in twenty-two years of sermons has never once used the word 'haggle.' I checked. I have checked everything, Mrs. Verity. That's rather the point of me."

"And finally—" she unfolded the last page of her mind, though her hands stayed empty in her lap, the notebook still shut, still blank, still symbolic— "Silas Marsh, the harbormaster, who told me: '*Some nights the fog comes in like a guest who won't leave, and a man learns to stop asking it questions.*'"

Six witnesses. Six phrases. And now, the seventh voice — the one that had never spoken any of them aloud, because it had written them.

Marguerite leaned forward. "Here is my favorite part. Here is the thing I have turned over perhaps nine hundred times since Tuesday. These six people have almost nothing in common. Different churches, different grudges — Silas Marsh hasn't spoken to Tomas Reyes since the flood year, over a dispute about a mooring I could describe to you in detail if you'd like. They don't share idioms. They don't share sermons or newspapers or even, in two cases, a mutual tolerance. And yet they share sentences. Whole sentences. With each other's rhythms in them — the Reverend's cadence shows up in Mrs. Brannigan's mouth, and Mrs. Brannigan's maritime habit of speech turns up in the Reverend's. They've been *cross-contaminated*. Deliberately. By someone who sat with each of them, separately, and fed them lines until the lines grew roots."

The wind found the conservatory properly then, shivering the panes, and in the shifting light Celeste Verity's face was for one instant a landscape without a map.

"Someone with access to the original death file," Marguerite said softly. "Someone who knew what the investigators would need to hear, years before any investigator came. Someone who understood that a town's loyalty isn't a wall, Mrs. Verity. It's a script. And scripts need a hand to write them."

* * *

For three seconds, nothing happened that any ordinary witness would have noticed.

Marguerite counted anyway. It was not a skill she had chosen; counting was simply what her mind did with silence, the way other people's minds hummed. One. The dove-gray shoulders settled a quarter inch, a queen adjusting a crown that had begun, very slightly, to slip. Two. The folded hands separated — fully now, palms turning upward on Celeste's knees, the gesture of a woman setting down something heavy, or preparing to catch something falling. Three. And in her eyes, behind the composure that had held against five weeks of investigation like sea glass holds against surf, something moved that Marguerite had no clean word for. Not guilt. Guilt looks cornered. This looked *tired* — tired the way a lighthouse keeper is tired, the way someone is tired who has stood a watch so long they've forgotten whether anyone ever asked them to.

Four.

On the fourth second, Celeste laughed. It was a good laugh, warm and low, and it fit her face the way the restored ferry log fit its binder: perfectly, and falsely.

"Well," she said. "You *are* worth the fee your firm is charging. Though I'll confess I don't follow half of what you're implying. People repeat things, Miss Sandoval. That's what towns are. Echoes with houses attached."

"They don't repeat *sentences they've never heard anywhere else*," Marguerite said. "Not six times. Not verbatim. Not with each other's grammar in their mouths." She opened the notebook at last, deliberately, and let Celeste see the blank page. "I haven't written a word. I don't need to. Every phrase I just gave you is stored in here, complete, permanent, timestamped to the hour I heard it. If you asked me tomorrow what Mrs. Brannigan wore, I could tell you: the blue cardigan with the mended elbow, left sleeve, and she tugged that cuff eleven times in fourteen minutes because her late husband bought the cardigan and she still argues with him through it. That's what you're dealing with, Mrs. Verity. Not an investigator. An archive that walks around and asks questions."

Something flickered across Celeste's face at *archive* — and Marguerite caught that too, catalogued it, hated herself a little for how completely she caught it. Pity. It had been pity. Brief and unguarded, aimed not at herself but at Marguerite, the way one pities a child carrying water in a sieve who believes the sieve is a gift.

"You sound proud of it," Celeste said.

"I am proud of it."

"Yes." Celeste rose, unhurried, and crossed to the fig tree, where she performed the small domestic sacrament of touching a leaf and finding it satisfactory. "My husband used to say something like that. About his own memory. Aldous forgot nothing either — names, debts, slights, the exact wording of promises made fifty years ago over brandy. Everyone thought it was a gift. Living with it, I can tell you, it was a *ledger*. He kept us all in it. Every kindness entered, every failure carried forward with interest." She turned. "Do you know what a town does, Miss Sandoval, when the man who founded it keeps a ledger on everyone in it? It learns its lines. It learns them early and it learns them well, because the penalty for improvisation is a lifetime of being remembered wrong."

The words landed in Marguerite's chest with the specific weight of a key fitting a lock she hadn't known was there.

"So when he died," Celeste went on, in the tone of a woman discussing drainage, "the town already knew its parts. I only had to remind them. Gently. Over tea, over casseroles, over thirty years of funerals where I stood at every graveside and showed them how grief was done in Marrow Bay. Is that a crime? Teaching people the words that keep them kind to one another?" Her chin lifted. "They were *better* with the script. Ask any of them. Ask Odette Brannigan whether the last five years haven't been the most peaceful this town has known."

"They buried a lie in peace," Marguerite said. "That's not the same as peace."

"No," Celeste agreed, and the agreement was worse than any denial. "It isn't."

* * *

Marguerite had prepared for many versions of this room. She had prepared for denial, for the veiled threats reprised, for the elegant suggestion that a woman with Marguerite's sealed file might prefer to close this case the way she'd closed the last one. She had not prepared for Celeste Verity to sit back down across from her, fold her hands, and look at her with something dangerously close to respect.

"You think I killed him," Celeste said.

"I think someone did. Or someone staged it, which may amount to the same thing, or may not. The trust pays out on proof of fraud, Mrs. Verity. Someone wanted that coffin empty and found."

"Mm." Celeste gazed past her, through the fogged glass, toward the orchard hills invisible in the gray. "You've found the trust, then. Iris will be pleased. She always was the clever one — took after Aldous, God help her." A pause, and then, quietly, in a voice stripped of all its varnish: "Do you know what I buried out there, Miss Sandoval? Under the orchard? Have you gotten that far?"

Marguerite's pulse ticked up one notch. "Should I tell you?"

"No." Almost gently. "Because you'll only remember it perfectly, and some things are kinder carried loose." She smoothed her skirt, and the composure reassembled itself, pane by pane, until the woman in the dove-gray wool was once again a finished structure. "Here is what I will give you, since you came all this way and recited so beautifully. You're right about the script. I wrote it. I taught it. I would teach it again, because this town was drowning in that man's ledger and I threw it a rope made of words. But you are wrong about the rest, and the difference matters, and one day — soon, I think — you'll stand somewhere with your flawless archive wide open and discover that being right about everything is not the same as knowing what happened."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Celeste said, rising, gathering her gloves, "that you should ask yourself why the empty coffin was *found*. Forty witnesses, Miss Sandoval. Exhumations require petitions, signatures, judges. Somebody wanted that ground opened in front of the whole town, and it wasn't me — I fought the petition, as you'll recall, politely and at length. So while you've been admiring my handwriting in other people's mouths, someone has been using yours." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, jasmine and cold air moving between them. "An archive that walks around and asks questions. What a marvelous thing to build a stage for."

Then she was gone, her footsteps fading down the gravel path, and Marguerite sat alone in the conservatory with four seconds of a breaking face preserved forever in her head and the sudden, vertiginous understanding that she had just been handed a question she couldn't put down:

If Celeste hadn't fought the exhumation — if she'd opposed it, delayed it, argued it — then who had filed the petition?

And why did the answer, wherever it lived in her memory, refuse to surface — as though some part of her perfect archive had already been read by someone else, and edited?

Outside, the fog pressed its face against the glass, patient as a witness waiting to be called.
