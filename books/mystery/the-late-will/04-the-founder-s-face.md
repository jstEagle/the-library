## 4. The Founder's Face

Mabel Dunn had been cataloguing the Voss collection for eleven years, and in all that time she had developed exactly three rules, which she considered a reasonable number for a woman of seventy-four who had spent her working life as a school librarian and her retirement as the unpaid, unthanked, and entirely indispensable archivist of the Meridian Falls Historical Society.

Rule one: never trust a donation that arrives with its own press release.

Rule two: if a box smells like mice, wear the gloves, and if it smells like perfume, wear two pairs.

Rule three: everything Harlan Voss ever touched was arranged to make him look taller than he was. This was not strictly an archival rule. It was more of a life rule. But Mabel had found it held up remarkably well across disciplines.

She arrived at the historical society on the morning after the exhumation, as she arrived every Tuesday and Thursday and, lately, most Saturdays too, letting herself in with the key that hung on a nail inside her own back door because Edna Pruitt, the society's president, kept losing hers. The building had once been the First Congregational Church, deconsecrated in 1971 and reconsecrated almost immediately to the town's favorite religion, which was itself. Stained glass on one side, filing cabinets on the other. God in the rafters, Harlan Voss everywhere else.

The radio on the front desk was already murmuring—Mabel left it tuned low to the station out of Klamath Falls because silence made her think about her hips, which had begun making negotiations on her behalf without consulting her first. She hung her coat, filled the kettle in the little kitchenette where the Sunday school used to meet, and settled into her chair at the worktable with the day's assignment: accessioning the latest crate from the Voss estate, sent over by Whitaker Voss's people with, she noted, a press release stapled to the lid.

"Rule one," she told the crate, and peeled it off.

The crate contained what the press release called *artifacts of civic significance* and what Mabel called the usual: commemorative plates from the Founders' Day centennial, a ceremonial ribbon-cutting scissors the size of a fishing gaff, four framed photographs of Harlan at various ages looking variously stern, sternly benevolent, benevolently stern, and, in one unfortunate 1970s effort, stern in a leisure suit. There was a ledger of the Meridian Falls Mining & Timber Company, 1909–1923, water-damaged but legible. There was a shoebox of correspondence that smelled faintly of cedar and ambition. And there was a brass plaque, freshly polished, reading HARLAN VOSS — FOUNDER — VISIONARY — BENEFACTOR, which Mabel set face-down on the table without deciding why.

She worked the way she'd taught thirty years of children to work: one thing at a time, hands clean, mind open, opinions filed separately. She wrote accession numbers in pencil because ink was for people who never changed their minds, and she had changed hers many times, mostly about men.

It was while she was photographing the framed pictures for the record that she noticed it.

Not in the new crate. In the old display.

The historical society kept a permanent exhibit along the north wall—*Meridian Falls: A Legacy in Portraits*—and at its center, in the place of honor, hung the great oil painting of Harlan Voss done in 1958 by a traveling artist whose name nobody remembered but whose habit of adding an inch and a half of shoulder to every subject was, in Harlan's case, barely necessary. The portrait showed him at fifty, silver-templed, one hand resting on a granite boulder as though he and the boulder had come to an understanding. Beneath it, on the wall, a small card: *Harlan Voss (1908–1994), Founder of Meridian Falls.*

Mabel stood in front of it with her camera still raised and did not take the picture.

The portrait had been moved.

Now, moved was a strong word for what she was seeing. The frame sat in the same spot on the same hook. But the painting itself had been shifted within the frame—or rather, the frame had been shifted on the wire—and the effect was subtle, the kind of thing only a person who had dusted that wall every week for eleven years would catch. The portrait hung a half-inch lower than it should, tilted a degree to the left, so that Harlan's painted gaze—which had always surveyed the room with the mild satisfaction of a man reviewing his own eulogy—now looked slightly off toward the corner. Toward the dark end of the exhibit hall. Toward the auxiliary shelves.

Toward, though Mabel would not have phrased it this way even to herself, the boxes.

She took hold of the frame with both hands and lifted it square. The wire was fine. The hook was fine. Someone had nudged it—recently; there was a faint scuff of fresh dust below the frame's right corner where a knuckle or a sleeve had brushed the wall.

"Well," said Mabel, to Harlan, "you always did hate being ignored."

Harlan, painted, said nothing, which was unlike him. The living Harlan had never once let a silence stand. He'd been dead thirty years and he was still the loudest man in every room he hung in.

She went to put the kettle on, mostly because her knees wanted the walk, and partly because a mystery this small deserved tea before it earned coffee.

* * *

The trouble with knowing a town for seventy-four years is that you can't look at anything in it without the whole of it arriving at once. When Mabel poured her tea at the little counter and looked out the window at Fogarty Street, she didn't see the street. She saw 1984 sitting on top of it like weather.

June Calloway had worked at the Voss estate the summer she turned nineteen. Everyone knew June, or everyone thought they did—she was the Calloway girl, Danny and Rosa Calloway's youngest, the one with the laugh that carried clear across the Presbyterian parking lot, the one who'd waitressed three summers at the Bluebird Diner and could balance six plates up one arm and a seventh on her palm like a circus act. She'd taken the estate job because it paid real money, housekeeping and serving at Mr. Harlan's dinners, and because her mother wanted her somewhere respectable until fall, when she was supposed to start at the community college in Bend.

Mabel remembered the last time she'd seen her. It was the second week of June, 1984, outside the pharmacy of all places—June in her blue estate smock, buying a paperback, laughing about something with Ruthie somebody, one of the sanatorium girls, and Mabel had said something foolish and grandmotherly like *don't grow up too fast*, and June had grinned and said, "Too late, Mrs. Dunn."

And then June 12th came, and June didn't.

They dragged the river for nine days. They walked the logging roads in lines. Harlan Voss himself had appeared at the search staging area in his good coat and offered a reward of five thousand dollars, which in Meridian Falls in 1984 was roughly the price of a soul, and the whole town had watched him do it and felt better, because when the richest man in the county takes your trouble seriously, your trouble must be serious.

Emil Brandt, the caretaker, was arrested the following spring. He confessed. Then he recanted. Then he hanged himself in the county jail with his own belt, and the town closed the file the way you close a creaky door—with your shoulder, quickly, not looking.

Mabel had been forty-four that summer. She remembered thinking, at the funeral-that-wasn't—a memorial service, no body, just photographs and hymns—that she couldn't picture June's face anymore. Not the features. Just the laugh. Grief does that; it keeps the sound and loses the picture, like a radio station fading as you drive.

She had thought about June maybe ten thousand times since, mostly in flashes—the paperback in her hand, the blue smock, *too late, Mrs. Dunn*—and each time with the same small dishonest comfort: *they never found her, but surely, surely, if she were anywhere to be found—*

Yesterday they had opened Harlan Voss's coffin and found river stones and a rotten coat.

Mabel set down her tea. Her hands were steady, which annoyed her. At her age a person deserves a tremor when it's warranted.

* * *

By mid-afternoon she had abandoned the Voss crate entirely, which violated her personal policy against letting donations boss her around, and was instead standing on the rolling ladder in the auxiliary stacks, pulling boxes she hadn't opened in years.

The historical society's collection was organized by a system only Mabel understood, which was fine, because only Mabel needed to. Local families got letter codes. Institutions got numbers. Events got whatever Mabel happened to be feeling that decade. The Calloway material lived under C, naturally—but not under CA for Calloway. Under DI.

Disappearance.

One box. That was all. One gray archival box, labeled in her own younger handwriting, *Calloway, J. — disappearance, 1984 — clippings, cards, misc.* She carried it down the ladder rung by rung, holding it against her hip like a grandchild, and set it on the worktable next to the face-down brass plaque, and for a moment she simply looked at it.

Then she took off her glasses, polished them on her cardigan, put them back on, and opened it.

Newspaper clippings, yellowed and soft as moth wings. *LOCAL GIRL MISSING.* *SEARCH CONTINUES FOR CALLOWAY YOUTH.* *VOSS OFFERS $5,000 REWARD.* A candle from the vigil, burned down to a stub, kept because in 1984 Mabel had been the sort of person who kept candles. A sympathy card signed by forty-one townspeople, its message printed in a font designed to look handwritten, which fooled no one then and fooled no one now. And at the bottom, wrapped in a page of the *Klamath Falls Herald* from 1985, a photograph.

Mabel had forgotten the photograph. That was the thing that undid her—not the memory of it but the forgetting. She had catalogued this box herself, itemized it herself, and her own mind had quietly set the photograph aside the way you set aside a dish too hot to hold.

It was a gala photo. The Voss estate, Christmas 1983, printed in the society pages of the regional paper. Harlan Voss center, tuxedoed, hand on the mantel, wearing the expression of a man who has just been told the fish course is excellent and intends to verify personally. Around him, the founding families in their finery—young Whitaker in a clip-on bow tie, Camille with the family jaw and a dress worth more than the diner. And at the edge of the frame, half-turned toward the camera as though caught between duty and escape, a girl in a server's uniform with dark hair pinned up and a smile that hadn't decided yet whether to be polite or real.

June. Nineteen years old, eight months before the river and the roads and the reward.

Mabel's thumb hovered over the girl's face. And stopped.

Because beside June—close beside her, shoulder nearly touching shoulder, close enough that whoever cut the paper had clearly noticed—stood another young woman. Tall, fair, in a white uniform with a small collar: a nurse's uniform, Mabel realized, the kind the girls from St. Ambrose Sanatorium wore to town on their half-days. The woman's arm was angled toward June's, familiarly, the way friends stand when they've forgotten anyone is watching.

Her face had been scratched out.

Not torn—scratched. Deliberately, furiously, with something sharp, penknife or pin, gouged deep enough to tear through the newsprint and into the cardboard backing beneath. The scratches crossed and recrossed in a small dense storm, and around them the glossy paper had buckled, and Mabel, who had handled ten thousand damaged documents and repaired flood-soaked ledgers and once un-stuck a photograph from an album with steam and patience and prayer, felt the hair rise on her forearms all the same.

You scratch out a stranger's face by accident. You scratch out a friend's face because you can't bear it. You scratch out an enemy's face because you want to hurt them and they aren't there to hurt.

She turned the clipping over. On the reverse, in ballpoint, faded to gray, someone had written a date: *6/12/84.* No name. No explanation. Just the date, written the way you write a date when you intend to remember exactly where you were.

Mabel sat down slowly on the work stool, which complained, as it always did, and she let it.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Down on Fogarty Street a delivery truck idled outside the pharmacy, and a woman in a white coat came out to sign for a package, and Mabel watched her without seeing her, because she was seeing 1984 instead, and the nine days of dragging the river, and Harlan Voss in his good coat offering five thousand dollars with his hat over his heart, and Emil Brandt's belt in a jail cell, and a whole town closing a creaky door with its shoulder.

And now, thirty-one years later—no. Forty. Forty years later, God help her, forty—they had dug Harlan up and found stones where a founder should be, and some stranger with a microphone was asking questions in a voice Mabel had heard through the library window yesterday, bright and careful and hungry, and Harlan's portrait had been moved half an inch by somebody's careless elbow, and none of it meant anything, probably. Probably it was coincidence stacked on coincidence, the way old newspapers stack.

But Mabel Dunn had spent her life teaching children the difference between a fact and a story, and she knew this much: facts don't scratch faces. People do. Somebody, sometime, had held this photograph in their hands and hated what was standing next to June Calloway so much they'd tried to erase her with a knife point—and then, instead of throwing the picture away, had dated the back of it and kept it. Kept it. Filed it, or lost it, or hidden it in a box that ended up in a church basement under a code only one living person could read.

Only one living person.

Mabel looked at the scratched-out face for a long time. Then she did something she had never once done in eleven years of archiving, something that violated every rule she had, including several she invented on the spot: she slipped the photograph out of the box, slid it into the inner pocket of her cardigan, buttoned the cardigan, and closed the gray box without writing an accession number.

"Some cases," she informed the silent portrait of Harlan Voss, who gazed mildly off toward the auxiliary shelves, "should stay shut."

But her hand, when she patted the pocket, stayed there a moment longer than a hand ought to, the way you keep a hand on a wound.

Outside, the fog was coming up Fogarty Street the way it always did at dusk, unhurried, thorough, erasing things one porch light at a time. And Mabel found herself thinking—not as an archivist, not as a librarian, but as a woman who had lived in Meridian Falls long enough to know how the town kept its secrets, tucked away neat and dusted weekly, half an inch off true—

She found herself wondering who else in this town had ever moved something, just slightly, and hoped nobody would notice.

The phone on the front desk rang. She jumped, pressed her hand flat to her cardigan pocket, and let it ring twice more before she answered.

"Mabel Dunn."

A pause. Then a voice she recognized from the radio, bright and careful: "Mrs. Dunn? This is Sebastian Eriksen. I'm the podcaster—I hear you're the one who knows where everything's buried in this town." A light laugh. "I was hoping you might have twenty minutes. I'm told you were here in '84."

Mabel looked down at the gray box, unnumbered. At the face-down plaque. At Harlan, hanging crooked again, watching the corners of the room.

"Twenty minutes," she said, and heard her own voice go carefully pleasant, the voice she'd used for forty years on parents asking why their child got a B. "Come by tomorrow morning, dear. Bring your questions."

She hung up, and stood a while in the lamplight with the photograph ticking softly against her heart like a second pulse, and wondered what on earth she was going to tell them—and what, God forgive her, she was going to leave out.
