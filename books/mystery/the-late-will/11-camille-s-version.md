## 11. Camille's Version

The invitation arrived on cream cardstock, hand-lettered in a slanted copperplate that had probably cost more per word than Sebastian's first car.

*Camille Voss requests the pleasure of your company for tea. Thursday, four o'clock. The Voss House.*

Sebastian read it twice, then held it up to the window light like a jeweler checking for flaws.

"Nobody sends an invitation to an ambush," they said to the empty kitchen of their rental. The rental agreed by dripping somewhere behind the walls. "You just get ambushed."

But that was exactly the point. Ambushes were information. A person who invited you to tea before destroying you wanted you to understand *why*, and a person who wanted you to understand why was a person who still cared what the record said. Camille Voss cared about the record. That told Sebastian more than the letter itself did.

They spent Wednesday evening preparing the way other people prepared for job interviews—except instead of rehearsing answers, Sebastian rehearsed questions, and instead of picking out a flattering shirt, they picked out one that said *I am harmless and slightly underdressed and you will underestimate me at your peril.* It was a good shirt. It had done excellent work in three states.

Thursday came down gray and soft, fog sitting on the town like a lid on a pot. The Voss House stood at the top of Founders' Hill, a Victorian gone granite-gray with age, all gables and turrets and windows dark as held breath. It had been built to look like wealth had always lived there, which was its own kind of lie—the kind Harlan Voss had apparently specialized in.

A housekeeper let Sebastian in without a word and led them through a hallway lined with portraits. Harlan Voss hung at the center of everything, of course, painted in his fifties, silver-haired and serene, wearing the expression of a man who has never once been surprised and intends to keep it that way. His eyes followed Sebastian down the hall. Painted eyes always did. But these seemed to do it with particular patience, like a man willing to wait forty years for a conversation.

"Mr. Eriksen." Camille rose from a wingback chair by the fire when Sebastian entered the drawing room. "Or is it Ms.? I confess I couldn't find a reliable answer."

"It's Sebastian," they said. "That's the reliable answer."

"Sebastian, then." She extended a hand cool and dry as paper. "Thank you for coming. Sit. The tea will be terrible—I make it myself, which is my one act of rebellion against a lifetime of staff—but the cups are good."

She was not what Sebastian had expected. In the two interviews he'd given since the exhumation, Whitaker Voss had performed grief the way a matinee actor performs it—beautifully, visibly, with pauses calibrated for effect. Alder had offered nothing at all, a man-shaped absence at the end of the table. But Camille was something else entirely: small, precise, seventy-some years old with her white hair cut short and practical, dressed in a charcoal sweater that probably cost four hundred dollars and looked like it cost forty. Her eyes were quick and unimpressed. She looked like a woman who had spent decades being underestimated by men and had long ago stopped bothering to correct them.

"I'll be honest," she said, pouring tea with the steady hand of a surgeon or a very experienced liar. "I asked you here because my brother is making a mess of things."

"Whitaker?"

"Whitaker thinks charm is a strategy. He's been charming since the womb. It worked on everyone but me." She handed over a cup. "He wants this story to go away quietly. Alder wants it to go away loudly, or possibly wants to go away himself, it's difficult to tell. And I want something different."

"What's that?"

"I want it to be *accurate*." She settled back into her chair and regarded Sebastian over her own cup. "You've been talking to people around town. I know you have. Mabel Dunn talks to everyone; she can't help herself. And I imagine what you're hearing is a story about poor June Calloway. Sweet girl. Estate worker. Disappeared into the fog, never seen again. Victim."

"The thought had crossed my mind."

"Mm." Camille set down her cup with a small click. "And how much of that story have you verified?"

Sebastian sipped the tea. She was right—it was terrible, oversteeped and bitter, and there was something almost endearing about the fact that she'd served it anyway. "Some of it. June Calloway worked at the estate in 1984. She vanished on June 12th. No body was ever found. No charges were ever filed. Emil Brandt confessed, recanted, and hanged himself the following year. Those are facts."

"Facts are lovely things," Camille said. "So pliable. You can arrange them into any shape you like." She leaned forward. "Let me tell you about the shape I watched being arranged, Mr.—Sebastian. I was thirty-one in 1984. I lived in this house. I saw June Calloway nearly every day for two years before she disappeared. And I am going to tell you something no one else in this town will say out loud, because they either don't know it or they preferred not to."

She reached into the basket beside her chair—a sewing basket, of all things—and drew out a manila envelope, softened at the corners by handling.

"My father did not kill June Calloway," she said. "June Calloway was killing him. Slowly. By degrees. For money."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a clock struck nothing in particular.

"She was blackmailing him," Camille said flatly. "For two years. And when she finally got what she wanted—or when he finally stopped paying, we may never know which—she left. On her own terms. Alive, I'd wager my share of the estate on it, and unlike my brothers I actually like my share of the estate."

Sebastian kept their face pleasant and their voice even. Inside, something shifted—not toppled, but tilted, the way furniture tilts when a house settles. "That's a serious accusation."

"It's a serious house," Camille said. "We keep serious things in it."

She held out the envelope. "Read it yourself. I'm not asking you to take my word. I've watched this family run on taking people's word for eighty years and look where it's gotten us—one empty coffin and a podcast."

Inside the envelope was a single sheet of paper, old, yellowed at the edges, typed on a machine that had pressed hard enough to emboss the letters. Sebastian read it twice.

*You know what I know. The payments stop, the knowing stops being private. You have until the 12th. — J.C.*

No date beyond that. No address. Just the initials, typed, and beneath them a postmark smudge that might have been Meridian Falls and might have been anywhere.

"When did your father receive this?" Sebastian asked.

"According to him, three weeks before she disappeared." Camille's voice didn't waver, but her hands, folded in her lap, had gone very still—the stillness of someone holding a door shut. "He showed it to me after. After she was gone and the sheriff was sniffing around and Emil Brandt had done whatever Emil Brandt did. He wanted me to understand why he wouldn't cooperate with the investigation. Why he paid Brandt's family's debts afterward. He said—" and here, for the first time, something moved behind her eyes, something old and tired, "—he said, 'She made her choice, Camille. People forget that women make choices too.'"

"And you believed him."

"I believed that he believed it." She lifted her chin. "Which is not the same thing, I grant you. My father was capable of believing anything that let him sleep. But I'll tell you what I know that isn't belief. I know that in the spring of '84, June Calloway wore new boots. Good ones. I know she had cash in an envelope in her room at the staff cottage—my father's housekeeper saw it and mentioned it to me, because the housekeeper mentioned everything to me, God rest her gossiping soul. And I know that my father, who counted pennies the way other men count sins, began making withdrawals that year that he never explained. Cash. Regular as rain."

The ledger. Sebastian kept their breathing slow. Every June 12th, 1985 until his death. If Camille was telling the truth—if the payments had started *before* the disappearance—then the ledger wasn't hush money for a crime. It was tribute. Or blackmail continuing past the grave, which raised a question so strange Sebastian set it aside carefully, like glassware.

"You kept this letter for forty years," Sebastian said. "Why show me now?"

"Because now there's a hole in the ground where my father should be, and half this town is writing fairy tales about why." Camille's composure cracked, just at the edge, just enough to show the woman underneath—seventy-eight years old, orphaned twice over, once by death and once by history. "Because if June Calloway is alive somewhere, then she took my father's money and my father's peace and forty years of this family's name, and I would like her to answer for at least one of those. And because—" she hesitated, and the hesitation was the most honest thing in the room, "—because I'm not sure anymore. I was sure for forty years. Now there are river stones in his coffin and strangers with microphones on my lawn, and I find that certainty doesn't survive contact with either."

She refilled Sebastian's cup without asking, an old hostess reflex.

"Ask your questions," she said. "You came to ask them. Ask."

So Sebastian asked. Where was Camille on June 12th, 1984? At a garden party in Corvallis, forty witnesses, thank you for asking. Did her father ever say what June knew? He said it was private, and private meant financial, and financial meant shameful, and shameful meant hers forever. Did anyone else see the letter? Whitaker, once; he'd laughed, which told Sebastian something about Whitaker. Alder? Alder was fourteen. Alder saw nothing and remembered everything, which was its own kind of curse.

"And the scratched-out photograph?" Sebastian asked, watching her face. "The gala photo, the nurse beside June?"

Something flickered there—genuine puzzlement, or a performance of it fine enough to fool a polygraph. "There were hundreds of photographs from those galas. Half the county passed through this house. Nurses, certainly—Father donated to St. Ambrose every year, liked the publicity of it. If someone scratched out a face, that's not my doing. This family scratches things *out of* records, Mr. Eriksen, not off of photographs. We prefer our erasures tidy."

It was a chilling sentence delivered like a weather report, and Sebastian wrote it down mentally in the file marked *things Camille says that are either confessions or jokes, possibly both.*

* * *

The walk back down Founders' Hill took twenty minutes and Sebastian spent all of them arguing with themselves.

The letter was either authentic or a very good forgery. If authentic, it rewrote everything: June Calloway as predator rather than prey, the ledger as ransom rather than guilt, the empty grave as a woman walking away under her own power. It fit. God help them, it fit well—the cash, the timing, the boots, the way no body had ever surfaced despite forty years of river dredging and amateur dowsing. Missing persons who stay missing usually stayed missing because they wanted to.

But.

But Emil Brandt had confessed. Confessed, specifically, to killing her—and then recanted, and then died. An innocent blackmailer doesn't produce a confession from a caretaker. Unless the confession was bought. Unless Brandt had taken the fall for pay, and the recantation was conscience, and the hanging was—

Sebastian stopped at the foot of the hill, fog curling around their ankles.

Unless none of it was what it looked like, from any angle, in any decade. Unless the whole town was a hall of mirrors where every version reflected another version and nobody, including the dead, had ever stood in front of the actual thing.

Their phone buzzed. Their producer, Dev, sending the weekly numbers with a note: *Ep 3 tracking huge. Also a legal firm in Portland called asking if you'd be interested in 'a conversation about defamation exposure.' I said no comment. Say no comment, Bas.*

Sebastian typed back: *Who retained them?*

Dev's reply came fast: *Wouldn't say. But the retainer's billed through a holding company called Meridian Holdings LLC. Ring any bells?*

It rang several. Sebastian stood there in the fog at the bottom of Founders' Hill, holding a copy of a letter that might be a bombshell or a plant, feeling the vertigo Camille had presumably designed—because of course she'd designed it. That was the trick of a good counter-narrative. It didn't need to convince you. It only needed to make the floor feel provisional.

Sympathy, Sebastian had learned, was a load-bearing wall in every investigation. Remove it and you found out fast whether anything else was holding the structure up.

They looked up the hill. The Voss House sat in the mist, windows dark, and high in one gable—third floor, the old nursery wing, according to Mabel—a light was on. Someone was up there watching the street below.

Watching Sebastian.

Sebastian raised a hand, slow, deliberate. The light went out.

They walked back to the rental the long way, past the pharmacy with its green awning and its window display of cough syrup and greeting cards, past a middle-aged woman in a cardigan sweeping the sidewalk with unhurried strokes. She nodded at Sebastian. Sebastian nodded back.

Neither of them smiled.

That night, Sebastian pinned the photocopied letter to the corkboard between the quarry map and the gala photo, and stared at the two small letters at its bottom—*J.C.*—until the letters stopped looking like initials and started looking like a door.

Someone knocked on it, forty years ago. Sebastian intended to find out who had answered.
