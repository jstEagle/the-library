## 15. Reyes File

Cora Reyes arrived at the rental at seven in the morning carrying a thermos, a paper bag of something that smelled gloriously of cinnamon, and a manila folder wrapped in a dish towel like it was a casserole going to a wake.

"Inside," she said, when Sebastian opened the door still half-asleep in yesterday's shirt. "Not out here. Not on the porch. Inside."

Sebastian stepped back and let her pass, noting the way she checked the street over both shoulders before crossing the threshold—a habit so ingrained it looked less like caution and more like breathing.

"You slept?" Cora asked, setting the folder on the kitchen table with the reverence other people reserved for sleeping infants.

"Badly. You?"

"I don't sleep much during cases." She unscrewed the thermos and poured two cups of coffee that smelled like it had been brewed by someone who took coffee personally. "My father used to say insomnia was just the mind doing its job after hours."

"He sounds like he'd have liked podcasts."

"He'd have hated them." She said it with the ghost of a smile. "He thought everything important should be written down. Ink and paper. Things you could hold." Her hand came to rest on the folder. "Which is why we're here."

The cinnamon turned out to be pan dulce from the bakery two doors off Main, and Cora watched with open satisfaction as Sebastian ate—feeding people, Sebastian was learning, was how the Reyes family said things they couldn't say out loud.

Then she unwrapped the towel, and the morning changed temperature.

"This is his case file," Cora said. "Not the official one. The official one lives in a records room downtown and says what everyone agreed to say. This is the one he kept at home, in the bottom of his gun safe, under the ammunition boxes, because nobody looks under ammunition boxes." She slid it across the table. "I found it three days after his funeral. I've had it for twenty-two years, and until this week I never understood why he hid it."

Sebastian wiped their hands on their jeans before touching it. Some instinct insisted on cleanliness.

The folder was soft at the corners, the cardboard gone furry with age. Inside, the first thing was a photograph: a man in his forties standing beside a rusted pickup, squinting into a sun that hadn't yet burned through Meridian Falls fog. He was lean, weathered, with hands that looked like tools themselves. Written across the bottom in pencil: *Emil Brandt, caretaker, Voss estate. April '85.*

"Emil Brandt," Sebastian said. The name rang faintly, a bell heard through walls. "That's not a name anyone's mentioned to me."

"No one would mention him. That's rather the point." Cora sat down across the table, folding her hands. "Emil Brandt worked for Harlan Voss for nineteen years. Kept the grounds, fixed the fences, drove the family where they needed driving. Quiet man. Lived alone up past the quarry road. And in March of 1985—" she tapped the photo, "—he confessed to the disappearance of June Calloway."

Sebastian set down their coffee. "Confessed."

"Walked into the sheriff's office on a Tuesday morning, according to my father's notes, and said, 'I did it. She's gone. I'm sorry.' Wouldn't say more than that. Wouldn't say where. Wouldn't say why. Just those words, over and over, like a man reciting something he'd been made to memorize."

"And your father believed him?"

Cora was quiet a moment. "My father wanted to believe him. Every investigator wants the confession, Sebastian. It's the finish line. You chase it for years and when someone hands it to you, the last thing you want to do is look at it too hard." She opened the folder fully now, spreading pages across the table with the care of someone laying out surgical instruments. "But he did look. Because there were things that didn't fit."

She laid them out one by one, narrating like a woman who had rehearsed this presentation for two decades with no audience.

"Item one. Brandt claimed he took June Calloway from the estate on the night of June twelfth, 1984. But the estate's own delivery logs—and my father got these from the dairy driver, who kept carbon copies because he didn't trust the Vosses—show Brandt was in town that evening, picking up feed. Three witnesses at the co-op saw him between six-thirty and eight. June Calloway was last seen at the estate at nine-forty."

"Alibi."

"A soft one. Witnesses can be wrong, memories can shift. But it bothered him. Item two." A second page, this one covered in small, cramped handwriting, the ink faded to brown. "Brandt's statement, taken down verbatim. Read it."

Sebastian read it. It was short. *I did it. She's gone. I'm sorry.* Then, further down, appended in different handwriting—Reyes senior's, presumably—a note: *Asked him four times how. Four times he said: 'You know how.' Asked what he meant. No answer. Subject then requested a lawyer, which he had never previously done or could apparently afford.*

"'You know how,'" Sebastian repeated. "Like he was quoting someone."

"Or being quoted at." Cora's jaw tightened. "Item three."

Item three was a single sheet of lined paper, torn from a legal pad, containing a typed retraction. *I wish to withdraw my statement of March 3rd. I did not take June Calloway. I said what I said because I was told to say it and I was afraid. I am still afraid. — E. Brandt.* Dated eleven days after the confession.

"He recanted," Sebastian said slowly. "And then?"

"And then nothing happened." Cora's voice went flat in a way Sebastian recognized—the flatness people develop around old wounds. "No follow-up interview logged. No investigation into who told him to say it. The retraction went into a drawer. My father pushed. His sheriff told him the case was closed, the town could rest, and if Deputy Reyes had concerns about the mental state of Mr. Brandt, perhaps he should take some leave. My father did not take leave. He started keeping this file at home instead."

* * *

The story unspooled across the table page by page, and Sebastian recorded none of it—not because they'd forgotten the recorder sitting in their pocket, but because Cora had asked, quietly, before knocking, that this first telling be just for them. Paper first. Microphones later, if it ever came to that. Sebastian had agreed without hesitation. There were stories you gathered like fruit, and there were stories you had to be handed, and the difference mattered.

"In January of 1986," Cora continued, "eleven months after the confession, Emil Brandt hanged himself in the toolshed behind his house. Left no note. The county ruled it suicide arising from guilt. Case closed forever. The town got its ending: the caretaker did it, the caretaker couldn't live with it, God rest everyone." She looked up. "Except my father never believed the ending. He spent the last nine years of his life collecting reasons not to believe it, and every reason is in this folder, and the last one—" her voice caught, just slightly, the first crack in all that composure, "—the last one is why I'm here."

She turned to the final section of the folder. It was thinner. A few loose pages, a newspaper clipping about the mine closure, and then a photocopied document that made Sebastian go very still.

It was a hospital admission slip. St. Ambrose Sanatorium, the private facility upriver that had closed its doors decades ago and been demolished before Sebastian was born. The form was standard issue for its era: patient name, admitting physician, date, nature of admission.

The name field read: **J. Calloway.**

The date field read: **September 14, 1986.**

Seven months after Emil Brandt died.

Sebastian stared at it for a long time. Outside, a truck ground past on the gravel road, and somewhere a dog announced itself to no one, and the ordinary world went on making its ordinary noises while the paper in Sebastian's hands quietly rearranged everything.

"This has to be a mistake," they said finally, and hated how hopeful they sounded. "A clerical error. Wrong year. Someone wrote an eight where there should have been an eight-five."

"That was my father's first theory too. He spent two years trying to prove it." Cora produced another page—a letter, typewritten on St. Ambrose letterhead, responding to an inquiry. "He wrote to the sanatorium in 1991 asking them to check their records for admissions errors. This is their reply."

Sebastian read it aloud, slowly, because some sentences need air put under them:

*"We have reviewed our admission registers for the period in question and confirm the following: J. Calloway, female, admitted September 14, 1986, under the care of Dr. E. Marsh, for observation following physical trauma. Patient discharged into private care May 1987. We are unable to provide further information without proper authorization. Please note that our records indicate only one admission under this name in the relevant decade."*

"Only one admission," Sebastian said. "In the relevant decade."

"My father cross-checked. There was no other Calloway in the county registry. No relative. No namesake. One J. Calloway, admitted alive and breathing seven months after the whole town had agreed she was dead in a hole somewhere, killed by a man who was himself already dead." Cora leaned back. "And here's the detail that kept him up nights for the rest of his life. Look at the nature-of-admission line."

Sebastian looked. The clerk's hand was loopy and old-fashioned, but the words were legible enough: *Observation following physical trauma. Patient non-communicative upon intake.*

"Non-communicative," Sebastian murmured. "Traumatized. Alive."

"Alive," Cora said, "and hurt, and silent—which, if you think about it, is exactly what a witness would be. Not a victim dragged into the dark by a caretaker. A witness. Someone who saw something so bad that powerful people preferred her erased, and who was hidden away instead of killed, because even Harlan Voss—" she paused, choosing the words like stepping stones, "—because somebody drew a line somewhere. Somebody decided she'd disappear but not die."

"The payments," Sebastian said. The ledger entry surfaced in their memory, bright as a match strike. "Every June twelfth. From 1985 until Voss died. I found the withdrawals in the county bank records. I thought they were hush money to an accomplice. Or guilt money to a family."

"Or upkeep," Cora said softly. "Forty years of upkeep. Room, board, a new name, a quiet life, in exchange for staying quiet yourself." She shook her head slowly. "My father had a phrase for what he thought happened. He wrote it on the inside cover of this folder. I want you to see it, because I've read it ten thousand times and I still don't entirely understand it, and maybe fresh eyes will."

She turned the folder over. On the inside of the cardboard cover, in the same cramped pencil that filled the margins throughout, was a single line:

*They didn't hide the crime. They hid the witness. Ask why.*

* * *

For a while neither of them spoke. Sebastian got up and refilled both coffees without asking, an act that felt suddenly ceremonial, and Cora accepted hers with a nod that acknowledged the ritual.

"Your father," Sebastian said at last. "What happened to him?"

"Heart attack. Nine years ago, in his chair, with this folder open on his lap." Cora's mouth twisted. "The irony is not lost on me. He spent twenty-five years chasing a woman the whole town said was dead, and the town outlasted him. When he died, I nearly put the folder in the fire. Genuinely considered it. Thought—it's a dead man's obsession, let it be dead with him." She looked down at the table, at the scattered pages, at the face of Emil Brandt squinting into the sun. "But I couldn't. Because of one thing he said to me, near the end. He said, 'Cora, mija, everybody in this town is protecting somebody. The question you have to keep asking is: who protects the person with no one?'"

"And June Calloway had no one."

"Not a soul. Orphan, foster system, came to work at the estate at seventeen. If she vanished clean, there'd be no one left to wonder. Except my father wondered anyway, and now—" Cora spread her hands, and Sebastian saw that they trembled slightly, and understood that beneath the deputy's steadiness was a daughter who had inherited a question the way other people inherit houses. "Now there's an empty coffin full of river stones, and a podcaster asking questions on the record, and a folder that says the dead girl may have been alive this entire time. Buying groceries. Walking past us. For forty years."

The thought landed in the room like cold water. Sebastian felt the hair rise on their arms. Somewhere out there, beyond the fogged window, past the pharmacy and the diner and the church with its crooked steeple, a woman in her late fifties or early sixties might be living a life built on top of a grave—her own grave—waking every morning inside a lie so complete she'd had forty years to grow comfortable in it. Or not comfortable. Never comfortable. Just practiced.

"If she's alive," Sebastian said carefully, "then whoever arranged her disappearance is either dead—Voss—or still keeping the arrangement running. And the statute expires in thirty days. Which means if someone wants the arrangement to end permanently—"

"They have thirty days to make sure the only living witness stays a dead one." Cora met their eyes. "Yes. I've done that math too. It's why I stopped sleeping in February."

Sebastian stood and walked to the window. The fog was burning off, the way it did most mid-mornings, peeling back from the hills in gray rags. Down the street, the town went about its business: the hardware store opening its shutters, kids cutting across the green toward school, the pharmacy on the corner with its striped awning and its neat window display. An ordinary town. A town that had been ordinary for so long, so expertly, that the ordinariness itself now looked like craftsmanship.

"Cora," they said. "The sanatorium. St. Ambrose. It's demolished—but the records. Discharge papers, transfer orders, anything about where a patient goes when she's 'discharged into private care.' Who paid? Where did she go? There'll be something."

"There will be. And I know exactly where the sanatorium's administrative files ended up when it closed." Cora began gathering the pages back into the folder, her movements brisk again, purpose restored. "County archive annexe. Box eighty-something, uncatalogued, because the county has the archival instincts of a raccoon. I pulled the box index last week."

"You pulled it last week?" Sebastian turned. "Before you came to me?"

Cora paused, folder half-closed, and for a moment she looked younger, and tired, and honest. "I've known since the coffin came up empty that I was going to show you this file. I just needed a few days to decide whether I was handing you a story or handing you my father." She closed the folder and pressed it flat with both palms, like sealing something. "I've decided it's the same thing."

Sebastian crossed the room and put out their hand, and Cora took it, and the grip held a beat longer than a handshake strictly required—an oath conducted in the medium of grip strength.

"One more thing," Cora said, shrugging back into her deputy's posture as she moved toward the door, voice dropping. "There's a name on the discharge paperwork I haven't been able to run down yet. The signature authorizing the private-care placement. Whoever signed it arranged where June Calloway spent the rest of her life—and given the timing, given the money trail, I have a guess. But guesses are how good deputies end up suspended, so I want the document first." She glanced back, and there was something almost apologetic in it. "Fair warning, though. If my guess is right, the person who signed for her isn't Harlan Voss."

Sebastian felt the floor tilt a degree. "Then who?"

Cora opened the door onto the brightening street, fog lifting, town waking, all its careful ordinariness on display.

"That's the thing," she said. "The signature's going to tell us who's been guarding her all these years. And whoever it is—they're still here. Still in town. Probably still watching her right now." She tapped the brim of an imaginary hat. "Box eighty-four. Tomorrow, dawn. Bring the recorder."

The door clicked shut behind her, and Sebastian stood alone in the kitchen with the folder, the cooling coffee, and the growing, electric certainty that somewhere within walking distance of this table, a dead woman was doing something utterly alive—folding laundry, ringing up purchases, watering a window box—and that tomorrow's paperwork would point straight at the person who had spent forty years making sure no one ever noticed.

They picked up the admission slip one more time and read the date again, letting it burn all the way through them.

September 14, 1986. Seven months after a hanging. Alive.

"Hang on, June," Sebastian said quietly to the empty kitchen. "Whoever you are now. Hang on. We're coming."
