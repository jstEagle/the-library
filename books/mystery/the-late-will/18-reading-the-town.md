## 18. Reading the Town

The enhanced photograph sat in the center of Sebastian's kitchen table like a guest nobody had invited.

They'd spent two nights on it—layering filters over the scratched face until the gouges in the emulsion softened and something underneath began to breathe. It wasn't restoration, exactly. It was more like listening to someone through a wall. You couldn't make out the words, but you could catch the shape of a voice.

The woman in the photo stood a half-step behind June Calloway at the 1983 Voss Founders' Gala, her hands folded, her chin lifted with the particular patience of someone whose job required standing at parties she hadn't been invited to. The nurse's uniform was unmistakable now that Sebastian knew to look for it: the high collar, the small silver pin at the throat. St. Ambrose Sanatorium, according to the county historical society's one surviving brochure—a facility that had closed in 1989 and been demolished so thoroughly that even its foundations were now a strip mall parking lot.

Paper trails into ash. That was the phrase Sebastian kept using, mostly because it was true and partly because saying it out loud made the ash feel less like a dead end and more like a dare.

If the records were gone, then the records lived somewhere else. Records always did. They lived in the heads of people who'd been in Meridian Falls long enough to remember when the sanatorium bus ran on Tuesdays, who remembered which families sent their difficult relatives up the hill, who remembered a girl named June Calloway not as a case number but as a person who once won a pie contest or dated somebody's brother or left a tip too big at a diner counter.

So Sebastian printed eleven copies of the photo at the library—the librarian, a young man named Dev with an encyclopedic knowledge of local gossip and no apparent interest in concealing it, asked twice if they wanted them laminated—and set out to do the oldest work in journalism. Not searching. Asking.

* * *

The first rule of canvassing a small town, Sebastian had learned years ago covering a missing fisherman in coastal Maine, was that you never start with the people you most want to talk to. You start with the edges and work inward, the way you'd clean a window. Start in the center and all you get is smears.

The edges of Meridian Falls were the church basement, the hardware store, the barber shop with the pole that hadn't spun since 2011, and the Legion hall where Thursday nights meant bingo and Friday mornings meant every retired man in town holding court over coffee that had been brewed sometime during the previous administration.

Sebastian started at the Legion hall.

The room smelled of burnt coffee and floor wax and the faint ghost of forty years of cigarette smoke that no amount of paint could fully evict. Eleven men sat at folding tables arranged in a loose horseshoe, and conversation didn't stop when Sebastian walked in—it paused, the way a river pauses around a new rock, and resumed on the other side of them.

"Podcast fella," said a man in a cardigan the color of oatmeal. "Or podcast lady. Or—" He squinted. "Whichever."

"Both," Sebastian said. "Neither. I'm the one with the microphone."

That got a laugh, which Sebastian counted as a door opening. They introduced themselves around the table—Earl, Gus, Walt, another Earl ("we call him Early Earl"), a silent man everyone called Doc despite there being no evidence he'd ever practiced medicine—and Sebastian bought a round of coffees they didn't need and slid the first copy of the photograph onto the table between the creamer and a plate of donuts that had achieved a texture best described as architectural.

"You folks have been here a while," Sebastian said. "I'm hoping you can help me with something."

"We've been here forever," said Gus. "Some of us against our will."

The laughter again. Sebastian waited it out, then tapped the photo. "This is from a Voss gala in 1983. I'm trying to identify this woman."

The table bent toward the picture. There was a quality to the silence that followed that Sebastian had learned to read very carefully: not blankness, but calculation. Eleven men deciding, individually and simultaneously, how much they knew and how much of it they were willing to say.

"That's a Voss party," said Walt finally. "Big tent out on the lawn. I catered the beer."

"It is," Sebastian agreed. "And this woman here, behind June Calloway—"

"I knew June," said Early Earl, unexpectedly. His voice had changed; the joke was gone out of it. "She used to babysit for my sister's kids. Sweet girl. Quiet. The kind of quiet that makes you nervous later, when you think back on it." He looked at the photo a long moment. "Never seen the other one."

"You're sure?"

"Sure as I'm sure of anything at my age, which is nothing." But his eyes flicked sideways—to Doc, of all people—and Doc looked down into his coffee with the studied intensity of a man reading tea leaves at the bottom of a cup he'd already drained.

Sebastian let it sit. One of the first lessons of this trade: the answer isn't always the thing people say. Sometimes it's the thing they pass to each other without words, like a note under a desk.

"Anyone else?" Sebastian asked the table generally.

Gus shrugged. Earl shook his head. Cardigan Man said, "Nurses all look alike in them uniforms. Could be anybody. Could be my second wife, God rest her, if she'd ever worked a day."

More laughter. But Doc still hadn't looked up, and Early Earl was watching Doc look down, and Sebastian filed all of it away in the growing cabinet labeled THINGS THAT AREN'T NOTHING.

On the way out, Sebastian stopped by Doc's chair. "You okay? You got quiet on us."

Doc raised his eyes. They were pale blue and startlingly direct. "I'm eighty-six," he said. "I've been quiet longer than you've been alive. It's not a symptom." A pause. Then, quieter: "Come back sometime when the choir isn't watching."

* * *

The hardware store yielded nothing except a free key copy and a lecture on the proper winterizing of gutters, delivered by a proprietor named Sal who had opinions the length of the building. The barber shop yielded less than nothing—the barber, mid-shave on a customer who appeared to be asleep, waved Sebastian off with a straight razor and an expression suggesting that questions about 1983 were bad for business.

But the church basement was different.

Sebastian found it by following the smell of cinnamon rolls, and inside discovered the Tuesday quilting circle: six women ranging from roughly seventy to roughly geological time, working on a quilt whose pattern Sebastian genuinely could not identify and suspected might not have one. Their leader was a tiny woman named Mrs. Okafor, ninety-one years old, hands like knotted rope, eyes like a hawk that had seen everything and forgiven maybe half of it.

"A podcast," she repeated, when Sebastian explained. "People listen to strangers talk about dead things. And they pay for this."

"They do."

"The world has gotten strange." She snapped a thread with her teeth. "Sit down, then. You'll have a roll. Don't argue."

Sebastian sat. Sebastian ate the roll. It was, objectively, the best thing that had happened to them in weeks, and they said so, and Mrs. Okafor accepted the compliment as no more than her due.

The photograph went around the table the way the rolls had gone around, each woman holding it a moment longer than necessary. And Sebastian watched—not the photo, the women. Because here was the thing about small towns that outsiders always got wrong: it wasn't that everybody knew everything. It was that everybody knew exactly what they knew, and had spent decades deciding what to do with it.

"This is the Voss gala," said a woman named Dot. "I remember the year. My husband won the raffle and never let anyone forget it."

"And the woman behind June Calloway?" Sebastian prompted gently.

Dot squinted. Turned the photo sideways, as though the answer might be hiding at a different angle. "Sanatorium girl?" she said at last, half to herself. "They used to send a couple of nurses down to the big parties. Standing duty, Mr. Voss called it. In case anybody took ill." She snorted. "At a garden party. Like anybody was going to take ill between the lemonade and the string quartet."

"But you remember nurses attending."

"I remember the uniforms." Dot handed the photo back with a small shudder Sebastian chose not to interpret. "White, with those little pins. St. Ambrose. My aunt was up there two years, God rest her, before they closed it. Sad place. Beautiful grounds, though. You could see the whole valley from the porch."

"Did your aunt ever mention the staff? Names?"

"Oh, honey, my aunt thought the orderlies were communists and the head doctor was her secret fiancé. She mentioned lots of things." Dot patted Sebastian's hand. "None of them useful."

Mrs. Okafor had been silent through all of this, her needle moving in small, precise strokes. Now she spoke without looking up.

"You're asking about the wrong woman," she said.

The room went still. Even the needles paused.

"Ma'am?"

"You're asking who she was." The old woman's needle resumed. "In this town, that question only matters if you also ask who she became. People here don't disappear, young person. They just stop being looked at." She threaded a new color—deep green—and tied it off. "Look at faces. Not photographs. Faces change. Habits don't. The way a person stands. The way they fold their hands." She nodded at the photo, at the woman with the folded hands and lifted chin. "That's a person waiting. Find who's still waiting."

Sebastian walked out into the gray afternoon with cinnamon sugar on their fingers and the distinct sensation of having been given a riddle instead of an answer—which, from a ninety-one-year-old quilter, was probably the most honest help available.

* * *

By Thursday, Sebastian had shown the photograph to nineteen people and collected everything and nothing: two shrugs, four outright refusals, one man who tried to sell them a boat, and a retired schoolteacher named Miss Priddy who wept briefly at the sight of June Calloway's face and then apologized for crying, as though grief were a social error.

It was Cora Reyes, of all people, who pointed them at the diner.

They'd been meeting in the corner booth at the Bluebird—neutral ground, good coffee, a waitress named Tammy who had mastered the art of hearing everything and repeating nothing—and Cora stirred her coffee and said, offhand, like she was mentioning the weather:

"You been to the Wagon Wheel yet?"

"The diner on Route 9? No. Why?"

"Hollis has cooked there since the Nixon administration. Before that he washed dishes at the Legion. Before that—" she shrugged, "—he was everywhere. Hollis was the kind of kid who got hired for everything because he never repeated what he heard. Forty years of secrets, and the man talks about nothing but gravy." She smiled into her cup. "Best place in town to ask a question, if you ask it right."

"How do you ask it right?"

"Buy the meatloaf special. Compliment the gravy. And don't bring a recorder." Her eyes came up, sharp under the deputy's flat expression. "Hollis can smell a recorder. Everybody can. That's why I'm telling you and not your microphone."

* * *

The Wagon Wheel at noon on Friday was a study in controlled chaos: farmers, school buses' worth of field-tripping children, two truckers, and a line out the door that Hollis commanded from behind the counter like a general who had long ago stopped caring whether the war was winnable. He was seventy-something and built like a fire hydrant, forearms that suggested decades of lifting stockpots, and a hairnet he wore with the defiance of a man daring the health inspector to comment.

Sebastian ordered the meatloaf special. Complimented the gravy. Meant it—the gravy was, frankly, unreasonable—and watched Hollis absorb the compliment the way stone absorbs rain.

"New face," Hollis said, sliding the plate across. "Well. Semi-new. You're the podcast."

"I'm the podcast."

"Heard your show." Hollis wiped the counter with a cloth that had seen service. "Talk too fast. But you get things right, mostly. That's rare."

"Thank you. Actually, I wanted to ask you—"

"Hold that thought." Hollis pointed a spatula at the register. "Lunch rush. Eat. Come back at three, when it's just the old boys playing dominoes and nobody cares what anybody says." He leaned in slightly, voice dropping beneath the clatter. "And leave the phone in the car."

* * *

Three o'clock found the Wagon Wheel transformed: lunch crowd gone, lights half-dimmed, four elderly men at a corner table engaged in a dominoes game of such glacial pace it might have been a form of meditation. Hollis poured Sebastian coffee unasked and sat down across from them in the booth, which felt, somehow, like being granted an audience.

Sebastian left the phone in the car. It nearly killed them.

"So," Hollis said. "Ask."

Sebastian laid the photograph on the table between them, face up, and turned it gently toward him. "This is from the 1983 Voss gala. I'm trying to name this woman."

Hollis picked it up. Held it at arm's length, then close, then arm's length again, the universal choreography of the aging eye. And Sebastian watched his face with everything they had—watched for the flicker, the flinch, the tell.

What they saw instead was stranger. Recognition arrived slowly, like dawn, and when it finished arriving, Hollis's expression held no fear and no guilt. It held something almost like fondness, braided through with a bewilderment so profound it bordered on the comic.

"Well, I'll be," he said softly. "That's Ruthie."

The word landed in Sebastian's chest like a dropped glass.

"Ruthie," Sebastian repeated, keeping their voice level through sheer professional will. "Ruthie who?"

"Ruthie from the pharmacy." Hollis set the photo down and tapped it twice, certain now. "Ruth Callahan. Works the counter at Miller's Drug, right on Main. Been there forever—since before I took this place, and I took it in '86." He shook his head, marveling. "Though I'll tell you what's funny. This picture's from '83, and she looks—well, she looks like her, but younger, obviously, we were all younger—but the same. Same way she stands. Same little lift of the chin, like she's waiting for somebody to give her the next thing to do." He chuckled. "Thirty-seven years behind that counter and she hasn't aged a day past stubborn."

The dominoes clicked softly in the corner. The coffee machine hissed. Somewhere in the kitchen, a timer went off and nobody moved to answer it.

Sebastian's heart was doing something complicated and arrhythmic. Because this was the moment—the moment every investigator lives for and dreads in equal measure—when the shapeless thing you've been chasing suddenly puts on a name and a street address and starts existing in ordinary daylight. When hidden-in-plain-sight stops being a figure of speech and becomes a woman who fills prescriptions six blocks from where you sleep.

"Ruth Callahan," Sebastian said carefully. "How long has she been in town?"

"Forever. Longer than forever." Hollis frowned, casting backward. "Must've come around... late eighties? Right after the sanatorium shut down, I think. Came in for coffee one morning, asked if Miller's was hiring. Been there ever since." He shrugged. "Quiet woman. Polite. Remembers everybody's birthday, which in a town like this counts for more than a medical degree. Never married. Never leaves. Goes up to the cemetery regular as clockwork—I see her walk past with flowers sometimes, always the same route." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes, some small domestic mystery he'd carried for years without examining. "Always the same date, too, now I think on it. Every June. Used to wonder about that."

Every June. June twelfth. The ledger withdrawals. The lilies on the empty grave.

"Hollis," Sebastian said, and their voice came out lower than intended. "Does anyone else know about this photo? Have I shown you something nobody else would recognize?"

Hollis looked at them for a long moment. Then he glanced at the dominoes table, at the four men absorbed in their glacial game, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"Son," he said—then caught himself, and corrected, with a small apologetic tilt of his head, "Person. I've been cooking in this town for fifty years. I know what I know and I keep most of it in the gravy, if you follow me." He pushed the photograph back across the table, face down, deliberate as a card player. "But I'll tell you one thing for free, since you liked the gravy. Ruthie Callahan has filled more prescriptions for sleeping pills and nerve medicine for half the founding families of this town than any doctor in the county. She knows what everybody takes. She knows what everybody's afraid of. And in thirty-seven years, I have never—not once—heard that woman repeat a single thing told to her across that counter."

He stood, gathered the plate, and paused.

"If you go asking her questions," Hollis said, "you'd best be ready for hers."

* * *

Outside, the fog was coming in off the river early, the way it did in Meridian Falls, softening the streetlights into halos and turning Main Street into something out of an older century. Sebastian stood on the sidewalk with the photograph in their coat pocket and their breath hanging in the cold air, and let themselves feel it—that uncanny electric thrill, equal parts triumph and vertigo, of a hunt narrowing to a point.

Ruthie. Ruth Callahan. The pharmacy on Main.

Forty years. Walking the same route past the same windows, filling the same prescriptions, remembering everybody's birthday. Waiting, Mrs. Okafor had said. Find who's still waiting.

Sebastian took out their phone, hesitated over it—Cora should know first, Cora deserved to know first—and then didn't call anyone at all. Some steps you take alone.

Across the street, two blocks down, the green cross of Miller's Drug glowed softly in the fog. Through the window, behind the counter, a woman with silver hair and an unhurried posture was tidying shelves, her hands folding and unfolding with the patient precision of someone who had done this ten thousand times and expected to do it ten thousand more.

Sebastian started walking.

The bell above the pharmacy door chimed at 6:47 on a Friday evening, and Ruth Callahan looked up from her shelf of cough syrup, and for one suspended second—one heartbeat in which forty years balanced on a knife's edge—her calm eyes met Sebastian's and did not blink.

"Help you?" she said.

Sebastian reached into their coat pocket, and found the photograph, and found that their hand was shaking.
