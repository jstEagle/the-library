## 20. Ruth's POV

Ruth Callahan had been counting things for forty years.

Pills counted into amber bottles. Change counted back across the pharmacy counter. The days between June twelfths, which she no longer counted but which arrived anyway, punctual as taxes. She had learned early that a life could be measured in small, repeatable numbers, and that the smaller the number, the safer the day.

Tuesday's count: eleven customers before ten o'clock, one dropped carton of antacids, and a tourist who asked her where to get "the good fog pictures." She had directed him to the overlook and thought, not for the first time, that if she ever wrote her memoirs it would be titled *Directions I Have Given*, and it would be very short, because she had spent four decades pointing people away from everything that mattered.

"Ruthie," said Dot Pringle from the greeting-card rack, "you're out of the lavender sympathy cards again."

"I'll order more."

"You say that every week." Dot held up a card featuring a cartoon cloud weeping onto a smiling sun, which was either deeply comforting or deeply disturbing depending on your grief. "You know what your problem is? You never let anyone help you."

*If only you knew,* Ruth thought, and rang up a box of birthday candles for a woman whose grandson was turning nine, and smiled the smile she had practiced in a motel mirror in Spokane in 1985 until it stopped feeling like a mask and started feeling like a face.

That was the trick nobody warned you about. Everyone assumed the hard part of disappearing was the running. It wasn't. The hard part was the standing still. Anyone could run. Running was adrenaline and bus stations and a new name scrawled on a rental application with a hand that shook. Standing still — being *Ruthie* at the pharmacy counter for forty years, remembering birthdays, asking after hip surgeries, laughing at Dot Pringle's jokes about the mayor's hairpiece — that took a discipline most people couldn't have sustained for a week.

She had gotten good at it. That was the thing that frightened her now, in the quiet of the stockroom, among the boxes of bandages and the calendar from the pharmaceutical rep: she had gotten so good at being Ruth Callahan that some mornings she had to check.

She'd stand at the sink washing her hands and look at them — Ruth's hands, soft, age-spotted, smelling faintly of the lemon soap she'd used for decades — and she would think: *whose hands are these?* And then, a beat later, always a beat later: *mine. They're mine. They're both mine.* Because that was the truth nobody put in novels. June hadn't died when Ruth was born. June had just gone somewhere deep inside and sat down, like a woman in a waiting room, and stayed there, patient, all these years, reading old magazines.

Lately, though, June had been getting restless.

* * *

It started, Ruth supposed, with the machines digging up Harlan Voss.

She had heard them from the pharmacy, actually heard them, the diesel growl carrying down Meridian Street the way sound carried in a town built in a bowl. Her hands had gone still over the register drawer. Eleven customers had come and gone since then and she could not have told you a single one of their names, which had not happened since 1986, the year Emil Brandt hanged himself in the county jail and she understood, finally and completely, what kind of town she lived in and what kind of man had saved her.

Harlan Voss. Dead six years, and still reaching out of his grave to rearrange her life. Except his grave, it turned out, was empty, and wasn't that just like him. Even his coffin was a locked door.

She had known, of course. She had helped carry the stones herself — no, not carried; she had *chosen* them, that was the strange detail that surfaced at three in the morning sometimes, that she had stood at the quarry in the rain in her dead sister's coat while Harlan held the lantern, and she had picked the stones herself, smooth gray ones from the river shelf, because if her body was going to be represented by objects she wanted them to be honest ones. River stones. Things the water had made gentle. Not quarry rock, cut and squared by men.

Someone had put quarry rock in her coffin instead. She'd read that in the paper — the *Meridian Falls Courier* ran the story under a headline that managed to be both breathless and prim, EXHUMATION REVEALS NO REMAINS, and beneath it a photograph of the open box that the editor had mercifully printed small. Quarry stone. Cut, squared, recent. Someone had opened her grave within the last decade and refilled it, and she did not know who, and not knowing had rewired something in her chest that had been steady for forty years.

There were only a handful of people alive who knew what belonged in that coffin. She was one. Harlan was dead. Which left the others, and *the others* was a list she kept in her head the way other women kept recipes.

Dot appeared at the stockroom door. "Ruthie. There's a young person at the counter asking for you specifically."

"Which young person?"

"The podcaster one." Dot lowered her voice with the delicacy of a woman announcing a pregnancy. "The one on the radio about the empty coffin. They've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look people get when they're going to keep asking until someone answers." Dot patted her arm. "I told them you'd be right out. You don't have to be, you know. I can tell them you're at lunch. I can tell them you're dead. Honestly, at our age nobody checks."

For one absurd moment Ruth wanted to laugh until she cried. Instead she straightened her badge — RUTH CALLAHAN, MERIDIAN PHARMACY, a name she had worn longer than she had worn her own — and went out to meet the future.

* * *

The podcaster was younger than their voice suggested. They always were. Sebastian Eriksen stood at the counter with a notebook already open, wearing the particular expression of a person trying very hard to seem casual, which in Ruth's long experience was the expression of someone who had already decided how this conversation would go and was merely waiting for reality to catch up.

"Ms. Callahan? Thanks for seeing me. I know you're working."

"I'm always working. This town keeps an impressive supply of poison ivy." She set her hands flat on the counter, a posture she had perfected: open, unhurried, faintly amused. A woman with nothing behind her eyes but refills.

"Just a few questions. About the Voss estate. I understand you grew up here?"

"Grew up in Fairhaven, actually. Moved here in '86. Took the job at St. Ambrose before it closed, then the pharmacy." Every word true. That was the architecture of a good cover story — she had learned it from Harlan himself, who had told her once, in the car on the night that split her life in two, *"Never lie, June. Liars need memory. Just tell the truth sideways."*

"So you weren't here in 1984. When June Calloway disappeared."

And there it was. Sideways, he thought he was approaching. Straight on, more like.

"No," Ruth said. "I'm sorry. I know everyone hopes the pharmacist will remember something useful. We mostly remember who stopped buying laxatives in March."

Eriksen blinked. Something flickered across their face — not suspicion, not yet, but the thing that came before suspicion, the hunter's ear twitching at a sound too low to name. "You knew her, though. June Calloway. People say she worked at the estate."

"People say a lot of things about 1984. It's become a hobby in this town." She slid a white paper bag across the counter toward a customer waiting for a prescription, signed the slip without looking, and returned her attention to Eriksen with the mild patience of a woman who had all day. "I didn't know her. I knew *of* her. Pretty girl. Estate family treated her well, or that was the story."

"And you don't have an opinion about what happened to her?"

"I have opinions about everything. Ask Dot." The customer paid and left, the little bell over the door chiming, and in the space the bell opened Ruth looked directly at Sebastian Eriksen for perhaps two seconds longer than politeness required. *You have no idea,* she thought. *You are standing in front of the answer and asking me for directions to it.* "What I think is that a woman has been missing for forty years and this town has spent those forty years practicing its sad faces. If you want my opinion, that's it. Free of charge."

Eriksen wrote something down. Then they did the thing — the thing she would think about later, turning it over at night like a stone of her own — they reached into their jacket, produced a card, and laid it on the counter between them, square and plain, the way you'd lay down a winning hand you weren't sure you wanted to win with.

"If you ever think of anything," Eriksen said. "Anything at all. Day or night."

Ruth looked at the card. Black ink on cream stock. A phone number, an email address, and beneath them, in small letters, a title: *THE LATE WILL — an investigation.*

Something in her chest, the thing that had been sitting patiently in the waiting room for forty years, stood up.

"I'll keep it," she said, and put the card in her apron pocket, next to the scissors and the roll of antacid tablets she chewed like candy, and smiled Ruth Callahan's smile, and watched Sebastian Eriksen leave, the bell chiming behind them like a small, persistent alarm.

* * *

That night she walked.

She always walked on bad nights — the town was safe enough at midnight, safer than most, because everyone in it was asleep or pretending to be — and tonight her feet chose the route they always chose when her mind refused to choose anything: up Meridian Street, past the shuttered diner, along the fence line of the old Voss estate, where the grounds had gone wild and the iron gate had rusted into a sculpture of itself.

The cemetery lay beyond, on the rise. She didn't go in. She never went in. But she stood at the fence the way she had stood there every June twelfth for thirty-nine years, watching the dark shape of the Voss monument against the sky, and she talked to Harlan Voss in her head, because there was no one else living who could hear the whole story and stay calm.

*They dug you up, you old bastard,* she told him. *And you weren't home.*

The wind moved through the firs. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Forty years ago she had been a girl of twenty-two with a nurse's training and a temper, working private duty at St. Ambrose, and she had seen a thing in the mines' paperwork — no, that wasn't honest even in her own head; she had seen a *man*, carried out of the east shaft on a stretcher with coal dust in his lungs and frost in his fingers, a man who had been sent down into a shaft the inspection reports had condemned, and she had seen the reports themselves afterward, in the sanatorium office, in Harlan's brother's own hand, dated months before. And she had made the mistake of saying so. Out loud. To people.

Within a week, Emil Brandt had confessed to murdering her. Within two, she had understood that the confession was a door closing on her behalf, and that whoever had arranged it expected her to walk through it, and that if she did not walk through it willingly she would be walked through it otherwise. And then Harlan Voss — Harlan, whose family name was on the mine, whose brother was the dead man, who had come to her room at St. Ambrose at two in the morning looking like a man who had aged a decade since supper — had said the sentence that had governed her whole life since:

*"They want you gone so the town stays standing. I want you gone so you stay breathing. Same direction, different reasons. Will you let me help you disappear?"*

He had never asked her to lie. He had paid for her silence, yes — she'd found the ledger entries years later, or rather Cora Reyes's father had, God rest that stubborn man — but what Harlan had actually bought, year after year on the same date, was not her mouth. It was her safety. The payments went to the people who might otherwise ask questions. He had spent his fortune annually, like insurance premiums, on the continued existence of a woman the town believed was in his coffin.

And now the coffin was open, and the premiums had stopped six years ago, and the list of people who knew was down to her and whoever had put quarry stones where river stones should be.

Her feet had carried her, without permission, to the corner of Ash and Third. The pharmacy's back window glowed faintly. Above it, the apartment she'd rented for thirty-one years. On the counter inside, in her apron, a cream-colored card with a stranger's name on it.

She should burn it. That was procedure. Forty years of procedure. Cards got burned, notes got burned, the one photograph of herself from before had been burned in a barrel behind a motel in Boise while she'd watched her own face curl and go black and felt nothing, or told herself she felt nothing.

Instead Ruth Callahan climbed the stairs, sat down at her kitchen table under the single lamp, and took the card out of her apron and set it beside her tea.

*Sebastian Eriksen.* She turned it over. Blank on the back. Of course. Even the card was careful.

June stirred in her waiting room, restless, forty years of magazines finished. And Ruth realized, with the calm of a woman who has finally run out of a medication she's taken her whole life, that she was tired. Not weary-tired. Finished-tired. The tiredness of a performance that has run so long the actress can no longer remember whether there was ever anything underneath it — except that there was. There was June. There had always been June. And June had watched, through Ruth's eyes, as a young person with a recorder asked her, politely, *do you have an opinion about what happened to her?*

"Yes," Ruth said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the card, to the girl in the waiting room. "As a matter of fact, I do."

Outside, far off toward the cemetery hill, a car engine started in the dark, idled a moment, and drove slowly away.

Ruth sat very still and listened to it go, and her hand, without consulting her, closed around the card.
