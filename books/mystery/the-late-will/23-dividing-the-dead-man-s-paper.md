## 23. Dividing the Dead Man's Paper

Sebastian had a theory about rich families, refined over years of covering their funerals and their frauds: they were like antique clocks. Beautiful on the surface, full of interlocking parts, and utterly dependent on no one ever looking inside. The moment you opened the case, every gear began blaming every other gear.

The ledger gave them three gears to blame.

They spent the morning preparing, which in Sebastian's case meant sitting at the kitchen table of the rental with three manila folders, a pot of coffee going cold, and the particular pleasure of a person about to do something slightly unethical for entirely ethical reasons. Cora Reyes sat across from them, arms crossed, radiating the disapproval of a woman who had sworn an oath.

"Let me make sure I understand what you're doing," she said.

"I'm having three separate conversations."

"You're leaking."

"I'm *sharing*. Selectively. With people who have a right to know what was in their father's papers." Sebastian tapped the first folder. "Whitaker gets told there are financial records that implicate the family. Camille gets told there's correspondence that contradicts her version of June Calloway. Alder gets—" They paused. "Alder gets nothing yet. Alder gets silence. Silence is the thing that works on Alder."

"And this is legal?"

"It's not illegal." Sebastian smiled the smile they'd been told, by more than one lawyer and one ex-partner, looked like a cat watching a birdfeeder. "That's the standard we're working to this week."

"Wonderful," said Cora. "I'll be in the car."

* * *

Whitaker Voss received them in his office at Voss Development, a room designed to communicate wealth through restraint—restrained walnut, restrained leather, a restrained view of the river that had made all the restraint possible. He greeted Sebastian with the warmth of a man who had decided, sometime overnight, that charm was still his best available option.

"Sebastian Eriksen. The podcast voice itself. Can I get you coffee? Water? I have some very good sparkling water."

"I'm fine. I'll be brief, because I know your time is valuable, and mine is measured in days now." Sebastian set a single sheet of paper on his desk, face down. "Twenty-nine annual cash withdrawals. Every June twelfth, from 1985 until your father's death. Substantial amounts. Cash. No corresponding entries anywhere else in the estate accounts."

Whitaker's hand moved toward the paper and stopped halfway, as if the desk had become hot. "Where did you get that?"

"County records are public, Mr. Voss. That's the tragedy of them—they're just so rarely read." Sebastian let the pause stretch until it creaked. "Here's my question, and I'm asking it as someone who would genuinely prefer your answer to be a good one. Do you know who those payments went to?"

The transformation was fascinating to watch, if you had the stomach for it. Whitaker's face went through surprise, then calculation, then something rawer underneath—the look of a man discovering that the foundation under his house is not stone but packed sand. He laughed, a beat too late.

"My father did a lot of things with money. Charity, mostly. You've seen the hospital wing."

"The hospital wing has plaques, Mr. Voss. This has nothing. Twenty-nine payments into silence." Sebastian stood, leaving the paper where it lay. "You can turn it over when you're ready. But I'd think about who else knows what's in those records—and whether you want to be the last Voss standing next to them when it comes out."

In the hallway, past the receptionist who was absolutely listening, Cora fell into step beside them. "He turned the paper over before we reached the elevator," she said. "I watched him in the window reflection."

"Of course he did." Sebastian pressed the elevator button. "Whitaker doesn't panic outwardly. He panics *inwardly*, at high speed, and then starts moving pieces. Watch. By Friday he'll have called a family meeting, or a lawyer, or both."

It took him until Thursday morning, and he skipped the family meeting entirely. Sebastian learned about it from Mabel Dunn, who called at seven a.m. with the breathless authority of a woman who considered the historical society's front window a news bureau.

"Whitaker Voss came to the archive yesterday evening," she reported. "After hours. Asked to see everything we hold from 1984 through '86. Everything, Sebastian. He brought boxes. He took home four crates of material and signed a receipt like he was renting a car." A pause, freighted. "He also asked me, very casually, whether anyone else had been researching the Calloway file lately. Very casually. The way a man asks a question when he's already afraid of the answer."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I don't discuss patrons' research with other patrons." Mabel's voice acquired a certain dry satisfaction. "Which is technically true. I discuss it with podcasters, who aren't patrons. They haven't paid their dues."

* * *

Camille Voss did not panic. Camille *fortified*.

The summons arrived as a cream-colored card, hand-lettered, inviting Sebastian to tea on Saturday afternoon—an invitation with the unmistakable texture of a subpoena wearing perfume. Cora declined to attend ("If she serves me papers I have to arrest somebody, and it won't be her"), so Sebastian went alone, up the long drive to Camille's house, which was the Voss estate's original farmhouse renovated into something that pretended it had always been elegant rather than merely old money with good lighting.

Camille received them on a sunporch full of ferns, poured tea herself with hands that did not tremble, and introduced her attorney before she introduced the topic.

"This is Margaret Osei. She'll be present."

"Delighted," said Sebastian, meaning it. A lawyer's presence was a confession with better posture. "Margaret Osei—you handled the Brandt estate probate in '86, didn't you? I've read the filings. Very tidy work."

Osei's expression suggested she was updating her invoice already. Camille's teacup paused, fractionally, above its saucer.

"My mother retained Margaret after Emil Brandt died," Camille said smoothly. "Estate matters. Unrelated."

"Of course." Sebastian accepted a scone they had no intention of eating. Scones at hostile teas were like flowers at funerals—ceremonial, and best left untouched. "Mrs. Voss, I'll come to the point, because I suspect Margaret bills by the point. In your father's private papers—which your brother Alder and I have been reviewing together—there's correspondence from 1984 and 1985. Letters. Some of them appear to relate to June Calloway."

The word *together* landed exactly where Sebastian aimed it. Camille's spine straightened by a degree measurable only to professionals.

"Alder," she repeated. "Alder has been showing you Father's papers."

"Alder has been *cooperating*. There's a difference, and I think you can feel it." Sebastian set down the cup. "Here's what I can't figure out, Mrs. Voss, and I'd rather hear it from you than from a document. You told me June Calloway was blackmailing your father. That she was the villain of the piece. But if that were true—if she had leverage over Harlan Voss—why did he pay her for twenty-nine years instead of destroying her? Men like your father didn't pay blackmailers. They buried them. Sometimes literally, in this town."

The ferns seemed to lean in. Camille's composure held, but it held the way ice holds—a solid surface conducting every crack beneath it.

"I don't discuss my father's affairs with strangers," she said.

"No," Sebastian agreed pleasantly, rising. "You discuss them with lawyers. Which tells me everything about how worried you are, and nothing about why." At the door they paused, and delivered the parting shot they'd been saving since the driveway: "For what it's worth, Mrs. Voss—the letters aren't in her handwriting. When the document examiners finish, everyone will know whose hand wrote them. You might ask yourself whether you want to have been the sister who helped, or the sister who lawyered up. History tends to remember the second one less kindly."

Outside, the fog was coming off the river in slow white ropes. Sebastian sat in the car for a moment, letting the adrenaline settle into its familiar aftertaste—that faint metallic tang of having manipulated people who deserved it, mostly, and wondering where the line had gone. Their producer, Dev, had once told them: *the day the lying feels easy, quit.* It hadn't felt easy. It had felt necessary. Those were different words, and Sebastian knew the distance between them was exactly the width of a career.

Their phone buzzed. Dev, with the weekly numbers and the weekly worry.

*"Three sponsors paused again,"* Dev said, without greeting. *"And I got a call from a network attorney asking, quote, whether our methods are defensible. What did you DO?"*

"I had tea."

*"Sebastian."*

"I played three heirs against each other using public records and excellent manners. Nobody was harmed. Several people were frightened, which in this town counts as progress." Sebastian started the engine. "How's the download curve?"

There was a pause on the line, the particular pause of a man deciding whether to be a colleague or a conscience.

*"Vertical,"* Dev admitted. *"Terrifyingly vertical. We're getting mail from mining towns. From coroners. From two people who claim to be June Calloway, neither of whom can spell it the same way twice."* Another pause. *"Be careful. When the numbers go vertical, the targets stop being abstract."*

"I know," Sebastian said, and hung up, and drove back through the fog feeling the truth of it settle over them like weather.

* * *

The call came at 3:07 in the morning.

Sebastian had fallen asleep at the kitchen table over the ledger photocopies, cheek on a page of Harlan Voss's small, vicious numerals, and woke to the phone buzzing against the wood with a violence that suggested either emergency or Alder. Given the hour, the distinction had collapsed.

"Alder."

Silence first. Then breathing. Then, low and slurred around the edges but not drunk—not anymore, maybe never again after tonight—the voice of the youngest Voss.

"You talked to Camille."

"I had tea with Camille."

"She called me. Two o'clock. Crying, then yelling, then crying again. She thinks I sold Father's papers to you." A sound that might have been a laugh, wrung out of him like water from a rag. "She's not wrong. That's the hell of it. She's not wrong, and she called me a traitor, and I lay there thinking—traitor to *what*, exactly? To the lie? Is the lie a country now? Do we owe it allegiance?"

Sebastian sat up slowly, fully awake, reaching for a pen without turning on a light. "What happened tonight, Alder?"

"Whitaker came to my house. Eight o'clock. Brought the crates from the archive—he'd been through everything Mabel had, everything, forty years of clippings and photographs, and he'd made piles. Piles, Sebastian. Like laundry. One pile was things that hurt us. One pile was things that hurt *other people*." Alder's breath caught. "He wanted to burn the second pile. In my fireplace. He stood there holding a photograph of June Calloway—I recognized her, from the gala picture, the one with the scratched-out woman—and he said, 'This all goes away if we burn it,' like that was a reasonable sentence. Like fire was a filing system."

"Did you burn it?"

Another silence, longer. When Alder spoke again, the drunkenness was gone entirely; what remained was quiet and terribly clear.

"I told him to get out of my house. And then I sat here for six hours reading what he left behind—the pile he didn't take, the pile he thought wasn't important. Financial stuff. Boring stuff." A paper rustled near the phone. "Except it isn't boring. There's a second ledger, Sebastian. Different handwriting. Same dates. Whoever kept it recorded the same June twelfth payments—but from the *other side*. As income. Someone was receiving what Father was paying. And the last entry isn't 2013, when Father died. The last entry is from this year."

The fog outside the kitchen window had gone luminous under the streetlight, thick as milk. Sebastian wrote *second ledger — current* on the pad and underlined it twice, and heard their own heartbeat in the pen strokes.

"Alder. Whose handwriting?"

"I don't know. That's the thing—I should know. Everyone in this town writes the same way, we all learned from the same teachers, it's all loops and flourishes. But there's a letter at the front of the book. An instruction. It says: *Continue as arranged. H.V.' and then it says—" Alder stopped. Sebastian heard him gather himself the way a diver gathers breath.

"It says what?"

"'The arrangement protects us all. E.C.'"

"E.C.," Sebastian repeated. "Not H.V. signing off. E.C. Countersigning."

"Yeah." A hollow laugh. "My father's initials were the request. Somebody else's initials were the *permission*. I've been staring at it for six hours trying to find a name in this town that fits E.C., and there's only one I can come up with, and he's been dead thirty-eight years."

Emil Brandt. Dead men did not countersign ledgers. Dead men did not receive payments into the present tense. Somewhere between the fog and the ink, the case had just shifted its weight—and Sebastian felt the floor of it move, the way you feel ice shift underfoot on the river: not breaking, not yet, but no longer to be trusted.

"Alder," Sebastian said carefully. "I need that book."

"I know." The youngest Voss exhaled, long and shaking, a man setting down something heavy after carrying it alone in the dark. "Come tomorrow. Come early, before Whitaker sobers up and remembers what else he left behind. And Sebastian—" his voice dropped, almost shy, almost young, "—when you write whatever you're going to write about my family. About me. Make it true. That's all I'm asking. Forty years of this town, everybody's been making it *quiet*. Just once, make it true."

The line went dead.

Sebastian sat in the dark kitchen a while longer, the pad glowing faintly under the streetlight's borrowed light, and read back what they'd written: *second ledger — current — E.C. — permission, not payment.* Then, below it, without quite deciding to, they added a third line:

*Never take a case you can't close.*

They looked at it for a long moment. Then they got up, put the kettle on, and began packing the bag they'd take to Alder's house at dawn—because somewhere out in the fog, a dead man's initials were still signing things, and whoever held the pen had been writing in this town for forty years without once running out of ink.
