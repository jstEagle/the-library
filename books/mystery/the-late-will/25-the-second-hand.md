## 25. The Second Hand

The county library's microfiche room smelled like pencil shavings and the particular despair of machines that had outlived their warranty. Sebastian Eriksen sat at the one functional reader—the other two had been "temporarily out of order" since roughly the Clinton administration—and fed Alder's ledger pages through the scanner they'd bought with money they were supposed to be spending on groceries.

The rental kitchen table had become a war room. Photographs of Harlan Voss's ledger entries pinned along one wall. Alder's third of the private papers spread across the other. And in the center, weighted down with a coffee mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST SLEUTH—a gift from a listener in Duluth—two ledgers side by side like rival witnesses who'd finally been seated in the same room.

Sebastian's producer, Dev, appeared on the laptop screen, mid-yawn, his apartment in Chicago visible behind him in the blue glow of a monitor he'd clearly never turned off.

"Walk me through it again," he said. "Slowly. I've been awake for eleven minutes and you're already talking about handwriting."

"Two ledgers," Sebastian said. "Harlan's is the first one. Annual payments, every June twelfth, 1985 to 2019. We've been over that. But inside Alder's papers there's a second book—smaller, cheaper paper, different hand entirely. Same dates. Same amounts. It's a *mirror*. Someone was keeping their own record of what Harlan paid out."

"A co-conspirator with good bookkeeping habits."

"A partner," Sebastian corrected. "You don't keep your own duplicate ledger for forty years unless you're protecting yourself. Unless you want proof of every transaction in case your partner ever tries to make you the fall guy." They tapped the page. "Sound familiar? It's how Emil Brandt happened. Somebody took the fall in '86. Whoever kept this ledger wanted insurance against being next."

Dev rubbed his face. "Okay. So whose hand is it?"

"That's the fun part."

"I hate when you say that."

Sebastian held the second ledger up to the camera, open to a page from 1991. Then they held up a photograph they'd pulled from the historical society's files three days earlier: a dedication ceremony, 1988, the new wing of Meridian Falls General Hospital. A younger man stood beside Harlan Voss, holding the ceremonial scissors, beaming with the unguarded joy of someone who still believed the world rewarded loyalty.

Below the photo, in the caption: *Mayor-to-be Dale Pruitt cuts the ribbon alongside benefactor H. Voss.*

And on the guest card reproduced in the archive file—because Mabel Dunn filed everything, God bless her, including things she probably shouldn't have—was a handwritten note in Dale Pruitt's youthful script: *Harlan — to thirty more years.*

"The letterforms match," Sebastian said. "Look at the way he makes his lowercase *e*—that little tail, like it's trying to escape. Look at the *y* in 'thirty' versus the *y* in 'June' in the ledger. Same descender loop. Same pressure. He was left-handed; the slant runs the same way. I sent scans to a document examiner in Spokane. Retired FBI. She owes me a favor because I didn't run a story in 2021 about her nephew."

"You're telling me the current mayor of Meridian Falls kept a parallel accounting of hush money for four decades."

"I'm telling you it's worse than that." Sebastian set the ledger down and leaned toward the screen. "Dev. Harlan died in 2019. The payments stopped. But look at the last entry in the second ledger."

They turned the page. The final line wasn't in the old hand at all. It was newer ink, bluer, ballpoint instead of fountain pen, dated June 12th of *last year*.

An amount. And beside it, in that same escaping-*e* hand, a single word: *Continued.*

Dev was quiet for a long moment. On screen, he'd gone very still, the way he did when a story stopped being content and started being dangerous.

"He's still paying," Dev said.

"Somebody's still paying. Or somebody's still being paid. Either way—" Sebastian exhaled. "Harlan Voss has been dead for six years, and the arrangement outlived him. This isn't a cold case, Dev. It never froze. It just changed management."

* * *

The trouble with small towns, Sebastian had learned across nine seasons of the podcast, was not that everyone knew everyone's business. It was that everyone knew everyone's business *wrong*—in comfortable, mutually agreed-upon versions that fit together like a quilt, each patch stitched by somebody with something to protect.

Dale Pruitt's version, as told at the Rotary lunch, the chamber of commerce dinner, and approximately four hundred church suppers, went like this: local boy, son of a schoolteacher, worked his way up from the hardware store to city council to the mayor's office on nothing but elbow grease and love for Meridian Falls. He'd been Harlan Voss's protégé, sure—everybody knew that, wore it like a medal. *Learned everything from that great man,* he liked to say, hand over heart.

What the quilt version left out was where a hardware store clerk had been on June 12th of every year since 1985.

Sebastian spent two days finding out where he was now. That part was easy. Mayors are public creatures; they *want* to be seen. Dale Pruitt cut ribbons, kissed babies, judged the pie contest at the harvest fair with the gravity of a Supreme Court justice. He was seventy-one years old and had the tanned, unhurried vitality of a man who played eighteen holes every Thursday and had never once lost sleep.

The hard part was the past. For that, Sebastian went back to the source.

Mabel Dunn's archive occupied the ground floor of the old Carnegie building, and it operated on what Mabel called "the honor system, which around here means I know exactly who takes what, and I remember." She was at her desk beneath the portrait of Harlan Voss—that same portrait she'd once noticed had been moved, though it hung in its proper place again now, watching the room with its painted patience.

"Mabel," Sebastian said, "what do you know about Dale Pruitt's early career?"

She looked up over her glasses with the expression of a woman deciding how much truth a given Tuesday could hold.

"Dale," she said slowly. "Nice boy. His mother taught fourth grade for thirty years, poor woman. Dale worked at Pruitt's Hardware—his uncle's store—and then Harlan took him under his wing somewhere around '84, '85. Fast rise after that. City council at twenty-eight. Mayor at thirty-four." She paused. "Fastest rise this town ever saw. People used to say Harlan must have seen something special in him."

"And did he? See something special?"

Mabel took off her glasses entirely, which Sebastian had learned was significant. It meant she was about to say something she'd normally file away and deny owning.

"I'll tell you what I saw," she said. "I saw a hardware clerk start dressing better than the bank president. I saw him get the corner office at city hall that used to belong to nobody. I saw him drive a new car every three years on a councilman's salary, and when folks joked about it at the diner, he'd laugh and say his uncle paid well." Her mouth thinned. "His uncle's store closed in 1989. Sold to a developer. Guess who approved the rezoning."

Sebastian felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it always did when a theory walked up and introduced itself as fact.

"Mabel. Did Dale Pruitt have any connection to the mines?"

"Everybody had a connection to the mines. Half this town's grandfathers died in them." She frowned. "But if you mean the *dispute*—the inspection reports, the safety citations, all that ugliness in '84—well. Harlan chaired the mine commission. And who do you suppose took the minutes?"

The room went very quiet. Outside, a truck rumbled past on the wet street, and the portrait of Harlan Voss seemed, in the gray light, almost amused.

"Minutes of the mine commission," Sebastian repeated.

"Typed them up himself, every meeting. Neatest minutes you ever saw. Kept the originals too, which was unusual—county usually got the only copies." Mabel's eyes met Sebastian's, steady and sad. "I've wondered for years why a man keeps the originals of documents everybody else wants forgotten. Now maybe you can tell me."

* * *

That evening, Sebastian laid it all out on the kitchen table for Cora Reyes, who arrived off shift with her hair still damp from the shower and a paper bag of tamales from the gas station on Route 9, which she insisted—against all available evidence—were the best in the state.

"Okay," she said, mouth full, studying the two ledgers. "Explain it to me like I'm a deputy and not a document examiner."

"You're neither. You're suspended."

"Suspended deputies retain full investigative opinions. It's in the union contract." She tapped the second ledger. "So Pruitt kept his own books. Why would he? If Harlan was paying him, Harlan's records prove it. Why duplicate?"

"Because Harlan was paying him to do something, and Pruitt needed proof of what that something was—in case Harlan ever decided Pruitt was expendable." Sebastian pulled out the timeline they'd built, index cards strung on butcher's twine like the world's grimmest laundry line. "Think about the sequence. Summer of '84, the mine collapse kills Harlan's brother Edmund. The official report says equipment failure, act of God, terrible tragedy. Emil Brandt, the caretaker, confesses to falsifying an inspection—then recants, then hangs himself. June Calloway disappears. And in the middle of all of it, a twenty-something hardware clerk becomes the most important man in town."

Cora set down her tamale. "My dad's file said the collapse wasn't equipment failure."

"It wasn't. The shaft was condemned twice before it failed. The inspection reports proving it got buried—with Edmund Voss, we think, or near enough. Harlan covered it up to protect the founding families' mining interests, and June saw him do it. That's why she vanished. That's why Harlan paid her—or paid *for* her—for thirty-five years." Sebastian's finger moved to the second ledger. "But Harlan couldn't bury those reports alone. Somebody typed the minutes. Somebody knew which inspector to lean on. Somebody handled the parts of the cover-up that required a face people trusted."

"Pruitt."

"Pruitt. And here's the part that keeps me up." Sebastian turned to the final entry, the fresh blue ink. *Continued.* "Harlan's dead. The original crime is ancient history. The statute expires in weeks. There is no earthly reason to keep paying—unless the payment isn't for the past. Unless it's maintenance."

Cora stared at the word a long time. When she spoke again, her voice had changed, gone flat and careful, the way it had when she talked about her father.

"Maintenance," she said. "Like the grave."

"Like the grave," Sebastian agreed quietly. "Somebody refilled that coffin with quarry stones recently. Freshly. Deliberately. We assumed it was to hide that the body was missing. But what if it was the opposite? What if the empty coffin was the *plan*—a controlled leak, timed to the land dispute, designed to close the whole question forever? Exhume, find nothing, declare the mystery unsolvable, let the statute lapse, and put a nice memorial on top of it. Case closed by the town itself. Nobody ever looks again."

"And the person with the power to arrange an exhumation, control the coroner's schedule, and stage a healing ceremony at the cemetery—"

"—is the mayor." Sebastian sat back. "It's not a cover-up that ended, Cora. It's a cover-up that got promoted."

Cora was silent. Then she laughed—one short, unhappy bark with no humor in it anywhere.

"My father spent nineteen years believing June Calloway was alive. The department retired him for it. Called him obsessed. And the whole time, the man signing off on his case reviews—" She shook her head. "Pruitt signed my dad's suspension paperwork. Did you know that? I found it in his boxes. Dale Pruitt, councilman, recommending Deputy Reyes be reassigned for 'unproductive fixation.' Nineteen ninety-nine."

The fog pressed against the kitchen window, thick and white, and for a moment neither of them said anything. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"We need the originals," Sebastian said finally. "The mine commission minutes. Mabel says Pruitt kept them. Where does a man like Dale Pruitt keep forty years of secrets?"

Cora looked at them with an expression that was equal parts admiration and pity.

"Sebastian. He's the mayor. He keeps them at city hall. In the building. Probably in his office, behind the plaque where he keeps his gavel." She stood, gathering her coat. "Which means tomorrow morning, while he's cutting a ribbon for the new crosswalk, some fool is going to have to figure out how to get into the mayor's private files."

"I'm not going to like your plan, am I?"

"No," Cora said, pulling on her gloves. "But you're going to help me with it anyway, because you've got that look."

"What look?"

"The look my dad used to get." She paused at the door, fog swirling in around her boots. "Right before he did something that got him suspended."

* * *

Alone, later, Sebastian sat at the table with the two ledgers and did the thing they always did at the end of a long day: recorded a voice memo, raw and unedited, the way they thought aloud.

"Day thirty-two," they murmured. "We came here to cover an empty coffin. Turns out the coffin was the least empty thing in this town. Every institution, every family, every friendly wave on Main Street—it's all load-bearing. Take out one stone and you find another stone underneath, and underneath that, bedrock made of June twelfths." A pause. The recorder's red light glowed steadily. "Harlan Voss at least had the decency to die. His protégé is still in office. Still shaking hands. Still planning a ceremony to bury the truth with honors. The rot isn't history here. It's the present tense. It's the mayor."

They stopped the recording. In the silence, the house ticked and settled, and outside the fog moved down the street like something with errands to run.

Sebastian didn't see the pickup truck parked two blocks down, engine off, lights dark. Didn't see it pull away, slow and unhurried, ten minutes after Cora left.

But the truck's driver had seen the kitchen light. Had seen the two silhouettes bent over the table. And the next morning, at the historical society on Cedar Street, Mabel Dunn would arrive to find the smell of smoke hanging over the archive like a warning—

—and by nightfall, the room where forty years of Meridian Falls kept its memory would be burning.
