## 30. Ambush

The mine gave them back the way it had taken them: slowly, grudgingly, one cough of dust at a time.

Sebastian came up the ladder first, because Cora Reyes had insisted—*you're the one carrying the photographs, and I'm the one who knows how to fall*—and now they stood blinking in the gray afternoon light like two creatures surfacing from a dream neither would admit to having. The fog had thickened while they were underground. It hung between the pines in soft gray ropes, and somewhere up the ridge a bird was making a sound like a rusty gate complaining about its life.

"You look terrible," Cora said, hauling herself over the lip of the shaft and sitting down hard on a root.

"You should see yourself."

"I have. In mirrors. For forty years." She brushed grit from her uniform sleeve. "It's not getting better."

Sebastian laughed—a real laugh, startled out of them—and then stopped laughing, because their hands were shaking. Not from cold. From what was in the strongbox. They'd photographed every page down in that black throat of a tunnel, headlamp clamped between their teeth, fingers numb, whispering page numbers to themselves like a prayer: *inspection report, March '84; inspection report, April '84; memorandum, unsigned; confession, signed H.V.* Forty-one photographs. The whole rotten architecture of Meridian Falls, documented in a dead man's careful hand and now living in a phone's memory card, which was currently zipped into an inner pocket against Sebastian's ribs, where it felt less like evidence and more like a second heartbeat.

"Say it back to me," Cora said. She'd made them do this twice already, down in the dark, and she did it again now, watching Sebastian's face with those cop's eyes that missed nothing except, apparently, her own exhaustion.

"Inspection reports prove the collapse was foreseen. Harlan Voss knew the timbers were failing for eleven months before his brother went down there. The confession names the men who helped him bury it. And someone else held that confession over him—" Sebastian tapped their chest pocket, "—because he kept paying, all those years, June twelfth after June twelfth. We just don't know who held it yet."

"Yet," Cora repeated, and something almost like hope crossed her face before she smothered it under professionalism. "Okay. My truck's at the pull-off. We drive straight to the state police barracks in Kettle River. Not the county office. *Kettle River.*"

"Not the sheriff's department here?"

"Deputy Wexler plays poker with the mayor every Thursday." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd note that water is wet. "Kettle River."

They climbed up through the trees toward the road, and for one hundred yards everything was ordinary. Fog, mud, the smell of wet pine and old iron. Sebastian's boots squelched. Cora hummed four notes of something tuneless. It was so mundane that when the engine sound came, low and wrong, idling somewhere below the ridge, Sebastian's mind refused it for a full three seconds—the way your mind refuses a word on the tip of your tongue, or a face at a funeral that shouldn't be there.

"Cora."

"I hear it."

They froze among the trunks. Below them, maybe sixty yards off, at the gravel pull-off where Cora's truck sat alone under a rusted sign reading MINE ACCESS — CLOSED BY ORDER, another vehicle waited. A dark SUV, engine running, headlights off. Two silhouettes inside. One of them got out as they watched, unhurried, and leaned against the fender like a man waiting for a bus he knew would come.

"Is that—" Sebastian started.

"Town fleet," Cora breathed. "That's the mayor's office SUV. I've washed my hands of knowing that, but I know it."

"Maybe they're just—"

"Don't say 'lost.' Nobody gets lost going *to* a closed mine. You can only get lost leaving it, and they're facing the exit."

The man by the fender straightened. He didn't shout. He didn't wave. He simply looked up into the treeline, directly at the place where they stood—which meant he'd known, or guessed, or been told—and then he opened the SUV's rear door in a small courteous gesture, like a maître d' presenting a table.

"Back door," Cora whispered. "There's a logging cut, half a mile north, hooks back to the county road. We go around."

"We could just talk to them."

"We could also just hand them the box and save everyone the exercise." Her jaw was set. "Move."

They moved. It was not dignified. Sebastian, whose relationship with cardio was best described as *epistolary*, crashed through ferns and bracken while Cora moved ahead with the economical silence of someone who'd grown up hunting deer with her father in these exact woods. Twice Sebastian went down and twice got up, and by the time the logging cut appeared—a lane of churned mud wide enough for a truck—they were both breathing hard and the engine noise behind them had changed pitch. Moving. Following.

"He's calling ahead," Cora said grimly. "There'll be someone at the county road junction. Which means we take my truck from the pull-off after all, right past them, and pray they're committed to the junction."

"That's a terrible plan."

"It's the only plan. Terrible plans are the honest ones—you can tell exactly how they fail." She glanced back at Sebastian, and for a moment her cop face cracked into something younger and scared and stubborn. "You've got the photos memorized? Page numbers?"

"Forty-one. Headlamp, teeth, the whole liturgy."

"Then even if the worst happens, the truth survives. Say it."

"The truth survives."

"The truth survives," she agreed, "but let's not make it a martyr."

* * *

The pull-off came up fast through the fog, and Cora drove out of the trees at a jog, keys already out, and that was when Sebastian understood the shape of the trap fully, because the SUV was no longer at the mine access. It was on the road. Between them and town. Parked at an angle across the gravel shoulder with its hazards blinking, patient as a spider's web, and the man who'd leaned on the fender was standing beside it with his hands in his pockets and a smile that never reached anything above his cheekbones.

"Afternoon, Deputy," he called pleasantly. "Road's washed out ahead. I'd wait a bit if I was you."

Cora put herself between him and the truck. "Wexler. This is county property you're blocking."

"Is it?" He tilted his head. "Funny. Feels like private land to me. Feels like most things around here are private, when you get right down to it." His eyes slid past her to Sebastian, and the smile widened by a professional millimeter. "Mr. Eriksen. Big day underground, I hear. Find any treasure?"

"Just rocks," Sebastian said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Mostly rocks."

"Mm. Well. Rocks are heavy." He pushed off the fender. "Heavy things slow you down. Be a shame if you were slowed down."

And there it was—not a threat, exactly. Something worse: an offer shaped like a threat, delivered with the mild courtesy of a man who had done this before and found it effective. Sebastian felt the phone's weight against their ribs and thought, absurdly, of Mabel Dunn in her hospital bed, of the smell of smoke, of the archive room's blackened shelves. Of the rule they'd broken for Alder Voss and the clean hands they no longer had.

"Get in the truck, Sebastian," Cora said quietly.

They got in the truck. Cora got in the truck. The engine turned over on the first try, which was the last mercy the morning intended to give them.

For eleven seconds, it was going to work. Sebastian counted them later, in the hospital corridor, the way you count anything when your mind is trying to build a wall between itself and a memory. Eleven seconds of gravel, of the mirror showing Wexler's SUV swinging out behind them, of Cora saying, "Hold on to something," in the tone people use for weather reports about hurricanes.

Then the pickup truck came out of the fog cut at the junction ahead, moving fast, sliding sideways into their lane with the deliberate grace of a thing that had rehearsed.

"Cora—"

"I see it."

She wrenched the wheel. The world became a smear of pine and gray. The truck's left tires left the asphalt, caught the soft shoulder, and then the world tilted with a horrible leisurely patience, the way disasters do in the moment before they stop being patient, and the truck went over.

* * *

Sound came back before sense. Rain on metal—it had started raining, or maybe it had been raining and Sebastian hadn't noticed. Glass everywhere, glittering in the wheel well like a nest built by a magpie with money. The smell of gasoline and pine air and blood, and underneath it all, faintly, the fog pressing in at the shattered windows like something curious.

"Cora." Sebastian's voice sounded far away to them. "Cora. Cora."

"I'm here." A groan. Movement in the passenger seat, where she'd ended up because physics has a sense of humor. Her arm hung oddly. There was red on her temple, running thin and bright down past her jaw. "Are you—"

"I'm fine. I'm fine." Sebastian checked anyway, frantically, patting their own ribs the way you'd check for a wound, and found the inner pocket intact, the phone intact, the memory card a small hard secret against their heartbeat. Forty-one photographs. The truth survives.

Headlights bloomed through the rear window. Doors slammed. Boots on gravel, unhurried.

"Out," Cora hissed. "Back window. Go, go—"

"There's no time, they're—"

"*Go.*" And then she did the thing Sebastian would replay for months afterward, the thing that would keep them up at night in a good way and a bad way both: she reached across herself with her good arm, dragged the strongbox from beneath the seat where it had flown, and wrapped her body around it like a mother, like a shield, like a woman whose father had died believing in something and who had decided, somewhere in the last thirty seconds, that she was done letting other people decide what was worth dying for.

The driver's-side door tore open. Hands—gloved, efficient, impersonal—hauled Sebastian out onto the gravel. The rain hit their face. Through the windshield, upside down and distorted, they saw the second man wrench open the passenger door and try to pull the box from Cora's arms, and heard her say, clear as a bell through broken glass:

"You'll take it out of my cold hands, and I mean that as a scheduling suggestion."

He took it anyway. It cost him. Whatever he did to make her let go, it was quick and it was ugly, and afterward she lay still in the wrecked cab with her bad arm cradled against her chest, breathing, conscious, cursing with impressive fluency and creativity—Sebastian would treasure some of those phrases forever—but not moving to follow.

Because that was the arithmetic of it, wasn't it. The box for the witness. They'd traded. Everyone had gotten what they'd come for, and everyone had lost the thing they couldn't afford to lose, and the fog closed over the whole transaction like a ledger being shut.

Sebastian lay on the wet gravel with a knee screaming and rain in their eyes and watched the taillights dissolve, and thought, with a clarity that felt almost holy: *I have forty-one photographs.*

They had taken them twenty minutes ago. Down in the dark, by headlamp, page by page, whispering numbers like a rosary. The strongbox was gone. The confession was gone. Every original page, every signature, every scrap of paper that could be burned or buried or denied—

Except it couldn't be. Because paper burns. But a photograph lives in more than one place, and Sebastian Eriksen had learned, over years of chasing the dead and the disappeared, to back up everything twice. One card in their pocket. One card taped inside the toilet tank of a rental cottage on Larkspur Lane, because paranoia, it turned out, was just foresight with anxiety.

"Sebastian." Cora's voice, weaker now, from the wreck. "Hey. Podcast guy. You alive?"

Sebastian crawled back through the glass to the overturned cab and took her good hand, and pressed it, and said the truest thing they'd said in weeks: "I'm alive. And they got nothing that matters."

Cora looked at them—one eye swelling shut, blood in her eyebrows, rain dripping off her chin—and began, painfully, to laugh.

"They got a box of paper," she wheezed, "and you've got a photograph of every word of it, don't you. Don't you."

"Page forty-one is my favorite. Very incriminating margins."

"Oh, that's beautiful. That's the most beautiful thing I've ever heard. Tell my father." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she turned her face away into the crook of her good arm, and Sebastian pretended very hard not to notice, and held her hand anyway until the ambulance lights came swimming up through the fog like something arriving from a kinder country.

* * *

At the Kettle River clinic—small, fluorescent, smelling of coffee and antiseptic—a doctor with kind eyes and a name tag reading DR. OKAFOR put Cora's arm in a cast and her pride in traction alongside it.

"Fractured radius, seven stitches, mild concussion," she reported to Sebastian in the hallway, in the tone of someone delivering a weather forecast. "She'll live to be ornery for decades. Are *you* hurt?"

"Bruises. A knee that's filing a complaint."

"Ice it. And Mr. Eriksen?" Dr. Okafor lowered her voice. "Whoever did this will assume you're finished. That's the gift in it. Nobody guards a door they believe is closed."

Sebastian sat in the plastic chair outside Cora's bay for a long time after that, watching rain run down the window, turning the evening over in their mind like a stone. The strongbox was gone—but Whitaker Voss had been asking pointed questions about "closure" for weeks, and Camille had lawyers, and Alder had called at 3 a.m. ready to talk. Three heirs. Three thirds of a dead man's papers. If the box was truly beyond reach, then the papers were the only remaining originals—and one of those heirs had spent a sleepless night deciding which side of history to stand on.

Their phone buzzed. Unknown number. Local prefix.

Sebastian answered.

Alder Voss's voice came through thin and shaking, and underneath the fear in it there was something new, something that sounded almost like resolve: "They told me what happened on the mine road. I need you to come tonight, Sebastian. Before they decide I'm next. I know where my father kept the rest."
