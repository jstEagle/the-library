## 36. The Healing Ceremony

The fog came in early that morning, the way it always did in Meridian Falls, rolling off the river like something with an appointment to keep. By eight o'clock it had swallowed the cemetery whole, and by nine the town had arrived to be swallowed along with it.

Sebastian stood at the back of the crowd, hood up, recorder off, and counted.

Two hundred and eleven people, give or take. Four local news cameras. One regional affiliate van with its satellite dish raised toward the sky like a metal flower seeking a sun that hadn't shown itself in Meridian Falls since roughly 1984. A folding stage. A podium draped in bunting. And at the center of it all, a fresh granite marker where an empty grave had been, because apparently nothing said *healing* quite like a headstone for a man who wasn't in the ground beneath it.

The mayor had outdone himself. There were lilies—white ones, arranged in low banks around the grave—and there was a string quartet from the high school playing something mournful and vaguely familiar, and there were printed programs on every chair, heavy cardstock, embossed:

**A CELEBRATION OF THE LIFE AND LEGACY OF HARLAN VOSS — FOUNDER OF MERIDIAN FALLS**

*A Town United in Healing*

Sebastian picked one up off an empty chair and read it twice. The paper stock alone must have cost four hundred dollars. You could fund a decent records search on paper stock like that. You could almost fund a decent cover-up.

"You came," whispered a voice at their elbow, and Sebastian didn't jump, because they'd known Cora Reyes would find them. She was in civilian clothes—a gray coat, hair down—which somehow made her look more like a deputy than the uniform did. "I half thought you'd skip it."

"And miss the eulogy?" Sebastian murmured. "I've been waiting forty years for this eulogy. Well. Other people have."

Cora's eyes moved across the crowd with the flat professional sweep of someone who'd spent her career watching funerals for signs of foul play. "Mayor's got three of his councilmen flanking the stage. His wife's up there too. He's nervous."

"He should be."

"He's not showing it."

"He's showing it," Sebastian said. "Look at his hands. Every time he touches the podium he straightens the microphone. He's done it six times since we got here. Men who are calm don't adjust microphones, Cora. Microphones are the least of their problems."

Cora almost smiled. Then she didn't. "Ruth?"

"Ready." Sebastian touched their coat pocket, where a phone sat warm as a coal. "She'll come when I signal. Not before. If this goes wrong, she was never part of it—she's just a pharmacist who came to pay her respects like everyone else."

"And you?"

"I'm just a podcaster," Sebastian said, "who came to pay his respects."

They both let that lie sit between them for a moment, the way you'd let a cat sit on your lap—aware that at any moment it might turn into something with claws.

The quartet finished. The crowd settled into the folding chairs with the particular rustle of two hundred people trying to be reverent at once. Somewhere near the front a baby started to fuss and was gently shushed. The fog pressed closer, beading on the bunting, softening the edges of the headstones until the whole cemetery looked like a photograph left out in the rain.

And then the mayor stepped up to the podium.

* * *

Dale Hutchins had been mayor of Meridian Falls for nineteen years, and he had the voice for it—the kind of warm, gravelled baritone that made you think of woodstoves and county fairs and the general rightness of small-town life. He'd used it at a hundred ribbon-cuttings. He'd used it over the PA at football games. He'd used it, if Ruth Callahan's testimony held, in 1984, in a room above the mine office, telling four frightened men exactly what they were going to say about a collapsed shaft and a dead brother.

He adjusted the microphone.

"Friends. Neighbors." He paused, letting the words settle over the crowd like the fog itself. "Forty years ago, this town lost something. We lost trust in each other. We lost our certainty. And last month, when we opened that grave—" he gestured, gravely, at the granite marker, "—we opened more than a coffin. We opened old wounds."

Sebastian, at the back, watched the crowd nod. Watched them *want* it. That was the thing nobody told you about towns like this: people didn't conspire so much as they agreed. Slowly, comfortably, over coffee and church and decades of not asking questions, they agreed. The mayor wasn't lying to them. He was offering them permission to stop looking.

"I knew Harlan Voss," the mayor continued. "I was a young man when I came to work for him. He taught me everything I know about building something that lasts." A pause, expertly timed. "And I believe—I *know*—that whatever darkness has been dug up alongside that empty coffin, Harlan Voss would have wanted us to face it together, as a family, and then to *close it*. To heal. To put the dirt back, friends. To put the dirt back and move forward."

There it was. Sebastian felt the sentence land in their chest like a coin dropped down a well. *Put the dirt back.* Forty years of buried things, and here was the town's father figure, on live television, asking everyone to do it one more time—politely, ceremonially, with a quartet.

Beside them, Cora breathed out slowly through her nose.

At the front, in the second row, sat the Voss heirs. Whitaker in a charcoal suit, hands folded, face composed into something that might have been grief or might have been indigestion. Camille beside him behind enormous sunglasses, though the sun hadn't appeared. And Alder at the end of the row, unshaven, in a jacket that didn't fit him, staring at the coffin-shaped hole in the world with an expression Sebastian could only describe as *waiting.*

Alder caught Sebastian's eye across two hundred heads. He didn't nod. He didn't look away either. He just sat there, a man watching a fuse he'd helped light.

"—and so today," the mayor was saying, "we return Harlan Voss to his rest. Symbolically. Spiritually. As a community. And we say to the past: *enough.* We say to the rumors and the podcasts and the outsiders trading on our grief: enough. This town heals *today*—"

Sebastian was already moving.

* * *

Later, replaying the footage—and there would be footage, hours of it, frame by frame—people would ask what it looked like, that walk up the aisle between the folding chairs. Some said Sebastian Eriksen strode. Some said they drifted, ghostlike, out of the fog. The truth was less cinematic. The truth was that Sebastian walked the way you walk to the dentist: fast, because slowing down gives you time to change your mind.

Their heart was going like a drum line. Their mouth was dry. Forty episodes, four hundred thousand subscribers, one ruined reputation, one burned archive, one deputy suspended, one archivist in a hospital bed, one woman who'd spent her whole adult life being somebody else—all of it funneling down into the next thirty feet of wet grass.

The mayor saw them at twenty feet. Something flickered across his face—not fear yet, just recognition, the way a man recognizes weather coming in.

"—and while I know some have come among us seeking only to profit from our pain—"

Ten feet.

"—I would ask that we—"

Five feet.

Sebastian stopped at the edge of the little stage, reached into their coat, and took out the microphone.

Not a weapon. Not a sign. Just a handheld mic, cordless, borrowed from the affiliate van that morning by a sound engineer named Deb who owed Sebastian a favor dating back to a story in Duluth, and who had asked exactly zero questions, which is the highest form of journalistic loyalty.

"Mayor Hutchins," Sebastian said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect, carried clean through the PA system, through every speaker, into two hundred ears and four camera feeds. "I'm not here to accuse anyone. I'm not here to profit from anyone's pain. I'm here because I brought a microphone to a healing ceremony, and it seems to me that's exactly what a healing ceremony needs."

The crowd made a sound—a collective intake, a rustle, the sound of two hundred people leaning forward at once.

The mayor recovered beautifully. Nineteen years of practice. He smiled down at Sebastian with warm, weary patience, the smile of a man humoring a child. "Friend, this is a private service. If you have grief to share, there are cards at the—"

"It's being streamed," Sebastian said pleasantly. "Your service. Right now. Live, to about half a million people." They held up their phone in the other hand, screen out, red dot pulsing. "Deb from Channel Six was kind enough to run the feed. So really, Mayor, this stopped being private the moment you invited the cameras. Which—you did. Beautifully done, by the way. The bunting especially."

Somewhere in the crowd, a laugh escaped before its owner could strangle it. The mayor's smile stayed fixed but his eyes went cold and quick, flicking once—just once—toward the edge of the cemetery, toward the parking area, toward escape routes, and Sebastian noted it the way you note anything: automatically, permanently.

"All I want," Sebastian said, turning to face the crowd now, letting the mic pick up the fog-damp air, the quartet frozen mid-cradle of their instruments, the white lilies trembling slightly in a wind nobody had ordered, "is ten minutes. Ten minutes of this ceremony. And then you can heal however you like. Put the dirt back. Sing hymns. I'll even help shovel."

Silence. The baby fussed again and was not shushed.

"Sir—" began one of the councilmen, stepping forward, and Cora was suddenly there, offhandedly, casually, positioning herself between the councilman and the stage with the effortless geometry of a woman who'd spent fifteen years managing crowds at accident scenes.

"Let them talk, Gary," Cora said mildly. "It's a free country. Still, last I checked."

The mayor's jaw worked. Sebastian watched him do the math—watch him weigh dragging them off the stage on live television against letting them speak, watch him choose, wrongly, the second option, because he still believed, after everything, that no one would actually say it. Out loud. In front of God and the cameras and the string quartet.

That was the thing about men like Dale Hutchins. They believed in the dirt. They believed it always held.

"Ten minutes," the mayor said quietly, stepping back from the podium. "Then security walks you out."

"Fair," said Sebastian, who had no intention of needing security walked anywhere, because in nine minutes and forty seconds, give or take, none of this would be theirs to control anymore.

They looked out at the crowd—at Mabel's empty chair with flowers laid across it, sent from Room 304 of St. Ambrose Regional; at old Tom Vasquez from the diner, sitting with his arms crossed; at Whitaker Voss, whose hands had come unfolded; at Alder, who had closed his eyes like a man praying or bracing—and Sebastian pressed record on the backup unit in their pocket, took a breath that tasted of fog and river water, and spoke.

"My name is Sebastian Eriksen. Some of you know me as the outsider trading on your grief. Fair. But before I was that, I was somebody who couldn't stop thinking about a girl named June Calloway, who disappeared from this town in June of 1984, and about a caretaker named Emil Brandt, who hanged himself in 1986 for a crime that—if the documents I'm holding are real—was never his to confess."

They held up a sheaf of photographs. Grainy, flash-lit, taken by phone light forty feet underground in a sealed mine shaft. On top: a page in a careful, old-fashioned hand, the signature legible even from the third row.

*Harlan Voss.*

"This is a confession," Sebastian said. "Written by the founder of your town. It describes a collapse at the Meridian mine in the winter of 1984. It describes inspection reports that warned, in writing, eighteen months earlier, that Shaft Three was failing. It describes a decision—made in a room above the mine office—to bury those reports, seal the shaft, and call what happened to Daniel Voss an act of God." A pause. "Daniel Voss did not die of an act of God. He died because fixing the shaft would have shut the mine down for a year, and shutting the mine down for a year would have cost five founding families everything they had. So they chose. They chose the money. And then they chose the silence. And then they chose the dirt."

The crowd had gone very still. Not hostile. Worse than hostile. *Attentive.*

"There was one witness," Sebastian said. "One person whose account couldn't be bought, because she wasn't offered money—she was offered exile. A young nurse's aide named June Calloway, who saw the reports, who saw the meeting, who was told she could spend the rest of her life in Meridian Falls—or the rest of her life somewhere else, alive, on Harlan Voss's dime. Emil Brandt agreed to confess and hang the crime around his own neck because his daughter was sick and the Voss estate paid her medical bills until the day he died. Everyone in this story was bought, or broken, or buried. Except one person."

Sebastian turned toward the second row of chairs.

Toward a small woman in a navy coat, sitting very straight, hands folded in her lap, who had come to the ceremony like everyone else, who had signed the guest card like everyone else, whose badge—pharmacy blue, RUTH CALAHAN—was pinned to her lapel like a shield she was about to take off.

"Except the person this town forgot to keep looking at," Sebastian said, "because hiding in plain sight only works if nobody ever looks."

The fog hung motionless over the lilies. Two hundred and eleven people were not breathing. Four cameras were not blinking. Somewhere in the trees beyond the fence, a crow complained once and went quiet, as if it too wanted to hear.

Ruth Callahan rose from her chair.

She rose slowly, the way you rise when your knees have been carrying a secret for forty years and the secret turns out to weigh nothing at all. She smoothed her coat. She reached up to her lapel, unfastened the pharmacy badge with fingers that did not shake, and set it down on the folding seat beside her—face up, name visible, a small blue rectangle catching what little light the morning had left to give.

Then she looked at Sebastian, and nodded once, and started walking toward the stage.

And behind her, in the second row, Whitaker Voss put his face in his hands, and Camille took off her sunglasses, and Alder opened his eyes, and Dale Hutchins gripped the edge of the podium hard enough that the microphone—adjusted all morning, straightened and re-straightened, the least of anyone's problems—finally, audibly, squealed with feedback.

The whole town leaned in.

June Calloway climbed the steps.
