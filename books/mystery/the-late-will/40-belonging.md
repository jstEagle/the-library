## 40. Belonging

The fog came in on a Tuesday in October, the way it always did, rolling up from the river like something patient that had learned to wait. Sebastian Eriksen stood at the window of the apartment above the historical society archive with a mug of coffee going cold in their hands and watched it swallow the streetlights one by one, and for the first time in their adult life, the fog did not feel like an enemy.

It felt like weather.

That was the difference, they supposed. Eight months ago they had arrived in Meridian Falls with a recorder, a rule, and a suitcase light enough to leave in ninety seconds flat. The rule had been simple: never take a case you can't close. The suitcase had been simpler: never own enough of anything that leaving would hurt.

The suitcase was still under the bed. It had been there since spring. Sebastian kept meaning to put it in the closet, and kept not doing it, the way you keep not doing anything that would make a temporary arrangement permanent.

Below them, through the floorboards, came the familiar sound of Mabel Dunn's radio — she'd left it with the apartment when she moved into her sister's place over in Cedar Hollow, "for company," she'd said, "and because at my age I shouldn't be climbing those stairs just to water a fern." The radio played the same station it had played for forty years, which meant it was playing something from 1979, which meant Mabel was, in spirit, entirely present.

Sebastian drank the cold coffee anyway. It was six-forty in the morning. In forty minutes, June Calloway was going to kneel down in the dirt over her own empty grave and plant tulip bulbs, and Sebastian was going to be there to watch, because some appointments you keep even when nobody's asked you to.

* * *

The cemetery sat on the hill above town like a sentence someone had started and never finished. Harlan Voss's monument still stood at its center — granite, obelisk-shaped, inscribed with FOUNDER and BENEFACTOR and a list of civic achievements long enough to constitute its own small library. The county had voted in July on whether to remove it. The vote had been close. In the end they'd kept the stone and added a second plaque beneath it, smaller, plainer, in letters you had to crouch to read:

*In memory of those whose labor built this town, and whose truth was buried with it.*

Mabel had written the line herself. She'd submitted four drafts. The historical society had approved the third without reading it closely, which Mabel considered the single greatest victory of her seventy-one years.

Sebastian walked up the hill with their collar turned against the damp, past rows of leaning stones, and found her already there.

June Calloway knelt at the edge of the open plot — the plot that had held river stones and a rotted wool coat for forty years, the plot that had started everything — with a trowel in one hand and a paper sack of bulbs between her knees. She wore a canvas jacket two sizes too big and gardening gloves with the fingertips worn through, and her gray hair was pinned back with what appeared to be a pencil.

She looked, Sebastian thought not for the first time, like exactly what she was: a woman who had spent four decades being no one in particular and had finally, at seventy-three, decided to be someone specific.

"You're early," June said, without looking up.

"You're earlier."

"I've been early my whole life. Habit." She patted the soil flat around the first bulb. "You learn to arrive before anyone expects you. It's the last thing he taught me that was actually useful."

*He* being Harlan Voss, founder, benefactor, dead man of the empty coffin. Sebastian had learned, over the months, to let those references pass without flinching. June spoke of him the way you might speak of a dangerous dog that had once, inexplicably, been kind to you — with caution, with a strange grudging tenderness, and with the understanding that kindness and danger had never been opposites in him, only neighbors.

"Ruthie at the pharmacy says the tulips won't take this late," Sebastian said.

"Ruthie," June said, "is a fiction I maintained for thirty-nine years, and I'll thank you not to cite her as a horticultural authority." She pressed a bulb into the earth. "They'll take fine. They're stubborn. Like everything else that survives here."

Sebastian crouched beside her, careful of the mud. Below, the town was waking — lights coming on in the diner, the pharmacy's green cross flickering awake, smoke rising straight from the bakery chimney in the windless air. From up here Meridian Falls looked almost gentle. Almost ordinary. Which, Sebastian had come to believe, was not a lie so much as a half-truth, and half-truths were the only kind a town could survive on.

"Can I ask you something?" Sebastian said.

"You can ask. Whether I answer depends on whether it's one of your podcast questions or one of your real ones."

"It's a real one."

June sat back on her heels and looked at them. Her eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, the eyes of someone who had spent years watching without being watched. "Go on."

"When you took the badge off. At the ceremony. Ten minutes before the state police walked in." Sebastian turned the mug in their hands. "Were you scared?"

June considered this with the seriousness she brought to all questions, as if each one were a bulb that might or might not take.

"Yes," she said. "Terribly. My hands shook so badly I nearly dropped the badge. But it was a good scared. There's a difference. Most fear is the fear of losing something. That was the fear of becoming something." She smiled, small and crooked. "I hadn't been June Calloway out loud since I was thirty-two. You forget how heavy a name is until you pick it back up."

"And now?"

"Now I water things." She gestured at the plot, at the dark turned earth, at the sack of bulbs. "It turns out that's most of what a name is good for. Something to sign the garden center receipt with."

Sebastian laughed, and June laughed too, and for a moment the two of them knelt together in the fog over an empty grave, laughing, which was not in any way how either of them had imagined this story ending, back when it was still a story about a body.

* * *

At nine, Sebastian walked down the hill and along River Street to the archive, where Mabel's old apartment waited above and the historical society's front room waited below, and where Cora Reyes was already waiting inside the latter, drinking coffee from the pot Mabel had left behind and pretending she wasn't.

Cora was out of uniform these days — suspended in the spring, reinstated by summer, resigned by September, in that order, each step taken with the calm of a woman who had known exactly what the stairs cost before she climbed them. She worked private security now for the mine remediation project, which meant she spent her days guarding a hole in the ground, and she claimed, with a straight face, that it was the most honest work she'd ever done.

"Morning," she said. "You look terrible."

"I've been up since five."

"That's not new. What's new is you're smiling about it. It's unsettling. Stop it."

Sebastian set the cold coffee mug on the counter and started a fresh pot, because the archive ran on Mabel's rules even in her absence, and Mabel's first rule had always been that the coffee must be strong enough to strip paint. "How's the shaft?"

"Flooded to the third level, just like the reports said. The state crew starts the formal recovery next month. They found another strongbox last week." Cora's voice went carefully neutral. "More inspection reports. More signatures. Including—" she paused, savoring it, "—the mayor's predecessor. And the mayor himself, age twenty-six, initialing a memo marked DO NOT FILE."

"The rot goes all the way down."

"The rot *was* the foundation. That's the whole finding." Cora stretched, joints cracking. "Whitaker testified for six hours yesterday. Camille's lawyers are negotiating from somewhere in Portugal. And Alder—" she shook her head, half pity, half wonder, "—Alder sent flowers to June. A whole cart. The florist didn't know what to write on the card so he wrote 'Sorry about everything' and honestly? Accurate."

"He's doing better, though."

"He's doing *honestly*, which for an Alder is better." Cora drained her cup. "Anyway. I didn't come for the gossip. I came because Mabel called. She wants you to record something for the archive. She wouldn't tell me what. She said, and I quote, 'Tell that podcaster to bring the good microphone and their listening face.'"

"My listening face."

"Her words. She also said to tell you the fern needs watering, which I suspect is code for something, because that fern has been dead since June and she refuses to throw it out."

Sebastian glanced toward the corner, where the fern stood in its pot, brown and brittle and dignified, like a small preserved piece of the previous year. "We don't throw things out here," they said. "We just find out what they were really holding."

Cora snorted. "God. You've been here eight months and you're already talking like a local. It's terminal, you know. There's no cure. Ask anybody."

* * *

The call to Mabel came at noon, video crackling over the archive's ancient wifi. Mabel filled most of the screen, silver-haired and sharp-eyed, propped up in her sister's guest room with a quilt over her knees and the same radio murmuring 1979 behind her.

"There's my archivist," she said, by way of greeting. Sebastian had stopped correcting her on the title months ago. "Did you water the fern?"

"The fern is dead, Mabel."

"The fern is *dormant*. There's a difference, and the difference is faith." She waved this aside. "Now. I want you to record something. For the collection. Not for the podcast — for the *archive*. There's a distinction, and the distinction matters, because the podcast belongs to the world and the archive belongs to us."

Sebastian set up the good microphone, the one with the foam windscreen shaped like a tiny cloud, and pressed record. "Go ahead, Mabel. Whenever you're ready."

Mabel was quiet for a moment. Behind her, the radio played something with horns in it.

"For sixty years," she began, "I catalogued this town. Every clipping, every photograph, every funeral notice and harvest fair ribbon. And I told myself that keeping the record was the same as telling the truth. It isn't. A record is just a pile of facts, waiting. Somebody has to *say* them. Out loud. To somebody who's listening." She leaned closer to the camera, and her eyes were fierce and wet at once. "So I'm saying it now, for the record, for whoever finds this in fifty years: we knew. Not everything — but enough. We knew June Calloway didn't run away. We knew the mines were killing men and calling it weather. We knew, and we filed it, and we shelved it, and we called that history." She sat back. "It wasn't history. It was inventory. And I am ashamed, and I am also — this is the part I need recorded — I am *proud* of what this town did this year. Both things are true. File them together."

She reached forward and the recording stopped.

For a while Sebastian just sat there in the quiet archive, surrounded by sixty years of inventory, listening to the building settle.

Then they saved the file, labeled it carefully, and went upstairs to pack — not the suitcase, but the recorder, because at four o'clock they had an appointment with a microphone and a blank season.

* * *

The studio, such as it was, occupied the corner of Sebastian's living room: a desk, a microphone, acoustic panels made from moving blankets thumbtacked to the wall, and a window overlooking River Street where the fog was lifting for the evening, going gold at the edges. On the desk lay a legal pad covered in crossed-out opening lines.

*This is The Late Will, season three.* No. Too neat.
*Eight months ago, a coffin opened and nothing was inside.* No. That was last year's story, and last year's story was closed.
*Some towns bury their dead.* No. Too grim, and worse, too easy.

The trouble, Sebastian had realized over weeks of false starts, was that they no longer knew how to introduce themselves. For ten years they had been a voice with no face, a byline with no body, invisible by design — the podcaster who never showed their face, never gave interviews, never stayed anywhere longer than a case required. Anonymity had been armor, then habit, then identity. And then a mayor with a fabricated scandal had stripped it all away in a single news cycle, and Sebastian had discovered, standing ruined in the wreckage, that underneath the invisibility there had been — what? Nothing? Or everything?

They knew the answer now. It had taken a garden over an empty grave to teach it to them.

There was a knock at the door. June stood on the landing with dirt on her knees and a jar of something amber in her hands.

"Honey from the farmers' market," she said. "The beekeeper asked if I was the woman from the trial. I said yes. He gave me a discount." She handed over the jar with visible satisfaction. "Turns out infamy has a loyalty program."

"Was it strange? Being recognized?"

June considered. "The first hundred times, yes. Then it became something else. People don't look at me like I'm a ghost anymore. They look at me like I'm a *neighbor* who survived something. It's remarkable how much better that is." She peered past Sebastian into the apartment, at the desk, the microphone, the legal pad. "Recording day?"

"Season opener. Due Friday. Currently winning zero to nothing against a blank page."

"What are you calling it?"

"That's the problem. Everything sounds wrong. Too dramatic, too tidy, too—" Sebastian gestured vaguely. "Too much like a person narrating instead of a person talking."

June stepped inside — she did that now, entered rooms like she belonged in them, a skill she was visibly still practicing — and looked at the legal pad. She read the crossed-out lines upside down, lips moving slightly.

"You know what your problem is?" she said. "You keep trying to open with the case. Coffins, graves, cover-ups. But that's not why people listened to you. They listened because you sound like someone who actually cares whether the truth gets found." She tapped the pad. "Open with yourself. You're the only thing in this whole story you haven't catalogued yet."

She left shortly after, declining tea, citing bulbs. At the door she paused.

"Oh — Whitaker called the pharmacy today. Wanted to know if I'd consider speaking at the dedication for the new miners' memorial in the spring. Publicly. As myself." Her mouth twisted, wry and wondering. "Harlan Voss's son, asking June Calloway for a favor. Forty years ago he'd have crossed the street to avoid my shadow." She shook her head. "I told him I'd think about it. I'm going to think about it for a very long time, and then probably say yes, because that's apparently who I am now. A person who says yes."

The door closed behind her. Sebastian looked at the legal pad for a long moment.

Then they picked up the pen.

* * *

Friday morning arrived clear — genuinely clear, no fog, the first such morning in weeks — and at nine o'clock Sebastian Eriksen sat down at the desk in the corner of the apartment above the archive, adjusted the microphone, and pressed record.

The red light glowed. The blankets on the walls swallowed the echo. Down on River Street, someone was sweeping a sidewalk; the sound came faintly through the window, rhythmic and ordinary and alive.

Sebastian took a breath.

"This is The Late Will," they said. "Season three. And I need to tell you up front that this season is different, because I'm different, and I know how that sounds, so let me prove it." Another breath. "For ten years I hid behind this microphone. No face, no name, no address that stayed an address. I told myself it was safety. It was actually fear wearing a lanyard. And then I came to a town where everybody had been hiding — a witness hiding in a pharmacy, a deputy hiding in a uniform, an archivist hiding in her files, a family hiding inside its own legend — and I watched what it cost them, and I watched what happened when they stopped." They smiled, though no one could see it. "So this season, you get my face. You get my name. You get the fact that I live above a historical society in a fog-bound town I have absolutely no intention of leaving, in an apartment where a dead fern sits in the corner because we don't throw things out here — we find out what they were really holding."

On the desk, the recorder's level meter danced.

"Some cases you close," Sebastian said. "And some cases close you. This is the story of the one that closed me — and the town that, against every expectation, including mine, opened back up."

They let the silence sit for exactly three seconds, the way Mabel had taught them — *a pause is a place where the listener lives* — and then they pressed stop, saved the file, and labeled it with a date and a name and no alias at all.

Outside, the fog was gone. The hills across the river stood sharp and blue in the October light, and somewhere up on the hill, in the dark soil over an empty grave, forty tulip bulbs waited for spring with the patience of everything that had ever survived Meridian Falls.

Sebastian got up, put on a coat, and went downstairs to return Mabel's coffee pot — and found, taped to the archive door, a note in handwriting they didn't recognize:

*Mr. Eriksen — I have something I think you should hear. It's about the winter of 1985, and it's not in any file. I've been waiting forty years to say it out loud. Are you free Sunday?*

It was signed with a name, and the name was one Sebastian had never heard before.

They stood on the archive steps in the clean cold air, the note in their hand, and felt the old hunger stir — gentler now, warmer, but unmistakable.

Some cases close you. And then, if you're lucky, you open again.

Sunday, then. Sebastian folded the note into their pocket, whistled once, low and surprised, and went to buy stronger coffee.
