## 2. Alphabet of the Dead

The archive beneath the cathedral kept its own weather. While Marrow's Bend above shivered through a gray morning — the kind that pressed the fog down into the streets until the lamplighters' brass posts wore halos of damp — the sub-crypt rooms below the north transept held a stillness that never quite warmed. Beatrix Ashgrove had spent eleven years learning that stillness the way other people learned a language: its dialects of dust, its grammar of shadow, the way the stone breathed cold through the floor and exhaled the smell of vellum, iron, and old paper's slow sweet rot.

She had not slept. That was not unusual — she was a woman who slept the way she did most things, sparingly and with a certain suspicion — but this was a different species of sleeplessness. Not the ordinary insomnia of a mind that would not stop sorting. This was the sleeplessness of a mind that had stopped sorting entirely, that sat in the dark of her flat above the vestry offices staring at the ceiling and hearing, over and over, the sound the gravedigger's spade had made when it struck the coffin lid.

A hollow sound. That was what haunted her. Not the dense, muffled knock of wood against wood and earth against wood, but a ringing, empty note, like a bell cracked at the casting. Old Tom Ferrier had heard it too. She'd seen it cross his face — a flicker, quickly buried under forty years of professional indifference — before he'd levered the lid and they had both looked down into a coffin that held no Aldous Vane.

Only a brass key, green with a century of tarnish. And a scrap of ledger paper, folded into a square no larger than a communion wafer.

She had not told the dean about the scrap. She had not told anyone. She had let the coroner's men seal the coffin, let the ceremony proceed — the bishop's prayers, the mayor's speech, the schoolchildren's hymn, all of it performed over a box of nothing while the fog pressed down like a hand — and she had carried the paper home in her glove like a stolen relic, and she had unfolded it at her kitchen table under a single lamp, and she had read it.

One line, in a hand she did not know, brown ink gone the color of dried tea:

*The order holds. A through Q. The rest is silence.*

She had read it eleven times. Then she had put on her coat and her boots and walked back through the sleeping town to the cathedral, because there was only one place in Marrow's Bend where a question could be answered, and it was hers.

The archive's outer room — the public reading room, with its green-shaded lamps and its long oak tables scarred by a century of student initials — she passed through without turning on the lights. The inner stacks required her key. The inner stacks were where the cathedral kept what it did not wish to be found: the suppressed parish registers, the letters of bishops who had died in disgrace, the financial ledgers with their uncomfortable columns, and the coroner's and constable's papers that the town of Marrow's Bend had, in a fit of civic embarrassment sometime in the 1950s, deposited with the church rather than the county, on the understanding — never written, always understood — that they would be consulted rarely and remembered never.

Beatrix unlocked the door with the small iron key she kept on the ring against her breastbone, and stood a moment in the dark, letting her eyes adjust, letting the archive's cold settle over her like a garment.

"Good morning," she said quietly, to the room, because she always did, because the dead deserved courtesy, and because — though she would have denied it under oath — she had never entirely stopped believing that the archive was inhabited. Not by ghosts. By attention. By the accumulated attention of every hand that had ever filed a paper here, a kind of standing pressure, like water behind a dam.

She lit the lamps. She went to the 1920s.

The exhumation records, if they existed, would be filed under the year of the ceremony's authorization or the year of the burial itself — she tried both. The centenary commission's own papers she had catalogued herself, two years ago, and she knew them by heart: invitations, correspondence with the diocesan surveyor, the mason's estimate for resetting the ledger stone. Nothing about the original interment. Nothing about 1924.

But 1924 was a year she knew held a wound. Every archive had one — a year with gaps in it, a year where the folders went thin, where the clerk's hand changed or the numbering stumbled. In Marrow's Bend, 1924 was the year the constable's day-books went from meticulous to absent. She had noticed it years ago and filed the observation in the part of her mind where she kept such things, the way a good archivist keeps anomalies: labeled, dated, unexamined.

Now she pulled the box.

The constable's papers for 1924 sat in a gray archival carton, acid-free folder after acid-free folder, and she went through them the way she went through everything — left to right, top to bottom, no skipping, no assumption. Inquest into a fall from the quarry road. A dispute over tithe payments. A dog license ledger. A report of a drowned cart horse. And then, between the horse and a bundle of road survey notes, a folder that did not belong.

It was the wrong color. Everything else in the box was the county's standard buff manila, faded to the color of weak tea. This folder was darker, heavier stock, the kind the cathedral's own stationer supplied — she knew it intimately, had ordered it herself a hundred times. Someone had taken a folder from her own supplies and put it into the constable's box.

The label, in her own cataloguing ink — her own ink, the iron-gall she mixed herself because commercial inks faded — read: *VANE, A. — INTERMENT & SUBSEQUENT INQUIRY, 1924.*

She did not open it immediately. She stood there with her hand flat on the folder and felt her own pulse in her fingertips, and she made herself do the professional thing first, the thing that had kept her sane through eleven years of other people's secrets: she verified.

She took the folder to the reading table, set it under the lamp, and examined the label the way she would examine a suspect document for the diocese — because that was a thing she did, occasionally, quietly, when a parishioner produced a will or a deed too convenient by half. She tilted the label to the light. She looked at the ink's bloom, the way it had feathered slightly into the paper's grain, the way iron-gall does when it is genuinely old, when it has had decades to oxidize and bite. This ink had bitten. This label had been written decades ago.

And it was her handwriting.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. Identical — the particular forward lean she could never train out of herself, the way her capital V started with a small lead-in stroke like a bird's foot, the way her numerals 4 stood open-topped, the way she crossed her t's with a single flat sweep that sloped down to the right, a habit she had been mocked for in the orphanage schoolroom in Ashcombe and had never corrected because it was the one thing she owned outright, the one thing no institution had given her and no institution could take away.

*VANE, A. — INTERMENT & SUBSEQUENT INQUIRY, 1924.*

Her hand. Her ink. A date fifty years before she was born.

Beatrix Ashgrove had built her entire self on the principle that paper did not lie — that paper was the one honest witness in a world of unreliable flesh — and now the paper was lying to her in her own voice, and she found that her hands, when she finally lifted the folder's flap, were not steady, and that the cold of the archive had gotten inside her coat, inside her skin, into the marrow the town was named for.

Inside: a coroner's report on the death of Aldous Vane, dated the 14th of March, 1924. Cause of death: cardiac failure, certified by Dr. E. Loomis. A burial permit. An order of service. And then — this was where the folder stopped behaving like a record and started behaving like an accusation — a second document, unnumbered, on a different paper stock, a constable's memorandum headed with the words *PRIVATE — NOT FOR FILE*, and beneath them, a narrative.

She read it twice. The first time her eyes slid off it like rain off glass. The second time it went in.

*Received information this 2nd day of June from H. Pike, sexton, that the deceased Mr. Aldous Vane was observed by him at the railway station on the evening of the 30th of May last, in apparent good health, purchasing a ticket under the name —*

The name had been struck through. Not blotted, not faded — deliberately struck through with a heavy line, and beside it, in the margin, in that same impossible hand, her hand, a single word:

*Withheld.*

And below the memorandum, three more annotations, dated across the summer of 1924, each in that hand, each initialled with letters she did not use. She stared at the initials a long time. *B.A.* Her own initials. Beatrix Anne Ashgrove — the Anne her mother had given her before giving her up, the one fact of her origins she had carried like a coin sewn into a hem.

The last annotation was dated the 11th of September, 1924, and it said only:

*File closed by order. The order holds.*

The order holds. The same words, the same phrase, as the scrap in the coffin. A phrase that had waited a hundred years in the dark for her to read it, written in her own hand before her grandmother was born.

She became aware, gradually, of the sound of her own breathing in the stone room, too loud, too fast. She became aware of the lamp's hiss. She became aware that she was cold in a way that had nothing to do with the sub-crypt's temperature, and that the archive — her archive, her ordered, catalogued, trustworthy world — had just shown her its teeth.

She did what archivists do. She closed the folder. She squared its edges. She returned it to the box, in its place, between the drowned horse and the road survey, and she turned off the lamps one by one, and she locked the inner door, and she climbed the stairs into the fog-bound morning carrying the whole impossible thing inside her like a swallowed stone.

She did not see the figure at the top of the stairs, in the shadow of the north transept, watching.

* * *

Detective Inspector Ilsa Reyes had been in Marrow's Bend for six weeks, and in six weeks she had learned three things about the town. One: it was beautiful the way a painted egg is beautiful, all surface and shell. Two: everyone she needed to interview was either elderly, evasive, or both. Three: nothing here was ever said plainly when it could be said through an intermediary, a tradition, or a silence.

She had come for the exhumation the way she came to everything — early, plainclothes, and skeptical. The centenary of a founder's burial was not police business, but the coroner's office had asked for an observer — standard procedure when a coffin that old was to be opened, in case of anything — and Reyes had volunteered because the alternative was a morning of paperwork and because something about the file the coroner's clerk had shown her had itched. A town built by one man. A coffin opened after a hundred years. She had seen enough small towns to know that the bigger the statue in the square, the bigger the thing buried under it.

And then the coffin had come up empty, and the itch had become an interest.

She had watched the archivist — Ashgrove, the tall gray-eyed woman with the ink-stained fingers who moved through the ceremony like a metronome — when the lid came off. Everyone else had made noise: the gasp, the murmur, the bishop's voice cracking on like a machine that hadn't been told. Ashgrove had made no noise at all. She had looked into the coffin for perhaps four seconds, and in those four seconds Reyes had watched a face do something very controlled and very expensive: the face of a person deciding, in real time, how much of what they know to let onto the surface.

Reyes had made a note. *A. Ashgrove — saw it coming, or saw something else.*

Now she stood in the north transept at seven in the morning, having let herself in with the dean's cheerful permission — *the cathedral belongs to everyone, Inspector* — ostensibly admiring the stained glass, actually watching the archive door at the bottom of the stairs, which had been locked when she arrived and had been unlocked, briefly, at half past five, by someone who moved like they owned the dark.

Ashgrove came up the stairs into the fog-light, and Reyes noted the details the way she always did, automatically, the habit of twenty years: coat buttoned wrong at the second button. Eyes with the particular rawness of a sleepless night. Ink on the right middle finger, fresh. And a way of walking — this was the interesting part — a way of walking like a woman carrying something breakable, except nothing was in her hands.

"Inspector Reyes." Ashgrove saw her and did not startle, which Reyes noted too. "You're up early. The glass is best around nine, when the sun gets through the east windows. What you're looking at now is mostly lead."

"I like lead," Reyes said. "It holds things together. Can I ask you about the exhumation?"

"You can ask."

"Your reaction yesterday. When the coffin opened. You weren't surprised."

Something moved behind the archivist's eyes — not guilt, Reyes thought, and she was good at guilt; more like a door being held shut against wind. "I was surprised," Ashgrove said. "I simply process surprises quietly. It's a professional habit. Archives punish drama."

"Do they." Reyes let a silence sit. She had learned in six weeks that Marrow's Bend hated silence more than any town she'd worked, that its people would fill a silence with almost anything, and that the almost-anything was always worth hearing. But Ashgrove filled it with nothing. She stood in the fog-colored light with her hands folded and let the silence be silence, and Reyes revised her note: *Not a townie. Trained patience. Trained by what?*

"There's a rumor going round," Reyes said instead, "that the coffin was robbed. Sometime in the last century. Grave-robbers, resurrection men, teenagers on a dare. The dean's office would very much like it to be grave-robbers. It's tidy. It closes the question." She paused. "You've catalogued this archive for eleven years, I'm told. Is there anything in it — any record, any rumor, any anomaly — that would support grave-robbers?"

The pause that followed lasted three seconds, and Reyes counted them, and in those three seconds she watched Beatrix Ashgrove decide something. She watched the decision happen the way you watch weather change over water — a darkening, a shift, and then the surface smooth again.

"Nothing," Ashgrove said. "The archive holds nothing about the coffin. I checked."

*The archive holds nothing about the coffin. I checked.* Reyes turned the sentence over all the way back to the police house, and by the time she reached her desk by the window with its view of the market square and its statue of Aldous Vane, arm raised in permanent benefaction, she had taken it apart and put it back together twice, and both times it came out the same way.

Ashgrove hadn't said *no.* She hadn't said *there's no such record.* She'd said *the archive holds nothing* — a statement about the archive, not the truth — and *I checked* — past tense, completed, this morning, before seven, in a locked room she'd entered in the dark.

Reyes pulled the thin folder she'd started on the exhumation and wrote, in her own blunt hand that no one would ever mistake for anyone else's:

*A. Ashgrove knows what was in that coffin besides a key. She knew before the lid came off, or she learned it between yesterday and 5:30 this morning. Either way, she's not telling. Question: why does the town archivist lie like a professional?*

She sat back and looked out at the statue through the rain-freckled glass. Aldous Vane, cast in bronze, gazing forever at the town he'd built, and Reyes thought about the empty coffin and the tidy theory and the dean's office wanting so very much for the question to be closed, and she wrote one more line at the bottom of the page, underlined twice:

*Who benefits from an empty coffin staying empty?*

She was still looking at the statue when the desk sergeant came in with the morning's post, and among the circulars and the licensing renewals was a plain envelope, hand-delivered, no stamp, addressed in a careful forward-leaning hand to *Detective Inspector I. Reyes* — and inside, a single sheet, on which someone had written, in that same hand, the words:

*Some files close themselves. Advise you let this one.*

Reyes read it three times. Then she got out her magnifier, and her evidence bag, and she began — carefully, professionally, with the particular pleasure she took in the moment a case stopped being a rumor and became a thing with edges — to examine the handwriting of the person who had just made her first mistake.
