## 7. Second on the List

The organ had a cold that belonged to no other room in Marrow's Bend Cathedral. It rose from the stone flags and pooled beneath the console like standing water, and after forty years Marguerite Cole had learned to dress for it as one dresses for weather — wool skirt, fingerless gloves, a shawl her mother had knitted before her mother had learned to be disappointed in her. She arrived at six each morning, when the light through the clerestory was still blue and uncommitted, and she played until her hands remembered they were hands.

This morning she did not play.

She sat on the bench with her gloves off and the note open in her lap, and she listened instead to the building breathe — the tick of cooling pipes, the settling of timber in the tower, the small industrious sounds of a town not yet awake. The note lay on her knee the way a dead bird lies: too still, too light for what it meant.

*You were there,* it said, in handwriting she knew better than her own. *Harold was first. You are second. The ledger remembers what you would rather forget.*

She had read it eleven times. She had counted. Eleven times, because counting was what she did instead of screaming; forty years at the console taught you that a phrase repeated becomes architecture, that terror set to rhythm becomes merely another tempo you can survive. And so she counted, and by the eleventh reading the handwriting had stopped being the worst part and started being the only part.

Because it was Beatrix's hand. There was no mistaking it. That upright, patient script, each letter seated in its place like a chorister in a stall — the peculiar way Beatrix crossed her *t*s with a single unhurried stroke, the *g* with its flat, closed descender, the small archaic flourish on capital letters that Marguerite had teased her about for years. *You write like someone apologizing to the nineteenth century,* she'd said once, over tea in the vestry, and Beatrix had smiled her narrow smile and said, *Someone has to keep the records legible for whoever comes after us.*

Whoever came after us. Marguerite looked at the note again and felt the words rearrange themselves into something older and colder. *The ledger remembers.* Harold Pike was dead nine days, laid out and buried while the whole town murmured about ladders and bad knees and a man of seventy-four who should have known better than to climb alone. Staged, Reyes had said at the funeral, though not loudly, and not to Marguerite, and not in words — but Ilsa Reyes had a way of looking at coffins that said more than sermons.

And now this. In Beatrix's hand.

Marguerite folded the note along its original crease, which was itself an answer of sorts — she had already decided, some hours ago in the grey dark of her kitchen, that she would not go to the police first. She would go to Beatrix. Because that was what family did. Because the cathedral staff was not staff, whatever the payroll said; it was the only family any of them had chosen or been chosen by, and families did not begin with constables. Families began with the terrible question asked face to face.

She put the note in her music bag, beneath the Bach, and went to find her friend.

* * *

Beatrix Ashgrove could be found, at that hour, exactly where the archive's dust found her: kneeling in the east stacks with a torch clipped to her cardigan and a pencil behind her ear, cataloguing a bequest of parish magazines from 1911. Marguerite stood in the doorway a long moment before speaking, watching her — really watching her, the way one listens to a familiar hymn played in an unfamiliar key, hunting for the note that has gone wrong.

Beatrix looked tired. Not guilty-tired; Marguerite had seen guilt before, on faces in the congregation, on her own once, long ago, in a mirror in a house she did not revisit. Guilt had a shine to it, a sweat. This was only exhaustion, the grey kind, the kind that comes from sleeping badly in a house where every sound is a question. Her hair was pinned carelessly. There was graphite on her cheekbone. She looked, in short, like Beatrix — like the woman who had arrived in Marrow's Bend twenty years ago with two suitcases and no surname worth keeping, and who had built herself, shelf by shelf, into the load-bearing wall of all their lives.

"Marguerite." Beatrix looked up, and something in her face flickered — relief, Marguerite thought, or its twin. "You're early. I thought we had lunch."

"We did." Marguerite came down the aisle slowly, her shoes loud on the flagstones in a way she resented. "Plans change. Sit up, dear. I can't interrogate anyone who's kneeling; it puts me at a moral disadvantage."

Beatrix rose, brushing dust from her knees, and waited. That was the thing about her — she never filled silence prematurely. She treated silence as a document to be read.

Marguerite took out the note. She held it a moment longer than necessary, giving Beatrix time to see the paper, the fold, the shape of what was coming. Then she passed it across.

"I received this yesterday," she said. "Under my door, sometime between compline and dawn. I'd like you to tell me what you make of it."

She watched Beatrix read. Watched the colour leave her face in stages, like a tide going out — first the lips, then the cheeks, then the skin around the eyes, so that by the end Beatrix seemed lit from within by something pallid. Her hand, holding the note, did not shake. That frightened Marguerite more than shaking would have. Innocent people shook. Innocent people said *what is this, whose writing is this, how horrible.* Beatrix said nothing at all, and her stillness had the quality of recognition.

"You know whose hand this is," Marguerite said quietly.

"It's mine." Beatrix's voice came out level and strange. "Or rather — it isn't. But it's my hand. Someone has written this as if — " She stopped. Started again, slower, choosing each word like a step across ice. "I didn't write this, Marguerite. I have never seen it before this moment. But the hand is mine. The letterforms. The crossing of the t's. Whoever wrote it has studied my handwriting for a long time."

"And the 1924 file?" Marguerite had heard the story secondhand, as the whole staff had, in the way news moves through a close household — pieced from half-conversations in the refectory. "The annotations in your hand, in a document from before you were born. Edmund told us. He asked us — gently, in his way — to give you room. To let you work. He said you were under a strain."

Something moved through Beatrix's face then, quick and involuntary, at the mention of the dean's name — a flinch so small that a stranger would have missed it, but Marguerite had spent twenty years learning to read this woman the way she read scores, and nothing in her was illegible. Strain, yes. But also something else. Something that looked, horribly, like fear of the wrong person.

"Edmund has been very kind," Beatrix said, in a voice that made *kind* sound like a word under investigation.

"Yes," Marguerite said. "He has. He's been kind to both of us for twenty years, which is why I am here asking you this directly instead of telephoning Detective Reyes, whom I do not know, and do not trust, and who looks at me as though I'm a suspect in a play she hasn't finished reading." She drew breath. The next part had been rehearsed on the walk down, in the cold, and it still came out rougher than intended. "So I will ask it plainly, Beatrix, and I ask you to answer plainly, because we are too old and have been through too much together for anything else. Did you write this note? Did you write those annotations? Is there something happening in you — grief, illness, some breakage — that you've hidden from us?"

"No."

It came fast and clean, without the hesitation that would have made it a lie. Marguerite searched her friend's eyes and found them steady, wet at the rims but steady, and felt the first hairline crack appear in the certainty she had carried since dawn — the certainty that had been, if she was honest, a kind of comfort. Because if Beatrix had done it, then the world still had rules. A friend had broken; friends could be grieved, forgiven, or cut away. But if Beatrix had not done it —

Then someone else had. Someone who knew Beatrix's hand intimately. Someone with years of access to the archive, to the staff, to the doors of old women's houses. Someone who wanted Marguerite Cole to look at her dearest friend and see a monster wearing her handwriting.

"Then someone is doing this to you," Marguerite said slowly. "To both of us."

"Someone is doing it to all of us." Beatrix set the note down on the catalogue table with exaggerated care, as though it might detonate. "Harold Pike died nine days ago, and everyone wants it to be a ladder. I don't think it was a ladder. I think — " She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes for a moment, and when she lowered them she was the archivist again, precise and pale. "There's a ledger. An old one, from the 1920s, a list of names connected to Aldous Vane's estate. Harold's name is in it. Yours is in it. Mine is in it. Ada Quill's. The names are in alphabetical order, and Harold was the first living name on the list, and he is dead, and I catalogued that ledger myself last month, and I cannot stop thinking that I may have handed someone a programme."

The word *programme* landed in Marguerite's chest like a dropped hymnal. She thought of the order of service, the neat printed sequence, everything in its place, everyone knowing their cue.

"That's absurd," she said, and heard how thin it sounded. "That's — Beatrix, that's the sort of thing that happens in novels. Nobody murders their way through an alphabet."

"Somebody murdered their way through Harold Pike," Beatrix said. "Somebody wrote this note in my hand. Somebody annotated a ninety-year-old file with letters that hadn't been invented yet. I agree it sounds like a novel. I have begun to wish I lived in a less well-plotted town."

Marguerite turned away, walked to the window at the end of the stack, and looked out at the close, where the morning fog was lifting off the grass in rags. Below, two vergers were stacking chairs from last night's meeting, their movements small and ordinary and suddenly precious. She thought: *any of them.* She thought: *the dean.* And was ashamed of the thought even as it formed, and more ashamed of how easily it had formed, how little resistance her mind offered. Nine days ago she had trusted everyone in this building with her life and her keys. Now she caught herself inventorying them — who had access to the archive, who knew her morning routine, who knew that she left the organ loft door unlatched because the key stuck and she'd complained about it for a decade and no one had ever fixed it.

That was the true poison in the note, she realized. Not the threat. The threat was almost restful — a defined enemy, a defined danger, something one could report and guard against. No: the poison was that it had made her stand in a room with Beatrix Ashgrove and wonder. Had made betrayal arrive before any betrayal occurred, a guest ahead of its host. Whatever else happened, whoever was caught or convicted, that couldn't be undone. You could repair a friendship the way you repaired a cracked bell, but it rang differently afterward. Everyone in the tower could hear it.

"Marguerite." Beatrix had come to stand beside her, not touching — Beatrix never touched first; twenty years and she still asked permission with her nearness. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide what I am. The brass key from the coffin. I tried it. On the inner crypt door. It fits a lock that was installed after Vane was buried. I haven't told Reyes yet. I haven't told Edmund. You're the first."

"Why?" The word escaped before Marguerite could dress it. "Why am I the first, if you trust me so completely? Why tell me now, today, minutes after I put a death threat in your hands?"

"Because you brought me the note instead of the police," Beatrix said simply. "That bought you the truth. Truth is the only currency I have left, and I'm spending it where it earns interest."

Marguerite laughed — a short, appalled sound, entirely against her will. "You dreadful creature. Even now you're filing me."

"Everyone gets filed eventually." But Beatrix was smiling, faintly, crookedly, and for a moment they were only themselves again: two women past sixty standing in a cold room full of other people's paper, holding the fort against the dark the best way they knew, which was to name things accurately.

Then the moment passed, as moments do, and Marguerite looked down at the note where it lay on the table between them, and the fraying showed itself plainly. Because she understood — she understood with terrible clarity — that she would never again receive a message from Beatrix without checking the hand. That when Beatrix next wrote to her, warm and ordinary, some traitor corner of her mind would hold the page to the light. That the note had accomplished, with eight lines of borrowed ink, what twenty years of friendship might not survive.

"Do you know what hurts most?" Marguerite said. "Not the threat. I'm sixty-eight, dear; threats and I have come to an arrangement. What hurts is that whoever wrote this knew precisely what they were doing. They didn't want to frighten me. They wanted to separate us. They wanted me standing in this room looking at you and seeing the forger instead of the friend. And the wretched thing is—" her voice caught, and she hated it, hated the tremor in it, the organist's vibrato betraying her at the worst possible cadence— "the wretched thing is that it worked. Some part of me will always be standing here, looking. You understand that. You must understand that."

Beatrix nodded slowly. "I understand," she said. "I'd be looking too." She picked up the note, folded it, and held it out. "Keep this. Don't give it to Reyes yet — let me show her the 1924 annotations first, side by side, so she sees the pattern before she sees the accusation. And Marguerite — lock your doors. All of them. Latch the loft. Stop being predictable, starting tonight."

"Starting tonight," Marguerite repeated. "And until tonight?"

"Until tonight," Beatrix said, "we are two women having a perfectly normal morning in the archive, and no one watching us — and someone is always watching us now — learns that the second name on the list knows she's on it."

They walked out together through the stacks, past the parish magazines of 1911, past the ledgers of the dead and the nearly dead, and at the door Marguerite paused and looked back at her friend — at the graphite on her cheek, the tired eyes, the upright spine of her — and made herself say it aloud, because some things must be said while they are still true:

"Whatever happens, Beatrix. Whoever did this. Find them before they finish the alphabet."

Behind them, far above in the tower, the bells began the hour — and Marguerite, who had tuned those bells herself, heard in the third stroke a faint new crack, high and thin, that had not been there nine days ago.
