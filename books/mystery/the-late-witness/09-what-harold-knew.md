## 9. What Harold Knew

Detective Ilsa Reyes had learned to read a town the way other people read a face — the set of the jaw, the twitch at the corner of the eye that meant a lie was being swallowed whole. Marrow's Bend had been lying to her since the day she arrived, three years ago, with a transfer letter and a bad knee and a promise to herself that this posting would be quiet. The town lied politely, in the way of cathedral towns: with cassocks and flower shows and a smile that said *nothing here has ever gone wrong, officer, not once in a hundred years.*

Now it was lying badly.

She parked outside the Pike cottage on Corder Lane just after eight, when the light came low and amber through the yew hedges and made even the shabbiest houses look like paintings of themselves. Harold Pike's house was the shabbiest on the lane and did not benefit from the light. Paint peeling in long curls from the doorframe. A garden gone to seed except for one stubborn row of marigolds that someone — Harold, presumably, before he became a body on the bell-tower floor — had kept alive out of pure habit.

The constable on watch nodded her through. "Nothing's been touched inside, ma'am. Well. Except by the sister."

"Sister?"

"Comes every morning. Sits with him." The constable hesitated. "He's not there, ma'am. She sits with the chair."

Reyes filed that away, the way she filed everything now, in the drawer at the back of her mind marked *Marrow's Bend is stranger than you think.* It was getting crowded in there.

* * *

Edith Pike was seventy-some, small as a wren, with hands that had clearly spent a lifetime doing work no one thanked them for. She sat in her brother's front room beside an empty armchair, a cup of tea going cold on the table between them, poured for a man who would never drink it. When Reyes sat down across from her, Edith looked up with the flat, unsurprised eyes of someone who had already grieved everything worth grieving and was simply waiting for the rest.

"You're the detective," she said. Not a question.

"I'm Detective Reyes. I'm sorry about your brother."

"He fell." Edith's voice was dry as old paper. "That's what they're saying. Fell down the tower stairs. Harold climbed those stairs twice a day for forty years. He knew every one of them like his own teeth. He could have done it blind drunk in the dark, and Harold never touched a drop." She turned the teacup a quarter-inch on its saucer, precise as a clock hand. "You don't believe he fell either. I can see it in your face. You've got the same look Beatrix Ashgrove gets."

"And what look is that?"

"The look of someone counting." Edith smiled without any warmth in it. "Harold counted too. All his life. Steps, bells, names. He'd have liked you, I think. He didn't like most people. He liked people who noticed things."

Reyes leaned forward. "Mrs. Pike — Edith — I need to ask you about 1924."

The quarter-inch turn of the teacup stopped.

"You were a child then," Reyes went on, gently. "But you lived in this house. Your brother was already sexton. And I think something happened that year that this town decided, all together, to forget. I think Harold never forgot it. I think he kept it, the way he kept everything — counted and ordered and put away. And I think whatever he kept got him killed."

For a long moment the only sound was the mantel clock, ticking with the patient authority of furniture that had outlasted everyone who wound it.

Then Edith Pike stood, crossed to the writing desk in the corner, unlocked the bottom drawer with a key she wore on a ribbon around her neck, and brought back a tin biscuit box so old its painted roses had faded to ghosts of pink.

"Harold told me," she said, setting it on the table between them like a bomb being defused, "that if anyone ever came asking about 1924, I was to give them this. He said it the week before he died. He'd never said such a thing in ninety-one years. That's how I knew, before they ever told me, that he hadn't fallen." Her hands, steady until now, trembled once. "He said, *Edie, if they come, tell them the founder caught his train.*"

* * *

Inside the biscuit box: a railway timetable from October 1924, soft-cornered with handling; a photograph, creased twice; and a single sheet of paper covered in Harold Pike's spidery, meticulous hand — the handwriting of a man who had spent decades keeping registers, every letter formed with the care of a stone being laid.

Reyes put on gloves before she touched any of it. Beatrix would have insisted, and for once Reyes found herself agreeing with the archivist's liturgies.

The photograph showed the platform at Marrow's Bend station, taken from the footbridge. Ordinary enough — a porter with a barrow, a woman in a cloche hat, steam. But someone had drawn a small, careful circle around a man standing at the far end of the platform, half-turned toward the departing train. Tall. Broad-shouldered under a good overcoat. His face was in profile, blurred by the camera's slow shutter, but the posture was unmistakable to anyone who had seen the portrait of Aldous Vane that hung in the cathedral nave: the same carriage of the shoulders, the same way of standing as though the world had been built slightly too small for him.

On the back, in pencil, in that same spidery hand: *A.V. — Oct. 14th, 1924. Six weeks after we buried the empty box. Boarding the 4:10 under the name MERRICK.*

Reyes felt it happen — the thing she lived for and could never admit she lived for, because detectives were supposed to be instruments of procedure, not appetite. The click. The moment a case stops being a folder of sad paperwork and becomes a living animal with a pulse. Six weeks. The funeral had been the first week of September 1924. She'd checked the parish register herself, twice, unable to shake the archivist's insistence that the dates mattered.

Six weeks after burying an empty coffin, Aldous Vane had stood on a platform in broad daylight and boarded a train under a new name — and Harold Pike had seen him do it, and photographed him, and then folded the evidence into a biscuit tin and kept silent for a hundred years.

"Why?" Reyes asked aloud, though she was looking at Edith. "Why not go to the police? Why keep it hidden?"

Edith's laugh was short and bitter. "You've met this town, Detective. Who would he have gone to? The police magistrate took Vane's money for the new courthouse. The doctor's surgery was built with Vane bequests. Half the shops on the high street have a little brass plaque saying *the gift of Aldous Vane.*" She tapped the photograph. "Harold used to say the founder wasn't dead, he was *everywhere* — in the stones, in the ledgers, in the plaques. You can't report a ghost to men the ghost pays. So Harold did what sextons do. He buried it. Kept it deep and kept it quiet and kept count."

"And the name. Merrick. Did Harold ever learn anything more about—"

"Merrick." Edith said the name the way you'd spit out a stone from bread. "Oh, he learned plenty. Everyone learned plenty, eventually. Merrick & Sons Shipping. Merrick Hall — that's the school, isn't it, where the dean was raised. Merrick Wing at the hospital. Merrick Fountain on the green, with the little brass plaque." Her mouth twisted. "The gift of Josiah Merrick. Harold used to stand at that fountain some mornings, just looking at it. I asked him once why. He said, *Because I'm the only one in this town drinking from a lie, Edie, and somebody ought to know the taste.*"

Reyes copied the penciled inscription into her notebook, letter by letter, though she hardly needed to. It was already burned in. *Boarding the 4:10 under the name MERRICK.*

The founder hadn't died. The founder had *rebranded*. And the fortune that flowed back into Marrow's Bend for a century — the school, the hospital wing, the fountain, God knew what else — was the vanished man watering the ground he'd fled, buying himself a town full of people who would never dream of digging up his secret because their livelihoods were planted in it.

And somewhere in that century of quiet money, a boy named Edmund had grown up at Merrick Hall, and become Dean Vane, and inherited — what? Just the money?

Or the secret too?

* * *

She found Beatrix Ashgrove in the archive, of course. Reyes had a theory forming that the woman actually slept there, curled among the card catalogs like a fox in a den it had dug itself. The lamps were on against the grey afternoon, and Beatrix stood at the long reading table surrounded by shipping registers, her hair escaping its clip, a pencil tucked behind her ear like a carpenter's.

"You've heard," Reyes said.

"I heard you went to Edith Pike." Beatrix didn't look up. "I also heard you came out with a biscuit tin. The constable's sister works in the bakery on Corder Lane. This town has a nervous system faster than the telegraph ever managed." She finally raised her head, and Reyes saw the sleeplessness in her face, the bruised skin under her eyes. Two deaths. One forged note in her own handwriting pinned to a drowned woman's robe. The archivist was running on order and willpower alone, and both were nearly spent. "Tell me anyway. I want to hear it from the source."

Reyes laid the photograph on the table between the shipping registers. Then the timetable. Then, last, Harold's page of careful script.

Beatrix read in silence. Reyes watched her do it — watched the professional stillness hold for perhaps ten seconds, and then crack along fault lines the detective was beginning to know by heart: the breath drawn through the nose, the fingers flattening on the table as if steadying paper that wasn't moving.

"October fourteenth," Beatrix said at last, very quietly. "I can check the burial register against the parish calendar, but — six weeks. He let them bury an empty coffin, say the prayers, carve the stone. He stood in that nave, Ilsa, or he didn't, but either way he let a whole town mourn him while he bought a train ticket." Her voice thinned. "That's not a man fleeing scandal. Scandal runs at night with a bag. That's a man conducting his own funeral like a rehearsal, checking his blocking, making sure the play lands."

"That's one reading."

"It's the only reading that fits the lock." Beatrix pulled a register toward her, opened it with the practiced violence of someone who handled rare books daily and had stopped being gentle about it. "Josiah Merrick. If that's the name, the manifests will show it. Shipping interests appear in port records, insurance filings, company registrations—" She stopped. Looked up. Something moved behind her eyes that Reyes recognized from their first meeting over the empty coffin: dread wearing competence as a coat. "The Merrick benefactions. The school. The hospital wing. The dean's own education, Ilsa. Edmund Vane was raised at Merrick Hall on Merrick money. If Aldous Vane became Josiah Merrick, then the dean isn't just the founder's great-grandson by blood and legend. He's the heir to the *vanishing*. To the method. To the whole architecture of the lie."

"Or he knows nothing about it," Reyes said. "Inherited money doesn't come with a confession attached."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "It comes with something better. It comes with lawyers, and trustees, and family papers, and a great-grandmother's letters, and a hundred years of Christmas stories about wise Grandpapa Josiah. It comes with everything a curious boy would need to piece it together by the time he was twenty." She pressed her palms flat against the table. "I catalogued this archive for eleven years, Detective. I know what's in the restricted stacks. And I know that the 1924 file — the file with my handwriting in it, the file the dean asked me to lose — is missing exactly the pages that would name Josiah Merrick."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere above them, in the tower, workmen were still repairing the stair rail where Harold Pike had died, and the sound of their hammers came down through the stone like a slow heartbeat.

"So we follow the money," Reyes said. "Port records. Company registrations. If Josiah Merrick was real, he left a paper trail a mile wide, and paper is your country, not mine."

"Yes." But Beatrix wasn't looking at the registers anymore. She was looking at the photograph — at the circled figure on the platform, tall and unhurried, boarding a train while his own funeral flowers wilted behind him. "Ilsa. There's something else. Something I haven't said, because saying it makes it real." She drew a breath. "The ledger. My ledger — the one I keep of the elders' testimony, the oral history project. Harold Pike was first on it. Marguerite Cole second. Alphabetical. And now Harold is dead, and Marguerite is dead, and the pages are turning in order."

"I know."

"Do you understand what that means?" Beatrix's voice dropped to almost nothing. "It means whoever is killing them isn't working from memory or grudge. They're working from *my book*. From a list I wrote, in a room I control, in a building where the dean has walked past my desk every day for eleven years." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "Harold saw the founder catch his train. Marguerite — Marguerite played the funeral mass, Ilsa. In 1924 her mother played it. The Cole family has kept the organ and the organist's papers for a century. Whatever Harold knew, Marguerite's family kept the *music* of that day. And the next name on my list—"

She couldn't finish it. She didn't have to. Reyes was already flipping forward in her own mind through the alphabet of Marrow's Bend elders, and arriving, with a cold settling in her chest, at the same place Beatrix had.

Ada Quill. Housekeeper. Keeper of the dean's household for forty years. Keeper, if half the gossip in this town was true, of everyone's secrets — including, quite possibly, the letters of a certain great-grandmother, and the confidences of a boy who grew up at Merrick Hall learning exactly what his grandfather had done.

The hammers overhead struck on, patient as a countdown.

"We need to get to Ada Quill," Reyes said, "before the list does."

Beatrix was already reaching for her coat.
