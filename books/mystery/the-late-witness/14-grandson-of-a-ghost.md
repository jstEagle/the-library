## 14. Grandson of a Ghost

Detective Ilsa Reyes had learned, over eleven years of homicide work, that the truth rarely announced itself. It seeped. It came in through the cracks of a case the way damp came through old stone — slowly, invisibly, until one morning you pressed your hand to the wall and it crumbled.

She was pressing her hand to a wall now, figuratively speaking, and it was crumbling under her fingers in the county records office in Ashford, forty minutes' drive from Marrow's Bend, surrounded by the smell of dust and toner and the particular despair of civil servants who had been asked to do more with less since before she was born.

"Shipping manifests," she said to the clerk, a young man named Dev with reading glasses pushed up into his hair. "Coastal freight, 1924 through 1931. Outbound from Marrow's Bend or the nearest port."

"You know how many manifests that is?"

"I know you're going to help me anyway, because I brought biscuits." She set the tin on his desk. Her mother's recipe, almond and anise, the kind of thing that made grown men cooperative past all reason.

Dev sighed the sigh of the conquered and pulled up the digitized index.

Reyes had come here because Beatrix Ashgrove had come here first — or rather, because Beatrix had left a trail like a woman walking through snow. The archivist had spent three evenings last week pulling records, and every record she'd touched had a request slip in her small, upright hand: *Vane, A. — estate settlement. Vane, A. — probate. Marrow's Bend Cathedral Trust — benefactor correspondence.* Reyes had photographed each slip with the mild guilt of a woman spying on someone she was starting to trust, and then she had followed the trail herself, because the trail led somewhere Beatrix hadn't gone yet.

It led to a name.

*Hollis.*

The name appeared first in a shipping manifest dated the 14th of November, 1924 — six weeks after Aldous Vane had been lowered into the ground before three hundred mourners, six weeks after the entire town had watched a coffin sealed with wax and ribbon and grief. A passenger manifest, coastal steamer *Corvid*, out of Greyhaven port. One traveler listed in a hand that trembled slightly at the descenders:

**Mr. Edmund Hollis. Age 41. Gentleman. Luggage: two trunks, one strongbox.**

Aldous Vane had been fifty-one years old when he died. Or when he didn't die. Reyes stared at the entry for a long time, letting the arithmetic settle in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. Forty-one. He'd taken twenty years off himself along with his name. He'd boarded a boat with a strongbox full of whatever he could carry of his own fortune, and he had let a town bury an empty box and weep over it, and he had never once looked back.

Or so it seemed. Because Hollis came back.

***

Dev found him in the land registry, eventually — Hollis, E., purchasing property. First a house on the coast at Wrenmouth. Then, in 1929, land. Land in Marrow's Bend itself, bought through a solicitor in Ashford, bought quietly, bought by a gentleman who had never lived there but who paid cash and asked no favors.

And then the donations began.

Reyes sat in the records office as the afternoon light went amber through the frosted windows, and she built it piece by piece on a legal pad, the way she built everything, one fact laid against another until the shape emerged and she could no longer pretend it wasn't what it was.

1929: The Hollis Charitable Trust established, "for the moral and educational improvement of the town of Marrow's Bend." Endowment: considerable.

1931: The cathedral roof restored. Plaque: *By the generosity of a friend of this parish.* No name. Of course no name. You don't put your own name on a gift to the church that buried you.

1934: The school. St. Aldous's School — and Reyes actually laughed aloud at that, a short, ugly sound that made Dev look up — founded with Hollis money and named for the man whose corpse it commemorated. A school built by a ghost and dedicated to his own headstone.

1936 through 1958: endowments, scholarships, the organ fund, the crypt restoration — *the crypt restoration,* she wrote, and underlined it twice — the clock tower, the almshouses, the library where Beatrix Ashgrove had spent her whole working life shelving books bought with dead-man's money.

Half the town, Beatrix had said. Half the town built by the vanished founder. And now Reyes could put a signature to it. Every deed, every trust instrument, every charitable filing carried the same authorizing mark, the same looping initials: **E.H.**

Edmund Hollis.

Except — and here was where the stone dropped all the way down the well and hit water — Edmund Hollis the passenger on the *Corvid* would have been seventy-five by 1958, and the trust filings from that year bore a signature still firm and fluent. So either the founder lived to a remarkable age on top of everything else, or —

Or there had been a second Edmund. A son. And after him, perhaps, a grandson.

Reyes flipped to the genealogical files Dev had grudgingly unearthed for her, parish registers and civil records stitched together by some long-dead county researcher with more patience than sense. There it was, in the marriage register of a coastal parish at Wrenmouth, 1927: *Edmund Hollis, gentleman, of this parish, to Constance Merrilees.* A son born 1930: *Edmund Merrilees Hollis.* The son had married in 1952 — a local girl, a Marrow's Bend girl, a Vane connection smoothed over with a new surname — and their son, born 1955, christened at St. Aldous's School chapel with the Hollis Trust paying for the christening feast—

*Edmund.*

Three generations of Edmunds, like a bell struck over and over, the same note ringing down the years. The founder had named his son after himself and his grandson after them both, and the grandson had come home to Marrow's Bend wearing the family's oldest name and its newest fortune, and the town had welcomed him without knowing they were welcoming back the man they'd buried.

Dean Edmund Vane.

Who was not, strictly, a Vane at all. Who was a Hollis three times over. Who had taken his great-grandfather's original surname — because that was the final joke, wasn't it, Reyes thought; Vane was the real name, Hollis was the mask, and the boy had chosen to wear the face underneath — and had been ordained, and had risen, and had become the shepherd of the flock his ghost of a grandfather had fleeced.

She sat back. The records office was nearly empty now; Dev had gone for tea; the radiators ticked like something counting.

Horror, she thought, is supposed to have a face. Fangs, or a mask, or at least a stranger's eyes. But horror wearing a familiar face — horror that had held doors open for her, that had offered her tea in his study with both hands wrapped around his own cup like a man who needed warming, that had said *I trust you'll find whoever did this, Detective, for the sake of the town* — that was a different animal entirely. That was the kind of horror that got comfortable. That got *loved.*

That got defended by archivists who called him family.

***

She drove back through the dark with the legal pad on the passenger seat, weighted down with the biscuit tin so it wouldn't slide, and she did not turn on the radio because she wanted to think, and thinking, for her, had always meant silence and the white lines coming at her like a rosary being counted.

The chain of custody ran clean. Aldous Vane fakes his death in October 1924 — with help, and she'd get to the help later, because Harold Pike's name was sitting in that 1924 file like a lit fuse — takes ship as Edmund Hollis, marries, has a son, builds a second fortune on the skeleton of the first, and spends thirty years funneling it back into the town that thinks him dead. Why? Guilt? Sentiment? Control? She'd known men who faked deaths for debt and men who faked them for love, but she'd never known one who faked a death and then spent the rest of his life buying his way back into the graveyard of his own reputation. Unless it wasn't sentiment. Unless it was insurance. Unless a man who knew he'd be remembered as a saint wanted to make very sure the record stayed exactly as he'd written it.

And then the son dies — car accident, 1961, she'd check — and the grandson inherits. Not just the money. The secret. Because a secret like that doesn't survive being written down unless the inheritor also inherits the keeping of it, and somewhere in that inheritance there must have been papers, a confession, a ledger — *a ledger* — listing everyone still living who remembered too much.

Harold Pike. Sexton in 1924. Nineteen years old, probably, digging the grave that held nothing, sworn to silence by a frightened beautiful liar of a founder.

Marguerite Cole. Whose mother had played the organ at the funeral and might have noticed things — a coffin too light, a sexton too pale.

Ada Quill. Housekeeper to the dean himself, sixty years in service, who would have dusted the study where the papers slept.

Alphabetical. It was alphabetical, just as Beatrix had said, and Reyes had half-hoped the archivist was wrong, because alphabetical meant methodical, and methodical meant patient, and patient meant this had been planned not in panic but in *advance* — that somewhere in a dean's study or a crypt or a locked drawer there was a list, and the list had been waiting decades for a reason to shorten itself.

The centenary exhumation. That was the reason. Someone had decided to open the coffin, and the empty coffin would have opened everything else, and so the keeper of the secret had begun closing mouths in the order the list prescribed.

She took the turning for Marrow's Bend. The cathedral rose out of the dark ahead of her, floodlit, its spire needling the low clouds, and she felt the specific nausea of a cop approaching a crime scene that wasn't a scene yet — a place where the next bad thing was already scheduled, already rehearsed in somebody's mind, waiting only for the courage or the desperation to be performed.

Her phone buzzed against the console. Beatrix.

She answered on speaker. "Reyes."

"I found the shipping manifest," Beatrix said, without preamble, her voice tight and low, the voice of a woman talking in a room where walls had ears. "The *Corvid*. And the land registry, and the trust filings. I've been following it for a week."

"I know. I read your request slips."

A pause. Then, dry as dust: "You photographed my slips."

"I photograph everything. It's why I'm still alive professionally." Reyes slowed at the lychgate, where a constable she didn't recognize was standing post — new orders, presumably, though whose orders she intended to find out. "Beatrix. Listen to me. The Hollis Trust's current registered trustee — I pulled the charity commission file tonight. There's one name. It's been the same name since 1998, when the second Edmund Hollis died and the trusteeship passed down."

Another pause. Longer. When Beatrix spoke again, her voice had gone very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when someone inside it stops moving.

"Say it."

"Edmund Vane. Dean Edmund Vane is the trustee of the fortune his grandfather stole from his own funeral. He's not the founder's great-grandson, Beatrix. He's the heir. To the money, to the name, and to whatever's left of the secret. And Ada Quill has kept his house for forty years."

The line was silent for so long Reyes checked whether the call had dropped.

"He taught me to catalogue," Beatrix finally said. "My first week. He stood behind me and showed me how to hold a folio so the spine wouldn't crack. He—" A breath, unsteady. "He gave me a family, Detective. And you're telling me the family crest is a lie."

"I'm telling you worse than that." Reyes watched the constable at the lychgate shift his weight, check his watch, glance up at the dean's residence window — where, she noted, a light burned even now, past eleven. "I'm telling you that everything kind he ever did for you was paid for with a dead man's money by a living man's design. And I'm telling you that Marguerite Cole is dead, Harold Pike is dead, and the alphabet has one letter left before it runs out of names."

"*Ada,*" Beatrix whispered.

"Ada Quill. Q comes after C, Beatrix. Whatever you're going to do, do it fast, and do it quiet, and for God's sake don't go to the dean for help."

"No," Beatrix said, and there was something in her voice now that Reyes recognized from interrogation rooms, from the moment a witness stops being a witness and becomes a decision. "No. I won't need his help."

The line went dead.

Reyes sat in the car outside the lychgate, engine ticking, and looked up at the lit window of the dean's residence, at the shadow that moved briefly across the curtain — unhurried, dignified, a man saying his prayers or locking a drawer or crossing a name off a list — and she understood, with the cold certainty that arrives too late and exactly on time, that the most dangerous thing in Marrow's Bend had never been hiding in the dark.

It had been standing in the light for thirty years, holding the door open, calling everyone *my child.*

And tomorrow morning, if she read the pattern right, the alphabet would reach its final letter — unless she and a grieving archivist could get to an old housekeeper first, and get her out, and get her testimony somewhere the dean's money couldn't reach.

She put the car in gear and drove toward Ada Quill's cottage, praying the whole way that Q had not already been crossed off.
