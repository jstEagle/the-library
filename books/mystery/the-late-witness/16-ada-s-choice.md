## 16. Ada's Choice

The housekeeper's key to the cathedral opened more doors than the dean knew.

Ada Quill had kept that small iron key on a ribbon around her neck for forty-one years, and in all that time she had used it for dusting the vestry, for letting herself into the nave before dawn to lay the kneelers straight, for slipping out through the north porch when her knees ached too much to climb the tower stair. It had never once occurred to her, until this week, that a key could be a weapon. That it could be a promise. That it could be the last thing an old woman did with her hands.

She woke at four in the morning, as she always did, in the narrow room above the vestry where she had slept since she was nineteen years old and came to Marrow's Bend with nothing but a carpet bag and a name borrowed from a dead aunt. The room smelled of beeswax and lavender and, beneath both, the cold stone breath of the cathedral itself — a smell she had once thought of as safety and now understood to be only proximity. She had lived her whole life beside the thing that was killing people. She had polished its brass. She had loved its people.

Marguerite was dead three days now, and the town still rang with it like a struck bell that would not fall silent.

Ada rose, dressed in the dark by touch, and took the ledger page from where she had hidden it — sewn into the hem of her winter coat, because no one searches a housekeeper's coat, and no one ever had. She unfolded it on the little table beneath the window. The paper was soft with age, gone the color of weak tea, and the ink had faded to brown except where someone had pressed hard: *A.V.*, and beneath it a column of names, and beneath the names, in a hand steadier than any old man's should be, the words Ada had read so many times now that she could recite them with her eyes closed:

*They will keep quiet, or they will be kept.*

Nine names. Harold Pike first. Marguerite Cole second. And down the column, seventh from the top, in the same faded brown ink: *A. Quill*.

She had known since the night of the funeral — Aldous Vane's centenary funeral, God forgive them all, they had buried an empty box and sung the Te Deum over it — that her name would come due. She had watched Harold die the way the old watch things happen: slowly at first, then all at once. A fall in the bell tower, they said. Harold Pike, who had climbed those stairs ten thousand times, who knew every worn tread like his own teeth. Then Marguerite, drowned in the baptistry like a christening gone wrong, with a page of Ada's own handwriting pinned to her robe — Beatrix's handwriting, though the girl hadn't written it, though Ada would have sworn it on any book you liked, because Ada had taught that hand its letters. She had held six-year-old Beatrix Ashgrove's fist in her own weathered one and walked her through the loops of a capital B. *Round the bowl, then up tall, then two bumps like a bird landing.* No forger alive could copy that without learning it from somewhere, and there was only one place, and only one person who had stood behind Beatrix's shoulder often enough, patiently enough, year after year —

But that thought led where it always led, and Ada was not ready to go there yet. Not yet. First the work.

* * *

The organ loft at half past four in the morning was a country unto itself.

Ada climbed the spiral stair with the page folded inside her glove, pausing twice on the way up to let her heart settle — not from fear, she told herself, but from the stairs, from the stairs alone. The loft smelled of wood polish and old felt and the faint sweet rot of leather bellows, and the great case of the organ rose against the east window like a city built by patient giants, gold pipes catching the first grey light, ranks upon ranks of them ascending toward the vaulting. Marguerite's territory. Marguerite's kingdom. Forty years of Sunday mornings lived inside that sound.

Ada set her lamp on the music desk and stood a moment with her hand flat against the console, feeling the ivory keys cool under her palm. She was not a musician. She had never learned past hymnbook plunking. But she had cleaned this instrument every week of Marguerite's tenure, and before that she had cleaned it for old Mr. Fenwick, and before that for his father, and if you clean a thing long enough you learn its body the way you learn a child's — where it is tender, where it hides things, where it opens.

She knelt at the bass end, where the pipes crowded close and the floorboards were scuffed pale from decades of pedal work, and found the access panel she knew was there because she had oiled its hinges herself every Michaelmas. It swung open onto the windchest, the great wooden lung of the instrument, and beyond it a cavity between the chest and the case wall where a toolbox had lived once and mice had lived since. Dust everywhere, soft as ash. Ada reached in with the page held flat between both palms, the way you'd carry water, and laid it against the back wall of the cavity, face inward, and weighted its corners with two slates she had brought up in her apron pocket for exactly this purpose.

Then she sat back on her heels and looked at what she had done, and found she was weeping — not loudly, not with any drama, just the steady leak of an old woman's eyes, tears running into the creases of her face and dripping off her jaw onto the floorboards, each one a small dark star in the dust.

"There," she said to the organ, to Marguerite, to whoever was listening in whatever country the dead had moved to. "There, my dear. It's safe now. Nothing gets in here but me."

It was not entirely true and she knew it. Things got in. Mice got in. Time got in. But it was the truest sentence she had, and she said it again anyway, because the dead deserve the courtesy of being lied to gently.

* * *

Beatrix Ashgrove came to her at seven, as Ada had asked her to, by note — a note written, Ada reflected with a grim little humor, in her own unmistakable hand, which these days was worth about as much as a forged banknote. The girl looked terrible. Ada had been watching her come up the vicarage path through the kitchen window, and even at a distance she could see it: the bruised hollows under her eyes, the way she carried her shoulders up around her ears like a woman walking through rain without a coat. Twenty-nine years old and already bent under a weight that should have broken a mule.

Ada let her in, put the kettle on, and did not say anything until the tea was poured and Beatrix's hands were wrapped around the cup and had stopped shaking. Some things you cannot rush. Grief is a proofing dough; you let it rise or you ruin it.

"You shouldn't have come," Ada said at last. "You know that."

"I know." Beatrix's voice was hoarse. "I came anyway."

"Yes." Ada sat down across from her, in the good chair, the one with the embroidered cushion that Beatrix herself had given her eleven Christmases ago. "That's rather the whole trouble with you, isn't it."

The kitchen was warm and yellow-lit and smelled of yesterday's bread. Through the window the cathedral rose against the paling sky, its spire going up and up into cloud, and Ada looked at it the way you look at a husband who has disappointed you — with love and fury in the same breath.

"They've taken your statement again," Beatrix said. "Reyes told me. I'm sorry, Ada. I'm sorry you had to—"

"Say my piece? Girl, I've been waiting sixty years to say my piece." Ada wrapped her hands around her own cup. "Harold Pike was a gossip and a coward and he owed me four pounds from 1971, and I loved him like a brother. Marguerite Cole couldn't carry a tune in a bucket until she was thirty and then she made the angels weep, and she was vain about it, and I loved her like a sister. And somebody is walking through my family killing them in alphabetical order, and using your handwriting to do it, and everyone in this town looks at you like you're the devil's clerk." She sipped her tea. "So yes. I'll say my piece. I'll say it standing on the courthouse steps if they let me."

"Ada—" Beatrix set her cup down. Her hands were still again now, but her eyes were wet. "If your name is on that list, if you're next, then you can't stay here. You can't stay in the close. Come away with me. Tonight. There's a train at six, we could be in Ashford by dark, nobody would—"

"And leave?" Ada said it gently, the way you correct a child who has suggested something sweet and impossible. "Leave the cathedral? Leave the archive? Leave him?"

The word fell between them like a dropped glass. Neither picked it up. Both looked at it.

"You know," Beatrix whispered.

"I've known since I was nineteen years old and the new sexton's boy told me he'd seen the founder walking on the platform at Marrow's Bend station, hale as a horse, boarding the London train under a name that wasn't his." Ada smiled, and it was an old smile, worn smooth. "I didn't believe him. Then I started noticing things. The donations that arrived from nowhere. The lock on the crypt door that no one ordered. The way certain questions stopped being asked the moment certain people were paid to stop asking them. I've spent my whole life in that building, Beatrix. You learn a house by cleaning it. And that house has been keeping a secret longer than either of us has been alive."

"The dean."

"Don't." Ada's voice cracked like a whip and she heard herself do it and did not soften it. "Don't say it aloud in my kitchen. Not yet. Not while the walls are thin and grief makes people stupid. I know what you know. I've known it longer. And knowing it has cost me everything I have left to lose, which is why I called you here at seven in the morning instead of telling the detective, God bless her, who is a good woman but a stranger, and strangers get lied to in this town as a matter of civic pride."

She rose, went to the dresser, and took down the tin where she kept her sewing things. Inside, beneath the buttons and the spare needles, was a cassette tape in a cracked plastic case, and beside it a small recorder, the kind churches bought in the eighties for recording sermons and never threw away.

"Harold came to me in September," Ada said. "Before any of this started. Before the coffin was opened. He'd found something in the tower records and it frightened him and he wanted — he wanted someone older than him to tell him it was nothing. I couldn't tell him that. So we talked. For three evenings, in this kitchen, I talked and he talked, and I recorded it, because I am an old woman and my memory is a sieve, and because some part of me — the part that has cleaned that cathedral for forty-one years and knows what lives in its cellars — some part of me already knew that someday the truth would need to outlive us all."

She set the tape on the table between them. Beatrix stared at it as though it might bite.

"That's Harold's testimony. His voice. His dates, his names, the ticket number he saw, the name Aldous Vane traveled under — all of it, in his own mouth, dated the ninth of September, five weeks before he died. And after him—" Ada's throat closed briefly; she waited it out. "After Harold died, Marguerite came to me. She'd found the note in her music, the one in your handwriting, and she was frightened, and angry, and she came here ready to accuse you and stayed four hours and left holding my hand. I recorded her too. Everything she remembered about the Vane bequests, the school, the money that raised a certain boy in a certain house with a certain name. Her voice is on that tape, Beatrix. And mine. Mine is on it too — my testimony, all of it, everything I saw and suspected and chose, God help me, to keep quiet about, because I loved this place and I thought love meant keeping its secrets."

She pushed the tape across the table.

"I can't testify," Ada said. "We both know how this ends for the names on that list. Harold. Marguerite. Me, soon, unless I run, and I'm not going to run, so don't ask me again. But paper burns and old women drown and falls happen on stairs that men have climbed ten thousand times. Tape is harder to kill. And you—" she reached across and took Beatrix's hand, and the girl's fingers were ice, "you are the late witness, my darling. That's what they'll call you at the trial, if we get there. The witness for the ones who died unheard. So you'll carry this. Not in the archive, where he watches. Not anywhere he can find it. Somewhere only you would think of. And when the day comes — and it's coming, sooner than any of us wants — you'll play it in a courtroom and every dead soul in Marrow's Bend will speak at last."

Beatrix closed her hand around the cassette. She was crying openly now, without shame, the way she had cried at nineteen when Ada had first held her, and Ada let her, and did not tell her to be brave, because bravery was a luxury the young were always being billed for and rarely delivered.

"Why me?" Beatrix managed at last. "Why give it to me? I'm the suspect. My handwriting is on the murder pages. If they find this on me—"

"Because you're the only one he can't predict," Ada said simply. "He learned your hand, girl, but he never learned your heart. He thinks you're order itself — files and shelves and rules. He doesn't understand that you break rules for people. That's why he'll never find it. And that's why, when the trap springs — and I know you, Beatrix Ashgrove, don't look at me like that, I've known you were setting a trap since the moment you asked me casually over supper whether the crypt door locks from the inside — when it springs, you'll need something stronger than cunning. You'll need the dead themselves standing behind you." She squeezed the girl's hand hard. "Now they are."

* * *

Beatrix left at half past eight, the tape sewn — at Ada's insistence, with Ada's needle and Ada's thread — into the lining of her coat, mirror to the hiding place Ada herself had used, mother-and-daughter symmetry that made the old woman's chest ache.

Alone, Ada washed the teacups. She swept the floor. She wound the kitchen clock. Small offices, the liturgy of an ordinary morning, performed with the particular care of a woman who suspects her mornings are numbered.

Then she put on her winter coat, took her bucket and her cloths, and let herself into the cathedral through the north porch, because it was Thursday, and Thursday was polishing day, and whatever else was true — however many days she had left, however long the shadow on the ledger column — the brass of Marrow's Bend Cathedral would not tarnish on her watch.

The nave was cold and vast and full of light falling in dusty columns through the clerestory, and Ada stood just inside the door a moment, looking down the length of it toward the altar, toward the crypt stairs beyond, toward the locked door she had never opened and never wanted to.

"Right," she said aloud, to the building, to the dead, to the man in the shadows she could feel the way you feel weather coming. "Let's get to work."

From the darkness past the font, unhurried, familiar, terribly gentle, someone answered: "Good morning, Ada. I hoped I'd find you here."
