## 4. A Witness Who Recants

The bell over the door of Crane & Co., Books had a voice like a startled cat, and on the morning after the funeral it announced the arrival of Dottie Pell with all the drama the occasion demanded.

Noor looked up from the register, where they had been pretending to do arithmetic and actually reading the confession letter for the eleventh time. The letter lived now in a manila folder labeled *INVOICES — PENDING*, which was either brilliant tradecraft or a cry for help.

"Morning, Dottie," Noor said. "You're my first customer in three days. I'd offer you coffee, but the machine and I are no longer speaking."

"I don't want coffee." Dottie was seventy-one years old, built like a fire hydrant, and had run the town's only laundromat for so long that half of Bellwether Lane's secrets had passed through her dryers at one temperature or another. She planted both hands on the counter. "I want to know why you were asking Marisol about tide charts."

Ah. Small towns. Noor set down the pen. "I'm curious about the night Wren died."

"You're always curious about something. Last year you were curious about whether the Hendersons' divorce was real. It wasn't, by the way. They did it for the insurance. Everyone knows."

"Not everyone. I didn't."

"You weren't supposed to." Dottie glanced over her shoulder at the empty shop, then lowered her voice to a register usually reserved for confessions and gossip, which in Dottie's case were the same activity. "That's not why I'm here anyway. I'm here because I've been thinking about what I said. Five years ago. To the police."

Noor went very still, in the way they'd learned to go still during twenty-two years of detective work — still enough that people forgot you were there and kept talking.

"What did you say?" Noor asked mildly.

"That I saw Wren walking down toward the water around nine o'clock that night. Alone. Happy as anything, eating chips out of the bag." Dottie's jaw worked. "I said she was alone."

"And she wasn't."

Dottie sat down heavily on the ladder stool behind the counter, uninvited, which was also very Dottie. "There was someone with her. Further up the lane, under the lamp by the chandlery. I couldn't see who — hood up, hands in pockets. But they were talking. Wren was laughing at whatever they said, and then she wasn't laughing anymore, and then she walked off fast toward the pier and the other one stayed under the lamp a long time. Just standing there. Watching her go."

"Why didn't you tell the police this?"

"Because they asked me if I'd seen anyone *with* her, and I said no, because technically nobody was *with* her, they were twenty feet behind her, and I'm seventy-one years old, Noor, I don't do nuance before ten in the morning." Dottie spread her hands. "And then the whole town decided it was an accident, and Amos Petty stood up at the memorial and cried real tears, and who was I to go stirring? A woman who sees things from her laundromat window. They'd have put me away next to the fabric softener."

Noor turned this over. "Why are you telling me now?"

Dottie looked at the folder marked INVOICES — PENDING, then at Noor, with the flat directness of a woman who had spent decades folding other people's underwear and therefore other people's business.

"Because yesterday I watched you stand at the back of that church looking like a woman doing long division. And because this morning I found something in my lint trap that doesn't belong to me, and I've decided I'm too old to be the last person in Bellwether Lane who knows things."

She reached into her coat pocket and produced a small plastic bag — a sandwich bag, zip-locked, the kind Noor associated with evidence bags and, less professionally, with trail mix. Inside was a button. Brass, nautical, stamped with an anchor and a fish.

"This was caught in the cuff of a jacket I was cleaning. Belonged to one of the Petty boys — the nephew, Callum. He brought in a load the week after Wren died, in a hurry, paid double, wouldn't wait for it. Jacket came back clean but the button came off in the dryer and I kept meaning to return it." She set the bag on the counter between them. "I never did. And now his uncle's dead and buried and I keep thinking about that jacket. It smelled like salt and diesel, and there was a tear in the left sleeve, like it had caught on something."

"Like a nail on a pier railing," Noor said quietly.

"Or a nail on a pier railing." Dottie stood, retrieved her bag, and pointed one stubby finger at Noor. "I'm not saying anything. I'm returning property. That's all this is. You didn't hear a witness recant, you heard a laundromat owner being honest about lost and found."

"Dottie—"

"Don't thank me. Thanking makes it official." She marched to the door, paused with her hand on it. "One more thing. The person under the lamp — when they finally moved, they walked toward the harbor office. Not down the lane. Not home. Toward the harbor office, where there was no reason on God's green earth to be at nine o'clock at night unless you worked there."

The bell gave its startled-cat yowl as she left, and Noor stood alone among the books with a button, a recantation, and the growing, unwelcome certainty that the tidy accident five years ago had been hemmed by somebody very good with thread.

* * *

By noon Noor had rearranged the front table four times — a nervous habit disguised as merchandising — and had learned precisely nothing new except that a display of maritime histories sold better when angled toward the door. The button sat in the INVOICES — PENDING folder now, beside the letter, two pieces of a puzzle that refused to admit how many pieces it had.

At 12:40, the bell yowled again.

Marisol Vega came in the way she always did, like weather — a gust of scarf and opinions, already mid-sentence. "—and I told him, Gerald, if you stack the biography section one more time by *height* instead of alphabetically I will end you, some of us have systems—" She stopped. Looked at Noor's face. "Oh no. You've got your thinking face on. The one where you look like you're enjoying yourself but in a way that means something terrible is happening."

"I need a favor."

"No."

"You haven't heard it."

"The last favor was 'hold this package for me and don't open it,' and I held it for three weeks while it *ticked*, Noor."

"It was a metronome."

"It was *menacing*." Marisol dropped into the armchair by the window, the good one, the one Noor reserved for customers they liked, which was a short list with Marisol at the top and the rest vacant. "Fine. Favor. What."

"Tide charts. The harbor office keeps thirty years of them. I'd like to see the ones from the spring Wren died."

Marisol's eyebrows performed a small, complicated dance. "You could just ask Amos—"

"Amos is dead."

"Ask whoever replaced him, then. There's an interim fellow. Terrible handshake. Smells like pencil shavings."

"I'd rather not explain why I want them."

"So you want me to walk into a government building and steal public records through charm."

"I want you to *request* them. Through charm. If they happen to be photocopied and handed to you voluntarily, that's hardly theft."

Marisol stared at her for a long moment. Then she sighed the sigh of a woman who had been enabling Noor Crane since primary school and saw no reason to reform at fifty-eight. "What am I even looking for?"

"Honestly? I don't know yet. Anything odd. Tides that don't match what people remember. Dates that don't line up."

"A tide chart that lies," Marisol said slowly, tasting the idea. "You know, most retired detectives take up gardening."

"My garden died. It was suspicious. I investigated. It was me."

"I refuse to find that funny." She stood, straightened her scarf, and paused at the door. "For what it's worth — and I know you'll pretend this isn't worth anything — I always thought it was strange. Wren Halloway grew up on that water. Swam in it since she was four. Kids who grow up on the water don't drown in it on calm nights. It's like watching a librarian die in a bookshop avalanche. Possible. But you'd ask questions."

She left. Noor filed that away — *a tide chart that doesn't lie* — and went back to pretending to do arithmetic.

* * *

The afternoon brought rain, the fine gray kind that made Bellwether Lane look like a photograph of itself, and with it came Callum Petty.

He came in shaking rain off his jacket — a dark canvas jacket, newer than the one Dottie remembered, but cut the same, harbor-issue, anchor buttons glinting at the cuffs. He was twenty-six, broad, with his late uncle's heavy jaw and none of Amos's warmth, if Amos's warmth had ever been more than varnish. He browsed the mystery section without appearing to read any titles.

"Help you find something?" Noor asked.

"Just killing time." He pulled out a paperback, checked the price, put it back crooked. "Place looks different. Smaller than I remember."

"Everything does, from the outside."

"Yeah." He drifted closer to the counter. His eyes went, briefly and deliberately, to the folder marked INVOICES — PENDING. "Heard you were at the funeral. Didn't know you and my uncle were close."

"We weren't. I knew him the way everyone knew him. He fixed boats and remembered birthdays."

Callum nodded slowly. "Everybody loved him. That's the thing everybody says." He picked up another book, put it down. "Funny, though. Five years ago, when the Halloway girl drowned, he took it hard. Real hard. Wouldn't talk about it. My aunt said he barely slept for a month." His eyes came up and held Noor's, and there was something underneath the grief there, something with edges. "Grieving like that over a kid he barely knew. You're a detective. Was. Is that normal?"

"Grief isn't orderly, Mr. Petty."

"No," Callum agreed. "It isn't." He bought the paperback — a western, wrong for him entirely — paid cash, and at the door he stopped. "My uncle left everything in order. Ledgers, licenses, the boat titles. All of it squared away, like he knew. Like he'd spent thirty years making sure every paper matched every other paper." The bell yowled. Rain swallowed him.

Noor stood at the counter for a long moment, heart tapping out a rhythm that had nothing to do with commerce.

Either Callum Petty was a grieving nephew making conversation.

Or he was warning them. Or testing them. Or — and this was the possibility Noor liked least — telling the truth about a dead man whose papers were too perfect, whose grief had been too loud, and whose nephew had found something in those immaculate ledgers that he didn't understand yet.

* * *

Marisol returned at closing time, damp, triumphant, and carrying a manila envelope like a trophy.

"Charm," she announced, dropping into the good armchair. "Also the interim harbormaster owes me money from a raffle in 2019, so really it was justice." She slapped the envelope on the counter. "Tide charts, five years back, March through June. Photocopied. Volunteered. I even got him to make tea. I'm considering a career in espionage."

Noor opened the envelope with hands that were almost steady and spread the charts across the counter, weighting the corners with books — a dictionary, a field guide to birds, a romance novel that had been languishing for months and deserved the work.

The night Wren Halloway died had been, per every account, calm. Flat water. No wind worth naming. The coroner's report had leaned on it: no storm, no swell, no reason for a girl who knew the water to slip from the pier.

Noor ran a finger along the column for that date and stopped.

High tide that night: 11:47 p.m. Low tide: 5:32 p.m. Between them, six hours of slack, gentle water. Except — Noor checked it twice, then a third time, then made Marisol check it — the chart showed a low tide at 9:15 p.m. that simply did not exist anywhere else on the page. Every other day in April obeyed the moon's arithmetic. That single evening had an extra low tide, inked in slightly darker, slightly later hand, wedged exactly into the hour Wren went into the water.

"Somebody altered this," Marisol said, her humor gone quiet. "Somebody wrote in a fake low tide. Why would anybody—"

"Because a falling tide pulls things out," Noor said. "If anyone ever asked how a strong swimmer drowned on a calm night, the answer was right there in black and white: the water was going out. Undertow. Drag. An accident the sea arranged all by itself." They looked at the small, careful forgery — careful enough to pass a glance, careless enough to fail a second reading. "Someone wanted the tide to confess so no one else had to."

The shop was silent except for the rain and the tick of the old wall clock. Outside, down at the bottom of the lane, the harbor lights came on one by one, and Noor thought about a figure under a chandlery lamp, hood up, watching a laughing girl walk toward the pier — and then walking, unhurried, toward the harbor office, where the charts lived, where a man with clean hands could rewrite the sea itself.

"Noor," Marisol said carefully. "Who had access to these?"

Noor was already reaching for the phone directory, an actual paper one, because some habits survived retirement. Their finger came down on a name that had appeared, in one form or another, in every thread of the last two days: the funeral, the tears, the buttons, the ledgers, the lamp by the chandlery.

The harbormaster's office had been Amos Petty's kingdom for thirty years.

But Amos Petty hadn't existed thirty years ago — and tomorrow, Noor intended to find out who had.
