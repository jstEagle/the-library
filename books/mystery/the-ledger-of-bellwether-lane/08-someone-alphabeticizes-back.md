## 8. Someone Alphabeticizes Back

Noor Crane had exactly one rule about the shop's fiction section, and it was the sort of rule that had kept them sane through two decades of police work and three years of retail: *A is where A goes.* The alphabet was not a suggestion. The alphabet was not a living document subject to community input. The alphabet was the last honest institution left in Bellwether Lane, and Noor defended it the way other people defended churches.

Which is why, on the morning after finding their carefully annotated tide chart missing from the back of the register drawer, Noor stood in front of the fiction shelves with a cup of tea going cold in one hand and an expression that made the shop cat, Babbage, retreat under the armchair.

The M's were wrong.

Not catastrophically wrong — not shuffled, not vandalized — but *shifted*. McCracken had migrated two slots toward the window. Marlowe sat flush against McEwan instead of leaving its customary breathing room of misfiled paperbacks. And wedged into the gap where McCracken used to be, spine out, perfectly aligned, was a book Noor did not own.

It was a tide almanac. 1994 edition. Salt-swollen at the corners, the cover the faded blue of a work shirt washed too many times, and on the flyleaf, in pencil, in handwriting Noor recognized from five years of case files and one very long funeral: *Property of A.P.*

Amos Petty. The harbormaster. The man everyone had blamed, right up until they'd buried him and moved on to blaming the sea.

"Well," Noor said to Babbage, who re-emerged just far enough to blink judgmentally. "Someone's been shopping."

The bell over the door jingled before the cat could answer, and Priya Chandrasekaran came in shaking rain off her umbrella like a woman personally offended by weather. She ran the bakery two doors down, which meant she ran Bellwether Lane's intelligence service, which meant Noor had spent three years feeding her gentle misinformation to keep the shop's business private. It had mostly worked. Mostly.

"You look like someone rearranged your alphabet," Priya said.

Noor slid the almanac off the shelf and held it up.

Priya's eyebrows went up. "Is that—"

"Yep."

"And it was just *here*? In your M's?"

"Between McCracken and MacLeod. Alphabetized back into my own collection like it belonged there." Noor turned it over. "Whoever put it there wanted me to find it. Wanted me to find it *calmly*."

"Or wanted you to find it thinking you'd found something they wanted you to find," Priya said, "in which case it's a trap, and you should give it to me to hold while you think."

"That's very generous of you."

"I'd keep it behind the counter at the bakery. Nobody suspects the croissants."

"Everyone suspects the croissants, Priya. You know everything that happens on this street."

Priya smiled the smile of a woman who considered this a professional credential rather than an accusation, bought a paperback romance she absolutely did not need, and left. Noor waited until the bell stopped jingling, then carried the almanac to the back room, set it on the desk beside the lamp, and put on white cotton gloves for the second time that week.

The 1994 tide almanac for the Bellwether coast. Amos Petty's name inside the cover. Delivered by hand, in the night, into a locked shop, and filed under M.

The message wasn't the book. The message was the *filing*. Someone was telling Noor: *I know how you think. I know you'll notice. I'm playing your game, and I play it well.*

There were two categories of people who could file a book correctly into another person's shelves. Librarians, and people who had spent years watching librarians. Noor had spent thirty-one years as the latter category's cousin — detectives — and knew exactly what it felt like to be read this fluently. It felt like being handled.

They opened the almanac to March.

Five years ago, Wren Halloway had gone into the water on the night of March 14th. The official ruling — the ruling Noor had signed off on, the ruling that had ended their career when they'd tried to un-sign it — was accidental drowning during an unauthorized night swim. High tide at 11:40 p.m., currents running hard past the breakwater, a strong swimmer caught by a stronger sea. Tragic. Unfortunate. Closed.

March 14th, 2019, in this almanac, had a small pencil mark beside it. Not a circle. Not an underline. A single tick, neat as a ledger entry, in the margin.

And beside March 15th — the day after, the day the search parties walked the shore — a second tick.

Noor sat down slowly.

Two ticks. One for the night Wren died, one for the morning after. That wasn't grief. Grief doesn't annotate. That was *bookkeeping*. Somebody had marked those dates the way you mark dates that matter to an account — dates that need reconciling.

Except the handwriting in the margin wasn't Amos Petty's. Noor had spent enough hours with Petty's harbor logs to know his hand: cramped, forward-leaning, the letters of a man perpetually writing against wind. These ticks were small and upright and unhurried. A different hand entirely.

"Hello," Noor murmured to the empty room. "Who are you, then?"

Babbage jumped onto the desk, stepped directly onto the open almanac, and sat down on March 14th with the gravity of a cat who understood evidence better than most detectives.

"Yes, thank you," Noor said, lifting him aside. "Very helpful."

* * *

The problem with running an investigation out of a bookshop is that the bookshop insists on being a bookshop. At eleven o'clock, Mrs. Okafor arrived for her standing appointment to complain that literary fiction had become "an endurance sport," and Noor spent forty minutes steering her toward Barbara Comyns while mentally cross-referencing every person in town whose handwriting might match the ticks. At noon, a tour group of retirees from Inland came in seeking books about lighthouses, and Noor sold four and learned nothing. At half past, Dev from the chandlery leaned on the counter and asked, with elaborate casualness, whether Noor had "heard anything about the harbor lease," because apparently the town council was reviewing it now that Petty was gone, and wasn't that interesting, and didn't Noor have opinions.

"I have opinions about everything," Noor said. "That's why I own a bookshop."

"Yeah, but do you have opinions about *who takes over the harbor*?" Dev's eyes were too bright. Everyone in town had opinions about who would take over the harbor; the harbormaster's office sat empty as a pulled tooth, and ambition in Bellwether Lane was circling it like gulls round a fish cart.

"No one yet," Noor said, which was true, and watched Dev leave satisfied, which was useful.

By closing time, Noor had narrowed the list of possible book-filers to nobody, which was the correct answer, because the list kept collapsing. The ticks weren't Petty's hand. They weren't Wren's — Noor had Wren's school notebooks, recovered from the case boxes, all slanting leftward and dotted with i's like exclamation points, a teenager's handwriting in a hurry to be somewhere else. They weren't the current police chief's, unless Chief Vasquez had taken up filing books in shops she'd never entered, which Noor doubted, though after five years in Bellwether Lane Noor doubted almost nothing anymore.

They locked up, took the almanac home wrapped in a tea towel like contraband pastry, and spent the evening comparing the ticks against everything in the old case files spread across the kitchen table: witness statements, the coroner's report, Petty's harbor logs, the single page of Wren's diary the police had deemed relevant (it concerned a boy named Callum and said nothing about water).

At midnight, Noor found it.

Not in the files. In the *gaps* in the files. Page 47 of the incident log had been removed — not torn, removed, the way pages are removed when someone signs a log out properly and forgets to sign it back in. Noor remembered the removal. Noor had *reported* the removal, five years ago, and been told it was a clerical error, and had eventually, tiredly, believed it.

A clerical error. Filed away. Alphabetized into the record like a book into a shelf, so neatly that no one noticed it didn't belong.

The same hand. The same tidy, patient mind. Someone had been curating this case for five years — removing things, marking things, keeping the account balanced — and now that Amos Petty was dead and buried, that someone had begun *returning* items to circulation.

Starting with a tide almanac, delivered to the one person in town who would understand the filing system.

Noor looked across the kitchen table at the wall of case boxes and felt the specific chill of realizing you are not investigating alone. You are being *assisted*. And assistance, in Noor's long experience, always came with an invoice.

* * *

The invoice arrived the next morning.

Noor opened the shop at nine to find the mail slot stuffed — not with the usual ration of bakery flyers and a hydro bill, but with an envelope. Cream-colored, heavy stock, addressed in fountain pen: *N. Crane, Proprietor.* No stamp. Hand-delivered again. Inside, a single sheet, and Noor read it twice before setting it down flat on the counter, because hands that shake make bad impressions on evidence.

*Detective Crane,*

*You marked the chart correctly. The 14th was a spring tide, and the breakwater gate was chained at ten, which means Wren Halloway did not walk out to the point that night. Someone unlocked it. The key hung in the harbor office, and only three people could reach it without signing the board: the harbormaster, his deputy, and whoever the harbormaster trusted enough to lend it to.*

*I have the deputy's statement. It was never filed. You will want to know why.*

*Come to the chart room Thursday, after the council meeting, when the building is empty. Come alone. Come before the lease decision, because after it, certain records will be "archived," and we both know what happens to archived records on this lane.*

*You filed me under M once. I have decided to forgive you.*

*— A friend of the late A.P.*

Noor read it a third time, then a fourth, hunting the trick in it, because there was always a trick. The breakwater gate. Noor had flagged the gate in the original investigation — the chain, the padlock, the question of how a boy got out to the point at high water without a boat — and had been told, in a meeting that still lived behind Noor's sternum like a swallowed stone, that the gate was "irrelevant to cause of death." Drowned is drowned, wherever you drown from. Case closed. Career closed shortly after.

But the letter didn't say *I killed her* or *Petty killed her* or even *here is the truth.* It said *come alone, come Thursday, come before the lease decision.* It was an invitation shaped like a summons, and it assumed — correctly, damn it — that Noor would come, because the alternative was letting the harbor change hands and the last loose papers of Wren Halloway's death disappear into somebody's archive box.

"You filed me under M once." Noor turned the phrase over. Under M. Under *Miscellaneous*? Under—

Noor went very still.

Under M. Five years ago, in the final weeks before the case was closed, Noor had kept a personal working file — the doubts, the anomalies, the gate, the tide chart, the missing page 47 — labeled, in Noor's own tidy hand, *MISCELLANEOUS — HALLOWAY.* When the case closed, that file had vanished from Noor's desk. Stolen, Noor had assumed. Destroyed, Noor had hoped, in the way you hope a wound closes.

*You filed me under M once.* Whoever wrote this letter had seen that file. Had perhaps *made* that file disappear — or rescued it. And now they were alphabetizing it back, piece by piece, into Noor's life. A tide chart here. An almanac there. A letter with the rest of the entries promised, if Noor would just come to the chart room and be sorted.

Babbage wound around Noor's ankles, and Noor realized they'd been standing motionless long enough for the kettle to go cold.

"Thursday," Noor told the cat. "Alone. Into an empty harbor building, following instructions from an anonymous correspondent with excellent penmanship and access to sealed statements."

Babbage blinked.

"Yes," Noor agreed. "It's a terrible idea. It's also the first terrible idea in five years that comes with paperwork."

They spent Wednesday preparing, because a detective who walks into a trap unprepared deserves the trap. Noor photocopied the letter and hid the copy inside a hollowed-out copy of *The Moonstone* in the shop's crime section — between Marsh and McDermid, where no one would ever think to look, because no one respected the alphabet anymore except, apparently, the person they were chasing. They told Priya, in the language of plausible deniability, that if Noor missed the Thursday bake sale, the shop keys were with the solicitor. Priya looked at Noor for a long moment over the counter and said only, "Wear the good coat. The one with pockets." Which was either a blessing or a warning, and with Priya it was always both.

Thursday came in grey and low, the kind of sky that makes the whole coast feel like it's holding its breath. The council met at seven in the community hall; by eight-fifteen the lights of the harbor office burned alone above the black water, and Noor walked down Bellwether Lane with the good coat's pockets full — torch, phone, the letter itself, and a small crowbar acquired from Dev at the price of pretending to care about the lease.

The chart room door was unlocked. Of course it was.

Inside, the room smelled of paper and diesel and thirty years of salt. Charts hung in long drawers along the wall, each labeled in that cramped, wind-leaning hand Noor knew so well. A lamp had been left on over the central table. On the table lay a folder, squared to the table's edge with a precision that made Noor's chest ache with recognition.

And pinned to the folder, a note in the same upright, patient hand:

*Before you open this — check the chart drawer marked M. He kept his real accounts where no one alphabetizes.*

Noor stood in the lamplight, listening to the water slap the pilings below, and reached for the drawer marked M.

It was heavier than it should have been.
