## 16. What Noor Crane Buries

The bookshop smelled like burnt paper and regret, which was, Noor reflected, not so different from how it had smelled before the fire — only more honest about it now.

They stood in the doorway at seven in the morning, holding two coffees from Dot's cart because Dot had pressed one on them and refused payment with the ferocity of a woman defending her honor. The fire had come in through the back window sometime around midnight, eaten its way up the shelving behind the register, and then — this was the part Noor kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it — simply stopped. The fire brigade said it was luck. The damp night, the brick party wall, the way the wind had turned.

Noor did not believe in that much luck. Noor believed in tide charts and ledger entries and people who wanted a message delivered without signing their name.

"Could've been worse," Dot said beside them, hands jammed in her cardigan pockets. "Could've been my cart."

"God forbid."

"I'd have burned down whoever did it myself. With what's left of your stock as kindling." She patted Noor's arm with a hand that smelled of cinnamon and diesel, and trundled off toward the harbor, where she sold coffee to fishermen who pretended they didn't need it.

Noor stepped inside.

The damage was contained to the back third of the shop, but smoke had gotten everywhere, the way smoke does — into the spines of books that hadn't burned, into the curtains, into the very grain of the counter where Noor had spent eleven years making change and small talk and, for the past several weeks, quietly dismantling a murder. The poetry section had survived almost entirely intact, which felt like a personal insult. Nobody bought poetry. It would outlive them all.

There was work to be done, and Noor found, to their mild surprise, that they were glad of it. Grief is unbearable when your hands are empty. Give them a mop and a box and they'll manage.

* * *

By nine, half of Bellwether Lane had shown up.

This was the thing about the town that Noor could never quite reconcile: it had discarded them five years ago, politely, the way you return a library book you didn't finish — no hard feelings, just a quiet sense that things had gone wrong somewhere and it was easier not to look at why. And yet here they all were, hauling water-damaged stock into the lane, draping wet mysteries over the iron railing like strange laundry, arguing cheerfully about whether *The Hound of the Baskervilles* could be saved or had earned its drowning.

Mrs. Okonkwo from the bakery arrived with a tray of rolls and the announcement that sugar helps everything, including arson. The twins who ran the chandlery climbed a ladder and started boarding the broken window without being asked, hammering with the rhythm of men who had been waiting years for an excuse to hammer something. Even old Pell, who hadn't spoken to Noor since the funeral — since Noor had stood up at the wake and said the words *I don't think it was an accident* and watched the room close like a fist — even Pell came, silent, and began sorting paperbacks by size with the concentration of a monk.

"You didn't have to," Noor told him.

Pell shrugged. "Bookshop's the only place in town that stocks the good crossword dictionaries."

It was, Noor understood, the closest thing to an apology Bellwether Lane was capable of producing. They took it.

The morning passed in labor and low laughter. Someone found the tin of emergency biscuits behind the register, miraculously unburnt, and declared it a miracle. Someone else found Noor's ancient radio, also unburnt, and put on the maritime forecast, and everyone pretended not to notice when the announcer read the tide times and Amos Petty's name came up — *harbormaster Petty reminds all vessel owners that the spring mooring inspections begin Monday* — and the whole lane went briefly, carefully quiet, before resuming its hammering twice as loud.

So they'd heard. Of course they'd heard. A town like this heard everything; it simply agreed, collectively and without discussion, on what it would admit to hearing.

Noor carried a stack of ruined books to the skip at the curb and stood there a moment, looking down the lane toward the water. The harbor was visible from here, a bright wedge of blue between the rooftops, and beyond it the headland where Wren Halloway had died five years ago, where the tide came in over the rocks twice a day like a patient witness returning to give the same testimony nobody wrote down.

Amos Petty was down there. Noor could see him, small at this distance, in his immaculate harbormaster's coat, directing a visiting sloop into a berth with gestures as precise as calligraphy. Thirty years under a dead man's name. A ledger full of careful fictions. And now, apparently, a fire that had stopped exactly short of destroying the evidence Noor had been assembling — or rather, short of destroying the *shop*. The evidence itself lived elsewhere now. Noor had learned that lesson early: never keep the truth in the same building as your bed.

Which raised the question Noor had been circling all morning: had the fire been meant to destroy evidence, or to send a message? *We know where you sleep.* Or — and this possibility sat colder in Noor's chest — was it someone else entirely? Someone who wanted the case kept open? Someone who wanted Noor frightened enough to move fast and make mistakes?

The letter-writer. The confession postmarked the day after Edmund Voss's funeral. Whoever held the pen had been steering this investigation from the start, dropping letters like bread crumbs, and Noor had followed every one. But a person who lays a trail can also lay a false one.

Noor went back inside and got to work.

* * *

At noon, Detective Sergeant Ilsa Reyes arrived.

She came down the lane in her county-issued coat that never quite fit her, and she stood in the doorway of the ruined shop with her hands in her pockets and an expression Noor had learned to read over the past weeks: the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Arson investigator says accelerant," she said, by way of hello. "Poured along the base of the back shelving. Deliberate, targeted, and set by someone who knew the building." She looked at Noor. "You want to tell me again where you were last night?"

"Asleep. At home. Alone, if you're asking, which I suspect you are."

"I'm always asking." She stepped over a sodden atlas and lowered her voice. "Noor. Off the record. The accelerant was lamp oil. Not petrol, not paint thinner. Lamp oil, the cheap kind, the kind sold in exactly three places in this town." She let that sit. "One of them is the chandlery. One of them is the marina store."

"And the third?"

"The hardware shop on Ferry Street, which closed in March." Reyes rubbed her eyes. "Two places, Noor. And one of those places belongs to a man whose real name we still haven't confirmed, whose fingerprints we still haven't matched to anything pre-1994, and who has, as of yesterday afternoon, requested a meeting with the chief constable to discuss, quote, *the harassment of a longtime public servant by a disgraced former officer*, end quote."

Noor laughed — a short, involuntary sound, quickly strangled. "Harassment."

"He's good, Noor. That's what I keep trying to tell you. He's not some thug with a secret. He's thirty years practiced at being beloved. Every document he's ever touched is clean. Every witness likes him. Half the force worked security details with him at regattas." Reyes picked up a charred paperback, turned it over, set it down. "And now his shop — sorry, *a* shop — burns, and he's got a sympathetic story and a fire marshal's report pending, and you've got a houseful of wet books and a theory."

"It's not a theory. It's a tide chart."

"It's a tide chart *and* a theory, and right now the chart is sitting in whatever undisclosed location you've stashed it, which means legally speaking it doesn't exist, which means—" She stopped herself. Took a breath. "I'm not supposed to be telling you any of this. I'm telling you because five years ago I watched this town hand you your career in a box, and I said nothing, and I've regretted it in a low-grade way ever since, like a splinter." She met Noor's eyes. "Bring me the evidence. All of it. Through the front door, properly logged, witnessed. Because if you keep it buried somewhere and he gets to it first, it stops being evidence and starts being a story he tells about you."

After she left, Noor stood a long time among the wet books, listening to the hammers on the window.

* * *

That evening, Noor walked out past the churchyard to the strip of scrubland behind the dunes, carrying a shovel and a waterproof case, and committed the act the chapter of their life would be named for.

Not far. Not deep. Just deep enough, beneath the roots of a wind-bent gorse bush that bloomed yellow and vicious every spring and discouraged casual digging. Inside the case: the original page from the harbormaster's old ledger — the one with the erasure ghosting through from the page beneath, the name written and scraped away and written over. The photograph of the fish stamp. The copy of Wren's tide chart with the discrepancy marked in red. And the second letter, the one that had come last week, unsigned, postmarked locally, containing a single sentence: *He watches the water at high tide. So should you.*

Noor had made copies, of course. Copies lived in three places now, a fact Noor intended to keep to themselves the way one keeps a knife in a boot. But originals mattered. Originals had weight — literal, legal, evidentiary weight — and Reyes was right: they needed to go through the front door, soon, properly. Just not yet. Not until Noor understood the one thing still missing.

Because here was the shape of it, the shape Noor had been staring at for days: the letters had begun the week after Edmund Voss's funeral. Voss — the man everyone had blamed, the drunk, the scapegoat, dead at last of his liver and his grief. Whoever wrote the letters had waited for Voss to die. Why? Either because they feared him, or because they needed him dead before the truth could surface — or because they were *mourning* him, and guilt has a way of finding the mail.

Three possibilities, and each one pointed at a different person, and Noor had spent weeks assuming all roads led to Amos Petty because the ledgers said so. But the ledgers were Petty's own. A man who could forge a life could forge a ledger. What if the trail through the numbers wasn't a trail at all, but a fence — built to funnel a retired detective exactly where someone wanted them?

The gorse scratched at Noor's sleeves as they tamped down the earth. Somewhere behind the dunes, a curlew cried its bubbling cry, and the light went long and gold across the water, and Noor thought: *Wren walked here too, probably. Five years ago. Carrying something they shouldn't have seen.*

Noor straightened, brushed off their knees, and allowed themselves one small, human moment — standing in the evening light, mourning the shop, mourning Wren, mourning the version of this town that had existed before Noor knew what it was built on. Then they shouldered the shovel and walked back toward the lane.

Halfway home, their phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number.

Noor stopped under the streetlamp and read it twice.

*You buried it under the gorse. Yellow flowers, north side of the path. You always were predictable, Crane. Come to the chart drawer tomorrow night and I'll show you what you're actually looking for. Bring nothing. Tell no one. — A friend of Wren's*

Noor looked up. The lane was empty. The harbor lights blinked on, one by one, out across the darkening water.

Whoever it was had watched them dig. Had watched, and waited, and chosen this exact moment — which meant they weren't warning Noor off. They were inviting them in.

Tomorrow night. The chart drawer.

Noor put the phone away, and walked home the long way, checking the reflections in every dark window.
