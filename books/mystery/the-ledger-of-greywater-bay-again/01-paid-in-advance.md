## 1. Paid in Advance

The rain came in off the bay the way it always did in October — sideways, patient, like something that had all the time in the world and intended to use it. August Joubert watched it from the window of his office above the old pharmacy on Cannery Row, a cup of coffee gone cold in his hand, and told himself for the fourth time that morning that he would get around to re-caulking the frame before winter. He had been telling himself that for three winters now. The wood had swollen to fit the rot, and so, he supposed, had he.

Greywater Bay in the off-season was a town holding its breath. The tourist shutters were down along the waterfront, the charter boats rode their moorings like sleeping dogs, and the fog came up from the water each evening and lay against the windows of the houses on the bluff until morning gave it permission to leave. August had certified two hundred and eleven deaths in his nineteen years as coroner. He knew the number the way other men knew their wedding anniversary. Most of them were kind deaths — hearts giving out in warm beds, lungs filling quietly at the ends of long illnesses. The bay took its share too. It always had. That was the bargain a town made when it built itself on the edge of cold water.

He was thinking about none of this when he went downstairs at nine-fifteen to unlock the office door and found the envelope already inside.

That was the first wrong thing. Before the money, before the letter, before any of it — the simple fact of an envelope sitting on his desk when he had locked that door himself at six o'clock the previous evening and walked home through the drizzle with his collar turned up, past the shuttered cannery, past the church with its leaning fence, home to a house that smelled of dust and single-malt and nothing else. He had locked the door. He remembered the specific small resistance of the deadbolt, the way it always caught and needed a second push with the heel of his hand. Nineteen years of habit. He could have sworn to it.

But the envelope was there, square and heavy, lying in the exact center of his blotter as if placed by someone who understood geometry, or ceremony.

August stood in the doorway a long moment, rain dripping off his coat onto the floorboards, and let the room tell him what it could. Nothing disturbed. Files squared. The brass scale on the sideboard — his father's, a relic from the days when coroners weighed more than opinions — sat where it always sat. No smell of tobacco or perfume. The window above the sink in the little washroom was latched from the inside. Which left the front door, and the spare key.

Everyone in Greywater Bay knew about the spare key. It hung on a nail behind the drainpipe at the corner of the building, had hung there since before August took the office, because half the town's doctors and deputies and grieving widows had needed to reach him at odd hours over the years and August had never possessed the temperament for a telephone that rang at night. A key on a nail was a small act of civic trust. In nineteen years no one had abused it.

Someone had abused it.

He crossed the room without touching anything and looked at the envelope properly. Heavy cream stock, no watermark he recognized, his name typed on the face in a font he'd have to think hard to name — not a typewriter, then, but a printer good enough to pretend. *A. Joubert, Coroner.* No title of address. No return address. The flap had been sealed with a strip of paper rather than licked, which told him the sender cared about fingerprints, or wanted him to think they did.

He put on a pair of cotton gloves from the supply drawer — habit, not suspicion; he handled everything that way when it might matter later — and opened it along the top with a letter knife.

Cash. Banded bricks of it, twenties and fifties, the bands crisp and bank-issued. August counted by reflex, the way he'd once counted sutures in a Y-incision, and stopped at eight thousand and had to start again because his hands had developed a fine tremor he did not appreciate. Eight thousand dollars. He counted it twice more to be sure. Ten thousand dollars in used-but-clean bills, sitting on his blotter in a town where the median house sold for less than that.

Beneath the money, a single sheet of the same cream stock, typed:

*Mr. Joubert —*

*I am retaining you to establish one fact: that Councilman Elias Vann will not be murdered.*

*You are the only person in Greywater Bay qualified to do this, and the only person who will be believed when you do.*

*Prove it hasn't happened yet. Prove it can't happen again.*

*The retainer is paid in advance. There is more where it came from, and there is no deadline except the anniversary.*

*— M. Cole*

August read it four times. The rain filled the silence against the window. Somewhere down the street the diner's grill kicked on with its familiar electric cough, and the smell of bacon grease climbed the stairwell the way it did every morning at nine-thirty, and the world continued being the ordinary world, and the letter sat on his desk saying things that did not belong in it.

*Will not be murdered.* Not *was not*. Not *hadn't been*. Will not. Future tense, wrapped around a man seven years dead.

Elias Vann. August said the name in his head and it arrived whole, unbidden, complete with details he hadn't consulted in years: sixty-one years old, retired schoolteacher turned councilman, widower, fisherman. Found on the rocks below the north jetty on the morning of October 14th, seven years ago this month, his boat accounted for at its mooring, his lungs full of seawater, his blood alcohol at nearly twice the legal limit. Accidental drowning. August had signed the certificate himself. Case closed in a town that preferred its cases closed, especially when the deceased had spent his last council meeting asking loud questions about the Harrow shipping accounts.

Vann had fished barefoot. That detail surfaced now, unasked-for, the way old details do — the man's boots found neatly stowed in his skiff, his feet bare in October water, because Elias Vann claimed he could read the bottom of the bay through his soles better than through leather. Everyone had laughed about it fondly at the funeral. August had noted it in his file and thought nothing of it, because there had seemed to be nothing to think.

He sat down slowly in his chair, which complained the way it always complained, and looked at the money, and looked at the letter, and did the arithmetic a coroner does.

Grief cases wrote letters. Grief cases wrote long, looping, handwritten letters full of underlining and capital letters, letters that accused the tide, the weather, the husband, the government, God. Grief cases sent those letters for years and then, mostly, they stopped. August had a drawer of them. He reread them sometimes on bad nights, not for their content but for their temperature — the human heat still coming off them, years after the fact.

This was not a grief case's letter. This letter was cold. This letter was typed, measured, punctuated like a legal instrument. And it was accompanied by ten thousand dollars, which was not grief. Grief could not count that high. Whoever M. Cole was, she had not written this letter weeping. She had written it the way one writes a contract, or a threat, or both.

And she had known about the nail behind the drainpipe.

August got up, poured the cold coffee down the washroom sink, and stood looking at his own reflection in the dark window while the rain drew long silver lines down the glass. Fifty-eight years old. Gray coming in at the temples like frost on a fence post. A face that had learned to keep its opinions behind the eyes. He had built a reputation in this town on two things: that he was careful, and that he was honest. Both reputations were true, as far as they went. He had never examined too closely how far they went.

*Prove it hasn't happened yet.* The sentence turned over in him like a body in slow water.

Because here was the thing he did not write down, did not say aloud, barely permitted himself to think: the letter was insane, and it was also, in some cellar-deep chamber of him he kept locked, not entirely insane. Seven years ago he had signed a drowning certificate at the end of a week when the sheriff's office — when *Dane*, though he hadn't framed it that way then, had never allowed himself to frame it that way — had mentioned, casually, over coffee, that the Vann family situation was delicate, that the Harrows hoped the matter would be handled with discretion, that discretion was a quality August was known for. And August had handled it with discretion. He had run the tox screen the county lab required and filed what came back, and he had signed, and he had slept, eventually, though he couldn't now remember how many nights that had taken.

There had been nothing wrong with the report. He was almost sure there had been nothing wrong with the report. He was sure enough that he had never once, in seven years, gone back and opened the file.

He realized, standing there with the rain ticking against the glass, that this was the first morning in seven years he had wanted to.

* * *

By noon the rain had settled into its long gray argument with the coast, and August had made three decisions, each of them small enough to deny later.

First: he did not take the money to the bank. He locked it in the firebox under the floorboards with his father's instruments, which was either prudence or evidence, depending on how the next few weeks went.

Second: he did not call Dane Harrow. This was the decision that mattered, and he knew it even as he made it, and he made it anyway. Nineteen years, every strange death, every drowned boy and burned barn and body off the jetty, and his first call had always been Dane — Dane Harrow, who'd held his hair back over a toilet at sixteen after Marty Kessler's graduation whiskey, Dane whose brother Roman had married into the cannery money, Dane who called him *Gus* in a voice that hadn't changed since they were boys running the tideline with buckets. One phone call. *Dane, somebody broke into my office and left me ten grand and a letter about Elias Vann.* And Dane would come down, and look at the letter with his cop's flat eyes, and say something easy and reasonable, and the letter would go into a file, and the file would go into a drawer, and the drawer would close with the soft competence of the Harrow machine, which had been closing drawers in this town for a hundred and forty years.

August stood with the phone in his hand for a full minute. Then he set it back in its cradle and heard, in the quiet click of it, something that felt like a door.

Third: he opened the Vann file.

It was where it should have been, third drawer down, alphabetical between *Valdez* and *Vandermeer*, the manila softened at the corners by seven years of drawer-darkness. He carried it to the desk under the window where the light was best and sat with his hands flat on the cover for a moment, the way he'd sit with a widow before letting her see the face.

Then he opened it.

Photographs first, because photographs were how he always read a body — the rocks, black and wet; the skiff riding its mooring forty yards out, untouched; Vann himself on the gurney, gray as driftwood, mouth slightly open as if interrupted mid-sentence. August had taken these photographs. He remembered the light, low and gold, early morning, the gulls screaming their idiot commentary. He remembered the smell of the jetty at low tide — kelp and diesel and old iron.

The narrative report, in his own handwriting, tidy and clinical. *Deceased recovered below north jetty. No signs of struggle at scene. Lividity consistent with prone position post-mortem. Lungs: marked edema, consistent with drowning. Blood alcohol 0.16%. Manner of death: accident.*

His signature at the bottom. His signature, unmistakable, the heavy downstroke on the *J* that his fourth-grade teacher Sister Agnes had never managed to beat out of him.

He turned to the toxicology annex.

The page was there. The county lab header, the case number, the results — ethanol, negative for everything else, the standard litany. His initials in the corner confirming receipt and review. Everything correct. Everything in order.

Except that August Joubert had reviewed approximately four thousand tox pages in his career, and he knew paper the way a fisherman knows weather, and this page was wrong. Not the content — the content was banal, exactly what everyone had expected. Wrong in its body. The staple hole at the top left didn't match the holes on the pages before and after it; it sat a quarter inch lower, punched by a different hand or a different machine. The paper stock was a shade whiter than the county's institutional buff. And the ink of his own initials — he tilted it under the lamp, brought up the loupe he used for wound margins — the ink of his own initials sat *on top of* the toner rather than beneath it, ballpoint over laser, which meant the initials had been added after the page was printed, which meant—

Which meant he had never initialed this page.

Or he had, and didn't remember, and the memory of not remembering was itself the most frightening document in the file.

August set the loupe down very carefully, as if it might go off. Outside, the rain kept up its gray percussion, and the diner smell had faded, and the office was so quiet he could hear the building settle around him like an old dog lying down.

He was being managed. The word arrived without drama, flat as a gauge reading. Not deceived — managed. Someone had replaced a page in his file, seven years ago, well enough done that he'd never noticed, and had initialed it in his hand well enough that he'd doubted himself before doubting it. You didn't do that to a man unless you knew him. Unless you knew he was careful and honest and tired, and knew precisely how much friction it would take to slide a page past him.

Ten thousand dollars lay in the firebox under the floorboards. A stranger named Cole had known where the key hung. And somewhere in this town — or beyond it, at the end of a phone line, at the head of a table — someone had decided seven years ago that Elias Vann's death would read as an accident, and had made it read as one, using August's own hand as the signature on the lie.

He closed the file. He did not put it back in the drawer. He sat holding it on his knees for a long time, listening to the rain, feeling the first cold seam open in a certainty he had lived inside for seven years like a heated house.

Then the phone rang, and he flinched like a man shot at.

He watched it ring. Four times. Six. The caller ID glowed a local prefix he didn't recognize. On the ninth ring, the answering machine picked up, and the tape hissed, and a woman's voice came through the speaker — level, unhurried, faintly accented with somewhere far from here, a voice that sounded like it had rehearsed this and decided against rehearsing it.

"Mr. Joubert. You've opened the file. I know because I'm watching the light in your window." A pause, and the rain, and then, quieter: "Don't call the sheriff. When you're ready to talk, walk down to the cemetery at dawn tomorrow. I'll find you. I'll be the one standing at Elias Vann's grave."

The line clicked dead.

August sat in the failing afternoon light with the file on his knees and the receiver silent in its cradle, and outside, far up the hill through the rain-grayed air, the lights of the Harrow house burned on the bluff above the bay, steady and golden and patient as they had burned for a hundred and forty years.

He found that he was already deciding what coat to wear at dawn.
