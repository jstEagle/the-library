## 2. Every Grave by Name

I have walked Greywater Bay Cemetery so many times that my feet know the ground better than my eyes do. They know where the gravel path gives way to packed clay at the third elm, know the two flagstones that rock underfoot no matter how many times the sexton swears he's leveled them, know the exact spot where the hill crests and the whole town lays itself out below — the harbor like a gray coin, the cannery roofs rusted to the color of old blood, the Harrow Shipping cranes standing over everything like patient herons.

Dawn is the only honest hour this place has. By ten o'clock the tourists come through with their cameras and their ice cream cones, reading the oldest stones aloud in voices pitched for children — *look how young she was* — and the cemetery becomes a performance of grief rather than the thing itself. But at five-thirty, when the fog is still peeling off the water and the grass holds last night's cold in its blades, the dead are allowed their privacy. I try not to disturb it.

I've been coming here for thirty-one years. First as a deputy coroner learning the names from the back of the register, then as coroner, walking the rows after every burial because I made myself a promise early on: every grave by name. Not a plot number, not a file reference. A name, said out loud, once, so that whatever I signed at the bottom of a death certificate would be attached to a person and not a paperwork problem. My father was a fisherman who went into the bay on a Tuesday in November and came back as a closed casket and a rumor, and I swore I would never let anyone in my care become a rumor.

For thirty-one years I kept that promise.

The retainer money sat in my desk drawer all night like something drowned. Eleven thousand dollars in banded twenties, wrapped in a manila envelope with a single line typed on the front: *PROVE THAT ELIAS VANN WILL NOT BE MURDERED.* I hadn't touched it since Maren Cole walked out of my office. I'd poured two fingers of rye instead and sat with the lamp off watching the harbor lights, and around midnight I caught myself doing arithmetic I didn't want to do — seven years since Vann went into the quarry pond, seven years of certainties stacked like sandbags against a rising question — and I put the glass down unfinished.

You don't prove a murder hasn't happened yet. That's not an investigation; that's a prayer. And people don't pay eleven thousand dollars to hear prayers.

So at half past five I laced up my boots, took my coat off the hook, and drove out to the cemetery to do the one thing I could still do honestly: stand in front of Elias Vann's grave and look at it properly, the way I should have looked at everything else.

* * *

The gate was unlocked, as it always is. Old Tom Bexley stopped locking it years ago — said the dead deserved visitors more than they deserved protection, which is either wisdom or laziness depending on how much you've had to drink. The fog was thick enough to taste, iron and kelp and cold stone, and my breath came out ahead of me like a small ghost leading the way.

Vann's stone sits in the newer section, third row from the fence, where the ground is still settling and the grass comes in patchy. Granite, standard issue, the kind the funeral home offers in three shades of gray and calls them all "dove." I remember approving the placement — the family was gone, or gone enough, and the town paid for the plot the way towns pay for things they'd rather not discuss: quickly, quietly, and without looking too closely at the receipt.

*Beloved of Greywater Bay.* I'd read those words a hundred times in other contexts, but standing there in the wet dark I heard them for what they were — a eulogy written by a town that had already decided what it wanted to believe. Beloved of Greywater Bay, and buried by it, too. There was no mention of how. There never is. The stones here record the fact of death and leave the manner to people like me, and for seven years the manner had been *accidental drowning*, certified by August Joubert, coroner, in his own careful hand.

I stood there a long time. Long enough for the fog to start lifting off the low rows and the first gray light to find the granite and turn it the color of a fish's belly.

Here is what I remembered, standing in front of his stone. Not the certificate — certificates are typed and bloodless and I could recite mine in my sleep, cause of death: asphyxia due to submersion, manner: accident, no signs of foul play. What I remembered was the body itself, laid out on my table on a morning much like this one, and the strange intimacy of the dead, how they tell you things their families never knew.

Elias Vann had been sixty pounds overweight and proud of it, a big soft-bellied man with a laugh you could hear across the harbor. He'd fished the quarry pond every Sunday for twenty years — everyone knew that, it was practically civic lore, the councilman and his rod, keeping the working man's pastime alive even after he made council. He'd been found floating near the north bank, three days missing, and the water had done its usual work on him, and I had done my usual work on the water.

But it wasn't the lungs I kept coming back to, standing there. It wasn't the petechiae or the froth or any of the textbook architecture of drowning. It was his feet.

His feet were bare.

No — wait. I pressed my palm flat against the cold granite as if the stone might correct me. No. His feet had been bare when they pulled him out, yes, that was right, the waders were found folded on the bank, neat as laundry, which had struck even the responding deputy as odd at the time — a man takes off his waders to go swimming? In October? — but on my table, when I dressed him for viewing, I had put shoes on him. Brown loafers. Because the mortuary supply closet was out of his size in black, and because nobody had claimed the body yet, and because the dead deserve shoes even if the living can't be bothered to provide them.

And that was when the crack came. Not a loud crack. The quiet kind, the kind that starts inside a foundation and works its way up over years until one morning the wall is leaning and everyone acts surprised.

The loafers were wrong.

Not morally wrong — there's nothing wrong with putting shoes on a dead man, God knows I've done it fifty times and would do it fifty more. Wrong the way a key is wrong for a lock. Because Elias Vann did not own brown loafers. I knew that the way I knew the tide tables. Every Sunday for twenty years that man had walked from his house on Cannery Row to the quarry pond in canvas sneakers, summer and winter, rain and shine, and when the weather turned cold he fished in rubber boots over two pairs of wool socks, and when it was warm enough he — 

He fished barefoot.

It came back to me with the force of a slap: a June afternoon, years before his death, Vann standing hip-deep in the shallows with his trousers rolled and his shoes planted on the bank like sentries, laughing at some joke of Dane's, saying, *you can't feel the bottom through leather, Augie, a man ought to know what he's standing on.* A throwaway sentence. A sentence I had carried for fifteen years without knowing I was carrying it, the way you carry a splinter too deep to dig out, and now the flesh had finally grown around it far enough to push it to the surface.

A man who fished barefoot. A man whose shoes stayed on the bank because he liked to feel the mud between his toes. And when they found him, the waders were folded neatly on the bank — folded, mind, not dropped, not tangled, not torn off in a struggle — and somewhere between the quarry and my table, someone had put that man in brown loafers he had never owned, and I had signed my name beneath the fiction without ever asking where the shoes came from.

Because the family hadn't claimed him yet. Because the closet was out of black. Because it was easier.

I sat down on the wet grass beside the stone. I'm not ashamed of it. A man my age kneels when his knees tell him to, and besides, it seemed fitting — talking to Elias Vann at eye level for the first time in seven years.

"I put shoes on you," I said. My voice sounded obscene in all that quiet, too loud and too alive. "Brown ones. You'd have hated them."

The fog was burning off the harbor below, and the cranes at the Harrow dock stood up out of it one by one, black against the paling sky. From up here the whole town looked orderly, arranged, every roof and road in its place — the way a ledger looks orderly, before you read the entries.

* * *

Here is the thing about certainty: it doesn't feel like a choice. That's what makes it dangerous. Nobody decides to be certain; certainty arrives wearing the uniform of diligence, salutes smartly, and you let it in because it looks like your own reflection. For seven years I had been certain Elias Vann drowned. The water in his lungs said so. The report in my files said so. The sheriff's office said so, the newspaper said so, the town said so in its low persistent murmur — *terrible thing, but these ponds, you know, the cold, the current, a big man, his heart* — and every voice agreeing with every other voice built a choir so complete that doubt couldn't have found a seat in it.

But certainty, I understood now, sitting in the wet grass with my hands going numb, is just memory that's stopped being checked. And memories are like the graves in this cemetery. They need tending. Leave them alone long enough and the grass grows over, the stones tilt, and you find yourself standing in front of a name you're sure you know, discovering you never really looked at it at all.

What else hadn't I looked at?

I started the inventory right there, kneeling in the dew, because I am a coroner down to the marrow and inventory is what I do when the world stops making sense. Vann's hands — had there been defensive marks? I couldn't say. I remembered the nails being clean, or I remembered deciding they were clean, which is not the same animal. The toxicology screen — negative, routine, alcohol 0.04, nothing else flagged. Had I actually read the lab's raw sheets, or just the summary page the lab tech slid across the counter with his coffee breath and his tired smile? Seven years ago. I couldn't swear to it. That was the horror of it, unfolding slowly like a body bag unzipped: not that I remembered evidence of murder, but that I couldn't remember evidence of anything. The case lived in my mind as a finished room, and now I saw that I had furnished it from photographs, never once walked the floor.

And the shoes. God, the shoes. Where had the loafers come from? I'd taken them from the supply closet, I was nearly sure — but the supply closet stocked what the funeral home sent over, and the funeral home sent what fit the deceased, and somebody had measured a dead man's feet and ordered brown loafers for a man who owned none. Somebody had dressed Elias Vann for his own cover story while he lay on my table under my light, and I had handed them the pen by signing where they told me the line was.

I got up. My knees cracked like green wood. Down in the harbor the first ferry horn sounded, flat and mournful, and the gulls came up off the pilings in a ragged white sheet.

"Every grave by name," I said to the stone. "That was the promise. I said your name, Elias. I said it at the burial and I said it when I filed the certificate and I said it every time I walked this row. But saying a name isn't the same as knowing a man, and I filed you away as an accident because an accident asks nothing of anybody." I wiped my face with my sleeve, and my sleeve was soaked, and I couldn't have told you whether it was dew. "An accident lets everyone sleep."

There was a woman in my office yesterday with eleven thousand dollars and a stranger's name, asking me to prove a murder that hadn't happened yet. I'd thought her a crank. Grief does strange arithmetic in people; I've seen it spend inheritances on psychics and lawsuits against the weather. But grief doesn't usually know where a man hangs his spare key. And grief doesn't usually pick its words so carefully — *prove that he will not be murdered* — present tense, future-proofed, a sentence built like a trap to see whether the coroner would flinch at the grammar.

She hadn't asked me if Vann's death was suspicious. She'd assumed I already knew it was, and was testing whether I'd admit it.

I walked back down the row toward the gate, and I did what I always do, what thirty-one years of habit compels: I said the names. Okafor. Lindqvist. Pell. Marsh. The old names and the new, the fishermen and the cannery girls, the infants in the shaded row that no one walks twice. Every grave by name. But this morning the ritual felt different — less like a promise kept than a debt being counted, name after name after name, and somewhere in the middle of it I realized I was no longer walking the cemetery to honor the dead.

I was walking it to find out which of them I'd failed.

At the gate I stopped. Behind me the fog was almost gone, and Elias Vann's stone caught the first true sunlight, and for a moment it looked less like a marker than a door left open.

Seven years ago I sealed a file and called it closed. This afternoon, God willing, I was going to open it again — pull my copy of the Vann autopsy out of the locked cabinet where it had slept through six anniversaries, and read it the way I should have read it the first time. Not as a coroner confirming what everyone already believed.

As a man checking whether his own signature was telling the truth.
