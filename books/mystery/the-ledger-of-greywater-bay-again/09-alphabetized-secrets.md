## 9. Alphabetized Secrets

The ledger began as a school exercise book I bought at the drugstore on Cane Street — the same kind I'd used for chemistry notes in 1974, marbled cover gone soft and grey at the corners, the spine crackling like river ice when you opened it flat. Two dollars and forty cents. I stood at the register holding it and understood that I was buying the instrument of my own ruin, and that I had never in my life purchased anything so necessary.

I started with the letter A because that was the only honest way left to do anything.

*Albright, Ruth.* County clerk, 1989 to 2003. Signed off on the Harrow Shipping rezoning that moved the fuel depot onto wetland her own husband had fought to protect as a bird sanctuary. Her son got a full ride to Tulane the following autumn — a scholarship no one could name the benefactor of.

I wrote it all down in my coroner's hand, which is a small, closed, unlovely script, the handwriting of a man who has spent thirty years recording how people stopped breathing. It seemed fitting. The ledger was, after all, a record of deaths — just the slow kind, the kind that take decades and leave the body walking around.

*Brandt, Ilsa.* Deputy. Rookie the year Vann died. Saw something at the quarry she has never said aloud, or if she has, no one alive will confirm it.

*Broussard, Tomás.* Dock foreman. Overtime tripled the month after the Vann ruling. Retired early. Boat he never uses.

The names came easier than I wanted them to. That was the horror of it, once I let myself feel it instead of simply writing: thirty years of favors, and they were not hidden. They were *arranged*, the way furniture is arranged in a room you've lived in so long you've stopped seeing it. Every one of them sat in plain view, and every one of them had been sitting in plain view of me, and I had called the arrangement *a town*. I had called it *home*.

Maren arrived at nine with two coffees and a paper bag of beignets from the place on Delacroix that opens before the shrimp boats do. She'd taken to letting herself in with the spare key — the same key, I remembered with a chill that had nothing to do with the night air, that she had known about before she ever knocked on my door. She never explained how. I never asked. There is a species of trust that grows only between people who have both decided the truth matters more than their own safety, and we were cultivating it the way you cultivate mold: in the dark, without quite meaning to, until one day you notice it has spread across everything.

"You look terrible," she said, setting the coffee down on top of the Albright page.

"I've been up since four."

"Doing what?"

"Remembering." I pulled the notebook back out from under her cup. "That's most of the work now. Not finding things. Remembering things I spent years not-finding."

She read for a while. I watched her read — watched her lips move slightly over the names, watched her finger hover over *Vann, Elias* where I'd written it first, before any of them, because he was the reason for all the rest. She touched the entry the way you touch a headstone. Then she took the pen out of my hand without asking, turned to a fresh page, and wrote, in a quick slanted hand nothing like mine:

*Harrows don't buy silence. They sell belonging.*

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means my father wasn't paid off. He was *invited in*. Two hundred thousand dollars isn't a bribe, August. It's a membership fee. They weren't telling him to shut up — they were telling him he could be one of them, if he'd just stop being himself." Her voice stayed level but her knuckles had gone white around the pen. "And when he wouldn't, they didn't punish him. They *unmade* him. Drowned man. Bad luck. Sad story. Case closed."

I thought about that for a long time, long enough that the coffee went cold and the light through the office window shifted from grey to the pale washed-out gold that means the fog is burning off the bay. Outside, a gull argued with something invisible. Somewhere down the street, the hardware store rolled its gate up, and the sound was so ordinary it felt obscene.

"Then the ledger's wrong," I said finally. "It can't just be a list of who took what. A list of bribes proves corruption. Corruption gets settled out of court, gets a fine, gets a resignation and a pension. We need to show the shape of it. The architecture."

"So what's the shape?"

"Silence," I said. "Every entry needs a second column. What they knew. Not what they did — what they *knew*, and chose to carry quietly, because carrying it quietly was the price of staying warm."

Maren looked at me for a moment with an expression I couldn't read — something between grief and appraisal, the way you might look at a house you're deciding whether to burn down or renovate. Then she drew a line down the right side of every page we'd filled, and ruled the second column in, and handed me back the pen.

"Start with yourself," she said.

* * *

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the whole currency of what follows: I did not want to start with myself. I wrote eleven more entries first — *Delacroix, Fontenot, Guidry* — filling the second column with small betrayals I had witnessed and filed under *town business*, and my hand was steady the entire time, and I felt clean, useful, righteous. It is remarkable how much righteousness you can generate by cataloguing other people's sins.

But Maren sat across from me eating her beignets in small patient bites, saying nothing, and the silence she left was shaped exactly like the entry I was avoiding, and eventually I ran out of other people.

*Joubert, August.* Coroner, Parish of Greywater Bay, 1991–present.

I stared at my own name for a long time. Maren got up, refilled the coffee, came back. She didn't prompt me. That was the worst kindness anyone has ever done me.

In the second column, after a while, I wrote:

*Knew the shoes were wrong. Said nothing. Certified drowning. Slept well.*

Because that was the thing — God help me, that was the thing I had never told anyone, the thing I had managed to bury so deep even I only dug it up again at dawn in a cemetery seven years too late. I *had* noticed. Standing over Elias Vann on my table, under the good fluorescent light, I had noticed that the dead man wore deck shoes, laced, dry-rotted at the eyelets, and that every fisherman on this coast knew Elias Vann fished barefoot — said it made him feel the water, said a man who can't feel the water shouldn't be on it. And I had looked at those shoes, and I had felt something flicker, some small animal alarm in the base of my skull, and then Martin Harrow had put his hand on my shoulder in the hallway afterward and talked to me about my mother's medical bills, gently, sympathetically, the way you'd talk to a friend, and the alarm had gone quiet, and I had signed the certificate, and I had slept well.

Slept *well*. Seven years of sleeping well. That was the sentence that cost me the most ink, because I had to blot it twice.

Maren read it when I finished. She didn't touch my hand or tell me it wasn't my fault, and I loved her for that, because it was my fault, and comfort offered against a true thing is just another way of lying.

"My father liked you," she said at last. "There's a letter — March, the year he died. He writes that the new coroner is 'the only man in this parish who looks at bodies like they're still people.' His words." She folded the memory away carefully, the way you fold a flag. "He'd have understood this page. He might even have forgiven it. But he'd have wanted it written anyway."

"That's why you came to me. Not despite what I did. Because of it."

"You're the only witness who doesn't know yet what he saw." She smiled, and there was no warmth in it, only a kind of grim arithmetic. "I need your shame, August. It's the most reliable evidence in this town. Everything else can be bought or burned. Shame can't. Shame wants out."

* * *

We worked through the afternoon and into the blue hour, and the ledger grew fat and ugly and true. *Landry, Mercier, Naquin* — oyster leases reassigned, building permits fast-tracked, a fire marshal's report softened, a school board election nudged. Thirty years of Harrow gravity, every planet in its orbit, each one convinced it was moving freely.

Somewhere past dark, Maren fell asleep in the chair across from me, her cheek on her forearm, the pen still in her hand, a smear of blue ink along her jaw where she'd pushed her hair back. The lamp made an island of us. Rain had started somewhere out over the water — I could hear it coming in across the flats, a sound like static, like the bay whispering through its teeth — and I sat very still and let myself look at her, this woman who had crossed an ocean to hand a town its bill.

She had her father's brow. I'd seen it in the photograph she kept folded in her wallet, Elias Vann squinting into the sun on his boat, barefoot, grinning. I had laid that man out on my table and called his death an accident, and now his daughter slept in my office trusting me with her life, and the strange thing — the thing I will carry to whatever grave I get — is that being trusted by her felt less like forgiveness than like sentencing. She hadn't absolved me. She'd deputized me.

When the rain reached the window I covered her with the coat off the hook by the door, and she woke halfway, disoriented, eyes wide and dark in the lamplight.

"Keep going," she murmured. "I'll spell you at midnight."

"There's no midnight deadline."

"There's a gala Saturday," she said, already drifting back under. "And there are only twenty-six letters."

I went back to the P's. *Pelletier, Prejean.* And then, near the bottom of the page, a name I had been circling all day the way your tongue circles a broken tooth:

*Pruitt, Dale.* County archivist. Helpful. Careful. The man who had pulled the Harrow Shipping ledgers for me three days ago without asking why, and who had mentioned, offhand, closing his eyes as he shelved a box, that he'd always wondered about the Vann file. That the toxicology pages in old cases used to come back from the lab on different paper. Thinner. That he'd noticed, years ago, and decided it wasn't his business.

I had written him into the ledger that morning under S — *silence, benign* — a good man who looked away, one of dozens.

Now I picked up the phone and dialed the archive number, though it was past ten, because there was a question I suddenly needed answered: whether Pruitt had ever mentioned the thin paper to anyone else. Whether someone else might have heard him wonder out loud.

The line rang. And rang. And rang, out into the empty county building, into the dark, into the rain coming hard off the bay now, and no one answered, and I stood there in my island of lamplight listening to it ring, and beside me the ledger lay open to my own name, and outside, far off toward the archive, I thought I heard — though I would never swear to it, though it may have been only the storm — a siren start up, and stop, and start again.
