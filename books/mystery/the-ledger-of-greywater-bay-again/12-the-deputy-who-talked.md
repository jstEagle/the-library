## 12. The Deputy Who Talked

The radio in Ilsa Brandt's cruiser had a habit of swallowing the first syllable of every dispatch, so that for seven years she had lived in a town where everything important arrived half-heard — *—all units, 10-31 at the marina* — and she'd learned to fill the missing word with whatever her stomach told her it would be. Tonight her stomach said *Vann*. It had been saying *Vann* for two weeks, ever since the coroner started pulling old files and asking questions in that careful, apologetic voice of his, like a man apologizing to furniture he was about to move.

She sat in the cruiser at the end of Pelican Row with the engine off and the windows cracked, watching the fog come off the bay in slow gray ropes. The dash clock read 11:47. Her shift ended at midnight. She had thirteen minutes to decide whether to become, officially and irreversibly, the kind of deputy who talked.

Seven years ago she had been twenty-three years old and three weeks out of the academy, still shining her own boots, still believing that the badge was a promise you made to strangers. She remembered the night of Elias Vann's death the way you remember your first fire — not the details but the temperature of them. The quarry road slick with drizzle. Sheriff Dane Harrow's voice on the radio, calm as bathwater, telling her to hold the north access while the adults handled the south. And then the headlights coming up behind her, close enough to silver the rain on her windshield, and the second car idling there in the dark for eleven minutes — she knew because she checked her watch twice, nervous, wanting it gone — before it turned around without ever switching off its high beams, and drove back down toward town like a man leaving a church he hadn't liked the sermon in.

She'd written it in her log. She'd shown it to Sergeant Pruitt, who read it slowly, licked his thumb, and turned the page.

"Rookie," he'd said, not unkindly, "you saw a trucker taking a wrong turn. Happens all the time on that road." And then he'd torn the page out — actually torn it, she heard the paper give — and folded it into his breast pocket like a man pocketing a match, and looked at her with something that wasn't quite a threat and wasn't quite mercy, and said, "You're going to do fine here."

She had done fine here. That was the sickness of it. Seven years of fine. A commendation for the drowning rescue off Gull Point. A key to the evidence room. A Christmas card from Odile Harrow every December, signed in that tall, elegant hand, *With gratitude for your service to Greywater Bay*, and every year Ilsa had stood at her mailbox holding the card and feeling the exact weight of what gratitude cost.

Tonight the card was going back.

She got out of the cruiser. The fog took her immediately, beading cold on her forearms, muffling the harbor bells down to a single drowned note. August Joubert's office was four blocks up, above the old pharmacy, its windows dark except for one lamp burning behind the frosted glass of the door — a light left on for her, because she'd called him at ten o'clock and said six words into the phone, *I need to give a statement,* and then hung up before either of them could hear themselves think better of it.

Her hand shook on the stair rail. Not fear exactly. Fear was a clean thing; this was older and stickier. This was the feeling of a woman about to unscrew the floorboard she'd been standing on for seven years, knowing there was nothing underneath but the same dark water everyone else had been standing over too.

* * *

August Joubert opened the door before she knocked. He looked like a man who had been awake for days in a way that sleep wouldn't fix — shirt collar open, reading glasses pushed up into gray hair, the smell of cold coffee and old paper coming off him like weather. Behind him the office was lit by a single desk lamp, and on the desk lay an array that made her breath catch: manila folders fanned like a losing hand, a legal pad dense with his small upright handwriting, and a ledger — an actual ledger, cloth-bound, its spine cracked white — with tabs of colored paper sticking out of it like feathers.

"Deputy Brandt." He stepped back to let her in. "I didn't think you'd come."

"I almost didn't." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "I've been sitting outside for forty minutes arguing with myself."

"Who won?"

"It's not over yet." She hung her uniform cap on the hook by the door — an absurd, automatic gesture, the cap of a woman who still believed in the hook — and sat in the wooden chair across from the desk. Up close, August's eyes were bloodshot and steady, and looking into them she understood why Maren Cole trusted this man: he had the particular patience of someone who had spent thirty years listening to dead people through the mouths of the living.

He didn't reach for a recorder. He reached for the legal pad instead, and uncapped a pen, and waited.

"You can record it," she said. "I want it recorded. I want it dated. I want my name spelled right — Ilsa, L before the S, my mother was stubborn about it."

"I know how it's spelled." He set a small recorder between them and pressed the button. The red light came on, and something in Ilsa's chest unclenched and clenched at the same time, the way a fist does around a coin.

"My name is Ilsa Marie Brandt. I've been a deputy with the Greywater Bay Sheriff's Department for seven years, two months. On the night of October ninth, seven years ago, I was a probationary deputy assigned to hold the north access road to the Kestrel Quarry—" she stopped. Swallowed. "That's what we called it. Nobody calls it anything else. Sheriff Harrow assigned me there himself, by radio, which I remember because it was the first time he'd ever spoken to me directly on a shift. His voice was very calm. Calmer than dispatch. Calm like someone reading a list he'd already written."

August wrote nothing. He just watched her, and let the recorder watch too, and the lamp hummed.

"At approximately nine-forty p.m., a vehicle came up the north access behind me. Sedan, dark, maybe black, maybe dark blue — the fog was heavy and the high beams were on, so I couldn't get the plate. It stopped maybe fifteen feet behind my unit and idled there. Eleven minutes. I know it was eleven because I checked my watch at the start and again when it left. The driver never got out. Never flashed lights. Never used the radio. Just sat there with the engine running and those beams washing over my windshield, and I remember thinking it felt like being held under a lamp — like something being inspected, or weighed. Then it turned around and went back down the hill, and I logged it."

"And the log?" August asked quietly. "What happened to your log entry?"

"Sergeant Pruitt tore the page out." The words came easier now, seven years of silt finally moving. "In front of me. He folded it into his shirt pocket and told me I'd seen a trucker take a wrong turn. And then he said — I've had this sentence in my head for seven years, Mr. Joubert, word for word — he said, *You're going to do fine here.*"

August's pen had stopped moving. When she glanced at the pad, she saw he'd written only one word, underlined three times: *Pruitt.*

"There's more," she said. "There's the part I've never told anyone, not even myself, really, out loud. While the car was idling behind me, I heard something carry up the hill from the quarry. Through the fog. Sound does that down there — the walls bounce it. I heard a voice. One voice, male, raised — not screaming, more like — bargaining? Pleading? The kind of voice a man uses when he's trying to make a deal he already knows is lost. And then a splash. One splash. Not big. The kind of splash a body makes, not a car. I told myself it was a rock. Somebody skipping rocks at night in the rain. I was twenty-three years old and I told myself it was a rock, and then I went home and slept, and in the morning Elias Vann was dead in that water, and I let myself believe the timing was a coincidence because the alternative was that my whole life — the academy, the badge, the card from Odile Harrow every damn December — was built on top of a lie I had personally agreed to stand on."

The recorder's red light burned. Somewhere below them, on the street, a car passed, tires hissing on wet asphalt, and neither of them moved until the sound faded.

"Why now?" August asked. Not accusing. Genuinely asking, the way a coroner asks — *what killed this, and when.*

"Because of the archivist." Her jaw tightened. "Wendell Moss. He helped you pull the shipping ledgers, and two days later he's floating in the retention pond behind the county annex, and the report says he slipped. I processed half that scene, Mr. Joubert. I bagged his glasses. They were folded. Folded, and dry on the inside of the lens, and lying six feet from where they say he went in. You don't fold your glasses to slip on a bank. You fold them to see something clearly, or you fold them after somebody takes them off your face." She heard her own voice rise and made herself lower it. "I've been doing fine for seven years. Wendell did fine for thirty-one. Fine isn't protection. Fine is just the queue."

August closed the legal pad. For a long moment he looked at her across the lamplight, and then he said, "You understand what happens next. Once this recording exists, you're not a witness anymore. You're a problem. To people who solve problems the way we've been discussing."

"I know."

"They'll come at you gently first. A transfer offer. A promotion. Odile Harrow will probably send flowers. And if gentle doesn't work—"

"I know." She stood, and her knees ached like she'd been kneeling. "My sister lives in Spokane. My mother's in a home in Coos Bay with round-the-clock staff and no idea what year it is. There's nobody to squeeze, is what I'm saying. I made sure of that a long time ago, without knowing why. Maybe I always knew." She took the cap off the hook and turned it in her hands once, twice. "Type it up. Sign it yourself as witness. Keep the original somewhere that isn't your office."

"Why not my office?"

"Because your office has a spare key that more than one person in this town knows about," she said, and saw him go still, "and because I've watched what happens to places where evidence sleeps in Greywater Bay. Paper doesn't drown, Mr. Joubert. But buildings burn."

She put the cap on. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, fog pressing against the frosted glass behind her like something waiting to be let in.

"He was a good man," she said. "Elias Vann. I met him twice — he came into the substation once about a noise complaint, and he thanked me. Actually thanked me, like I was a person and not a uniform. When I heard the splash, some part of me already knew whose voice that was. I've known for seven years. I just needed somebody to make it official." She managed something that was almost a smile. "Congratulations. You're official."

Then she was gone, boots on the stairs, and the street door closing, and August Joubert alone with the red light still burning and the ledger open to a page where, Ilsa would learn later, he had already written her name in pencil — waiting, all along, for her to make it ink.

* * *

The trouble began at 6:12 the next morning, though Ilsa wouldn't understand until much later that it had begun the moment the recorder clicked on.

She came on shift early, as always, signed the board, poured the terrible station coffee, and was halfway through the overnight log when Sergeant Pruitt walked past her desk and set something down beside her keyboard without breaking stride: a plain white envelope, no stamp, her name typed on it in a font she didn't recognize.

Inside was a single sheet. *Effective immediately, Deputy Brandt is reassigned to court liaison duties, County Seat, effective Monday. Housing stipend included. Congratulations.* No signature. Underneath, in different ink, in handwriting so elegant it looked engraved, someone had added: *Spokane is lovely this time of year. So is Coos Bay. — O.H.*

Ilsa sat very still. Around her the station went on breathing — phones, radios, the coffee maker gurgling, Deputy Cho laughing at something on his phone — and none of it knew, none of it could know, that the room had just tilted. She thought about the envelope. She thought about the recording, sealed in an envelope in August Joubert's safe, or under a floorboard, or wherever careful men kept things in towns like this. She thought about Wendell Moss's folded glasses, dry on the inside.

At 9:40 — the same minute, she noticed, the same minute to the minute, and wondered if she imagined it — she radioed dispatch: *Unit 4, out of service, personal.* And she drove, not to the county seat, not to the courthouse, but out the coastal road past the shuttered cannery, to the one place in Greywater Bay where the fog lifted earliest and a person could see a long way in every direction, and she parked where she could watch the road behind her.

They came at noon. A gray sedan, county plates, two men she didn't know, moving unhurried, the way men move when they are certain. They parked behind her cruiser, boxing it in with a courtesy that was worse than force, and the passenger got out and walked to her window and smiled, and Ilsa Brandt — twenty-three years old for seven years, and suddenly, finally, thirty — rolled the window down and put her hand in her lap, on her phone, screen already dark, already recording.

"Deputy Brandt," the man said pleasantly. "Sheriff Harrow would like a word. About your future."

And Ilsa smiled back, and thought: *dominoes fall both ways,* and got into their car.
