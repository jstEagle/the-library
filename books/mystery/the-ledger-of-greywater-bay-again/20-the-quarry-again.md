## 20. The Quarry, Again

The quarry had not changed. That was the first thing August noticed, standing at the rim where the gravel gave way to the drop, and it was the thing that frightened him most. Seven years of rain and rust and rot in the town below, and the water up here lay the same flat grey-green, patient as a held breath. The cliffs still wore their scars of pale stone. The old crane arm still leaned out over the deep like a gallows that had never been taken down.

He had certified one death here already. He could feel the place remembering it.

"August." Maren's voice came low from behind him, and he turned. She stood with her coat collar up against the wind coming off the water, her arms wrapped around a weatherproof case hugged to her chest like an infant. Inside it: the original toxicology report, Vann's name on it, the numbers that proved Elias Vann had been poisoned before he ever touched the water. Two days out of the drowned truck, sealed in plastic, photocopied three times over, scanned to four separate servers. And still she carried it like it was the only copy in the world, because in every way that mattered, it was.

"You didn't have to come," she said.

"Yes, I did." He looked back out over the water. "This is where my part started. It's where it ends."

They had agreed on the exchange at noon — Maren's terms, delivered through the lawyer who was handling the reopening petition: the original report would be surrendered to the state investigators tomorrow morning, but tonight, one last look, one last verification, at the site itself. August had known even as he signed off on the arrangement that it was a mistake. You did not bring the only true thing you owned back to the place where lies had been manufactured. But Maren had insisted — *he died here, August. He should get to be present for this* — and grief made its own arguments, and he had been too tired, too wrung out from the fire and the courthouse filings and the photograph of Ilsa Brandt behind the window of the Harrow guest house, to fight her.

Now, standing in the long slanting light of late afternoon, he understood how completely they had been expected.

The car came up the quarry road without hurry. A dark sedan, county plates, the kind of vehicle that belonged everywhere and nowhere. It stopped fifty yards away and sat there, engine ticking, and August felt the skin of his forearms tighten.

"Maren." He said it quietly, without moving his lips much. "Behind me."

She saw it a half-second after he did. The driver's door opened and a man stepped out — big through the shoulders, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had done violence often enough that it no longer interested him. August knew the face from thirty years of Harrow functions: Ray Cutler, Odile's man, the one who fixed things so quietly that most of Greywater Bay believed nothing had ever been broken. He had carried Odile's coat at the gala. He had held doors. He had smiled at August across canapés for three decades.

He was not smiling now.

"Dr. Joubert." Cutler's voice carried easily across the gravel. "Mrs. Harrow would like the case."

"And I'd like my friend Elias Vann alive," August said. "We're both going to be disappointed."

Cutler walked toward them, boots crunching, and August put his body between the man and Maren and hated himself for how little protection that was. Up close, Cutler smelled of cigarettes and engine oil, and his eyes went past August to the case in Maren's arms the way a man looks at a wound he intends to close.

"There doesn't need to be a scene," Cutler said. "You hand it over, you walk down the hill, and Monday morning nobody remembers any of this. That's been the arrangement in this town for a hundred years, Doctor. It's worked fine."

"It's worked fine for the Harrows."

"It's kept the lights on." For just a moment something moved behind Cutler's face — not conscience, August thought, but memory, the dull resentment of a man who had spent his whole life being the instrument rather than the reason. "You think this town runs on fishing and tourism? It runs on people deciding, once in a while, that some things are better under the water. Mrs. Harrow is offering you the same courtesy she offered everyone else. Don't make me offer less."

"The report's copied," Maren said from behind him. Her voice was steady but August could hear the wire pulled taut inside it. "Four places. Burning it changes nothing."

"Burning it changes what a court sees," Cutler said. "And courts are all that matter now, thanks to you two." He took another step. "Last time. The case."

August's mind was doing what it always did in crisis — going cold, going procedural, cataloguing. Fifty yards to the sedan. Thirty feet of gravel slope to the water's edge. The crane arm overhead. His own heart, sixty-eight years old, hammering like a boy's. He thought, absurdly, of Dane — *Dane will come, Dane has to come* — and then thought of the note Dane had slipped him two nights ago, folded into the file of reopened cases at the sheriff's office: *Anniversary Eve. Quarry. If you're going to do something stupid, do it where I can see.* Dane knew. Dane had known since the fire, maybe since the archivist, maybe since seven years ago when he signed off on his brother's story with his eyes carefully elsewhere.

But knowing and arriving were different verbs, and Cutler was ten feet away now, and the wind off the water smelled of stone and old iron.

"No," August said.

Cutler sighed, and the sigh was worse than a shout, because it was the sigh of a workman facing a job he'd hoped to avoid. He reached into his coat.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way violence does — each fragment complete and terrible and disconnected from the ones around it.

The gun coming out, black and small against the grey sky. Maren screaming his name. August grabbing for her, dragging her sideways, the case swinging between them like a pendulum. The shot — not at them, at the ground, a warning that kicked up gravel and rang off the cliff faces in a dozen false echoes. Cutler advancing, saying something about *the case, give me the goddamn case*, and Maren backing toward the edge with it clutched to her chest because she would rather fall than surrender it, and August seeing that, seeing her heels find the lip of the drop, and doing the only arithmetic left to him.

He threw himself at Cutler.

He was not a fighting man. He was sixty-eight years old and he had spent his life among the dead precisely because they asked nothing of him but accuracy. But grief and shame are fuels, and he had seven years' worth banked, and he hit the bigger man low and hard around the waist and felt them both go down together in a grinding sprawl of gravel and breath. The gun skittered loose. Somewhere above him Maren was shouting — not for help, he realized dimly; she was shouting into her phone, the address, the quarry, *now, please, now* — and then Cutler's fist found the side of his head and the world went white and ringing.

August rolled. Gravel tore his palms. He got a knee under himself and saw the gun lying between them, equidistant, absurd, like the start of some schoolyard game neither of them wanted to play. Cutler lunged for it. August lunged too, and got there second, but he got there angry — he raked the pistol away with his fingertips and sent it spinning toward the water, and heard it go over the edge with a sound too small to matter.

Then headlights swept the rim of the quarry.

An engine roared up the access road, and a truck slewed to a stop with its door already open, and Dane Harrow came out of it with his weapon drawn and his badge held high and his face lit up with something August had never seen on it in fifty years of friendship: open war.

"Sheriff's office!" Dane's voice cracked across the quarry like a whip. "Cutler! Hands! *Hands!*"

Cutler froze, crouched, calculating. August knelt in the gravel between them, blood running warm from his scalp into his eye, watching his oldest friend hold a gun on his family's man and understanding that everything — the whole grey architecture of Greywater Bay, the hundred years of quiet arrangements — hung on the next three seconds.

"Ray," Dane said, quieter now. "Put your hands up. I'm taking you in. Assault, brandishing, whatever Mrs. Harrow told you to do out here — it's done. It's all done. The report's real, the copies exist, the state's coming tomorrow. There's no version of this where you win."

"There's a version where I finish," Cutler said.

"No." Dane's voice broke on it. "There isn't. Not anymore. Get on the ground, Ray. Please. I've known you since Little League. Don't make me—"

Cutler moved.

Later, August could never say whether the man meant to rush Dane or simply lost his footing on the wet gravel at the lip of the slope — whether it was choice or chance or the quarry itself reaching up after seven years to collect. What he saw was this: Cutler coiling to lunge, Dane firing a warning that went wide, and Cutler's boot finding the slick seam of stone where the runoff had polished forty years of rain into glass. The big man's arms windmilled. For one suspended instant he hung at the edge, upright, almost dignified, his eyes finding August's with something in them that was not fear exactly — recognition, maybe, the look of a man meeting the consequence he had always known was waiting somewhere.

Then the water took him.

The splash was enormous and then, immediately, small. The surface closed. Rings spread outward, silvered in the dying light, widened, and were gone.

For a moment nobody moved. The wind came off the cliffs and stirred the flat water as if nothing had happened, and August knelt in the gravel with blood in his eye and thought: *that's where they put Elias. That's where they put all of it.* The bay taking its own back. The ledger balancing itself in ways no court could order.

"Dane—" Maren started.

"He went in," Dane said. His voice sounded far away. He hadn't lowered the gun. "I saw. He slipped. I *saw* him slip." He was saying it to the water, or to himself, or to some jury that existed only in the future he was already dreading. Then he holstered the weapon and was moving, shrugging off his jacket, kicking off his boots, and August grabbed his arm.

"Dane. The cold. At this depth, the cold—"

"I know what's down there." Dane looked at him, and his eyes were wet and furious and older than either of them. "I know exactly what's down there, August. I've known for seven years." He pulled free. "Get a light. Get a rope. Anything. Move."

* * *

They found him in eleven minutes, which was nine minutes longer than anyone survives that water in October.

Dane went in twice. The first time he came up gasping, blue-lipped, empty-handed, and August hauled him onto the rocks by the collar while Maren screamed herself hoarse calling 911 with a signal that flickered like a candle. The second time Dane tied the rope from his own truck around his chest and let August and Maren pay it out hand over hand, and when he came up the third time he had an arm locked under Cutler's chin and nothing left in his face at all.

They got him onto the gravel. August did what he had trained his whole life to do and had prayed he would never do here — he knelt, he checked, he pressed, he counted. The body under his hands was heavy and cooling and utterly beyond anything he could offer, and some part of him, the coroner part, the part that had been keeping this town's ledger of deaths for thirty years, noted the facts with terrible clarity: no pulse, no respiration, fixed pupils, water in the airway consistent with drowning while alive, duration consistent with—

He stopped himself. He put his hands flat on the dead man's chest and bowed his head.

Maren knelt beside him. She didn't touch him. She just stayed there, close enough that he could feel her breathing, while sirens finally began to climb the coast road, thin and far away and much too late.

"He was going to kill us," she said quietly. "Or take the report. Either way."

"I know."

"He slipped."

"I know what I saw," August said. And he did know — he had seen the wet stone, the failed footing, the physics of it. He also knew that he had thrown the first blow, that his rage had put them all at that edge, that a man was dead on the gravel in front of him and August Joubert had wanted, in the white-hot half-second before the fall, for something exactly like this to happen. Catharsis, he thought. They write about catharsis in books. Nobody writes about how it tastes. It tastes like quarry water. It tastes like everyone who ever went in.

Dane sat apart from them, soaked to the skin, staring at the water. When the first cruiser's lights washed blue across the cliffs, he stood, and squared his shoulders, and became for one last moment the Sheriff of Greywater Bay.

"When they ask," he said, not looking at August, "you tell it straight. All of it. Your swing, my shots, the slip. Every piece. Because if we lie about one inch of tonight, Odile wins anyway. She'll have made liars of us all, and that's the same as winning." He finally turned, and what was in his face was not forgiveness and not accusation but something rawer — the end of a friendship's innocence, and underneath it, stubbornly, the friendship itself. "Tomorrow you testify. Tomorrow I serve the warrant on my own mother. Tonight—" his voice caught, and he mastered it the way he'd mastered everything his whole life, barely, "tonight we tell the truth about Ray Cutler, and God help us, we mean it."

The paramedics came down the slope. The floodlights came on, turning the quarry into a stage, turning the water into hammered tin. August gave his statement to a state trooper with numb hands and blood drying on his face, and told it straight, every piece, and watched them zip the bag.

Before they lifted it, he did the thing he had done for thirty years, the thing he had promised Maren he would keep doing until the ledger was closed. He leaned close and spoke the dead man's name aloud, so that somebody in this town would mark that he had been here, that he had been a man, that he had gone into the water the way they all eventually go into the water.

"Raymond Cutler," August said. "Entered the record."

Then he looked up at the rim of the quarry, where the road climbed away toward the Harrow house and its lit windows, and saw, briefly, a shape at the top — small, upright, motionless, watching. Watching the lights, the bag, the water. Watching the century end.

By the time the trooper followed his gaze, the rim was empty.

But August knew who it had been. And he knew, with the certainty of a man who has read a town's death records his whole life, that Odile Harrow had seen everything she needed to see — and that whatever she did with the hours remaining to her, she would not be waiting calmly at home for dawn.

Maren's phone buzzed in her pocket. She read the screen, and went white, and turned it toward him.

A single message, from a number with no name:

**Come see me tonight, children. Before the lawyers wake up. Bring the ledger. — O.**
