## 9. The Constable's Quiet Doubt

Constable Emilio Reyes had been a policeman for nineteen years, and in all that time he had learned exactly one thing worth teaching: that the truth in Little Hawley did not live where the paperwork said it lived.

He sat in his office at the station — *office* was generous; it was a converted broom cupboard with a radiator that clanked like a man in chains — and looked at the file box on his desk. It was brown, ordinary, and heavier than any box its size had a right to be. Stenciled on the side in faded block letters: **ELLERY, R. — DECEASED ESTATE / CLOSED**.

Seven years closed. He had requested it twice. Twice refused.

He poured himself coffee from the dented percolator and drank it black, because milk was a comfort he'd stopped allowing himself somewhere around the second refusal. Outside the window, October rain came down in long gray threads, stitching the market square to the pavement. The bookshop across the way had its lights on. He could see Ada Villalobos moving behind the glass, shelving or pretending to shelve, her silhouette sharp and upright as a bookmark left standing in a closed book.

She didn't know yet what she'd inherited. Or maybe she was starting to. The dead man on the marsh road had been wearing her clothes, and Reyes had watched her face when she'd identified the cardigan — watched the exact moment her careful, alphabetized life developed a hairline fracture. He'd seen that look before, in other towns, on other faces. It was the look of a person realizing the ground under them had been poured by someone else's hands.

Reyes set down his cup and opened the box.

* * *

The Ellery file was thin. That was the first wrong thing. A woman found dead at the bottom of her own cellar stairs, alone in a locked shop, no witnesses, an inquest ruled accidental — such a death should have generated paper the way a storm generates debris. Statements, photographs, diagrams of the staircase, the coroner's measurements of the steps themselves, the angle of descent, the height of the banister. Instead there were eleven pages. Eleven pages for a human life ending at the foot of a staircase.

He read them again anyway, though he knew them by heart. He knew the way Constable Bramley — retired now, dead two years — had written up the scene in a hand so neat it looked printed. He knew the single photograph: Ruth Ellery on the flagstones, one arm flung out, her reading glasses folded neatly beside her head as if she'd taken them off to die tidily. He knew the witness statements, four of them, each short as a shopping list. Edith March, sister-in-law: *Ruth was unsteady on the stairs, always had been.* The doctor: *consistent with a fall.* Two neighbors whose statements said nothing at all in forty words apiece.

And he knew the eleventh page, which was not a page from the investigation at all but a memo on county letterhead, dated nine days after the death:

> *In light of the coroner's finding, no further inquiry is warranted. File to be sealed at the direction of the Chief Constable's office. No copies to be circulated.*

No reason given. No signature beyond an illegible flourish. Nine days. In his experience, files got sealed after years, after appeals, after lawyers made nuisances of themselves. Nine days meant someone had picked up a telephone before the ink on the inquest was dry.

Reyes had been twenty-nine when Ruth Ellery died. New to Little Hawley, new to the collar, still believing the job was mostly about finding things out. He remembered the funeral — half the town there, flowers like a florist's bankruptcy, everyone saying *such a lovely woman, kept herself to herself, terrible accident.* He remembered noticing, even then, that nobody cried very hard. Grief has a texture. This had been relief wearing grief's coat.

The first time he'd requested the file reopened was three years later, after he'd found something odd. A delivery receipt, misfiled in a box of traffic citations, showing Ruth Ellery had received a parcel the morning of her death — heavy, per the courier's note, *one wooden crate, 11 kg*. No crate was ever logged among her effects. He'd written his request carefully, respectfully, citing the discrepancy. The reply came back in six days: *Request declined. Matter closed.* No name. Just the seal of the Chief Constable's office, stamped so hard it had embossed the paper.

The second time was two years after that. By then he'd learned to be quieter about things. He'd found that Ruth Ellery had made a will eight months before her death leaving the bookshop — freehold, not leasehold — to a trust, with instructions that it be let "at nominal rent to a suitable tenant." A suitable tenant. Seven years later, Ada Villalobos arrived from nowhere and signed a lease so cheap Reyes had assumed it was a clerical error. Nominal rent. Suitable tenant. Someone had gone looking for a person, and Ada had walked into the net without ever seeing it.

His second request was declined in four days. This time there was a note, handwritten, unsigned, in blue ink: *Constable — some doors are shut for the town's good. Don't knock again.* He had sat with that note for a long evening, turning it over, deciding whether to be afraid or angry. In the end he'd settled on tired, which was both and neither, and filed the note behind the radiator where no one would find it unless they went looking.

Now there was a dead man on the marsh road wearing Ada Villalobos's cardigan, clutching a page of ciphered ledger, and the door Reyes had been told not to knock on was kicking itself open.

* * *

At ten o'clock, Inspector Dane came through the station like weather.

Dane was Chief Inspector now — Reyes corrected himself automatically; rank mattered to Dane the way water mattered to fish — and he filled the corridor the way he filled every room, broad and unhurried, smelling faintly of pipe tobacco he wasn't allowed to smoke indoors anymore. His father had been Chief Superintendent here before him. Before that, his grandfather had chaired the weir commission. There were Danes in the foundation stones of this town the way there were bones in a churchyard.

"Morning, Emilio." Dane never called him Constable. First names were a leash disguised as a collar. "How are we getting on with our marsh road gentleman?"

"We're getting on," Reyes said. "Identity's still open. No wallet, no papers. Dental records requested."

"And the clothing?"

"Confirmed purchased by Ms. Villalobos. Local shop, eighteen months ago. She recognized it straight off."

"Mm." Dane picked up a paperweight from the corner of Reyes's desk — a lump of polished marsh stone, a gift from his daughter — and turned it over as if assessing it for evidence. "Sad business. Drifter, most likely. These roads pull them in like moths. I'd keep the inquiry narrow, if I were you. Cause of death, identity, next of kin. Don't go wandering."

"Wandering where?"

Dane smiled. He had a good smile, well maintained, the smile of a man who'd been photographed shaking hands outside fêtes. "Wherever it is you wander, Emilio. You've always had a bit of the bloodhound. It's a virtue. Mostly." He set the stone down precisely where it had been, rotated so its flat face aligned with the desk edge. "The Villalobos woman. How is she bearing up?"

"She identified the clothing. She's shaken. She's also asking questions."

"About?"

"The ledger page. About Ruth Ellery."

Something moved behind Dane's eyes — not quickly, nothing quick about Dane, but certainly, the way a shadow crosses a field when cloud passes over the sun. "Ruth Ellery died seven years ago. Accident. Sad. Closed." He patted the doorframe twice, a little drumroll of finality. "Keep it tidy, Emilio. You're better at tidy than you let on."

Then he was gone, down the corridor, his footsteps unhurried as a tide going out.

Reyes sat very still for a moment. Then he reached behind the radiator and took out the blue-ink note, and laid it on the desk beside the Ellery file, and looked at the handwriting for a long time.

He had never been able to prove whose hand it was. But he had spent nineteen years reading reports, and Dane's father had signed Reyes's first commendation, and the letter *g* in *good* looped the same way. Like a noose drawn by someone fond of drawing nooses.

* * *

He went to see Ada Villalobos at closing time, when the square was emptying and the rain had thinned to a mist that made the streetlamps bloom.

The bell over the shop door announced him and she looked up from the counter with the wariness he'd come to expect — the wariness of a woman who had learned, somewhere before Little Hawley, that official attention was rarely benign. Her eyes went to his uniform first, then to his face, doing whatever arithmetic she did.

"Constable Reyes."

"Ms. Villalobos. Five minutes, if you've got them."

"I've got them." She didn't offer tea. He liked that about her; she didn't perform hospitality she didn't feel.

He stood in the aisle between Biography and Travel, because sitting felt too much like an interrogation and he needed her to understand this wasn't one. Or wasn't only one. "I want to tell you something, and then I want you to decide whether to trust me. I'm aware those are bad odds."

"That's honest, at least."

"The file on Ruth Ellery's death is sealed. Sealed nine days after she died, on the order of the Chief Constable's office. I've asked twice to have it reopened. Refused twice. The second refusal came with a note telling me not to ask again." He watched her take that in — watched her hands go still on the counter, the way a person goes still when the floor shifts. "I can't show you the file. If I do and anyone learns of it, it'll be taken from you and my career ends, and worse, we'd both know who took it and neither of us could prove anything. That's how it works here. That's how it's worked for a long time."

"Why tell me this?" Her voice was level but he heard the strain under it, wire under cloth. "You don't know me. Seven years in this town and you've exchanged perhaps forty words with me."

That was true, and it deserved a true answer. "Because a man died on the marsh road wearing your clothes, holding a piece of paper that matches a ledger I believe belonged to Ruth Ellery. Because whoever killed him wanted that to point at you, or wanted you frightened, and either way you're inside something now whether you chose it or not. And because—" He paused. Nineteen years of keeping his mouth shut made the next sentence expensive. "Because I requested that file reopened twice, and both times I was refused by people above me, and I have spent seven years wondering whether Ruth Ellery fell down those stairs or was helped. If there's a third option — that someone else is asking the same question — I'd rather stand next to them than watch them drown alone."

The rain ticked against the window. Somewhere in the stacks, the old boiler knocked twice and went quiet.

"You think I'm in danger," she said.

"I think the last person who connected herself to that ledger died at the bottom of a staircase, and the paperwork was finished before her body was cold." He held her gaze. "I'm not telling you to stop. I've learned you can't stop people like you, and frankly I don't want you to. I'm telling you that when you need a policeman, you should decide fast whether I'm one."

"And which are you?"

He almost smiled. "Ask me again when it matters. You'll know by what I do, not what I say." He turned for the door, then stopped. "One more thing. The inspector running oversight on your case is Chief Inspector Dane. If he offers you help, take it politely. If he asks you questions, answer none of them fully. And if he ever invites you anywhere — anywhere at all — you send word to me first. However late. However inconvenient."

Her eyes narrowed. "You suspect your own inspector."

"I suspect everyone," Reyes said. "It's the job. What keeps me awake is knowing how few of them I can actually catch." He pushed the door open onto the mist. "Lock this behind me. Not because of tonight. Because of the next seven years."

* * *

He walked back to the station the long way, past the church and down toward the cut of the river, because he wanted to see the water.

Hawley Weir stood dark against the last gray light, the sluice gates dripping their slow arithmetic into the pool below. Twenty years ago a young man had died up there — Reyes had been a constable in another county then, but he'd read the old report when he transferred in, back when he still read everything. Misadventure. Slippery footing, high water, a lad named— he'd forgotten the name, which shamed him; he made a point now of never forgetting names. The family had moved away within the year. The file was thin too. Everything in this town was thin, once you got close enough to see through it.

He stood on the footbridge and watched the water fold over itself, black and patient, and thought about the shape of the thing he was caught in. A death sealed in nine days. A ledger paying nine people for seven years. A stranger dressed in a bookseller's clothes, murdered on the road where everything in this town eventually washed up. And above it all, the Danes, generation upon generation, their signatures looping like nooses down the decades of county paper.

Ally or bait. Ada Villalobos had the right of it; those were the only two things he could be, and he couldn't even swear to himself which one he'd turn out to be. Honesty was a muscle. He'd exercised it in small ways for years — the report he'd written accurately, the statement he'd refused to soften — small weights, low reps, nothing that would tear anything. Soon, he suspected, he'd be asked to lift something heavy.

When he got back to the station, there was a message waiting on his desk, taken by the night clerk in his round schoolboy hand:

*Call for you while you were out. Wouldn't leave a name. Said to tell you: check the ninth initial. She says you'll know what it means. She sounded calm, sir. Young, maybe. Said you'd want to hurry.*

Reyes stood holding the slip of paper while the radiator clanked its chains and the rain resumed against the glass.

*Ninth initial.* He hadn't decoded the ledger — he'd only glimpsed the torn page across a mortuary table, columns of dates and sums and letters. But he knew, with the flat certainty of a man who has spent nineteen years learning where truths hide, that the ninth initial was the door the blue-ink note had told him not to knock on.

Someone had just slipped the key under it.

He put on his coat and went to find Ada Villalobos.
