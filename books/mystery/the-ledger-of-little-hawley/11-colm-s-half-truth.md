## 11. Colm's Half-Truth

Colm Okafor had been many kinds of cold in his life — the cold of a ferry deck in February, the cold of a police cell in Cork with one blanket and a dripping pipe, the cold of a woman's silence after she learned what he'd done for money. But he had never been cold like this: the cold of Little Hawley in late October, when the fog came off the marsh at dusk and sat in the streets like something that had drowned there and refused to leave.

He crouched behind the skip at the back of the bookshop with a cigarette he wasn't smoking, because the ember would show, and watched the light in the upstairs window where Ada Villalobos was still awake. He knew her rhythms now the way he knew tide tables. Lamp on at seven, off at eleven-forty most nights, later when the ledger was out. He could tell by the angle of her shadow against the blind whether she was reading or writing. Tonight she was writing. The shadow's arm moved in small, deliberate strokes, like a woman signing her name over and over.

He hated that he knew this. He hated it the way a man hates a scar he can't stop touching.

The second page was inside his jacket, against his chest, folded into eighths. He had taken it three days ago from the strongbox they'd dug out of the marsh — taken it while she was photographing the cash, while her back was turned and the fog was coming in off the water like a curtain drawn for his benefit alone. He told himself it was for safety. Evidence. Leverage. He told himself a lot of things; it was the one talent he'd never had to pay for.

But it wasn't safety. It was the letters. The letters signed *R.E.*, and the fact that they had kept coming for six years after Ruth Ellery went into the ground, and the fact that he had never once said so out loud, and the fact that if he said it out loud, everything he'd told Ada — the confession he'd half-made, the redemption he'd half-hoped for — would curdle into the thing it had always been: another half-truth, told by a man who had never in his life given anyone the whole of anything.

The lamp upstairs went out. Eleven-fifty-two. He waited the customary ten minutes — she sometimes came back down for a forgotten book — and then he moved.

Not to the shop. To the yard behind the old tannery, where the brick kilns stood open like throats and the wind off the marsh funnelled through and carried the smoke away before it could be seen from the road. He'd found the spot his first week in Hawley, back when he still believed he was a tourist. It was where he burned the reports. He'd been burning them for six years, one every few weeks, small squares of cheap paper with a stranger's handwriting on them describing the comings and goings at Villalobos Books. He'd never kept one before. He didn't fully understand why he'd kept this page.

He did understand, though. That was the worst of it. He understood the way you understand, at three in the morning, exactly what you are.

He knelt at the mouth of the second kiln, struck a match, and fed the torn ledger page to the flame.

It caught at the corner. The fire walked up the fold, and in the light he saw, for half a second, the columns of figures, the dates, and — in the margin, in that same cramped hand — the initials *A.V.* with a sum beside them, and beneath it a word he had never managed to read before the paper blackened.

"You're burning my evidence."

He didn't jump. He'd known she was there for a full minute — had smelled the marsh mud on her boots, had heard her stop at the corner of the tannery wall and go quiet, the way quiet people go quiet, which is louder than shouting. He closed his eyes and let the last corner of the page go up.

"Technically," he said, "it's your evidence I'm burning. Which is worse. I know."

Ada came around the kiln into the firelight. She had her coat buttoned wrong — one hole off, the lapels uneven — and her hair was down and unbrushed, and she looked, in that moment, less like the composed ex-detective the town whispered about and more like what she was: a tired woman who had followed a liar through fog because she couldn't sleep with a question in the house.

"How long have you known I was out here?" she asked.

"Since the bridge."

"You let me walk the whole way."

"I wanted to see if you'd follow me in." He looked at the kiln. "You did."

"That's not an answer to anything, Colm."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. It's the shape of my whole life, actually. You might frame it."

* * *

She didn't sit, but he did, on the low brick lip of the kiln, and she stood over him with her arms crossed, and the fire between them burned down to a red breathing glow, and the fog pressed in at the edges of the yard like an audience.

"Start with the page," she said. "Where did you get it. When."

"The strongbox. The night we dug it up."

"You said you saw nothing worth taking."

"I said I saw nothing worth *showing* you. It's not the same sentence." He rubbed his hands together, not for warmth — the kiln was warm at his back — but because his hands wanted something to do besides shake. "There was a page folded into the lining of the lid. Taped. Older paper than the rest, older ink. I took it because I recognised the handwriting."

"You'd never seen Ruth Ellery's handwriting."

"Not hers." He made himself look up at her. This was the part. This was the part he had been carrying since the marsh, since before the marsh, since a wet Tuesday in spring six years ago when a brown envelope had come to the caravan park addressed to *C. OKAFOR* in handwriting he'd assumed belonged to some fussy solicitor. "Mine. I've been getting letters in that hand for six years."

The fog seemed to lean closer.

"Letters," Ada said.

"About you." He laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "That's — God, that's a terrible sentence. Let me do it properly." He pressed his palms flat on his knees. "When I came to Hawley, I wasn't drifting. I was hired. A letter arrived at the caravan site in Whitstable — I'd been there maybe a month, doing whatever it is I do, which is nothing, which is watching, it's the only thing I've ever been good at — and the letter said there was a bookshop in Little Hawley, and a woman who ran it, and that she would pay me — no name, just *I will pay you* — thirty pounds a week to note who came and went. Who stayed late. Who argued with her. Who she met on the marsh road. And to post my notes to a box number in Ashford."

"And you did it."

"For six years." His voice went flat and small. "Every week. Thirty pounds, later forty, in cash, in an envelope, no return address. And I told myself it was harmless. She's a retired copper, I told myself, someone's watching her for her own good, an old colleague, a pension thing, a — I could make up reasons faster than I could spend the money, that's the truth of it. And then a man turned up dead on the marsh road wearing her cardigan, and every reason I'd ever made up caught fire all at once, and I was standing in the ashes holding the match."

Ada hadn't moved. That was the thing about her that unnerved people, he'd learned it from the letters he wrote about her — she could go so still that she stopped being a person and became a fact.

"The letters," she said. "You said the handwriting. Show me."

"I burned them. Years of them. Every Friday after posting my notes, I'd burn the old one. Habit. Cleanliness. Guilt wearing an apron." He swallowed. "But the last one — the one that came after the body was found, telling me to stop, that the arrangement was concluded, that I was to leave Hawley — I kept it. I don't know why. I keep saying I don't know why and it's a lie every time. I kept it because the handwriting frightened me, and things that frighten me, I've learned to keep close. Things you throw away come back. Things you keep, you can face."

He took his wallet from his inside pocket — not the jacket pocket where the page had been; the other one, the one over his heart, which was either sentiment or superstition or both — and drew out a small envelope, soft with handling, and handed it up to her.

She opened it under the kiln's glow. He watched her face while she read, and it was like watching ice form on a pond: the change moved across her slowly and then all at once.

"This is Ruth Ellery's hand," she said. "I've spent a week with her ledger. I'd know it upside down. This is it — the same cramped slant, the same way she crosses her sevens." She looked at the signature. "*R.E.*" Then at the postmark. Then at him. "Colm. This is postmarked four years ago. Ruth Ellery died eleven years ago. I checked the stone in the churchyard the first day."

"I know."

"You *know*."

"I checked it the first week I got here." He heard his own voice go quiet, the way it did when the truth finally got its boots on. "I stood in that churchyard with a letter in my pocket signed by a dead woman, and I told myself it was a relative. A sister. Someone with the same hand, or a hand copied from hers — families do that, don't they, they write alike. I told myself it was an eccentric. I told myself thirty pounds a week was thirty pounds a week." He shook his head. "I'm very good at telling myself things. It's the same talent as watching. You point it outward and never once at the glass."

"Who else knew about the letters?"

"No one. That's why I'm telling you now, at a kiln, at midnight, like a criminal, because that's —" he gestured at himself, at the fog, at the whole damp geography of his life — "that's the register I operate in."

Ada turned the envelope over. "The box number. Ashford. Did you ever collect from it? Ever see who paid?"

"Never. Cash came to the caravan office. The manager said a courier left it. I asked once, the first year — asked the manager to describe her. He said *her*. That's all I got. Her." He looked up. "But there's more, and you're going to want to hit me, so I'd sit down if I were you, only there's nowhere to sit, so just — brace."

"Colm."

"The night of the body. The night the man in your cardigan was dumped on the marsh road." He made himself hold her eyes, because the alternative was the fire, and the fire was easier. "I saw it. I told you I saw it from the sea wall, at a distance, in fog. That part was true. What I left out — what I've been choking on for a week — is that I wasn't out there by accident. The last letter told me to be. It said: *the arrangement concludes on the fourteenth. Be at the sea wall after midnight. Watch the marsh road. Note the registration of any vehicle that stops.* And I went, because I always went, because six years of obedience doesn't fall off a man like a coat, it comes off stitch by stitch, and I hadn't finished unpicking the last one."

"And what did you see?"

"A van. No lights. It stopped where the body was found. One person got out, dragged something to the verge — I thought it was rubbish, God help me, I *thought it was rubbish* — and got back in. And the person was wearing a long dark coat." His mouth had gone dry. "Your coat, Ada. The one with the missing button. I've seen it hanging behind your shop door two hundred times. I wrote about it in my notes — *subject wears a dark wool coat, missing button, left cuff* — because the letters asked for detail, and detail is the one honest thing I've ever given anyone."

The fog took her stillness and made it terrible. When she spoke, her voice was level in the way that ice is level.

"You watched a man being dumped on the road, wearing my coat, and you didn't come forward. You waited a week. You waited until you'd worked out which way the wind was blowing, and then you came to me with a story with holes in it and let me walk around inside it."

"Yes."

"Why now? Why the kiln, why tonight, why any of it?"

He looked at the fire. It had gone down to embers, and the embers were the colour of the postmark on the envelope in her hand.

"Because the letters stopped," he said. "The arrangement was *concluded*, the last one said. But this morning, before dawn, another envelope came to the caravan office. Same hand. Same *R.E.*" He reached into his pocket and laid a second envelope on the brick between them, unopened, its corner darkened with fog-damp. "I haven't read it. Six years, and I never once opened one before showing you, and I'm not starting now. Because whoever wrote it knew where the body would be. Knew what coat the killer would wear. Knew about a dead woman's handwriting well enough to wear it like a coat themselves." He looked up at her. "And I thought — you're the only person in this town who reads dead women's handwriting for a living."

Ada picked up the envelope. She turned it over once, twice, the way she'd turned the ledger pages, and he saw the detective come up in her like a lamp being lit in a window he'd been watching for six years without ever once understanding what the light was for.

"Colm," she said quietly. "This envelope is heavier than the last one."

"I know," he said. "I've been afraid to find out what's inside since four o'clock this morning."

* * *

She opened it with a pocketknife, cleanly, along the top seam, and tipped it over the flat brick beside the kiln.

A key fell out. Old iron, small, the teeth worn soft, tied with a length of faded green ribbon. And a card, written in that same dead woman's hand, in ink gone brown at the edges:

*For A.V. — Ruth's room. The house remembers what the town forgot. Come alone. He is watching the shop, not you. He has always been watching the shop.*

Ada read it twice. Then she read it aloud, and her voice didn't shake until the last line, and Colm — who had spent six years being paid to notice things — noticed that when it shook, it wasn't fear.

"Whose house?" he asked.

Ada was looking at the key, turning it in the firelight, and her face had gone the colour of the fog.

"Edith March's," she said. "The spare room over her garage. The one she's kept locked for seven years. The one she told me was full of Ruth's rubbish." She closed her hand around the key. "The one she's never once let me clean."
