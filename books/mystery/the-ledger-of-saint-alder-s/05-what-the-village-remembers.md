## 5. What the Village Remembers

The rain had stopped overnight, and Saint Alder's woke glistening.

Odile stood at the window of the keeper's cottage with a cup of tea going cold in her hand, watching water drip from the eaves like a slow metronome. Below her, the village arranged itself along a single crooked street: slate roofs, whitewashed walls gone grey at the corners, a church spire rising at the far end like a needle pinning the whole place to the earth. Smoke curled from chimneys. A dog trotted past the well with the self-importance of a creature on official business.

It looked, she thought, like a village from a painting sold in airport gift shops. The kind of image that existed to be consumed by people who would never have to live inside it.

She had been here four days. In that time she had read the ledger twice more, filled eleven pages of notes, and spoken to exactly one person — Wren Halloway, who had spent most of their conversations telling her to leave. Odile had been an estate lawyer for fourteen years precisely because she preferred documents to people. Documents did not lie about lying. Documents could be cross-examined. People simply looked at you with their grief or their guilt showing, and expected you to do something with it.

But the ledger had taken her as far as paper could go. Every generation's final entries were written in the same devotional hand — *the Lord provides, the Lord balances* — and beneath the piety, the arithmetic of death. If she wanted to understand why a village had spent two centuries treating murder as bookkeeping, she would have to ask the villagers themselves.

She finished the tea, pocketed her notebook, and went out into the wet morning.

* * *

Her first stop was the post office, which was also the general store, which was also — according to the faded sign above the door — Purcell & Daughter, Agents for Matters Spiritual and Temporal.

The daughter turned out to be a woman of about sixty named Edith Purcell, who was sorting letters behind a counter worn smooth by a century of elbows. She looked up when the bell rang, took Odile's measure in a single glance — coat, city shoes, notebook already half out of her bag — and said, "You'll be the lawyer, then."

"You know who I am?"

"Marlowe Vane sent word you'd come." Edith resumed sorting. "He sends word before he does anything. Always has. Man treats his own life like correspondence." She licked a stamp, pressed it down with unnecessary force. "What do you want to know?"

Straight to it, then. Odile appreciated that, even if it unsettled her. "I'm researching the parish ledger. The old one, kept in the church archive."

Something happened to Edith's face. It didn't close, exactly — it *settled*, the way silt settles in still water, obscuring whatever lay beneath. "Lovely book," she said. "Beautiful hand, some of those entries. My mother used to take me to see it when I was small. Feast days, mostly."

"To see it?"

"To be seen by it," Edith corrected mildly, and then seemed surprised at herself. She waved a hand. "Silly way of putting it. It's just a book. We're fond of it here. Very fond." She held out a letter. "This is yours, actually. Came yesterday. Cottage address."

Odile took it without looking. "Fond," she repeated. "That's an interesting word for it."

"Is it?" Edith smiled, and the smile reached her eyes without warming them. "We're fond of the sea too, and it's drowned three Finch men and a Purcell. Fondness isn't the same as trust, love. You'd know that, being in law." She nodded toward the door. "Try the inn. Tobias will talk your ear off. He's the only one who does."

Outside, Odile stood on the step and turned the envelope over. No return address. Her own name in a hand she almost recognized — a careful, old-fashioned script with a slight forward lean, as though the words were hurrying somewhere.

She put it in her coat unopened. One mystery at a time.

* * *

The Lamb and Lantern sat at the bend of the high street, its sign creaking on iron brackets shaped like ram's horns. Inside, low beams, a fire already lit against the damp, and the smell of bread and woodsmoke that hit Odile with a force entirely disproportionate to its cause — because it smelled, absurdly, impossibly, like her grandmother's kitchen in a house that had been demolished before Odile was six.

She stood in the doorway a moment too long, and the man behind the bar noticed.

"You've got the look of someone deciding whether to come in," he said. He was broad and easy-moving, maybe forty, with dark hair going silver at the temples and forearms dusted with flour. "Come in anyway. The decision's better made warm."

"I'm—"

"The lawyer." He was already drawing a pint of something amber. "Word travels here faster than the post. Tobias Finch. Sit anywhere; everywhere's the good seat this time of day." He set the pint in front of her unprompted. "On the house. Consider it a retainer."

"I don't work for free."

"Then consider it a bribe." His grin was quick and unguarded, and Odile felt something in her chest loosen a quarter-inch before she caught it and tied it back down. "You're asking questions about the ledger. Everyone knows. Nobody will tell you anything, which is why they're all talking about it."

"That's very circular."

"That's villages." He leaned on the bar. "Ask me what you like. I've no shame and a good memory."

So she asked. About the ledger's history — *kept by the Halloways as long as anyone remembers*. About the entries — *beautiful writing, Father Alder himself started it, or so they say*. About the balancing deaths — and here Tobias's hands, wiping down the bar, went briefly still.

"Every generation ends with one," he said. "Aye. We don't call them that in company."

"What do you call them?"

"The settling." He said it the way other men said *harvest* or *winter* — a season, a fact, neither good nor evil. "My grandfather called it the tithe. God's portion. Though he'd had a few pints when he explained it to me, so I'd take his theology lightly."

"And nobody finds that strange? A death every generation, on schedule?"

Tobias set down the cloth. For a moment the warmth in his face thinned, and she saw the thing underneath — not fear exactly, but the shape fear leaves behind after years of living beside it. "Strange is a city word, Ms. Brightwater. Out here we say *old*. There's a difference. Strange things you fix. Old things you keep." He picked up the cloth again. "You'll want to talk to Mrs. Tallow next door. And the schoolmaster. And—" he paused, "—you should know someone's been asking after you too."

"Who?"

"Didn't see. Saw the *asking*, though. Fella came round Tuesday wanting to know how long you'd be staying." He shrugged. "Could've been nothing. Could've been curiosity. We're short on entertainment."

"Or it could have been something."

"It could." He met her eyes, and there was nothing soft in it now. "Which is why you'll drink that pint slow, and you'll let me walk you back to the cottage when the light goes. Not because you need walking home. Because I'd like to know who else is on the road."

* * *

Mrs. Tallow proved to be ninety-one and sharp as a bramble, installed in a chair by her window like a magistrate. She remembered the last settling — sixty-one years ago, a Brightwater man found at the foot of the church steps, ruled an accident. "Ruled," she repeated, when Odile noted the verdict. "Yes. Everything here gets ruled, dear. That's what the ledger's for. So nothing has to be decided twice."

"Do you believe the ledger decides things?"

Mrs. Tallow looked at her for a long moment, and her eyes were clear and blue and terribly tired. "I believe a book that everyone believes becomes true," she said. "Same as a lie told often enough becomes history. Same as a debt unpaid becomes a curse." She folded her hands. "You want to know if I'm afraid of it. Everyone wants to know that. Here's my answer: I've lived ninety-one years under it, and I'm still here, and so are my children, and my grandchildren. Whatever the ledger takes, it takes rarely, and gently, and always someone whose time was nearly up anyway." Her voice dropped. "Until it doesn't. Ask the Finches about gentle."

At the door, she caught Odile's sleeve. "The girl Halloway," she said quietly. "Be kind to her. That post eats its keepers young. Her gran was the kindest woman in this county until the ledger got its teeth in, and then—" She released the sleeve. "Well. Then she was the keeper."

* * *

The schoolmaster, Mr. Ives, talked for forty minutes about paleography and the Alderite hand and the provenance of iron-gall ink, and managed to convey absolutely nothing except that he was terrified. He answered questions about the ledger's contents with questions about Odile's credentials. When she mentioned Marlowe Vane's name, he stood up so abruptly he knocked over a jar of pens.

"I have a class," he said. "I'm afraid I can't help you. I really am. Please don't come back."

* * *

By the time the light began to fail, Odile had collected a mosaic: reverence braided through dread, faith wearing the costume of folklore, and everywhere the same settled silence where the truth should have been. The village loved its ledger the way one loves a large animal in the field next door — admiring it, feeding it, keeping the fence mended, never quite turning one's back.

She was crossing the churchyard shortcut when she heard it: footsteps, a half-beat behind her own, stopping when hers stopped.

She did not turn around. Fourteen years of probate disputes had taught her that the person following you wants to know you've noticed, and she wasn't prepared to give it. Instead she lengthened her stride, cut between two leaning headstones, and came out onto the lane below the cottage — where Tobias Finch was leaning against a gatepost with a lantern, as if he'd been waiting all evening.

"There you are," he said pleasantly. Then his eyes moved past her shoulder, up the churchyard path, and his pleasantness drained away like water through gravel.

Odile turned. The path was empty. But the gate at the top stood open, and she was certain — certain — it had been closed when she entered.

"Who was it?" Tobias asked quietly.

"I don't know. I didn't see."

"Hm." He fell into step beside her without asking, lantern swinging. "Tuesday it was a question. Tonight it's a shadow. That's escalation, in my experience."

"Is that your experience often?"

"Often enough." They walked in silence for a stretch, gravel crunching, the cottage growing larger ahead. Then Tobias said, without preamble: "I had a sister."

Odile glanced at him. His face was lit and unlit by turns as the lantern swung.

"Maren," he went on. "Younger than me by two years. Bright as a new coin. Her name's in the ledger — written the week she was born, same as every child here, same as yours was, I'd wager, though you weren't born here." He stopped at the cottage gate and set the lantern on the post. "She died twenty-two years ago. Drowned in the mill pond, they said. Accident, they said. Ruled, they said." His jaw worked once. "I don't say anything anymore. But I remember everything. And if someone's following you, Ms. Brightwater, I'd take it kindly if you remembered that."

"Tobias—"

"Goodnight." He touched his cap, picked up the lantern, and walked away into the dark, leaving Odile standing at her own gate with the cold coming up off the ground and the word *Maren* ringing in her ears like a struck bell.

Inside, she locked the door, lit the lamp, and finally opened the envelope from her coat.

One line, in that hurrying old-fashioned hand:

*They will tell you the ledger is only belief. Do not believe them. It is belief — but belief has killed before, and it keeps excellent records.*

No signature. Only pressed beneath the note, thin and brown and long dead, a single leaf of alder.
