## 10. The Second Will

The rain had not stopped in three days, and Saint Alder's had begun to smell of it — wet stone and cold iron and the green rot of ivy giving up its grip on the churchyard wall. Odile had learned to read the village by its smells the way she once read clients by their silences. The inn smelled of woodsmoke and lies told kindly. The archive smelled of dust and patience. And the vestry office, where the parish clerk kept the registers that mattered, smelled of beeswax and something older underneath it, something like fear that had been polished over so many times it had become a kind of varnish.

She was there on a Tuesday morning, ostensibly to request certified copies of three burial entries from 1911 for a probate matter she had invented whole cloth — *the Brightwater estate, distant cousins, nothing you need concern yourself with* — because lying to clerks was a skill she'd honed across twenty years of estates, and because the truth would have gotten her shown the door.

The clerk, a narrow man named Pell whose collar always seemed a size too small for his neck, was slow about everything. He moved through the archive like a man wading through honey, and he asked questions the way other men fired warning shots. *And what relation were these Brightwaters to you, Miss Brightwater? A coincidence of surname, is it? Saint Alder's does seem to collect them.*

Odile answered each question with the flat pleasantness of a woman discussing weather, and while Pell climbed the ladder to the far shelf where the old burial registers slept under oilcloth, she let her eyes wander the room.

It was habit. It was also, by now, survival.

Since Marlowe Vane's confession — *I hired you to witness it, Miss Brightwater, not prevent it* — she had stopped trusting the architecture of her own safety. She checked the latch on Wren Halloway's cottage door twice each night. She noted which villagers watched her pass and which looked away too quickly. She had begun, without quite deciding to, cataloguing the parish machinery itself: who held keys, who held seals, who could sign what.

Because that was the thing Vane's confession had taught her, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: if her murder was already written in the ledger, then somewhere in this village there must be paper to match it. Death did not simply happen in places like Saint Alder's. It was *documented*. Certified. Filed. A body required a doctor's signature, a registrar's stamp, a line in a register. If the covenant intended to take her, the paperwork of her taking would have to exist — or be made to exist — before or shortly after the fact.

Lawyers thought this way. It was why she was good at her work. Every crime leaves a paper shadow, and if you know where the light comes from, you can find the shadow first.

Pell was still climbing. Odile drifted along the wall of filing cabinets, brass-handled, pre-war, their drawers labeled in faded copperplate: *BAPTISMS. BURIALS. MARRIAGES. NONCONFORMIST.* And at the end, half-hidden behind a stand of umbrellas, a drawer marked only *PENDING.*

Pending. An odd word for a parish archive. Pending implied something unfinished. Something waiting.

She glanced at the ladder. Pell's back was turned, his fingers walking the spines of registers with excruciating care.

She opened the drawer.

Inside lay perhaps two dozen folders, thin ones, the kind used for matters awaiting completion — unprobated wills, disputed tithes, correspondence awaiting reply. She fanned through them with one hand, keeping her ear tuned to the ladder's creak. Land disputes from the 1960s. A quaker marriage dispensation. A folder of undelivered letters tied with string.

And then a folder heavier than the rest, its card cover stiff with age but its contents crisp, new, out of place among the dust like a fresh coin dropped in a collection plate.

She opened it.

The first page was a certificate of death.

Her eyes went to the name field first, as they always did — lawyers read names before anything else, names being the load-bearing walls of every document — and the floor of the world tilted very slightly beneath her feet.

**Brightwater, Odile Maren.**

Her own middle name. Her mother's name, given to her at christening and never once spoken aloud in Saint Alder's, never written on any form she'd filled in at any hotel or bank. She had not used it since she was nine years old.

No one in this village could have known it.

Unless the ledger knew it. Unless whatever hand had entered her name in the ledger forty years ago had known it all along.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Some detached part of her mind observed the steadiness with clinical interest, the way she might observe a client's hands at the reading of an unexpected will. Below the name, the certificate proceeded with terrible bureaucratic calm. Date of death: left blank, though the year was printed — the current year. Place of death: *Saint Alder's parish.* Cause of death: blank, awaiting a doctor's hand.

But the certification block was not blank.

There, in faded iron-gall ink gone brown as old blood, was a signature: **Dr. Edmund Rookery.** Beneath it, the qualification: *Licentiate, Royal College of Physicians.* And beside the signature, a date of certification that made Odile's breath go shallow:

Forty-one years ago.

Edmund Rookery had signed her death certificate four decades before she walked into this parish. He had signed it — she did the arithmetic with the cold precision that was her last remaining comfort — when she was six years old. When her name had been entered in the ledger's margin in faded ink. When her mother's name had appeared in the ledger's grim accounting, unexplained, the same year.

Rookery was long dead. She had seen his headstone her second day in the village, lichen-eaten, in the corner of the churchyard where the stones leaned together like conspirators. A dead doctor had certified her death. Which meant the certificate was not a record of anything that had happened. It was a *permission*. A template. A document prepared in advance, waiting only for a date and a cause, so that when the ledger's debt came due, the machinery of the parish could grind forward without friction — no inquiry, no coroner's delay, no inconvenient questions. The paperwork of her murder, pre-signed by a man in his grave.

Someone had kept this folder for forty years. Someone had waited.

"Miss Brightwater?"

Pell's voice came from above her like a drop of cold water. She closed the drawer with a click no louder than a knuckle cracking and turned, her face arranged into polite vacancy.

"You've found me admiring your cabinets," she said. "My firm still uses the same make. Pre-war brass is better than anything they cast now."

Pell descended the ladder with the folder of 1911 burials against his chest, and his small pale eyes rested on her a moment longer than courtesy required. "We are careful here," he said. "Saint Alder's keeps everything. Nothing is lost. Nothing is *misplaced*." He set the register on the counter between them and opened it to the marked page with both hands, like a man unveiling a relic. "Everything finds its place, in time."

"I'm sure," said Odile, and began to copy the entries in her notebook with a hand that did not shake, while behind her sternum her heart performed its work with the methodical urgency of a woman digging.

* * *

She did not go back to Wren's cottage. Not directly. She had learned enough in ten days to know that the cottage sat in view of the lane, and the lane in view of the church tower, and the church tower — she was increasingly certain — in view of whoever maintained the machinery. Instead she took the long way, down through the water meadows where the rain had raised the river until it licked at the roots of the alders, and she stood in the grey drizzle with the folder inside her coat, against her ribs, feeling it there like a second heartbeat.

She had stolen it. There was no gentler word. She had lifted the folder from the PENDING drawer while Pell's back was turned, and she had replaced its weight with a folded newspaper from her satchel, an old trick from her early years chasing contested estates through hostile solicitors' offices. Her fingerprints were on the card cover. Her theft was discoverable. But leaving it there had been unthinkable; the folder was a loaded instrument, and she would not leave it loaded in a drawer for whoever came to fire it.

At the mill bridge she stopped and unwrapped the certificate again, sheltering it under her coat, and read it a third time as if repetition might change it into something survivable.

It did not. But the third reading showed her what the first two had missed.

Below Dr. Rookery's impossible signature, in the informant's block — the space reserved for the person reporting the death, next of kin or present-at-death — there was a second signature. Smaller. Tighter. A woman's hand, slanted and severe, each letter formed with the rigid discipline of someone taught penmanship by a ruler across the knuckles.

**E. Halloway.**

Odile stood on the mill bridge with the river roaring brown beneath her and felt the last warm room in her understanding of this village close its door.

Wren Halloway. The keeper. The prickly young woman who had warned her away from the ledger with genuine fear in her voice, who had grudgingly helped her decode the cipher, who had sat up with her two nights past over the devotional verses and the debts hidden beneath them, drinking bad tea and saying, in a voice that almost broke, *I didn't ask for this, I swear to you, I didn't ask for any of it.*

E. Halloway. Edith? Eleanor? Wren's grandmother — the keeper before Wren, the one who had died two winters past, the one whose journals Wren kept locked in a cedar box she would not open. The grandmother Wren barely knew, inherited post and burden both.

The grandmother had signed the informant's line on Odile's death certificate forty years ago.

Which meant either the old keeper had known, decades in advance, exactly how and when Odile Brightwater would die —

— or the certificate had been prepared more recently, in a dead man's forged hand, by someone who had access to Rookery's surviving signatures and the Halloway name to lend it weight.

Neither possibility was comfortable. One meant prophecy. The other meant that the person closest to her in this village — the only person she had begun, against every instinct, to trust — was either complicit or being used as a mask.

*Someone inside the parish machinery,* she thought. *Preparing the paperwork.*

And the machinery had a keeper's seal. She remembered it now from the cipher work: every balancing entry in the ledger bore a small pressed mark, a bird with a leaf in its beak, stamped in wax gone the colour of dried moss. Wren wore the seal on a cord around her neck; Odile had seen it once, glimpsed at her collarbone, and Wren had tucked it away quickly, the way people hide scars.

A forged certificate needed a keeper's cooperation. Or a keeper's seal, taken without her knowledge.

Odile wrapped the certificate carefully and walked on through the rain, and for the first time since arriving in Saint Alder's she caught herself doing what the hunted do: measuring distances, noting sightlines, memorizing which windows faced the road. The village that had begun, despite everything, to feel like a place she might belong — Tobias's firelight, Wren's reluctant loyalty, the strange gravity of being somewhere on purpose rather than by accident — now revealed its other face. Every kindness became a possible surveillance. Every familiar greeting became a roll call. *Is she still here. Has she gone. How many days remain.*

Three weeks, the ledger said. Less, now. Seventeen days, if the cipher's arithmetic held.

*O.B., paid in full.*

* * *

Wren was not at the cottage. Odile let herself in with the key Wren had finally, grudgingly cut for her — *so you stop breaking my latch, not because I trust you* — and built up the fire, and laid the certificate on the kitchen table under the lamp, weighting its corners with the salt cellar and two spoons, the way one lays out evidence. Because that was what it was. Evidence. The first hard proof that the ledger's prophecy was not merely ink and superstition but an operation, staffed and supplied, moving through the parish like blood through a body.

She put the kettle on because her hands needed occupation, and while it heated she wrote in her notebook, fast, the questions stacking like sandbags:

*Who had access to Rookery's signature samples? His casebooks? His surgery records — where are they archived?*

*Who could forge E. Halloway's hand — or who IS E. Halloway, and when did she sign?*

*Who controls the PENDING drawer? Who has keys to the vestry office besides Pell?*

*Why prepare the certificate forty years ago — or preserve it forty years? What changed? What triggered the final preparation?*

That last question stopped her pen. Because there was a discrepancy in the document she hadn't yet named aloud, even to herself. The paper was old — the card folder matched the others in the drawer, foxed at the edges, decades of damp in its fibres. But the certificate inside was crisp. Newer. Which meant the original had been created long ago and *replaced*, or supplemented, recently. Someone had refreshed the forgery. Someone had brought the paperwork current within the last weeks — since her arrival, perhaps. Since Vane's confession. Since the clock had started running in earnest.

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling, and in the window above the sink, in the black glass, she saw the lane behind her reflected: empty, dripping, ordinary.

Then a figure crossed it.

Not passing. Crossing — from the churchyard gate toward the cottage, quick and low, hatless, coat dark with rain. A stride she almost recognized, and then did, and relief flooded her so fast and so hot that it frightened her more than the footsteps themselves had. Because relief was a vulnerability. Because she had just spent an hour learning to suspect everyone, and here was her traitor heart opening its door at the mere shape of a silhouette.

The knock came — three raps, the agreed signal, Wren's invention, delivered with the air of a girl who found the entire apparatus of secrecy faintly humiliating and necessary anyway.

Odile opened the door. Wren stood on the step soaked through, grey-faced, and before Odile could speak she pushed past her into the cottage, dripping on the flags, and said in a voice scraped raw:

"You went to the vestry office." It wasn't a question. "Pell sent word to the rectory an hour ago. He knows the folder's gone, Odile. He counted them — he *counts* them, he counts everything — and he's sent for the council. They're meeting tonight."

"The council." Odile closed the door and turned the key, and heard the sound travel through the cottage like a verdict. "Then we have until tonight."

"Yes." Wren pushed back her hood. Her eyes went to the table, to the certificate weighted under the salt cellar, and Odile watched her read her own grandmother's name in the informant's line and go white to the lips. "That's—" She stopped. Started again. "That's my grandmother's hand. I'd know it anywhere. She made me copy it for practice when I was eight. *A keeper's hand must be exact, Wren. Exactness is everything.*"

"Wren." Odile kept her voice level, gentle, the voice she used with clients on the worst day of their lives. "When did she sign this?"

"I don't know." Wren's hand rose to her collarbone, to the cord beneath her collar, and gripped it. "I don't — she died two winters ago. She never spoke of you. She never spoke of any of it, she only trained me, drilled me, made me memorize the verses, and I thought — God help me, I thought it was devotion. I thought it was faith." Her voice cracked down the middle. "There's a box. Cedar. Her journals. I've never opened it. I couldn't bear to. But if her hand is on your death warrant, Odile, then I have to know what she did. What we did. What I'm *standing in*."

Outside, the rain thickened, and down the lane, faint through the noise of it, came the bell of Saint Alder's church ringing the quarter hour — and then, wrong, irregular, urgent, a pattern Odile did not know but Wren clearly did, because she turned toward the window as if struck.

"That's not the quarters," she whispered. "That's the council bell. They don't ring it except—" She swallowed. "Except when the ledger requires a ruling."

They looked at each other across the lamplit table, across the certificate with its two dead signatures, and Odile understood that whatever remained of their seventeen days had just been spent. The machinery had noticed the missing folder. The machinery was convening.

"They'll come for the seal," Wren said. Her hand tightened on the cord at her throat. "And Odile — if they rule the debt due — the seal can authorize the entry. My seal. Whether I consent or not."
