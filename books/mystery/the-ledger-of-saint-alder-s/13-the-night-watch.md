## 13. The Night Watch

The crypt beneath Saint Alder's had no door worth the name. Its entrance was a slab of weathered limestone set into the hillside behind the churchyard, half-swallowed by ivy, and it opened on iron hinges that had not been oiled since before either of us was born. I knew this because Wren Halloway had told me so at half past nine that evening, crouched beside me in the bracken with a thermos of tea going cold between us and her grandmother's journal open across her knees.

"He'll come after midnight," she'd said. "They always did."

"They?"

"The payments." She said the word the way you'd spit out a stone. "My grandmother's journals call them payments. Midnight, the new moon, the crypt. She wrote it like a recipe, Odile. Like she was baking bread."

The new moon had come that afternoon — a blind coin of darkness where the moon should have been — and so we waited.

I had never been good at waiting. Waiting was what clients paid me to avoid; my whole profession was the art of arriving before the other side expected you, of knowing which drawer held the document before anyone thought to hide it. But there in the cold, with the churchyard stones rising around us like bad teeth and the wind combing through the yew trees with its long dry fingers, I discovered a different kind of patience. The patience of prey.

Because that was what I had become, whether or not I believed the ledger. Three weeks. The cipher entry sat in my memory now the way a splinter sits under skin: *O.B., paid in full.* Dated for a night eighteen days from this one. And someone inside Saint Alder's parish machinery had already signed my death certificate with a dead doctor's pen.

"You're shivering," Wren said.

"I'm thinking."

"You're shivering *and* thinking. Drink the tea."

I drank the tea. It was terrible — stewed past bitterness into something almost metallic — but the heat of it was a small argument against the dark, and I took it gratefully. Wren watched me over the rim of her own cup. In the starlight her face was all angles and shadow, and I thought again how strange it was to be allied with her: the keeper of the book that wanted me dead, the granddaughter of the woman whose journals were teaching us both how the trap had been built.

"Tell me again why he'd come here," I said. "Tobias."

"Because his sister's name is in the ledger, and because someone has been asking questions about her." Wren's voice went flat. "You've been asking questions about her."

"I asked him about her once. Kindly. He told me she died of fever."

"And you believed him?"

I turned the cup in my hands. "I wanted to believe him. That's not the same thing, but it isn't nothing, either."

Wren said nothing to that. Below us, down the slope, the village of Saint Alder's lay scattered with lamplight — the inn's windows gold among them, and I caught myself looking at those windows longer than I meant to. Tobias Finch had fed me soup when I first arrived, unasked, unprompted, setting the bowl before me with the offhand authority of a man who had decided I was too thin and would hear no appeals. He had remembered how I took my coffee by the second morning. He had stood in the inn doorway the day the storm broke and told me, with a crooked smile, that the road out wouldn't reopen till noon, and that I might as well stop fighting it and have breakfast.

I had begun, without noticing the exact hour of it, to build small rooms in my mind with his name on the door. That was the danger of Saint Alder's. It made itself easy to stay in.

"If it's him," I said, "burning documents—"

"It won't change your entry. Burning paper doesn't burn debt. Not in their theology."

"But it changes what we can prove." I set the cup down. "Wren, whoever forged my death certificate needed the parish records to corroborate it. If someone is destroying evidence tonight, they're clearing the ground. They're making room for me to die neatly, on schedule, with paperwork that agrees."

The wind moved through the yews. Somewhere far off, a dog barked twice and gave up.

* * *

We waited two hours more. Wren dozed against a headstone with the journal under her coat; I did not sleep. Sleep had become a country I visited briefly and left unpaid. Instead I rehearsed the cipher in my head — the devotional verses, the little psalms printed in the ledger's margins, each one a lock, each one counting debts across generations the way an abacus counts beads. Brightwater. Halloway. Brightwater. My mother's name surfacing forty years back like a body coming up out of water, unexplained, unmourned in ink.

At ten minutes past midnight, the ivy moved.

Not the wind's movement — the wind came in gusts, wholehearted, shaking every branch at once. This was local, deliberate: a section of greenery shifting aside, then settling. A figure detached itself from the hillside below us, wrapped in a heavy coat, hood up, and stood a long moment at the crypt's stone slab as if listening to the earth.

Then the hinges groaned — a sound I felt in my own teeth — and the figure slipped inside, pulling the slab nearly shut behind them. Nearly. A finger's width of darkness remained, and from it, within a minute, came light. Not lantern-light. Something flickering, restless, orange. Firelight.

Wren was awake instantly, hand on my sleeve. "Odile."

"I see it."

"That's not a candle."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

We went down the slope fast and low, the way children run through graveyards in stories — except this was my life now, crouching among the graves of strangers while a fire burned in the belly of the hill. At the slab's edge I paused, heart hammering against the cage of my ribs, and looked at Wren. She nodded once. Together we pulled the stone open.

Heat rolled out to meet us, carrying the smell of burning paper — that particular sweetness, ink and ash together, the smell of a library dying. The crypt was a low vaulted chamber lined with niches, old bones sleeping in the walls behind slabs carved with names worn smooth. In the center, on a flat tomb-top that had clearly served this purpose many times — the stone was scorched in a pale ring — burned a small fire. And kneeling beside it, feeding pages into the flame one at a time with the terrible tenderness of a man burying a child, was Tobias Finch.

"Tobias."

He didn't startle. That was the first thing I noticed, and it hurt worse than a flinch would have. He simply closed his eyes, still holding the page in his hand, and said quietly, "Ah. You found me."

"What are you doing?" My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Tobias. What are you burning?"

"Old things." He opened his eyes. In the firelight they were wet. "Things that should have been let go of a long time ago."

Wren pushed past me, snatching toward the pile beside him — a leather folder, fat with papers, several already reduced to curling black lace. He moved faster than a man his size had any right to move, folding the folder against his chest, and for one suspended moment the three of us stood in the crypt like figures in a painting called *Betrayal*, lit from below by our own ruin.

"Don't," he said. "Please, Odile. Don't take them."

"Those are parish records." I heard my own voice go cold, professional, the voice I used in courtrooms, and I hated it and leaned on it all at once. "Those are evidence."

"Evidence of what?" He laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "Of my sister? Of Edith? You want to know what's in these pages? Baptismal records. Her marriage lines. Letters she wrote home from the coast the summer before she died. Things that prove she *lived*, Odile — that's what I'm burning. Because as long as they exist in your files and Wren's ledgers, she isn't my sister. She's an entry. A balancing. A line item in a book that killed her."

"The ledger didn't kill her," Wren said, very quietly.

Tobias turned on her, and the firelight made him enormous. "Your grandmother kept the book. Your grandmother's hands turned every page. Don't stand in front of me and split hairs, girl. I've watched you carry that thing around like a christening gown."

"My grandmother murdered her." Wren's voice didn't shake. I had never admired anyone more than I admired her in that moment. "Is that what you need to hear? I found it in her journals. Edith Finch wasn't taken by the covenant. She was killed to settle a private grudge, and the ledger was dressed up around it afterward like a costume. So yes, Tobias. Burn whatever you want. It doesn't bring her back, and it doesn't save Odile."

The words dropped into the crypt and lay there. Tobias looked at Wren, then at me, and something in his face came apart — slowly, the way a wall comes apart, course by course, until what's standing isn't a wall anymore, just rubble with a shape.

"You knew," he said to me. His voice had gone hoarse. "How long have you known?"

"Two days."

"Two days." He repeated it as if tasting it. "And you let me pour your coffee. You let me ask after your sleep. You sat at my table—" He stopped. Pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. When he spoke again it was barely above the crackle of the fire. "I liked you, Odile Brightwater. God help me, I liked having you in my house. And the whole time you were digging up my sister's bones."

"To find out who killed her," I said. "That's all I've ever been doing here, Tobias. Including with my own name in that book. I'm not your enemy. I'm the next name on the same list."

He lowered his hand. Looked at me for a long time, and I could not read what moved behind his face — grief and suspicion and something older than both, some inherited wariness that Saint Alder's had planted in its children generations before any of us were born.

"The list," he said finally. "That's all any of you ever see. The list." He gathered the folder against his chest again, and this time neither Wren nor I moved to stop him. Half its contents were ash already; the rest he held like a man holding the last of a house fire. "You want to know who forged your death certificate? Who's been tidying the parish files for thirty years, deciding which names get remembered and which get balanced?" He stepped backward toward the slab, toward the night beyond it. "Ask Councillor Aldous Crane. Ask him what his father's signature was doing on a certificate dated next month. And when you've asked him—" his jaw worked, "—ask him what happened to the last person who asked."

He was gone before we could answer, the slab groaning shut behind him, his footsteps fading up through the churchyard grass.

* * *

We stood in the dying firelight a long while. The flames had sunk to embers in the scorched ring, and among them, curled like black ferns, lay the edges of pages that would never be read again. Wren knelt and, with bare fingers, lifted one fragment free — a corner only, a few words surviving at the margin in faded brown ink.

She read it, and went still.

"Wren?"

She held it out to me without looking away from it herself. The fragment was small, no bigger than a playing card, but the handwriting was precise, clerical, the hand of someone trained in records:

*—transferred to the Crane account, per agreement, the balance of E.F., settled in full by the Keeper, this 14th of October—*

"Crane," I said. "Aldous Crane's father was the doctor."

"And the doctor signed your death certificate." Wren rose, brushing ash from her knees, and in the ember-light her face had gone hard and bright as struck flint. "Odile — this says Edith Finch's death was *settled*. Transferred. Paid. Forty years ago, someone recorded her murder as a completed payment — and filed it under the Crane family's account."

The cold of the crypt finally reached me then, climbing through my coat, through my skin, settling somewhere behind my sternum where no tea could touch it. Because I understood what a transferred balance meant in the grammar of that ledger. Debts didn't vanish in Saint Alder's. They only changed hands.

And if Edith Finch's death had been entered as payment on the Cranes' account — then the debt the ledger claimed *I* owed had never belonged to me at all. Someone had moved it. Someone had written my name into a column where it had no business standing, three weeks ago or thirty years ago, and every document since — Vane's false will, the pre-signed certificate, the cipher verse promising *paid in full* — had been built on top of that one transferred line.

Above us, muffled through the stone, the church bell began to toll the quarter hour. One. Two. Three.

Eighteen days, the ledger said. But ledgers, I was beginning to understand, could be edited — and so could I.

"We need the rest of that page," I said. "Whatever Tobias didn't burn. Tonight, Wren. Before Crane learns what we found down here."

Wren pocketed the fragment, and above us the bell finished tolling, and somewhere in the dark of the village a light went out in a window that had been watching the churchyard all along.
