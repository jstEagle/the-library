## 2. Cold Cases and Warmer Money

The cheque cleared on a Tuesday, which felt like an omen. Nothing good has ever happened to me on a Tuesday.

I sat in my flat above the old chemist's on Marlowe Street with the file spread across the kitchen table — the file Marguerite Voss had couriered over in a leather attaché case that probably cost more than my annual income. Inside: a death certificate, a coroner's report, eleven pages of inquest summary, two photographs, and a single sheet of paper with her instructions typed in a font so restrained it was almost apologetic.

*Prove that Edmund Voss was not murdered.*

Not *find out who killed him*. Not *was it murder?* Prove he *hasn't been murdered yet*. A man seven years drowned, and his widow paying me to establish that his death hadn't happened. Or hadn't happened the way it said. The grammar of it kept snagging something in the back of my skull, like a fishhook through wool.

The death certificate was straightforward enough. Edmund Alistair Voss, fifty-one, surveyor, died 9 March, cause of death: drowning. Place: the Halvermere Cut, Fenland. Coroner's verdict: accidental death. And then the thing that made me sit up straighter at my own kitchen table at eleven at night: *Inquest sealed by order of the Crown Court, 14 April, pursuant to Section 19.*

Sealed inquests are rare. Sealed inquests for a middle-aged man who fell into a ditch are rarer still. People drown in the Fens every year — drunk walkers, joyriders who misjudge a flooded lane, the occasional suicide with a pocket full of stones. Nobody seals those files. You seal a file when there's something inside it that someone with money or influence doesn't want read aloud in an open courtroom.

I made tea I didn't drink and read the whole thing twice.

***

Dee arrived at nine the next morning carrying two coffees and the expression she reserved for my worst ideas.

"Tell me you didn't take it," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"Wren." She set the coffees down on top of the inquest summary, deliberately, the way she does when she wants to physically interrupt my thought process. "Tell me you did not take money from a stranger to investigate a drowning that was ruled accidental before your podcast existed."

"I took the advance."

"Of course you did." She dropped into the chair across from me. Dee is thirty-four, built like she's braced against weather, and she has produced every episode of *The Ledger* since it was two people and a Zoom H5 recorder in this same kitchen. She can smell a bad story the way some people smell rain coming. "How much?"

"Enough."

"How much, Wren."

"Forty thousand. Advance against a hundred and twenty."

She closed her eyes. "For one case."

"For an impossible case. There's a premium on impossible."

"There's a premium on *stupid*, is what there is." She opened her eyes and looked at me with the flat, patient stare she'd perfected over six years of talking me out of things — and failing maybe forty percent of the time, which she'd tell you is a terrible success rate for both of us. "Rich woman walks into your life out of nowhere, pays you three years of income to prove a negative about a man who's been dead since before you had listeners. Walk me through how that sounds fine to you."

"It doesn't sound fine. It sounds wrong in about six different directions." I pulled the attaché case around so she could see it. "That's why I took it."

"That's not — Wren, that is the opposite of why anyone should take anything."

"Dee." I waited until she stopped glaring at the cheque stub. "Three years ago I solved the Harrow drownings because I couldn't stop looking at a number in an insurance filing. Two years ago it was the Kessler fire. Last year, Peter Amos. Every one of them was closed, ruled, stamped, filed. Every one of them was wrong. Somebody hands me a sealed inquest and a fee that smells like desperation and asks me to prove a man wasn't murdered — and you want me to walk away?"

"I want you to ask *why her*. Why you? Why now?" Dee picked up her coffee and didn't drink it either; we were mirroring each other's bad habits across the table, which happens when you've worked together too long. "You're good. You know you're good. Rich people know you're good. So either she picked you because she believes you'll find the truth—" she held up one finger, "—or she picked you because she believes you'll find whatever she wants found, and she's paying to rent your credibility." A second finger. "And I don't love either option."

"Option three," I said. "She's grieving and irrational and rich, and grief plus irrationality plus money looks exactly like this from the outside."

"Option three is what you tell yourself so you can sleep." She finally sipped the coffee. "The money smells wrong, Wren. Not dirty — wrong. Dirty money comes with lawyers and NDAs and a story about discretion. This came with a leather bag and a note in nice typography. That's not guilt. That's *intention*."

I didn't have an answer for that, mostly because she was right, so I changed the subject the way I do when Dee wins a round: I showed her the file.

***

We spent the morning going through it page by page, the way we do — her reading for gaps, me reading for texture. It's a good system. I find the lies people tell; she finds the truths they omit.

Edmund Voss, according to the material his widow provided, was a chartered surveyor, semi-retired, living alone at a property called Reedmarsh Hall near the village of Thurlow Staithe. On the night of 9 March, seven years ago, he had apparently driven out along the fen droves toward the Halvermere Cut — a drainage channel, straight as a ruler, running between two raised banks — and never come home. His car was found the next morning parked on the drover's road, doors unlocked, keys in the ignition. His body was recovered from the cut two days later, four hundred metres downstream, tangled in the reed beds below the sluice.

Accidental. Slipped from the bank in the dark, perhaps. Heart failure in cold water, perhaps. The inquest summary used the word *perhaps* more than any document I've ever read outside a politician's memoir.

"Look at this," Dee said, tapping the second page. "Time of death estimated between ten p.m. and midnight. But the police log says the last confirmed sighting of Voss alive was six-forty p.m., at the petrol station in Thurlow. Four hours unaccounted for, and nobody asked where he went?"

"Maybe they asked and it's in the sealed part."

"Maybe." She frowned at the page. "And look at the recovery. Body found two days later, four hundred metres downstream. In March, in a drainage channel with a sluice gate between here and here—" she moved her finger along the map I'd printed, "—that current would carry him, sure. But the sluice. Was the sluice open?"

I looked at her. "Why does that matter?"

"Because if the sluice was shut, he didn't float down. He went through it somehow, or he went in below it, or somebody put him there." She said it lightly, testing the weight of it, the way she tests everything. "Somebody with local knowledge would know the sluice schedule. It's the kind of detail a coroner waves past and a podcaster builds an episode on."

"You've been doing this too long."

"One of us has to be paranoid professionally. You're paranoid personally; it's inefficient." She flipped to the photographs. Two of them: Edmund Voss in colour, a heavyset man with silver hair and reading glasses pushed up on his forehead, caught mid-laugh at some garden party, holding a glass of white wine like he wasn't sure he wanted it. And a black-and-white aerial shot of the estate itself — Reedmarsh Hall, a long low house with a private stretch of water behind it, all of it grey and flat as a pencil drawing.

"He doesn't look like a man who wanders into ditches," Dee said.

"Nobody looks like a man who wanders into ditches. That's the whole genre."

But she was right, in the way she's usually right without needing to be told. Everything about the file was *smooth*. Too smooth. The widow's instructions were smooth. The inquest summary was smooth, every rough edge pre-sanded by whoever had assembled it. Even the grief in Marguerite Voss's letter — and there was grief in it, real grief, I've read enough of it to know the grain — even the grief sat on top of the page like varnish over older wood.

"Here's my problem," Dee said, setting down the photos. "Forget the husband for a second. Look at *her*. She hires you, gives you a curated file — and it is curated, Wren, don't make that face, widows don't keep court-sealed inquest summaries in matching leather unless someone gave them a copy, and someone only gives copies when they want them circulated — and she asks you to prove a sentence that doesn't parse. What does she actually want?"

"The truth?"

"The truth is free. She's paying a hundred and twenty thousand pounds. Whatever she wants, it isn't truth. Truth is what you settle for when you can't afford the thing you really want." She stood, gathering her coat. "I'll start pulling public records. Land registry, company filings, local news archives. If there's anything about this family in the last decade, I'll find it."

"And if I say drop it?"

She paused at the door and gave me a look that was almost fond. "You won't say drop it. That's the whole problem with you. You're a dog with a bone, except the bone is always somebody else's skeleton." She shrugged into her coat. "Call me when you find the first thing that doesn't fit. You always find a first thing."

She left. I sat at the table with the dead man's photograph and the dead man's file and the living woman's money, and I did what I always do at the start of a case, which is nothing. I just sat, and let the anomaly sit with me, and waited for it to show me its shape.

***

It took until nearly midnight.

I'd gone through everything twice more, made notes, abandoned the notes, made better ones. The file was clean the way a staged room is clean. And then, sorting the pages back into their original order — because order matters, because whoever assembled a file tells you things by how they stack it — I noticed it.

Eleven pages of inquest summary. Numbered. Page one through page twelve.

Page eight was missing.

Not torn out crudely — the numbering simply skipped, seven flowing into nine as though eight had never existed, the staple pattern slightly off, the paper stock of nine a hair different under my thumb from the stock of seven. Whoever put this packet together had removed a page and reassembled the rest with care. And then handed it to a stranger with a chequebook and asked him to prove a negative.

I sat very still in the dark kitchen, listening to the pipes tick, feeling the itch turn into something with edges.

Marguerite Voss had given me a doctored file. Which meant one of two things: she was hiding something, or she was pointing at something — and either way, she knew exactly what page eight said.

Seven years ago, Edmund Voss drove out to a drainage channel in the Fens and never came back, and a court sealed the questions, and tonight his widow had sent me a file with a hole in it shaped like an answer.

I reached for my phone to text Dee, then stopped. Some findings you say out loud, and some you sit on until you understand them. Instead I opened my laptop and searched two words I should have searched on day one:

*Halvermere sluice.*

The first result was a planning notice, seven years old, from an outfit called the Fenland Development Group.

The second result was a petition objecting to it. The lead signatory, in confident blue ink scanned onto the page:

**E. A. Voss, Chartered Surveyor.**

I stared at the screen for a long time. Outside, Marlowe Street did its midnight business — a taxi, a shout, a bottle rolling along the gutter — and somewhere east of here, under flat grey water, a man who had objected to something waited for someone to ask the right question.

Page eight could wait. Tomorrow I'd go and find the man who'd filed the inquest, and I'd charm him, and I'd be polite, and I'd leave with whatever he'd kept.

But first I wrote one line in my notebook, the line that every case of mine has eventually come down to, the line I'd already written for Harrow and Kessler and Amos without knowing I was writing it again:

*Who benefits from a sealed file?*

Underneath it, after a moment, I added a second line, smaller, the kind of thing you write when the itch has become a certainty you can't yet defend:

*And who benefits from hiring the man who asks?*
