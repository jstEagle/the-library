## 3. What the Water Keeps

The Fens announced themselves the way bad news does — gradually, then all at once.

One moment Wren was driving through ordinary Norfolk farmland, hedgerows and roundabouts and a garden centre with a banner reading SPRING SALE, and the next the land simply gave up on being land. The horizon flattened. Ditches appeared at the roadside, black water so still it looked poured rather than gathered, each one mirroring a sky the colour of wet slate. The road itself seemed to sit lower than the fields, as if the whole county had been pressed down by a thumb.

He'd read about drainage in preparation — everyone who wrote about the Fens did. How the Dutchman Cornelius Vermuyden had come in the seventeenth century to drain them for King Charles, how the locals had rioted because the fens fed them and the dry land wouldn't. How the water had been fought for four hundred years and never quite defeated. It was the kind of history that made podcast producers giddy: *a landscape that remembers what was taken from it.*

Wren mostly noticed that it was cold. A damp cold, horizontal, that got through the car's heater like it wasn't there.

The lockkeeper's cottage sat where the old navigation channel met a modern sluice, a squat brick building with a chimney smoking stubbornly against the wind. A hand-painted sign said HOLLAND'S LOCK — PRIVATE. The man himself was already outside when Wren parked, as if he'd been watching the road for an hour, which he probably had. Out here you saw everything coming from a long way off. That was the whole point of flat country.

"You'll be the podcast fellow." The lockkeeper was seventy if he was a day, tall and stooped like a heron, wearing a waxed jacket older than Wren. "Dennis Holland. I'd say welcome, but nobody comes here to be welcomed."

"Wren Okafor. I called last week."

"I know who you are. My daughter listens to you." He said it the way another man might have said *my daughter watches pornography.* "She says you solved those drownings up north. The Harrow business."

"That's the one."

"Then I expect you'll want tea before we talk about Edmund Voss."

They drank tea inside, at a table under a window that looked out onto the lock gates and the long grey throat of the channel running away toward the horizon. Dennis didn't ask questions about the case. He talked instead about the lock — how his father had kept it before him, how the water came down from the uplands after rain and they had to let it through in measured amounts or the low fields flooded, how he could read the level of the channel from the sound of it against the gates the way other men read weather in their knees.

It took forty minutes before Wren understood this was the interview. Not the questions. The waiting. Some men told you things only after they'd convinced themselves you weren't in a hurry.

"Seven years ago," Wren said finally. "March. A surveyor named Edmund Voss drowned out here somewhere. His body was found near the old Voss estate — there's a house on the fen that went under when they flooded the washes. You'd have been keeping this lock then."

"I keep it now," Dennis said. "I kept it the night he died, too."

Wren set down his cup. "What do you remember?"

Dennis looked out the window at the water for a while. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — flatter, more careful, the voice of a man reciting something he'd rehearsed against the day someone finally asked.

"It was foggy. March fog off the washes is the worst kind — comes up off the water at dusk and swallows everything. I'd closed the gates early because of it. And then—" He stopped. Started again. "There were lights on the channel. That night. After dark."

"Lights."

"Boat lights. Two of them, moving slow, no navigation lamps showing proper. One went upstream toward the estate. The other stayed out in the middle of the channel, just sitting there, engine idling. For hours." He turned and looked at Wren directly. "Nobody idles on that channel at night in fog unless they're waiting for something or hiding something. And the inquest never asked me about it. They asked about the weather, the state of the banks, whether Mr. Voss drank. Nobody asked what else was on the water."

"Did you tell anyone?"

"My wife told me not to." He said it without embarrassment. "She said men who talk about lights end up in the water themselves. She died three years back, my wife, so I suppose she was wrong about the timing but right about the principle." He refilled Wren's cup unasked. "You're going to write all this down and put it out to the world, are you?"

"That's the job."

"And when the people who own everything between here and Ely hear about it?"

"Then they'll know someone's asking."

Dennis studied him with pale, watery eyes that had spent sixty years measuring levels and depths. Whatever he saw in Wren's face seemed to satisfy him, or at least to settle him.

"There's one more thing," he said. "The night after they found the body, a car came down the lane. Big dark motor, city plates. A man got out and walked the bank with a torch, very thorough, quarter of an hour. Then he walked back to his car and stood there looking at the house across the water — the drowned house — for a long time. Like he was checking his work." Dennis's hand shook slightly around his cup. "I noted the plate. Old habit. My father kept records of every boat through this lock since 1961, and I keep the habit."

He slid a small leather notebook across the table. The entry was seven years old, in careful pencil: a partial plate, a make of car, a time.

Wren photographed it twice.

* * *

He drove back along the channel road with the fog thickening, wipers barely managing, thinking about lights on water and men checking their work. His phone buzzed at a red light — Dee, with a voice note:

*"Called the coroner's office again. The file on Voss is sealed — court order, not administrative. That's different, Wren. Accidental drownings don't get sealed. Also, fun fact: the development company that owns half this county — Fenland Development Group? Their solicitors are the same firm that petitioned for the seal. Sleep well."*

He didn't sleep well. He drove back to the lock at dawn and photographed the water, the gates, the drowned house a mile off across the washes — a roofline and a chimney breaking the surface like a skull — and the whole time he had the feeling, familiar from three previous cases, of standing in a room where something terrible had happened and feeling it still in the walls.

The Fens kept what the water was given. He was beginning to understand that some of it wanted giving back.

* * *

Forty miles south, in a house too large for one person, Marguerite Voss sat at a desk by a window overlooking a garden gone wild, and wrote a letter she would never send.

*My darling Edmund,*

*The fog has come to the garden this morning, and I thought of you. You always loved fog. You said it made the world honest — stripped everything back to what was nearest, and left the rest to imagination. You were a sentimental liar, my love. You lied to me in perfect clarity, under blue skies, with every document spread out on this very desk.*

*I have hired a man. He has your stubbornness and none of your charm, which is perhaps what the work requires. He is walking the fens now, asking questions I should have asked seven years ago, when the constable knocked and I performed the widow so well that even I believed it.*

*You would laugh at the irony. I pay him to prove you were murdered, when it was I who—*

The pen stopped.

She looked at the sentence for a long time. Then she lifted it from the page, carefully, blotting it away until the paper showed only a faint grey ghost of what had been written — visible, if anyone ever held it to the light, but legible to no one.

*I have lit a fire in the grate tonight, though it isn't cold. Your report is in it. Not the original — I would never burn the original; the original is the only honest thing you ever gave me. But the copy you thought I never found. It felt right. You burned the marshes for them on paper; I burn the paper back.*

*March 9th is coming. It comes every year, but this year someone will be watching the water when it does.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. Either way, the record will be corrected, and neither of us will be here to enjoy it.*

*M.*

She folded the letter, placed it in a drawer already thick with its predecessors — six years of letters, none posted, none destroyed — and locked it.

Outside, the fog lay on the garden like a held breath.

In the fens, forty miles north, it lay on the water the same way. And beneath it, in the black mirror of the channel, the drowned house waited with its secrets, patient as the dead.

Wren Okafor had three days of interviews ahead of him and no idea yet that the woman paying him had chosen the date herself.

But the water knew.
