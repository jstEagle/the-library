## 9. An Old Friend on the Payroll

The name was in the eleventh document of a folder that had already ruined Wren's morning.

He'd been at Dee's kitchen table since eight, working through the FDG filings she'd pulled from Companies House — the boring scaffolding of the development empire, all shell entities and interlocking directorships, the kind of paper most people's eyes slid off. Wren had trained himself to read it like a novel. Every name was a character. Every payment was a plot point.

*Reyes Consulting Ltd — risk assessment and community liaison services.*

*Annual retainer: £48,000.*

*Director: A. Reyes.*

He read it three times. The kettle clicked off in the kitchen and neither of them moved to it.

"That's her," Dee said quietly. She'd come to stand behind him, reading over his shoulder, and now she stepped back like the page was hot. "That's *your* Ada."

"It's an A. Reyes." His voice came out flat, reasonable, the voice he used on recordings when he wanted to sound calmer than he felt. "It's a common enough—"

"Wren."

He looked up at her. Dee had a face built for skepticism — one eyebrow permanently half-raised, mouth set in a line that said *I've heard better lies from better people*. Right now there was no skepticism in it at all. Just sympathy, which was worse.

"Community liaison," he said. "Risk assessment. That's — you know what that is? That's what they call it when they pay a cop to look the other way and feel good about it. That's the vocabulary of it."

"You don't know that yet."

"I know the vocabulary." He pushed back from the table. The chair legs shrieked on the tile. "Forty-eight grand a year. For what? Ada retired on a superintendent's pension. She lives in a cottage in Norfolk with a garden she talks about like it's a third child. What does she need forty-eight grand for?"

"People take consulting work. It's not a crime."

"No," Wren agreed. "It's not a crime. It's just a question. And I've been asking questions about this company for three weeks, and every question keeps coming back to the same place: who made sure Edmund Voss drowned without a ripple? Who made sure the inquest stayed sealed? Somebody with institutional knowledge. Somebody who knew how investigations breathe, so they'd know exactly where to squeeze."

Dee didn't say anything. She didn't have to. He could hear her thinking it too, and he hated her a little for thinking it, and hated himself more.

"I'm going to Norfolk," he said.

"Yeah," Dee said. "I figured."

---

The drive took two hours and Wren spent all of them rehearsing.

That was the thing about betrayal — it required rehearsal. You couldn't walk into it cold. You needed a script, because the alternative was standing in front of someone you loved with nothing prepared and letting your face do the talking, and his face had never once kept a secret in its life.

Ada Reyes had taught him everything. Not formally — she'd never been his trainer, never signed anything — but he'd been twenty-six and a true-crime obsessive with a police scanner and a press badge he'd mostly printed himself, and she'd been the detective inspector on the Harrow case before it was ever his case, and she'd let him sit in the observation room. She'd taught him that documents lie but patterns don't. She'd taught him that the first answer is always the alibi and the second answer is always the money. She'd taught him to write down what people *don't* say, in the margins, in pencil, because pencil means you might be wrong.

*What people don't say.* He'd written that down about her, somewhere along the way, without realizing it applied.

Her cottage sat at the end of a lane lined with poplars, whitewashed walls, a blue door she'd painted herself the summer after her retirement and complained about ever since. The garden was going wild in the way only a gardener's garden goes wild in early spring — disciplined chaos, everything seeded on purpose. She was on her knees in a bed of something green when he came through the gate, and she looked up, and for one full second her face did something complicated before it settled into surprise.

"Wren Okafor," she said. "As I live and breathe. Nobody told me the famous podcast was coming to town."

"Nobody knew."

"Even better." She stood, brushing soil from her knees, and held out a hand — then abandoned the handshake and hugged him instead, brief and hard, smelling of earth and rosemary. "You look terrible. Have you been sleeping?"

"Not really."

"Good. Sleep is for people without work." She studied him, and the warmth in her face cooled by degrees into the old professional attention he knew so well — the look she'd given suspects through one-way glass, though she'd have denied it. "But you didn't drive two hours to talk about my dahlias. Sit down. Tell me why you're here."

They sat at a wrought-iron table under a trellis that wouldn't leaf out for another month. She brought tea without asking. He wrapped his hands around the cup and didn't drink.

"Do you remember Edmund Voss?" he said.

Something happened in her stillness. Most people would have missed it. Wren had been trained — by her — not to miss it. A fractional pause before the blink. The cup set down a half-second later than natural.

"Voss," she repeated. "The surveyor. Drowned in the Fens, what, seven years ago? Before my time on the major crimes side. Why?"

"You were still serving then. You'd have seen the file cross a desk."

"A lot of files crossed a lot of desks." She smiled, easy and unbothered, and it was a good smile, and Wren thought: *she learned that smile from thirty years of interviews, and she's using it on me.* "What's the interest? New episode?"

"Something like that." He set his own cup down. "I've been reading FDG's filings. Fenland Development Group. You know them?"

"They drained half the county. Hard to miss them."

"They keep consultants. Risk assessment, community liaison. Forty-eight thousand a year." He watched her hands. "Reyes Consulting Limited. Director: A. Reyes."

The garden was very quiet. Somewhere down the lane a pheasant called, that absurd rattling scream they make, and cut off.

Ada didn't flinch. That was the worst part. She looked at him across the table with an expression of mild, tired recognition, as if he'd finally asked a question she'd been expecting for years, and she said:

"Yes. That's me."

Wren had prepared for denial. He had prepared for anger, for hurt, for the wounded dignity of an innocent woman accused. He had not prepared for simple confirmation, delivered like a weather report, and it knocked the script clean out of his head.

"Why?" he managed.

"Because it was legal, it paid well, and I was bored." She refilled her tea, unhurried. "Retirement is slower than people tell you. They wanted a former senior officer to review their site security and liaise with local councils. I reviewed their site security. I liaised. I sent invoices. End of story."

"End of story," Wren said. "Edmund Voss's report stalled their flagship project. Two weeks before he died, someone in their orbit was being paid to manage 'risk.' Their chief surveyor ends up in a flood channel. And the woman who taught me that the second answer is always the money is on the payroll."

"And there it is." She set the teapot down with a small, precise click. "There's the podcast voice. The one that makes ordinary arrangements sound like conspiracies."

"It's not a conspiracy if it's true."

"It's not true because it rhymes, Wren." Her voice hardened — not angry, something older than anger. Disappointed, maybe, which from her was far heavier. "You've got a company, a dead man, and a coincidence, and you're building a cathedral out of it. I've watched you do it before. With the Harrow case it happened to be right. Being right once doesn't make the method sound."

"The method found four bodies."

"The method found *three*, and one of them was a widow who didn't leave her house for a year afterward because strangers camped on her lawn calling her husband a murderer." She said it flatly, without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You want to talk about costs? Talk about those."

The pheasant screamed again, farther off. Wren realized his hands were shaking around the cup and made them stop.

"Tell me about Voss," he said. "Not the file version. Your version."

"There isn't a version." She met his eyes and held them, and her gaze was steady and clear and completely unreadable, and Wren understood suddenly that he had never once been able to read her — that everything he'd taken for openness for fifteen years had been skill. "I never worked the case. I never saw the file. I took a consulting contract with a development firm two years after the man died. If you print otherwise, I'll sue you, and I'll win, and it will break my heart. Is that what you want?"

"No," he said honestly. "I want you to tell me it's nothing."

For a moment — one moment — something moved behind her eyes. Something that looked almost like grief, or like warning, or like a door opening a crack onto a long dark corridor. Then it closed.

"Then find something real," Ada Reyes said. "And until you do — drop it, Wren. Drop the whole thing. The client, the money, the marshes, all of it. Some files stay sealed because sealing them was the kindest thing anyone could do."

She stood, gathered the cups, and carried them inside without looking back. He heard the tap run. He sat alone under the bare trellis for a long time, watching the wind move through the poplars, listening to the water running in her kitchen — water, always water, in this whole wet county, water under everything.

When he left, she didn't come to the gate. But as his car turned out of the lane, he checked the mirror, and saw her standing at the window, watching him go, one hand pressed flat against the glass.

---

Ada washed the teacups slowly, thoroughly, the way she did everything she didn't want to think about.

Through the window she watched his car shrink down the lane and felt the familiar shape of the day rearranging itself around a new weight. Fifteen years. She'd known him since he was a boy with a recorder and no judgment, and she had told him to drop it, and she had seen in his face — God help her, she'd *taught* him that face — that he would do anything but.

She dried her hands. Went to the hall. Took the key from the tin on top of the wardrobe, the one she'd told herself for seven years was just sentiment, and opened the locked drawer of the writing desk.

Inside was a folder. Inside the folder was a copy of an internal memo she had written in her last year of service, marked *restricted*, addressed to Professional Standards, regarding Fenland Development Group and certain irregularities she had been quietly documenting for eleven months. A memo that had vanished into the machinery and never come out. A memo whose existence, she suspected, was the reason a consultancy contract had appeared in her postbox six weeks after retirement — not a bribe, exactly. A leash. *We know what you wrote. Stay close. Stay quiet.*

And stapled behind it, a single sheet she had never shown anyone: a photocopy of one page of the sealed inquest file, obtained by a source she still protected, showing a payment authorization bearing initials she had spent seven years telling herself meant nothing.

A.R.

Her initials. And not her signature. Someone had used her initials the way you'd use a glove.

She had told Wren to drop it because it was true advice — the only kind she had left to give. Because the last person who'd asked her about Edmund Voss had stopped returning calls, and the one before that had transferred counties, and because she was sixty-four years old and tired and had convinced herself that silence was survival.

She looked at the memo for a long moment. Then she put it back in the drawer, locked it, and returned the key to the tin.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow she'd decide.

In the garden, the wind picked at the bare trellis, and down at the bottom of the lane, past the poplars, a grey sedan sat parked where no one visiting the dahlias had any reason to park — and had been sitting, Ada noted, since roughly ten minutes before Wren arrived.
