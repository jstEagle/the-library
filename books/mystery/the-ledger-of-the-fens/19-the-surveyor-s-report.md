## 19. The Surveyor's Report

The archive was in the basement of the East Anglian Institute, beneath a reading room that smelled of floor wax and old ambition. Wren had called ahead, used his real name for once — a podcast with a hundred thousand listeners was its own kind of credentials — and been told that yes, they held a deposit of Fenland drainage surveys from the 2010s, and no, he couldn't photograph anything without a reading-room pass, which would take three working days.

He drove out anyway. Three days was two days too many. The hearing was on March 10th, and whatever Edmund Voss had put into that report, Wren wanted it in his hands before Brecht's lawyers could remember it existed.

The archivist was a young woman named Priya with cardigan sleeves pushed to her elbows and the particular patience of someone who spent her life saying no to academics.

"Deposit 447," she said, scrolling. "Fenland Development Group, environmental impact assessments, 2011 through 2016." She looked up. "You should know there's a gap."

"A gap?"

"Two boxes are missing from the sequence. 2013, mostly. There's an access restriction flag on them — 'withdrawn by depositor.'" She said it the way people say *the dog ate my homework*, with the flat tone of long familiarity. "It happens more than you'd think. Companies donate their archives for the tax benefit, then get nervous about what's actually in them."

"Who's the depositor of record?"

She turned the screen toward him. FDG Holdings Ltd. And beneath it, countersigned as the donating officer: **C. A. BRECHT**.

Wren felt something turn over in his chest, slow and cold. Brecht hadn't just buried the report. He'd donated it — laundered it into respectability, locked behind an institutional door where no journalist would think to look because archives were boring and boring was safe.

"When can I see what's left?"

"Now, if you like. The unrestricted material's on open shelves." She paused. "But between us? If you're looking at the 2013 survey, the one everyone cites but nobody's read — we have a duplicate. Not official. A former staff member deposited a photocopy years ago under a personal name, before the withdrawal order. It's catalogued as miscellaneous papers." She shrugged. "Archivists keep things. It's the whole job."

* * *

The photocopy lived in a grey Hollinger box on a shelf marked MISC. E–F, and it was thinner than Wren expected. Forty pages, third-generation copy, the type slightly bloomed and soft-edged, like something remembered rather than recorded.

**ENVIRONMENTAL SURVEY: HALLOW BANK AND ADJACENT FENLAND PARCELS. PREPARED FOR FENLAND DEVELOPMENT GROUP PLC. SURVEYOR: E. J. VOSS, C.WEMT. DATED OCTOBER 2013.**

Edmund Voss. In the flesh of his own handwriting, or at least the ghost of it. Wren put on white cotton gloves — Priya insisted, even for a photocopy, and he understood: you handled the dead gently — and began to read.

The first ten pages were what he'd expected. Soil composition. Water table readings. Drainage rates. Competent, methodical, dry as the paper it was printed on. This was the surveyor FDG had hired: a man doing exactly what he was paid for.

Then, page eleven, the tone shifted.

*The submitted development plan assumes a drainage capacity of 4,200 litres/second across the Hallow Bank cut. My measurements over six months indicate a sustained maximum of 2,900 l/s during winter saturation. The difference is not marginal. It is the difference between a managed wetland and a flood channel.*

In the margin, in ballpoint, pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

***They know. I told Hartley twice. He smiled at me.***

Wren sat very still.

Hartley. Gerald Hartley, FDG's project director through the whole build-out — the man whose signature sat on half the planning documents Dee had pulled. Dead four years now, heart attack, sixty-one. One of the four names on the ledger that hadn't drawn a payout so much as a salary.

He kept reading. Page fourteen: peat subsidence projections. Page fifteen: a hydrological map annotated in three colours of ink, the red annotations clustering around the low-lying parcel where the Voss estate itself stood — *retained, not sold*, noted in the surveyor's hand, *see §9*.

Section nine was titled **CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS**, and it was where the report stopped being a report.

*I cannot certify this site as developable to the density proposed. The eastern parcels will flood. Not may. Will. I have modelled it three ways and the water does not care about the business case.*

And below that, the sentence that had ended a marriage, a career, and eventually a man:

*I am aware this finding is unwelcome. I am aware of the pressure applied to my predecessors on adjacent surveys. I wish to state for any future reader that my conclusions are my own, that they are based on twelve months of instrumented measurement, and that I decline to amend them.*

*Decline to amend.* Four words, and every one of them a nail in a coffin.

The margins grew denser toward the end of the document. Whoever Edmund Voss had been in October 2013 — careful, professional, legally circumspect in the body text — had stopped being careful in the margins. The margins were where he told the truth.

Page twenty-two, beside a table of groundwater monitoring stations:

***Station 7 reads wrong. Someone has been at the instruments. Checked against my own gauge — discrepancy of 40cm. Who signs off the maintenance logs? Ask B.***

*B.* Wren wrote it down, though his hand wasn't steady. Bernard Kessler, FDG's site operations manager. Another ledger name. Another man who'd retired comfortably to the gated village outside Ely, where the coroner who'd signed Edmund's death certificate played golf on Thursdays.

Page thirty-one, beside a cost-benefit appendix:

***This appendix was added after my draft was returned. I did not write it. My name is on it anyway.***

And then page thirty-six, the last page of substantive findings, and the margin note that made Wren close his eyes for a moment behind the gloves:

***Brecht came to the house in September. Stood in my kitchen drinking tea and told me that reports are like rivers — they find their level. Told me Marguerite deserved a certain standard of living. I have reported this conversation to no one because who would I report it to? Everyone is paid. I am paid too. The only honest thing I own is this document.***

*If anything happens to me, it did not happen by accident.*

Wren read the line three times.

*If anything happens to me, it did not happen by accident.*

Seven years ago, a surveyor had written his own epitaph in the margin of a photocopy and hidden it in a university basement, and the world had obligingly ruled his death accidental, sealed the file, torn out the page that mattered, and paved the fens above him.

Wren lifted his head. The reading room was quiet. Somewhere overhead, a door closed. He became aware of his own breathing, shallow and fast, and made himself slow it down.

*The dead man speaking at last,* he thought. *So listen.*

* * *

Priya found him an hour later, still at the same table, surrounded by notebook pages.

"You've gone quite pale," she said. "That's usually the effect of the microfilm room, not the misc. shelf."

"Can I get a certified copy of this? Officially. With provenance documentation — deposit date, donor record, the works."

"That I can do properly. Certified copies take—" she caught his expression, "—I'll do it today. But Wren." She lowered her voice. "The personal deposit — the photocopy — the donor was a woman. Retired secretary from FDG's planning office. She told me when she brought it in that she'd copied it because she was asked to shred it and couldn't make herself do it." Priya's mouth twisted. "She said, 'Someone should be able to prove what he wrote.' She died two winters back. Never gave a name beyond Mrs. E. Collier."

Mrs. E. Collier. Wren filed it. Another small ghost in the machine — another person who'd chosen the truth over the payroll, decades apart, in different directions.

"One more question," he said. "The withdrawn boxes. 2013. Is there any way to know what was in them?"

"The catalogue lists titles only." She scrolled, then turned the screen again.

**BOX 447/13a: VOSS, E.J. — FIELD NOTEBOOKS, JAN–DEC 2013 (12 vols).**
**BOX 447/13b: VOSS, E.J. — INSTRUMENT CALIBRATION RECORDS & CORRESPONDENCE RE: AMENDMENT REQUESTS.**

*Correspondence re: amendment requests.* The paper trail of the pressure. Every letter Brecht's office had sent, every reply Edmund had refused to sign — withdrawn, sealed, owned.

"Where do withdrawn deposits physically go?"

"Back to the depositor, normally. Or their solicitors." She hesitated. "Though sometimes they're destroyed. There's no requirement to tell us."

Wren nodded slowly. So the notebooks might exist, or might be ash. Either way, the photocopy in front of him was enough. More than enough. It was a dead man's testimony, witnessed, dated, catalogued, certified by morning.

* * *

He didn't go straight home. He drove to the edge of the fens instead, to the layby where the road ran along the top of the Hallow Bank cut, and got out into the wind. Below him, the drained land stretched away in geometric fields, green and orderly and utterly false — a landscape pretending the water had never been in charge.

*The eastern parcels will flood. Not may. Will.*

And they had. That was the thing that kept snagging in him as he stood there. Seven years ago, in the first spring after Edmund's death, there had been flooding on the eastern parcels — minor, localized, written up in the local paper as a weather event. Dee had found the clipping weeks ago and neither of them had known what to do with it. Now it slotted into place with an audible click. The water had done exactly what Edmund said it would, on exactly the land he said it would reach, and the finding had been buried along with the man, because a confirmed flood risk would have triggered review after review — and the final planning permission hearing was on March 10th.

Everything converged. The date Marguerite had named. The legal deadline. The drowned estate sitting on the one parcel Edmund had refused to sell. Even the murder itself stopped being senseless and became arithmetic: a stalled project, a stubborn surveyor, a chairman who believed reports should find their level.

Wren took out his phone and recorded a voice memo, standing in the wind, because some things needed to be said aloud to be believed.

"March 9th," he said. "Marguerite says her husband dies again on March 9th. The hearing is March 10th. Edmund Voss wrote that his death would not be an accident, and seven years later the machinery is still running — still scheduled, still maintained. They don't need to kill anyone new. They need the old death to stay accidental for one more day." He paused, watching a heron lift off the cut below him, unhurried, ancient. "Which means tomorrow isn't about preventing a murder. It's about interrupting one that's been in progress for seven years."

He pocketed the phone. Then he saw the car.

Grey sedan, far side of the layby, engine off, windows tinted past legality. It hadn't been there when he arrived. Or it had, and he'd been too deep in the report to notice, which was worse.

Wren didn't run. Running was what prey did, and besides, he'd learned something in the past week: the sedan wanted him scared, and scared men made mistakes, and he could not afford mistakes with less than forty-eight hours on the clock. He walked back to his own car at a normal pace, noting the sedan's plate — same partial registration as before, Crane's vehicle, or Crane's employer's — and drove away with his headlights off until the bend, watching the mirror.

The sedan did not follow. It didn't need to. It had seen where he'd been; Priya would get a phone call by morning, and the certified copy would become a problem, and maybe the deposit would be *withdrawn* too, quietly, the way everything in this story got withdrawn.

Except Wren already had the photographs. All forty pages, shot in the stacks while Priya fetched the certification forms, each frame with the box label in the corner for provenance. He'd learned that much from the ledger: evidence existed only in duplicate, triplicate, broadcast.

* * *

Dee was waiting at the studio when he got in, feet on the desk, and she read his face before he'd cleared the door.

"You found it."

"I found him." He set the printouts down in front of her, page thirty-six on top, the margin note facing up. *If anything happens to me, it did not happen by accident.* "Dee — he knew. He knew in October 2013, in his own hand, in his own words. We don't have to prove intent anymore. We don't have to argue. We just have to be loud enough that nobody can pretend they didn't hear him."

Dee read the page twice. When she looked up, her eyes were bright and hard.

"Brecht's going to say it's forgery."

"He'll try. That's why the provenance matters — deposit records, the secretary who saved it, the catalogue trail. And it's why the timing matters. We air it before the hearing, not after. Tomorrow night."

"Tomorrow's the ninth."

"Yeah." Wren sat down heavily. "Tomorrow's the ninth."

Dee was quiet a moment, turning pages. Then, without looking up: "There's something else. While you were playing archivist." She slid her laptop across. On the screen, a property record. "I went looking for the parcel the Voss estate sits on. Thinking about access, sightlines, where Crane would stage himself." She tapped the trackpad. "The estate transferred out of Marguerite's name four years ago. Buyer of record is a shell company — Ashcott Holdings. And Ashcott's registered director is—"

"Don't say Brecht."

"—not Brecht. That's the thing." She looked up. "It's Ilya Crane."

Wren stared at her. The drowned house — the place Edmund had hidden the original ledger, the place the pattern pointed to, the stage set for March 9th — belonged to the fixer himself. Not a corporate asset. Personal.

"Why would Crane buy a flooded ruin?" Dee said. "Unless he was told to keep it. Unless something in it still mattered enough to own the ground it was buried in."

Wren looked at the margin note again. *The only honest thing I own is this document.* Edmund had hidden the original somewhere on his own land, in the water, in the dark — and the man who'd killed him had spent four years standing guard over it without being able to touch it, because destroying the estate would raise questions, and draining it would raise more.

Tomorrow night, Wren thought, someone was finally going to walk into that house and take it.

He just had to survive being the one who did.
