## 30. Preparations

Dee taped a map of the Fens to the wall of her flat with masking tape, the way she used to tape story arcs in the old studio, back when the biggest risk we faced was a libel letter from a golf club.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly. I want to hear how insane it sounds out loud."

"The March ninth accident happens whether or not I'm there," I said. "That's the whole point. Brecht isn't hiring Crane to kill me. He's hiring Crane to close a file. The file needs a body, or at least a disappearance, dated March ninth, so that on March tenth, when the last legal challenge to the planning permission expires, Edmund Voss's death stands confirmed as accidental and the record is sealed forever. If I don't show up, they'll find another way to put a Wren-shaped ending on that date. A car in a drainage channel. A fall off the causeway. They've had seven years of practice making water look like coincidence."

"So the plan," Dee said, "is to attend your own murder."

"With a live microphone."

She sat down on the arm of the sofa. She'd been back from the FDG property for six days and she still flinched at the sound of a car door closing outside. She'd lost weight. Her hands were steady, though, when she wanted them to be, and right now they were steady.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. If we're doing this, we do it properly. Not hero stuff. Engineering."

* * *

Ada arrived at nine with a carrier bag of groceries she didn't mention and a laptop she did. She set the laptop on Dee's kitchen table and opened it like a surgeon laying out instruments.

"Crane's order came through a burner relay routed through a logistics firm in Rotterdam," she said. "I captured the full text. 'Subject to be handled on site, ninth, water preferred, no recovery necessary.' No recovery necessary. You understand what that means."

"It means they don't plan to find me," I said.

"It means they don't plan to look." She looked at me over the top of the laptop, and for a second I saw the woman who'd taught me to interview twenty years ago, before the pension and the gated village and the frame job on the ledger. "Wren. There's a version of this where you don't go. Where we take the order to the police now, tonight, and let them handle it."

"And Brecht's lawyers bury it for eleven months while the hearing lapses. We've been through this."

"We've been through it. I'm allowed to hate it every time."

"You are," I said. "That's part of the plan too."

Dee snorted from the kitchen, where she was making tea like it was a military operation, three mugs, sugar in one, because Ada took sugar and always pretended she didn't.

Ada turned the laptop toward me. On the screen was a schematic of the drained fen around the Voss estate, the one Dee had built from old survey maps and drone footage she'd commissioned through a wedding-photography company so it wouldn't trace back to us.

"Walk me through the ground," Ada said. "You've been out there more than either of us."

I stood. The map was good. The causeway ran east to west, a raised track of compacted gravel across what used to be open marsh, and the Voss estate sat at the far end, a roofless Georgian shell standing in a shallow permanent flood where the drainage had failed. The flood channel — the one Edmund Voss had walked into, or been walked into — curved around the north side of the house, six feet deep in March, cold as a held breath.

"He'll want me at the house," I said. "That's where Edmund's evidence is, or where he thinks I'll find it. Crane's smart enough to let me walk in on my own. He won't touch me on the causeway — too open, too many sightlines from the lockhouse. He'll wait until I'm inside the ruin, where the walls eat sound and there's water on three sides."

"Then we make sure the walls don't eat the sound," Dee said.

She came back to the table with the tea and a small black box, which she set down with the reverence other people reserved for relics.

"Two transmitters," she said. "Not one. One goes in your coat, standard live feed to the show's server, the way you've done a hundred times. The second is the insurance. It's a dumb device — no internet, no app, just a radio link to a repeater I'm going to hide on the lockhouse roof. Even if they jam the cellular network out there, even if they take your phone, the repeater keeps recording to an SD card and streaming on a separate band. Two independent pipelines. To kill the story, they'd have to find both, and the second one they can't find because it's not on you."

"And if they find the repeater?"

"They won't. I'm hiding it, not you. I'm better at hiding things." A beat. "Mostly."

The joke landed soft and nobody smiled. She'd been held for nine days by men waiting for instructions. She'd earned the right to make the joke and the right to not have it land.

"There's a third layer," she went on, and now the steadiness in her hands was the whole of her. "The stream doesn't just go to the server. The moment you go live, the episode auto-publishes. Not a draft, not a private feed. Live, public, pushed to every subscriber, every platform, timestamped. Thousands of people listening in real time. If anything happens to you, there are two thousand witnesses who heard it happen, and a recording that exists in however many thousands of caches before anyone can pull it down."

"That's the point," I said.

"It's also the risk," Ada said. "The moment you go live, Crane knows the plan is blown. He doesn't have to kill you quietly anymore. He just has to kill you."

I'd thought about this. I'd thought about it at three in the morning staring at the ceiling, and again in the shower, and again on the walk over, passing the grey sedan's old haunts. The fear didn't go away. I'd stopped expecting it to. What I'd learned, somewhere between Dee's disappearance and Ada's file, was that fear could be a material. You could build with it.

"He won't," I said. "Not immediately. Think about his position. His instructions are specific: ninth, water, no recovery. If he attacks me the second I go live, on the causeway, in the open, he's a murderer caught on a broadcast with two thousand listeners and a police response already moving. His only play is the original play — get me to the house, get me in the water, make it look like I went in after Edmund's secrets the way Edmund went in. He needs me to keep walking. He needs the performance. And a performance is something I can use."

"You're betting your life on a fixer's professionalism," Ada said.

"I'm betting it on his instructions. Crane follows orders. That's the whole of who he is. It's why he's dangerous and it's why he's predictable." I looked at the map. "Brecht wrote 'water preferred, no recovery necessary' on a channel you could capture. Arrogance is the flaw that runs through this entire case. Edmund's report stalled them because he was the one man they couldn't buy. Brecht has spent seven years proving he doesn't need to hide. Tomorrow he finds out what it costs."

* * *

We worked until two in the morning.

The plan, by the end of it, had the shape of a trap built by three people who had all, in different ways, been caught in one.

Dee handled the signal chain. She drew it out on paper first — mic, transmitter, server, repeater, auto-publish — and then she drew it again with failure points marked in red, and then we sat and killed it three times, hypothetically, and rebuilt it three times. The cellular jammer scenario. The dead zone in the fen's interior, which was real; the survey maps showed a stretch along the north channel where coverage dropped to nothing, which was almost certainly not an accident of geography. The power failure. The server subpoena. Each one had an answer, and each answer had a backup, and the final backup was Dee herself, sitting in the lockkeeper's cottage with the lockkeeper's consent and a shotgun he insisted on leaving by the door, watching the feed and calling a police number Ada had given her — not the local station, not the FDG-friendly inspectorate, but a direct line to the serious crime unit in Norwich that Ada had spent three uncomfortable phone calls cultivating.

"If the stream drops," Dee said, "I call it in as a missing person in progress, and I keep calling every ninety seconds until someone moves. They will know my voice by the fourth call."

"They'd know it by the second," Ada said.

Ada handled the police end, and hated every minute of it, because it meant telling the institution she'd served for thirty years a version of the truth in advance. She'd drafted and redrafted her statement — the captured order, the second ledger, the internal-affairs file, the frame job on her own initials — and in the end she'd cut it to a single page. Facts only. No history, no grievance, no defense of herself. "They can read the file when it's over," she said. "Tomorrow they just need to come when they're called, fast, with bodies."

"And when they ask why you didn't bring this in weeks ago?"

"Then I tell them the truth," she said. "That I was compromised, and I knew it, and I waited until I had something that couldn't be buried. It won't save my pension. It might save yours." She looked at me. "That's the trade. I've made it."

I handled the part nobody wanted to say out loud, so I said it.

"Crane follows me. That's not a guess; he's been following me since the sedan. He'll be on the causeway behind me or ahead of me, and when I go into the house he'll come in after, and at some point in there, it stops being a broadcast and becomes a fight. I can't out-fight him. I'm not going to try. What I can do is make sure that whatever happens in that house happens on the record, and that I've already said the things that need saying before he takes the mic away. The first four minutes are the whole episode. Everything else is aftermath."

"Say the names," Dee said. "Brecht, the coroner, the dates, the ledger entries. Say it like a closing argument."

"I'll say it like an opening one," I said. "Because it is one."

* * *

I went home at three. I didn't sleep. I sat in the kitchen with Edmund Voss's suppressed report open in front of me, the margins dense with his handwriting, the accusations against Callum Brecht underlined twice in fading ink, and I thought about the fact that in roughly eighteen hours I would be standing in the flooded house where that man had died, holding a microphone, waiting for the second man to come and finish what the first one started.

I did the thing I'd been avoiding. I opened my own archive and listened to the first episode of the Harrow case, the one that made me, the one the exchange with Crane had poisoned — *allowed, curated, built for this moment.* I listened for ten minutes and then I stopped, because I'd heard what I needed to hear. The work was real. Whatever they'd let me find, the finding had been mine, and the method was mine, and tomorrow the method was going to be pointed at the men who thought they'd built me. If they had built me, then they'd built the instrument that would take them apart. There was a symmetry in that I could live on.

I recorded a short note, just for the file. "If this ends badly, the story is in the feed. Everything's in the feed. Dee has the masters. Tell her she was right about the money smelling wrong." I deleted it, then didn't, then backed it up in three places, because I'd learned that from Marguerite — the woman who had spent seven years and her whole fortune buying back the truth piece by piece, and who was, at this hour, in a hospice bed in Norfolk, running out of the time she'd been spending so carefully.

I called her. She picked up on the first ring, which meant she'd been awake too.

"Tomorrow?" she said.

"Tomorrow."

"And the date holds?"

"The date holds. March ninth. He chose it. We're just going to be there when he does."

There was a long pause, and in it I heard the thing she'd never quite said, the thing underneath the confession and the ledger and the seven years of silence: that she had chosen the date herself, once, and it had been the single worst act of her life, and that tomorrow was not just exposure to her. It was arithmetic. Guilt, finally balanced.

"Mr. Okafor," she said, formal, because formality was how she held herself together, "Edmund used to say the fens keep everything. That nothing given to that water is ever really lost. I used to think it was a comfort. I've spent seven years learning it's a threat." A breath. "Make the water give him back."

"I will."

I hung up and looked at the map one more time, at the little pencil cross Dee had marked at the north channel, at the roofless house at the end of the causeway, at the date written in the corner in Ada's handwriting: *9 March.*

The fear was still there. It would be there tomorrow, walking the causeway with me, keeping pace behind Crane. But fear was a material, and we had spent two days building with it, and by tomorrow night the water would either keep me or give me back — and either way, the whole country was going to hear it happen.

* * *

At dawn the weather turned.

The forecast had promised a dry, clear evening — the kind of still, cold twilight that draws sightseers to the drained fen to photograph the drowned house. By eight the wind had come around to the northeast and the sky had gone the color of wet slate, and Dee stood in the doorway of the lockhouse with her phone in her hand and said the word I'd been dreading without knowing it.

"Rain."

Rain on the mic. Rain on the transmitters. Rain turning the causeway to slop and the flood channels to something faster and deeper, and — I realized, with a cold drop in my stomach — rain giving Crane exactly the cover he'd want, and exactly the alibi. *Accidents happen in weather. People slip. People fall. The fens take them.*

Ada was already on the phone to Norwich, her voice low and hard. Dee was re-taping the repeater housing against water ingress with the focus of a woman defusing a bomb. And I stood at the window looking out at the first grey curtains of it coming across the marsh toward us, and understood that the day had just stopped being the plan we'd made.

It had become the one we'd have to improvise — and somewhere out on the fen road, headlights off, a car was already waiting for me to start walking.
