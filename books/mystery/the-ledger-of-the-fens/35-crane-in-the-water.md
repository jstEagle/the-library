## 35. Crane in the Water

The water found us before I found it.

One moment I was backing through the flooded ground floor of the Voss house, the original ledger zipped inside my jacket against my chest like a second heartbeat, and the next my heel met nothing — the floor simply ended, dissolved into the channel that had eaten the eastern wing seven years ago. I went down hard on one knee into black water, cold as a held grudge, and the shock of it shot up through my hip and into my teeth.

The mic was still live. I knew that the way you know your own name. Forty-one minutes of broadcast, and somewhere out in the dark beyond the fens, thousands of people were listening to me breathe.

"Still here," I said, because it was true and because saying it kept me true. "Still recording."

Crane came through the doorway like the building had exhaled him.

He'd lost the polite fixer somewhere between the causeway and the house — the grey sedan man, the man who'd said *she's being kept comfortable* about my producer with the flat calm of a man discussing storage. What was left was bigger and blunter: a silhouette against the moonlit water in the broken roof, shoulders set, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had done this before and been paid well for it.

"Okafor." My name in his mouth sounded like a diagnosis.

"That's me." I got my feet under me. The water was waist-deep here, maybe more, and I couldn't see the bottom. Couldn't see anything. "You're on the air, by the way. Everything you say. Everything you do."

"I know." He said it without inflection, and that was worse than a threat. "I've known since the causeway. It doesn't change the arithmetic."

"What arithmetic is that?"

"Yours or mine?"

The question hung in the ruined room. Above us, the roof gaped where the storm of seven years ago had peeled it back, and moonlight came down in a wide silver column, lighting the water like a stage. I understood, with the strange clarity that arrives when the body knows it's in danger and decides to enjoy the view anyway, that we were standing in the exact room where Edmund Voss had died. The channel had been narrower then. The house had still been a house.

"Mine," I said. "Give me yours too. I've got time. I've got an audience."

Crane moved left, angling, cutting off the doorway to the stairs — the only dry way out. He wasn't rushing. Rushing was for amateurs and for men who feared witnesses. He had a witness in his pocket and he knew it.

"Mine is simple," he said. "One more accident in a fen. The pattern holds. The hearing tomorrow proceeds. Brecht's people finish what they paid for, and I retire somewhere with no canals." He tilted his head. "Yours is more interesting. You've got the ledger. You've got the broadcast. You think you've already won."

"Have I?"

"No. Because winning requires you to be alive to say you won. Dead men don't testify. Dead men's recordings get *challenged*. Chain of custody, Okafor. A dead man's phone is evidence a very good lawyer can make evaporate." He took another step, and the water barely rippled around his legs. He was six feet away. "You need to survive to make any of this real. That's your arithmetic. And I only need one number."

The cold had climbed past my waist and was working on my ribs. My fingers were going stupid around the phone in my left hand. Somewhere in my ear, faintly, I could hear the stream's soft hiss — and beneath it, if I strained, the knowledge that Dee was on the other end of it, alive because I'd pulled her out of an FDG property two days ago, watching a screen in a police car somewhere, screaming at it.

"Edmund Voss hid the ledger in this house," I said. Talking. Keep him talking. Every sentence was a rope, and rope was the only weapon I had that he couldn't take away. "He hid it because he knew they were coming for him. He was a surveyor, Crane. He understood water. He understood exactly how they'd stage it. And he still couldn't stop it."

"No," Crane agreed. "He couldn't."

"Did you do it? Seven years ago. Was it you?"

Something moved across his face — not guilt, nothing so useful. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man hearing his own work described.

"I don't discuss closed files," he said. "That's the trade."

"The trade."

"Everyone in this business has a trade. You trade in other people's grief, packaged weekly. Brecht trades in land. I trade in outcomes. Edmund Voss traded in a report he should have sold and didn't." He shrugged, and the water shrugged with him. "Everyone's ledger balances. Yours is about to."

He came at me fast then, and all my rope-talk was spent.

I got my right arm up — not to strike, I'm not that man, I've never been that man — but to shove, and the shove was like pushing a moored boat. He absorbed it, caught my wrist, and twisted, and the phone went spinning out of my hand into the dark water with a sound like a swallowed word. The stream light — that small green eye — vanished under the surface and the room went dark except for moonlight and the two of us.

"Still live," I gasped, because the phone was waterproof and because I needed it to be true. "Still live, still live—"

"Then they can hear this too." He drove me backward and I went under.

The fen water closed over my head and the world became pressure and cold and a roaring like the inside of a shell. My back hit something — a submerged wall, a lintel, the bones of the house — and Crane's hands found my jacket and pinned me against it. The ledger dug into my chest. I thought, with absurd detachment: *at least the ledger can't drown.*

I got my knee up. Once, twice, blind, into the general region of him, and on the third one he grunted and his grip shifted, and I tore sideways and came up coughing, hauling air that tasted of silt and seven-year rot.

He was already there. Of course he was. He'd been doing this since before I'd ever held a microphone.

"You should have taken the archive," Crane said quietly. "In the exchange. Your three cases, your whole career, signed over to me, and you walk away rich and clean. It was a generous offer."

"It was a confession."

"It was a *retirement*." He grabbed for me again and I twisted away, and my foot came down on nothing.

Not nothing. The channel. The flood channel — I'd been backing toward it the whole time, and now the ground fell away beneath me like a trapdoor and the fen took me whole.

* * *

People ask, later, what it's like to go into fen water in March, at night, in a drowned house. I never have a good answer, because what I remember most isn't the cold. It's the quiet.

Underwater, the world stops talking. The broadcast stops. Crane stops. Even my own heartbeat sounds distant, like someone else's story. The channel is deep — deeper than it looks, scoured out by decades of drainage flow — and I sank into it with my jacket filling, with the ledger pressing its long rectangle against my sternum like a hand, and I looked up through the black water at a coin of moonlight wavering far above, and I thought:

*This is how Edmund died. Not here, not exactly — but like this. Cold and quiet and alone, with men standing on the bank deciding whether to reach in.*

I have spent three years of my professional life reconstructing that moment from lockkeeper testimony and torn pages and a coroner's bought signature. I thought I understood it. I understood nothing. Understanding is what you do on dry land.

My boots found bottom. Silt, soft as surrender, and I pushed off it with everything my legs had left.

My head broke the surface and the noise of the world crashed back — my own gasping, water sheeting off me, and Crane, standing at the channel's edge, silhouetted against the moon column, watching me with the detached attention of a man confirming a result.

"Okafor." He crouched, unhurried, and extended one hand down toward me — not to help. To hold me under, if I came close enough. "You've made this harder than it needed to be. But the outcome's the same, and the water does the paperwork. That was always the elegance of this place. The fens keep the books."

I trod water, ten feet from his hand, and the cold was already in my muscles, turning them to borrowed equipment. I had maybe two minutes of useful body left. Maybe less. The current was real here — a slow, patient pull toward the sluice, toward the open fen, the same pull that had carried Edmund Voss half a mile downstream before they found him.

And in that moment — treading black water, freezing, live to thousands of people who could hear only my breathing — I understood the pattern completely. Not the ledger pattern. The older one.

Every death in this story had happened the same way. Not with violence, not with drama, but with a man in the water and other men on the bank, choosing not to reach. Edmund Voss. The Harrow drownings — I'd proved they were murders, but I'd never asked the question underneath: *who stood on the bank?* Someone always stood on the bank. Someone always watched. The fens didn't kill anyone. The fens just held people down while the arithmetic was done.

Crane was on the bank. I was in the water. And the pattern was waiting for me to complete it — waiting for me to drown, or waiting, later, for Crane to answer for it with his life, or his freedom, or whatever the system took from men like him. The pattern didn't care which. The pattern only needed the water to keep its books.

"No," I said.

Crane's head tilted. "No?"

"No." I said it again, louder, for the phone somewhere under the surface, for the thousands listening to a man's breath in the dark. "You hear that? Whoever's out there. The answer is no."

"Okafor, you're hypothermic and incoherent—"

"The pattern ends tonight." My voice was shaking and my teeth were chattering and I have never meant words more. "Not because I win. Because I'm done playing it. You hear me, Crane? Seven years ago men stood on a bank and let a surveyor drown for a land deal, and everyone who touched that decision told themselves it was arithmetic. Everyone balanced their ledger. And it worked — it worked for seven years — because everyone kept choosing the story. The clean story. The accidental death. The closed file. The tidy pattern."

I started swimming. Not toward him. Toward the near bank, the broken wall, away from his reaching hand — and he moved along the edge, tracking me, patient, letting the cold do his work.

"But it doesn't have to," I said. "That's the thing nobody in this whole rotten chain ever figured out. Brecht didn't have to. Ada didn't have to. Marguerite didn't have to. And you—" I got a hand on the broken wall, and the stone was knife-cold and I held on anyway. "You don't have to. Right now. You could be the first man in seven years to reach into the water."

He stood above me. Moonlight on the water between us, and his hand hanging at his side, and I watched him weigh it — I actually watched it, the small war behind his face, the fixer's arithmetic meeting something it had no column for.

"You're offering me a confession," he said.

"I'm offering you a choice. It's the thing you people keep forgetting exists."

And then the wall moved.

Seven years of water in the mortar, and my weight, and Crane's as he leaned out over the channel edge to finish it — the stone gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking, a whole section of the old garden wall sloughing into the channel, and Crane went with it.

He didn't scream. I'll say that for him. He went in the way heavy things go into water — a vast displacement, a wall of cold hitting me in the face, and then the moonlight where he'd been was just moonlight, and the water was boiling, and his hand broke the surface once, twice, grasping at nothing.

The pattern, arriving exactly on schedule. The fen keeping its books. The fourth accident, self-delivering, and every lawyer in Brecht's pocket would call it providence, and the hearing tomorrow would proceed, and the ledger in my jacket would be a dead man's phone and a drowned fixer and a story with a hole in it shaped exactly like my integrity.

I let go of the wall and went in after him.

The cold hit me like a verdict. I went under, into the churning dark, and found him by collision — his coat, heavy with water, dragging him down, and him fighting it the way strong men fight drowning, which is to say badly, wasting strength on panic. I got an arm around his chest from behind, the way the water-rescue leaflet says and every instinct screams against, and his elbow caught me in the face, once, twice — he was still trying to kill me even as he drowned, the trade running deep — and then the cold got into him too and the fighting stopped being aimed.

We came up together. He was coughing, huge racking coughs, and I was coughing, and the current had us both now, pulling us down-channel toward the sluice, and I kicked for the bank with legs that belonged to someone else.

"Grab — the wall," I got out. "Grab it or — the arithmetic — balances."

His hand found the broken stone a half-second before mine found his collar. I don't know which grip was desperation and which was decision. Maybe there's no difference. Maybe that's the whole lesson and it took me a drowned house and a frozen channel to learn it.

We came out of the water like something born, dragging each other up the bank and collapsing on the fen grass in the moonlight, side by side, two men who'd been trying to kill each other four minutes ago now bound by the only contract that matters — the one where you've seen each other drown a little.

For a long time neither of us could speak. The stream — wherever my phone was, whatever it was hearing — had us both: two men gasping in the dark, alive, the pattern broken on the bank of the channel where it was built.

Crane spoke first. His voice was wrecked, scraped raw by fen water and something else.

"You should have let me go."

"Yeah," I said. "I know. That's what they all said. That's what the bank always says."

He turned his head, and in the moonlight I saw something I hadn't seen in him before — not fear exactly. Recalculation. A man discovering his ledger had a page he'd never audited.

"Brecht will have people at the causeway," he said. "Backup. When I don't call in."

"Ada's already on the causeway," I said. "Has been since ten o'clock. Your order came through her channel an hour ago, Crane. In writing. Brecht wrote it down." I started laughing and couldn't stop, the mad gasping laugh of a freezing man, because it was true and it was done and the arrogance had finally, finally tipped. "You weren't the plan tonight. You were the evidence."

He stared at me. And then, distantly, across the black fen, lights. Moving fast. A convoy of them, coming down the causeway toward the drowned estate — and behind them, faint on the wind, the first sound of sirens.

Crane lay back on the wet grass and closed his eyes, a man surrendering to the only outcome left.

"Your phone," he said. "It's still in the channel."

I looked at the black water, and the moon on it, and the small green light that had to be still burning down there in the dark, forty feet out, keeping its own books.

"Yeah," I said, and started getting my ruined legs under me. "And it's still live."
