## 9. The Second Ledger

The rain had stopped by the time Mara reached the dispatch office, but the town hadn't dried. Water stood in the gutters like held breath, and the sodium lamps along Corvane Street turned every puddle into a small, trembling mirror of the night sky. She let herself in through the side door of Windrow Taxi with the key she'd been using for seven years—the one Ruth Calloway had used for eleven before her—and stood a moment in the dark of the entryway, listening to the building settle around her.

It was nearly midnight. Her shift didn't start until ten, but she couldn't sit in her flat another hour. The police had finished with it two days ago—dusted, photographed, returned everything to its place with the terrible courtesy of people who had catalogued your life and found nothing worth taking except your sense of safety—but the rooms still felt handled. She kept catching herself checking the closet, counting hangers, touching the sleeve of the cardigan that had hung there untouched while some stranger wore its twin down to the river and into the water.

So instead she came here, to the one room that had always made sense.

The dispatch office smelled of cold coffee and old carpet and the faint electrical warmth of the radio console, which never fully powered down. Mara switched on the desk lamp, hung her wet coat on the hook by the door—Ruth's hook, though the brass had lost its shine years before Mara ever arrived—and sat down in the swivel chair that still listed slightly to the left no matter how many times she tightened the bolt beneath it.

The logbooks lived on the shelf behind the desk. Seven of them now, hers, bound in green cloth covers that the company ordered from a stationer in Harrowgate: *Night Book, 2018–19*, through *Night Book, 2024–25*. Before them, eleven more in the same binding, all in Ruth Calloway's hand—a small, upright script with surprising flourishes on the capital letters, as if even her penmanship had been trying to be polite.

Mara had inherited those books the way you inherit anything from a predecessor whose face you never saw: without ceremony. On her first night, the then-owner, Mr. Pell, had gestured at the shelf and said, "Ruth's logs. Read a few so you get the feel of it," and then he'd answered his phone and forgotten her entirely. She had read them, all right—not for the feel of the work but because she was twenty-six and new in town and the logs were the closest thing Windrow had to a chronicle of itself. Drunks and insomniacs and nurses coming off shift, fare numbers and pickup points, the occasional note in the margin: *paid exact change, seemed frightened* or *asked me twice if I was alone tonight.*

She had read them the way she read everything—thoroughly, invisibly, filing away what mattered—and then she had shelved them and not opened them again in seven years.

Tonight she took down the last of Ruth's books. *Night Book, 2015–16.* The cover was faded unevenly, lighter where a window's sun must have crossed it summer after summer, and the spine was cracked in a way that suggested it had been opened often, and recently, before it was shelved forever.

Mara told herself she was looking for Edmund Voss. The man who had died wearing her clothes had come back to town for the anniversary dinner; he had taken a cab somewhere in his last week of life; maybe he'd called this number once, maybe he'd said something to a dispatcher the way people said things to dispatchers, the way they said things to bartenders and priests and women they would never see. It was a reasonable line of inquiry. Detective Renner would approve of it, or he would have, back when they were the kind of people who told each other about their days.

But that wasn't the whole truth, and Mara knew it, and sitting alone under the lamp she admitted it: she wasn't only hunting Edmund Voss. She was hunting the shape of her own situation. Someone had walked into her flat while she sat in this chair and taken her clothes off their hangers. Someone knew her shifts, her wardrobe, her habits, her solitude. Seven years ago, another woman had sat in this same chair, known the same streets, kept the same confidences—and then walked out of her life so completely that even Mr. Pell had only shrugged when Mara asked. *Retired, moved to the coast, I think. Or her sister's. Somewhere damp.*

Nobody moved *somewhere damp* and left no address. Nobody vanished from a small town without somebody noticing. Unless nobody was looking.

Mara opened the book to March 2016 and began to read backward.

* * *

Ruth Calloway's entries were spare, professional, almost identical in rhythm to Mara's own—she recognized the cadence the way you recognize a family resemblance in a stranger's laugh. *Fare 41. Pick-up, St. Brendan's car park. Drop, Latchmere Road. Passenger declined conversation.* But as Mara worked backward through the winter of 2015–16, she began to notice something else. Certain entries were marked with a small symbol in the left margin—a tiny circle with a tail, like a balloon on a string, or perhaps an eye looking sideways.

There were nine of them between November and February. Nine fares out of roughly four hundred, flagged with that sideways eye.

Mara pulled the book closer to the lamp.

The first was November 3rd. *Fare 12. Pick-up, Windrow House service gate, 11:40 p.m. Drop, undisclosed—"just drive." Passenger: male, older, agitated. Paid double, asked me to forget the route. Note: he watched the house until we cleared the hill.*

The second, November 17th. *Fare 27. Pick-up, bus depot. Young woman, crying. Asked if I knew "what happened to the Voss boy." Told her I drive nights, I don't know days. She got out at the church and ran.*

The third. December 2nd. *Fare 33. Windrow House again. This time they sent the driver away and made him wait at the gate forty minutes. When he came back he said the daughter was shouting.*

Mara went very still.

*The daughter.*

She turned pages with a care that felt ceremonial, the way you handle evidence, or letters from the dead. The flagged entries continued—January, February—each one a small, precise record of something Ruth had noticed and could not put down. A passenger who mentioned the pond. A delivery driver who said the Voss family paid him to take crates to the marsh at odd hours. A woman who booked a standing Friday fare to the county archive and cancelled it three weeks running.

And threaded among them, in the margins, in handwriting that grew smaller and tighter as the months advanced, fragments that were not fare notes at all:

*Asked Pell about '95 again. He changed the subject. He always changes the subject.*

*The constable's report is sealed. Why is a drowning sealed?*

*Friday the 14th: lights on at the House all night. All the cars there. Nobody leaves till dawn.*

Mara sat back. The chair creaked its familiar complaint, but she barely heard it. Her own Night Books sat on the shelf beside this one—seven years of the same town, the same streets, the same 3 a.m. confessions—and she thought of how many times she had written something in a margin and felt the small, guilty thrill of knowing too much, and how she had always believed herself alone in that. The watcher unseen. The keeper of the ledger of secrets.

She had a predecessor. She had always had a predecessor. And the predecessor had seen it too—or the beginning of it, the edges of it—had sat in this chair and felt the same cold finger trace her spine and had written it down in a private code of sideways eyes, and then—

And then nothing. Then the book ended.

Mara flipped forward through the remaining blank pages, half expecting the story to resume somewhere, a note tucked between leaves, anything. Nothing. The final dated entry was February 29th, 2016—a leap day, she noted, the kind of date that exists only because we insist on correcting our calendars, on making the record true—and it was ordinary. *Fare 58. Pick-up, hospital staff entrance. Routine.* Below it, in the margin, the sideways eye appeared one last time, but there was no fare attached to it. Just four words, written in that tight, flourished hand, pressed hard enough into the paper that Mara could feel the grooves with her fingertip:

*Windrow pays for silence. Ask the daughter.*

Then nothing. No forwarding address. No resignation letter in the company file—Mara checked, though she already knew; she'd helped Mr. Pell clear out the office cabinets during the flood year and there had been nothing but tax forms and a dead spider the size of a coin. Eleven years of night shifts, and Ruth Calloway had simply stepped off the edge of the town's attention, the way a fare steps out of a cab and the door closes and the light moves on.

Except Ruth hadn't stepped out. Ruth had been *removed*. Mara felt it the way she sometimes knew, before the radio crackled, which corner a drunk would call from—felt it as pattern, as inevitability. You didn't write *Windrow pays for silence* in a secret margin and then retire gracefully to somewhere damp. You wrote that sentence and then, sooner or later, someone came to talk to you about it.

Or someone came to do more than talk.

Her hands had gone cold. She wrapped them around the cooling mug of coffee she'd poured and forgotten, and made herself think it through the way she made herself think through everything: slowly, in order, writing nothing down yet.

Edmund Voss comes back to town after thirty years away, for the anniversary dinner. Within a week he is dead, drowned, dressed in my clothes. The first drowning—1995, the boy from the portrait, ruled accidental—happened at Windrow House, and everyone who was a child there that summer is circling the estate now like moths that have learned caution. Ruth Calloway, asking questions about '95 in 2016, disappears. And in the margins of her last book, in the last week of her last winter: *Ask the daughter.*

The daughter. Odile Voss had a daughter—Mara knew that much from the society page of the *Windrow Courier*, which she read the way she read everything, completely. Isolde? No—Ilse. Ilse Voss, married name something continental, living abroad, appearing in photographs beside her mother at charity functions with the smile of a woman posing for a passport. The daughter who, according to Ruth's margin note from December, had been *shouting* at the House while a driver waited forty minutes at the gate.

Shouting about what? About a brother drowned thirty years before? About money? About silence being renegotiated?

Mara looked at the sideways eye one more time. An eye, looking away. That was what it was—not a balloon on a string. A person glancing over their shoulder. Ruth had drawn herself into her own margins: the woman who watched, watching herself be watched.

"I see you," Mara said quietly to the empty office, and meant Ruth, and meant whoever had made Ruth disappear, and meant, with a chill that settled into her bones and stayed, whoever had been standing in her flat counting her cardigans.

* * *

She worked until four in the morning, and she worked the way she did everything: invisibly, thoroughly, leaving no mark.

She photographed every flagged entry with her phone, then photographed the margins, then photographed the book itself open to the final page, twice, in case the first images blurred. She compared Ruth's hand against the official log entries and confirmed what she'd suspected: the private notes were written in a different pressure, a different speed—composed first, transcribed carefully, the way you copy something you can't risk being caught drafting. Which meant Ruth had written her real thoughts somewhere else first, somewhere disposable, and preserved only the distilled version here. There might be other notebooks. There might be boxes. There might be a sister's attic in some damp coastal town after all.

She searched the office. Not frantically—methodically, the way she'd once searched her own flat for what else the intruder had touched. Behind the radiator, in the cabinet with the swollen drawer, under the false bottom of the stationery cupboard that wasn't false at all, just deep enough to disappoint. In the pocket of the company jacket hanging on the second hook—one of Ruth's, monogrammed *R.C.* on the tag in thread gone silver with age—she found a bus timetable from 2016 and a hairpin, and nothing else.

It was in the last place she looked, because it was always in the last place: the shelf itself. Running her fingers along the underside of the wooden lip where the logbooks stood, feeling for the dust-shadow of things long removed, her fingertip caught on a strip of tape that had dried to a brittle amber ribbon. Someone had taped something to the underside of the shelf, years ago, and torn it away in a hurry—except tape tears, and paper tears, and a corner the size of a postage stamp had stayed behind, folded under the adhesive like a moth under ice.

Mara peeled it free with her thumbnail and held it to the lamp.

Heavy cream stock, the kind the Voss stationery was famous for. On it, in a hurried hand that was not Ruth's—blunter, a man's or a woman pressed for time, the letters leaning as if written standing up—three words:

*She knows about*

Nothing else. No name, no date. A fragment of a message never delivered, or delivered and retrieved, or dropped and abandoned—torn from its sheet in anger or fear and shoved up against the underside of the shelf where it had waited eight years for someone with Mara's habits to find it.

*She knows about.*

About the pond. About the payments. About the boy in 1995. About the report. The sentence wanted finishing, and every possible ending pointed uphill, toward the House, toward the family that had owned this town's silence for thirty years and had just demonstrated, on a riverbank in October, that they still intended to keep it.

Mara copied the fragment into her current Night Book—her hand steady, her mind not—alongside the transcription of Ruth's final words. Two women, one chair, nine years apart, writing the same town down. Ruth had asked her question and vanished for it. Mara set down her pen and understood, with a clarity that felt less like courage than like gravity, that she was going to have to ask it too.

*Ask the daughter.*

Ilse Voss. Married name, foreign address, a lifetime of careful distance from Windrow. Finding her would take calls, records, patience—the invisible woman's particular craft. But Mara had spent seven years learning where everyone in this town went at 3 a.m., and nobody, she thought, picking up the phone and pulling the company's oldest client cards from the bottom drawer, nobody stays truly hidden from the night dispatcher.

Not even the ones who disappeared.

The card she wanted was third from the bottom of the 1990s box, yellowed soft as cloth: *Voss, I. — Windrow House — account settled in full, 1998.* And beneath the printed name, in pencil, in someone's old shorthand, a forwarding notation that made Mara's breath catch:

*R.C. handled personally. Do not book. Refer all to O.V.*

Ruth had driven the daughter. Ruth had known her route, her hours, her voice at 3 a.m.—and then Ruth had written *ask the daughter* and gone into the dark.

Mara closed the box, turned off the lamp, and stood a moment in the dark of the office, listening to the rain start again against the windows, softer now, patient, like something that had all the time in the world and knew exactly where she lived.

Tomorrow she would find Ilse Voss.

If the daughter was still alive to ask.
