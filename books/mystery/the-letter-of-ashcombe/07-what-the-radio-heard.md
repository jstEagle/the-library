## 7. What the Radio Heard

The name was in the oldest phone book Gideon owned — a 1989 Ashcombe directory with a cracked spine and a coffee ring on the cover that had been there longer than some of the town's current residents. *Hollis Wren, Dispatcher.* No relation to Edmund's alias, as far as Gideon could tell, though the coincidence sat in their chest like a swallowed stone.

Wren lived out past the feed store, in a low brick house with a satellite dish bolted crooked to the roof and a garden gone wild with late-season zinnias. A hand-painted sign by the door read NO SOLICITING. NO PITY. YES COFFEE.

Gideon knocked twice. From inside, a voice like gravel poured over gravel: "If you're selling gutters, I've got a roof and a shotgun and only one of them works."

"Dispatch," Gideon said. "Night shift."

A long pause. Then: "Come around back. Front door sticks and I don't feel like apologizing to it."

The backyard held a rusted patio set, a hummingbird feeder drained dry, and an old man in a canvas chair wearing a cardigan despite the warmth, his legs under a quilt even though it wasn't cold enough for one. Hollis Wren was maybe eighty, built like a fence post left out too many winters, with a radio scanner sitting on the table beside him, tuned to static. He looked at Gideon the way old dispatchers look at everyone — assessing, cataloguing, already deciding what kind of call they'd be.

"Gideon Blackwood," he said. "You took over the desk I sat in for thirty-one years."

"Somebody had to."

"Somebody did." He gestured at the second chair. "Sit. You want coffee?"

"I heard there was coffee."

"There's always coffee. It's terrible. That's how you know it's honest." He poured from a thermos without standing, hands steady in a way that surprised Gideon given the tremor in his voice. "You're here about the stones."

Word traveled fast in Ashcombe. Faster than any dispatch Gideon had ever run.

"I'm here about a lot of things," Gideon said. "But yes. The stones."

"Hm." Wren drank. Looked at the dead feeder. Looked at the static on the scanner like it might say something worth hearing. "I figured somebody would come eventually. Took thirty years, but time moves slow in this town unless it's covering something. Then it sprints."

Gideon leaned forward. "Mr. Wren—"

"Hollis."

"Hollis. The week Edmund Ashcombe died. October of '94. You were on shift."

"I was on every shift," Wren said. "That was the problem with me. Never learned to say no to a headset." He set down the cup. His eyes, when they came up to meet Gideon's, were clear in a way that made Gideon suspect the half-lucidity was partly performance — a fog he rolled out when he wanted people to underestimate him. "What do you want to know exactly? Because I can give you the version I logged, or I can give you the version that happened. They are not the same document."

"The one that happened."

Wren nodded slowly, as if Gideon had passed a test whose questions hadn't been announced. He reached into the pocket of his cardigan and produced a folded piece of paper, yellowed soft as cloth at the creases. He didn't open it. Just held it.

"October ninth, 1994. Night shift, my desk. Eleven-forty p.m., a fare request comes in from the Okafor woman. Ruth Okafor. She drove nights for your company — good driver, better than most of the men, never complained about the routes nobody wanted. Quarry road, she says. Pickup at her own apartment, drop-off quarry road, wait for return fare." He tapped the paper against his knee. "I logged it. 11:47 pickup. 12:15 last transmission."

"Last transmission saying what?"

"Saying she'd arrived." Wren's voice flattened into something recited, worn smooth by repetition. "'Passenger's out. Waiting on return.' Standard. Then nothing. Radio silence for two hours, which happens — drivers nap, drivers get chatty with whoever they're waiting on. At three a.m. I called the number she'd left. No answer. At four I flagged it to the owner. And at four-fifteen—" he unfolded the paper at last, smoothed it on the table with both hands, "—I got a call on the landline. Not the radio. The landline. Which meant someone who knew the office number, which meant someone official."

Gideon's mouth had gone dry. "Who?"

"He didn't give a name. Voice I knew, though. Knew it from council meetings, from the chamber of commerce dinners where they let me pour coffee because I wasn't anybody. Calm voice. Reasonable voice. Said there'd been a mix-up, that Miss Okafor had taken personal leave effective immediately, family matter out of state, and that the log for those nights needed correcting. Clerical error, he said. Dates entered wrong. He said it the way you'd say *the sky is blue* — like anyone who disagreed was the crazy one."

"And you corrected the log."

"I corrected the log." Wren said it without flinching, and Gideon understood that the flinch had happened decades ago and all that remained was the scar tissue of having said it ten thousand times to himself. "Three nights. Tore the pages out myself. Cleanest thing I ever did at that desk and the worst." He pushed the paper across the table. "I kept this, though. Carbon copy of my original entry. In case I ever grew a spine. Took me thirty years to grow it, and now I'm too old to carry it anywhere useful. So it goes home with you."

Gideon picked it up. The carbon was faint, ghost-gray letters:

*10/9 — 2347 — OKAFOR R — PICKUP ELM & 3RD — DEST QUARRY RD — AWAIT RETURN FARE —*

And beneath it, in different ink, different pressure, added later in Wren's own hand:

*NO RETURN. NO ANSWER. LOG ORDERED CLEARED BY PHONE. NOTED FOR THE RECORD THAT DOESN'T EXIST.*

* * *

They sat with it a while. The static hissed. Somewhere down the road a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You understand what this means," Gideon said finally. "For the company. For the desk."

"I understand exactly what it means." Wren refilled his cup, though it was still full, just to have something to do with his hands. "The cover-up ran through dispatch. Through the logs. Through the chair." He looked at Gideon with something that wasn't quite apology and wasn't quite warning. "Every record this town trusted about that night came off a headset like yours. You want to know who buried Ruth Okafor? Start with the people paid to write down where everybody was. We're the town's memory, son. And somebody taught our memory to lie."

Gideon felt it land in the place Wren had aimed it — not the chest, lower, somewhere structural. The chair. The headset. The sacred logbook Gideon had defended in a hundred small arguments, the one they'd told Vera Ashcombe was the closest thing Ashcombe had to a conscience. Thirty years ago, that conscience had been edited with a telephone call and a steady hand.

"Why keep the carbon?" Gideon asked. "Why risk it?"

Wren smiled, and it was a terrible smile, thin and old. "Because Ruth Okafor had a little girl. Maybe four years old. She used to come sit in the dispatch office sometimes when her mama's shift ran long. Colored pictures on the backs of trip sheets. Drew me once — big head, little body, headphones like Mickey Mouse ears." His voice caught, cleared itself. "When they told me she'd run off, abandoned the child, I knew it was a lie before they finished saying it. Women don't leave children. Not women like Ruth. So I kept the paper. Not because I planned to do anything. Because throwing it away would have made me the lie."

"Do you think she's alive?"

Wren was quiet long enough that the static seemed loud. "I think a fare went to the quarry road and no fare came back. I think the quarry road is where men go to make problems quiet. And I think if she were alive, she'd have come back for that girl, because I watched that woman check her watch every forty minutes like the girl was a fare she couldn't afford to miss." He shook his head. "No. I don't think she's alive. I think she's the reason the coffin's full of rocks. One empty box for another."

* * *

Gideon stayed another hour. Wren's lucidity came and went after that — mid-sentence he'd drift, calling Gideon by the name of a driver dead since 2001, asking whether the Henderson account had settled its tab. But between the drifts he gave up fragments, each one a splinter:

The quarry road fare had been requested by phone, cash arranged in advance — unusual, expensive, the kind of fare only the founding families booked that way.

Ruth's cab was found three days later, parked neat as a prayer behind the Voss grain elevator, keys in the ignition, tank half full. Reported abandoned. Impounded. Sold at auction within the month to a buyer whose name Wren had never been able to read on the paperwork.

And once — only once — Wren lowered his voice to almost nothing and said: "There was a fourth man on that road that night. I never logged him because nobody told me about him until later, and by then I'd learned what logging things costs. But the reasonable voice on the phone, the one who ordered the pages out — he slipped once. Said, 'We can't have another one asking questions.' Another. Like there'd been a first one." He looked at Gideon. "You find out who the first one was, you'll have found out everything."

Gideon folded the carbon copy into an inner pocket, next to the heart, which was where important documents went whether or not anatomy agreed.

At the door, Wren stopped them. The fog had lifted again; his eyes were sharp as tacks.

"They'll come for you now," he said. "Not with fists. With phones. With doubt. With nice men asking nice questions about your credentials, your history, your right to be poking at this. That's how it worked on me — they never threatened, they just made it very clear how alone I'd be if I remembered too loudly." He gripped Gideon's wrist, surprisingly strong. "So remember loudly anyway. Somebody has to be the town's memory. Might as well be the one who actually tells the truth."

* * *

The drive back into town took eleven minutes. Gideon knew because they timed everything; it was the occupational disease of dispatchers, converting life into intervals.

At minute nine, passing the shuttered Ashcombe estate, Gideon noticed the headlights.

A car idling at the estate's broken gate, engine running, lights off — no. Lights on, then off, a slow blink like something deciding whether to wake. Gideon slowed without meaning to, and the car pulled out, unhurried, and turned the opposite direction, and was gone before Gideon could take the plate. Old habit made them check the mirror anyway. New instinct, colder, made them note the shape: a sedan, dark, county-plate bracket, the kind of car that belonged to everyone and therefore no one.

Gideon sat in the middle of the empty road for a moment, hands on the wheel, listening to their own pulse do arithmetic.

Then the phone buzzed. Unknown number. Local prefix.

They answered the way they answered everything — flat, professional, unbothered. "Dispatch."

Silence. Three seconds of it, long enough to be a message itself. Then a voice, calm and reasonable, the kind of voice you'd trust with your mother's medical decisions:

"Tell Hollis he should rest. Old men who dig through old papers forget how fragile memory is." A pause. "Yours too, Mr. Blackwood. Yours too."

Click.

Gideon sat in the dark on the empty road with the carbon copy over their heart and the town's whole sleeping weight around them, and understood, finally and completely, that the case was no longer something they were working.

It was something that was working them.

Tomorrow they'd need to see Vera Ashcombe — and the sealed letter she carried like a lit fuse. Because if the reasonable voice was willing to threaten a retired dispatcher over thirty-year-old paper, then whatever Edmund Ashcombe had put in that envelope was worth burning a town down for.

And somebody, Gideon thought, pulling back onto the road, was already holding the matches.
