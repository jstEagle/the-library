## 8. The Letter's Seal

The letter sat on Vera Ashcombe's kitchen table like a patient animal.

Gideon had seen it twice before — once when Vera first hired them, once a week later when she'd laid it out alongside the photographs of her grandfather's empty coffin as if the two objects might explain each other. It was a heavy cream envelope, yellowed at the edges now, sealed with wax that had gone the color of old honey. The Ashcombe crest pressed into it: three rivers meeting under an oak.

Tonight it was out again, and this time there was a butter knife beside it.

Gideon noticed the knife before they noticed anything else. That was the job, mostly — noticing the wrong object in a room. A knife on a table with no food. A car idling too long outside an apartment building. A page missing from a ledger where no page should be missing.

"You're going to open it," Gideon said.

Vera stood at her counter with her back to them, making tea she clearly wasn't going to drink. She was fifty-one and carried her grandfather's name like a coat two sizes too large — shoulders squared to hold it up, hands never quite finding the sleeves. "I'm going to *consider* opening it."

"With a butter knife."

"It was the only thing sharp enough." She turned. Her face had the particular exhaustion of someone who has been losing an argument with herself for days. "Gideon. It's been six weeks."

"Five and a half."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Count things at me like I'm one of your dispatch logs." She came to the table but didn't sit. She looked down at the letter the way people look at water they're about to enter. "My grandfather has been dead for thirty years. His coffin is full of rocks. Half the founding families of this town are lying to me in identical sentences, and you've told me so yourself. And I am sitting here with his handwriting on the front of an envelope — my name, Gideon. He wrote my name. *For Vera, when the truth of him is settled.* And every day that passes, somebody else gets to decide what's true before I do."

Gideon didn't answer right away. They'd learned that silence did more work than argument, most times. In dispatch, you learned to let a caller fill the quiet — panic always talked faster than patience.

Vera filled it. "He wanted me to have this. Me. Not the town, not the historical society, not whatever committee Harlan Voss's son chairs these days. If he left it for me, then it's mine, and I should—"

"No," Gideon said.

The word landed harder than Gideon intended. Vera's chin came up.

"Sit down," Gideon said.

"I don't take orders from my own investigator."

"Then stand. But listen." Gideon nodded at the chair across from them anyway, and after a moment Vera took it, which Gideon noted and filed away: she was angry, but she still trusted the process enough to sit inside it. That mattered. That was the whole ballgame, really.

"The letter says *when the truth of him is settled*," Gideon said. "Not when you're tired of waiting. Not when the town gets loud. When it's settled. Those are different things, Vera, and the difference is everything."

"He wrote that thirty years ago. He couldn't have known how long it would take."

"He knew exactly how long it would take." Gideon leaned forward. "Think about what we know. Your grandfather faked his death — or someone faked it for him — and the people who buried the story built a town on top of it. Schools named after him. A fountain. A founder's day. Every year this town tells itself a story about Edmund Ashcombe, and every year the story gets a little smoother, a little less true. Now imagine that letter opened next week, next month, before we know who killed Ruth Okafor, before we know who bought those river stones, before we know which of the living partners will burn evidence rather than read it." Gideon tapped the table once, beside the envelope, not touching it. "You open that letter early, and it stops being evidence. It becomes a rumor. A rumor with your name attached. And rumors can be buried, Vera. We've both seen this town bury things."

Vera's jaw worked. "And if it's just a letter? An apology? An old man's last words to a granddaughter he never met? What if I'm guarding a love letter like it's a confession?"

"Then you'll have lost nothing but time."

"And if it isn't?" Her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated it — Gideon could see her hating it, see her press her lips together until the crack sealed over. "If it isn't just a letter. If it's the thing that ends three families. Then whoever opens it first owns it. And I would rather own it than watch Daniel Voss's lawyers get it into a room first and come out calling it a forgery."

"That's exactly why we wait," Gideon said quietly. "Because you're right. Whoever opens it first owns it. So we make sure that when it's opened, it's opened in a way nobody can take from you. Documented. Witnessed. With the quarry letters, the ledger, the logs — all of it lined up behind it like cars at a light. You don't want to be holding a letter, Vera. You want to be holding a verdict."

The kettle went off in the kitchen. Neither of them moved toward it. It shrieked itself hoarse and clicked off, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Vera put her hand flat on the table, an inch from the envelope. Gideon watched her fingers. Steady, mostly. There was a tremor at the very tips that she would have denied.

"My mother used to say the Ashcombes kept their promises in wax," Vera said. "Sealed things. Deeds, wills, apologies. She said it like it was a virtue." She laughed, short and airless. "I used to think it meant we were honest. Now I think it means we were good at hiding things where other people could see them and not reach them."

"That's most families," Gideon said. "Most towns too."

Vera looked at them then — really looked, the way she did maybe once a visit, dropping the client posture and studying Gideon like a document she hadn't finished reading. "You've never told me why you do this. The cold cases, the gaps in ledgers. Dispatchers don't usually moonlight as gravediggers."

"I don't moonlight," Gideon said. "I work nights anyway. This fills the day hours."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Gideon agreed. "It isn't."

Vera waited. She was better at waiting than she let on; the impatience was real but partly performance, Gideon suspected, a way of moving things along that she'd learned in boardrooms or inheritances or wherever Ashcombes learned to push. Underneath it she could hold still a long time.

Gideon found themselves looking at the window instead of at her. The streetlight outside made its usual orange pool. Somewhere a block over, a cab idled at the corner of Delmore and Fifth, waiting on a fare that was running late — Gideon knew this without checking, the way they knew the town's pulse by sound alone.

"There was a case," Gideon said finally. "Before your grandfather's coffin. Before any of this."

Vera said nothing. Waiting.

"When I was nine, my mother went out for cigarettes and a bus ticket and didn't come back. That's the version the family tells. The actual version is longer and doesn't get cleaner with retelling. She left on a Tuesday. There was a man she'd been seeing. There was money missing from a jar on top of the fridge, or maybe there wasn't — my aunt swore there was, my uncle swore my aunt took it. Everybody remembered a different woman leaving that house that night, and none of them remembered the same one." Gideon turned their cup a quarter-turn on the table. "I grew up inside a gap. You know what that's like. You've got one too — a grandfather who was a statue in the park and a stranger everywhere else."

"Gideon—"

"The point isn't sympathy. The point is I learned early that an unanswered question doesn't stay quiet. It just changes pitch. For thirty years my mother's leaving got quieter and quieter until it was below hearing, and everybody in my family adjusted their lives around a frequency only I could still pick up." Gideon looked up. "So now, when I find a gap in a log — three nights cut out of a dispatch book, a driver erased from a roster — I can't leave it. Not because I'm noble. Because I already know what it sounds like when everyone agrees to stop asking. It sounds like my aunt's kitchen in 1994. I'd rather hear anything else."

Vera's eyes were wet, but she didn't wipe them, and Gideon respected that — she let the grief sit where it landed instead of managing it away. It was the first unmanaged thing Gideon had seen her do.

"So when I tell you to wait," Gideon said, "it's not procedure talking. It's the kid at that kitchen table who watched people open the question halfway, get half an answer, and build a whole life on it. My family decided my mother abandoned us. It was the settled truth. It was also probably wrong — there are details I've never been able to shake, and I've spent twenty years not shaking them, because shaking them means admitting the case was never closed, just abandoned." Gideon spread their hands. "Don't abandon yours halfway. Settle it. All the way down to the floor."

The letter sat between them. The wax caught the overhead light and held it, unbroken.

"If I wait," Vera said slowly, "and it turns out the truth is worse than the myth — that he wasn't a founder, he was a fugitive, or a coward, or complicit in something terrible —"

"Then you'll grieve a real man instead of a marble one," Gideon said. "It's harder. It holds better."

Vera exhaled through her nose, almost a laugh. "You talk like a dispatcher even when you're not working."

"I'm always working."

She reached out — and Gideon tensed — but she only turned the envelope ninety degrees, so the crest faced her, and slid it across the table to the drawer beneath the window. She wrapped it in a linen cloth first, the way you'd wrap something breakable, though paper doesn't break. Some things do, though. Gideon understood what the cloth was for.

"It goes in the bank box tomorrow," Vera said. "Not my house. Not your office, since apparently your office gets ransacked by history." A thin joke, but she made it, and Gideon took it, because alliances run on thin jokes the way engines run on thin oil.

"One condition," Gideon said.

"Of course there's a condition."

"When it's time — and it's coming soon, Vera, closer than either of us planned — you don't open it alone. Whatever's in there, you shouldn't meet it alone."

Vera paused with her hand on the drawer. Something moved across her face that wasn't quite gratitude and wasn't quite fear, some third thing that lived between them. "Was your mother ever found?" she asked. "Your case. The one that wasn't closed."

"No," Gideon said. "That's kind of the definition."

"And you still look."

"Every shift," Gideon said. "Every fare, every log line, every name that comes through the radio. You never know which call is going to be the one that answers a question you stopped saying out loud."

Vera closed the drawer. The click of it was small and final, a period at the end of tonight's sentence.

They gathered their coat and notes, and at the door Vera stopped them with a hand on the frame. "The retired dispatcher," she said. "Milo Brandt, the one who worked the night Ruth Okafor disappeared. You said you were going to see him."

"Tomorrow morning. He's in the county home off Route 9. Sharp years behind him, but the nurses say nights come back to him clearest."

"Be careful with him," Vera said. "And be careful what he remembers. If the cover-up ran through the dispatch office—" She didn't finish it. She didn't have to. They'd both arrived at the same ugly arithmetic weeks ago: whoever ordered those pages cut out of the log had needed someone inside the radio room to do the cutting, or at least to look away while it was done. Gideon's profession. Gideon's chair, thirty years before Gideon sat in it.

"I know," Gideon said. "That's why I'm going alone, early, before the visiting hours bring anybody else's ears."

Outside, the night had gone cold and mineral-smelling, the quarry's breath riding in on the wind the way it did when the pressure dropped. Gideon walked to their car and sat a moment before starting it, hands on the wheel, doing what they always did after the hard conversations — letting the town's noise settle back over them like water closing over a stone.

On the seat beside them, their notebook lay open to the current page. At the top, in their cramped hand, the running list:

*Stones — quarry load, 30 yrs, funeral director's account.*
*Logs — 3 nights excised. Who held the blade?*
*Ruth Okafor — ask Milo.*
*Letter — sealed. Vera holding. Time limit unknown.*

Gideon stared at that last line a long moment, then added one more beneath it, small, almost private:

*Mine — still open.*

They started the engine. Two blocks away, at the corner of Delmore and Fifth, the waiting cab's lights came on, and it pulled out slow, heading east — toward Route 9, toward the county home, toward the flat dark stretch where the quarry road met the highway.

Gideon frowned at the rearview mirror.

Nobody fares to the quarry road at ten o'clock at night. Nobody had, in thirty years — except once, and that fare had never come back.

Gideon picked up the radio.

"All units, be advised, requesting a plate check on the eastbound out of Delmore and Fifth."

Static. Then the answer came back, and the plate belonged to a car that had been reported stolen an hour ago — stolen, the desk sergeant added, from the Voss estate.

Gideon dropped the car into gear and followed the red taillights east, toward Route 9, toward an old man's memories and whatever was driving ahead of them into the dark.
