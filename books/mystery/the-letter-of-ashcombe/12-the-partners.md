## 12. The Partners

Three names kept surfacing in the paper trail like bodies in a flooded river — down one moment, up the next, always the same three.

Edmund Ashcombe. Harlan Voss. Cecily Marsh.

Gideon had been pulling threads for two weeks now, and every thread led back to the same knot. The quarry deed of 1961: Ashcombe, Voss, Marsh. The mill charter: Ashcombe, Voss, Marsh. The land trust that had turned a scrubby river valley into a town with a church steeple and a street named Founders' Row: all three signatures, inked in a hand so formal it looked rehearsed.

Gideon sat in the dispatch office at four in the morning, the radio muttering its small overnight dramas — a locked-out motorist on Route 9, a bachelorette party that had lost half itself somewhere near the reservoir — and spread photocopies across the desk like a card player laying out a losing hand.

The town's official history was short enough to fit on a plaque outside the library. *Founded through the vision and industry of three families.* Schoolchildren learned it. The plaque didn't mention what the vision had cost, or who had been paid to look away.

But paper remembers what plaques don't.

* * *

Gideon started with Harlan Voss, because Harlan Voss had left the biggest shadow.

Voss had been the money. That much was clear from the county records — he'd arrived in the valley in 1958 with capital from somewhere back east and an appetite that the locals described, in the careful language of old newspaper profiles, as "expansive." He bought land the way other men bought cigarettes. By 1961 he owned most of the north bank of the river, and by 1963 he'd leveraged it into the quarry, the gravel concern, and a seat on every board that mattered.

He'd also, according to a 1972 zoning dispute file Gideon had requested from the county three days earlier, been a man who did not like witnesses.

The dispute file was thin — a boundary disagreement with a farmer named Pell — but buried in it was a deposition transcript, and in the deposition, opposing counsel had asked Voss about a meeting at the quarry office in the fall of '71. A meeting Voss denied attending. The counsel had then produced a cab receipt.

A cab receipt. From Ashcombe Taxi.

Gideon read the line twice. The lawyer's name was redacted from the copy, but the question survived:

*"Mr. Voss, can you explain how your signature appears on a delivery acknowledgment for the quarry office dated October 14th, 1971, when you have testified you were in Boston that week?"*

Voss's answer wasn't recorded. The transcript simply moved on. But somebody had called a cab that night, and somebody had signed for something, and thirty years later the paper was still sitting there, patient as a stone, waiting for someone to pick it up.

October 14th, 1971.

Gideon wrote the date on a napkin and stared at it. It meant nothing yet. But dates were like fares — they went somewhere, even when the passenger claimed they hadn't moved.

* * *

Cecily Marsh was harder. Cecily Marsh had left no shadows at all, which was its own kind of signature.

She'd been the youngest of the three — twenty-six when the town charter was signed, already widowed once, already in possession of the riverfront parcel that made the whole founding deal possible. Without her land, there was no mill site. Without the mill site, no town. Every account agreed on that, and every account also agreed, in the same breathless way, that she had "generously" contributed her parcel to the partnership.

Generously. Gideon had learned to hear that word the way a mechanic hears a knock in an engine. Generous people got their names on plaques. People who were paid got their names left off.

Marsh had married twice more, run the family's shipping interests, endowed the library wing, and lived to be ninety-one in a house on Founders' Row that still had its original shutters. She was, Gideon realized with a small cold jolt, still alive. Ninety-six now, in the Cedar Ridge home outside town, sharp as ever if the gossip at the diner was to be believed.

The diner gossip was one of Gideon's better sources. Dottie Kellerman had been pouring coffee in Ashcombe for forty years and remembered everything except what she'd been paid to forget.

"Three families," Dottie said, refilling Gideon's cup unasked, the way she did when she was deciding whether to talk. "People act like it was some holy trinity. It was a business. A hard one."

"What kind of hard?"

Dottie glanced toward the counter, where the morning regulars were arguing about the high school bond measure. She lowered her voice anyway. "Harlan Voss was the kind of man who'd shake your hand and count your fingers after. Edmund was — well, everybody loved Edmund, but loving a man isn't the same as knowing him. And Cecily." Dottie set the pot down. "Cecily Marsh could end you with a thank-you note."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Dottie agreed. "It's not." She wiped the counter in slow circles. "You want to know about them, ask about the year before the charter. Ask why the mill opened eight months late. Everybody says it was the flood. There wasn't any flood."

She walked away before Gideon could follow up, which was Dottie's way — she gave you the shape of a door and let you find the handle yourself.

Eight months late. Gideon filed it next to October 14th, 1971, and felt the napkin getting crowded.

* * *

It was the late mill opening that cracked it open, in the end. Not the exhumation, not the erased logs, not even Ruth Okafor's name — though all of those had led here. It was a delay.

Because delays cost money, and money leaves records.

Gideon spent a full day in the county recorder's office with a pencil and a roll of quarters for the photocopier, walking backward through the partnership's finances. The story assembled itself the way a route assembles in a dispatcher's head — not all at once, but stop by stop, until suddenly you could see the whole line.

In the spring of 1959, the three partners had needed a fourth.

His name was Aldous Wren.

Gideon actually laughed when they saw it — a short, disbelieving sound in the empty records room that made the clerk look over. Wren. The alias from the rent receipts, the fishing license, the photograph of the thin man by the water. Edmund hadn't chosen the name at random. He'd chosen his own ghost. Or maybe — and this thought settled over Gideon slowly, like weather — maybe the name had never been an alias at all. Maybe it was a memorial. A penance worn on paper.

Wren had been a surveyor. That was the term the deeds used — *Aldous Wren, surveyor, of Marlow County* — but his role in the partnership documents was larger than the title suggested. He held a quarter interest in the pre-charter land syndicate. He'd located the mill site. He'd negotiated the water rights. Three signatures appear on the founding papers; a fourth appears everywhere before them, and then nowhere after.

Because in the autumn of 1959, Aldous Wren died.

There was no death certificate in the county files. No obituary in the *Ashcombe Courier*. No grave. What there was, buried in the syndicate's dissolution papers, was a buyout clause executed in December of 1959: Wren's quarter interest, purchased by the remaining partners for a sum that the document recorded without itemizing, witnessed by a notary whose stamp Gideon recognized from a hundred other filings — the same notary who had witnessed Edmund Ashcombe's will thirty years later.

The same hand touching both ends of the lie.

And between Wren's last documented appearance — a water-rights amendment filed October 9th, 1959 — and the buyout, there was a gap. Six weeks. Six weeks in which the mill site sat idle, in which the syndicate's correspondence went quiet, in which nothing at all happened in the public record.

Except one thing. Gideon found it because they'd learned to look for absences the way other people looked for entries. The Ashcombe Taxi Company had been incorporated in June of 1959 — five months before Wren disappeared — with Edmund Ashcombe listed as sole proprietor. A strange business for a founding partner to start on a whim. A stranger time to start it.

Unless you needed cars on the road at odd hours. Unless you needed drivers who asked no questions, running routes no one logged, at times no one checked.

Unless you needed someone to drive three partners up a quarry road on a night in October, and needed to control exactly who knew it.

Gideon sat back in the creaking chair and let the whole thing stand up in the dark of the records room, the way a building stands up when you finally see the blueprint.

Four partners. One meeting. One dead man with no grave. And a taxi company born five months early, like a weapon manufactured before the war.

Ruth Okafor hadn't just vanished the week Edmund died. Thirty years earlier, another driver had done the driving — and the driving was the crime. Ruth hadn't been killed for what she saw on the quarry road in 1990. She'd been killed for what she knew about a road that had been used before. The silence wasn't new. It was inherited. Maintained. A town-sized secret with a maintenance schedule.

Ashcombe wasn't built beside the buried night. Ashcombe was built *on* it. The mill, the charter, Founders' Row, the library wing with Cecily Marsh's name carved over the door — all of it stood on the assumption that Aldous Wren would stay dead and unasked-about, and that anyone who drove the road that night would stay equally quiet, equally gone.

Two drivers. Thirty years apart. Same road.

Gideon thought of Amara Okafor's box of her mother's things, and the letter from E.A. promising protection, and understood for the first time what the protection had been *from*. Not scandal. Not shame. From the same men — or their children, or their memory — who had made sure the first driver never came home.

* * *

The weight of it didn't hit all at once. It came in pieces through the rest of the day, the way bad news does.

It came when Gideon stopped for gas and saw the Voss name on the feed store awning, and the Marsh name on the pharmacy, and realized that half the commercial strip in Ashcombe was still paying rent, one way or another, into accounts seeded by a dead surveyor's stolen share.

It came when Gideon drove past the elementary school and watched kids pour out at three o'clock under a banner reading FOUNDER'S DAY PARADE — FRIDAY, and thought: they're celebrating it. Every year. They march the streets and wave at the names and none of them know the ground under the parade route is load-bearing lies.

It came hardest at dusk, on Founders' Row itself, when Gideon parked across from the Marsh house — still in the family, still shuttered most of the year — and saw the light on in the front parlor, and a shape moving behind the glass. Old Cecily was in town. Old Cecily was awake.

Gideon sat in the idling cab and did the arithmetic they'd been avoiding. If the buyout was the payoff, and the taxi company was the instrument, and Ruth was the price of the second silence — then the only person alive who had been in the room for all of it, the beginning and the middle and the long quiet after, was ninety-six years old and drinking tea forty feet away.

Everyone else was a descendant defending an inheritance. Cecily Marsh was the inheritance.

Tomorrow, Gideon told themselves. Tomorrow I knock on that door.

They were reaching for the ignition when the radio crackled — the dispatch line, the private channel, the one that rang only for them. Static, then a voice. Not the anonymous whisperer from the 3 a.m. calls. This voice was older, dry, unhurried, and it used Gideon's name like it was handling something fragile.

"Gideon Blackwood." A pause long enough to pour tea through. "Miss Marsh would like to see you. Tonight, not tomorrow. She says—" the voice paused again, and Gideon heard paper turn, "—she says you've found Mr. Wren, and she'd rather you hear the rest from her than from the stones."

Gideon looked up at the lit window. The shape behind the glass had stopped moving.

It was standing very still, facing the street. Facing the cab.

Waiting.
