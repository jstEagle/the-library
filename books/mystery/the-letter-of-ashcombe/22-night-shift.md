## 22. Night Shift

The radio came back to life the way it always did, mid-sentence, someone's voice half-swallowed by static, and Gideon Blackwood was already reaching for it before the thought formed. That was the thing about the chair. You could leave it for weeks, for funerals, for fire and quarry dust and the sound of a town tearing itself open and sewing itself shut again, and when you sat back down your hands remembered everything.

"Dispatch, go ahead."

"Hey, Gid. It's Marisol. Cab four-one-seven, I'm dropping at the hospital, and there's a guy on the bench outside the ER who's been there since my last shift. Same clothes. Same position. I don't think he's waiting for anybody."

"Copy. Leave him be. I'll make a call."

Gideon hung up the mic and looked out the window at Ashcombe at eleven p.m. on a Tuesday in late October. The town had changed the way a room changes after you move the furniture — same walls, same light, everything in a slightly wrong place that was actually the right place for the first time. The water tower still said ASHCOMBE, FOUNDED 1894, but somebody had hung a banner from the chain-link around the cemetery: RUTH OKAFOR MEMORIAL FUND — DONATIONS AT THE LIBRARY. The library had never had a banner before. The library had never had a fund before.

Weeks since the town meeting. Long enough for the shock to settle into something quieter and more durable. Gideon had watched it happen the way you watch weather move across the valley — the denial first, the furious letters to the *Ashcombe Ledger*, the people who stopped making eye contact at the diner. Then the other thing, slower, the ones who started showing up at the cemetery with flowers they didn't know how to arrange, standing in front of a granite marker that said RUTH OKAFOR, 1958–1994, BELOVED MOTHER, and beside it the smaller stone, the one Edmund had asked for in a letter written twenty years before his death: E.A. — HE DROVE THESE STREETS AND LOVED THEM.

Nobody had argued about the second stone. That was the strange part. The town that had spent three decades agreeing on a lie found it couldn't quite work up the energy to fight a truth that had already been read aloud, in a dead man's handwriting, by his own granddaughter, in a room where half of them had been sitting.

Gideon had carried it. That was the phrase Vera used, at the reburial, her hand on Gideon's sleeve. *You carried it for us.* Gideon hadn't corrected her. The truth was less poetic — Gideon had carried it because there was nowhere to put it down, because the dispatch desk and the night shift and the town's whole grid of streets and secrets ran through this one chair, and when you sat in the chair you sat in everything the chair knew.

The phone rang. The old landline, the one that still took calls from people who didn't trust cell towers, which in Ashcombe was a meaningful population.

"Blackwood dispatch."

A pause. Breathing. Then a voice Gideon didn't know — young, a man, careful in the way people are careful when they've rehearsed a sentence and are afraid it will fall apart on delivery.

"Are you — is this the person who found out about Edmund Ashcombe? The one who — people say you find things."

"Depends who's saying it."

"My name's Theo Brandt." A swallow. "My sister's missing. Lena Brandt. She's nineteen. And before you tell me to call the police — I did. They said she's an adult, she can leave if she wants, she took her phone, she probably went to the city. But she wouldn't. She wouldn't go without telling me." The rehearsed sentence collapsed. "She wouldn't."

Gideon pulled the log book closer. Old habit. "When did you last see her?"

"Sunday morning. She left for her shift at the diner and never clocked in."

"And the police said—"

"They said what they always say." A bitterness there, quick and earned. Theo Brandt had already learned what it was to be a certain kind of person in Ashcombe — the kind whose disappearances got filed and forgotten. Gideon knew that file. Had been in it once, in a manner of speaking, a missing parent and a town that shrugged.

"Where do you live, Theo?"

"Cedar Row. The trailers behind the old Voss mill."

The old Voss mill. Even the geography had been renamed, quietly, by common consent, the way a town amends its own memory when the truth gets too heavy to carry under the old labels. Harlan Voss's mill had been shuttered since the nineties; nobody had wanted to say the name anymore.

"Stay by the phone," Gideon said. "I'll come by before my shift ends. Don't touch her room. Don't clean up. And Theo — did she take anything? A bag, money, the car?"

"No car. She walked everywhere. And — yeah. She took the photo. Off the mantel."

"Which photo?"

"Of our mom. She never goes anywhere without it." A breath. "So she meant to be gone a long time. Or she didn't mean to go at all."

Gideon wrote that down, and under it, without deciding to, wrote the date, and under the date, thirty years ago to the week, another date, and felt the old vertigo — the sense that Ashcombe's calendar had a fold in it, that certain weeks repeated, that the town lost people in seasons.

"I'll be there in an hour," Gideon said. "And Theo — you called the right place."

* * *

The ER bench guy turned out to be Earl Pruitt, waiting on his brother-in-law's test results since four p.m., and Gideon bought him a coffee from the machine and sent him home to sleep, which was the whole job some nights — not finding people, just keeping track of them, keeping count, being the one who noticed that a man had been on a bench too long. Ashcombe had gone a long time without anyone noticing. That was the disease. The cure, whatever it was, was slower and more boring: a dispatcher who counted.

Cedar Row at midnight was a corridor of porch lights, most of them off, two of them burning stubborn against the cold. Theo Brandt opened the third trailer door before Gideon knocked — a lanky kid, nineteen or twenty, wearing his worry like a coat too big for him. Inside, the trailer was clean and poor and warm, and on the mantel there was a pale rectangle in the dust where a photo had stood for years.

"She took the one of Mom," Theo said again, as if Gideon might have missed it. "Not the one of Dad. Not the graduation one. Mom."

"Tell me about your mother."

"Left when I was six. Lena was four. We don't — nobody knows where she went." He said it flat, the way you say a thing you've said so many times it's worn smooth. "Lena used to ask about her. I used to tell her to stop asking."

Gideon looked at the pale rectangle. Looked at Theo. Felt the fold in the calendar open a little wider.

"What's your mother's name?"

"Dana. Dana Brandt." He hesitated. "Why does that matter?"

"It might not." Gideon sat down at the kitchen table, because this was how it went — you sat in kitchens, you drank bad coffee, you let people talk until the rehearsed sentences ran out and the real ones started. "Tell me about Lena's week. Everything. The boring parts especially."

And Theo told it. The diner shift, the tips, the fight with the manager. A man who'd come into the diner twice and sat in her section and left big tips and asked her questions — about her family, about Cedar Row, about whether her mother had ever lived in Ashcombe. Lena had laughed it off. Lena laughed everything off. But Sunday morning she'd taken the photo and gone.

"Did she say anything about the man? Describe him?"

"Older. Sixty, maybe. Nice coat. Lena said he had a way of talking like he was reading off a list." Theo's face worked. "Gideon, is this — people at the diner are saying it's like the Okafor thing. Like it's starting again."

Gideon had heard it too, around town, in the weeks since the reading. A nervous joke at first — *better check the quarry* — then less of a joke. Ashcombe had learned that it was a town where people disappeared, and knowledge like that doesn't leave quietly. It changes how a place holds its children.

"It's not starting again," Gideon said, and meant it, and heard in their own voice how much they needed it to be true. "Because we're not the same town. Somebody's counting now. Sit tight."

Outside, the cold had teeth. Gideon stood in the trailer lot and looked at the dark hump of the old mill against the stars, and let the case assemble itself the only way it ever assembled — out of small things, out of routes and times and the grammar of who-was-where. A man in a nice coat asking about Dana Brandt in a diner. A girl who took her mother's photograph and walked into the dark. A town with one fold in its calendar and a new crease forming over an old one.

Gideon got in the cab and, out of habit older than thought, keyed the radio.

"Dispatch to any unit. Anybody run a fare this week, male, sixties, well-dressed, asking about Cedar Row families? Especially the diner crowd."

Static. Then Marisol, cab four-one-seven, half-asleep: "Funny you ask. Picked a guy like that up Tuesday outside the library. Took him out Route 9. Toward the quarry road."

Gideon sat with that. The quarry road. Of course. Ashcombe kept its own geography of guilt, and every road led back to the same cut in the hills eventually — but this time, this time, the town knew what the quarry was. This time there were people who would come running with flashlights.

"Copy," Gideon said. "Log it. And Marisol — you did good."

* * *

The quarry letters lived in a fireproof box under Gideon's bed, and had lived there since the night the archive burned, and would live there, Gideon suspected, for the rest of their life — because some documents you don't surrender to institutions, however honest, however new. But there was a copy of everything else in the box too, and at 2 a.m., off shift, Gideon sat on the floor of the apartment with the Ruth Okafor file open and went looking for a name.

Dana Brandt. Born Dana—? Gideon ran the maiden name down through the old ledgers, the way you'd trace a fare through a night thirty years gone. Dana Okafor.

Gideon sat very still.

Dana Okafor, born 1968, daughter of Ruth Okafor. Half-sister to Amara. The child Ruth had left behind when she drove up the quarry road and never came back — no. Not the child Ruth left behind. The child Ruth's sister raised, alongside her own, the one Amara had mentioned once, in the flat voice she used for the worst facts: *There was a younger cousin. Dana. She left Ashcombe at eighteen and never came back, and I never blamed her.*

Dana Brandt was Ruth Okafor's daughter. Lena and Theo Brandt were Ruth's grandchildren. And a man in a nice coat had come to town asking questions about them, and the girl had taken her dead grandmother's photograph — no, her missing mother's photograph, but the blood ran the same road — and walked into the dark.

Gideon's hands were steady. That was the training of thirty years of night shift: the hands stay steady so the mind can work. But underneath the steadiness, something was moving, something that had been moving since the reburial, since Vera's hand on their sleeve, since the moment the town had remade itself around a truth and left Gideon standing in the middle of it with a place to stand for the first time.

They had asked Gideon once — Daniel Voss, from the witness stand at his trial, or maybe just across the quarry's edge, Gideon never could keep the two straight in memory — *why do you care? You're not one of them. You're nobody in this town.* And Gideon had answered then with anger. The answer was different now.

*I'm the one who carried it.*

That made you somebody. That made you the person a scared kid on Cedar Row called at midnight, the person the whole rewired town knew to call when someone went missing — because Gideon Blackwood was the one who had proved that in Ashcombe, even the buried come home, even the lost get their names back, even the empty coffin gets filled with truth.

The phone rang. 2:14 a.m. Gideon picked it up on the first ring, the way they always had, the way they always would.

"Blackwood dispatch."

A pause. Then Theo Brandt, breathless, the rehearsed sentences gone entirely, just a kid's raw voice down a bad line:

"She called me. Lena. She called from a number I don't know, and she was whispering, and she said — Gideon, she said, *the man knows about Grandma Ruth, he knows what's buried up there, and he says I have to come see it or he'll—* and then the line went dead." A crack in the voice. "Gideon, what's buried up there? What's at the quarry?"

Gideon was already standing, already reaching for the coat, already keying the radio with the other hand, and the old familiar current was running through them — fear, yes, but under the fear the other thing, the thing that had carried them through every chapter of the town's long night: the knowledge that in Ashcombe, the ground gives up what it holds, eventually, if someone is stubborn enough to keep asking.

"Stay on the line, Theo," Gideon said, stepping out into the cold, into the dark, into the town that was theirs now not by blood or myth but by truth, carried and carrying. "And get everybody awake. I'm going up the quarry road."
