## 15. Reyes Found

The call came in at 4:47 in the morning, which is the hour the city keeps for its worst news. I know this because I've spent eleven years on the night shift, and I can tell you that nothing good has ever been said to me before five. Weddings, births, lottery wins — those all wait for daylight. The dark hours belong to drownings, disappearances, and men calling to say they won't be home.

So when Odile's hand came down over mine on the dispatch board — not a tap, a full press, like she was pinning something down before it could fly off — I already knew.

"Harbor patrol," she said. "Fishermen. Off the channel buoy at Marker Nine." A pause, the kind Odile never takes unless the words need to be handled with gloves. "They pulled someone out alive."

I didn't ask who. Asking would have made it real, and if it was real, then it was either Reyes or it wasn't, and both of those answers had teeth.

"Tomas Reyes," Odile said anyway, because she's known me long enough to know that mercy delayed is just cruelty wearing a polite coat. "Alive. Hypothermic, half-drowned, but talking. They're taking him to St. Ambrose."

I was out of my chair before she finished the sentence. Bram looked up from under the hood of cab fourteen, wrench frozen mid-turn, and Sef — Sef was already reaching for his keys, because that's what Sef does, he drives toward trouble the way other men drive away from it.

"I'll take you," he said.

"No." The word came out harder than I meant it. "Nobody drives me anywhere tonight. Nobody's car, nobody's route, nobody's anything. If Corvel's people are watching the depot — and we have to assume they are, because they've been watching everything else — I don't want them seeing who leaves with me or in what."

Sef's face did something complicated. Relief and hurt fighting for the same square inch of skin. "Quentin—"

"You've done enough driving at night for one lifetime," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it landed like an accusation and he flinched like one. But I didn't take it back. Some things you say and some things you mean, and lately the two lists had stopped matching up.

* * *

St. Ambrose Hospital at five in the morning smells of floor wax and old coffee and the particular institutional despair of vending machines that only work if you believe in them. I signed in as family, because the desk clerk didn't look closely and because, in every way that mattered since Tuesday night, Tomas Reyes was family — the kind you acquire through shared catastrophe rather than blood.

He was in a room with a window facing the parking lot, wrapped in blankets, IV line snaking into his arm, lips still blue at the edges. His eyes found me before the nurse finished closing the door.

"You're the dispatcher," he rasped. "The one from the letters."

"The one from the taxi company," I corrected. "The letters were never mine."

"They were always yours." He tried to sit up, thought better of it, sank back. "That's the thing I figured out, lying on that buoy waiting for the tide to decide about me. Everything — the letters, the warnings, all of it — it was addressed to you. Not to me. I was just the envelope."

I pulled the plastic chair close. Up close he smelled of salt and antiseptic and something underneath both, faint but unmistakable: engine oil. The same smell he'd told me about at the boarding house, the smell of the man who'd been following him. Except now I knew the oil wasn't a stranger's cologne. It was the depot's own scent, worn by every mechanic and driver who'd ever leaned into an engine bay on Bellwether Lane. It was *our* smell. Somebody from my world had been hunting him.

"Tell me what happened," I said. "From the night you booked the fare."

He talked for forty minutes. I let him talk. Interrupting a drowning victim is bad manners and worse investigation.

The night his fare was booked — the night named in Ada Voss's first letter — he'd closed early at the cannery and walked home along the waterfront, the way he always did. A car had pulled alongside. Not a Bellwether cab; a gray sedan with mud on the plates. Two men inside, and a third driving. The driver had smelled of engine oil and salt, and when Reyes got a look at him under the streetlight, the face had been ordinary, forgettable, the face of a man who does a job and goes home. That was the frightening part, Reyes said. Not menace. Routine.

They hadn't hurt him. Not at first. They'd taken him to a house out past the shipyard — he didn't know where, windows painted over, no landmarks, just the sound of cranes and the smell of creosote — and they'd kept him there for three days. Fed him. Asked him questions.

"What questions?"

"The same ones, over and over." His hands worked at the blanket edge, pleating it, unpleating it. "Who hired the taxis. Who paid you. Did anyone come asking about you before the letter came. Whether you'd ever heard the name Halden."

My spine went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the hospital's air conditioning.

"And what did you tell them?"

"That I book fares through Bellwether because it's cheap and the night dispatcher doesn't cheat you on the meter." He almost smiled; it cost him something. "Which is true. I never met any woman named Voss. I never saw a letter. Until you showed up at my door, I didn't know anybody thought I was going to die."

"But they thought you knew something."

"They thought I was bait." He said it flatly, without self-pity, the way working men say things that should break them. "One of them — the talker, the one with the soft voice — he said it like it was funny. Said the letters needed a body to point at, and I was the closest body to the water."

The room hummed. Somewhere down the hall a cart rattled, and a nurse laughed at something, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary while I sat there rearranging everything I thought I knew.

Because here's what I'd been carrying for days, heavy as ballast: that the letters predicted a murder, that the murder was real, that somewhere ahead of us a killer was counting down. I'd been running a race against a date. And dates, in my experience, are honest — they arrive whether you're ready or not.

But Reyes hadn't died. Reyes had been *kept*. Held past the letter's date, held past the second letter's correction, held until tonight, when somebody finally dumped him in the channel near a buoy — dumped him alive, or left him alive enough that the cold had to finish the job. Either way, the timing was wrong for murder and right for disposal. You don't predict a killing three days out and then store the victim in a safehouse like dry goods.

Unless the prediction was never the point.

"They weren't trying to kill you," I said slowly, testing each word like ice on a dock plank. "They were using you to find whoever wrote the letters."

"And whoever wrote the letters," Reyes said, "was using me to get your attention."

* * *

I called Ada from the payphone in the lobby, because I don't trust cell phones and I especially don't trust them now. She answered on the second ring, awake — of course she was awake; she'd been awake, I suspected, since 1987.

"He's alive," I said. "Reyes. They fished him off Marker Nine."

A sound came down the line that I couldn't classify for a moment — not a sob, not a laugh, something between, the noise a person makes when grief and relief try to pass through the same doorway at once. Then: "They held him. All this time."

"Three days. Feeding him questions about who hired the taxis and whether he'd heard the name Halden." I watched the parking lot through the glass doors, cataloguing cars on principle. "Ada. Think about it. Corvel's men grabbed the man named in your letter within hours of it arriving. How did they even know to look? The letter came to you. Anonymous. Unmailed — you told me yourself it was left at your door."

Silence. Long enough that I checked the receiver for a dial tone.

"Someone read it before I did," she said finally. "Or someone wrote it knowing Corvel would hear about it."

"Or," I said, and here was the thought I'd been circling since the buoy, the thought that turned my stomach and lit my brain at the same time, "the letter was written *for* Corvel. Written so it would reach him. Every letter, Ada. The first one names a victim — Corvel grabs the victim, shakes him, finds nothing. The second one corrects itself — keeps everyone looking forward, keeps the panic fresh. The third one comes to *my* door. The fourth one sets a time and place — the pilot house, high tide. Each one tighter than the last, each one drawing a circle smaller and smaller around Bellwether Lane."

"You think the writer is herding him."

"I think the writer is herding *everyone*." I pressed my forehead against the cool glass of the phone booth. "Corvel's being lured to the pilot house. I'm being lured. Marius — if Marius is getting letters too, he's being lured. Somebody wants every secret on Bellwether Lane standing in the same place at the same moment, at the highest tide in eleven years."

"Why?" And then she answered her own question, because Ada Voss had spent a week inside this mystery and had earned her conclusions the hard way. "Because a confession no one reads is just paper. Wim — if it's Wim — he's mailed his confession for years and nothing happened. The police buried it. The papers wouldn't print it. So now he's going to make the truth walk out onto a stage in front of witnesses, at the one moment the tide gives back what it took."

"And Corvel will come to destroy it," I said. "And whatever happens when he arrives — that's the fifth act. That's the murder the letters have been promising all along. Not Reyes. Never Reyes." I laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "The letter dated three days out was right. There *is* a death coming. We just had the victim wrong from the start."

Ada was quiet a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — steadier, colder, the voice of Lira Halden's sister rather than the nervous woman who'd paid triple rate in the rain.

"Then we make sure the stage is crowded," she said. "With people Corvel can't buy, can't scare, and can't make disappear. Fishermen pull a living man off a buoy, Quentin. Do you know what that means at the harbor? The crews are already talking. Half of them remember Lira. Half of them lost money or licenses to Corvel over the years and kept their mouths shut because the mouth-shutting was survivable. A drowned witness is a tragedy. A *rescued* one is a problem."

I looked at my watch. 6:12 a.m. The storm front was still a day out, but the barometer had started its slow fall, and the tide tables pinned above my desk at the depot — the same charts that had hung over Reyes's bed, circled in pencil — put the year's highest water tomorrow night. High tide at 11:40 p.m., within minutes of the time named in the fourth letter.

"Get some sleep," I told her. "Then start making calls. Every fisherman, every dockhand, every widow and whistle-blower you can find. Tomorrow night, Bellwether Lane needs an audience."

"And you?"

"I'm going back to the depot," I said. "There's a ledger of quiet fares with our name on it, and a dying man who's been writing confessions on our carbon paper, and a brother of mine who's apparently receiving mail." I hung up before she could hear whatever my voice did next.

Outside, the sky was doing that gray-pink thing it does before dawn, the color of a bruise deciding whether to become a scar. Somewhere out past the breakwater, Marker Nine bobbed on the swells, holding its position the way buoys do — anchored to something on the bottom that nobody sees.

Tomorrow night the tide would come up higher than it had in eleven years. Tomorrow night, everything the channel had swallowed would be within reach of the light.

And somewhere on Bellwether Lane, in a pilot house with salt-eaten windows, an old man with an oxygen tank was folding his last letter, addressing it to me, and waiting to see who arrived first: the family I'd built, the captain who'd bought them, or the truth.

I flagged a cab — a rival company's, yellow, meter honest, driver incurious — and gave him the lane.

Time to go count my people.
