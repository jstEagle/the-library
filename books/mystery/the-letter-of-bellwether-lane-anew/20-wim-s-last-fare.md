## 20. Wim's Last Fare

The oxygen tank ran out at 4:17 in the morning, and Wim Vandersloot, founder of Bellwether Taxi and the best liar I ever worked for, refused the spare.

"Save it," he said, waving off Bram with a hand that trembled like a leaf deciding whether to let go of the branch. "I've been breathing other people's air all night. It's rude to keep taking."

He was propped up on the cot in Odile's back office, where we'd carried him after the pilot house, after the storm, after Corvel went into the harbor police van with his cuffs too loose because Sef had put them on and Sef had never once in his life done anything at full strength. The ledger of quiet fares sat on the desk between us, water-spotted, its carbon copies fanned out like the wings of something that had finally stopped trying to fly.

Wim looked small. That was the thing that kept catching me wrong. For eleven years — for as long as I'd known him, which was longer than that, since I was nineteen and he'd hired me off a night shift at a fish cannery because I could read a tide chart and didn't flinch at drunks — Wim had been the fixed point. The man who'd built the depot out of a failed chandlery, who knew every driver's mother's name, who kept the coffee terrible on purpose so nobody lingered long enough to fall asleep. He was not supposed to be a shrunken old man in a cardigan gone gray at the elbows, his lungs full of the sea he'd spent his whole life driving alongside.

"You should be in a hospital," I said.

"I should have been in a hospital eleven years ago," he said. "Instead I wrote letters." He smiled, and it was the same smile he'd used when he taught me to run the board: patient, a little sly, like the truth was a fare he was going to get to eventually whether I liked the route or not. "You got them all, didn't you. Every one."

"All four."

"And you came anyway. Even knowing what they were." He turned his head toward the window, where the sky over the channel had gone the color of a bruise healing. "I wasn't sure you would. I made them ugly on purpose. A confession nobody wants to read is just litter. But a warning — people come for warnings. People come to stop things." His breath caught, held, released. "I counted on your stubbornness, Quentin. It's the only reliable thing in this whole city."

"Don't flatter me. You're dying."

"Everyone's dying. I'm just better scheduled than most." He coughed, wet and long, and when it passed he gripped my wrist with surprising strength. "Listen. I don't want the hospital. I want a ride."

"A ride."

"One last fare. Bellwether cab, meter running, proper dispatch procedure. You drive me past Bellwether Lane. Slowly. And then—" he shrugged, a small motion, an entire surrender folded into it, "—then wherever the road goes after that."

Odile made a sound behind me, half protest, half prayer. Bram stood in the doorway filling it entirely, his big mechanic's hands hanging useless at his sides, hands that had rebuilt half the fleet and couldn't rebuild this.

"Wim," Odile said, "the doctor said—"

"The doctor said months. The doctor said rest. The doctor said a great many things to a man who has spent ninety-one years not listening." He looked at her, and his voice gentled into the register he used for breaking bad news to drivers' widows. "Odile. My girl. I drove Lira Halden's killer home from the docks the night she died. I sat in this seat—" he patted the cot, or maybe the office itself, the whole depot, "—for eleven years knowing it, and I said nothing, and I mailed four letters instead of walking into a police station because I was a coward with good penmanship. Whatever time I have left isn't owed to doctors. It's owed to a street."

Nobody argued after that. There was nothing left to argue with.

* * *

Sef brought the car around at 5:02. Not just any car — Cab 7, the oldest in the fleet, the one Wim had driven himself until his hands started shaking on the wheel two winters ago. Sef had washed it. In the dark, before dawn, with the storm still dripping off everything, he'd washed the car and wiped down the seats and put one of those cardboard pine trees on the mirror, the kind Wim always called "an insult to both trees and pine."

"You take that out," Wim said, lowering himself into the back seat with Odile's help and a sound like a ship settling. "If this is my last ride, it will smell like engine oil and honest vinyl."

"Yes sir," Sef said, and his voice cracked on *sir*, and Wim reached forward and squeezed the back of his neck the way he used to when Sef was a kid running fares for pocket money, and neither of them said anything else, because some accounts between men are settled without receipts.

Ada waited by the curb with a blanket, which she tucked around Wim's knees with the brisk tenderness of someone who had buried a sister and a mother and had decided she was done being helpless about death. She'd stayed through the whole night at the depot, and she'd stay through the morning, and then she'd stay through the year — but none of us knew that yet. All we knew was that her hands were steady and her eyes weren't.

"High tide's at 6:41," she told me quietly. "Bellwether Lane will be under about a foot of water at the low end. The pilot house end."

"Then we'll see it the way it wants to be seen," Wim said from inside the cab. "Dispatch, are we going to stand around billing the customer for conversation?"

I got in. I took the board mic first, because procedure mattered to Wim more than God, and I keyed it and heard my own voice come out steady, which surprised me:

"Bellwether dispatch confirming fare pickup, 5:14 a.m., Cab 7. One passenger. Destination: Bellwether Lane, via the driver's discretion." I paused. "No return trip scheduled."

Static, then Odile's voice, rough: "Confirmed. Drive safe, Quentin."

"Copy," I said, and hung up the mic, and pulled away from the depot with the meter ticking its small faithful heartbeat.

* * *

The city at dawn after a storm is a city that's been crying all night and can't remember why. Streets washed pale. Gulls arguing over something in the gutters. The harbor police tape still fluttering around Corvel's office door like the world's saddest parade decoration. We drove through it all slow, because Wim asked for slow, and because the meter was running and there was no one left in the world I wanted to cheat less.

He talked. Of course he talked. Ninety-one years of talking, saved up.

"I bought the chandlery in '61," he said, watching the waterfront slide by. "Everyone said the taxis were a fool's business — who needs a cab at the docks? Everyone, it turns out. Dockworkers coming off shift. Widows going to the cemetery. Sailors who'd lost their ships and needed to be somewhere else fast. You know what a taxi is, Quentin?"

"A car you pay for."

"It's a promise," he said. "It's a machine that says: whatever happened to you tonight, someone will drive you through it. Eleven years ago a man got in my cab soaked to the bone and shaking, and I drove him where he told me to go, and I took his money, and I didn't ask. That's the promise, see. No questions. And I built my whole life on it, and it kept me fed and it kept this depot alive and it kept thirty families eating through three recessions." His breath whistled. "And it kept me quiet. The promise cuts both ways. Nobody tells you that."

"Marius signed the report," I said. "Corvel leaned on him. But you — you knew before any of it. You drove the man, you saw the boat, you found the weight belt in the trunk weeks later when you detailed the car."

"I found it and I sat in the garage holding it for four hours," Wim said simply. "Bram found me there. Never asked. That's the family for you — we don't ask, and look what it cost." He closed his eyes. "When I got sick in the spring, I did the arithmetic. Corvel would die comfortable. Marius would rot slowly in his own silence. Lira Halden would stay a drowned girl forever, and drowning girls don't get to be anything else unless somebody makes noise. So I made noise. Four letters. Took me six weeks to write the first one — my hand wouldn't hold steady, and Ada's mother's note kept smearing the ink where I cried on it."

I pulled onto Harbor Road. The channel opened up on our right, gray-green and glittering, deceptively calm now, the storm's tantrum spent. Somewhere under that glitter lay the weighted evidence the divers had raised at dawn — the belt, the launch parts, the truth, all of it bagged and tagged and photographed while Corvel watched from a distance he'd chosen himself.

"Quentin." Wim's voice had gone quieter. "Pull over up ahead. Before the lane."

I pulled over. The meter ticked. 6:12 a.m.

Bellwether Lane ran down ahead of us toward the water, its low end already shining with the tide coming over it, thin silver sheets of it sliding across the cobbles, retreating, sliding again. At the far end, the old pilot house stood black against the brightening east, its windows reflecting a sky going from bruise to gold. Eleven years ago a man had walked down that lane carrying a secret. Last night an old man had sat in it waiting to give the secret up. The tide didn't care. The tide came in either way, patient as arithmetic.

Wim looked at it for a long time.

"There she is," he said softly. "Forty years I drove past this lane. Picked up fares at the top of it a thousand times. Never once went down to the pilot house until last week." He laughed, a dry rustle. "Isn't that something. You can know every street in a city and miss the one that matters."

"You didn't miss it," I said. "You were circling it."

"Circling. Yes. Like a gull over a fish that's too big to swallow." He fumbled at his coat pocket and produced, of all things, a fare card — the old paper ones, pre-printed, from the seventies. He'd written on it in his careful draftsman's hand. He handed it back to me between the seats.

It said: **One (1) passenger. Bellwether Lane. Paid in full.**

"When?" I managed.

"Just now. While you were driving." He settled back against the seat, looking pleased with himself, the old fox. "A fare paid in advance. That's how you started this case, isn't it? Some nervous woman, triple rate. Well. Consider us even, dispatcher. I paid triple too. I paid eleven years of interest."

The meter was still running. 6:31. I reached down and — I don't know why I did it this way, except that it was the only way I knew — I let it run. I didn't clear it. I didn't turn it off. I let the little numbers climb while the tide climbed the lane, and I thought: *this is the job. This is the whole job. Somebody gets in the car broken and you drive them through it and the meter runs because the debt is real, and at the end you decide what to do with the receipt.*

"Wim," I said. "The ride's free."

He opened one eye. "Don't you dare void my fare. I'll haunt this depot."

"Too late. Dispatch's discretion." I turned in my seat so I could see him properly. Old man, blanket, oxygen tank he'd refused, dawn coming up gold behind the pilot house like a stage light finding its actor. "Case number whatever-it-was. Closed. Passenger delivered. No charge. That's not charity, Wim. That's the company policy I'm instituting as of this morning: nobody pays to go home."

For a moment he just breathed — shallow, rattling, enough. Then he smiled, and it was the smile from my first week, the one that meant *you'll do, kid, you'll do.*

"Well," he said. "Then drive me the rest of the way. Slowly. I want to hear the water on the cobbles."

I drove. Down the lane, into the shallows, the tires parting the silver water with a sound like applause, very soft, very far away. The pilot house grew in the windshield. Wim's breathing slowed beside me, and slowed, and I didn't look, because he'd asked for slowly and I am, whatever else they say about me, a professional.

At 6:44, three minutes past high tide, at the door of the pilot house with the water lapping the step, the meter read 4.60.

I cleared it to zero.

* * *

They buried him on Thursday. The whole depot went — Odile in her good coat, Bram scrubbed pink and uncomfortable, Sef holding the flag they gave us because Wim had served somewhere he never talked about, Ada standing at the edge of the grave plot like she was still deciding whether she belonged there. She did. She'd figured that out faster than the rest of us.

Marius came. Stood at the back, hat in his hands, not speaking to anyone, and I let him, because grief has its own dispatch protocol and I wasn't authorized to override it.

The state investigators came too, plainclothes, taking notes on the Halden reopening. One of them, a tired woman named Okonkwo, stood next to me at the graveside and said, "Your statement was thorough, Ms. Joubert. Unusual, in this town."

"I close cases," I said.

"So I hear." She handed me a card. "We'll have questions. About the ledgers. About the quiet fares. There are more names in that book than Corvel's."

"I know," I said. "I've read it twice."

She nodded and walked away, and I stood there while they lowered the box, and I thought about a promise that cuts both ways, and a machine that says *someone will drive you through it*, and a fare card in my breast pocket that said PAID IN FULL.

That night, back at the depot, I pinned the card above the dispatch board, next to the tide chart. Odile saw it and didn't ask. Bram saw it and didn't ask. Sef saw it and polished it with his sleeve.

At 11:58 p.m., the phone rang. I picked it up on the second ring, because that's the rule.

"Bellwether Taxi," I said. "How can we help you?"

There was a pause on the line — rain-sound, harbor-sound, the long breath of a city getting ready to lie to itself again. Then a voice I didn't know, young, scared, doing a very bad job of sounding casual:

"Yeah. Hi. I need a cab. And — look, this is going to sound crazy, but I think someone's following me, and I think — I think it hasn't happened yet. What I mean to say is—"

I reached for a pen, and smiled, and said the only thing I've ever known to say:

"Bellwether takes all fares. Where do you need to go?"
