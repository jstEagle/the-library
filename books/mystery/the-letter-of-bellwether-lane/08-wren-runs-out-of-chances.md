## 8. Wren Runs Out of Chances

The letter came on a Tuesday, which Wren would later decide was the whole problem. Tuesdays were restocking days, and restocking days were the only hours the shop ever felt like theirs—no customers, no Ines hovering over the register with that look she got when the numbers didn't come out right, just Wren alone in the stacks with the smell of old paper and lemon polish and the particular hush of a building that seemed, on certain mornings, to be listening.

The letter was on the doormat when Wren unlocked the door at seven. Cream envelope, heavy paper, the kind of stationery nobody in Marrow's Reach used except the school district and the bank, and Wren knew before touching it which one it was. The return address was printed in that institutional blue: *Marrow's Reach Unified School District — Office of Student Services.*

They stood in the doorway a long time, holding it, the fog pressing cold against the back of their neck, the letter warm as a coal in their hand.

*Office of Student Services.* Nobody in the history of the world had ever received anything good from an Office of Student Services. It was a phrase built out of other, worse phrases—*compulsory attendance*, *intervention protocol*, *custodial placement*—and Wren could feel all of them stacked inside the envelope like stones in a coffin.

They thought about not opening it. They thought about the little brass ashtray by the register that nobody used anymore, and how paper burns fast, and how the fog would eat the smoke. They thought about all of it for exactly as long as it took the radiator in the back to knock twice, the way it did when the pipes were waking up, and then they slit the envelope open with the letter opener shaped like a sword that some dead customer had left as a tip years ago, and they read.

*Dear Parent or Guardian of Wren Vasquez:*

*This notice is to inform you that your child has accumulated eleven (11) unexcused absences this semester, exceeding the district threshold of ten (10). Pursuant to district policy 4.7, a truancy hearing has been scheduled for Friday, the 14th, at 9:00 a.m. Attendance by a parent or legal guardian is required. Failure to appear may result in referral to Child Protective Services for evaluation of custodial placement.*

*Evaluation of custodial placement.*

Wren read that phrase four times. It didn't get softer. It didn't get smaller. It sat there on the page in its institutional blue ink like a hand reaching out of the paper to take them by the collar.

Their mother's address was listed below in the *cc:* line. An address in Bellhaven, forty miles down the coast, which meant either she'd moved again or the district hadn't caught up to the fact that she'd moved again. Wren hadn't seen her since March. Before that, since Christmas. Before that, since—well. The pattern had a shape, and Wren had stopped pretending, somewhere around the age of thirteen, that the shape was going to change. She always meant to come back. She always wrote it in her cards, in her voice mails, in the way she said *soon, baby, soon* like the word was a promise instead of a weather forecast. And then a man, or a job, or a mood would roll in off the water, and *soon* would dissolve into the fog like everything else in this town.

Wren folded the letter along its original creases, very precisely, and put it in their back pocket, and went to restock shelf nine.

That was the thing about work. Work didn't ask where you lived. Work just asked you to alphabetize.

* * *

They got through the morning on muscle memory. Biography, low shelves. Poetry, the case that leaned. Local history—shelf nine, the one with the ledger hidden behind the county atlases, though Wren didn't touch that anymore without Ines knowing; some things you learned to leave alone, like a stove burner you'd once put your hand on. By eleven the fog had burned off to a thin gray gauze, and the bell over the door had rung twice, and Wren had sold a paperback thriller to Mr. Okafor from the hardware store and a book of tide tables to a woman who bought one every month and never seemed to read them.

Ines came up from the basement at noon with dust on her sleeves and that expression she wore when she'd been down among the boxes too long—blinked at, like she'd been arguing with someone only she could see.

"Okafor bought the one with the serial killer on the train," Wren said.

"He always does. He says it's research." Ines set a box of new arrivals on the counter and began slitting tape with her thumbnail. "He's never on a train."

"Maybe that's the research."

Something that was almost a smile moved across her face and settled. Then her eyes came up, sharp and gray and far too accurate, and Wren felt the letter in their back pocket go hot as a brand.

"You're doing the thing," Ines said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you alphabetize a shelf that's already alphabetized." She nodded at Wren's hands, which were indeed straightening the poetry case for the third time, squaring spines that were already square. "I invented that thing. You're doing it badly."

"I'm being thorough."

"You're being thirteen kinds of nervous, and you've been that way since you opened the door." She slit another length of tape, unhurried. "I'm not going to ask. That's not a promise I can always keep, but it's a policy I believe in. People tell you things when the telling is their idea." She slid a stack of hardcovers onto the counter. "These go in the window. If you want to talk about whatever's in your pocket, the kettle's under the sink and I'm here until six."

Wren's whole body went still. "How did you—"

"You stood in the doorway for four minutes reading it. I could see you from the basement stairs. You read it like it was a summons." Ines didn't look up from the box. "I've gotten those. You read them the same way."

And that was all. She carried her tea into the back room, and Wren stood there among the new hardcovers with their heart going like a trapped bird, understanding that they had just been offered a door and left entirely alone to decide whether to walk through it.

They didn't walk through it. They put the hardcovers in the window, spine-out, the way she liked, and they hated themselves a little for the cowardice and were grateful a little for the mercy, and both feelings fit inside them at the same time the way two people fit in a coat, awkwardly, keeping each other warm.

* * *

The truancy officer's name was Deputy Halloway, and she came on Thursday.

Wren saw her through the window first—a county uniform, a clipboard, the walk of a person who delivered bad news for a living and had made her peace with it. By the time the bell rang, Wren had retreated behind the register with the tactical instinct of a small animal that knows the shape of hawks.

"Afternoon." Halloway's badge said *M. HALLOWAY*. Her voice was flat and kind at once, the way voices get when kindness has been worn down to its load-bearing parts. "I'm looking for Wren Vasquez."

"That's me." Wren kept their voice level. Somewhere in the back, the floorboard creaked—Ines, in the stacks, listening without coming out. Wren couldn't decide if that made it better or worse.

"Eleven unexcused absences. You got the notice?" Wren nodded. "Hearing's tomorrow, nine a.m., district office. Your mother's been notified by mail and phone. Phone's disconnected. Mail may or may not find her." Halloway said it without cruelty, which somehow made it crueler. "Here's what happens tomorrow, so there's no surprises. If a guardian shows up, we talk about a plan—makeup work, attendance contract, the usual. If nobody shows up, the district refers you to CPS, and CPS looks at placement. Group home, most likely, until they locate her. You follow me?"

"I follow you."

"You got questions?"

Wren had about four hundred questions, and every one of them was some version of *why does the system always show up right when you've finally built something that holds*, but what they said was, "Is there an option where I just keep working here?"

Halloway's eyes flicked around the shop—the dust motes, the leaning poetry case, the window display arranged spine-out with a care that had nothing to do with retail. Something moved behind her flat kindness, brief as a fish under ice.

"You'd need a guardian to sign off on a work permit amendment," she said. "And a guardian's the thing you don't have." She clicked her pen closed. "Nine a.m. Tomorrow. Come alone if you have to. Coming alone's better than not coming." She put a card on the counter—her direct line, handwritten on the back—and left, the bell chiming behind her like a small, stupid celebration.

The floorboard creaked again, closer. Ines appeared in the aisle with a book in her hand she clearly hadn't been reading.

"I wasn't eavesdropping," she said.

"You were absolutely eavesdropping."

"I was dusting in the vicinity of a conversation." She set the book down on the counter—*Great Expectations*, of all things, the old battered copy from the ten-cent shelf—and looked at Wren for a long moment. "Tomorrow. Nine o'clock."

"I can handle it."

"I know you can handle it. Handling things is the whole problem with you." She said it dry, but her eyes weren't dry at all. "I'm coming with you."

"You don't have to—"

"I'm coming with you," Ines said again, in the voice she used for closing the shop, which was not a voice that entertained discussion. "Wear something with a collar. The district respects collars. It's the only thing they respect."

* * *

But the mother came first.

She called the shop at six-forty that evening, when the light was going copper in the window and Wren was counting the register. Wren knew it was her from the first syllable—the way she said *hey, baby* like no time had passed, like the phone line between them wasn't a hundred miles of silence with a few voice mails scattered along it like buoys.

"Hey, Mama."

"You got a letter. The school called my—there was a message, at Denise's, I just got it." Background noise: a television, a man's laugh, a life Wren wasn't part of. "Baby, I can't get there tomorrow. I got the shift, and gas is— you know how it is. You know I'd come."

Wren looked at the register. Four hundred and twelve dollars and change. They looked at the window, where the fog was coming back in off the water, folding itself down the lane like something coming home. They thought about saying *you always would*. They thought about saying it mean, and they thought about saying it soft, and they discovered, with a strange adult tiredness, that they didn't want to say either. Both versions were just ways of handing her the knife.

"I know," they said.

"There's a woman there, right? The bookshop lady. Ines." His mother's voice went careful, the way it did when she was about to ask for something. "She seems—solid. Steady. Maybe she could go with you. Maybe she could sign something. People like that, they've got standing, you know? They've got standing."

*People like that.* Wren closed their eyes. Their mother was forty miles away asking a stranger to be the parent she was too tired to be, and the worst part, the part Wren would carry for a long time, was that it wasn't even a bad idea.

"I'll ask her," Wren said.

"Thank you, baby. You're my good thing. You know that, right? You're the one good thing I ever—"

"I gotta finish the register, Mama."

A silence on the line, hurt and understanding each other perfectly. "Call me after. Tomorrow. Promise?"

"Sure," Wren said, and hung up, and stood in the copper light with the drawer open and the coins cold under their fingers, understanding that they had just been given permission to do the thing they'd already been doing for months, which was letting Ines Iversen matter.

* * *

They didn't sleep.

They tried. They went back to the room they rented over the laundromat on Cordelia Street—a room with a hot plate, a shared bathroom down the hall, and a window that faced the dryer exhaust and let in a smell of warm lint and other people's lives. They lay on the bed with the letter on their chest and the hearing running through their head in every possible version, and every version ended the same way: a caseworker's smile, a group home in some inland town, a bed that wasn't theirs, a door that locked from the outside. Eleven absences. Eleven. They could count them all—days when getting out of bed had been a physics problem, days when the laundromat landlord had been drunk and loud, days when walking past the school had felt like walking into weather. Eleven days, weighed against everything.

At two in the morning they got up and dressed in the dark.

They didn't decide to go to the bookshop. That was the thing they'd understand later, standing in the attic with the first gray light coming through the round window—there was no decision, there was only the body knowing where home was before the mind caught up. Their feet carried them down Cordelia Street, past the shuttered cannery, along the seawall where the fog stood thick as wool, and up Bellwether Lane to the shop with the green door and the brass bell, and their hands—Wren's own hands, acting on their behalf—found the spare key under the loose brick by the step, the one Ines pretended not to know they knew about.

Inside, the shop was dark and breathing. Wren stood in it for a moment, letting their eyes adjust, letting the smell of it—paper and lemon polish and the faint coal-smoke ghost of the radiator—close over them like water. Then they climbed the attic ladder.

The attic was low and dry and full of the shop's overflow: boxes marked in Ines's slanted hand, a dressmaker's dummy wearing a coat from some previous decade, the round window looking east over the rooftops toward the water. There was a cot back there behind the boxes—old, canvas, army-surplus, but there'd been a blanket folded on it since October, since the first cold snap, since a morning when Wren had mentioned once, in passing, that the room over the laundromat ran cold. Ines had said nothing. The blanket had appeared. Neither of them had ever said a word about it, and the not-saying had become, without either of them naming it, the truest conversation they had.

Wren lay down on the cot. The blanket smelled faintly of cedar. The building ticked and settled around them, and somewhere below, in the walls, the pipes knocked once, twice, like a heart slowing.

*It's just for tonight,* they told themselves. *Just until after the hearing. Just so I don't have to walk back across town at seven in the morning looking like what I am.* They were asleep before they finished the thought.

* * *

Ines found them at dawn.

Wren woke to the sound of the ladder—weight on the rungs, slow and deliberate, the creak of someone giving the sleeper upstairs every chance to be awake and decent before arriving. By the time Ines's head came through the hatch, Wren was sitting up, blanket clutched around their shoulders like armor, every excuse they'd ever constructed lined up and ready.

None of them survived the look on her face.

It wasn't anger. Wren had prepared for anger; anger had a script, and Wren was good at scripts. But Ines stood there in her cardigan with her gray hair uncombed and the dawn light making her look older and softer and infinitely tired, and her expression was something Wren had no script for. It was recognition. Plain and total, the way you recognize a face in a crowd that you'd know anywhere, that you'd been looking for without knowing it.

She looked at the cot. At the blanket. At Wren.

"The hearing's at nine," she said.

"I was going to— I only came to—"

"You were going to walk back across town at seven and come in the front door at eight-fifty like you'd slept in your own bed." Ines crossed the attic, ducking under the low beam, and sat down on the dressmaker's dummy's stand like it was a chair, which it had clearly been used as before. "You were going to do the whole thing alone, and then come back here and alphabetize something until your hands stopped shaking."

Wren's throat closed. "It's eleven absences. It's not— it's a paperwork thing. I can explain the days, I have reasons for—"

"I know you have reasons." Ines said it quietly. "I've read enough files to know that the reasons are never the question. The question is whether anyone shows up for you when they read them out loud." She was quiet a moment. The fog outside the round window was going from gray to white, and the light came through it the color of milk. "Your mother called you. I saw your face after."

"She can't come. The shift. The gas." Wren heard their own voice go flat and rehearsed, and hated it, and couldn't stop it. "It's fine. It's how she is. I'm used to—"

"Don't," Ines said, not unkindly. "Don't do the thing where you defend her before anyone's accused her. I did that for years, with someone. It doesn't make the waiting shorter. It just makes you fluent in excuses." She looked out the round window at the fog lifting off the lane. "I slept in this attic for three months, when I first came back to Marrow's Reach. After I retired. Before the shop was the shop. There was a mattress where the cot is now, and I told myself it was temporary, and I lay awake every night listening to the building settle, and it was the only place in this town where I didn't feel like I was trespassing in my own life."

Wren stared at her. In all the months at the shop, Ines had given them pieces of herself the way she gave change—exactly counted, no more than owed. This was not change. This was the whole drawer, open.

"So," Ines said. She stood, and smoothed her cardigan, and became again the woman who closed shops with her voice. "Here's what happens. You wash up in the back, there's a toothbrush under the sink that's been there since November, and we walk to the district office at eight-thirty, and we wear collars, and I do the talking until they ask you a direct question, and then you do the talking. And afterward there's pancakes at the diner, because hearings are worse on an empty stomach and I refuse to let the Marrow's Reach school district ruin breakfast."

"And after that?" Wren's voice came out small. "After the hearing. What happens to me?"

Ines paused at the ladder, one hand on the rail, and looked back. And whatever she saw in Wren's face made her answer the real question instead of the easy one.

"After that," she said, "you come home. Whatever the district decides, whatever paperwork they want signed—there's a cot up here with your name on it now, whether it has your name on it or not. That's not a promise I can make official. It's just the one I'm making."

Then she went down the ladder, and Wren sat alone in the milky light with the blanket around their shoulders, and did not cry, or maybe did, a little, where nobody—not even the dressmaker's dummy—could see.

Below, the kettle went on. The pipes knocked. And out on Bellwether Lane, the fog was lifting, the way it did some mornings, reluctantly, like something that knew it would have to come back.

They had forty minutes until they had to leave for the hearing. Wren got dressed, found the toothbrush under the sink exactly where she'd said it would be—since November, since before Wren had ever earned it—and understood, standing at the sink with water running cold over their hands, that the letter in their back pocket had stopped mattering somewhere in the night.

What mattered was that Ines had said *home* and hadn't corrected it.

What mattered was that in forty minutes they'd walk into a room full of people with clipboards, and Ines Iversen would be sitting beside them, and for the first time in longer than Wren could measure, they wouldn't be coming alone.

They just didn't know yet what Cassian Bellwether's arrival that same morning would cost them—or that the man in the good coat, watching the shop from across the lane as they locked the door, was already counting the days until the anniversary dinner, and already asking, in that soft charming voice of his, what the bookshop girl knew about shelf nine.
