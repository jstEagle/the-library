## 10. Maintenance

The Marrow's Reach Municipal Archives occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, a granite building that had been built to withstand weather, war, and the particular malice of the sea. It had withstood all three. What it had not been built to withstand was Theo Marchetti at seven in the morning, three days into an obsession, with cold coffee going bitter on the reading table and a ledger open before him like a wound.

He had not slept properly since Ines had come to him. That was how he thought of it—not *visited*, not *asked for help*—*come to him*. After eleven years of careful distance, of nods across the market square and the occasional stiff exchange about preservation grants, she had walked into his archive carrying a book bound in oxblood leather and said, in the voice she used for things she meant, "I need your eyes."

She could have asked him anything else and he would have managed his composure. But *I need your eyes*—as if she hadn't spent years looking at him without seeing what was there.

He turned the page again. The ledger's entries were written in a cramped, disciplined hand, columns of figures in faded iron-gall ink: shipments of lamp oil, crates of provisions, payments rendered. And recurring through it all, quarter after quarter, decade after decade, like a heartbeat under the skin of the town:

**H.B., maintenance. £4. 1927.**
**H.B., maintenance. $6.40. 1931.**
**H.B., maintenance. $11.00. 1938.**
**H.B., maintenance. 1946. 1953. 1961. 1972.**

Harlan Bellwether had died in 1924. Harlan Bellwether lay—or was supposed to lie—in the marble-fenced plot on Cemetery Hill, beneath a stone so grand it looked less like a memorial than a monument to the family's opinion of itself. And yet here was his initials, paid out quarterly, faithfully, for a hundred years. Someone had been maintaining something in Harlan Bellwether's name long after Harlan Bellwether stopped being able to require it.

Or long after anyone believed he'd stopped.

Theo pulled his glasses down his nose and rubbed the bridge of it. The archive smelled the way it always smelled—paper dust and beeswax and the faint mineral breath of the harbor coming through the stone—and normally that smell settled him the way no other place did. He had spent thirty years among these records. He knew their moods, their silences, their little treacheries. He had believed himself immune to them.

He had been wrong. The itch had started behind his sternum on the first night and had not let go since. It was the old feeling, the detective's feeling, the one he'd shared with Ines across a decade of cases before everything between them went quiet: the sense that a document was lying to him, and that documents, unlike people, could be made to tell the truth if you were patient enough.

"You're here early." Petra Okafor, his assistant archivist, stood in the doorway with her coat still on, snowmelt dripping off her hem. She was twenty-six and treated the archive's contents with the reverent suspicion of someone who had grown up on stories about the Bellwethers. "Again."

"The centennial," Theo said. "Preparations. Records requests."

"You haven't processed a records request since Tuesday." Petra hung her coat and came to stand over the ledger, arms crossed. Her eyes moved down the columns and stopped. "Is that—"

"It's nothing you need to worry about."

"Right." She said it flatly, the way young people said *right* when they meant *I see exactly what this is and I will be asking questions later*. She retreated to her desk. A moment later: "For what it's worth, the burial register from 1924 has a gap. Page forty-one. I noticed it when I was digitizing last spring. Somebody cut it out very neatly, but paper remembers."

Theo looked up. "Paper remembers?"

"There's a shadow where the page was pasted over. You can see the ghost of ink through it if you hold it to the light." She shrugged, already turning back to her terminal. "I catalogued it as water damage. It isn't water damage."

*Paper remembers.* Theo wrote it on the pad beside him and underlined it twice, and felt the itch behind his sternum sharpen into something with edges.

* * *

By noon he had assembled the architecture of the payments, and the architecture was beautiful in the way that only lies can be beautiful—load-bearing everywhere, seamless, designed by someone who understood exactly how towns forget.

The disbursements did not come from any Bellwether account directly. They flowed from something called the Gull Point Trust, established—according to its charter, which Theo found filed, absurdly, under *G* for *Grants, Ecclesiastical and Otherwise*—in March of 1925, eleven months after Harlan Bellwether's death. The trust's stated purpose was "the upkeep of coastal aids to navigation and associated structures within the bounds of Marrow's Reach." Its trustee of record, from 1925 until the present day, was a single law firm: Whitlock, Ferris & Bellwether.

&Bellwether. Of course. The family's own lawyers. The firm had changed names four times over the century—Whitlock & Sons, Whitlock-Ferris, Ferris-Whitlock, and now simply Ferris & Associates—but the succession of partners read like a relay race in tailored suits, each man passing the trust to the next with the smoothness of long practice. The current holder was Ambrose Ferris, third of his name, a dry, precise man who attended every civic function and whose handshake Theo had always found slightly too calibrated.

Quarterly, the trust paid out. Quarterly, some invoice or receipt must have justified the payment. Theo searched for those justifications and found them—or found their shapes. Every twenty-five years or so, the supporting documentation thinned, then vanished entirely, replaced by summary certificates signed by whichever Ferris held the pen. The paper trail didn't end. It *attenuated*, deliberately, the way a path through fog attenuates: still walkable, but only if you already knew where it went.

And where did it go? That was the question that kept him at the table while the light through the tall windows moved from white to gold to the bruised violet of early dusk. The trust's disbursement address never changed. Not a bank, not a solicitor's office, not a parish. An address Theo had to look up twice because his mind refused to accept it the first time:

**The Keeper's Cottage, Gull Point.**

There was no keeper at Gull Point. There had not been a keeper at Gull Point since 1931, when the lighthouse itself had been automated and decommissioned and left to the gulls and the salt wind. Theo knew this the way everyone in Marrow's Reach knew it—as background noise, as fact without weight. The cottage still stood; you could see its silhouette from the north road on clear days, a low dark shape against the sky like a comma in a sentence nobody had finished.

A century of payments, flowing to an empty house.

Unless the house wasn't empty.

* * *

He should have called Ines. He knew that. They had agreed—he had insisted, actually, in one of the few moments of the past week when he'd trusted his own voice—that they would work in parallel and compare notes, that neither of them would go down a corridor alone. Old protocol. Their protocol, from the years when they'd been good together, when the whole department had joked that Iversen and Marchetti shared a brain and argued about who got to keep it.

But it was nearly eight in the evening now, and the archive was dark except for his desk lamp, and the pull of the next document was stronger than the pull of the telephone. One more hour. One more file. The obsession had him fully by the throat now, and he recognized the sensation with a kind of helpless nostalgia—it was the feeling of being *useful*, of mattering inside a puzzle, and he had missed it more than he had ever admitted to anyone, including himself.

He pulled the lighthouse commission records. Gull Point Light, constructed 1849, staffed continuously by keepers of the Bellwether-connected Mowbray line—the same family, he noted with a small cold shock, that had intermarried with the Bellwethers twice in the nineteenth century. The final keeper of record was one Elias Mowbray, appointed 1888, retired 1931 upon automation. Retirement record signed by—Theo exhaled slowly—A. Whitlock, solicitor, on behalf of the Gull Point Trust.

A retired keeper whose retirement was administered by the family lawyer. Who retired to a cottage that then received quarterly maintenance payments in the name of a dead founder, for ninety more years.

Theo sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the walls, the old building ticked and settled, granite remembering the day's warmth.

He tried a different angle: photographs. The archive's photographic collection was his particular pride—fourteen thousand images, catalogued by hand over decades. He searched the index for *Gull Point* and found forty-one entries spanning 1890 to 1987. He pulled the boxes and laid the prints out on the long table under the lamp, and there it was: the cottage through time. Whitewashed and bright in 1902, a woman hanging wash. Storm-battered in 1913. Repainted in 1926—*repainted*, a year after the founder's death, when the trust's first payment cleared. Smoke from the chimney in the winter photograph of 1940. Smoke again, fainter, in 1955. Smoke in 1968, 1974, 1981—

Smoke meant fire. Fire meant fuel. Fuel meant someone.

In the 1987 photograph—the last one—the cottage was shuttered, its paint gone gray, the lamp tower above it blind. But even in that image, even at that distance, Theo could see that the path from the road had been recently swept. Grass does not lie. Grass grows where feet don't fall, and around that cottage, in 1987, the grass was thin as a scar.

He gathered the photographs back into their boxes with hands that were not quite steady, and he understood, sitting alone in the lamplight, that he had crossed some line. This was no longer a favor to Ines. This was his case now too, in the marrow-deep way cases became yours, the way a song becomes yours after the hundredth hearing. Somewhere out at Gull Point, at the end of a trail that a hundred years of careful men had tried to sweep clean, something was being maintained. A structure. A story. Or a person.

* * *

Petra found him there at midnight, having come back for her forgotten scarf, and stood in the doorway taking in the spread of documents, the cold coffee, the photographs, the look on his face.

"You've gone full archivist," she said. "The dangerous kind."

"The worst kind," Theo agreed.

She hesitated in the doorway, coat half-on, and then—because she was braver than her age suggested—said, "My grandmother used to tell a story. About the light at Gull Point staying lit years after it was decommissioned. Fishermen swore to it. They called it the widow's light, because it burned steady and sad, like someone keeping watch." She pulled her scarf through her fingers. "Everyone assumed it was a reflection, or a trick of the fog. But my grandmother said her father delivered firewood out that way once, in '44, and saw a man on the gallery rail. Tall. Old-fashioned coat. Watching the water like he was waiting for something to come home."

"What happened to the wood delivery?"

"He left it at the gate. Nobody ever paid him. But the money showed up in his coat pocket anyway, exact change, when he got home." Petra smiled uncertainly. "Ghost story. That's all. Goodnight, Mr. Marchetti."

"Goodnight, Petra."

The door closed. Theo sat for a long time in the humming lamplight, looking at the last photograph, the swept path, the blind tower.

Exact change. From a trust that paid quarterly. To a cottage that received it.

He thought about Ines—Iversen, he corrected himself, though the correction rang false even in the privacy of his own skull—and about the way she had stood in his archive holding the ledger, guarded and tired and still, after everything, the only person whose puzzles he had ever wanted to solve. He thought about how she would take this news: the quick stillness, the eyes going distant and sharp, the almost-smile she'd never know she made. He wanted to be the one who brought it to her. He hated how much he wanted that, and he picked up the telephone anyway.

It rang four times before she answered, her voice rough with sleep and instantly, completely awake.

"Iversen. Tell me you have something, because I have something, and mine doesn't make sense."

"Mine doesn't either," Theo said. "Mine ends at a lighthouse."

A pause. Down the line, he heard her sit up, heard the creak of the attic stairs—no, not the attic; the shop floor, the old armchair by the window—and then her voice came back stripped of sleep, honed to the edge he remembered.

"A lighthouse," she repeated. "Theo. Whose light is still burning?"

He looked at the photograph, at the thin scar of a path through the grass, at a hundred years of payments flowing like blood to a heart that should have stopped beating before anyone alive today was born.

"That," he said, "is what I intend to find out tomorrow. Bring your coat. And Ines—" he caught himself on the name, too late, and decided not to apologize for it, "—bring Wren. Whatever we find at Gull Point, I don't think either of us should stand in front of it alone."

Outside the customs house windows, the fog had come up off the harbor the way it always did, swallowing the streets, softening the town into rumor. And far out at the edge of it, invisible but suddenly undeniable, Gull Point waited in the dark—with its swept path, its warm chimney, and its light that somebody, somewhere, had never stopped tending.
