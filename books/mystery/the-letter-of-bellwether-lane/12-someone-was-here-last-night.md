## 12. Someone Was Here Last Night

Wren had learned to read a room the way other kids learned to read books—by necessity, by lamplight, by the particular grammar of doors left open and drawers left shut. A foster house taught you that. You learned which floorboards sang and which ones kept secrets. You learned the temperature of a silence, whether it meant someone was asleep or someone was listening.

So when Wren came down the attic ladder at six in the morning, barefoot on the cold treads of Iversen & Daughters Books, they knew before their feet touched the shop floor that something was wrong.

The air was different. That was the first thing. The shop had a smell the way a person has a smell—old paper and lemon polish and the ghost of pipe smoke from some previous century—and underneath it this morning ran a thread of something else. Woodsmoke. Not the good kind from the stove Ines lit in January, all dry applewood and patience. This was sharper. Coal-smutty. Recent.

Wren stood at the bottom of the ladder with one hand still on the rail and listened.

Rain against the front window, soft as fingers tapping. The tick of the old clock above the register. Somewhere down the lane, a gull arguing with the foghorn about whose job it was to wake the town. And beneath all of it—the settling creak of a building that was not empty. Or had not been empty for long.

The stove in the reading nook was cold now. But when Wren crossed the floor, careful as a cat burglar in sock feet, and laid two fingers flat against its iron flank, the metal held a memory of warmth. Faint. The kind of warmth a stone keeps an hour after the sun leaves it.

Someone had lit the fire last night. Someone had sat here, in the green wingback chair with the torn arm, in the dark, in a closed bookshop, and warmed themselves like they belonged.

Wren's heart did a strange double-beat—not fear exactly, or not only fear. There was a shameful sliver of recognition in it too, because Wren knew what it was to need a warm room you had no right to. They'd slept in three bus stations and a laundromat before the bookshop attic had offered itself up like an answered prayer. Whoever had been here last night understood something about cold.

Then Wren saw the footprints.

The rain had come in under the door overnight—a thin tongue of water reaching across the boards toward the biography section—and whoever had walked through it had left prints going in and coming out. Boot prints, smallish, worn at the heel. They went from the door to the stove, from the stove to shelf nine, and stopped there.

Shelf nine.

Wren's mouth went dry. Shelf nine, where the books were numbered like entries in a ledger, where Ines had gone quiet as a held breath when she'd seen them, where her late mentor's handwriting waited in the margins like a voice under a floorboard. Shelf nine, which had become the center of everything without ever moving an inch.

The prints ended at the shelf, but the shelf itself told the rest. Three books stood out of alignment, spines pushed back further than their neighbors, the way a book looks after hands have pulled it out and put it back in a hurry. And on the lip of the third shelf, where the varnish had worn away over a hundred years of resting fingers, there was a smudge of gray—coal dust, or ash, transferred from a glove or a sleeve.

Wren should have called Ines. That was the obvious thing. Ines was upstairs, asleep, or pretending to be; she rose late these days and moved through the mornings like a woman rationing her energy for a war only she could see. She would want to know. She would come down with her glasses pushed up into her gray hair and her detective's face on—that flat, attentive look that made even the creditors sit straighter—and she would read the room the way Wren had, except better, except she'd find the things Wren hadn't even known to look for.

But Wren didn't call her. Because Wren turned around, then, and saw the table.

* * *

It was the worktable behind the counter, the one Ines used for repairs—cracked spines waiting for glue, a jar of brushes, the bone folder worn smooth as a river pebble. Morning light came through the back window and lay across it in a pale rectangle, and in that light Wren saw the scratches.

Not old scratches. The wood around them was raw and bright, splintered at the edges, curlings of pale shaving still caught in the grain like snow that hadn't melted. Someone had taken a knife—or a nail, or the bone folder itself, God help them—to the surface of that table sometime in the last twelve hours, and they had written:

**STOP COUNTING**

Three words, gouged deep enough that Wren could feel them with a fingertip like braille. The letters were uneven, hurried, the O's ragged circles, the T's driven in hard enough to split the grain. It wasn't elegant. It wasn't a threat from a man in a good suit who paid people to do his threatening. It was desperate. It was the handwriting of someone who had been afraid for a very long time.

Wren stood there with their hand hovering over the words and felt the morning tilt.

*Stop counting.* The ledger. Shelf nine. The numbered books, the margins full of a dead man's handwriting, the entries that went on decades past a death date. *H.B., maintenance,* quarter after quarter, year after year, like a heartbeat that refused to stop. Ines counted pages the way other people breathed—it was why she was who she was, why the whole town half-feared and half-revered her, why Wren had trusted her before they'd trusted anyone in six years. And somebody out there in the fog knew it, and was afraid of it, and had come in the night to say so in the only language that couldn't be ignored.

Or—worse, so much worse—in the only language they thought wouldn't be heard.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it? The warning hadn't been left on the counter. It hadn't been slipped under the door or mailed in a plain envelope. It had been scratched into a table in a dark shop, hidden behind the register, low and private. If Ines hadn't bent over that table every day with her repair work, she might not have found it for weeks. This wasn't a message meant for Ines.

This was a message meant for nobody. A message written by someone who needed to say it anyway.

And that frightened Wren more than any threat could have. Threats came from enemies. This came from someone drowning.

* * *

The bell over the front door chimed at seven-forty, and Wren nearly went through the ceiling.

It was Mrs. Okonkwo from the bakery, delivering the morning's misprints—day-old rolls and a loaf shaped like a question mark—for the shop's honor-system basket. She squinted at Wren, who was standing behind the counter holding a dust cloth like a shield, and said, "You look like you've seen the fog lady."

"The what?"

"Fog lady. Marrow's Reach legend. Appears before trouble." Mrs. Okonkwo set down her basket, patted Wren's shoulder with a flour-warm hand, and left. The bell chimed again, cheerful and oblivious, and Wren exhaled for what felt like the first time in ten minutes.

They spent those minutes doing the worst thing they knew how to do: deciding.

Option one, tell Ines. Consequences: questions. Questions about how Wren had been in the shop at dawn to discover any of this, which led to questions about the attic, which led to the truancy officer, which led to the group home in Corvallis with the flannel blankets and the 9:30 lights-out and the staff who called everyone "buddy." Wren had done the math on that future already, more than once, lying awake under the eaves between boxes of unsold romance novels. Every calculation ended the same way: sent away.

Option two, hide it. Cover the table with a cloth. Say nothing. Let Ines keep counting her pages in peace while Wren carried the warning around like a stone in the shoe.

Wren dragged a canvas drop cloth over the worktable, arranging it to look accidental, and hated themselves the entire time. It took four minutes. It felt like burying something alive.

The problem with guilt, Wren had learned, was that it didn't stay buried either. It leaked. It got into your voice. By noon, when Ines came down—cardigan buttoned wrong, tea mug in hand, eyes going immediately to the shelves the way they always did now, to shelf nine, to the count—Wren was polishing the same spot on the counter for the ninth time.

"You've missed a day of school," Ines said. Not accusatory. Just noting it, the way she noted weather.

"Feeling ill."

"Mm." Ines sipped her tea. Her gaze drifted over the reading nook, the stove, the biography section, and snagged—Wren felt it snag, like a fishhook catching cloth—on the covered worktable. "What's under there?"

"Glue mess. Drying."

Ines looked at Wren for a moment longer than was comfortable. That was the thing about her; she didn't interrogate, she just *looked*, and looking was worse. Then she nodded and went to shelve a cart of returns, and Wren's heart started again.

* * *

But hiding a thing in a small shop is like hiding a smell. By afternoon Wren couldn't stand near the counter without feeling the words through the canvas, radiating cold like the stove's remembered warmth. Twice they caught themselves almost saying something. Once they actually opened their mouth and Ines glanced up and Wren converted it into a cough.

At four o'clock the rain quit and the light came low and gold through the front windows, and Ines stood at shelf nine with her notebook, copying entries, murmuring numbers under her breath like a rosary. Watching her, Wren felt the war in their chest reach open hostilities.

Loyalty said: *she trusts you. She found you sleeping in her attic and said nothing. She feeds you toast cut on the diagonal because you once mentioned liking it. She is the first adult in years who hasn't tried to fix you, just make room for you.*

Guilt said: *and you're lying to her. She's counting pages and someone carved STOP COUNTING into her own table and you know and you're letting her walk blind into whatever comes next.*

Loyalty said: *if you tell her, you lose the attic, the toast, the whole fragile architecture of this life you've built out of someone else's spare room.*

Guilt said: *if you don't tell her, and something happens to her, you'll have built it on a grave.*

Wren went home—no. Wren went *up*. Up the ladder, into the attic, among the boxes, and sat with their knees drawn up and their phone glowing uselessly in their hand. Their mother's number, unchanged for three years though she never answered. The truancy officer's number, saved under a name that wasn't hers. Cassian Bellwether's card, smooth and heavy stock, tucked in their jacket pocket since the committee errands began—*call me if you think of anything useful, Wren, I mean it, I take care of people who take care of me.*

Three doors out of the attic. All of them leading somewhere Wren didn't want to go.

Below, faintly, they heard Ines moving. The scrape of the repair cart. The clink of the brush jar. And then—silence. The particular silence of someone bending close to something.

Wren froze.

Footsteps crossed the floor below. Stopped. A long pause, the length of a person standing very still. Then Ines's voice, quiet and level, pitched not upward to the attic but downward, toward the table, as if addressing it directly:

"Well," she said, to the empty shop, to the carved words, to whoever had knelt here in the dark. "That's rude."

Wren pressed both hands over their mouth.

She'd found it. Of course she'd found it—she read rooms the way Wren read them, except she'd been doing it for sixty years. The canvas, the glue lie, the polishing, the cough—all of it, undone by a woman lifting a drop cloth.

And now she knew Wren had known. She had to. The cloth arranged too carefully, the kid jumpy as a struck match all day. Ines Iversen missed nothing; that was her whole legend, her whole curse.

Wren sat in the darkening attic with their heart slamming and listened to the shop below. Ines didn't climb the ladder. Didn't call up. She simply went back to work—the scrape of the cart, the soft ring of the bell as she flipped the sign to CLOSED, the creak of the stairs to her own rooms.

She was giving Wren the night. Wren understood that with terrible clarity. One night to come down and confess, like a kid deciding whether to turn themselves in.

They lay awake until the foghorn sounded midnight, watching rain trace the little window, running the conversation in advance. *I'm sorry. I hid it because—I was scared you'd—* Scared she'd what? Send them away? Ines, who said nothing at dawn? Maybe. Maybe not. That was the horror of trust: you couldn't know until you fell, whether anyone would catch you.

Toward morning, Wren made the decision the way you make all real decisions—suddenly, after ages of pretending not to.

They climbed down the ladder into the gray light, and Ines was already there, sitting in the green wingback by the dead stove, fully dressed, a cup of tea steaming on the table beside her. Waiting. She'd known she wouldn't sleep either.

Wren opened their mouth to confess everything—the footprints, the ash, the warning, the day of lies—

And Ines said, quietly, without looking up from her tea: "Before you start. The boot prints are a size eight, worn on the outside heel. The ash is coal, not wood, which means a stove older than mine. And whoever wrote that message used the bone folder, which means they knew my tools—which means they've been in this shop before, more than once."

She raised her eyes at last, and they weren't angry. They were tired, and sharp, and—worst of all—kind.

"So," Ines said. "Tell me why you didn't wake me."
