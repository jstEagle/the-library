## 28. Cornered at the Archives

The rain had been falling on Marrow's Reach for three days, a fine gray insistence that made the whole town feel like something left out too long. Ines Iversen stood beneath the awning of the county records annex, watching water sheet off the gutters and pool in the cracked flagstones, and told herself—for the fourth time that evening—that there was no reason to be here.

Theo had called an hour before closing. *The burial registry's been pulled,* they'd said, voice low and careful the way it always went careful when Theo was frightened. *Signed out under the lawyer's name. Ambrose Kettle. He returned it this morning, but the pages are wrong. The paper's wrong. Someone's been making copies of copies for years, and tonight he asked for after-hours access to the vault. Said he needed to "reconcile discrepancies" before the anniversary.*

*So call the sheriff,* Ines had said.

*I did. Sheriff Pell said she'd send someone tomorrow.* A pause down the line, long enough to hear Theo breathing, or maybe the rain on their end too. *Ines. Tomorrow is Saturday. And if I were a man with a shredder and a hundred-year-old secret to bury, I would not wait for Monday.*

That was the trouble with Theo Marchetti. Even after eleven years and one unfinished sentence between them, they still knew exactly which lever to pull.

Now Ines stood in the dark with her collar up and her bad knee aching, and admitted the truth she'd been dodging since the coffin came up empty: retirement was a vow she'd made to herself, and like all vows made by people who used to carry badges, it was mostly theater.

The annex was a squat brick building at the edge of the civic square, built in 1931 by the same WPA hands that had laid half the town. Its windows were high and narrow, barred since some burglary in the seventies that everyone had forgotten except the bars. But the loading door at the back—there was always a loading door—faced the alley where the delivery trucks came, and its lock was older than Ines's mother.

She didn't go in through it. She went around front, tried the main entrance, found it locked as expected—and then heard, from somewhere deep inside, the sound of a machine running. Not a copier. Something hungrier. A rhythmic mechanical chewing, patient as a moth.

Ines took out her phone and texted Theo: *He's here. Back way?*

Three dots appeared, vanished, appeared. *Side door by the microfilm room sticks. Lift and push. I'm two minutes out—I was already coming. Don't go in alone.*

Ines looked at the message. She thought about going in alone. She thought about how many times in her life she had gone in alone, and what each of those times had cost, and how the last of them—the case that ended her career, the one nobody in Marrow's Reach spoke of near her—had cost her nearly everything but the bookshop.

Then she lifted the side door and pushed, because some doors you open even knowing what waits behind them.

* * *

Inside, the annex smelled the way archives always smelled: cold paper, dust, the faint vinegar breath of old film stock decaying in its canisters. Emergency lighting painted everything the color of weak tea. Ines moved down the corridor past the microfilm room, past the map cabinets with their brass handles gone green, following the sound.

It led her to the vault room.

The door stood open six inches. Through the gap she could see a desk lamp burning, and beyond it, framed in that small warm circle, Ambrose Kettle.

He was not what the town imagined when it imagined the Bellwether family lawyer. People pictured silver hair and a firm handshake; Kettle was sixty-ish and soft-bodied, with reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair and a cardigan buttoned wrong, one hole lower than it should have been. He sat at the work table with his sleeves rolled, feeding documents into a cross-cut shredder the size of a bread box, and he worked with the terrible calm of a man who has done arithmetic and accepted the answer.

He was shredding the burial registry. Not copies. The original. Page by page, 1924 first, each leaf fed in with both hands, the machine chewing it into confetti that spilled into an overflowing bin at his feet like gray snow.

Ines watched him destroy the record of Harlan Bellwether's grave—the grave that had never held Harlan Bellwether—and felt something cold settle behind her sternum. Because this wasn't panic. Panic grabs handfuls. This was method. He'd started with the most incriminating volume and was working outward in order of damage. Which meant he had a list. Which meant he'd planned this the way you plan anything worth doing badly.

"You're doing it wrong," Ines said, and stepped into the doorway.

Kettle froze with a page half-fed into the slot. For a moment he simply stared at her over his glasses, and then, absurdly, he smiled—a small, apologetic, professional smile, the smile of a man greeting a client in a waiting room.

"Ms. Iversen." His voice was mild. Mild as milk. "I wondered when you'd come. Mr. Bellwether said you would."

"Cassian told you I'd come?"

"He told me you couldn't leave it alone." Kettle turned back to the shredder, almost gently, and pushed the page through. The machine ate it. "He said you were the sort who counts other people's pages."

"I've heard that phrase before," Ines said, moving slowly along the wall, putting the map cabinets between them. "From someone else. It's funny how often your family's secrets come pre-quoted."

"They aren't my family." Kettle said it quickly, and then, hearing himself, more slowly: "They were never my family. That was rather the problem."

Behind him, faintly, the side door groaned. Theo, letting themselves in. Good. Ines kept her eyes on Kettle and her voice level.

"The maintenance trust," she said. "You've been administering it for thirty years. Quarterly disbursements to the keeper's cottage at Gull Point. Except the keeper stopped drawing full payments decades ago, and the fund kept paying anyway. Where did it go, Ambrose?"

Something changed in his face. The mildness stayed—it was clearly load-bearing, that mildness—but underneath it, something shifted like furniture being rearranged in a dark room.

"You've been talking to Marchetti."

"I've been talking to ledgers. Ledgers don't lie. They just wait."

Kettle laughed. It was a horrible little sound, dry as the confetti at his feet. "Ledgers. Yes. You'd know." He stood, brushing paper dust from his cardigan, and Ines saw his hand tremble—not with fear, she realized, but with exhaustion, the deep tremor of a man who hadn't slept properly in weeks. "Do you know what I inherited, Ms. Iversen? Do you know what was handed to me, fresh out of law school, by old Cornelius Bellwether, with a pat on the head and a retainer my father wept over? A shoebox of obligations. Pay the cottage. Keep the records tidy. Ask nothing. My entire career has been keeping books for a dead man's lie, and now—" he gestured at the shredder, at the bin, at the whole gilded century of it—"now the audit comes, and the lie is the only thing standing between me and a cell."

"So burn the evidence. Burn the man's grave while you're at it."

"The grave was already empty!" It burst out of him louder than he meant, echoing off the vault shelves, and he flinched at his own voice, dropped it to a furious whisper. "There was never anything in it. There was never anything anywhere. Just paper. Paper and payments and a cottage full of photographs, and every year the numbers got harder to hide, and every year I told myself one more quarter, one more reconciliation—" He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I tipped the sexton about the exhumation, you know. Did you work that out? I wanted the scandal. A scandal drowns the paperwork. Everyone so busy gasping about an empty coffin that no one audits a trust."

"And instead you woke the whole thing up," Ines said. "You dug up the message."

"What message?" He lowered his hands. He genuinely looked lost, and that was somehow worse than menace. "What message? It's money, Ms. Iversen. It was always just money."

"No," Ines said quietly. "For you it was money. For them it was a brother. That's the difference, and it's why you'll never understand what you've been shredding."

The side corridor creaked. Theo came into the lamplight with rain still beaded on their shoulders, a camera bag slung across their chest, and Kettle went very still.

"Hello, Ambrose," Theo said.

"Marchetti." Kettle's voice flattened. "Of course. The archivist. The one who remembers everything." He looked from Theo to Ines and back, and something in him seemed to finish a calculation and arrive at zero. "Well. It hardly matters now, does it. The registry's half gone. The rest goes tonight. By morning there will be nothing in this building proving anyone named Hollis Bellwether ever existed—or that anyone ever paid to keep him existing."

"There's a copy," Theo said.

Kettle laughed again, shorter this time. "There is no copy. I checked the digitization schedule myself. This floor isn't scanned until next fiscal year. I made sure of that eighteen months ago."

"I didn't say the copy was digital." Theo unslung the camera bag, opened it, and drew out a fat envelope, which they set on the work table with the deliberate care of someone laying down a winning card. "Photographic plates. Nineteen twenty-four series, donated by the *Marrow's Reach Courier* in 1988. Including, as it happens, a courthouse fire-safety inspection photograph taken inside this very vault. Shelf by shelf. Every spine legible."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the walls, old pipes ticked as the temperature dropped.

"You're bluffing," Kettle said.

"I'm an archivist," Theo said. "Bluffing is for people whose materials decay."

And that was when Kettle reached for the shredder.

Not to use it—to lift it. He hauled the machine off the table with both arms, awkward, wheezing, and swung it toward the bin of shredded registry, meaning, Ines understood a half-second too late, to dump the confetti into the wasteline chute behind him, to mix the evidence into the building's general refuse where no one could ever reassemble it—

"Don't," Ines said, and when he didn't stop, she crossed the room faster than her knee liked and put her hand flat on the machine itself, stopping its arc. They stood there, the two of them, holding a shredder between them like a piece of furniture in a divorce.

"Let go, Ms. Iversen."

"No."

"You don't understand what's coming for you. Cassian won't stop at the papers. When this surfaces, he'll bury *you*. He's already drafting it—the disgraced detective, obsessed, unstable, harassing a grieving family during their centennial. He showed me the statement. It's good. It's very good. He's had it written for weeks." Kettle's voice cracked, and for one naked moment he was not a lawyer but a tired man in a wrongly buttoned cardigan. "I'm the sacrifice, do you see? I was always the sacrifice. He lets me fall, the family survives, the land deal dies with me, and you—you get dragged down in the same flood. Two scandals cancel out. That's his arithmetic."

"Then let's talk to the sheriff before his arithmetic finishes," Ines said. "Sheriff Pell. Tonight. Your statement, your cooperation, your side of it, in your own words, before he gets his version printed."

Kettle looked at her hand on the shredder. At Theo, who had already raised the camera and, without asking permission, photographed the open bin, the half-shredded registry, the lamp, the room. At the envelope of plates sitting on the table like a headstone.

"You'd offer me that," he said wonderingly. "After everything. Why?"

Because mercy was the only thing Ines had left that she trusted, though she didn't say so. What she said was: "Because I've read the letters, Ambrose. I know what this family does to the people who keep its secrets. I'd rather break the pattern than extend it."

Kettle's shoulders dropped. All at once, entirely, like a marionette with the strings cut. He sat down heavily on the work stool, and the shredder sagged in Ines's grip until she let it lean against the table, and he put his face in his hands and said, in a small voice, "The audit was Tuesday. I could have paid it back. I was going to pay it back."

"I know," Ines said, which was a lie, and a kind one, and she hated how easily it came.

* * *

Sheriff Pell arrived at 11:40 p.m., unhurried, rain-wet, and deeply unimpressed to find the town's oldest mystery, its leading family, its most prominent attorney, and its retired detective all occupying the same basement. She listened. She photographed what Theo had photographed. She read Kettle his rights while he stood with his wrists out, cooperative as a churchgoer, and asked Ines only one question, quietly, at the door:

"You sure you want your name on the complaint? Once it's on, the Bellwethers will make your life a weather system."

"It's already raining," Ines said.

Pell almost smiled. "Yeah. It is." She walked Kettle out into the wet, one hand on his elbow, gentle as an usher.

* * *

By morning, it was worse than a weather system.

Cassian Bellwether did not deny the arrest. Denial would have been clumsy, and Cassian was never clumsy. Instead, by nine o'clock, the *Courier*'s website carried a statement so polished it squeaked: the Bellwether family was *shattered* to learn of Mr. Kettle's *long-suspected financial misconduct*, a betrayal of a trust the family had *generously maintained for a century out of pure historical devotion*. The words *devotion* and *century* did heavy lifting. Buried in the fifth paragraph, wearing the disguise of grief, came the knife: the family regretted that certain individuals—a retired investigator with a documented history of grievance against Marrow's Reach institutions—had chosen to exploit this painful moment to advance personal narratives and commercial interests, namely the continued operation of a certain debt-burdened bookshop.

By noon, the phrase *the disgraced detective* was being said aloud at the bakery. By two, Mrs. Aldous, who had bought her mysteries from Ines for nine years, stood at the counter and asked, not unkindly, whether it was true Ines had been run out of the city force. By four, the landlord's notice had been slipped under the shop door: thirty days, breach of lease terms, inquiries directed to a post office box that Ines recognized, with a lurch, as belonging to a Bellwether holding company.

Wren found the notice first. They read it twice, folded it into precise quarters, and set it on the counter between them without a word, and their face did something complicated—young and fierce and scared all at once, the look of a kid watching a second home catch fire.

"We'll fight it," Wren said finally.

"Yes."

"With what?"

Ines looked at the notice. At the shelves. At shelf nine, where a dead mentor's handwriting waited in the margins of a ledger that had been addressed, all along, to whoever kept this shop. Outside, the fog was coming in off the water early, swallowing the lane lamp by lamp, and somewhere across town Cassian Bellwether was raising a glass to a centennial built on a lie, confident that the flood would take the bookshop and spare the house.

"He wrote his statement weeks ago," Ines said. "Which means he thinks the story's finished." She picked up the notice and filed it, deliberately, in the drawer beneath the register, next to the unpaid invoices and the spare keys. "Stories aren't finished until somebody reads them out loud. And Odile Bellwether hasn't given hers yet."

Wren frowned. "You think she'll turn on him?"

"I think," Ines said, turning the CLOSED sign with a click that sounded louder than it was, "that a woman who has paid a keeper's trust out of her own pocket for forty years knows exactly what her nephew is—and that Friday night, in front of three hundred guests, one of us is going to tell the truth. The only question is who gets there first."

She locked the drawer. Above them, in the attic, the cot was still made up, and neither of them mentioned it, and neither of them needed to.

"Make tea," Ines said. "We've got five days."
