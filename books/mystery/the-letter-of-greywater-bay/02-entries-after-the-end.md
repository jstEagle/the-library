## 2. Entries After the End

The rain came in off the bay around nine, and by ten it had worked its way under my door in that sideways Greywater way, finding the gap beneath the threshold no matter how many times I'd stuffed it with weatherstripping. I lit the lamp on my desk instead of the overhead because the overhead buzzed, and buzzing was a disorder, and disorders were things I catalogued rather than suffered. That's what I told myself, anyway. The truth was simpler: some documents you read by lamplight. Some you read like a vigil.

Marina Voss's diary sat on the blotter where the cold-case box had been all afternoon. I had cleared everything else away—the pending claims, the correspondence, the invoice for the dredging company that had pulled the silt-crusted satchel from the harbor floor in March and mailed it to me with a note saying *found property, per policy 4471-C*. Even the box itself I'd moved to the shelf, because its smell of brine and diesel kept climbing into my nose and rearranging my thinking.

The diary was not leather. People always assume diaries are leather, with gilt edges and a ribbon marker, because grief likes to dress itself up. This one was a school composition book, the kind sold at the drugstore for a dollar ninety-nine, marbled black-and-white covers gone soft at the corners, the spine cracked into white seams like old porcelain. Someone had loved it hard. Someone had carried it against her body through weather.

I made tea I didn't drink. I put on gloves—not for forensics; this wasn't an active scene anymore, seven years of salt water had seen to that—but because touching other people's private pages barehanded has always felt to me like reading their mail over their shoulder. A formality of decency. My own small ceremony.

Then I opened it.

* * *

Her handwriting changed three times in the first forty pages, and I noted each shift the way a birdwatcher notes plumage. In the earliest entries—dated six years before her disappearance—it was round and dutiful, a young wife practicing penmanship the way she'd been taught, every letter sitting obediently on its ruled line. *Aldous took the board out on the Aurora today. Dinner with the Harmons. I burned the sauce again.* Sentences like inventory. Sentences like a woman writing from inside a display case.

By the middle of the book the letters had grown taller, looser, slanting left as though leaning away from something just off the page. The content loosened too. She wrote about the fog coming in so thick the ferry horn sounded like it was grieving. She wrote about a cormorant that dried its wings on the pilings every morning at the same hour, and how she timed her walks to meet it. She wrote, once, in a line that seemed to have escaped her before she could stop it: *He says I imagine things. I am starting to wonder if he is right, and if that is worse than him being wrong.*

I know that sentence. I have written versions of it in my head since I was eight years old, when Mrs. Calloway asked the class to name our favorite memory and I recited, verbatim, the argument my parents had behind the bedroom door eleven days earlier, word for word, including the sound of my father's belt buckle hitting the floor. I didn't understand then why the room went quiet, why my mother's face went the color of dishwater, why I was moved to the back row and then, eventually, out of birthday parties altogether. I only understood that I had seen something true and spoken it, and truth spoken at the wrong volume is a kind of violence.

So I read Marina Voss leaning away from her husband, letter by letter, and I recognized the posture.

* * *

The last entry anyone official ever saw was dated October 3rd. I knew this because the case file said so—or rather, because the case file's absence said so. There was no diary in the original investigation. There were witness statements, a coast guard report, a single recovered shoe found on the rocks below the Voss property, and a death certificate signed by Dr. Edmund Pell declaring cause of death as accidental drowning, body unrecovered. The file had crossed my desk years ago in the ordinary course of claims processing, and I had filed it in the archive of my head the way I file everything: completely, involuntarily, forever.

October 3rd. That was the date Marina Voss walked out of the world. Her coat on the beach. Her shoe on the rocks. The bay accepting her the way the bay accepts everything, without comment.

The entry dated October 3rd was short. *If you are reading this, I didn't come back. Tell Ruth the cormorant was right.* No explanation of who Ruth was. No farewell to Aldous. Just that, and then nothing—or so the world believed.

But I had turned past October 3rd, because turning past the end of things is my profession, and there were more pages. Twenty-two more days of them. Dated. Written in that same leftward-slanting hand, growing smaller and faster as the days went on, as though the words themselves were trying to take up less room in the world.

October 5th: *The grey house smells of kerosene and mouse. It is perfect. Nobody looks at the pilot's cottage anymore; they look at the water, always the water, because that is where they expect grief to be.*

October 7th: *Ruth came after dark with a basket. She didn't knock, just set it inside the door and said "eat" through the wood. Seven years I've known her and tonight is the first time I've heard her afraid.*

October 9th: *He counts the boats.*

That was the whole of it. Four words, written twice, the second time pressed so hard into the paper that the graphite had torn through. *He counts the boats. He counts the boats.* I sat with the lamp turned low and felt the sentence settle into my chest like a swallowed stone.

Because I knew what it meant. Anyone who has lived in a town built on water knows what it means. A man like Aldous Voss doesn't search for a missing wife—he audits for her. He walks the dock at dawn with his hands in his pockets and his eyes moving down the moorings, and he knows which skiff belongs to which family, and he knows if there's one extra hull riding low in the water somewhere, one engine running at an odd hour, one wake heading out toward the islands when no one has business out toward the islands. He counts the boats. And everyone in town knows he counts them, and so everyone keeps their boats exactly where he expects them to be, and the arithmetic of a whole harbor bends itself around one man's suspicion.

She hadn't drowned. She was hiding four hundred yards from her own memorial service, in a house the town had agreed not to see, waiting for a boat that would carry her out—and she was writing down, day by day, the terror of being counted.

* * *

I should have closed the book there. I want to be honest about that, even if honesty is a currency I've spent most of my life hoarding. Every instinct I have—the ones that kept me invisible for thirty-one years, the ones that taught me to memorize exits and never names, to know everything and volunteer nothing—every one of those instincts said: close the book, log the discrepancy, mail the summary to the regional office, let the machine do what machines do with anomalies, which is grind them smooth.

Instead I read on.

October 11th: *Saw his car pass the lane twice today. Slow both times. Ruth says he does that everywhere now, that grief gives a man license to loiter. She says it loud enough for neighbors to hear. She is better at this than I am.*

October 14th: *The ferry didn't come. Ives sent word it was "engine trouble." Third time. I don't think it's trouble. I think someone bought the engine.*

October 17th: *Wrote letters today and burned them. What is the point of a letter when every envelope gets weighed, every postmark gets read? He taught me that without ever teaching me that. You learn a marriage the way you learn a language—by immersion.*

October 19th: *I ate the last of the tinned peaches. Ridiculous thing to cry about. The cormorant came anyway.*

October 22nd: *Last page. If someone finds this, find Ruth Okafor first. She'll know whether it's safe. Don't trust the sheriff. Don't trust the church. Trust the woman who feeds people and the man who runs the boats badly enough to be beneath suspicion. And if it's Aldous holding this book—if you got it from the water, Aldous, if you're reading my handwriting by your good lamp—then know that I am nowhere you will ever think to count.*

The pencil lines ended there. Not trailed off. Ended, deliberate as a door closing.

I looked at the date. October 22nd. Twenty-two days after the town buried an empty coffin and the county filed a certificate and Aldous Voss began his long public performance of bereavement, his wife had been alive and hiding and hungry and writing, in a house I could probably see from my office window if the fog lifted.

And then the book had gone into the water somehow, and she had not.

* * *

I did what I always do with information that frightens me: I organized it. I took a fresh legal pad and drew the timeline, entry by entry, cross-referenced against tide tables and the ferry schedule from that autumn, which I remembered, because I remember everything, which is less a gift than a condition. The entries held together. They held together the way a well-built lie holds together, or a well-built truth—I couldn't yet tell the difference, and that was the part that kept the lamp burning past midnight.

Because here is the thing about a diary recovered from a harbor: it can be planted. Here is the thing about twenty-two days of entries in a dead woman's hand: dead women don't write, but living women who need to stay dead sometimes do, and so do living men who need a widow to stay dead. The document was either the most important thing ever dredged out of Greywater Bay or the most carefully placed. Both possibilities pointed at the same grey house. Both possibilities pointed at the same counting man.

I wrote *grey house — pilot's cottage — north shore?* on the pad and stared at it until the question mark seemed to mock me. Of course I knew where the pilot's cottage was. Everyone knew where the pilot's cottage was; that was the genius of it. It sat on the north shore above the old pilotage station, visible from the road, visible from the water, so thoroughly in plain sight that it had become furniture. I had catalogued it years ago along with every other structure in town: abandoned since the pilot service consolidated, roof intact, chimney standing, assessed value negligible. Filed under F, for forgotten.

Filed under F. Everything dangerous in this town was filed under F.

The rain had stopped. Through the window, the harbor lay flat and black, the boats at their moorings in their proper places, each one counted, each one accounted for. Somewhere out there a cormorant slept on a piling, keeping its own schedule, loyal to no one.

I looked at the final entry again. *Nowhere you will ever think to count.*

And then I noticed what I should have noticed hours ago, what my memory had flagged and my attention had deferred while the story carried me forward: the composition book had two hundred pages. The entries stopped on page 187. The remaining pages weren't blank.

They'd been torn out. Carefully, close to the spine, one by one—all except one, the very last, which someone had missed or meant to leave. A single page remained, and on it, in handwriting I did not recognize—heavier, hurried, a man's hand or a woman's in a terrible hurry—four words:

*Stop looking. Please.*

I set down the diary. I turned off the lamp. And in the dark of my office, listening to the harbor breathe, I understood that the case had stopped being paperwork somewhere around October 9th, and that whoever had torn out those pages wanted me to believe it never started.
