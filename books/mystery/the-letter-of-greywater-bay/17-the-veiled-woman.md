## 17. The Veiled Woman

The courthouse clock said four-forty when the woman in the veil stood up.

She had sat through the entire hearing the way furniture sits—present, unremarkable, part of the room's architecture. Back row, third seat from the aisle, a black scarf knotted under her chin and dark glasses even though the day outside was the color of dishwater. Jonah had catalogued her the way she catalogued everything: without wanting to, without being able to stop. The hands were wrong for the clothes. Working hands. A cook's hands or a fisherman's hands, knuckles broadened by decades of rope and heat, emerging from sleeves that were trying to be somebody's Sunday best. The shoes had been resoled twice. The veil itself was old-fashioned, the kind of thing that came out of an attic trunk, and it smelled faintly of camphor when she passed—Jonah caught it as the woman moved up the aisle ahead of her, mothballs and lavender and something underneath, something like woodsmoke and salt.

Judge Halloway hadn't signed yet. That was the thing Jonah kept returning to as she followed the woman down the courthouse steps into the fog-thick afternoon: *he hadn't signed yet.* The pen had been uncapped, the paper squared before him, Aldous Voss standing at the plaintiff's table with his grief arranged on his face like flowers at a funeral—and then the recess, called suddenly, some procedural question about notice requirements that the clerk couldn't answer. Seven days, the judge said. Come back in seven days with the paperwork complete.

Seven days. Marina Voss had been dead for seven years, and now she had seven days left to stay that way.

The woman turned left on Water Street. Not toward the parking lot. Not toward the bus stop. Toward the water, where the street gave out into gravel and gull noise and the long gray shoulder of the breakwater. Jonah hung back half a block, close enough to keep the camphor-and-woodsmoke thread of her, far enough to be one more figure in a town where everyone walked hunched against the wind. She had spent thirty-one years perfecting this—the art of being no one in particular, a woman with a folder under her arm going nowhere important. It was the only inheritance her memory had ever given her that she'd been glad of.

The veiled woman walked like someone who knew every crack in the pavement. That was the detail that made Jonah's chest tighten. Visitors looked around. Locals stepped over the broken flagstone by the chandlery without looking down, and this woman stepped over it without looking down, and then took the unmarked path behind the fuel dock that wasn't on any map Jonah had ever filed, the one that climbed through blackberries and rusted crab pots toward the headland.

Toward the grey house.

*He counts the boats,* the diary had said. Jonah counted her own footsteps instead—one hundred and forty from the fuel dock to the bend in the path—and told herself she was following a lead, doing her job, assembling evidence. But the truth was simpler and colder: she was following the only person in Greywater Bay who might be what everyone swore was impossible, and her heart was going like a gull's wings against the inside of her ribs.

The house appeared the way it always did in her mind when she thought of it now—gray shingles gone silver, windows like closed eyes, sitting above the waterline with the patience of something that had decided not to be seen. But today there was smoke. A thin ribbon of it rising from the chimney, straight up in the still air, visible for miles if anyone cared to look. Someone had been careless. Or someone had stopped caring whether they were found.

The veiled woman stopped at the gate.

She didn't go in. She stood with her hand on the latch, and then, without turning around, without raising her voice, she said:

"You walk quiet, but you breathe loud."

Jonah went still. Fog dripped off the blackberry leaves. Somewhere below, a boat engine turned over and faded.

"I've been listening for footsteps on that path for seven years," the woman said. "You learn the difference between a deer and a person. Deer don't hesitate." She turned then, and lifted the glasses, and lifted the veil, and the face beneath it—

Jonah had seen photographs. Claim photos, news photos, the smiling woman at the christening of the *Aldous IV*, pearls at her throat, everything curated and lit. This face had the same architecture underneath—same cheekbones, same level gray eyes—but the sea had gotten into it. Weather had gotten into it. She was fifty-three and looked like she had earned every year of it honestly, out of doors, in all kinds of light. Her hair was iron-gray and tied back with what appeared to be a piece of net twine.

"You're her," Jonah said. It came out barely above breath. "Marina Voss."

"No." The woman's voice was flat and hard as beach stone. "There's no such person. You watched them bury that name last week—a box of wet sand and Dr. Pell's good intentions. Whoever you think you followed up this hill, she doesn't exist, and neither does whatever you're carrying in that folder." Her eyes dropped to the folder under Jonah's arm, and something flickered across her face—not fear exactly. Recognition. "Let me see it."

"It's a copy."

"Of course it is. You're the kind who makes copies." Something almost like respect crossed her face and was gone. "I know your type. I married your type. Men—and women—who keep records. Who think a thing written down is a thing made true." She pushed the gate open. "Inside. If we're going to do this, we do it where the walls are mine."

* * *

The grey house smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and, beneath it, the sea itself, soaked so deep into the boards that the whole structure seemed less built than grown. Inside it was one room and a loft, a cast-iron stove, a table scarred white with knife work, shelves of jars catching the gray window-light like a wall of small amber lanterns. On the table lay a mending basket, a coil of line, and a paperback novel with a bus ticket tucked into it as a bookmark—unused, creased soft with handling, dated seven years ago.

Jonah knew the ticket. She had held its twin in a cache beneath the floorboards three weeks ago. Seeing its partner here, worn smooth by a thumb, did something to her throat.

"Sit," the woman said. She didn't sit herself. She stood with her back to the stove, arms folded, and studied Jonah with the unhurried attention of someone gutting a fish—looking for the spine. "Ruth Okafor saw you at the ferry landing Tuesday. Ives says a woman's been asking questions at the diner. And yesterday somebody went through my cache under the floor like a raccoon with a law degree." Her jaw tightened. "That was you."

"Yes."

"You took her ring."

"I have it. Safe."

"That ring is *evidence*, is what it is. That ring is a dead woman's ring, and a dead woman can't wear it, and the day anyone sees it on a living hand—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again her voice had dropped, gone quieter and more dangerous. "Do you understand what you've been doing? Walking around my town with my diary in your briefcase? Asking my friends questions they've had to lie about for seven years? Every interview you do, every record you request, you're leaving a trail, and Aldous Voss has people whose whole job is reading trails."

"I know."

"Do you?" She came forward, and up close her eyes were the exact color of the bay in February, and just as cold. "Then tell me why. Tell me why an insurance investigator from—" she glanced at the folder, read the letterhead upside down without touching it, another thing they apparently shared— "from Meridian Mutual spends her evenings digging up a settled claim nobody paid her to reopen. Nobody hired you. I checked. Ruth checked. So either you're working for him, in which case you're more dangerous than his fixer, or you're working for yourself, in which case you're more dangerous than anybody, because he can't buy you and I can't predict you."

The stove ticked. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like something listening.

"I'm not working for anyone," Jonah said. "Not anymore. I handed in my report on the Voss claim seven years ago. Drowning, accidental, case closed. It was the first file I ever closed without checking every page." She heard her own voice come out level, which surprised her; inside, something was shaking loose. "I didn't check because I didn't want to know. That's the only dishonest thing I've ever done, and I've regretted it for seven years in a way I couldn't name until I read the diary. The entries after the end. Twenty-two days of a dead woman writing about the weather." She looked down at the folder. "I don't want anything from you. I wanted the truth to exist somewhere, even if only in me. That's how I've always kept things. In here." She touched her temple, awkward, aware of how it sounded. "I remember everything. Everything I've ever read, signed, filed, overheard. It isn't a gift. It's a room I can't leave. And for thirty-one years the only way to survive in it was to make sure nobody looked at me, because people who are looked at get asked questions, and I remember the answers whether I want to or not."

The woman had gone very still.

"The death certificate," Jonah continued, quietly. "I processed it. July, seven years ago. Signed by Dr. Edwin Pell. I remembered it the way I remember everything—every hesitation mark in the signature, the ink feathering where his hand shook. Three months later I filed Pell's obituary. Heart attack, aged sixty-four. Except I stood in line behind him at the pharmacy six weeks after the date on the certificate, and he asked me for the time, and I gave it to him, and I never put those two facts together because I didn't let myself." She looked up. "I'm not here to expose you. I'm here because I finally understood that pretending not to know things is just a slower way of drowning."

For a long moment the only sound was the fog dripping from the eaves, patient as a metronome.

Then Marina Voss—or whoever she was now—pulled out the other chair and sat down, slowly, like someone setting down a weight carried a long way.

"Pell used to say you reminded him of me," she said. "He wrote that in a letter he never sent. He was going to give it to you someday, he told Ruth, if you ever came asking. Said the girl with the memory deserved to know she wasn't alone in the world." Her mouth twisted. "I hated that letter. I hate that he saw it. Because he was right, and I know exactly what he saw." She leaned forward. "You erase yourself so completely that you've become very good at seeing other people who've done the same. That's what happened at the hearing today, isn't it. Everyone watched Aldous cry, and you watched me."

"I memorized your hands," Jonah said. "Working hands. The veil was attic camphor. You stepped over the broken flagstone by the chandlery."

Something broke open in the woman's face then—not tears, nothing so simple, but a settling, like a house deciding to stay standing. "Seven years," she said. "Seven years of being no one, and the first stranger who looks at me for ten seconds knows me better than my husband did in twenty." She laughed, short and bitter, and rubbed her eyes with those working hands. "Ruth will say I should feed you. Ruth says that about hurricanes. Don't tell her I didn't."

"He's going to win," Jonah said. "In seven days. Unless—"

"Unless nothing." Marina stood, sudden, and went to the window, her back a rigid line. "You think I haven't run the arithmetic? The trust dissolves. The judge signs. Aldous gets everything he strangled twenty years of marriage to reach, and I stay dead, and Ruth keeps bringing soup, and Ives keeps drinking himself quiet, and Pell stays in his grave holding our secret like a stone." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, Jonah could see her hating it. "That was the bargain. That's what staying hidden *costs*, and I've paid it every single day, because the alternative was his fixer finding this house and finding Ruth and finding Ives, and I will not—"

"Aldous forged the certificate."

Marina went silent.

"The original is in a marina locker—Ives's key, locker 114. Pell signed the real one, dated the day you 'drowned.' Someone added a second certification afterward, different ink, different hand, backdated. I have it memorized. Line by line. Registration number, timestamp, the pen pressure." Jonah rose, and her voice was steady now, steadier than it had been in years, because this was the one language she had ever spoken fluently. "It isn't testimony yet. Testimony needs a witness willing to stand in a courtroom and be looked at. But it exists, Marina. It exists in a place Aldous can't search, can't buy, can't burn down. It exists in me."

Marina turned from the window. The fog-light made her face unreadable, silver and shadow.

"And if he comes for you," she said, "the way he came for Pell? You think a good memory stops a man who owns the sheriff and half the fleet?"

"No," Jonah said. "But it stops him owning the truth. There's a difference. I spent my whole life learning it."

Outside, down the headland path, a gull screamed once and cut off. Marina's head turned toward the sound—sharp, automatic, a wild animal's motion—and Jonah saw it plainly then, the thing seven years had built into the woman's bones: the permanent listening. The flinch tuned to engines on the water road, to footsteps, to voices raised anywhere within a mile.

And then they both heard it. Not a gull. An engine, low and deliberate, coming up the coast road too slowly for a passerby, stopping somewhere below the bluff with a diesel idle that hung in the fog like a held breath.

Marina blew out the stove.

"They know about the house," she whispered. "Get down."
