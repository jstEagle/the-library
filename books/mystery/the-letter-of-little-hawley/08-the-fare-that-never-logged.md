## 8. The Fare That Never Logged

The morning after I cataloged my own life like evidence, I did something I almost never did: I slept. Not well, and not long, but I slept, which for a night dispatcher is roughly the equivalent of a religious experience. I woke at noon to gray light and the smell of my own coffee gone bitter in the pot, and I lay there staring at the ceiling, thinking about the one item on my list that didn't fit.

Everything stolen from my apartment made sense. The jacket, the scarf, the gloves — those were costume pieces, a Mirabel Kavanagh kit assembled by someone who knew me only as a silhouette. The photograph of my mother was cruelty, plain and simple, a message that said *we were inside your home, we saw the soft parts of you.* But the taxi logbook — my father's logbook, 1989 through 1992, a water-stained green ledger I'd kept in a shoebox under the bed for thirty years without opening it twice — that made no sense at all. Unless the thief hadn't known what it was. Unless it had simply been in the closet, and closets get emptied wholesale when a stranger is dressing himself in your life.

I told myself that. I made coffee and told myself that, and the coffee did not believe me, and neither did I.

Here is what I knew by then, laid out in the cold clarity I'd earned over three solved deaths the county called accidents: Lorna Pike had requested a copy of the 1992 file — the file on Dr. Ellen Marsh, found at the bottom of the stairwell at St. Ambrose Clinic, ruled a fall, flagged by a nineteen-year-old dispatcher who noticed the ambulance was called eleven minutes after the responding officer wrote the word *accident* in his log. The responding officer's name had been redacted from every copy I'd ever seen. The evidence clerk who controlled those copies was Edmund Voss. And the statute of limitations on whatever crime had been committed in that stairwell was closing like a door.

Which left the witness. Every case has a witness. In the 1992 file, softened and sanded until she was barely a person anymore, she was listed as *Mrs. H. Okafor, neighbor, saw nothing of note.*

Nobody sees nothing of note. That's not how witnessing works. You see everything of note, and then someone helps you forget it, and the helping is the crime.

Mrs. Henrietta Okafor, according to the county's assisted living directory — a document I had memorized the way other men memorize box scores — lived at Rosewood Commons, out past the shuttered cannery, in a brick building that had been a sanatorium before it was a care home, which is the kind of joke Little Hawley likes to make without knowing it's joking.

I called in sick to Delmar, who owned the cab company and had owned it since before my father drove for him, and who said, "You're never sick," and I said, "I know," and he said, "Fine, don't tell me anything," which is Delmar's way of loving people.

Then I put on the least memorable clothes I owned — a man in my line of side work develops a wardrobe like a chameleon develops opinions — and drove a company car out to Rosewood Commons, because a taxi in the parking lot of a care home is invisible. Nobody wonders why a taxi is anywhere. A taxi is the town's furniture.

* * *

Rosewood Commons smelled like lavender and floor wax and, underneath it, faintly, like 1974. The woman at the desk had a name tag that said BEV and a manner that said she had seen every version of every visitor there ever was.

"Visiting hours start at two," she said.

"It's ten past one," I said.

"It starts at two," she agreed pleasantly, and then looked at me, really looked, the way desk people do, sorting you into a drawer. "Who are you here for?"

"Mrs. Okafor."

The sorting took a moment longer than it should have. "You family?"

"Old family friend," I said. "My father knew her. I'm passing through."

"Passing through Little Hawley," Bev said, with the flat wonder of a woman who had heard that sentence used as a lie approximately four thousand times. "Nobody passes through Little Hawley. They come here on purpose or they're lost." She slid a visitor's log across the desk. "Sign in. And I'll need to let her nurse know, because Mrs. Okafor has — routines."

"Routines," I repeated.

"Mrs. Okafor has said the same thing at three o'clock every afternoon for eleven years," Bev said. "You'll see." There was something in her voice — not warning, exactly. More like the tired tenderness of someone who has watched a locked door for a long time and stopped asking who locked it.

I signed *M. Kavanagh* in a handwriting that was not mine, which is a skill I don't advertise, and sat in a lobby with a plastic ficus and a television playing a game show with the volume at a level designed to be heard by the deaf and the dead. At five past two, a nurse with kind eyes and a lanyard full of cartoon animals walked me down a corridor that smelled increasingly of vanilla and said, "She'll like you or she won't, but either way she'll tell you the same thing. She always tells everybody the same thing."

"What thing?"

"You'll see," the nurse said again, and knocked on a door with a silk flower wreath on it, and called out, "Henrietta, you've got a visitor," in the bright, projected voice of a woman who has learned that the elderly are not deaf so much as selective.

* * *

Mrs. Henrietta Okafor was small and neat in a wingback chair by the window, with a cardigan the color of butter and hands folded in her lap like she was posing for a portrait titled *Patience.* Her eyes were quick, though. Quicker than I'd expected. Ninety-some years old and her eyes went to my face, my hands, my shoes, and back to my face, in the order of a woman who had once assessed strangers for a living — or at least for a night, once, a long time ago.

"You're not from the church," she said.

"No, ma'am."

"You're not from the county."

"No, ma'am."

"Then you're either selling something or asking something." She gestured at the room's second chair with the graciousness of a duchess. "Sit down, whichever you are. The selling ones don't sit. The asking ones always do."

I sat. "Mrs. Okafor, my name is—"

"I know who you are," she said, and my blood did something complicated. "You've got his chin. The Kavanagh chin. Like a shovel with opinions." She smiled, and the smile was warm and terrible at the same time, the way old smiles are when they've been waiting decades. "Sammy Kavanagh's boy. You used to come by the house with him when you were small. You ate all my lemon drops and apologized for it in complete sentences. Four years old, talking like a lawyer."

I had no memory of this. I had no memory of it, and it was the most real thing anyone had said to me in years.

"You knew my father," I said, when I could.

"Knew him?" She laughed, a dry rustle. "Sammy Kavanagh drove me to church every Sunday for six years and to the clinic every Tuesday for longer than that, and he never once took a fare for it, and he never once let me thank him properly, because he said thanks were for strangers and we were colleagues. Colleagues. Me and a taxi man, colleagues." Her eyes shone. "That man had more manners than the whole town council and more secrets than both of them put together."

"Did he," I said carefully. "Have secrets."

"Everyone has secrets, child. The question is what kind." She leaned forward, and something shifted in her face — a door closing behind her eyes, a recitation beginning. "Now. If you're here about that poor doctor, I've told my story. I told it in 1992 and I've told it since, and it's the truth, and I'm tired of it, but I'll tell it again because it's the truth."

"Okay," I said.

And she told it. Word for word.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is my religion: Mrs. Henrietta Okafor recited her statement the way a choir recites a hymn. Same cadence, same pauses, same little laugh at the same spot. *"I saw Dr. Marsh that evening on the clinic steps, she was in good spirits, she waved to me from the top of the stairs, and I went inside, and later I heard she had fallen. It was a tragedy. These old buildings, the stairs are steep. I saw nothing of note."*

*I saw nothing of note.* The exact phrase from the file. Not a paraphrase — the file's own language, worn smooth, coming out of a living mouth.

I let her finish. I let the silence sit. Then I said, "Mrs. Okafor, that's a lovely story. Who taught it to you?"

The quickness came back into her eyes. For one second — one full, unguarded second — she looked at me the way a bird looks at an open window. Then the shutters came down, gentle and total.

"Taught it to me?" she said. "It's what happened. It's what I saw."

"Henrietta." I said her first name deliberately, softly, the way my father would have. "Dr. Marsh waved to you from the top of the stairs. Think about that. You were at the bottom. She was at the top, by the door. How did she wave to you from up there if you were already going inside?"

The silence stretched. Her hands, folded in her lap, had gone very still.

"Steep stairs," she said finally, but the hymn had a crack in it now. "These old buildings."

"Henrietta, did someone come to see you after it happened? Someone kind? Someone who said he was helping?"

"Everyone was kind," she said. "Everyone was so kind." And there it was — the horror inside the tenderness, braided so tight you couldn't tell which strand was which. She said *kind* the way prisoners say *walls.* "A gentleman came. He was very concerned for me. He said the town was grieving and I shouldn't be troubled, that he would handle the questions so I could rest. He came every week for a while. Brought me lemon drops."

My stomach turned over. "Lemon drops."

"He knew I liked them," she said simply. "He knew everything about me. It was like he'd studied me. It made me feel safe." She looked out the window at the parking lot, at my taxi sitting there in plain view. "Safe," she said again, and the word had rusted through.

* * *

We sat a while without talking. Outside, a groundskeeper was fighting a losing war with a hedge. I was turning over how to ask the next question when she asked one of her own.

"You'll think me foolish," she said. "But who is it that's asking after all this, after so long? Is it the police?"

"It's someone who loved her," I said. "Someone who loved the truth."

"Mm." She studied me. "Your father loved the truth too. It got on my nerves sometimes, if you want the honest account of it. Sammy Kavanagh could not let a thing be wrong. A fare misprinted in his book, he'd cross it out and rewrite the whole page so it was clean. I used to tell him, Sammy, the tax man doesn't care, and he'd say, *I* care, Henrietta. The book is the truth of the day. You log the day or the day never happened." She smiled at the memory, then the smile faltered into something else. "He stopped logging, near the end. Did you know that? Last few months of his life. I'd ask him about it and he'd get this look. He said some fares don't go in the book."

My heart was doing something I'd have called arrhythmia if I were a weaker man. "What kind of fare doesn't go in the book?"

"Off-book fares," she said. "Everybody did them, cash jobs, but Sammy hated them. Said they were how men lied to themselves." Her eyes came up to mine, and they were clear as winter water. "The last one he ever mentioned to me — and he mentioned it because it frightened him, I could see it frightened him — was the night of the doctor. He drove someone home from the clinic that night. Someone who wasn't supposed to be there. He told me he'd logged it and then he told me he hadn't, and I never knew which was the truth, and then he was gone that winter, and it didn't seem to matter anymore."

"It matters," I said. "Henrietta, it matters more than anything."

"Does it." She looked at her hands. "Then I'll tell you the last thing, and then I'm going to ring for my tea, because this has been a long afternoon and I'm ninety-one years old and entitled."

"Please."

"Your father had a name. Not Samuel, not Sammy. The people who really knew him — his mother, and me, and one or two others — we called him something else. Something from when he was small." She said it gently, watching my face. "He was *Miro* to us. Little Miro. Because he was always looking, even as a boy. Watching everything, missing nothing. His mother said he was born with his eyes open."

The room went very quiet. I could hear the hedge war continuing outside, the shears going *snick, snick* like a slow clock.

Nobody knew that name. It wasn't in any file — I'd have found it; I'd read everything the county had on my father's death twice, looking for a crack, and there was no *Miro* anywhere. It lived in exactly two places: the memory of an old woman, and — until this moment — nowhere else at all.

And she had given it to me freely, this woman who could not deviate one word from a statement she'd been fed, this woman kept safe and kept silent by the same kindness for thirty years. She'd handed me the one thing that proved she remembered the real night underneath the rehearsed one. She'd handed it to me the way you slip a note to a prisoner through bars — quietly, and trusting the visitor to be the right kind of visitor.

"Henrietta," I said, "when the kind gentleman came with his lemon drops. Did he ever tell you what to say?"

"Oh, no," she said. "He never told me what to say." She smiled at me, sweet as the tea she hadn't rung for yet. "He just told me what *had* happened. Over and over. Until I remembered it properly."

* * *

I drove back through town with the windows down and October coming through them like a rumor. At a red light on Corliss Street I sat with my father's absence sitting shotgun, thirty years in the grave and still teaching me things.

*Miro.* She'd said *Miro* and the world had rearranged itself. Because if she remembered the nickname, she remembered the night — the real one, the one under the varnish — and if she remembered the night, then somewhere under thirty years of lemon drops and gentle corrections, the truth was still in there, intact, like a coin at the bottom of a well. All I had to do was find the rope.

And there was the other thing, the thing I kept circling like a moth at a porch light: the last fare. My father drove someone home from the clinic the night Dr. Marsh died, a fare that frightened him, a fare that may or may not have been in the book. The book. The green ledger in the shoebox under my bed, the one item the thief took that made no sense as a costume piece and perfect sense as evidence.

I'd assumed the logbook was taken at random. I'd told myself a comforting story, the way Henrietta had been told hers.

I pulled into the lot behind the dispatch office at 4:40 in the afternoon, and Delmar was standing in the doorway holding an envelope, and his face was the face of a man who has opened mail he wishes he hadn't.

"This came for you," he said. "Not the business. You." He handed it over. "No return address. And Mirabel—" he hesitated, and for once Delmar, who never hesitated, hesitated, "—it's typed. On a typewriter. Who types letters anymore?"

I turned the envelope over in my hands. My name and the dispatch office address, centered, in the flat gray ink of a ribbon run nearly dry. I knew that ink. I knew that alignment. I had spent fifteen years producing exactly this.

I slit it open with my thumbnail, and read my own words coming back to me like an echo from the bottom of a well — and every one of them was a lie with my signature on it.

The letter said I had killed Lorna Pike.
