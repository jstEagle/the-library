## 17. Five Days

Ruiz had been a cop long enough to know that the law was not a sword. It was a filing cabinet. You could hit someone over the head with it, technically, but mostly it just sat there, heavy and indifferent, while you begged it to open.

The filing cabinet opened on Tuesday morning at 9:14 a.m., and out of it came Voss's lawyer, a silver-haired man named Delacroix who smelled like money that had been laundered twice.

"Detective Ruiz." He set a leather folder on her desk with the tenderness of a man laying flowers on a grave. Hers. "My client requests that all materials derived from the interview of Mr. Daniel Okafor be excluded from any proceeding. The statement was obtained without counsel present, under conditions of significant psychological pressure, from a young man who — I'll be blunt — was frightened of you."

"He wasn't frightened of me," Ruiz said. "He was frightened of Edmund Voss. There's a difference, and it's the whole point."

"Be that as it may." Delacroix smiled the smile of a man who had never once lost an argument he'd been paid to win. "Mr. Okafor has since retained independent representation and recanted."

"Recanted."

"Recanted." He let the word sit there like a dead fish. "He now states that he entered the records room after hours to retrieve personal effects, became confused by your questions, and agreed to statements he did not understand. He's twenty-four, Detective. Confusion is practically his native language."

Ruiz watched him go and then put her forehead down on her desk, which was not professional, but which no one saw except the ficus, and the ficus had seen worse.

* * *

By noon, the Okafor statement was gone. Not weakened — gone. Quashed, sealed, and treated by the DA's office as though it had never existed, like a photograph you burn because the person in it turned out to be terrible. The chain-of-custody theory — the beautiful, elegant theory that thirty years of "corrected" documents constituted Voss's sworn testimony, one alteration at a time — was suddenly built on sand. Or rather, on paper that Voss himself had touched last, which meant it was built on Voss.

"They're saying the personnel history is inadmissible too," Sergeant Boyle told her, appearing at her desk with two coffees and the expression of a man delivering a eulogy. "Chain of custody through his own office? Delacroix says it proves nothing except that records pass through a records office. Which is, you know. What records offices are for."

"That's circular."

"That's legal." Boyle handed her a coffee. "Different thing entirely. Also — and I'm sorry to be the guy who tells you this — the captain wants to see you."

The captain's office had a window that looked out on the parking lot, which Ruiz had always considered a fair metaphor for the job. Captain Marsh was behind his desk with his reading glasses pushed up into what remained of his hair, and he did not ask her to sit, which was its own kind of sentence.

"Ruiz."

"Captain."

"You reopened a 1992 case against direct advisement. You conducted an off-book interview of a town employee. You have been named — informally, so far — in a complaint filed by the town attorney regarding investigative conduct." He took off his glasses, folded them, unfolded them. "I'm suspending you. Administrative leave, pending review. Two weeks minimum."

"Two weeks," Ruiz said. "The statute expires in five days."

"I know how many days it expires in."

"Do you?" She heard her own voice go flat and cold, the voice she used on suspects, and knew she should stop, and did not stop. "Because from where I'm sitting, sir, it looks like everyone in this building knows exactly how many days are left, and everyone is very comfortable watching them go by. Edmund Voss corrected this town's records for thirty years. He killed Lorna Pike. He probably killed a doctor in 1992 and Mirabel Kavanagh's father besides, and we are going to lose all of it because a lawyer said the word 'recanted' in a nice suit."

Marsh was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped out of captain-register into something more human, which was somehow worse.

"You think I don't want him? Ada. I've known Edmund Voss for twenty-six years. He ran the toy drive." He rubbed his eyes. "That's the hell of it. He ran the toy drive. And every instinct I have says you're right, and every rule I have says you're off the case, and if I let you keep going and Delacroix gets one whiff of procedural error, everything you found dies with the statute anyway. Suspension isn't me giving up. It's me keeping what you built alive long enough for the DA to use it."

"And if the DA doesn't use it?"

"Then in five days, it's over, and I'll buy you a drink and tell you I'm sorry, and we'll both hate ourselves efficiently for the rest of our careers." He slid a form across the desk. "Badge and gun stay. That's standard."

She signed it. Her signature looked angry even to her.

* * *

Suspension, it turned out, was a strange country. She slept until seven, which felt decadent and wrong. She cleaned her apartment, which took forty minutes because she was not a person who accumulated mess, only cases. By Wednesday afternoon she had reorganized her kitchen cabinets by frequency of use and was seriously considering alphabetizing her spices when she caught herself and went for a walk instead, because she could feel the edge coming, the particular madness of a cop with no case, and the last time she'd felt it she'd ended up re-grouting her bathtub at 2 a.m.

She walked past the dispatch office on Calloway Street without meaning to. The Little Hawley Taxi Company occupied a narrow storefront between a nail salon and a place that claimed to sell mattresses but appeared, from the window display, to sell only one mattress, forever. Through the glass she could see the dispatch desk, the old radio rack, the corkboard of maps — and Mirabel Kavanagh, hunched over his console like a man praying to a machine that answered rarely and in riddles.

She told herself she was just walking by. Then she was inside.

"We're closed to walk-ins," Mirabel said without looking up. "Unless you need a cab, in which case we're open, but I'd advise against it, because our newest driver takes corners like they owe him money."

"I've been suspended."

He looked up then. He had the kind of face that did its feeling quietly — you had to watch the hands, the stillness, the way he set down his pen with exaggerated care, like a man putting down something fragile.

"Sit down," he said, and pulled a chair out from beside the desk with his foot.

She sat. The office smelled like burnt coffee and old upholstery and, faintly, like rain on asphalt — the smell of a thousand nights of calls.

"Delacroix quashed the Okafor statement," she said. "Recanted. The chain-of-custody angle is dead. Personnel file's tainted. And Marsh suspended me, which — honestly — might be the only move that keeps anything salvageable, but it doesn't feel that way from inside my kitchen, where I have now organized my spices."

"Alphabetical?"

"Frequency of use."

"Amateur," Mirabel said, and for a second they were just two tired people in a taxi office, and Ruiz laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of her — and realized she couldn't remember the last time she'd laughed on duty. That was the trouble with suspension. It gave you back the parts of yourself the job had been holding in escrow.

"The statute's in five days," she said. "Friday midnight. After that, the 1992 obstruction charge evaporates, and the murder charge for Lorna rests entirely on whatever physical evidence we have, which is thin, and whatever testimony we have, which is an eighty-nine-year-old woman whose memory has been edited for three decades like a manuscript."

"So we need Voss to say it out loud."

"We need Voss to say it out loud to someone who can prove it happened." She looked at him. "And before you say anything noble and stupid: no wire, no warrant, no admissibility. A secret recording of a private conversation won't survive Delacroix. We'd need consent. One-party consent doesn't apply if—"

"I know the law," Mirabel said mildly. "I've spent fifteen years studying what holds up and what doesn't. It's why my tips were always structured like evidence packages. Dates, sources, corroboration. Nobody ever followed up on them, but they were *clean*."

There was a silence. In it, Ruiz heard the thing she'd been circling for weeks: this man had done her job better than her job did, alone, unpaid, in the dark, and the system had rewarded him with a crank-file designation and a nickname among detectives — "the Night Clerk" — that nobody used kindly.

"How many did you actually solve?" she asked. She'd asked it before, in another tone. This time it was a real question.

"Nine," he said. "Ruled accidents or overdoses. Three tied to Voss directly. Six just... wrong, and fixed by nobody, because the corrections stopped mattering once the files were tidy." He turned his pen over in his fingers. "I used to think the silence was the price of doing it right. Now I think the silence was just another correction. My own."

Before she could answer — before she could decide whether to comfort him or arrest him, a tension she was growing accustomed to — the phone on the dispatch console rang.

Mirabel glanced at the caller ID and went very still.

"It's the front desk," he said. "We don't have a front desk. We have a phone and me." He picked up. "Little Hawley Taxi."

Ruiz watched his face do something complicated. He listened for maybe ten seconds, said "Understood," and hung up with the care of a man defusing something.

"What," she said.

He held up a finger, crossed to the front door, looked both ways down Calloway Street like a man checking for weather, then locked the door and flipped the sign to CLOSED. When he came back, he was holding an envelope. Cream-colored. Hand-addressed. Expensive.

"This came by courier ten minutes ago," he said. "Left at the nail salon next door, addressed to me here. The girl thought it was a tip." He sat down across from her. "It's not a tip."

"Open it."

He opened it. Inside was a single card, heavy stock, and beneath it — Ruiz leaned forward — a voucher. An actual Little Hawley Taxi voucher, the old printed kind, pre-paid fare, good for one ride anywhere in town. Dated tomorrow night. Signed at the bottom in a small, precise, schoolmaster's hand:

*E. Voss.*

Mirabel read the card aloud, his voice level in the way voices get when they're holding a lot of weight very carefully.

"*Mr. Kavanagh — You have studied me for years; I confess I have studied you longer. I believe we have arrived at the part where we speak plainly. One fare. No police. Friday evening, before midnight, when the town's long arithmetic finally closes. Come alone, and we will discuss what Little Hawley owes you. — E.V.*"

Ruiz stared at the voucher. A summons. A gentleman's invitation to a confession with no witnesses, scheduled neatly inside the shrinking window of the law, like a man scheduling his own acquittal.

"He knows about the suspension," she said slowly. "He knows I'm off the case. He knows the statement's quashed. He knows the clock is at five days. This—" she tapped the card "—this is a man who thinks he's already won and wants to enjoy the winning."

"He wants an audience," Mirabel said. "He's always wanted an audience. Thirty years of correcting records and telling no one — do you know what that costs a man? All that work, invisible. He sent me his handwriting. His *voucher*. He's signing his work now."

"Or it's a trap."

"Of course it's a trap." Mirabel smiled, and there was nothing warm in it, and Ruiz understood suddenly why nine families had gotten anonymous letters that changed their lives. "But it's a trap with a door in it. He wants me there. Which means he'll talk. Which means—"

"Which means if you go alone, whatever he says is worth nothing legally, and if you're wearing a wire, Delacroix will shred it, and if you bring me, he walks, and if you don't go—" she stopped.

"If I don't go," Mirabel finished quietly, "he spends the rest of his retirement knowing I blinked. And Lorna Pike stays a girl who drowned by accident in a jacket that belonged to a dispatcher nobody ever noticed." He set the voucher down on the console, square with the desk's edge, the way he squared everything. "Five days."

"Five days," Ruiz agreed. Then, because she was suspended and off the case and officially a civilian in a cardigan standing in a taxi office, she said the thing she'd been not-saying since the pond, since the tapes, since the letter written in his typewriter's broken e:

"Whatever you're planning — plan it with me. Not because I can authorize it. Because I can't anymore, and that means for the first time in this whole rotten business, neither of us can." She held out her hand. "Off-book. Both of us. If it goes wrong, we go down together, and I'd genuinely like the company."

Mirabel looked at her hand for a long moment. Outside, the rain started again, soft on Calloway Street, and somewhere across town a records room light was almost certainly burning late.

He shook it.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "One fare. But Ada — we're not going to Miller's Pond to listen to him."

"No?"

"We're going to make sure that whatever he says, the town hears it. Every word. Even if the law can't." He reached under the console and lifted out something she hadn't noticed before: an ancient portable tape recorder, its plastic yellowed with age, a strip of masking tape on the side reading *NIGHT LOG — DO NOT ERASE* in his father's handwriting.

"His company," Mirabel said. "His rules. His archive."
