## 17. One Name Short

The crypt smelled of cold limestone and old candle smoke, and Kestrel stood in it for a long time before she could make her hands move.

Merrin's notes lay open on the stone shelf where she'd found them, tucked behind the niche that had held Ambrose Vane's true remains — his actual bones, wrapped in canvas, disturbed recently enough that the dust still showed the drag marks of careful hands. The notes were written in Merrin's small, upright hand, the same hand Kestrel had admired a hundred times on archive index cards. *Such beautiful penmanship,* she'd thought once. *The kind they don't teach anymore.*

Now the penmanship read like a sentence being passed.

Kestrel had spread the pages across the shelf by the light of her phone, and she read them again from the top, because the first time through she had been shaking too hard to trust her own eyes.

The ledger's cipher, decoded further than Kestrel had ever managed. Dates she hadn't yet mapped. Names she hadn't yet paired. And then, on the last sheet, a column headed in Merrin's neat capitals: **PENDING.**

Three names.

*Corliss, R.* — struck through, with a note beside it: *cooperative. deferred.*

*Wrenn, I.* — no strike. A date written next to it. The date of the anniversary dinner.

And beneath it, in the same unhurried script, as if it were one more archival task among many:

*Coldharbour, K.*

Kestrel read her own name and felt nothing for a moment — a white silence, a place where her mind simply declined to go. Then her body answered for her. Her knees softened. She caught the edge of the shelf, and the stone bit her palm, and the small sharp pain was somehow the thing that brought the world back.

*Reinterment after the toast.* That was the phrase Merrin had used, in parentheses, as though it were a joke only the two of them would understand. As though it were a kindness. Kestrel understood it now, understood it the way you understand a trapdoor only after the floor gives: the anniversary dinner was not a celebration. It was a schedule. Two hundred people gathered in one hall, glasses raised, attention fixed on a stage — the perfect hour for two women to slip out of the story of Saint Alder's forever.

She had found the notes at four in the afternoon. She did not know how long she had been standing there. The light through the crypt's high vent had gone from gold to grey.

She gathered the pages with hands that would not quite obey her, photographed every one twice, replaced them exactly as they'd lain — Merrin would know, Merrin would *always* know, but there was nothing to be done about that now — and climbed the crypt stairs into the last of the daylight.

The cemetery spread out below her, two hundred years of Saint Alder's in ranked and weathered stone. She knew every grave. She had said so in her own head a hundred times, had worn the knowledge like a medal. *I know every grave in this town by name.*

She looked out at them now, at the long rows going soft-edged in the dusk, and the knowledge turned over and showed its other face.

*Every grave by name. And some of them empty. And some of them full of the wrong people. And one of them, if Merrin Hale had her way, waiting for me.*

Kestrel Coldharbour walked down through the cemetery past the Vane obelisk, past the founding-family plots with their granite grandeur, past the plain stones of the people who had lived and died here without ever being asked what they knew — and she did not feel like a coroner walking her rounds. She felt like a woman crossing a field at night, in a country that had always been at war, only now understanding which side of the line she'd been born on.

* * *

She did not go home.

She thought about it — the impulse rose in her like a tide, *go home, lock the door, pack a bag* — and she made herself stand at the corner of Alder and Second under the old gas-style lamp and reason it through, because if she let the tide carry her she would do exactly what fear wanted, and fear, she was beginning to understand, was the instrument Merrin worked with. Not a knife. Not a gun. Fear, applied slowly, in the right place, the way water finds the crack in a foundation.

If she went home, she'd pack. If she packed, she'd hesitate over her mother's things. If she hesitated, she'd be an hour later than she should be. And if Merrin was watching — and Merrin was always watching; the archive, the crypt, the margins of every conversation for nine years — then a light burning late in the Coldharbour house would tell her everything she needed to know.

So Kestrel turned the other way and walked, unhurried, a coroner taking an evening constitutional, past the bakery with its lights off, past the war memorial, to the boarding house on Tanner Street where Ida Wrenn had taken a room.

Ida answered the door with her jacket already half on, as if she'd been standing behind it waiting for bad news. She took one look at Kestrel's face and stepped aside to let her in.

"Tell me," she said.

Kestrel told her. She laid Merrin's photographed pages out on the bedspread in order, and she watched Ida's face as Ida read — watched the grief come up in her like floodwater and then, remarkably, watch it *set*, the way mud sets, into something that would hold weight.

"That's the date of the dinner," Ida said, tapping the column. Her voice was flat and steady, which Kestrel had learned was the sound of Ida at her most dangerous. "She wrote the date of the dinner next to my name."

"She's efficient," Kestrel said. "It's the most horrifying thing about her. There's no rage in any of this. It's housekeeping to her. You're a loose page and she's rebinding the book."

"And you're after me. Alphabetically or otherwise." Ida looked up. "Kestrel. You know what you have to do. Get in your car tonight. Drive until the town stops being real. I'll drive the other direction. We compare notes by phone from two states away and let the state police earn their pay."

"I've thought about it."

"For how long? Since the cemetery? That's not thinking, that's a panic attack with a map."

Kestrel almost laughed. It came out of her sideways, cracked, half sob. She sat down on the edge of the boarding-house chair and put her face in her hands and let herself shake for exactly one minute — she timed it, some old habit of discipline — and when the minute was up she took her hands away and said, "If we run, she wins the way she's always won. Not by killing us. By making the town close ranks around the story again. Two more names that 'left town.' The ledger goes back in the dark for another generation, and somewhere there's a girl with my mother's stubbornness who finds her grandmother's name in it and starts asking, and there's no one left alive to help her."

Ida was quiet.

"Also," Kestrel said, "she scheduled us for after the toast. Which means she's given us the date. She's given us the *time*, Ida. People who think they're merciful always make that mistake. They can't resist announcing the shape of the thing." She looked at the pages on the bed. "She thinks she's holding the pen. Let's find out what happens when the pen writes back."

* * *

They worked through the night at Ida's small table, and by three in the morning Kestrel had stopped being afraid.

Not brave — she was honest enough with herself to know the difference. The fear was still there, sitting in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed. But it had changed states. Somewhere between the second pot of boarding-house coffee and the fourth time she read her own name in Merrin's hand, the fear had stopped being a wall and become fuel. She recognized the feeling from her mother's marginal notes, from the fierce cramped handwriting in the ledger's margins: *Ask what happened to the Alder girls.* Her mother had been afraid too. Her mother had been afraid and had written in the margins anyway, and the fear had not saved her, and the writing had not saved her, but the writing was the only reason Kestrel knew where to dig.

Terror transmuted. Her mother had done the chemistry. Kestrel was only carrying on the reaction.

They made lists.

What they had: the photographed ledger pages, the cipher key Kestrel had cracked, Merrin's own notes — which were, Kestrel realized with a cold thrill, better than a confession, because they were a *work product*, a keeper's log, nine years of erasure documented in the killer's own immaculate hand. What they had: Tobias Wrenn's remains, positively identified, with embalming chemistry that would point to whoever had supplied the compounds — and there were exactly two suppliers of embalming-grade chemicals within fifty miles, and Kestrel knew both owners, and one of them was on the anniversary committee.

What they had: Corliss's key, and Corliss's half-confession, and the knowledge — confirmed now by Merrin's notes — that Corliss was marked *deferred, cooperative*. He wasn't a target. He was a retained asset. Which meant the founding families' protection ran one layer shallower than anyone believed. Merrin kept the ledger; Merrin kept *them*, too.

What they didn't have: anyone outside Saint Alder's who knew any of this existed.

"That's the whole problem, isn't it," Ida said, pen tapping the table. "Evidence in a room is just paper. We need it out of the room. We need it in hands that don't eat at the anniversary dinner."

"Then it leaves the room tomorrow," Kestrel said. "Copies. Not photographs on my phone — paper, sealed, addressed, mailed from the next county over. State police, the attorney general's office, and a forensic document examiner I trained with in Fellmarsh who owes me a favor and doesn't own a single share of Saint Alder's history." She was writing as she talked, the list growing down the page. "And one more copy that goes somewhere Merrin can't burn, burn down, or bury. I'll decide where by morning."

"And us?"

Kestrel set the pen down.

"We attend the dinner," she said. "Both of us. In plain sight. We smile at the mayor and applaud the choir and raise our glasses to Ambrose Vane, the founding father whose coffin I opened and whose bones I've held." She heard her own voice go quiet and hard. "Because she's expecting us to run. The whole design of it — the date, the toast, the *reinterment* — it only works on people who scatter. She's a keeper, Ida. Keepers are patient, and patience is a strategy built entirely on the assumption that everyone else flinches. So we don't flinch. We stand in the middle of two hundred people, and when the glasses go up, we make sure the toast is the last lie this town tells about itself."

Ida looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a truck went down Tanner Street, headlights sweeping the ceiling, and neither of them moved until it passed.

"My brother used to say," Ida said finally, "that the worst thing about this town was that it was *nice*. Nine years I've been angry at a place for being polite to me while it forgot Tobias existed." She gathered Merrin's pages into a neat stack, squaring the edges with a care that was almost ceremonial. "I'd like to be impolite. Publicly. With witnesses."

"Two hundred witnesses," Kestrel said. "Minimum."

* * *

Near dawn, Kestrel finally went home — briefly, deliberately, lights on and curtains open, nothing hidden, because hiding was the one thing she could no longer afford. She showered, changed, and stood for a moment in the doorway of her mother's old room, where the annotated ledger lay on the desk where she'd left it.

*Ask what happened to the Alder girls.*

"I'm asking, Mama," she said to the empty room. "Tomorrow night, with the whole town listening. I hope that's soon enough."

Then she sat at her own kitchen table with the phone in her hand and made the calls that could not be unmade. The state police tip line. The attorney general's office. Her old colleague in Fellmarsh, who listened for four minutes without interrupting and then said, "Kestrel, are you safe?"

"No," Kestrel said. "But I will be. Or I'll be a headline. Either way, you'll have everything you need by noon tomorrow."

She slept two hours on the couch, dressed, and was at the cemetery by seven, walking the rows one more time — not the coroner's rounds this time, but something else. A survey. A soldier counting the ground she meant to hold.

At the far edge, where the pines came down to the wall, she stopped at a small stone she'd passed a thousand times: *SUSANNA ALDER, 1871–1889. Taken by God, whom we cannot question.*

She crouched and brushed the moss from the letters.

"Taken by somebody," Kestrel said. "And I'm done not questioning."

Behind her, up the hill, a figure in a long grey coat stood for a moment at the cemetery gate — there and not there, still as a held breath — and then turned away, unhurried, toward the archive where the lights were already burning.

Kestrel watched her go and felt the stone in her chest settle into something like calm.

The dinner was tomorrow night. The toast would land at eight.

And at one minute past, the ledger would speak.
