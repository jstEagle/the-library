## 7. A File Better Read Than the Police's Own

The missing-person file arrived on a Tuesday, in a grey police-issue box that smelled of damp cardboard and other people's endings.

Marguerite had signed for it at the archive desk with the particular care he reserved for things that could ruin him. DS Imogen Vale had released it under a freedom-of-information request he'd framed in the most anodyne language he could construct—*research into archival record-keeping practices in historical missing-person cases, 1990–2005*—and even so, the box had been photocopied rather than lent, every page stamped COPY in red, as though the originals might be contaminated by his hands.

He understood the instinct. He shared it, most days. Paper was the most dangerous substance in any building, and he worked in a cathedral of it.

The reading room was empty at half past four in the afternoon. October had come down on Ely like a lid; the light through the north windows was the colour of old pewter, and the radiators ticked in the way that old buildings tick when they are pretending to be warm. Marguerite set the box on the felt-covered table, put on white cotton gloves out of habit rather than necessity—the copies hardly required them—and lifted the lid.

The file inside was a monster. Not in size; in *shape*. Missing-person files are never tidy. They accrete. They grow appendages. Ada Fenwick's had been opened in October 1998, when a dog-walker found a woman's shoe on the bank of the Old West River near Coveney, and it had swallowed everything that came after: statements from Nell Fenwick, from neighbours, from the verger of St Mary's where Ada sometimes attended; a transcript of the 999 call; maps with pencil crosses; the pathologist's interim report on the shoe (leather, size five, no remains attached); and finally, eleven months later, the coroner's finding of death by misadventure in absentia—drowned, presumed, the body never recovered but the river confident enough for the law.

Marguerite read the way he catalogued: once fast, for the architecture; once slow, for the seams.

The architecture was familiar. A frightened woman, a river, a shoe. The fen had been eating people since before the drainage, and the police file read like a form filled in by the fen itself—*no evidence of foul play, no known enemies, subject described as "quiet, solitary."* Nell's statement was there, and Marguerite read it twice, feeling the woman's frustration come up through the typed transcription like heat through a floor. *She would not have gone near that river. She was frightened of water since she was six years old. I told them. I told them and they wrote it down and then they did nothing with it.* The officer's note in the margin, in ballpoint: *Sister in denial. Common.*

He made a note of that note. Marguerite made notes of everything; it was the one form of prayer he trusted.

* * *

It was on the third pass—slow, seam-hunting—that he began to see the pencil.

Not the ballpoint of the investigating officers, not the red of the COPY stamps. Pencil. Graphite, soft, applied with a hand that pressed hard at the downstrokes and lifted almost apologetically at the ends. The first instance was trivial: beside the date the shoe was found, someone had written, in letters no taller than a grain of rice, *low water that week — bank exposed 40 yds.*

Marguerite sat back. He looked at the sentence for a long time.

It was true. He knew it was true because he had spent the previous week, in the hours when the archive was closed to him and open to his own curiosity, reading the Environment Agency's historical tide tables for the Great Ouse system—a habit he could not have explained to anyone and had not tried. The third week of October 1998 had been a dry, low-water week. The riverbank at Coveney had indeed been exposed further than usual. That fact appeared nowhere in the police file. It appeared in no newspaper. It was the kind of detail that existed only in the memory of someone who had stood on that bank and looked at the mud.

He turned the page with the care of a man defusing something.

The pencil was everywhere once he knew to look for it. Not corrections, exactly—corrections would have been alarming enough. These were *amendments*. Refinements. The file said Ada Fenwick was last seen on the fourteenth of October; the pencil, beside it, *seen at the market, the 16th — bought flour.* The file listed her known addresses; the pencil added one, a room above a shop on Broad Street, with a date range. The file recorded that a search of her cottage on the Stuntney road had yielded nothing of note; the pencil, in the margin, three words: *cellar not searched.*

Marguerite's heart was doing something unprofessional. He made himself breathe the way he'd been taught by a conservator in Cambridge, years ago—four counts in, six counts out—because the thrill rising in him was exactly the thrill that made archivists dangerous to themselves. This was the feeling he lived for: the moment a document stopped being a record and became a witness. Somewhere in his chest an older, colder Marguerite was already composing the footnote.

But another part of him, the part that had noticed Cyril Brandt's too-precise recollections and the replaced page in the 1998 burial register, was beginning to say something quieter and much worse.

*Who corrects a police file?*

Not a journalist. Not a historian. Not Nell Fenwick, who had never seen the file. The pencil belonged to someone who had held this document in its original, unphotocopied form—someone with access before the copies were made, or someone whose annotations had been copied along with everything else because whoever made the copies had never looked closely enough to notice that graphite doesn't photograph well and hadn't cared.

The pencil belonged to someone inside the case.

* * *

He worked through the evening. The reading room steward came at six to say the building closed at seven, and Marguerite showed him the dean's letter permitting out-of-hours access, and the steward looked at the letter, and at Marguerite, and at the police box, in that order, and left without another word. Ely was a small city. Word travelled in it the way damp travelled through a wall: invisibly, and into everything.

By lamplight, the file changed character. Marguerite had spent eleven years learning to read documents the way other people read faces—the hesitations, the over-writing, the places where the hand sped up because the mind wanted to be elsewhere—and the pencil annotations had a rhythm he was coming to know. They were never explanatory. They never said *this is wrong*. They said *this is incomplete*, and then they completed it, in the smallest possible number of words, as though the writer were not improving the record but keeping his own private ledger inside the official one. A ledger within a ledger. An archive inside an archive.

The handwriting itself he studied for an hour, photographing each annotation with his phone and blowing the images up until the grain of the paper showed through. Cramped. Clerical. The letters were old-fashioned in a way that had nothing to do with age and everything to do with training—the a's and e's formed with the closed, economical loops of someone taught penmanship in the forties or fifties, someone who had spent a life writing labels and entries and headings in a hand designed to be small, legible, and impersonal. Marguerite knew that hand. Not this hand, specifically. But that *kind* of hand. It was the hand of a man who kept books.

It was, he admitted to the empty room, not unlike his own.

He did not enjoy the admission.

* * *

The dental records note came at twenty past nine.

He had reached the pathologist's supplementary report, dated March 1999—the document that had, more than any other, closed the case. A partial mandible had been recovered from the river silt near the shoe, too degraded for visual identification but suitable for dental comparison. The report recorded that records for Ada Fenwick had been obtained from her former dentist in Ely and compared. The conclusion, typed, signed: *consistent with.*

Consistent with. The three most powerful words in forensic science, and among the vaguest. Marguerite had read enough inquest files to know that "consistent with" was what a report said when it wanted to be helpful without wanting to be sworn at.

The pencil had been there first.

Beside the typed conclusion, in that cramped clerical hand, in graphite pressed hard enough to emboss the page: *mismatched, resolved.*

Marguerite read it four times.

Mismatched. The word sat there like a stone in a shoe. The dental records had *not* matched. Someone in the process had known it—the comparison had failed, or been ambiguous, or had contradicted the identification outright—and the file's tidy "consistent with" was the public version, the version that let a coroner sign, let a sister be told, let a missing woman become a dead one so the paperwork could stop.

And then: *resolved.* Not *explained*. Not *re-examined*. Resolved—the word of a man closing an account. The word of someone for whom the mismatch was not a mystery to be pursued but a difficulty to be managed.

Marguerite sat very still in the pool of lamplight, and the intellectual thrill that had been carrying him all evening—the pure, clean joy of the hunt, the footnote composing itself in his head—curdled.

Because here was the arithmetic, and he made himself do it slowly, the way he did everything now.

Whoever wrote *mismatched, resolved* had known, in 1999 or shortly after, that the dental comparison of Ada Fenwick's "remains" had failed—and had known it before or outside the official record, because the official record said the opposite. That knowledge existed in perhaps three places: the pathologist's office, the coroner's chambers, and the investigating team. And whoever wrote it had not gone to the police with the mismatch. Had not gone to the coroner. Had not gone to Nell Fenwick, who had spent eleven years being told her sister was in denial.

Whoever wrote it had corrected the file. Quietly. In pencil. For himself.

People annotate documents for two reasons, Marguerite thought. Because they are studying them. Or because they are *keeping* them.

He looked down at the file, at the whole grey mass of it, and saw it differently now—not as a record of an investigation but as a maintained thing. Tended. The way a verger tends a building. The way a man who had controlled a crypt for fifty years, who signed a survey report and buried a registrar's signature under his own, would tend anything: patiently, privately, with an eye to what should be found and what should not.

The pencil had never been meant for the police. The pencil had been meant for the pencil's owner—a private index of the true record, kept inside the false one, in the one place no one would ever think to look, because who searches a file for the truth? You search a file for the file.

Marguerite thought of the diary in his safe, its entries continuing three years past Ada's death. *He found the ledger. If I stop writing, look where the dead are kept.*

Ada had known there was a ledger. She had not known where.

He was holding it.

* * *

He photographed every pencil mark. All forty-one of them. He photographed them at three exposures, logged the file references, and made a handwritten index on his own paper, in his own hand, which he then locked in the safe beside the diary—because he had learned, in the past two weeks, what happened to things left in the open in this building. They moved. A few inches. Enough.

Then he sat for a while with the last photograph on his screen: the pathologist's page, the typed *consistent with*, the pencil's *mismatched, resolved*, small and hard and patient as a nail.

He should take it to DS Vale. He knew that. It was evidence, or adjacent to evidence, and he was an archivist, not an investigator, and the correct professional instinct was to hand the dangerous thing to the people paid to hold it.

But there was a problem, and the problem was the photograph he'd taken of the box itself when it arrived—the outer packaging, the transit label, the chain-of-custody slip. Because the slip listed every person who had handled the file since its copying, and Marguerite had read such slips his whole working life, and this one had an entry that did not belong. Between the Cambridgeshire constabulary records office and the cathedral archive, the file had passed through one intermediate hand. A courier. The signature was a scrawl.

The printed name beneath the scrawl read *C. Brandt, authorised carrier, diocesan services.*

The verger had carried the file to him. The verger had carried the file—this file, annotated in a hand like a ledger-keeper's, corrected with knowledge only the killer could have—directly into Marguerite's hands, and had smiled while doing it, and had said, *Take your time with it. She deserves a thorough reading.*

Marguerite turned off the lamp.

In the dark of the reading room, the cathedral settled around him with its thousand-year creak, and he understood two things with perfect clarity. First: that Cyril Brandt knew exactly what the file contained, and had wanted it in the archive, catalogued, accessioned, *filed*—absorbed into the one place where a document, once entered, becomes invisible. And second: that the pencil annotations, the whole private ledger of the true record, had been left in the file deliberately.

Because a man who writes things down, Cyril had told him once, over the burial registers, with something like affection—*a man who writes things down can always be contradicted by what's written.*

Marguerite locked the file in the safe with the diary and the letter, and stood a moment with his hand flat on the cold steel, listening to the building breathe.

The pencil hand was cramped, clerical, old-fashioned.

So, he now admitted, in the privacy of his own skull, was the hand on the 1970s survey report he had photographed three nights ago in the diocesan registry—the signature of a young Cyril Brandt, authorising the closure of a crypt for fifty years.

He had compared them at two in the morning, the two images side by side on his screen, and told himself the similarity was an artefact of era, of training, of the fenland schools that taught the same closed loops to every child of the fifties.

He told himself that again now, in the dark.

He did not believe it.

Tomorrow he would call DS Vale. Tomorrow he would lay the whole pencil ledger before her, forty-one annotations, and watch her face when she read *mismatched, resolved*. Tomorrow the file would stop being his and become the law's, and that was correct, and he was a man who did correct things.

But tonight—tonight there was one more thing, one small thing, that he could not stop himself from doing. He reopened the safe, took out the photograph of the transit slip, and looked again at the printed line beneath the courier's scrawl.

*Authorised carrier, diocesan services.*

Cyril Brandt had carried the file. Which meant Cyril Brandt had access to the police records office. Which meant that when Marguerite had requested the file under freedom of information—three weeks ago, in anodyne language, through channels—someone had read the request before it was fulfilled, and had decided not to stop it.

Had decided, in fact, to deliver it by his own hand.

Marguerite stood in the dark and felt the floor of his certainty tilt, very slightly, the way the fen drained and settled and tilted under everything built upon it.

The file had not been sent to him despite the annotations.

It had been sent to him *because* of them.

Somewhere above his head, in the dark of the nave, a door closed—softly, with the care of a man who had been closing doors in this building for fifty years—and Marguerite switched off his phone screen and stood absolutely still among the shadows of the shelves, listening, while the reading room clock counted the seconds toward midnight.
