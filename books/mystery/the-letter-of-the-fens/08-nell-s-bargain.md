## 8. Nell's Bargain

The house at the edge of the drained fen had once been a pump keeper's cottage, and it still leaned into the wind as if braced against water that hadn't come in sixty years. Nell Fenwick had lived in it all her life, first with her parents, then alone, then with the silence that came after Ada.

She saw him coming up the lane from the kitchen window—the archivist, the one who'd telephoned twice and written once, polite letters on cathedral letterhead that she'd propped against the fruit bowl like unpaid bills. He walked carefully on the gravel, a thin man carrying a document case the way other men carried sleeping children, and she thought: he doesn't know what he's walking into. They never do.

She'd put the kettle on anyway. That was the trap of courtesy; it made you complicit in your own interrogation.

"Mrs. Fenwick." He stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands—a young man's manners in a middle-aged body, spectacles already fogging in the warmth of the stove. "Thank you for seeing me."

"You wrote that you'd found her diary." She didn't ask him to sit. He sat anyway, because people like him always did, drawn to chairs the way moths were drawn to lit windows. "That's what you said on the telephone. Found her diary. With writing in it after she went."

"Yes."

"After they said she drowned herself."

"That's why I've come."

Nell poured the tea because her hands needed something lawful to do. Eleven years she had waited for someone to say those words—not *I'm sorry for your loss*, not *these things are often the way they seem*, but *that's why I've come*, as if there were somewhere this could finally go. Her hands shook on the pot. She was fifty-eight years old and she had been waiting since she was forty-seven, and in all that time no one had come up the lane except the man with the money orders.

"Ada was afraid of the water," she said. "You'll want to know that first, before anything else. Before you write anything down." She nodded at his case. "Put it in your book. First line. Ada Fenwick, born 1954, was afraid of open water her whole life. Our father took us bathing at Littleport when we were girls and Ada screamed until she was sick. Not crying-sick. Frightened-sick. The kind where your body decides for you. She never went near the river again. Never near the drains, never near the floods. When the Ouse came over in '78 she slept in her coat by the front door so she could run inland if it rose. A grown woman, thirty years old, sleeping by the door like a soldier." Nell set down the pot. "So whatever they told you about her walking into the water—you can carry that back to whoever told you and tell them Nell says they're liars, or fools, or both."

Marguerite Okonkwo had gone very still. She noticed that about him even then: stillness like a held breath, as though moving might disturb whatever was settling in the room.

"The police file says—" he began.

"I know what the police file says. A woman alone, money troubles, low water that summer, no note but women like her don't leave notes, do they, that's how they said it, *women like her*. As if they knew her. As if anyone in this county knew her but me." She looked out the window at the flat country, the black peat going to frost, the ditches cut straight as ruled lines. "They dragged three stretches of river and found nothing. Did you know that? Nothing. No coat, no shoes. Women who drown themselves take their shoes off. Everyone knows that. It's the one thing they do properly. There were no shoes on any bank between here and Ely. I told them that. I told them and they wrote it down and then they closed the file, because a file that stays open is a bother and a file that closes is a number."

* * *

He asked her about the last year. She had expected that; she had been rehearsing against it for eleven years without knowing it, the way you rehearse against a funeral.

"She changed," Nell said. "In the spring of '98. It wasn't sudden—that's what people don't understand, how slow these things are, how a person can be frightened for months and everyone around them calls it tiredness. She came here Sundays. We'd have the roast, we'd listen to the wireless, she'd do the crossword wrong on purpose to annoy me. And bit by bit the Sundays got shorter. Then she started looking out of my window. My window faces the lane, and she'd sit sideways in the chair so she could see down it, and if a car slowed—and cars did slow, out here, lost drivers mostly—she'd go quiet in a way I could feel across the room. Like a draught."

"Did she say who?"

"She said—" Nell stopped. Here it was, the place she had circled for eleven years. She picked up her tea and put it down again untouched. "She said, *there's a man who keeps the books.*"

The archivist's pen had stopped moving.

"Not the bank. Not the council. She meant it the way you'd say a man keeps bees or keeps dogs. Something he tended. She said he knew things about people, the sort of things you only know if you've read them somewhere, and she said—" Nell's voice caught and she hated it, hated the catch, hated the old woman's wobble of it. "She said, *he's kind to me, Nell. That's the worst part. He's kind.*"

The stove ticked. Out on the fen a heron lifted off the ditch, unhurried, ancient, indifferent.

"I laughed at her," Nell said. "I want that in your book too. Last entry of the year: Nell laughed at her. She said a man was watching her and keeping accounts of her life and I said, Ada love, you've been reading too many library books, and she smiled and agreed with me, because that's what you do when you're frightened and nobody will hold it for you—you put it down and smile. And six weeks later she was gone."

* * *

It was nearly dark when she finally told him. She had known from the moment he arrived that she would tell him—it was why she'd let him in, why the kettle had gone on—but knowing and doing were separated in her by a distance she'd spent a decade crossing, an inch a year.

"There was money," she said.

He looked up.

"After. After they declared her dead and the vicar did the service and everyone stopped saying her name in the present tense. Money orders came. Cashable at the post office in Ely, which meant a bus trip each time, which I think was the point—to make me earn it, make me remember with my legs why I was taking it." She got up and went to the dresser, the big pine one their mother had scrubbed white every week of her life, and took down the biscuit tin. It was the tin the shortbread had come in at Christmas 1989; their mother had kept sewing needles in it; Ada had kept buttons. Nell set it on the table between them like evidence at a trial, which, she supposed, was exactly what it was.

Inside were the counterfoils. Ten of them, rubber-banded, soft as cloth with handling.

"One a year. Every March, the week of her birthday—which I never told anyone outside the family, mind, so whoever sent them knew her birthday, knew my debts, knew the post office would cash them. The first year I took it straight to the police. Sergeant Weller, nice enough lad, said anonymous gifts weren't a crime, said likely it was a relative or a well-wisher ashamed to be named. But I'd no relatives left but Ada, and well-wishers don't count to the pound." She fanned the counterfoils on the table with one finger. "Fifty pounds the first year. Fifty-five the next. It went up as my debts went up—as if he *knew* my debts. As if somewhere there was a page with my name on it and a figure underneath, kept current, kept tidy. You understand what I'm telling you? Whoever paid me didn't pay me out of kindness. He paid me the way you pay a bill. Regularly. Against a service."

"What service?" Marguerite asked quietly.

Nell sat down across from him and made herself look at him, this stranger with ink on his fingers, because the confession deserved a witness with clean hands.

"Silence," she said. "About a letter."

* * *

It had come the week after the dragging ended, after the file closed, when the world had finished deciding that Ada Fenwick was dead. An envelope, hand-delivered—no stamp, no postmark, pushed through the door sometime in the night. Ada's handwriting, shakier than Nell had ever seen it, the letters leaning like drunks.

"I read it standing in this doorway," Nell said. "Where you're sitting now. I read it twice and then I heard a car on the lane and I—" Her hands flattened on the table. "I burned it. In the grate, with the dampers pulled, and I stood there till it was ash, and then I raked the ash fine, and I have regretted it every single day since, including this one, especially this one."

"Why?" The question wasn't accusing. That was somehow worse. He asked it the way a doctor asks where it hurts.

"Because I was afraid. Because whoever sent the money orders had just shown me they could find my door in the night. Because the letter said things that couldn't be true unless everything else was also true, and if everything else was true then Ada hadn't drowned, she'd been *taken*, and I was a widow-woman of forty-seven alone on the fen with a coal fire and a stick by the door, and I chose—" She made herself say it plainly. "I chose being safe over being her sister. That's the whole of it. That's the bargain. Ten years of money orders and I never refused one. I cashed every one. I bought a new boiler. I had the roof done. I lived on my sister's ransom and called it grief."

The archivist didn't speak for a long moment. When he did, his voice was careful, the voice of a man handling something brittle. "Do you remember any of it? The letter itself?"

"All of it," Nell said. "That's the curse of it. Burning a thing doesn't burn the reading of it. I can give you the paper, the fold, the smudge where her thumb had dragged the ink—I can't give you the words entire, but I can give you the beginning, because I've said it to myself ten thousand times, in the bath, on the bus, at her grave that hasn't got her in it." She closed her eyes. The kitchen fell away; the fen fell away; it was 1998 and the envelope lay cold in her hands.

"*If anything happens,*" she recited, "*the crypt knows my name.*"

She opened her eyes. Marguerite Okonkwo had gone white to the lips. Not pale—white, the white of paper, of bone, and she watched him arrive somewhere she had arrived alone eleven years ago, and she felt the terrible comfort of company.

"The crypt," he said. "At the cathedral."

"Our Ada cleaned there. Part-time, vestries and such, through an agency. She liked the old buildings, the dry ones, the ones that held still. She used to say the fen took everything that wasn't nailed down but stone stayed put." Nell's voice roughened. "And the rest of the letter—what I can hold of it—said she'd left something where no one would ever throw it away. Said if I was reading it, then he'd won the first round, but not the last. Said I wasn't to blame myself." Her mouth twisted. "Well. Too late for instructions."

* * *

He stayed another hour. She let him copy the counterfoils, all ten, and watched him log them into his case with numbered slips, each item wrapped and labelled, and she understood suddenly why Ada had trusted him before she'd ever met him—people who keep things properly are rare, and the frightened find them the way roots find water.

At the door, in the blue dark with the wind coming flat off the fields, he paused.

"Mrs. Fenwick. The money orders. Was there anything else? Anything that came with them, any year. A note. A mark. Anything."

Nell hugged her cardigan shut against the cold and searched backward through a decade of Marches.

"Once," she said slowly. "The fourth year, maybe the fifth. The post office girl was new and chatty, and she said, *oh, the gentleman's here again, he does like his routine*—and I asked her what gentleman, and she said the one who brought the payment orders in person, didn't he always, tall older fellow, very correct, grey coat." Nell's breath smoked in the dark. "She said he always smelled of candle wax. And he carried a great ring of keys, and when he counted out the counterfoil for me to sign, they sang. All those keys together. Like church bells, only smaller."

Marguerite stood very still at the bottom of her step, and above his shoulder, far off across the black fen, the lights of Ely Cathedral rose out of the dark like a lit ship riding water that was no longer there.

"He signed the counterfoil book himself, some years," Nell added. "When I couldn't get the bus. Delivery signature, the girl called it. I never thought to look at the name." She watched the archivist's face and saw the thought land in him like a struck match. "But the book's still at the post office, isn't it? Books like that, they keep them. People who keep things properly are rare, Mr. Okonkwo. Go and find one."
