## 11. The Second Missing Woman

The registers did not want to be read that night. I felt it the way you feel weather coming through stone—a pressure in the archive, a stillness, as though the building itself had drawn a breath and was holding it. I had been back at my desk for three days since the search of my office. Nothing taken; everything moved. The blotter two inches left of true. The pens reordered by length rather than by age of acquisition, which is how I keep them. Someone wanted me to know they had been there, and wanted me to know they could be there again.

So I worked by lamplight with the door open, and I went backward instead of forward—away from Ada Fenwick, away from 1998, into the older registers, the ones nobody had touched in decades because nobody had reason to. If Cyril Brandt had replaced a page in the burial register, he had done it because the page said something. And if a man rewrites one page, you must ask what the other pages say when no one is watching them.

I began with the parish rolls of the fen villages in the cathedral's orbit: Witchford, Coveney, Little Downham, Haddenham, Stretham. Places where the land lies below the rivers and the churches stand on islands of clay like animals standing in water. The records were mine in the way old paper becomes yours after enough nights—you learn their weight, their smell, the particular reluctance of a vellum roll that has been rolled too long in one direction.

I was not looking for anything specific. That is the honest truth, and I have learned to distrust it only slightly less than I distrust certainty. I was looking for Ada's shape repeated—the abrupt departure, the thinning of documents, the silence where a life should continue—and I told myself I would know it if I saw it.

I saw it three times before midnight.

* * *

The first was Edith Marsh, of Coveney, who left her parish in the spring of 1961. I found her not in the departure but in the residue: a marriage register entry from 1954, a baptism for a daughter in 1957, and then nothing. No burial. No transfer of letters testimonial—the document a parish issued when a communicant moved elsewhere, the ecclesiastical equivalent of forwarding an address. In forty years of archiving I can count on one hand the women who vanished from these rolls without either dying or moving, and both of those events leave paper behind. Edith Marsh left none.

What she did leave was a memorial.

It stopped me with my pen in the air. On the Coveney parish roll of benefactions—a dry list of gifts to the church, brasses cleaned, linens donated—there was an entry in a hand I now knew better than my own: cramped, clerical, upright, each letter formed with the patience of a man copying scripture. *In memory of Edith Marsh, given by C. Brandt, verger, a new frontal for the high altar.* Dated 1963. Two years after she disappeared from every record in the county.

Cyril Brandt would have been a young man in 1963. Old enough, certainly, to hold the office of verger; the survey report had already taught me he signed his name to cathedral business in the early seventies. But a verger does not give altar frontals out of his own purse—not in those years, not on a verger's wage. The gift was a memorial, and memorials are bought by the bereaved, or by the guilty.

I wrote the date on my card and sat very still for a moment, listening to the radiators tick, telling myself that a shared surname of circumstances proves nothing. Women do leave. Women did leave, especially in 1961, especially from the fens, where a woman with a bicycle and a bus timetable could reinvent herself in Peterborough or King's Lynn inside a single afternoon. It happens. It happened.

Then I found Rosemary Quill.

* * *

She surfaced first in the Haddenham registers, 1976, and the shape of her absence was so like Edith Marsh's that my hand actually shook as I turned the pages—shook with something that was not fear yet but its apprentice, the body understanding before the mind consents to. Baptism entries for two children. A signature as churchwarden's witness on a marriage in 1975. Then the record simply stops, mid-parish, mid-decade, like a sentence abandoned.

No burial entry. No letters testimonial. And again—God help me—again the memorial. This time in the fabric accounts: *Rosemary Quill, ob. 1978, restoration of the south aisle glass, given by C. Brandt.*

*Ob.* Obiit. She died. The abbreviation says she died, entered into a fabric account as though death were a line item, and yet there is no burial register entry anywhere in the diocese, no certificate trail I could follow even partially, no coroner's file referenced in any of the parish correspondence I pulled afterward until two in the morning. A woman marked dead in one book and unrecorded in every other.

And the date. 1978. Two years after she stopped existing in the living records. The same interval—two years—that separated Ada Fenwick's disappearance from the resumption of her diary. Two years, as though that were the standard period a thing needed to lie fallow before it could be safely commemorated. As though grief, or guilt, or the simple cooling of attention, ran on a fixed calendar in one man's mind.

I got up and walked the length of the archive aisle, because my legs wanted to move whether I agreed or not. Past the charter presses, past the sealed crypt stairs with their iron gate and their notice about structural survey, past the ossuary niche where the medieval bones sleep in their ordered strata. The cathedral at night is never silent—it breathes, settles, ticks—but that night it seemed to breathe around me deliberately, the way a house breathes around an intruder.

Three names. I had two. I made myself go back to the desk.

* * *

Doris Ellery was the hardest, because Doris Ellery was the most recent before Ada, and recency means witnesses, and witnesses mean the possibility that someone alive remembers her face. She appears in the Stretham registers through the eighties: sideswoman, flower rota, a confirmation list in 1984 with her name at the bottom in her own handwriting—round, confident, the writing of a woman who expected to be asked things. In 1989 the handwriting stops.

The gap where her documents should be was not empty this time. It was *tidied*. That is the word I keep returning to, and I use it advisedly, because tidying is a skill and this gap had been tended. A note in the parish correspondence file, dated 1991, recorded that Mrs. Ellery had "gone to live with family in Norfolk" and that her remaining effects had been distributed. The note was unsigned. The paper was newer than the papers around it—I could tell by the tone of the stock, the way you can tell a patched sleeve—and it had been inserted into the file at the correct place with the correct spacing, which takes care, which takes knowledge of how files breathe.

Someone had written Doris Ellery out of Stretham the way a good clerk closes an account: completely, correctly, and without a trace of haste.

And there, in the benefaction roll, the third memorial. *Doris Ellery, in memoriam, kneeler cushions embroidered by the parish, given by C. Brandt, 1992.*

Three women across four decades. Three departures without arrivals. Three gaps in the record, each one clean, each one maintained. Three memorials purchased or provided by the same man, each dated roughly two years after the woman last appeared in any living document.

I laid the cards side by side on my desk—Marsh, Quill, Ellery, Fenwick—and looked at them the way you look at a constellation once someone has shown you the lines. Four points. One pattern. The private mystery of Ada Fenwick, which I had carried for weeks like a coal in cupped hands, suddenly opened outward into something cold and architectural. This was not a crime. Crimes are singular; they have scenes, moments, weapons. This was a practice. This was a method, refined over fifty years, with a filing system.

Ada was not the first. Ada was simply the first who had fought back with paper—who had understood, in whatever terror her final years were, that the way to survive a man who controls the record is to become a record yourself. To write. To hide writing where writing is protected. To trust the dead to keep your name.

*He found the ledger,* she had written. *If I stop writing, look where the dead are kept.*

I understood now that the ledger was not one book. It was the whole apparatus—the registers, the rolls, the fabric accounts, the benefactions, the tidy notes in unsigned hands. Cyril Brandt had spent half a century keeping books, and the books kept everything else: the silences, the absences, the women who chose—or were chosen—to be no one. Every memorial was a receipt. Every gap was a signature. He had built his impunity out of the same material I built my life out of, and until tonight I had never considered that the archive could be a weapon rather than a refuge.

* * *

Toward dawn I did the thing I least wanted to do. I checked the memorials against the services.

If Cyril had replaced the 1998 burial register page for Ada's memorial service, then perhaps other service records had been touched too. I pulled the service registers for 1963, 1978, 1992—the years of the three memorials—and read them slowly, twice.

Edith Marsh, 1963: a memorial service recorded, conducted by the then-incumbent of Coveney, attended by "the family." Fine. Ordinary. Except that the incumbent's own visitation book for that year contains no reference to conducting such a service, and incumbents record everything—they record the christenings of children they privately disapprove of; they record funerals of parishioners who never paid their dues. A memorial service leaves a mark on a priest the way a stone leaves a mark on water.

Rosemary Quill, 1978: service recorded, again fine, again ordinary—except the organist's payment ledger for that month shows no fee for a memorial service, and organists are paid for everything, including weddings of couples everyone knew would end badly.

Doris Ellery, 1992: service recorded. The verger's duty roster for that week—his own roster, in his own hand—lists him preparing the church for a weekday service. No incumbent's mark. No organist's fee. A service that exists in one book and nowhere else, witnessed by no one whose job required witnessing it.

Or witnessed by no one except the man who prepared the room.

I sat back, and the lamplight seemed very small all of a sudden, and the dark beyond the desk very large. Three memorial services that may never have happened, entered into registers by the one person with keys to the registers, the keys to the church, and—for fifty years now—the keys to the crypt. Services held for women who may never have died, or whose deaths no one was permitted to examine. Either way the entries served him: if the women were dead, the services buried suspicion under piety; if they were alive somewhere, the services declared them dead in the one place the county trusted above all others to tell the truth.

A cathedral is believed. That is its whole terrible power. People will doubt a coroner, doubt a constable, doubt their own memories—but they will not doubt a register kept in a building that has been telling England the time for nine hundred years. He had hidden four women inside the credibility of the Church of England, and it had held them as securely as any grave.

I thought of Nell Okonkwo—no, Nell Fenwick, Ada's sister, out on the drained edge at Welney with her anger banked like a fire covered overnight—and I thought of how I would say this to her. *Your sister was the fourth. There were three before her, and we will probably never find them all, because the man who took them also filed them.* I thought of how her face would change, and I found I could not finish the thought.

There was one more check, and I made myself do it. I went to the accession files—the cathedral's own intake records, my department, my territory—and searched for any deposit connected to the three names. Estates, papers, photographs, anything a family might have left in trust.

Edith Marsh: nothing. Rosemary Quill: nothing. Doris Ellery: a single item, deposited in 1993 by "a well-wisher," catalogued by a predecessor of mine—a hymn board, of all things, from Stretham church, retired when the boards went electronic. An object with no obvious connection to Doris Ellery at all, deposited anonymously a year after her memorial, catalogued and shelved and forgotten.

Except that whoever deposited it had specified, in the donor file, that it be stored in the lower stacks.

The lower stacks. Behind the crypt gate. Where the structural survey of 1974 closed the stairs indefinitely, and where, according to the paperwork I had traced, one signature had kept them closed ever since.

I stood in the aisle with the donor file open in my hands and realized that my heart was going hard and fast, and that the sound I could hear was not the radiators but my own blood. He had been using the crypt as a storehouse for fifty years. Not only for bones. For objects, for deposits, for the physical residue of the women he erased—kept close, kept dark, kept where no inventory would ever reach them because no inventory was permitted below.

Ada had told us where to look. She had told us in the plainest words available to a frightened woman writing by lamplight: *look where the dead are kept.* And I had assumed she meant the bones of the medieval dead, the honest dead, the dead who died of plague and childbed and old age. But the dead were not only below. The dead were in the registers. The dead were in the benefaction rolls, in the fabric accounts, in the tidy unsigned notes. The dead were a filing system, and Cyril Brandt was its sole custodian, and I had just spent one entire night reading his files over his shoulder.

Somewhere above me, in the residence, or across the green in the verger's cottage by the lychgate, a light might be burning. Or not. He was old now, and careful, and careful men sleep well.

I put the cards in order, photographed them with the little camera I use for fragile folios, and returned every register to its shelf exactly as I had found it—exactly, down to the millimetre, because I had learned in the last fortnight that exactness is armor. Then I sat back down at my desk and faced the question I had been avoiding since the constellation assembled itself.

Evidence gathered alone, by a suspended-adjacent archivist already accused of tampering, is worth nothing. Worse than nothing: it is a gift to a man who constructs culprits for a living. Four index cards in my handwriting would become, in his hands, four more exhibits of my obsession, my fabrication, my decline. I could not take this to the dean—he received his complaints from somewhere, and I no longer doubted the direction of the post. I could not take it to the police as I was; they had a file on me now, however informal.

But the police had failed Ada once already, and there was one officer among them who had signed her original case and been stonewalled by the diocese, and I remembered her name from the old correspondence: Detective Sergeant Vale. Imogen Vale. A woman who had asked questions in 1998 and been told, politely and permanently, no.

I did not know her. I had no reason to believe she would believe me—a diary that writes after death, a verger with fifty years of memorials, four index cards and a hymn board. My evidence was the kind that sounds like madness when spoken aloud by a stranger at a police desk.

But it was also the kind that sounds like murder when laid out on a table by someone who knows how to lay things out.

I took out a clean sheet of paper, and before the light came up over the tower, before the building filled with the innocent noise of morning prayer, I wrote her a letter. I kept it to one page. I gave her dates, names, and register references, and nothing else—no theory, no accusation, no adjectives. Just the skeleton of the thing, the way I would catalogue it: four women, four gaps, four memorials, one hand.

Then I addressed it to the cold case unit at Huntingdon, and I sat holding it for a long moment, understanding that once it left my hands I could no longer control what it woke.

Outside, the fen was turning grey, and the river was coming up on the tide, and somewhere beneath my feet, behind a gate that had been locked since 1974, a hymn board from Stretham was waiting in the dark to tell me what else was waiting beside it.
