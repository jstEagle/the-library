## 18. Counting the Bones

The warrant arrived at 6:40 in the morning, and Imogen Vale read it twice before she let herself feel anything at all.

That was her discipline, learned over nineteen years of cold cases: never let hope precede proof. Hope was the thing that made officers sloppy, that made them see patterns in damp and coincidence. But this morning the paper in her hand was not hope. It was a district judge's signature authorising entry to a sealed crypt beneath Ely Cathedral, and beneath the judge's signature sat Marguerite Okonkwo's inventory extract—the forged accession number, the false shelf location, the whole elaborate fiction he had built to bait a killer—annotated in Vale's own hand as *evidence of method, not fabrication*. She had argued for an hour with the duty solicitor about that distinction. She had won.

She folded the warrant into her coat pocket and drove to the close through fog so thick the cathedral appeared only in sections: first the west tower's base, then a suggestion of buttress, then nothing, as if the building were being assembled out of the weather as she approached.

The workmen were already there. Two men from a Cambridge conservation firm, chosen because they had no connection to the diocese and because their contract specified, in writing, that they took direction from police. They stood by the north transept door with their tools in canvas rolls, drinking tea from a thermos, stamping against the cold, and looking up at the great grey mass of the cathedral with the particular unease of men who had been told they would be working underground in a place where people had been buried for nine hundred years.

Vale signed them in herself. She did not want the close's own staff touching anything—not yet, not today.

"Where's the verger?" asked the older workman, a broad-shouldered man called Petrie who had done crypt surveys before. "Usually there's a verger. Keys, history, all that."

"He's been asked to remain elsewhere," Vale said.

It was true, though it was also the thing that had kept her awake since two in the morning. Cyril Brandt had not been arrested. There was nothing yet to arrest him *for*—no body formally identified, no cause of death established, only soil samples still at the lab and a diary whose provenance a defence barrister would shred before lunch. What there was, was a warrant, and a plan, and Marguerite Okonkwo standing at the edge of the lamplight in his rumpled coat, looking like a man who had slept in his clothes because sleep had seemed beside the point.

"You sent him word," Vale said quietly, drawing Marguerite aside while the workmen unloaded their gear. "Last night. The message about the file."

"I sent exactly what we agreed." Marguerite's voice was hoarse; he had been suspended for eleven days and had spent them at Nell Fenwick's kitchen table with the diary spread out under a lamp like a patient on an operating slab. "A note, unsigned, through the verger's pigeonhole. It said the original annotated file had been re-catalogued under a false entry in the public index, and gave the number. He'll have checked the index by now. He'll have found the entry. And the entry will tell him the item is held *below*."

"And he'll know it's a trap."

"Of course he'll know it's a trap." Marguerite looked up at the tower, lost in fog. "But he'll also know that once your team opens the crypt, everything down there becomes evidence with a chain of custody. Whatever he left behind, whatever he couldn't destroy in fifty years because destroying it meant unsealing what he'd sealed—he loses it today unless he comes for it first. Cyril Brandt has spent his whole life keeping the record tidy. A man like that doesn't run from a loose thread. He pulls it."

Vale had heard killers described many ways—predators, opportunists, monsters—but she thought Marguerite's description was the most accurate one she'd ever heard. Tidiness. That was the shape of it. Every document she'd traced in the past week had been neatened: pages replaced, registers recopied, dates reconciled, a dental mismatch *resolved* in pencil by a hand that wrote like a Victorian clerk. Somewhere under her feet was the place where all that tidying had ended up, and by nightfall it would be open to the air.

"Positions," she said, and turned to her team.

There were four of them: DC Anwar, young and careful; DS Kelleher, who had carried the dental records across three counties without letting them out of his sight; a scenes-of-crime officer named Pryce who had worked more than one burial site and said little; and Vale herself. Uniformed officers would hold the perimeter above—discreetly, in plain clothes, because the last thing anyone wanted was a crowd of tourists photographing police tape strung across the Octagon while the choir rehearsed evensong overhead.

Marguerite had offered to stay away. Vale had refused.

"You're the only person alive who knows what Ada's handwriting looks like when it deteriorates," she'd told him. "If there are pages down there, I need them read *in situ*, before anyone can argue they've been planted. You're my witness, Mr Okonkwo. Not my suspect. Try to remember which."

He had almost smiled at that. Almost.

* * *

The way down began behind the choir, through a door that most visitors walked past without seeing—a low arch, a flight of worn steps, air that tasted of stone dust and something older, sweeter, like the inside of a very old book. Vale went second, behind Petrie, with her torch cutting a cone through the dark.

The crypt of Ely Cathedral was not large. It was a low vaulted undercroft, part Norman, part later patchwork, its floor uneven with the graves of centuries. In the 1970s a structural survey had declared it unsafe, and it had been closed ever since—closed, Vale now understood, by the signature of a registrar conveniently dead and the countersignature of a young man named Cyril Brandt, who had then spent fifty years holding the only keys.

Those keys hung now on a ring in Petrie's hand. The diocesan registrar, served with the warrant at seven o'clock, had surrendered them with a face like curdled milk and had asked, twice, whether the cathedral would be mentioned in the newspapers.

"Ossuary niche," Marguerite said from the rear, his voice flattened by the low vaulting. "Against the east wall. The survey photographs show a recess where bones were stacked during the nineteenth-century restorations. Ada's letter says the chamber is behind it."

The niche was where she had said. A shallow arched recess, perhaps six feet wide, its lower courses bricked up—not recently, but not medieval either. The mortar was grey and hard, machine-mixed. Petrie ran his palm along it and grunted.

"This is post-war brickwork behind. Someone's faced it over." He looked back at Vale. "You want me to take it down?"

"I want you to take it down slowly," Vale said. "Pryce, lights."

They worked for forty minutes in a cone of portable lamps while the cathedral went about its morning above them—Vale could hear, faintly, through nine feet of stone, the organ tuning for a service, a single note held and released, held and released, like something breathing. She stood with her notebook and watched the bricks come out one by one, each one numbered and photographed before it moved, and she made herself think about nothing at all except procedure.

Then Petrie's chisel went through the last course, and the wall breathed out.

Everyone felt it. A movement of air from the cavity beyond, cool and utterly still, carrying a smell that Vale knew from other rooms in other years and had hoped never to smell again: the sweet-dust smell of a place where human beings had decomposed and dried and waited.

Nobody spoke. Petrie set down his chisel with exaggerated care, as if it might wake something.

"Torch," Vale said, and took it herself.

* * *

The chamber behind the niche was small—perhaps eight feet by ten, its ceiling following the curve of some older foundation, its floor swept clean. That was the detail that lodged in Vale's mind first and would not leave: the floor was *swept*. Someone had maintained this room. Someone had come down here with a brush.

Against the far wall, laid out with terrible neatness, were the remains.

Three sets. Even in the torchlight, even before Pryce got the proper lights rigged, Vale could see the separation: one skeleton articulated, lying on its back with hands folded—an old burial, decades old, the bones gone the colour of driftwood. A second, less complete, partially disturbed, tumbled toward the corner as if moved to make space. And a third—

Vale crouched. She did not touch. She looked.

The third skeleton was smaller. Female, she noted, from the pelvis, though she wrote the observation with a scientist's caution and a heart that was hammering. The bones were lighter than the others, less mineralised. Clothing fragments clung here and there—a button, a scrap of fabric gone to lace. And around the skull, arranged with a care that made Vale's throat close, someone had placed a circle of small stones, river-smoothed, the kind a child collects.

"She kept herself," Marguerite said behind her. His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared his throat. "I'm sorry. I mean—she wasn't thrown here. Look at the position. She lay down. Or she was laid down gently, near the end. And the stones—" He stopped.

"The stones are hers," Vale said. "Or someone put them there for her. Either way, photograph everything before we breathe on it."

Pryce worked the room methodically while Vale catalogued, and it was Anwar—kneeling by the third skeleton, photographing the folded hands—who found the tin.

It was a biscuit tin, rusted at the edges, tucked into a gap in the wall as if hidden rather than stored. Inside, wrapped in a plastic bag gone brittle with age, were notebooks. Three of them. Water-stained, swollen, their covers soft as cloth.

Vale put on fresh gloves and lifted the top one into the light. The handwriting on the first page was cramped, urgent, slanting downhill as though the writer had been lying on her side. She had seen enough of Ada Fenwick's diary in photocopies to know it instantly—and to know that this was different. These pages were darker. The ink thinned toward the ends of entries, the way a pen dies, and then resumed in a different colour, ballpoint blue, as if another pen had been found or begged or stolen.

"Marguerite."

He came and knelt beside her, and she watched his face as he read. She had seen witnesses break at scenes like this; she had never seen a man look at a dead woman's handwriting and *listen* to it the way he did, head tilted, lips moving slightly, as though the words were being spoken to him alone.

"It's her," he said finally. "The last pages. The ones missing from the diary I found—there's a gap, the final section was torn out. This is it. This is her end." He turned a page with a fingertip, barely touching the paper. "She writes about the chamber. About hearing him move the bricks. About counting the days by the bells."

"Can you read me the last entry?"

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. The organ above had stopped tuning; the silence below was total.

"'To whoever finds this,'" he read. "'I don't know your name, but you came, and that is the whole of what I ask. Tell Nell I am not angry with her. She burned the letter because she was afraid, and fear is not a crime, whatever he thinks. Tell her I loved her every day of my life and half of every night. Do not let them say I despaired. I counted the bells. I counted the stones. I counted everything, because as long as I was counting, I was still a person and not a secret. Count the bones. Count them all. Then write our names down where no one can take them out again.'"

Vale looked at the swept floor, the folded hands, the circle of river stones, and understood that she was looking at eleven years of a woman refusing, with the only tools left to her, to become paperwork.

"Bag them," she said, and her voice was steady, which she counted as the professional achievement of her career. "All of it. And Anwar—get me the dental team on the phone. Kelleher's records. If that third skeleton is Ada Fenwick, I want it confirmed before the ink on the warrant is dry."

* * *

They surfaced at noon into thin sunlight, blinking, smelling of stone and worse. The consignment of evidence filled four cases. The remains would wait for the pathologist and the archaeologists Vale had already summoned; you did not hurry the dead out of a place they had been kept so long, and besides, the scene needed to be documented to a standard no defence counsel could pick apart. She had learned that lesson too.

It was Petrie, packing his tools, who said it.

"That verger fellow. Brandt." He nodded up toward the transept door. "He was watching from the lady chapel when we carried the first case through. Half eight, maybe. Just standing there among the chairs."

Vale went still. "Watching how?"

"Watching like a man at a funeral," Petrie said. "Like he'd already said goodbye to the place." He shrugged. "Then he took off his jacket, folded it over his arm, and walked out through the west door. Didn't come back."

She radioed the plainclothes team on the perimeter. Negative sightings since ten o'clock. His lodgings off Barton Road: empty, door unlocked, bed made with hospital corners, kettle rinsed and inverted on the drainer. On the kitchen table, squared to the table's edge, were three objects: his key ring, complete; his prayer book; and a single sheet of paper, typed, reading:

*I have always tried to leave things better found than kept.*

No signature. None needed.

Vale stood in Cyril Brandt's tidy kitchen and felt the triumph of the morning drain out through the floorboards, replaced by something colder and more familiar. Fifty years of disappearances, and the man had vanished himself between half past eight and ten o'clock, in a city of thirteen thousand souls, on the one morning when every eye should have been on him. He had seen the workmen arrive. He had understood. And instead of running—which they would have caught, eventually, because men like Cyril Brandt did not know how to live untidily—he had simply *stopped*, the way he had taught three women to stop, folding his jacket over his arm and walking out of the story.

Except.

Vale drove back to the close and found Marguerite sitting on the steps of the archive building, the four evidence cases at his feet like luggage, waiting for transport. He looked up, and before she said a word he read her face.

"He's gone."

"Gone."

Marguerite nodded slowly, and then, to her surprise, he did not look defeated. He looked like a man doing arithmetic.

"There's one thing he doesn't know we have," he said. "One thing left in the world that he must believe is still findable only through him. The annotated police file. My false index entry. The number I gave him points *here*, to the crypt—but the crypt is now full of your officers, and the file isn't in it. I moved it yesterday. It's somewhere he can't guess." He stood, brushing off his coat, and Vale saw in his face the same expression she had seen in the photographs of Ada Fenwick: the look of a quiet person deciding to stop being invisible. "He'll come back for it, Inspector. Tonight, tomorrow—it doesn't matter. Men like Cyril Brandt cannot bear an unclosed entry. So we're going to make sure that when he comes to close it, the last thing he finds in this cathedral is us."

Above them, the fog had burned away entirely, and the west tower stood clear against a hard blue sky, ringing the hour.
