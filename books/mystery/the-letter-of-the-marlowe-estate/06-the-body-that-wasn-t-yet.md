## 6. The Body That Wasn't Yet

The call came at 6:14 on a Tuesday morning, which is the hour the river gives up its secrets. It always has. Drowned things rise with the dawn cold, when the water is at its heaviest and least willing to hold them, and Marlowe's Bend has been feeding men to the Kettle River for a hundred and forty years. I have a standing arrangement with the dispatcher: if it's a floater, call me before you call anyone else, because the first hour on a body belongs to me and everyone else can wait their turn.

"Blackwood," said Dispatch, whose name is Terry and who has never once in eleven years pronounced my first name correctly. "Fisherman out past the old tannery dock. Says there's a man in the water."

"Alive?"

"Not from what the fisherman said."

"The fisherman," I said, pulling on my boots, "is not qualified to make that determination."

"He also said the man's got no shoes on."

I stopped with one boot half-laced.

"No shoes," I repeated.

"That's what he said."

I finished lacing. My hands were steady, which surprised me, because inside my chest something had begun to move — that slow, cold turning-over feeling you get when a coincidence knocks on your door wearing another coincidence's coat.

Three weeks ago — nineteen days, if we're being precise, and I am always precise; precision is the only religion I've ever kept — a well-dressed stranger sat across my desk and paid me an obscene amount of cash to prove a murder would not happen. He described the victim to me in detail. Male, mid-thirties to forty, tall, brown hair going gray at the temples, a scar through the left eyebrow. And then he'd paused, the way a man pauses when he's deciding whether to say the thing that makes him sound insane or the thing that makes him sound certain, and he'd added:

"When you find him, he won't be wearing shoes."

I'd written it down because I write everything down, and I'd told myself it was a detail invented for verisimilitude, the way a con man sprinkles specifics into a prophecy so the mark remembers how right he was. No shoes. A flourish. Rivers take shoes; rivers take everything loose. It was the kind of detail a fraud includes precisely *because* it sounds like foreknowledge and isn't.

The fisherman out past the old tannery dock had found a man with no shoes on.

* * *

The Kettle runs brown-green behind the tannery ruins, slow as syrup this time of year, and the body had lodged against a fallen sycamore about thirty yards downstream of the dock. The fisherman — a retired mail carrier named Duane Priddy who fishes that stretch every morning and has never once caught anything worth the word — stood on the bank wrapped in his own jacket, looking grayer than the morning.

"Don't come closer," he said, which was considerate of him, since I was walking directly toward the body with a kit bag over my shoulder.

"I'm the coroner, Mr. Priddy. Closer is the entire job."

"Oh." He considered this. "Well. Don't fall in."

Solid advice. I've taken worse.

He was face-down when I reached him, snagged by the elbow in the sycamore's root cage, and the river had already started its work on him — skin macerated to a waxy white, the bloat just beginning, hair drifting around his head like ink poured backward. Mid-thirties, maybe forty. Tall. Brown hair, and when I turned the head with two fingers, careful as a librarian handling a rare book, the gray at the temples was exactly where the stranger had put it.

The scar through the left eyebrow was there too. An old one, healed white and smooth, running clean through the brow like a signature.

I knelt there in the mud for longer than was professional, I'll admit. Long enough that Duane called out, "You alright down there?" and I said, "Fine," in a voice that fooled neither of us.

Because here is the thing I need you to understand about dates. The client had named a date. Three weeks out from our meeting, circled, specific — a Tuesday in October, a date I had written on a card and pinned above my desk like a threat assessment. Today was not that date. Today was nine days early. Nine days is not a rounding error. Nine days is either a broken promise or a changed plan, and both of those possibilities made my spine feel like a zipper someone was slowly undoing.

I photographed him where he lay, tagged him, and waited for the sheriff's people to arrive so I could be annoyed at them properly.

* * *

Deputy Cora Voss came down the bank instead of the sheriff himself, which told me something before she said a word. Sheriff Dunmore does not do floaters before eight a.m., and he especially does not do floaters that might turn into work. Cora did the work. That was the whole difference between them, and the whole reason I'd started keeping her name on my short mental list of people in this town who were not decorative.

"Morning, Doc." She crouched beside me, hands in her jacket pockets, eyes doing the sweep — face, hands, clothing, the water line, the banks. She was good. I'd known that for years the way you know the weather is good: without thinking much about it.

"What've we got?" she asked.

"Adult male, unidentified, recovered from the river. Preliminary interval since death, twelve to twenty-four hours, pending autopsy. No identification on the body."

"Clothes?"

"Work shirt, canvas jacket, jeans. All soaked through, all consistent with a working man. No wallet, no phone, no keys."

"And no shoes."

"And no shoes," I agreed.

She looked at me sideways. "That mean something to you?"

"It means rivers are hard on footwear," I said, and heard how thin it sounded even as it left my mouth. Cora Voss is many things, but stupid is not among them, and she filed my answer away somewhere behind her eyes with an efficiency I found genuinely unsettling.

"You want us to run the description through missing persons?"

"Yes. Tall, forties, scar through the left eyebrow. Get it out this morning."

She wrote it down. Then she paused, pen hovering. "Funny thing," she said slowly. "We ran a BOLO on a description close to that about three weeks back. Never panned out. Man matching it was seen at the bus depot, then nothing."

My heart performed a small, private act of vandalism against my ribcage.

"Seen by whom?" I asked, keeping my voice at the level pitch I use for grieving families.

"Desk deputy took the report. I can pull it." She squinted at me. "Doc, you look like you've seen a ghost, and I don't mean the one in the river."

"I haven't had coffee," I said, which was true, and which has the advantage of explaining almost any expression a human face can make.

* * *

The autopsy began at ten-forty. By noon I had stopped taking notes in full sentences.

Let me walk you through what a drowning looks like, because it matters, and because I need to say it out loud to someone, even if the someone is a page.

A drowned man tells you his story in his lungs. Water in the airways, froth in the trachea — fine, white, persistent foam, the kind that doesn't wipe away. The lungs themselves heavy, waterlogged, sometimes weighing double what they should. In the stomach, swallowed water, often with the diatoms and debris of whatever water he went into. Petechial hemorrhages. The blood dilute. The whole body a signed confession: *this man breathed in a river.*

This man had none of it.

His lungs were dry. Dry, pink, and collapsed — the lungs of a man who had not drawn a single breath underwater, because dead men don't breathe. There was no froth. There was no swallowed water. There was no water anywhere in him except on him, soaking the clothes, wetting the skin, performing the role of a river with the dedication of a community theater actor who has memorized the part.

Someone had killed him on land and then introduced him to the Kettle afterward.

I sat back on my stool and looked at him under the lights for a long time. Post-mortem submersion has a signature, and once you know it you can't unknow it: the water gets into the clothes and the surface tissues but doesn't drive the decomposition the way true drowning does, doesn't distribute itself, doesn't get *inside*. This body read like a book with a forged cover. The outside said drowning. The inside said homicide, method undetermined pending further findings — though I did find the finding, eventually, tucked behind the right ear where the scalp meets the skull: a depressed fracture, small, precise, delivered by something rounded and heavy while he was alive, because there was bleeding into the tissue beneath it. Alive when struck. Dead within minutes, most likely. Then dressed, weighted lightly at the ankles — the kind of improvised weight that holds a body down for a day or two and then politely lets go — and given to the river.

And the shoes. Here is where I want you to lean in, because this is the part that turned my professional curiosity into something colder and more personal.

The shoes weren't missing. They'd been *removed*. There were sock lines on his ankles — pale bands where socks had been, compression marks that only form when fabric sits on living skin long enough. He'd worn socks. Socks leave lines; bare ankles don't. Someone had taken off his shoes and his socks after death, carefully, and left the sock lines as evidence of their absence. Why would a killer bother? To make the scene match a description? Who describes a victim by his missing shoes?

I knew who. I was looking at my own handwriting on the card pinned above my desk, that's who. *No shoes,* the client had said, and here was a corpse curated to match, down to the dermatological detail. Except the client had named a date nine days from now, and this man was dead today.

I wrote the post-mortem interval into my report and then sat staring at the phrase *evidence staged post-mortem* until the words lost their meaning, the way words do.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic that kept me company through the afternoon, and I'll share it because misery loves a spreadsheet.

Possibility one: the client was wrong. His date was off, his prophecy was guesswork, and this death was a coincidence of such baroque specificity that I would sooner believe in ghosts. Reject.

Possibility two: the client moved up his schedule. Which means the murder I was paid to prevent had already happened, which means I hadn't prevented it, which means — and this is the thought that made me put down my coffee — the retainer wasn't payment for prevention at all. It was payment for *witnessing*. He hadn't hired me to stop a murder. He'd hired me to watch one happen and confirm it matched the script. I wasn't a coroner in this transaction. I was an audience.

Possibility three, the one I liked least: the client didn't kill this man. Someone else did — someone who had access to the same script, the same description, the same theatrical instinct for a staged death. Two people reading from the same page, and only one of them paying me.

I cleaned my instruments. I dictated notes. I filled out the intake paperwork for an unidentified male and left the name field blank, and then, because I am apparently incapable of leaving things blank, I wrote in pencil, lightly, in the corner of the file where no one official would look: *the rehearsal.*

Because that's what it felt like. Not a performance — a rehearsal. Everything correct, everything in place, every beat hit — the river, the missing shoes, the scar, the age, the build — and yet somehow hollow, somehow early, the way a dress rehearsal is early. You run it before opening night. You run it to find the flaws.

Which raised a question I did not enjoy: if this was the rehearsal, what was opening night?

* * *

Cora came by at four with two coffees from the diner, which in Marlowe's Bend constitutes either a bribe, an apology, or a declaration of alliance, and I still don't know which it was.

"Missing persons came back empty," she said, setting a cup on my desk without asking. "No match on the description. No reports of a tall forties guy with an eyebrow scar gone missing anywhere in the county. Or the next county. I called."

"Thank you."

"So we've got a dead man nobody's missing." She dropped into the chair across from me, uninvited, and studied me over her cup. "Autopsy done?"

"This morning."

"And?"

"Drowning."

She waited. Cora is very good at waiting; it's her superpower, and she deploys it on me specifically because she knows silence makes me talk. I held out for eleven seconds, which for me is heroic.

"Staged," I said. "Killed on land, put in the river after. Blunt force to the skull. The drowning was set dressing."

She absorbed that without blinking. "Homicide."

"Homicide."

"And you're telling me this in your office instead of shouting it across the sheriff's department because—"

"Because your sheriff closed the file before the body was warm. Because the last thing this town needs is a panic over a drifter nobody can name. And because—" I stopped myself. Too late; she'd already seen the door I was keeping shut.

"Because?" she prompted, pleasant as a bear trap.

"Because there are details in this case I can't account for," I said carefully, "and I'd like to account for them before I hand them to a department that files things by how inconvenient they are."

Cora looked at me for a long moment. Outside my window, the light was going amber over the rooftops of Marlowe's Bend, and somewhere a dog was announcing the evening to no one.

"You know what's strange, Doc?" she said, standing, collecting her cup. "Three weeks ago you started asking odd questions. Access logs, records requests, who's seen what file. And now a body turns up that matches a description I half-remember from a report I probably shouldn't remember, and you're holding pieces of it back from me." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "I'm not accusing you of anything. I'm telling you I count things. It's the job."

She let herself out. I sat there with my cooling coffee and my pencil note — *the rehearsal* — and understood that I had just acquired a second problem to stack on top of the first: the deputy was watching me now, and she was better at watching than I was at being watched.

* * *

That night I went home and pulled the card from above my desk. The client's date, circled in my own blue ink. Nine days from now. I laid it on my kitchen table next to my autopsy notes and looked at them together for a long time, the prophecy and the corpse, the script and the stage.

If the client had moved up his timeline, the money in my safe was hush money, or bait, or a ticket to a show I'd already missed the first act of. If he hadn't — if the date still stood — then this poor unnamed man in my cooler was practice, a dress rehearsal performed on a body that fit the costume, and somewhere out there the real performance was still scheduled, still cast, still waiting for its night.

I thought about the sock lines. About the care of it. Someone had removed those shoes deliberately, lovingly almost, to honor a detail they shouldn't have known. Someone who had read the same page I had. Someone rehearsing.

I got out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote at the top, in block letters: *WHO ELSE KNOWS THE SCRIPT?*

Underneath, I wrote four names. I'm not ready to tell you them yet. Honestly, I'm not sure telling you them wouldn't endanger you, and I've learned — expensively, recently — that knowing things in Marlowe's Bend is not a neutral condition.

But I will tell you this: as I turned off the kitchen light, I caught my reflection in the dark window, and for a moment I didn't recognize the woman looking back at me. She looked like someone who had been cast in a play without her consent, holding a script she couldn't put down.

Nine days. If the date held, I had nine days to figure out whether I was the detective in this story or just another prop.

The river, meanwhile, had already given up its secret and asked for nothing in return. It occurred to me, somewhere around two in the morning, that the client had chosen the Kettle for a reason. Everyone in this town knows the river gives up its dead at dawn.

He hadn't wanted the body hidden.

He'd wanted it *found* — by me.
