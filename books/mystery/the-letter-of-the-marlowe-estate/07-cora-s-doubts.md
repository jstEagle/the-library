## 7. Cora's Doubts

The report was due on Sheriff Dunmore's desk by five o'clock, and by four-fifteen I had written the word *drowning* eleven times and deleted it eleven times, which is a personal record for a woman who once rewrote a parking citation to avoid saying "vehicle" twice in one sentence.

The dead man in the river had a name — or half of one. Wesley "Wes" something, from a wallet that had been in the water long enough to grow its own ecosystem. Forty-ish, no fixed address, last seen alive by nobody who would admit to it. Cause of death, per Ada Blackwood's preliminary findings: asphyxiation due to submersion. Manner: pending.

*Pending.* That word was doing more work than the rest of my report combined.

"Voss." Dunmore's voice through the door, the way he always said my name — like he was reading it off a list and I'd scored lower than expected. "You got that river report?"

I carried it in. He didn't take it. He just looked at me across his desk with the expression of a man watching weather come in.

"Blackwood flag anything?"

"She flagged that she wants more time."

"On a drifter?" He said *drifter* the way other men said *stray dog*. "Man fell in a river. Rivers do that. Write it up."

"The autopsy's not finished."

"It's a drowning, Cora. The water did what water does." He leaned back, chair creaking, and gave me the smile he used at Rotary meetings, the one with all the warmth of a laminated menu. "We got a town full of tourists coming for the Harvest Festival, and you want me to tell the paper we've got a suspicious death? You know what that does to the motel bookings?"

"I know what an unfinished autopsy does to a case file," I said. "It makes it wrong."

He studied me for a moment too long, and I watched him decide something behind his eyes — some small calculation about how much trouble I was worth. Then the smile came back down over his face like a shop shutter.

"Accidental drowning. Pending tox screen if it makes you feel better. Put it in the file, close the file, go home." He picked up his phone. "That's not a request, Deputy."

Here is the thing about me that people get wrong: they think I'm obedient because I'm careful. I am careful because I'm obedient, which is different, and worse. My father wore this same badge for twenty-two years and taught me that the uniform isn't armor, it's a promise — and promises are only good if you keep them when keeping them costs something. So I wrote *accidental drowning* in the manner-of-death field, and my hand didn't shake, and I hated myself with a quiet thoroughness that would have impressed a monk.

Then I went to see Ada Blackwood.

* * *

The county morgue sits behind the clinic like an apology nobody finished saying out loud. It smells of formalin and floor wax and, faintly, of coffee that has been reheated past the point of theology. Ada keeps a pot going that I'm fairly sure predates her employment.

She was at her desk when I came in, writing in one of those ledger books she favors instead of a computer — actual ink, actual paper, the whole thing looking less like modern forensic science and more like a Victorian medium's appointment book. She didn't look up.

"Deputy Voss," she said. "Come to tell me the case is closed? Because I heard. Small towns. The walls have ears and the ears have phones."

"Sheriff closed it. I haven't."

That got her attention. She set down her pen with the deliberation of someone laying down a card she'd been holding all game.

"Coffee?" she asked.

"That stuff could dissolve a boot."

"It has dissolved a boot. Sit down anyway."

I sat. She poured two cups of something the color of creek water after a storm, and we drank it, and for a while neither of us said anything, which is its own kind of conversation. I've always liked that about Ada Blackwood. Half the town thinks she's cold. She's not cold. She's just not interested in filling silence with noise, which in Marlowe's Bend is practically a criminal offense.

"You performed the autopsy," I said finally.

"Yesterday. Preliminary findings are in your inbox."

"I read them. Water in the lungs. Submersion asphyxia. All consistent with drowning."

"But," she said.

"But you requested additional time, and you don't request additional time. I pulled your last forty cases. Your average turnaround is under three days. You closed a decomposed hiker in thirty-six hours. So either Wes-from-the-wallet was fascinating, or something bothered you."

Ada turned her cup slowly on its saucer, one full rotation, like she was winding something.

"Petechial hemorrhaging was consistent with drowning," she said. "So were the diatoms. The water in his lungs matched the river. Everything matched the river." A pause. "Except the bruising pattern on his right shoulder and the back of his neck. Post-mortem. Someone held him under after he was already gone, or moved him in a way that left marks. And there was sediment in a pattern I didn't like — clustered, deliberate-looking, like it had been placed rather than deposited."

"Placed."

"If I were building a drowning," she said, "that's how I'd build it."

The heater ticked in the corner. Somewhere down the hall a freezer compressor cycled on with a sound like a long sigh.

"You're telling me you think it's murder."

"I'm telling you the body reads like a forgery done by someone talented. Which is almost worse." She looked at me straight on then, and her eyes are pale gray, the color of the river in November, and they don't blink much. "What I can't figure, Cora, is why a man drowns himself so carefully three days before anyone expected him to."

I should have asked what she meant by *expected*. That was the door, standing wide open, light spilling out of it. Instead I heard myself say:

"Expected by who?"

And she smiled — a small, dry, unhappy smile — and said, "Exactly," and refilled my cup without asking, and I understood that I had just told her I knew nothing, and that she now knew I knew nothing, and that this exchange had cost us both something we weren't ready to name.

* * *

I went home and didn't sleep, which is normal, but I didn't sleep *productively*, which is new.

Here's what kept circling: Ada Blackwood has closed every case she's ever touched. Not most. Every. In nine years as coroner of a town with more history than population, she has never once filed an open verdict, never passed a hard call upstairs, never let a family leave her office without a name and a reason. People joke that she knows the dead better than the living. It's not entirely a joke. She walks the cemetery at dawn — I've seen her, everybody's seen her, lips moving like she's taking attendance.

A woman like that doesn't ask for extra time on a drifter unless the drifter matters.

And a woman like that doesn't look at me the way she did today — measuring, weighing, deciding how much truth I could carry — unless she's already carrying more than she wants company for.

I made a list at my kitchen table, because lists are how I pray. Things I knew: the dead man matched no missing-persons report in three counties. He'd been in Marlowe's Bend maybe a week, sleeping rough near the rail spur. He had forty-one dollars and a bus ticket stub dated eight days before he died — a ticket he'd apparently never used. And he had, tucked in his hatband where the water hadn't reached, a scrap of paper with a single line written on it in pencil: *the house remembers.*

Things I didn't know: everything else.

I'd logged the note, of course. Logged it, photographed it, entered it into evidence like a good soldier. But I hadn't mentioned it to Dunmore, because when I'd started to mention it he'd been on the phone laughing with somebody about fishing, and something in me had folded shut like a knife. That's the part that scared me. Not the note. The folding.

My father used to say the badge doesn't make you brave, it just makes your cowardice visible. He said a lot of things like that, usually around his third beer, usually staring at the wall. He also said, once, only once, when he was very drunk and I was sixteen: *There are cases you work and cases that work you, and the trick is knowing which one you're in before it's too late.*

I put the list away and lay awake listening to the house settle, thinking about a bus ticket nobody used, and a house that remembered something, and whether I was working this case or it was working me.

* * *

Friday morning I found Ada at the cemetery.

Not by accident. I'd driven past her office, found it dark, and known exactly where she'd be, the way you know where water goes when it rains. She was three rows deep among the older stones, coat collar up against the wind, moving down the line with her hands clasped behind her back like a general reviewing troops that couldn't desert.

"Morning attendance," I said, coming up the path.

"Everyone's present except the Henderson plot, which flooded again. Third time. I've complained to the groundskeeper twice." She glanced at me. "You look terrible."

"Didn't sleep."

"Neither did I. Misery loves a witness." She started walking again, slow, between the rows. I fell in beside her. The wind came off the ridge and moved through the grass with that dry whispering sound, like the whole hillside murmuring behind its hand.

"I read your preliminary report," I said. "The real one. The addendum you sent to the state lab, not the summary that went to the sheriff."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, quickly banked. "How did you see that?"

"I have access to the case file. I'm the investigating deputy. It's almost like the system works sometimes." I kept my eyes forward. "Post-mortem bruising. Deliberate sediment placement. You wrote, quote, 'findings consistent with staged submersion.' You wrote that to the state lab and told our sheriff 'pending.' Those are two different truths, Ada."

"Yes."

"One of them is a lie."

"Yes." She stopped walking. We stood between two rows of stones — the Pruitts on one side, generations of them, and on the other a lone marker for a woman named Ruth Okafor, 1949–1994, the granite gone soft-edged with weather. Ada was looking at that stone, I noticed. Looking at it a beat too long.

"Why?" I asked.

"Because the truth, delivered at the wrong moment, to the wrong audience, dies. And I need this particular truth to live long enough to matter." She turned to face me fully. "You reported the case closed. Under pressure. I know Dunmore's pressure when I see its fingerprints. So here's my question for you, Deputy Voss, and I'd like a real answer: when you wrote *accidental drowning* in that box, did you believe it, or did you write it because you were told to?"

The wind moved the grass. Somewhere a crow commented on the proceedings.

"That's insubordinate," I said.

"It's a question."

"And it's none of your business which—"

"You're right." She said it simply, without heat, and somehow that was worse. "It's not. Forget I asked."

"No." I heard my own voice come out harder than I meant. "No, you don't get to ask that and then un-ask it. You want a real answer? Here it is. I didn't believe it. I wrote it anyway. And I've been sitting with that for two days like a stone in my shoe, and I don't know yet whether that makes me a bad deputy or a smart one, and I resent you for making me say it out loud."

Ada regarded me for a long moment. Then, gravely, she extended her coffee cup, and we clinked them together like the world's saddest toast.

"To stones in shoes," she said.

"What is going on, Ada?" And I meant it — dropped the deputy voice, dropped the circling, just asked, plain, the way you'd ask a friend, if I had friends, which officially I don't, being a cop. "The drifter. The bruising. The way you keep looking at Ruth Okafor's stone like it owes you money. Something walked into your office recently and changed the weather, and I've been a deputy long enough to smell it."

For a second I thought she'd give me nothing. Her face did that thing it does — the shutters coming halfway down, not all the way, leaving just enough gap to see light inside. Then she exhaled through her nose.

"Three weeks ago," she said, "a man came into my office and paid me a great deal of money to prove that a murder will not happen. He named a date. He described the victim." She nodded, very slightly, toward the river side of town, invisible from here but present anyway, the way gravity is present. "Last week, a man matching that description floated into the sluice gates. Early. Wrong day. Like a rehearsal that jumped its cue."

The bottom dropped out of my stomach with a sensation I recognized from crime scenes — that lurch when the picture rearranges itself and the ordinary becomes otherwise.

"He predicted it," I said.

"He predicted it in detail. Details that were not public. Details that—" She stopped herself. Closed her mouth. Recalculated. "Details I'm still accounting for."

"Who was he?"

"Anonymous. Represented by counsel. Paid through a shell company." She looked down at Ruth Okafor's stone again, and this time I followed her gaze properly and saw what she was seeing: a woman dead since 1994, buried alone, no family plot, no second name, nothing. Just a name and two dates, sitting in a row of Pruitts like a guest who'd never been introduced.

"Ada," I said slowly. "Why does a thirty-year-old grave matter to a fresh body in the river?"

She was quiet long enough that I thought she wouldn't answer. Then she said, "Because I think the two questions are the same question. And I think whoever answers one answers both." She started walking again, faster now, purpose re-entering her spine. "Dunmore closed your case. Fine. Let it be closed — officially. Unofficially, I need someone inside the department who hasn't decided what the truth is yet. Someone who writes things down and keeps copies."

"That's called a leak."

"That's called a record. There's a difference, and the difference is whether you're ashamed of it later." She paused at the cemetery gate, hand on the iron post, and looked back at me. "I'm not asking you to break rules, Cora. I'm asking you to notice them breaking themselves. There's a trial I need to read. 1987. State archive, microfilm, public record — technically. I'll be at the library tomorrow morning when it opens."

"Why are you telling me the time and place of your investigation?"

"Because if I'm going to be watched," Ada Blackwood said, "I'd rather it be by someone taking notes."

* * *

I sat in my cruiser outside the library the next morning for twenty minutes, engine off, arguing with myself in both voices — the badge voice and the daughter voice — while frost melted on the windshield in long wet runnels.

The badge voice said: *She's a civilian playing detective with a closed case. Report her. Go home.*

The daughter voice said: *Dad stood in a courtroom in 1987 and came home gray as ash and never said why, and drank himself to death over ten years, and the only time he ever cried in front of you was the night the verdict came down, and he wasn't crying for the dead man.*

I went inside.

Ada was at the microfilm reader in the back corner, coat still on, feeding a reel of the *Marlowe's Bend Courier* from August 1987 through the machine with hands that were perfectly steady, which is how I knew they shouldn't be. The green glow of the screen lit her face from below. On it, frozen mid-scroll, was a headline I'd walked past my whole life in the town museum without ever reading:

**HOUSEKEEPER HELD IN MARLOWE DEATH**

She didn't look up when I sat down beside her. She just advanced the film one frame, so I could see it clearly, and slid a second chair out from under the table with her foot.

"Pull up the trial coverage," she said quietly. "Start to finish. Read everything. Then tell me, Deputy, whether you believe a jury in this town convicted the right person — because I've read it twice now, and I'll tell you what I believe." The reel turned. The headlines crawled past, thirty years old and patient as graves. "I believe somebody taught this whole town a story," Ada said, "and everybody learned their lines."

I reached for the crank. My hand was shaking, and I let her see it.

"Then teach me the real one," I said.
