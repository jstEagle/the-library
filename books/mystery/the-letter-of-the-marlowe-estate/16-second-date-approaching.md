## 16. Second Date Approaching

Four days. That's what the calendar on my office wall said, though I'd stopped trusting it the way you stop trusting a witness who keeps changing his story. The date the client had named — the date a murder was supposed to *not* happen, per the terms of a retainer I couldn't return and couldn't refuse — sat on that calendar like a stain. I'd circled it in red, then drawn a box around it, then written NO in the margin like a schoolgirl defacing a textbook, which is about the level of efficacy that gesture deserved.

The town of Marlowe's Bend had noticed something was up. Towns always notice. We like to pretend we're private people, but a town of eleven hundred souls functions on a nervous system finer than any police radio. The sheriff's office had doubled its patrols, and Deputies Okafor— no, that's not right, that's a name I'd learn to carry later — the deputies were driving their cruisers past the same four corners every twenty minutes, slow enough that Mrs. Pell at the diner had started timing them between refills of her coffee.

"They're looking for something," she told me, sliding my plate across the counter. "You know what it is, Ada. You always know."

"I know when the meatloaf's been sitting out too long," I said. "That's my whole jurisdiction."

She laughed, because Mrs. Pell laughs at everything, but her eyes stayed on mine a beat too long, and I understood that the town had decided I was either the cause of the extra patrols or the cure for them, and in either case I was now a person of interest in the only trial that matters in a small town: the trial of being talked about.

* * *

The sheriff made it official on Tuesday morning. I heard it on the radio in my office — WMRB, the local station that plays easy listening and reads obituaries with the reverence other stations reserve for box scores. Sheriff Dale Pruitt's voice came on, all gravel and reassurance, and announced that the death of the unidentified male found in the Calloway River on the fourteenth had been ruled accidental drowning, case closed, nothing to see here, drive safely.

I put down my coffee and stared at the radio the way you'd stare at a dog that just walked on its hind legs.

Case closed. The drifter — I'd started thinking of him as the drifter, though his name, once we'd finally gotten it, was Gerald Mott, forty-six, of nowhere in particular — had died with bruising under his restraint points and river water in lungs that had already stopped breathing. I knew that because I'd held his lungs in my hands. I'd written it up in language so careful it read like a love letter to ambiguity: *findings consistent with post-mortem submersion; antemortem drowning cannot be definitively excluded.* I'd hedged every sentence, and Dale Pruitt had read my hedging and heard a clean bill.

Which meant one of two things. Either Dale hadn't read my report, which was possible — Dale read reports the way cattle read fences, noticing only where the gaps were — or Dale had read it very carefully indeed, and wanted the file shut before anyone else opened it.

I called Cora Voss.

"You heard," she said, before I'd said anything. It wasn't a question. Cora answered questions before they were asked; it was her most irritating virtue and the thing that made her the only person in that building I trusted, which, given the shortlist of suspects I kept in my desk drawer, was saying something and also saying nothing at all.

"I heard," I said. "Did you sign off on that?"

There was a pause. Cora's pauses were different from other people's pauses. Other people pause because they're deciding what to say. Cora paused because she was deciding how much of the truth she could afford.

"I signed what I was told to sign," she said finally. "The sheriff's office has a closure rate to maintain, Ada. We're up for a state grant. Accidental drowning is a clean line item."

"Gerald Mott was strangled and then put in a river."

"Gerald Mott was a drifter with a blood alcohol of point-two-one and no family and no address," Cora said, and her voice had gone flat, the flatness she used when she was quoting a script she hated. "You want to know what I think? I think somebody wanted that file closed, and I want to know who has the authority to want things in this town."

"Shortlist?" I asked.

"Longer than yours, I'd bet."

I doubted it. My list lived in a locked drawer under a false bottom I'd installed myself, and it had four names on it, and every day it felt less like a lead and more like a prayer.

* * *

Here's the problem I'd been circling for a week, the problem that kept me up at night with the lights on, which is a particular kind of insomnia: the client had described his victim. Height, build, approximate age, a detail about the hands — *work-roughened, a scar across the left palm* — delivered in that same flat, courteous tone he'd used to hand me the retainer. And Gerald Mott had fit. Almost. Except the date was wrong, and the death was staged, a rehearsal, someone's dress rehearsal for a show with a real opening night four days away.

So who was the real target?

I'd pulled the county's employment records, the church rosters, the volunteer fire brigade list, the census supplements. I sat in my office at two in the morning with a legal pad and started matching the description against every man in Marlowe's Bend between forty-five and sixty who did physical work.

The list was eleven names long.

I sat there looking at it and felt the dread come up through the floorboards like cold air. Eleven men. A retired electrician with a scarred palm. The man who ran the feed store. Two of the groundskeepers from the cemetery where I knew every grave by name. A carpenter who'd rebuilt my own porch steps three summers ago and charged me half of what he should have. Eleven men who walked around this town every day, buying groceries, waving at traffic, completely unaware that somewhere out there was a man with a plan and a date and — I was increasingly sure — a method he'd already practiced.

And here was the detail that turned dread into something colder: the description didn't narrow things. It *widened* them. Half the working men in Marlowe's Bend had work-roughened hands. The scar narrowed it some, but scars are common as opinions. The client hadn't given me a name disguised as a description. He'd given me a *category*. And a category, in the hands of a man willing to stage a rehearsal, is a menu.

Unless — and this was the thought I kept turning over like a stone with something underneath it — unless the description wasn't meant to help me at all. Unless it was meant to occupy me. A coroner with eleven names and four days is a coroner who isn't asking other questions. Who is the client? Who paid the retainer through a shell company tied to the Marlowe Estate? Why does a man hire the town coroner to *prevent* a murder instead of just — I don't know — telling her where and when, or calling the police, or any of the hundred saner options available to a person with that much cash?

I was being managed. I'd felt it since the lawyer refused my returned money, that sensation of being a chess piece that has suddenly become aware of the board. And the worst part, the part that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, was that it was working.

* * *

The patrols got thicker. Wednesday morning I counted three cruisers between my house and the office, a distance of six blocks. The sheriff had deputized two volunteers — Earl Hutchins and his son, both of whom had the law-enforcement instincts of garden gnomes — and stationed them at the river access points. The *Marlowe's Bend Gazette* ran a front-page piece titled SHERIFF VOWS SAFETY, above the fold, next to the pie contest results, which struck me as about the right proportion of civic attention.

Vivian Marlowe called me at noon.

"Ada, darling. Lunch. Today. I won't take no." Vivian's voice was the voice of the town's better angels — warm, amused, faintly imperious, the voice of a woman who'd never once been told no by anyone whose opinion mattered. She controlled the Marlowe trust, which controlled half the storefronts on Main Street and the museum and, through channels I was still mapping, possibly the sheriff's grant funding. She'd appeared at the edges of my investigation three times now, always helpful, always a half-step ahead, and I'd catalogued that the way I catalogued everything: in the drawer, under the false bottom, in a section labeled *coincidence, suspicious*.

"I'm working," I said.

"You're always working. That's what I admire about you. The diner, one o'clock. I'm buying, and I've already told them to put the good coffee on."

She hung up before I could refuse, which was, I suspected, the point. Vivian Marlowe didn't issue invitations; she issued facts with a question mark.

At the diner she was already installed in the corner booth, wearing a camel coat that cost more than my car, looking like the town's patron saint of tasteful philanthropy. She poured my coffee herself.

"You look tired," she said. "All this fuss about the river man, I suppose. Terrible business. Though the sheriff says it was just an accident."

"The sheriff says a lot of things."

"He does." She smiled over her cup. "Ada, can I be candid with you? I find I can't be candid with anyone in this town. Everyone wants something from the trust. But you — you returned a retainer once, I hear. A very large one. Who *does* that?"

"A person who doesn't like strings."

"Mm." She stirred her coffee though there was nothing in it. "And did the strings come off?"

I looked at her, and she looked back, and for a moment — half a second, maybe less — the warmth in her face went somewhere else, somewhere assessing and cool, like a jeweler turning a stone under a lamp. Then it was back, the smile, the charm, the coat.

"Why do you ask, Vivian?"

"Because when money moves in this town, I usually know about it," she said lightly. "And lately money has been moving in ways I don't recognize. It makes a woman nervous." She patted my hand as she rose, leaving bills on the table that covered both meals and a tip the size of my grocery budget. "Four days, isn't it? Until whatever it is you're all waiting for. Try to get some sleep before then, darling. You're no use to anyone exhausted."

I sat there after she left, my coffee going cold, doing the arithmetic. Four days. I'd never told her the date. The sheriff hadn't announced it. It was in exactly one place outside my own head: the contract terms I'd never signed but somehow agreed to.

Which meant Vivian had seen the contract.

Or Vivian *wrote* it.

Or — and I wrote this in the drawer-list that night, my handwriting getting less steady as the list got longer — Vivian knew about the contract the same way the client knew about my autopsy reports: because the walls of this town had ears, and someone had built them.

* * *

That night I did something I hadn't done since I took the job: I drove out to the cemetery after dark. Not to work. Just to stand among the stones, where the dead keep better records than the living.

Frost was coming. My breath hung in the flashlight beam. I walked the rows I knew by heart — the Hollis plot, the Pruitts, the long row of Marlowes with their granite faces and their dates like a dynasty's ledger — and I found myself, as I always did, at the pauper's section where the stones are small and the names are fewer. Ruth Okafor wasn't buried there. Ruth Okafor died in the state prison and was buried in the prison cemetery, forty miles away, in a grave I'd looked up on a records site at three in the morning and then closed the laptop on, hard, like shutting a door on a crying child.

Her son had vanished. That was the file. Convicted housekeeper, dead in prison, son gone, case sealed, town silent, everyone reciting the same polished story like a script handed out at the door.

I stood there in the cold and thought: *eleven men, four days, one town that lies in unison.* And underneath that, quieter, the thought I'd been avoiding: *somebody wanted me to be standing exactly here, exactly now, asking exactly these questions.* The client hadn't just hired a coroner. He'd hired *me* — the woman who closes every case, who knows every grave by name, who cannot leave a file open without it itching. He'd hired the one person in Marlowe's Bend constitutionally incapable of letting this go.

Which meant the murder — the real one, four days out — might not be the point at all. It might be the *bait*. And I was the fish who'd already swallowed the hook and was now swimming, very diligently, in whatever direction the line was pulled.

My radio crackled. Cora's voice, low, off-channel, the frequency we'd started using for conversations that shouldn't exist.

"Ada. You awake?"

"Standing in a cemetery, so, philosophically yes."

A pause. I could hear her breathing, and under it something else — hesitation, which on Cora sounded like an alarm.

"There's something I need to show you," she said. "Not at the office. Not on the phone. And Ada — if anyone asks, I haven't called you tonight."

The line went dead. I stood in the frost among the dead of Marlowe's Bend, four days out from a murder that hadn't happened yet, holding a radio that had just made me complicit in something, and understood that whatever Cora Voss was carrying, she'd been carrying it alone for a long time.

I got in the car and followed her taillights out of town.
