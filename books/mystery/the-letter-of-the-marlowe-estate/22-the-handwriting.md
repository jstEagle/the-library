## 22. The Handwriting

There is a particular kind of intimacy in handwriting that most people never think about, because they've never had to. I have. Twenty years of reading the last things people ever wrote — suicide notes, shopping lists found in coat pockets, birthday cards signed three days before the aneurysm — has taught me that a hand is a confession in itself. It tells you how old the writer was, whether they were hurried or calm, right-handed or left, whether they'd been drinking, whether they were afraid. Handwriting is the body's autobiography.

So when I spread four documents across my kitchen table at two in the morning and realized they might all be related by blood, I felt something worse than fear. I felt like I was reading someone's family album without their permission.

The documents:

One. The retainer letter — the single sheet of heavy cream paper that had arrived with the stranger's money, typed except for the signature line, which bore a looping, confident *E.W.* No, wait. Let me be precise, because precision was about to matter more than it ever had in my career. The signature wasn't initials. It was a full name written in a cursive so formal it looked rehearsed: each letter formed with the kind of care you learn from copybooks, not from life. The capital letters were oversized. The descenders — the tails on the g's and y's — swept long and slightly left, like they were reaching back for something.

Two. The notes to Daniel Okafor. Three of them, photographed before I returned them to his trailer, because Daniel had enough sense not to keep originals of things that frightened him. Block capitals, all of them, which should have made comparison impossible — block print hides personality the way a mask hides a face. Except it doesn't, not entirely. Pressure still shows. Spacing still shows. And the way a person crosses their t's and dots their i's is a habit so deep it survives even the decision to disguise it.

Three. A photocopy of Ruth Okafor's 1987 confession, pulled from the microfilm archive at the county library at closing time, with the librarian's pitying glance following me out the door. The confession was two pages, signed *Ruth Adaeze Okafor* in a hand that trembled on the first page and steadied by the second — steadied too much, actually, into a kind of rigid perfection that no natural writer produces under duress. People who are forced to sign things either scrawl or overperform. Ruth had overperformed. Every letter identical to its siblings, like soldiers on parade. Like someone had made her practice.

And four — this was the one I'd almost overlooked — a letter from the Marlowe Estate museum's public display case. A 1984 household inventory, written in Ruth's own daily hand, the real one, the one she used for grocery lists and menus and notes to the cook. It was loose and warm and human, the cursive of a woman who wrote quickly because there was always another chore waiting. The museum had it behind glass because it was "period authentic." Nobody had looked at it as evidence in thirty-seven years.

I put on my reading glasses, which I resent, and got to work.

* * *

Here is what forensic document examination actually looks like when you're doing it alone at your kitchen table with a jeweler's loupe, a desk lamp, and a pot of coffee going cold: it looks like falling in love with strangers against your will.

I started with Ruth's inventory letter, because it was the only sample I trusted. Her lowercase *e* had a distinctive tilt — the crossbar sat high, giving every e a startled expression. Her *r* ended in a small upward flick, impatient. She connected some letters and broke others mid-word, inconsistently, the mark of someone who wrote fast and thought faster. And her capital *R* — this was the detail that stopped me — was built like a piece of architecture. A straight spine, then a bowl that didn't close, leaving a gap where the leg kicked out. An open-bowl R. Uncommon. Maybe one writer in twenty does it, and once you learn to see it, you see it everywhere, the way you can't unsee a word once someone points out its spelling.

Then I looked at the retainer signature.

Open-bowl R.

I sat back. The house ticked around me. Somewhere down the street a dog was complaining about the moon.

Coincidence, said the reasonable part of my brain, the part that had kept me employed and sane in a town that preferred its mysteries unsolved. Open bowls happen. Loops happen. You're seeing patterns because you want to see them, Ada, because a pattern would mean this whole nightmare has a shape, and a shape means an ending.

But then I did what I should have done first. I stopped looking at letterforms and started looking at pressure.

Pressure is the honest part of writing. Letterforms can be taught, copied, disguised. Pressure can't — not really, not over a full page. It's governed by habit and muscle, by the speed of the hand, by whether the writer hesitates. Under magnification, ink sits differently where a pen has been pressed hard: darker, wider, sometimes embossed into the paper deep enough to feel with a fingertip.

Ruth's inventory letter: light, quick pressure, consistent, the touch of a woman who wrote thousands of words a week and didn't waste effort on any of them.

Her confession: heavy pressure, uneven, digging. Written slowly, by someone fighting the words coming out of her own hand. Or — and here the cold moved up my arms — guided. There were places where the strokes wobbled and corrected, where the pen had paused mid-letter and resumed, and those pauses fell at exactly the spots where a coach standing behind a chair would say, *no, start again, like this.*

Someone had taught Ruth to write her own confession. Or rewritten it for her and made her copy it until it matched.

And the retainer letter? Light pressure. Quick. Confident. The same rhythm as the inventory letter, the same economy, the same impatience in the r's. But the letterforms themselves were wrong — too formal, too schooled, the copybook cursive of someone who had learned to write from a specific teacher rather than from the general culture.

Specifically: from Ruth.

I set the pages down and pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes until I saw constellations.

Because here's the thing about learning to write. Almost nobody learns it twice. Your hand is set by eight, ten, twelve years old, and after that it changes only by erosion — fatigue, age, injury, deliberate disguise. If the person who signed my retainer letter wrote with Ruth Okafor's rhythm, Ruth Okafor's economy, Ruth Okafor's open-bowl R dressed up in Sunday clothes — then that person had learned the bones of their handwriting inside Ruth Okafor's orbit. At her kitchen table. Under her correction. As a child whose letters she shaped the way she shaped everything else in her care.

Not Daniel. Daniel had been eleven when his mother went to prison; he'd have had her hand in him too, but Daniel's own writing — I'd seen it on his statement to Cora — was a cramped, angry print, all hard angles and gouged paper. Grief had gotten into his grip. Whatever Ruth had given him, prison had overwritten it.

But a nephew. A child raised in the servants' wing after the conviction, per the docent roster I'd memorized. A child who would have received her letters, if anyone wrote to the children of convicted murderers in Marlowe's Bend. Who would have kept them. Who might have learned to write by copying them, the way lonely children copy anything that feels like love.

*Elias Wren,* said the roster in my head, in its tidy little voice.

I hadn't confirmed it yet. I want to be clear about that, because the difference between suspicion and knowledge is the difference between a coroner and a lynch mob, and I have spent my whole career on the right side of that line. What I had was a hypothesis wearing a very convincing costume.

What I needed was the notes to Daniel.

* * *

Block capitals. I'd dismissed them as useless. Now I went back with new eyes.

Disguised writing always overcorrects. A forger hiding their hand makes the disguise too uniform — every letter the same height, every stroke the same weight, because they're drawing letters instead of writing them. Real spontaneous print breathes. Disguised print holds its breath.

These notes held their breath beautifully. Too beautifully. Every E identical, every T crossed at precisely the same angle, the whole thing executed with the discipline of a sign painter. And underneath the discipline, visible only at 10x magnification, the pressure told the truth: the vertical strokes were heavy, the connecting impulses were light-and-quick. A cursive hand pretending to print. A hand that thought in joined-up letters and was forcing itself apart, stroke by stroke, the way you force yourself to walk past a house you used to live in.

And the t-crossings. In the block print, the t-bars floated high above their stems — a floating t-bar is a classic marker of a cursive writer in disguise, because in cursive the crossing happens as part of the flow, and when you break the flow the bar loses its anchor and drifts.

Ruth's inventory letter: floating t-bars. High, horizontal, unhurried.

My kitchen was very quiet. The dog down the street had given up on the moon. I could hear my own pulse in my ears, and beneath it, faintly, the sound of a case rearranging itself around a new center of gravity.

The notes to Daniel weren't written by a stranger threatening an outsider. They were written by family. By someone whose hand had been raised on Ruth Okafor's letters — or, God help me, possibly by someone who had studied those letters the way I was studying them now, forensically, deliberately, with intent to impersonate. That possibility existed and I refused to look away from it. Because if the notes were genuine Elias, then Elias was threatening the man he should most want as an ally, and that made no sense unless he was protecting something. And if the notes were forged — if someone wanted Daniel scared, wanted him looking guilty, wanted him run out of town or into custody —

Then whoever forged them had access to Ruth's letters. Access to the estate. Access to the archive that had been sealed since 1987, sealed fast, sealed within the hour of my request, by someone who could move institutional walls without appearing to push them.

Vivian Marlowe controlled the trust. Vivian Marlowe funded half the town. Vivian Marlowe had been a step ahead of me at every turn, charming and generous and present, and I had liked her, which was exactly the trick, whatever the trick was.

Or Hollis Grange, terrified, blackmailed for decades, freed now that the records were stolen — freed, or unleashed. Blackmailers don't release their assets out of kindness. Unless the theft of the records hadn't been Elias at all. Unless someone else had read the plan — my own thought from days ago, returning like a boomerang — and was using it as cover.

I laid the documents side by side one final time and made myself say it plainly, the way I'd say it in a report, if reports could contain this:

Three hands, one inheritance. Ruth's living hand in the inventory. Ruth's coerced hand in the confession — coached, corrected, rehearsed. And a third hand, young and disciplined and grieving somewhere under its discipline, that carried Ruth's rhythm forward like a surname carried in the body instead of on paper.

Whoever wrote to me had been taught to write by Ruth Okafor, or taught to imitate the woman who was. And there were only two categories of people who fit: her children, and her students.

Daniel was accounted for. Which left the quiet man in the museum who dusted the display cases and knew exactly which documents sat behind which glass, including a 1984 grocery list that nobody had looked at in thirty-seven years.

Except — and this is the hook that kept me awake until the birds started — the display case had been locked when I'd left the museum Tuesday evening. I remembered checking, because checking locks is what I do.

Wednesday morning, when I'd come back to photograph the inventory letter properly, the case had been unlocked. Nothing missing. Nothing disturbed. Just unlocked, the way a door gets unlocked by someone who wants you to find what's inside.

Elias Wren had handed me the key to his own identity. Or someone had used his museum, his roster, his dead aunt's handwriting, to hand me a key that opened onto the wrong door entirely.

I picked up the phone and dialed the number Daniel had scratched on the back of one of the photographs. It rang four times. Five.

"Ada?" His voice was raw, sleepless. "I was going to call you. Someone was in my trailer last night."

"What did they take?"

"Nothing." A pause, and I heard him swallow. "They left something. A letter. In her handwriting, Ada. In my mother's handwriting. And it's dated tomorrow."
