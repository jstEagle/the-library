## 24. Who Taught You to Write

There is a particular hour — somewhere between the third cup of coffee and the moment you realize you've been staring at the same document long enough to memorize its typos — when an investigation stops being a puzzle and becomes an obsession with good posture. I was deep in that hour, sitting at my kitchen table with the contents of my evidence boxes fanned out around me like a very grim tarot spread, when the thought finally landed.

The loops.

I'd been looking at the pressure patterns for two days. Anyone can compare handwriting; that's forensic kindergarten. What I'd been doing was something less scientific and more unsettling: I was getting to know Ruth Okafor. Not the woman — the woman was thirty years dead and had left nothing of herself but a coerced confession and a prison record — but her hand. The way she crossed her t's with a slight upward flick, like a woman in a hurry to finish. The way her o's leaned left, against the natural drift of the alphabet, as if even her letters were bracing against something. The way she pressed hard on the downstrokes and barely touched the upstrokes, which is what you get from someone taught to write with a cheap pen on cheap paper, someone who learned that ink is a resource to be rationed.

And the notes left for Daniel Okafor — the ones designed to look like his own hand — carried all of it. The leftward o's. The rationed ink. The flick on the t's. Whoever wrote them had studied Ruth Okafor's handwriting with an intimacy that went past forgery and into inheritance.

You can learn to copy a hand from samples. Graphologists do it. Check fraudsters do it. But there's a difference between copying a signature off a check and writing a full page in someone's rhythm — the spacing, the fatigue patterns, the way the hand tires in the fourth line and recovers in the fifth. To do that, you don't study the writing.

You grow up watching it.

I set down my coffee and said, out loud, to an empty kitchen, "Oh, you absolute fool, Ada."

Because if the client had learned Ruth's hand by watching it — really watching it, day after day, letter after letter, the way a child learns everything — then Ruth hadn't just been a housekeeper who wrote letters home. She'd been raising someone. Teaching them. Sitting beside them at a table while they practiced their alphabet and she practiced her patience.

The client was connected to the household. Not a hired investigator who'd dug through the archive. Not a journalist with a microfilm reader and a hunch. Family. Or as close to family as the Marlowe house had ever permitted the help.

I looked at the retainer letter again, the one that had started all of this. I'd read it a dozen times for content. Now I read it for texture. The paper was good stock, expensive, the kind of thing a lawyer's office would supply. But the handwriting on the address line — my name, in block capitals, deliberately neutral — had a hesitation in it. A tiny stall before the B. The kind of stall a hand makes when it's fighting its own training, forcing itself not to do the thing it does naturally.

Whoever wrote my name had spent their whole life writing a certain way and had to suppress it, letter by letter, just to address an envelope.

* * *

I did what I always do when the ground starts tilting: I made a list. Lists are load-bearing. When everything else is falling down, you can stand on a list.

The Marlowe household, circa 1980 through 1987, per the trial coverage and what the town's memory had coughed up so far:

Edmund Marlowe, patriarch, deceased. Vivian's great-uncle, though the family tree had been pruned so aggressively over the generations that "great-uncle" might mean anything from two branches to six.

The business partner, whose name I still didn't have — the trial coverage had been oddly gentle with him, the way small towns are gentle with money.

Ruth Okafor, housekeeper, convicted, dead.

Daniel Okafor, her son, vanished, now found and broken and, as far as I could tell, innocent of the current mischief — a man who'd come home to dig up his mother's grave metaphorically and found someone else already holding the shovel.

And the servants' wing. Every old house like Marlowe House had one, and every servants' wing had its small population: cooks, gardeners, drivers, the occasional niece or nephew taken in when someone died or drank themselves out of options. The trial coverage hadn't named a single one of them. Servants don't make the paper unless they're on trial.

But museums make brochures.

I drove out to Marlowe House just after noon, when the light was flat and the parking lot was empty in the way it's always empty. The docent's car wasn't there — the docent whose name I was trying to remember and failing to, which should have been my first warning, because I forget faces, never names. A volunteer with a cardigan and a cough sold me a five-dollar admission ticket without looking up from her crossword.

"I'd like to see the staff exhibit," I said.

"We don't have a staff exhibit."

"Every house museum has a staff exhibit. 'The Unsung Hands of Marlowe House.' 'Below Stairs.' Something with a pun in it."

She almost smiled. "There's a photograph in the kitchen. The family didn't believe in naming the help, if that's what you're asking."

"It is."

The kitchen of Marlowe House had been preserved the way museums preserve kitchens: as a set dressed for a play that ended decades ago. Copper pots hung in a row, polished by volunteers rather than use. And on the wall, in a frame that had once been nicer than it now was, hung the photograph.

A dozen people, arranged on the back steps in 1981, per the caption. Edmund Marlowe front and center, wearing the expression of a man who believed the photograph was for his benefit. Beside him, a woman I recognized from her trial photo — Ruth Okafor, younger, standing slightly apart from the group in the way that people who are tolerated rather than included always stand. And around them, the unnamed. A gardener. A driver. Two women I took for kitchen staff. A boy.

I stepped closer. The boy was maybe ten, thin, standing at the edge of the frame as if he'd been added at the last minute, or had added himself. He was looking at the camera the way children look at cameras when they haven't yet learned to perform for them — directly, seriously, with the flat honesty of someone who doesn't know he's supposed to smile.

The caption named four people. Edmund. His late wife. The partner — *H. Hale, Sr.*, which made my stomach do something complicated — and Ruth Okafor, "housekeeper."

The boy was a captionless boy. A boy the family hadn't believed in naming.

"Who's the child?" I asked the volunteer, who had followed me at the distance museums train their staff to keep.

"Oh, that'd be one of the Okafor woman's relations, I expect. There was a nephew, I think, came to live in the wing after his mother passed. Elias? Edwin? Something old-fashioned." She frowned at her crossword memory. "He works here now, actually. Tuesdays and Thursdays. Quiet as anything. You'd like the tour if he's in — he knows where all the bodies are buried. Figuratively," she added, and then, hearing herself, went slightly pink.

"Does he," I said.

My voice came out perfectly level. Twenty years of delivering news to grieving families will do that for you. Inside, I was doing arithmetic at a speed that would have embarrassed me.

The docent. The quiet one. The man who'd refused my archive request within the hour — no. Wait. The request had been refused before he could have seen it. Or had it? I'd assumed the refusal came from the trustee's office, fast and smooth, an invisible wall. But walls are only invisible until you find the hand that built them, and the fastest hand in that building might have belonged to a man with a key ring and a Tuesday-Thursday schedule and twenty years of learning which drawers in which rooms were never locked because nobody thought the help would look.

Elias. Something old-fashioned. Wren. The name surfaced from the museum roster I'd skimmed a week ago and filed under *irrelevant*, because docents are furniture, furniture with name tags.

Elias Wren.

* * *

I didn't go home. I went to the diner, because I needed coffee I hadn't made myself and because Cora Voss had a sixth sense for finding me when I was in the middle of a thought I shouldn't be having alone.

She was already in a back booth, which either meant she'd been waiting for me or that Marlowe's Bend has exactly one diner and the laws of probability do the rest. I slid in across from her.

"You look like you've seen a ghost," she said.

"Worse. I've seen a photograph."

I told her. Not everything — I'm still capable of discretion, whatever my suspension-adjacent career might suggest — but the shape of it. The handwriting. The household. The captionless boy.

"So the client grew up in the servants' wing," Cora said slowly, stirring her coffee like it had personally wronged her. "Raised by Ruth. Learned her hand before he learned cursive in school, maybe instead of it."

"Maybe. It's a theory. Theories are cheaper than evidence, and I've been paying retail all week."

"And the docent — Wren — you're thinking he's Ruth's nephew, the boy in the photo."

"I'm thinking the roster says Elias Wren, hired eleven years ago, references unavailable, and nobody in this town remembers interviewing him. I'm thinking a man who wanted access to that archive couldn't have picked a better cover than the one position in the building that lets you handle every artifact without anyone asking why."

"Or," Cora said, "he's a quiet guy who likes old houses and got a job he's overqualified for, and you're about to terrify an innocent man because his name was in a caption."

"Also possible. That's what I like about this case. Every door opens onto either a revelation or a lawsuit."

She almost smiled, then didn't. "Ada. If you're right — if the client was raised by Ruth Okafor — then he's not just avenging her. He's avenging the only mother he had. And people who avenge their mothers don't quit because a coroner asks nicely."

"I'm not planning to ask nicely."

"What are you planning?"

I looked out the window at the street where I'd lived my whole competent, invisible life, and I told her the truth. "I don't know yet. But I know where I'm going next."

* * *

The museum closed at four. I arrived at ten past, in the gray hour when the volunteers go home and the building belongs to whoever has keys. The front door was locked. The side door, the one that opens onto the kitchen — the servants' entrance, because old houses never let you forget the geography — was unlocked.

I want to be clear that I stood outside that door for a full two minutes, weighing things. Breaking into a museum is a crime. Breaking into a museum to confront a man who may be a murderer is a worse idea. Breaking into a museum to confront a man who may be a murderer and who has, by every indication, been three steps ahead of me since the day he walked into my office with a cash retainer and a lie shaped like a request — that's not an idea at all. That's a compulsion wearing a trench coat.

I went in.

The kitchen smelled of lemon polish and dust. The photograph watched me from the wall, the captionless boy watching me back across forty years. I stood in front of it for a moment and let myself look properly this time — at Ruth's face, at the way her hand rested on the boy's shoulder, not possessively but protectively, the way you hold on to something you've been told you'll have to give back.

Then I heard it. From somewhere in the back of the house, past the velvet ropes and the do-not-touch signs: the small, particular sound of a drawer opening in a room where no one is supposed to be.

I didn't call out. Calling out gives away the element of surprise, and surprise was the only asset I had. I moved through the house the way I'd learned to move through crime scenes, heel to toe, avoiding the boards I could hear were loose. The sound led me down the east corridor, past the bedrooms with their velvet ropes, to the study.

Edmund Marlowe's study. Preserved, like everything else, mid-scene, as if the man had just stepped out to terrorize someone and would return shortly. The desk. The ledger. The window with its view of the river.

And at the desk, with his back to me, a thin man in a cardigan, going through the drawers with the unhurried familiarity of someone who has done it a hundred times.

"Elias Wren," I said.

He didn't startle. That was the first thing I noticed, and it frightened me more than a flinch would have. He simply closed the drawer, turned, and looked at me with the mild, patient expression of a man who has been waiting a very long time for this exact conversation and is mildly disappointed it took me so long.

"Dr. Blackwood." His voice was quiet, pleasant, the voice of a thousand tours. "You found the photograph."

"The caption doesn't name you."

"No. The family didn't believe in naming the help." He said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse. "Ruth named me, though. She named everyone. Said a thing without a name couldn't be grieved properly." He folded his hands. "You've been very thorough, Dr. Blackwood. Better than the police were. Better than the courts. I chose you for that."

"You chose me," I said, "to stop a murder."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt. Something older and sadder than guilt.

"Yes," he said. "That's what I told you."

He reached into the cardigan's pocket, and my whole body went tight — but what he drew out wasn't a weapon. It was a folder, thick, soft-cornered, the kind of folder that gets carried for years.

"Before you decide what to do with me," Elias Wren said, "you should read what's in here. All of it. Because I promise you, Doctor — the murder I hired you to prevent is real. It's just not the one you think."

And the ground, which had been tilting all day, finally gave way.
