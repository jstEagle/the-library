## 31. Falling

The knock came at 6:47 in the morning, which told me everything before I opened the door. Nobody knocks on a coroner's door at dawn with good news. Good news keeps banker's hours.

There were three of them on my porch: a state investigator whose suit had been pressed by someone who hated him, a deputy I didn't recognize, and Sheriff Dunmore himself, standing slightly behind the other two like a man who wanted to be able to say later that he'd merely been present.

"Dr. Blackwood." The investigator held up a folder the way a priest holds up a cross — not as an offering but as a defense. "We have a warrant for the seizure of materials from your office and residence, and a notice of administrative suspension, effective immediately."

I looked past him at the gray light coming up over the ridge, at the frost still silvering Mrs. Petraglia's hedges across the street, and thought: *he moves fast when he's scared.* Then I thought: *good.* Fear was information. Fear meant we'd gotten close enough to burn.

"You'll want to see the warrant," the investigator said, because I hadn't moved.

"I'm sure I will." I stepped back and let them in, because the fastest way to learn what a man is afraid of is to watch him search your house. He went straight for my filing cabinet — not the autopsy records, which were public-adjacent and boring, but the bottom drawer where I kept my private notes. The list of four names. The copies from the Marlowe archive. The timeline I'd built across my kitchen table with red thread like a madwoman's conspiracy wall, except mine was accurate, which is the difference between madness and method.

He photographed everything. He didn't ask me a single question, which was its own kind of answer. Men who want the truth ask questions. Men carrying out an errand just take things.

"Suspension pending investigation into evidence tampering," Dunmore said, when the others were loading boxes into their car. He said it to my porch railing rather than to me. "You know how it looks, Ada. Unofficial archive access. Documents copied without authorization. A private investigation running parallel to an open case."

"What open case?" I asked. "You closed it."

His jaw did something complicated. "The drifter case has been reopened," he said, "in light of new evidence."

"Whose evidence?"

He didn't answer that either. He got in his car, and as he pulled away he didn't look at me, and I understood that Marcus Hale hadn't even needed to call him personally. The machinery just knew. That's what thirty years of owning a town buys you: obedience so deep it doesn't require instruction, only expectation.

I stood on my porch holding a piece of paper that said I no longer existed professionally, and I did the arithmetic. Forty-eight hours ago I'd sat across from Judge Hale in his chambers and agreed, out loud, to trade my silence for my career. Cora had been listening through the wall and had looked at me afterward like I'd handed her a live grenade with the pin already gone. I'd told her: *he'll believe it until it stops being useful to him to believe it. And then he'll move. Our job is to make sure he moves too late.*

Well. He'd moved.

* * *

By nine o'clock they had Elias Wren.

I heard it from the docent's own lawyer, a tired man named Okafor—no relation, he assured me twice, though I suspected the name was why he'd taken the case—calling from the county jail. Arson. The care home fire. They had a witness who placed Elias's car on the service road the night of the fire, they had accelerant residue in a container recovered from the museum's maintenance shed, and they had, the lawyer said with the flat bitterness of a man reading someone else's bad handwriting, a motive theory: that Elias had set the fire to destroy the last living witness to Ruth Okafor's alibi, tying off the loose end of his own vendetta.

"That's—" I stopped. Started again. "That's the exact opposite of the truth. He's spent twenty years collecting proof. Why would he burn the one person who could corroborate it?"

"Because the truth doesn't have to survive contact with a jury," Okafor said. "It just has to survive a press conference. And this morning's press conference was very thorough." He paused. "They also found threatening letters in his quarters at the estate. In his handwriting."

"In his—" My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "Those letters were written *to* him. Someone forged them to frame him. I can prove the hand doesn't match his natural writing, I've done comparative analysis, I have the samples—"

"Then you should bring them to someone with the authority to look at them," Okafor said gently. "Do you know anyone with authority, Dr. Blackwood?"

I hung up on him, which was rude, and sat with the phone in my lap, which was honest.

Elias had planned for everything. Twenty years of planning, a dossier thick as a Bible, a scheme elaborate enough to use a coroner as his instrument of daylight. But he had planned for a town that would resist the truth, not a town that would be *led* against it. He'd assumed the conspiracy would hide. He hadn't accounted for the possibility that it would attack — that Hale, cornered, would stop being a judge and start being what he'd always been underneath: the son of a killer, guarding the family business.

And Daniel. God, Daniel.

* * *

I found out about Daniel from Cora, who called me from the road at noon, her voice stripped down to wire.

"They picked him up at the bus station in Halden. Flight risk, they're saying — he bought a ticket."

"He was going to visit his mother's grave," I said. "Ruth's anniversary is Friday. He told me. He bought the ticket two weeks ago, before any of this—"

"I know." A breath. "Ada, they're holding him in county, separate wing, and the intake paperwork says he's a 'person of interest' in the arson. They've assigned him a public defender who graduated law school in May."

"Who signed the detention order?"

Silence on the line. Long enough that I could hear the road under her tires.

"Cora. Who signed it?"

"Hale," she said finally. "Judge Hale signed it personally. Which is legal, technically, because it's a county matter and he's the sitting judge, but judges don't sign detention orders personally, Ada. Judges don't do anything personally. It's the whole point of them."

She was quiet a moment longer, and then she said the thing I think she'd been driving toward the whole call, saying it fast, like pulling a splinter:

"When I listened outside Hale's chambers — when you took his deal — I thought you'd lost your mind. Or sold out. I drove home that night and I couldn't decide which and I didn't sleep. And now he's suspended you anyway, and arrested Elias anyway, and detained Daniel anyway, and I keep thinking: if you'd actually been corrupt, none of this would've happened. He'd have left you alone. You'd still have your office and your name." Another breath. "So either you played him wrong, or you played him exactly right and this is what winning looks like right now. And I need you to tell me which, because I put my career in a box in my closet next to my father's jury notes, and I'd like to know if I buried it for nothing."

I stood in my kitchen, in a house that had been searched that morning, surrounded by the absence of my own files, and I gave her the truest thing I had.

"It's what winning looks like right now," I said. "Hale believes I took his deal. That's the only reason I'm suspended instead of dead or charged. Suspension is him cashing the check — he thinks he bought me, so he's spending me. Every move he makes while he believes that is a move made by a man who thinks he's safe. Safe men get sloppy."

"And if he doesn't believe it anymore?"

"Then I find out the way everyone finds out about Marcus Hale," I said. "Suddenly."

* * *

The long dark night, as the poets have it, arrived on schedule around eleven p.m., though mine started earlier and ran longer than most.

I sat at my kitchen table with nothing left but what lived in my head, which turned out to be more than I'd expected. They'd taken the copies, the photographs, the red-thread timeline. They had not taken my memory, and memory, it turns out, is the one archive that requires no authorization to access. I wrote down everything I could reconstruct: names, dates, the chain of custody of every document I'd touched, the exact phrasing of Hollis Grange's confession, the child's nickname he'd given me — *the little wren*, he'd said, and hadn't known why he remembered it until I told him.

But writing it all down meant writing it all down somewhere they could find it, and I had learned that morning that anywhere I could go, a warrant could follow. So I wrote it twice — once for myself, once to be destroyed — and then I sat there in the lamplight doing the other kind of arithmetic, the kind that doesn't resolve.

Here is what despair actually feels like, for the record, since I was the county's professional observer of human endings: it doesn't feel like sadness. It feels like paperwork. It feels like watching a machine grind forward with perfect procedural correctness — warrants served, orders signed, press conferences held — each gear turning legally, each step defensible, the whole apparatus rolling toward a verdict that was decided before anyone filed anything. Ruth Okafor had died inside that machine. Her son had been erased by it. And now it was eating Elias Wren, twenty years of patience and grief compressed into an arson charge, and it was eating Daniel, and it had spat me out onto a porch with a piece of paper, and every single part of it was *legal.*

That was the horror of Marcus Hale. Not cruelty. Correctness.

I thought about calling the journalists Elias had lined up. I thought about the dossier — where was it? Elias had handed me a full copy weeks ago; I'd hidden it, and the investigators hadn't found it, which meant either they hadn't looked hard enough or they had and were waiting to see what I did with it. A baited drawer. Possibly both.

I thought about the fixer. Because here was the thing nobody else seemed to be counting: whatever happened in the courtroom, whatever happened to me or Elias or Daniel, there was still a man walking around Marlowe's Bend who killed people for a living, who had staged two deaths as rehearsals of a murder committed in 1954—or '87, depending on which body you were counting—and whose final performance required a body in the Marlowe House study. Hale had cleaned up the paper trail. But the fixer wasn't paper. The fixer was the part of the machine that worked when the machine couldn't.

And Hale had just demonstrated, publicly, that he believed the threat was over.

* * *

At two in the morning I gave up on sleep and walked to the cemetery, because when the world goes wrong I go where the facts are. The gate was unlocked; it usually is; the dead of Marlowe's Bend have never once tried to leave.

Frost on the grass. My breath hanging in front of me. I walked the rows I knew by heart and stopped, without planning to, at the pauper's section where a small weathered stone read RUTH OKAFOR, 1931–1991, and nothing else. No epitaph. No *beloved mother*. Thirty years of the town's silence, cut into granite.

"They took everything," I said out loud, to her, because there was no one else to say it to. "Your file, your alibi, your son's freedom, your nephew's good name. They did it with signatures. Every page stamped and filed and correct."

The stone said nothing, having the good manners not to editorialize.

And then, because I am who I am, I did what I always do at that cemetery: I counted. Who was missing. Who was newly vulnerable. The alibi witness, Edith Marsh, alive in a care home with a burned-out room and a hospital bracelet. Hollis Grange, freed from blackmail and therefore suddenly dangerous to everyone who'd ever used him. Cora, holding her father's jury notes like a lit match. And Elias, in a cell, whose escape — whose *staged* escape — was supposed to be our bait.

Our plan had assumed we'd control the timing. That we'd choose the night, rig the rooms, place the witnesses. But plans are documents, and documents can be seized, and I had to consider, standing there in the cold with my hands in my pockets, the possibility that Hale's raid that morning hadn't been only about punishment. That somewhere in my seized files was the outline of the trap itself — the false detail, the rehearsal pattern, the study at Marlowe House. That the machine might not just be grinding us down. It might be *reading us.*

A car passed on the road beyond the fence, slow, and I watched its taillights without moving, and it kept going, and I told myself that paranoia was just pattern recognition with poor boundaries.

Mostly I believed it.

* * *

Cora found me at seven the next morning, still at the cemetery, because of course she did; she'd learned my habits the way I'd learned the town's. She came through the gate carrying two cups of gas-station coffee and a face like a closed door.

"Update," she said, handing me a cup. "Elias's arraignment is tomorrow, Hale presiding. Bail will be denied — I've seen the memo. Daniel's transfer to state custody was requested this morning. And the sheriff announced a press conference for Friday announcing the care home arson solved."

"Friday," I said. "Ruth's anniversary."

"Yeah." She drank her coffee and grimaced at it on principle. "One more thing. Last night, around midnight, someone accessed the evidence locker at the county building. Sign-in log shows a valid badge number."

"Whose?"

"Mine," Cora said. "I was home asleep. Ada — whoever's running this doesn't just have Hale. They have access. Real access. Badge-level access." She looked out over the graves, jaw tight. "Which means the fixer isn't outside the machine. He's wearing a uniform, or he's got someone who is. And I keep thinking about the sign-in log, and how easy it would be for my badge to end up attached to something worse than a locker visit."

I heard it then, underneath the words: she was scared. Not of the fixer. Of the frame. Of becoming the next Ruth Okafor — an innocent woman with a paper trail where her innocence used to be.

"They'll try," I said. "But they're working from a script written in 1987, and we've read it. We know how it ends for the innocent party." I turned to face her fully. "Cora. How fast can you get Elias out of a cell?"

Her eyes came up sharp. "That depends entirely on why you're asking."

"Because the trap we built assumed we'd have time," I said. "We don't anymore. Hale thinks he's won, which means he's relaxed, which means the fixer will move soon — maybe days, maybe hours. And when he does, he's going to Marlowe House, to the study, to finish the performance. If Elias is in that cell when it happens, he dies in custody and the file closes forever. But if Elias is *at the estate*—"

"—then the bait walks to the hook on its own schedule," Cora finished slowly. "Except Elias is in county lockup under a judge who hates him, and I'm one forged log entry away from a cell myself."

"No," I said. "You're one *real* log entry away from leverage. Whoever used your badge last night left a fingerprint of their own — a time, a door, a camera angle. Find them, and you don't just clear yourself. You find the fixer's face."

For the first time in two days, something that was almost a smile crossed her face. It wasn't a nice smile. It was the smile of a woman whose father had taught her, over ten years of drunk apologies, exactly how much a rigged system can take before it breaks.

"The arraignment's tomorrow," she said. "Hale will deny bail. But arraignments have recesses. And recesses have hallways. And hallways—" she drained her coffee and crushed the cup, "—have doors that don't lock properly if you know which ones. Give me until tonight."

She walked back through the gate, and I stayed a moment longer among the stones, looking down at Ruth Okafor's name, at the dates that bracketed a life the town had refused to record honestly.

"Hold on," I told her. "We're coming. We're just coming late, and badly, and probably in violation of several statutes."

The frost was melting off the grass by the time I left, the sun clearing the ridge, and behind me the cemetery lay quiet in the light — patient, thorough, keeping every name, waiting for the town to catch up to what it already knew.
