## 9. Wren Follows Someone

Wren had learned to read people the way other kids learned to read street signs—quickly, from a distance, without letting them know she was looking. It was a skill that came free with the life she'd lived before the bookshop: two years of bus stations and borrowed sofas taught you which men watched doors and which men watched girls, which women were kind and which were only bored. You learned to count exits. You learned that the person who mattered most in any room was usually the one pretending not to be there.

So when he came into Amberdale's Books on Thursday afternoon, Wren saw him before the bell over the door finished ringing.

He wasn't a reader. That was the first thing. Readers had a way of moving through a shop like swimmers entering water—a little hesitation at the edge, then surrender. This man moved like a surveyor. He stood just inside the door for a full ten seconds, hands in the pockets of a raincoat too clean for the weather, and his eyes went not to the shelves but to the corners. The security camera Kestrel had never gotten around to installing. The curtain behind the till that led up to her flat. The window with its view of the marsh road.

Then he smiled at Mrs. Fitch, who was holding court by the mystery section as usual, and asked—in a voice like dry paper—if they carried anything on local history.

"Local history!" Mrs. Fitch lit up like a shop window at Christmas. "Oh, we've got shelves of it. Kestrel keeps it all alphabetized, county first, then parish. Are you researching family? Everyone who comes in asking after local history is researching family."

"Something like that," he said.

Wren, crouched behind the counter reshelving a stack of returned paperbacks, kept her head down and her ears open. She'd gotten good at that too. Invisible work. She'd been invisible most of her life; it was only recently, in this dusty shop that smelled of tea and old glue, that anyone had bothered to look at her at all—and even now, she suspected, they looked through her more often than not. Which suited her fine today.

Because she knew him. Not his name. His face.

He'd been standing across the road yesterday, outside the butcher's, when she'd gone out for sandwiches for the shop. He'd been looking at the bookshop the way you look at a house you're thinking of buying—or burning. And when she'd come back out with the sandwiches, he'd been gone, but the space where he'd stood still felt occupied somehow, the way a chair stays warm.

Now here he was inside, asking about local history, and his eyes kept sliding past Mrs. Fitch's enthusiastic gestures to the curtain, the stairs, the flat above.

Kestrel was out. She'd left twenty minutes ago with her coat collar turned up and that look on her face she got lately—the one like a woman carrying something breakable across ice. She'd gone to see Constable Peake about the dead man on the marsh road, the one who'd been wearing her coat. The whole town was talking about it. The whole town talked about everything; that was its main industry besides sheep.

"I'll show you the county histories," Mrs. Fitch was saying, steering him toward the back wall. "Though if it's Windrow you're after, you'll want to mind what you read. Half those books were written by people who owed the family money."

"How interesting," said the man, in the tone of someone who did not find it interesting because he already knew.

Wren slid a paperback into place—*The Moonstone*, spine cracked, someone's old pressed violet still flattened between pages 200 and 201—and made her decision.

She would follow him.

Not because she was brave. She'd have told anyone who asked that she wasn't brave, that brave was a word for people with something to lose and a belief they'd keep it. She would follow him because she was fifteen, and small, and quick, and because nobody ever watched the girl shelving paperbacks, and because Kestrel had been walking around for two weeks looking like a woman waiting for a blow to land, and Wren was sick of watching it. Somebody in this town ought to do something. The adults were all busy being careful.

Careful was just another word for hiding. Wren knew all the words for hiding. She'd used most of them herself.

* * *

He didn't buy anything. Of course he didn't. He thanked Mrs. Fitch with another paper smile, declined her offer of the parish newsletter—"we do an excellent piece on the mill race, very controversial"—and let himself out into the gray afternoon.

Wren gave him thirty seconds. Then she pulled on her jacket, told Mrs. Fitch she was going for cigarettes she didn't smoke, and slipped out after him.

The town unspooled downhill from the shop in the usual order: bakery, butcher, the empty estate agent's office that had been empty so long its window display was just a photograph of itself. Rain had come and gone and left the cobbles shining like fish scales. The man walked without hurry, and that was the thing that frightened Wren most—not hurrying meant he wasn't worried about being seen, or it meant he wanted to be seen, or it meant he'd done this before, whatever *this* was, enough times that it had worn smooth as a stair stone.

He passed the church. Passed the war memorial with its list of names going soft-edged under lichen. Turned up Cutwater Lane, where the houses got older and closer together, and stopped.

Wren melted into the doorway of the old saddlery, heart going like a bird in a box. Through the gap between the door and its frame she watched him take out a phone—not to call, just to look at it, checking something—and then look up. At the row of buildings. At one building in particular.

Her stomach dropped.

It was the building above the bookshop. Not the shop itself—the narrow house behind it, the one Kestrel had lived in for eleven years, reached by the alley door and the outside stairs that groaned like a ship in any weather. Kestrel's flat. Kestrel's kitchen with its blue kettle. Kestrel's bedroom where Wren had slept her first week in town, on a proper mattress with a quilt that smelled of lavender, because Kestrel had said *you'll stay until you've somewhere better* and then never once mentioned it again, as though Wren living there was simply how the world was arranged now.

The man put the phone away. He walked down the alley beside the shop, unhurried, and disappeared from view.

For a long moment Wren stood in the saddlery doorway doing arithmetic. Kestrel was at the police station. Mrs. Fitch was in the shop, deaf to anything beyond her own monologue. The man was in the alley, at the bottom of the stairs, and the lock on the alley door was the kind of lock you could open with a firm opinion.

She should go get help. That was the sensible thing. That was what a responsible adult would do, if any responsible adults had been available, which they weren't, which was the whole problem with adults—they were always elsewhere, being careful.

Instead Wren took out her phone, thumbed the camera open, and went down the alley after him.

* * *

The alley smelled of wet stone and the chip shop's ghost-fry from three streets over. She kept close to the wall where the drainpipe shadow made everything dim. Her trainers made no sound; years of leaving rooms without anyone noticing had trained her feet as well as her eyes.

At the bottom of the outside stairs, the man stood with his hand on the rail, listening. Wren pressed herself flat against the bins. Above them, through the kitchen window, she could see the strip of Kestrel's kitchen—the edge of the blue kettle, the corner of the dresser where Kestrel kept the good teapot and, wrapped in a tea towel at the back of the second drawer, the torn ledger page she'd shown no one except Mr. Osei, and Wren, once, late at night, because Wren had been awake anyway and Kestrel had looked like she needed to say it aloud to somebody.

*Dates and initials,* Kestrel had said. *Half a ledger. The other half is somewhere, and whoever has it doesn't know I have this.*

Or hadn't known. Until the man in the too-clean raincoat had stood in the bookshop counting exits.

Above, a sound: the groan of the alley door. Not breaking—opening. A pause, the length of a held breath, and then the slow complaint of the stairs taking his weight, step by step, the same song they sang for Kestrel every night.

Wren raised her phone.

The angle was bad. The light was worse. But the stairs curved, and the kitchen window caught the reflection of the yard below, and when the man reached the top and let himself into the flat—no forcing, no glass, just the quiet click of a lock surrendering—his shape crossed the reflected window in the glass, and below it, at the mouth of the alley, sat a car.

Dark green. Old model, the kind of boxy sedan that towns like this ran until the wheels fell off. Its number plate was mud-splattered but legible, and Wren, hands shaking only a little, framed it in her screen and took the photograph. Then another. Then a third, wider, showing the car and the alley mouth and the corner of the saddlery sign, in case anyone ever asked her how she knew where she'd been standing.

Inside the flat, floorboards creaked. A drawer opened—the second drawer, the tea-towel drawer—and closed. Silence. Then footsteps again, unhurried, descending, and the man came back down the stairs carrying nothing visible at all, which was somehow worse than if he'd been carrying the page in his fist where she could see it.

He paused at the bottom. Turned his head. For one terrible second Wren was sure he was looking straight at the bins, straight at her, that the whole alley had gone quiet enough to hear her blood.

But he only checked his watch—an old-fashioned thing, gold-faced, catching what little light there was—and walked out of the alley to his car. The engine started. The car pulled away, wipers ticking once against the drizzle, and turned toward the coast road.

Toward Windrow. Wren knew the direction the way she knew the direction of the sea. Everything in this town eventually went toward Windrow.

* * *

She didn't go back into the shop. She climbed the outside stairs instead, taking them two at a time, and pushed open the alley door of the flat—which was unlocked, which was the first confirmation, though she didn't need it.

The flat had been searched the way a tide searches a beach: thoroughly, and with an awful politeness. Nothing broken. Chairs pushed in. Cushions plumped. But the books on Kestrel's bedside shelf were out of order—out of *alphabetical order*, which for Kestrel was a violation on the level of arson—and the drawer of the dresser stood open a finger's width, and the tea towel lay on the floor by the bed, folded neatly in half.

The page was gone.

Wren stood in the middle of the room with her heart hammering and her phone hot in her pocket, and felt the strange cold clarity that had carried her through bus stations and police stations and the worst year of her life settle over her like a coat. Someone had just robbed the only person who'd ever taken her in. Someone had walked into Kestrel's home while Kestrel was at the police station trying to get justice for a murdered stranger, and had helped himself, and had folded the towel.

She should feel scared. She registered, distantly, that she probably would later, at three in the morning, the way fear always collected its debts.

Right now she felt furious. It was a clean feeling, fuel-like, and she decided to run it as far as it would go.

Downstairs, the bell over the shop door rang. Voices—Kestrel's low and tired, Mrs. Fitch's rising with news. Wren took the inside stairs down and came through the curtain, and Kestrel looked up at her with that tired gray face and said, "Back already?" and Wren said:

"Someone's been in your flat."

The shop went quiet the way a forest goes quiet. Even Mrs. Fitch stopped mid-sentence.

Wren set her phone on the counter between them, opened the photographs, and turned the screen around. The dark green car. The plate. The alley mouth. "He came in asking about local history. I followed him. He went up your stairs, he was inside maybe four minutes, and he took the ledger page—it's not upstairs, I checked, the towel was folded." She heard her own voice going fast and hard and made herself stop, breathe, finish. "I got the car. All three angles. You can read the plate."

Kestrel stared at the phone. Then at Wren. Something moved through her face—fear first, sharp and immediate, fear *for* her, Wren recognized it with a jolt because almost nobody had ever worn that expression on her account—and then something else underneath it, slower and stranger. As if Kestrel were seeing her properly for the first time. Not the runaway sleeping on her mattress. Not the girl who shelved paperbacks and drank tea and pretended not to listen.

"You followed him," Kestrel said quietly.

"He was going to do it whether I followed or not. At least this way we know the car."

Mrs. Fitch had both hands pressed to her mouth. "The *nerve* of them. In broad daylight. While we were right downstairs—" She stopped. Her eyes went wide and round as teacups. "While *he* was downstairs. Talking to me. Asking me questions. Oh, that wicked—that absolute *snake*, smiling at me about the mill race—"

"We don't know it's connected to Windrow," Kestrel began, in the voice of someone performing a hope rather than feeling one.

"The coast road goes to Windrow," Wren said. "I watched him turn onto it."

Silence again. Outside, the drizzle ticked against the shop window, and somewhere down the street the bakery shutter rattled half-closed for the afternoon lull. Kestrel picked up the phone and studied the photographs for a long time, her thumb hovering over the plate, and Wren watched her do the same arithmetic she'd done in the alley—except Kestrel's sums had twenty years of numbers in them, and the total was making her face go old.

"He knew the lock," Kestrel said at last. "That lock sticks. You have to lift the handle as you turn the key. A stranger would have broken the frame." She looked up, and her eyes had gone flat and bright, the way they'd looked the day she'd told Wren about the dead man in her coat. "Whoever sent him has been inside my life before. Or he has."

"Then we tell Peake," Wren said. "Tonight. Before they—"

The shop door opened. The bell rang. They all three turned, braced—but it was only Mr. Osei, shaking rain off his umbrella, a paper bag of ginger nuts under his arm and his account books tucked beneath his elbow, arriving for the Thursday evening ritual of tea and gossip that none of them had ever officially agreed to and none of them had ever missed.

He took in the scene at a glance—Kestrel's face, Mrs. Fitch's hands, Wren standing guard over a phone on the counter like a soldier over a flag—and set down the ginger nuts very carefully, as though the room might go off.

"Right," he said. "Somebody had better tell me what's happened. And then," he added, taking off his coat, "somebody had better tell me why I have the feeling this is only the beginning of it."
