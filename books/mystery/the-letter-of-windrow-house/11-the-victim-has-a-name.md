## 11. The Victim Has a Name

The rain came back on Thursday, the fine gray kind that didn't fall so much as hang in the air, beading on the shop windows like condensation on a cold glass. Kestrel had opened at half eight as always, though the morning felt less like a beginning than a continuation—of the ledger page folded in her coat pocket, of the dead man's face she couldn't stop seeing, of the question that had kept her awake until nearly three: *who were you?*

She'd tried every trick she knew. She had written his description out longhand on the back of a delivery slip—the height, the hands, the healed break in the left index finger—and stared at it over her tea until the words lost meaning. She had walked the marsh road in her mind, retracing where he must have fallen, which direction he'd been walking. Toward town or away from it. It mattered. Everything mattered now, and nothing would hold still long enough to be understood.

Wren arrived at nine with wet hair and two paper bags of cinnamon buns from Halloran's bakery, shaking rain off her jacket in the doorway like a dog.

"You look terrible," Wren said, with the frankness Kestrel had come to find oddly restful.

"Thank you. I was going for *haunted*."

"You overshot." Wren set the buns on the counter, then hesitated, her bravado flickering. "There's people talking. About the coat. Mrs. Fitch told half the queue at the bakery before I even got served."

"Mrs. Fitch tells half the queue what she had for breakfast."

"Yeah, but this is better than breakfast." Wren pulled a bun free and tore it in two, offering half without being asked, which was its own kind of tenderness. "They're saying you knew him."

Kestrel took the bun. It was warm through the paper. "I don't know him."

"But you're going to find out who he is."

It wasn't a question. Kestrel looked at this girl of seventeen with her chipped nail polish and her fierce, unguarded eyes, and thought about all the reasons to say *no, stay out of it, go back to algebra and stolen afternoons among the shelves.* She said none of them.

"Yes," she said. "I am."

* * *

The answer, when it came, came not through deduction but through laundry.

It was Mr. Osei who noticed it first, later that morning, turning the photograph Peake had let Kestrel copy—a grainy image of the victim's effects laid out on the coroner's steel table. He held it at arm's length, then close, the way he read small print, his reading glasses pushed up into his silver hair.

"This shirt," he said. "Look at the collar."

Kestrel leaned over the counter. The shirt was plain linen, washed thin, unremarkable except—

"It's been mended," she said slowly. "At the collar. And the cuff."

"Twice, by the look of it. Different thread each time." Osei tapped the photograph with one careful finger. "A man who steals a coat doesn't mend his own shirt collar twice. That's a man who takes care of what little he has. That's not vagrancy, Kestrel. That's poverty with dignity."

She looked at the photograph for a long moment. Poverty with dignity. There was a whole life in that phrase, and she found, to her surprise, that it hurt to think about. She had spent three days treating the man as a message, a device, a glove the killer had put words into. She had not once treated him as a man who had once mended his own cuffs because there was no one else to do it.

"Peake said no identification papers," she said. "Nothing in the pockets but coins and the ledger page."

"Coins." Osei squinted. "How many?"

"A dozen or so. Old ones, mixed dates."

Osei smiled thinly. "Then ask yourself why a man carries coins he can't spend in any shop in town. Some of those are foreign, aren't they? Kept for luck, or memory. Men like this don't accumulate money, Kestrel. They carry it like photographs."

* * *

It was Mrs. Fitch, inevitably, who gave her the name—but not before giving her an hour of everything else.

Kestrel went to her cottage just past the churchyard at four o'clock, when the rain had thickened and the light was going amber and unreliable. Mrs. Fitch answered the door in a cardigan the color of marigolds, took one look at Kestrel's face, and stood aside without a word, which from Mrs. Fitch was practically a vow of silence.

Inside, the cottage smelled of bergamot and toast. A fire tickled in the grate. On the mantel stood a regiment of framed photographs—children and grandchildren and, in pride of place, a faded snapshot of a scullery girl in a starched apron, squinting into sun that had gone out forty years ago.

"That's you," Kestrel said. "At Windrow House."

"That's me." Mrs. Fitch lowered herself into her armchair with the care of a woman whose knees had opinions. "Nineteen years old and convinced I was the ugliest creature ever to scrub a pot. You've come about the dead man."

"I've come about the dead man."

"Well." She folded her hands. "Ask."

Kestrel sat on the edge of the sofa, forward, elbows on knees—the posture of someone ready to leave quickly if she had to. "Did you know him? The man on the marsh road?"

"No."

"Did you know anyone who might have—" She stopped. Started again, differently. "Mrs. Fitch. Twenty years ago, who worked the grounds at Windrow House?"

The old woman's eyes sharpened. For a moment she looked less like a gossip and more like what she had once been: a witness.

"Groundskeeper was Silas Crane," she said. "Came on the same year I did, more or less. Quiet man. Hands like tree roots. He kept the roses and the hedges and the gravel paths swept so clean you could have eaten off them. And he kept things." She paused. "He kept *things*, if you take my meaning. Bits of paper. He had a drawer in the potting shed nobody was allowed near."

Kestrel's pulse ticked up. "You saw the drawer?"

"Saw it, never opened it. But I'll tell you something else, and you'll want your tea for it." Mrs. Fitch reached for her cup, drank, set it down with a small decisive click. "Silas Crane married Vane's sister. Before Vane was housekeeper—she came later, married into it, but the Crane connection was already there. Silas and Agatha Windrow's gardener before him were cousins twice over. Half the staff that estate ever hired was related to the other half. That's how the old families keep their secrets, dear. They don't hire strangers. They hire *family*, because family can be leaned on."

"And Silas left the estate when?"

"Oh—" Mrs. Fitch waved a hand, calculating backward through decades. "Must be fifteen years ago now. Left sudden. No notice, no goodbyes. One day the roses went untended and the next week there was a new fellow. People said he'd quarreled with Mrs. Vane. People said he'd quarreled with *her ladyship*. People said a lot of things." Her voice dropped, and the firelight caught the lines of her face. "But I'll tell you what I never said, because saying it wouldn't have done any good. Tamsin Reed used to walk the garden path with Silas some evenings. Talking. Just talking—he was kind to her, I always thought, in his silent way. And after Tamsin died, Silas changed. Went quieter still. Like something had been scooped out of him."

Kestrel sat very still. Outside, the rain wrote its endless gray shorthand against the window.

"Mrs. Fitch," she said carefully. "When Silas left—did he leave rich?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment, and Kestrel watched understanding arrive in her face like weather.

"No," Mrs. Fitch said softly. "He left with a suitcase and that same mended shirt, I'd wager. Whatever he had on the Windrows, dear—and I'm an old woman and I know blackmail when I hear it described around the edges—he never spent it. Which means either he hadn't got it yet, or..." She trailed off, then finished anyway, because Mrs. Fitch finished everything. "Or he wasn't in it for the money at all."

* * *

Kestrel walked home through the churchyard in the failing light, and the name followed her like her own shadow.

*Silas Crane.*

She stopped at the lych-gate, rain dripping from the iron scrollwork, and let the truth settle over her the way the weather did—gradually, then all at once.

Silas Crane, groundskeeper. Brother-in-law to Edith Vane, the housekeeper whose handwriting marched down the ledger page in Kestrel's pocket. Kind to Tamsin Reed in his silent way. Changed forever after her death. Gone fifteen years, carrying a grudge or a secret or both, living poor somewhere with a drawer full of paper and a pocket full of unspendable coins.

And dead on the marsh road, dressed in Kestrel Amberdale's own coat.

She understood the shape of it now—or the beginning of its shape. If Silas had been blackmailing the Windrows, then the ledger page in his pocket wasn't evidence he'd stolen. It was evidence he'd *kept*. Insurance, the way Mrs. Vane had kept hers. Two members of the household, bound by blood and by knowledge, each holding a piece of the truth against the day it might be needed.

And someone had decided, twenty years too late, that the day had come.

But here was the thing that sat wrong in her chest, heavy as a swallowed stone: she could not make herself hate him. Blackmail was a crime; she had spent eleven years on the county force arresting men for lesser versions of it. And yet—what else does a grieving man do when the law has already looked at a dead girl and shrugged? What else does a groundskeeper with root-hard hands and a mended collar do with the knowledge that the woman everyone adores killed a child who trusted him? He cannot go to the police; he tried that, perhaps, or knew it was useless. He cannot speak; speaking destroys him too. So he waits. He keeps a drawer. He writes letters he may never send. He turns his grief into arithmetic and his silence into a slow, patient debt, and he tells himself it is justice, and maybe some days he even believes it.

Kestrel knew that arithmetic. She had invented her own version of it, twenty years ago, when she buried her testimony to protect Agatha Windrow and called the burial discretion. She had been a detective, with a badge and a procedure and colleagues, and she had still chosen the quiet lie. What excuse had Silas had, with nothing but roses and a locked drawer?

*We were the same,* she thought, standing in the churchyard rain. *He just picked a different cowardice.*

No—not quite the same. He had stayed near the truth all these years, circling it, feeding on it. She had fled to a bookshop and alphabetized herself into oblivion. If anything, Silas Crane had been braver than she was, in his crooked, corrosive way.

And now he was dead, and someone had dressed his corpse in her coat, and the message was no longer ambiguous. Whoever killed him wanted Kestrel to know that they knew—that the chain of witnesses and keepers-of-paper was being read aloud, name by name, and that her name was on the list. The coat said: *I know what you buried.* The staging said: *you are next in the grammar of this sentence.*

She walked the last stretch to the shop with her collar up and her mind working coldly, clearly, the old machinery engaging despite itself. Silas Crane had been blackmailing the family for years—which meant the family had known, all along, exactly how much he knew. Which meant that when the killer needed a first victim, the choice wasn't random at all. Silas was the archive. Kill the archive, take the ledger page—but the page had survived, passed somehow toward Kestrel, and now the killer had a problem: the truth was loose, and it was loose specifically in *her* direction.

Unless the page hadn't been sent to her at all. Unless Silas himself had been coming to her—walking the marsh road toward town, toward the bookshop, toward the one person alive who had heard Tamsin Reed's last hours and lived to keep quiet about it.

Toward her. With the page. Perhaps with a warning. Perhaps with an offer, or a plea, or simply the need, at the end of a long and lonely extortion, to hand the weight to someone else.

He had died a mile short of her door.

Kestrel stood in the dark shop, dripping, and turned the sign to CLOSED without lighting the lamps. In the gloom the shelves rose around her like a congregation. Somewhere in the fiction section, spine out, waited the first edition the Windrows had donated years ago—Mr. Osei's cipher key, still undeciphered. Somewhere in her pocket, the torn page with Mrs. Vane's careful columns. Somewhere across town, Edmund Windrow was polishing crystal for an anniversary dinner and smiling his charming smile at people who still believed the family fortune grew from orchards and good management.

And somewhere, in a potting shed long since cleaned out, a drawer had once held the whole rotten history of Windrow House, and the man who kept it had mended his own collars because there was no one in the world left to mend them for him.

Kestrel hung her damp coat on the hook—*her* coat, the twin of the one on the dead man—and made herself a promise in the dark.

*I'll give you your name back, Silas Crane. Not the blackmailer's name. Yours.*

She was reaching for the lamp switch when she saw it: an envelope on the mat, slipped under the door sometime in the last hour. No stamp. No return address. Her name written across the front in a hand she almost recognized—small, precise, the letters of someone accustomed to keeping accounts.

She knelt and drew it out. Inside, a single sheet, and on it, in that same precise hand, a column of figures—dates and initials, matching the ledger page exactly, continuing it. And beneath the column, five words:

*The other half knows where.*

Kestrel sat down slowly on the floor of her own shop, the paper trembling in her hand, and realized that Silas Crane had not been walking toward her that night by accident at all.

He had been delivering the second half of the ledger—to her. And whoever killed him had searched the body, found nothing, and was now looking for what Silas had already sent ahead.

The knock, when it came, was soft. Three raps. Patient.

Through the glass door, in the lamplight of the street, stood Constable Idris Peake—soaked through, hatless, holding his helmet under his arm like a man come to confess something.

Behind him, at the corner of the lane, a car idled with its lights off.
