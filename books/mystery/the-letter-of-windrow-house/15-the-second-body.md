## 15. The Second Body

The rain came in off the marsh that morning like something with an errand, driving sideways against the shop windows until the panes wept and the street outside dissolved into a gray smear. Kestrel Amberdale stood behind her counter with a mug of tea gone cold between her palms and watched the water trace its branching paths down the glass, and she thought—not for the first time, but with new and terrible clarity—that water remembers everything it touches.

She had learned this from her mother, who had been a woman of small superstitions and large silences. *Water keeps what you give it,* her mother used to say, wringing out dishcloths at the sink. *That's why you never wash away grief too fast.* Kestrel had been nine and had thought it nonsense. She was fifty-eight now, and she understood that her mother had simply been describing evidence.

The bell above the door had barely finished shivering when Constable Idris Peake came through it, hatless, his coat dark across the shoulders, his face wearing the particular expression of a man who has spent the last hour deciding whether to tell someone something and has finally lost the argument with himself.

"Kestrel." He did not take off his coat. That was the first wrong thing. In all the weeks since the body on the marsh road, since Silas Crane's nameless corpse dressed in her stolen tweed, Idris Peake had maintained a careful etiquette of grief—hat removed, voice lowered, the small courtesies of a man raised by a churchgoing mother. Today he stood dripping on her floorboards like a messenger from a country where manners had been abolished.

"Where?" she asked.

He blinked. "I haven't said anything yet."

"You don't have to." She set down the cold tea. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside, some animal part of her was already running. "You came yourself instead of ringing. You're not taking off your coat because part of you hopes to be back out that door before I understand. So. Where?"

Peake looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face softened—not pity, exactly, but the recognition of one exhausted creature by another.

"The millpond," he said. "Behind the old tannery. A dog walker found him at first light."

*Him.* The word landed and kept landing, like a stone dropped down a well.

"Is it—" She stopped. Started again. "Do you know who?"

"We think it's Gerald Mowbray."

The name meant nothing for a heartbeat, and then it meant everything. Gerald Mowbray. Solicitor. The tidy office above the ironmonger's with the brass plate gone green at the edges. The man who had drawn up half the wills in Ashcombe and witnessed the other half, whose handshake was dry and brief and whose eyes never quite met yours when Windrow money changed hands. Kestrel had seen him twice in twenty years, both times at a distance, both times leaving the estate with his document case held against his chest like a shield.

"Silas Crane's solicitor," she said quietly.

"You knew?"

"I suspected. Someone had to have moved Crane's blackmail payments around over the years. Someone discreet, local, trusted by the family." She pulled her own coat from the hook behind the counter. "Someone who could be paid in silence as easily as in pounds."

Peake held the door open for her, and the rain took them both.

* * *

The old tannery had been dead for thirty years, its brick bones softening into the hillside, and behind it the millpond lay flat and colorless beneath the rain, ringed with alders that had grown up wild since the last wheel turned. A constable Kestrel didn't recognize stood at the perimeter under an umbrella, looking grateful for the assignment and sickened by it. Beyond him, at the pond's near edge, a canvas tent had been rigged against the weather, and beneath it—

Beneath it, Gerald Mowbray lay on his back on the trampled grass, and he was dressed in a woman's clothing.

Kestrel stopped walking. The rain drummed on her hood. She made herself go forward anyway, one step, then another, because this was the price of the life she had chosen and then abandoned and then chosen again, and the bill always came due in places like this.

The suit was gray wool, well-cut, decades out of fashion but lovingly kept—a skirt, a jacket with padded shoulders, a silk blouse buttoned to the throat. On the dead man's feet: low-heeled shoes, polished, size five at most, laced with difficulty over his larger frame. His hands were folded on his chest with a formality that was worse than violence. His face, drained of blood and of everything else, wore the mild perplexity of a man interrupted mid-thought.

"I know that suit," Kestrel heard herself say.

Peake nodded grimly. "So do I. So does half the town, if they ever look at the photographs in the parish hall. It's Agatha Windrow's."

The name went through Kestrel like a blade finding the gap between ribs. Agatha Windrow, who had died six years ago in the great bed at Windrow House with her hands folded and her affairs immaculate. Agatha Windrow, whom Kestrel had protected with her silence twenty years ago, testifying to nothing, seeing nothing, burying the truth of Tamsin Reed's death so deep that even she had almost convinced herself it had never surfaced at all.

"That suit would be in the estate," Peake said. "Kept with her things. There'd be no reason for anyone outside the household to have access."

"No reason," Kestrel agreed. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Except grief. Except reverence. Except someone who goes through a dead woman's wardrobe the way other people visit a grave."

The forensic photographer crouched at the tent's edge, and the flash went off, and for one white instant the whole scene burned itself onto the back of Kestrel's eyes: the pond, the alders, the man in the dead matriarch's clothes lying in the rain like a sentence waiting for its verb.

"He wasn't drowned here," Peake said quietly. "No mud on the backs of the shoes beyond what you'd expect from carrying. The ground under him is barely disturbed. And—" he hesitated, then committed, "—there were reeds in his hair. Pond reeds, but not these reeds. Different silt. Coroner thinks he went into the water somewhere else and was brought here after."

"Brought here. Dressed like that. Laid out." Kestrel crouched, carefully, outside the tape, and studied the folded hands, the buttoned blouse, the terrible tidiness of it. "This isn't concealment, Idris. Concealment would have left him in the water. This is presentation. He's been arranged."

"Like Crane was."

"Like Crane was." She rose. Her knees protested; she ignored them. "Crane in my coat. Mowbray in hers. Do you see it yet? Because I'm starting to, and I wish to God I weren't."

Peake looked at her. Rain ran off the brim of his recovered hat. "Tell me."

"Not here." She glanced at the young constable, at the photographer, at the tent and the pond and the whole listening landscape. "But Idris—whoever did this isn't hiding bodies. He's publishing them. And he's using wardrobes as the printing press."

* * *

They sat in Peake's car with the engine running for the heat and the wipers going, two figures in a moving box of noise, and Kestrel laid it out as much for herself as for him.

"Silas Crane was found in my tweed coat. My coat, stolen from my flat, put on a dead blackmailer. At the time I thought it was misdirection—frame the retired detective, muddy the waters. But that doesn't hold. If they wanted to frame me, there are cleaner ways than a coat that any constable could trace back to my closet in an afternoon. No." She watched the rain crawl sideways across the windscreen. "It wasn't misdirection. It was attribution. He was dressed as me because the message was about me. *This death belongs to your story, Kestrel Amberdale. Read it.*

"And now Mowbray, in Agatha Windrow's clothes. What did Mowbray have to do with Agatha? He handled her legal affairs, yes—but more than that. Think about what a solicitor knows. Every transfer, every trust, every quiet payment routed through the estate accounts. If Agatha was moving money—if she was paying people to be silent about Tamsin Reed—it would have gone through Mowbray's hands, or at least past his eyes."

"So the killer dresses him in her clothes because—"

"Because Mowbray *was* her, in a sense. Her instrument. Her pen." Kestrel's breath fogged the glass and she wiped it clear with the heel of her hand. "The killer isn't disguising the dead, Idris. He's identifying them. Each body wears the person it served. Or the person it betrayed. I don't know which yet. But it's grammar. It's a language, and it's being spoken to someone—and given that the first message was delivered in my coat, I rather think I'm the intended reader."

Peake was silent for a long moment. The wipers beat their patient rhythm. Somewhere under the rain, the millpond lay flat and secret over whatever second pond had actually taken Gerald Mowbray's life.

"There's something else," he said finally. "Coroner's preliminary on Crane came back formal last week. Poisoned—foxglove extract, slow dose. I told you that already." He flexed his hands on the wheel. "Mowbray's got fluid in the lungs consistent with drowning, but there's bruising at the jaw. Both sides. Someone held his mouth shut while the water went in. Held it gently, by the look of it—no tearing. Like you'd hold a child's face to wash it."

Kestrel closed her eyes. "Careful," she said. "The killer is careful. Patient. Almost tender."

"And educated," Peake added. "Whoever staged Mowbray knew the suit existed, knew where it was kept, knew its history. That's household knowledge, Kestrel. Or knowledge passed down from someone who had it."

The words hung between them. Household knowledge. Twenty years of servants and solicitors and groundskeepers, a web of people who had touched the Windrow fortune and been touched by it, and now they were dying one by one, each corpse costumed in the guilt of another.

"Gerald Mowbray had a sister in Fenmarsh," Peake said. "I'll have to tell her today." He said it the way men say such things—as if naming the duty might make it lighter. It never did. Kestrel knew that too.

"Idris." She turned to look at him fully. "How many people are left who know? Really know—what happened at Windrow House twenty years ago? Servants, staff, the ones who saw things. Count them."

He counted, lips moving slightly, eyes fixed on the rain. "Mrs. Vane's dying, if she's not dead already. Her daughter, maybe. The gardener who replaced Crane—he'd have been a boy then. The cook, Mrs. Aldous, but her mind's been going for years. Edmund Windrow. And..." He stopped.

"And me," Kestrel finished.

"And you." He met her eyes. "Kestrel, if this is a list, and if it's being worked through—"

"Then I'm on it." She said it without drama, the way one reports the weather. "I've been on it since Crane washed up in my coat. The question isn't whether. The question is what order, and why mine comes when it comes." She opened the car door, letting in a slap of wet air. "Which means I have work to do before my turn arrives. Get me everything on Mowbray—his files, his client list, especially anything touching the estate trusts. Quietly. Your superiors are still pushing vagrancy for Crane, aren't they?"

Peake's jaw tightened. "They've upgraded it to 'pending.' Which in Ashcombe means 'until someone senior tells us to stop pending.'"

"Then pending faster," Kestrel said, and stepped out into the rain.

* * *

The bookshop, when she returned to it, was warm and lamplit and full of the particular hush of people pretending to browse. Wren was behind the counter, ostensibly shelving paperbacks, actually watching the door. Mr. Osei occupied his usual chair by the poetry section with a ledger of his own open on his knee—Kestrel had stopped being surprised by the symmetry of that. Mrs. Fitch held court by the window with tea and an audience of one damp postman.

All four faces turned when Kestrel came in, dripping, and all four read the news off her before she spoke. She saw it happen—the way a room learns a death, the way air changes weight.

"It's Gerald Mowbray," she said. "Found this morning behind the old tannery."

Mrs. Fitch's teacup paused halfway to her mouth. "Gerald Mowbray. Why, I danced with Gerald Mowbray at the harvest supper in—I want to say seventy-nine. He trod on my foot and blamed my shoe." Then, catching herself, catching the room: "Dead how?"

"Drowned." Kestrel unwound her scarf. She had decided, walking back through the rain, how much to tell them, and the decision had surprised her: most of it. All of it, maybe. These people had assembled themselves around her without invitation—Wren who slept in the storeroom and asked no questions about the past because she was busy surviving her own; Osei who balanced her failing accounts for free and never once made her feel audited; Fitch who remembered everything the town preferred forgotten. She had spent twenty years believing that solitude was safety. The last three weeks had dismantled that belief plank by plank, and standing here now, wet and tired and afraid in a way she hadn't been afraid since she was a young constable, she found she no longer wished to rebuild it.

"He was dressed," she said, "in Agatha Windrow's clothes."

The silence that followed had texture. Osei closed his ledger slowly, deliberately, the way a man closes a door on a draft.

"The same as the first one," Wren said. Her voice was steady but her knuckles, wrapped around the shelving cart, were white. "Dressed in someone else's clothes. Yours."

"The same as the first one," Kestrel confirmed. "And I believe it means precisely what it appears to mean. Whoever killed Silas Crane has killed again, and the killing has a method to it. The costumes aren't random. They're labels." She looked around the little shop—at the shelves she had alphabetized into a fortress, at the four faces that had breached every wall she built. "Mowbray was the Windrows' solicitor for forty years. If money moved in the dark, it moved through him. The killer put him in Agatha's suit to say so."

"To say so to whom?" Osei asked. His voice was mild, accountant's-precise. "The police?"

"The police are being managed. No—to whoever's next on the list. And to whoever's meant to stop it." She let that sit. "To me."

Mrs. Fitch set down her cup with a click. For once in her long career of narration, she had nothing to add, and her face showed what it cost her.

"Well then," Osei said at last, and reopened his ledger. "If you're the intended reader, Kestrel, we should read faster than he writes. Tell us everything you have. Dates, names, amounts. I'm rather good with numbers, as it happens, and I have nowhere better to be."

Wren was already pulling chairs into a circle around the reading table, dragging the lamp closer, feeding the small fire in the grate as though the shop were a garrison and winter a siege. Mrs. Fitch began, unprompted, to enumerate everyone who had ever worked at Windrow House between 1960 and the present, in order of hire, with commentary.

Kestrel stood among them a moment longer, rain still dripping from her hem onto the floorboards, and felt the strange vertigo of being seen—truly seen—by people who stayed anyway.

Then she fetched the ledger page from its hiding place inside the false spine of a battered atlas, laid it on the table under the lamp, and began to teach them the dead woman's cipher.

Outside, the rain went on falling on the marsh road, on the millpond, on the green-brass plate above the ironmonger's where a solicitor's office stood suddenly, permanently empty—and somewhere in the wide gray world, someone who counted everything was counting what remained.
