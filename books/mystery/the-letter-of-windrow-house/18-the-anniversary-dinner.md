## 18. The Anniversary Dinner

The invitation had arrived on cream card stock, edged in gold leaf, smelling faintly of lilies and something older beneath them—dust, perhaps, or the particular sweetness of money kept too long in a closed room. *Mr. Edmund Windrow requests the pleasure of your company at the Fortieth Anniversary Dinner of Windrow House.* Kestrel had read it four times before she understood that the word *pleasure* was doing more work than any word ought to do.

Now she stood before her narrow mirror in the flat above the shop, buttoning the collar of a black dress she hadn't worn since her mother's funeral. In the glass behind her, the evening light came down through the window in bars, striping the room like something institutional. She could see the empty space on its hook where the tweed coat had hung for eleven years. It still bothered her, that absence—the way a pulled tooth bothers the tongue.

"You look like you're going to a hanging," Wren said from the doorway. She'd dressed with surprising care: a dark green dress borrowed—Kestrel chose not to ask from whom—and her wild hair pinned back with what appeared to be two pencils. "Which I suppose is accurate."

"Something like that." Kestrel lifted the ledger from the writing desk. Even decoded, even translated into Mr. Osei's careful columns and her own marginal notes, it felt less like paper than like a live thing—a bird with a broken wing, beating against her palms. "You don't have to come inside, you know. Either of you."

Wren snorted. Osei, waiting at the foot of the stairs in his one good suit, pressed flat by Mrs. Fitch herself that afternoon, adjusted his glasses and said, "Miss Amberdale. I have balanced books for forty years. Tonight I would like to watch one be read aloud."

Constable Peake's car idled at the curb, engine running, headlights cutting cones through the first fog of autumn. He got out when he saw them come down the steps, and for a moment none of them moved. Four people on a darkening street, dressed for a funeral that hadn't happened yet.

"Superintendent Voss knows I'm attending," Peake said quietly. "He told me to enjoy the canapés." His jaw worked. "I've also filed a request for the original coroner's report on Tamsin Reed. Twenty years old. It'll take a warrant to stop me now that there's a paper trail."

"Paper trails," Kestrel said. "That's all any of this ever was."

"No," Peake said, opening the car door. "Tonight it becomes a voice."

* * *

Windrow House rose out of the marsh fog like a ship that had run aground decades ago and been too proud to admit it. Its windows burned amber against the gray; its gravel drive crunched under tires with the sound of something being crushed slowly. Kestrel had not stood in front of it since she was thirty-four years old and carrying a statement she would never give.

Inside, the great hall had been transformed. Candles everywhere—hundreds of them, their flames swaying in the draft from the door, throwing shadows that climbed the panelled walls and pooled in the corners where the light couldn't reach. Round tables dressed in white linen. A string quartet playing something baroque and patient in the gallery above. And people—half the town's founding families, it seemed, the Fitch cousins and the Hale brothers and old Mrs. Aldous propped in her wheelchair like a figurehead, all of them glittering and murmuring and turning, as one organism, when Kestrel walked in with an accountant, a runaway, and a constable.

She felt their eyes move over her like searchlights. Let them look, she thought. For twenty years she had practiced invisibility the way other people practice piano—daily, devotedly, until it became second nature. Tonight she set it down like a heavy coat.

"There she is." Edmund Windrow materialized out of the candlelight with a glass of champagne in each hand, silver-haired and handsome the way a well-preserved ruin is handsome. He offered her one. "Miss Amberdale. And friends. How wonderful—you've brought your book club."

"We're very devoted readers," Wren said sweetly.

Edmund's smile didn't flicker, but Kestrel saw the calculation behind it, quick as a shutter closing. He leaned in, and beneath the cologne she caught it again—that smell from the invitation, dust and sweetness, the house breathing through him. "I do hope you brought something to read," he murmured. "Dinners here can be so long. So full of old stories."

"I never leave home without one," Kestrel said, and touched the flat shape in her handbag.

His eyes followed the gesture. Something moved in his face—not fear, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has finally found the letter he's been dreading in among the bills.

"Ladies and gentlemen!" A woman at the dais—Edmund's sister, Constance, all teeth and turquoise—raised her glass. "If you'd find your seats! We begin!"

* * *

The dinner was pheasant and forced conversation. Kestrel sat between Mr. Osei and Peake, across from a Hale brother who talked about drainage easements as though they were a form of prayer. The quartet played. The candles burned down by fractions. Through the tall windows the marsh fog pressed against the glass like a face trying to get in.

Osei ate almost nothing. He kept one hand flat on the tablecloth, and twice Kestrel saw his fingers move—counting. Counting the tables, counting the exits, counting the years. When the fish course came he leaned toward her and spoke without moving his lips much, an accountant's ventriloquism.

"The seating chart is deliberate. You are placed between law and arithmetic. Someone wants you comfortable."

"Someone wants me contained."

"That also," he admitted. "But containment is a kind of acknowledgment. They only fence what they fear will wander."

Across the hall, Wren sat with the younger cousins, radiating the specific boredom of teenagers everywhere, but Kestrel caught her eyes flicking to the service door every few minutes. Wren had spent the afternoon learning the house's back passages from Mrs. Fitch, who had once carried trays through them and remembered every one. If anything went wrong, there was a route out through the stillroom that bypassed the front hall entirely.

Peake drank one glass of wine and left it. His hand rested near his pocket, where he'd promised—off the record, unofficially, in defiance of two phone calls from his superintendent—he was not carrying his radio.

Toasts began with the dessert. Constance Windrow toasted the founders. Old Mrs. Aldous toasted the land. Edmund rose last, and the room hushed for him the way a room hushes for weather.

"Forty years," he said, glass high, candlelight pooling gold in his voice. "Forty years since my mother took this house from a struggling farm to the pride of the county. Agatha Windrow believed in one thing above all others—" he paused, savoring it, "—that a house is only as strong as what it keeps hidden. That privacy is not shame. Privacy is *foundation*." Laughter, warm and obedient, rolled around the tables. "So tonight we raise a glass to foundations. To everything this house has kept. To everything it will keep still."

Glasses rose around the hall like a field of small bright flowers.

Kestrel stood up.

It was not dramatic. That was the strange part, the part she would remember afterward—how quiet it was, how ordinary, simply a woman rising from her chair with a leather ledger in her hands, the way someone might rise to fetch a shawl. But the sound died in the room the way sound dies in snowfall. One by one, the glasses came down. The quartet, uncertain, faltered into silence.

"Before we toast foundations," Kestrel said, and her voice carried further than she expected, clear and cold as water over stone, "I'd like to read a page from another foundation document. An accounting ledger, kept by Mrs. Vane, housekeeper of this estate, covering the year 1984."

"Miss Amberdale." Edmund's voice was still pleasant. Only his knuckles had changed; they were white around the stem of his glass. "This is a private celebration."

"It is," Kestrel agreed. "That's rather the point."

She opened the ledger. The pages whispered. Somewhere in the gallery a violinist shifted, and the creak of the chair was the loudest sound in the house.

"Mrs. Vane recorded payments in cipher—dates, initials, sums. Mr. Osei cracked the key; it was a first edition of *Collected Poems*, donated to my shop by this family, which I confess I found fitting. The entries record monthly payments to a man named Silas Crane, groundskeeper here, for services rendered. Services of silence." She turned the page. Her hands did not shake. Twenty years of shaking had apparently used up whatever trembling was left in her. "And then there is the final entry. Dated the eleventh of October, 1984. The night Tamsin Reed died."

"Stop." Edmund set down his glass. The click of crystal on linen was very small and very final. "Whatever fantasy you've constructed—"

"*Initials A.W.,*" Kestrel read, "*sum of four hundred pounds, notation: matter concluded.*" She looked up, and let her gaze travel the room, table by table, face by face, all the powdered and prosperous faces of the founding families. "A.W. Agatha Windrow. *Matter concluded.* Four hundred pounds, paid the night a sixteen-year-old girl fell—or was helped—from the east stair, because she had discovered that Agatha was embezzling this estate blind and had made the mistake of saying so."

The silence had a texture now. Kestrel could feel it pressing back against her, dense as the fog outside.

"Tamsin Reed was not careless," Kestrel said. "Tamsin Reed was *careful*. She counted what she saw, and she told the wrong person, and the person who should have protected her arranged instead for the matter to be concluded. I know because I was there. I was the detective who found her shoe on the landing. And I buried my report to protect this town's beloved matriarch, and I have spent twenty years alphabetizing books to avoid thinking about it." She closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a coffin. "So no, Mr. Windrow. I won't sit down. This house has kept its secrets long enough. Tonight the foundation cracks."

No one breathed. At the far table, old Mrs. Aldous had begun, very softly, to cry.

Edmund moved first. Of course he did—he had always been a man who moved first and let the lawyers explain later. "Get her out," he said to no one and everyone. "This is a private residence. Constable, do your duty."

Peake rose slowly, buttoning his jacket, and every eye in the room fixed on him—the honest man, the small man, the man who had been handed a hundred reasons to look away. "Actually, sir," he said, "as of four o'clock this afternoon, I'm not off duty. I'm the senior officer present at the scene of a confessed obstruction of justice." He produced a folded paper from his breast pocket. "Miss Amberdale, I'll take that ledger as evidence now, if you please. Witnessed." He glanced at Osei. "You don't mind?"

"Not at all," Osei said. "I made copies."

The word went through the room like current through water. Kestrel watched it move—saw the Hales exchange a glance, saw Constance's hand go to her throat, saw three separate guests reach, casually, terribly, for their telephones. The founding families, closing ranks for forty years, discovering all at once that the ranks had dissolved while they weren't looking.

Edmund stood very still amid the ruin of his toast. Then he smiled at Kestrel, and it was the smile from her bookshop doorstep, the predator testing the latch—but the latch was broken now, the door swinging wide, the whole cold night pouring in.

"You should have stayed in your shop," he said quietly.

"I know," Kestrel said. "I'm done staying anywhere."

And that was when the lights went out.

Not all of them—the candles survived, hundreds of small flames guttering upright again, so that the great hall became suddenly a sea of little fires and enormous shadows. The string quartet stopped altogether. Somewhere upstairs, a door slammed. And in the sudden dark between the candle-glow and the windows, Kestrel heard footsteps on the east stair—the same stair, she thought, the blood going thin in her—the same stair where a girl had fallen twenty years ago, descending now, slow and unhurried, toward the light.

Everyone heard them. Everyone turned.

An old man stepped out of the shadow of the gallery staircase, leaning on a cane, dressed in a suit two decades out of fashion, and in his free hand he held the second half of the ledger—the half stolen from Kestrel's flat—held up like a hymnal.

"I believe," he said, in a voice like dry paper turning, "the lady was reading aloud. I've waited forty years for someone to finish the entry."
