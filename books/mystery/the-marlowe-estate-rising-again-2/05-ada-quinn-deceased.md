## 5. Ada Quinn, Deceased

The Windham County Records Annex smelled like pencil shavings and cold coffee, the way all government buildings smell when they've given up pretending to be anything other than what they are. Marguerite signed the visitor log at 8:47 a.m. on a Tuesday in October, printed her name in careful block letters—*M. Eriksen*—and hesitated a half second before adding *Independent Researcher* in the purpose column.

Not a lie. Not the whole truth either. She was learning that the whole truth was a currency you spent carefully.

The archivist on duty was a man in his sixties named Prosser, according to the laminated badge pinned to his cardigan, with reading glasses pushed up into thinning white hair and the unhurried movements of someone who had spent forty years among paper and had made his peace with it. He looked up when she approached the desk, and she watched him do the thing that people in small counties did when they saw her face: a flicker of recognition, quickly suppressed.

"You're the podcast woman," he said anyway.

"I'm a researcher," Marguerite said. "I'm looking at some records from the early seventies. Staff employment files from Marlowe Estate, if they exist. And vital records for anyone who worked there."

Prosser took his time with that. He removed his glasses, polished them on the hem of his sweater, put them back on. "Marlowe Estate," he repeated, as though tasting an old food he'd once liked and now wasn't sure about. "That place is a tourist trap now. Half the county worked there one summer or another. What year are we talking?"

"1972 into '73."

"Huh." Something moved behind his eyes—not suspicion exactly, but a kind of weather. "Vital records are public after fifty years, so you're fine there. Employment files—" He shrugged. "Estate kept its own books. Some of it got donated to the historical society when the family stopped living there full-time. Some of it's probably still in boxes nobody's opened." He slid a form across the counter. "Fill out a request slip for each record. I'll pull what I can."

Marguerite filled out three slips. The first asked for death certificates filed in Windham County between January 1972 and December 1974—a broad net, because she didn't yet know whose name to fish for. The second requested any archived staff ledgers or payroll records from Marlowe Estate. The third was a long shot: coroner's reports and inquest files from the same window.

She carried the slips back to the counter, and Prosser read them over the top of his glasses.

"Inquest files," he said. "Those are sealed unless there's been a court order."

"They're fifty years old."

"Fifty years old doesn't unseal them. Fifty years just means nobody remembers why they were sealed." He stamped her first two slips without further argument, then held the third between two fingers like something that might be damp. "I'll see what the system says. Sometimes the system surprises us."

He disappeared into the stacks, and Marguerite stood at the counter listening to the building breathe—the tick of baseboard heat, the hum of fluorescent lights, somewhere deep in the shelves the dry whisper of a box lid sliding open. She thought about Elias Thorn's hands around the ledger yesterday, how he'd turned the pages as if they were warm. She thought about the scratch where A. Quinn's name used to be, the ink dug deep enough to tear the paper, as if whoever held the pen hadn't been striking a name but killing it.

*Ada Quinn.* Two syllables. A maid, twenty years old in the summer of 1972, per the census fragment Marguerite had found online at three in the morning. Born in County Cork, or maybe it was Galway—immigration records disagreed with themselves. Employed at Marlowe Estate as a housemaid from the spring of 1971 until—

Until nothing. Until the record simply stopped, the way a song stops when someone lifts the needle.

Prosser returned carrying a gray archival box and a single sheet of paper in a plastic sleeve, which he set down separately, almost gently, the way you'd set down something that might bruise.

"Death certificate came right up," he said. "The rest is going to take a while. We don't have a coroner anymore—we have a medical examiner's office two counties over—but old inquest materials sometimes got misfiled into probate. I'll look." He tapped the plastic sleeve. "This is your only hit on the name Quinn for that period. You want to look at it here, or do you want copies? Copies cost a dollar a page."

"Here first," Marguerite said. Her mouth had gone dry, and she didn't fully understand why. It was a piece of paper. It was fifty-three years old. Whatever it said had been true since before she was born.

But her hands weren't quite steady when she lifted the certificate out of the sleeve.

---

The document was typed on onionskin that had gone the color of weak tea, the typewriter font slightly misaligned the way everything was in 1973, every letter leaning a degree or two like guests at a party who'd had too much to drink. Marguerite read it standing up, both hands flat on the counter, and read it again, and then a third time slowly, aloud under her breath, because sometimes saying a thing made it real in a way seeing it didn't.

**CERTIFICATE OF DEATH — STATE OF VERMONT**

**Name of Deceased:** Ada Eilish Quinn

**Date of Death:** March 4, 1973

**Place of Death:** (blank)

**Cause of Death:** Pulmonary tuberculosis

**Informant:** Aldous Vane II

**Burial:** Cremation. Remains interred at family discretion.

**Attending Physician:** Dr. Raymond Ostrander, M.D., License #4417

Marguerite stared at the blank where the place of death should have been.

"That's odd," she said, not really meaning to say it out loud.

"What's that?" Prosser had drifted back, drawn by something in her voice, or maybe by the fact that she'd been silent too long. Archivists were like that; silence in a records room was a sound they could hear.

"There's no place of death." She turned the certificate so he could see. "Every death certificate has a place. Hospital, home, roadside, riverbank—it has to say *somewhere*. This one's blank."

Prosser leaned in. His eyebrows climbed. "Huh," he said again, and this time the word had a different weight. He reached past her and angled the certificate toward the light. "Cremation, no facility listed either. Usually there's a crematory name, a permit number. And the informant—" He squinted. "Aldous Vane. That'd be the estate. Family member usually informs, but employers did it sometimes, especially for live-in staff with no local kin." He straightened up. "Still. Blank fields like that would bounce today. In '73, if the doctor signed off, the clerk filed it. Clerks didn't argue with doctors."

"Who was Dr. Ostrander?"

"Before my time. There was an Ostrander practice in Dover Falls for years. Retired sometime in the eighties, I think. Might be dead by now." Prosser said it without ceremony, the way men in archives said everything, death being merely another kind of closed file. "Why does a housemaid from 1973 matter to a podcast?"

Marguerite considered several answers and gave him the smallest true one. "Three people died at Marlowe Estate in 1972. All ruled accidents. I'm looking at whether they were."

"And Miss Quinn?"

"Miss Quinn might be the reason I'm asking."

Prosser looked at her for a long moment. Then he glanced toward the front door, empty except for a janitor running a dust mop across the tile forty feet away, and lowered his voice in a way that told Marguerite more than the words themselves would.

"Tuberculosis," he said. "In 1973. That's late for TB to kill a twenty-one-year-old in Vermont. We had streptomycin by then. Had it for thirty years. People died of TB in this state, sure—but they died in sanatoriums, in hospitals, in their beds with their families around them. They didn't die off the books." He tapped the blank field with one blunt finger. "And they didn't get cremated with no permit number. Cremation was rare here then. Catholic families especially. An Irish girl getting cremated in 1973?" He shook his head slowly. "Her people would've had to sign off on that, and Irish Catholic people did not sign off on that. Not then. Maybe not now."

"So where are the remains?"

"That," said Prosser, "is a very good question, and I've been doing this long enough to know that very good questions in my building usually mean somebody, somewhere, didn't want them asked."

He took the certificate back, fed it into the photocopier behind the counter with the tenderness of a man handling a relic, and handed her the copy along with a request receipt. "Give me a few days on the inquest files. And the estate ledgers—I'll call the historical society, see what they'll release." He paused. "You're going to keep digging regardless of what I find, aren't you."

It wasn't a question, but Marguerite answered it anyway. "Yes."

"Then do me a favor." He leaned on the counter, and up close she could see the age spots on his hands and the tired kindness in his face. "When you write whatever you write—be careful with the dead. Even the ones who aren't where we think they are. Especially those."

* * *

She spent the afternoon in the basement annex, at a scarred wooden table under a caged bulb, working through the probate and cemetery indices while Prosser's assistant wheeled carts of boxes somewhere in the dark behind her. If Ada Quinn died in March 1973, Marguerite reasoned, she had to exist in at least one of the county's parallel bookkeeping systems: the burial index, the probate rolls, the property transfers, the newspaper obituaries on microfilm.

By four o'clock she had confirmed that Ada Quinn existed in none of them.

No grave. She checked every cemetery index in the county, then the neighboring ones—Mount Hope, Saint Brigid's, the little Quaker plot outside Townshend, the municipal grounds in Dover Falls. No Quinn, no plot, no marker. Cremated remains, she reminded herself, needn't be buried. But crematoria kept logs, and the county's single licensed crematory in that era—an adjunct of the Hollis & Sons funeral home—had donated its historical registers to the archive in 1998. Marguerite paged through 1973 twice. The register recorded eleven cremations that year, each with a date, a decedent's name, a permit number, and a receiving party.

There was no entry for March 1973. There was no entry for Ada Quinn anywhere in the book.

No probate. A twenty-one-year-old with no assets might legitimately skip probate, but the estate had employed her for two years; wages accrued, belongings existed, a trunk at minimum. Nothing filed. No next of kin notified on any record Marguerite could locate—no parents' names beyond a birthplace in Ireland, no siblings, no emergency contact. On the death certificate, in the space where kinship of informant was required, someone had typed *Employer*, and beneath it, in smaller letters, *no known family in U.S.*

No obituary. She sat in the microfilm room as the light outside the high slit windows went from gold to gray, cranking through the *Windham Courier* from March and April 1973. The paper in those months was full of gas prices and a school board fight and a feature about the Marlowe Estate's spring restoration gala—Aldous Vane II smiling on the steps of his ancestral home, handsome even in newsprint, one hand resting on the balustrade like a man touching his own face. Marguerite studied that photograph longer than she meant to. Ninety-one years old now, Prosser had said of the patriarch, still controlling half the institutions in the county. In the photo he was perhaps forty-five, and his smile had the particular warmth of a portrait painted by someone being paid to paint it.

Between the obituaries of farmers and church organists, Ada Quinn's death passed unremarked. A girl had died, and the newspaper had not seen fit to print a single line.

Marguerite switched off the microfilm reader and sat in the dark of the little booth, letting the machine's fan wind down, and made herself say it plainly, inside her own head, without drama:

*A girl was declared dead in March 1973, and nothing about the declaration is true.*

Not *might be*. Not *could have been*. Every thread she'd pulled that day had come apart in her hands the same way: the blank place of death, the missing cremation permit, the absent grave, the silent probate court, the nonexistent obituary. Individually, each anomaly had an innocent explanation—clerical sloppiness, family preference, small-town oversight. Together they formed a shape, and the shape was deliberate. This was not a death that had been poorly recorded. It was a death that had been *constructed*, assembled out of official paper by someone who understood that most people never look past the certificate. Why would they? A death certificate is the one document nobody checks, because checking it feels like disturbing the grave.

Except there was no grave to disturb.

And underneath the dread, underneath the cold arithmetic of it, Marguerite felt the other thing—the thing she'd never learned to be ashamed of, the thing Bram called her blood disease. The thrill. Electric and shameful and absolutely certain. Somewhere in the dark of the microfilm booth she caught herself smiling, and pressed her knuckles to her mouth to stop it, and failed.

Because here was the impossible thread, and it ran in only one direction. Dead people didn't need protecting. Dead people couldn't be hidden in plain sight for fifty years while someone scratched their name out of a ledger with enough force to tear the paper. You erased a living person's name. You struck a living person from the record. Everything Marguerite had touched today—the certificate, the blanks, the silences—was the paperwork of concealment, and concealment was a service performed exclusively for the living.

Ada Quinn had not died of tuberculosis in March 1973.

Ada Quinn had been *killed* on paper so that she could go on breathing somewhere the Vanes of Marlowe could not be blamed for her breath.

Marguerite packed up her notes with hands that wanted to shake and wouldn't let themselves. At the front desk, Prosser looked up from his crossword.

"Find what you needed?"

"No," Marguerite said. "I found what I didn't. Which is better."

He almost smiled. "Come back Thursday. I'll have what I can get you on the inquests." Then, as she reached the door, he added, quieter: "Miss Eriksen. The Vane name opens a lot of doors in this county. Just know it closes a few too."

Outside, the October dusk had gone blue and cold. Marguerite stood on the annex steps and pulled her coat tight and let herself feel the size of what she was holding. A forged death certificate signed by a doctor she needed to find. An informant named Aldous Vane II, ninety-one years old, whose philanthropy had built half the county and whose signature sat at the bottom of a girl's erasure. And somewhere out there—if Prosser's instincts and her own were worth anything—a woman in her seventies who had been legally dead for longer than Marguerite had been alive.

She got in the rental car and sat with the engine off, watching her breath fog the windshield, and spoke her next move aloud to the empty car the way she did when a story turned a corner and she wanted to hear how it sounded in the air.

"Dr. Raymond Ostrander," she said. "License 4417. Where did you die, and what did you leave behind?"

The name felt like a key turning somewhere far away. Because a doctor who falsified a death certificate was either bought, bullied, or broken—and in fifty years, men who carry things like that tend to set them down somewhere. In a drawer. In a letter. In a sealed envelope marked *to be opened upon my death*.

Marguerite started the engine. Behind her, the records annex went dark window by window, Prosser locking up the county's paper memory for the night, and she drove back toward town with the photocopy of Ada Quinn's death riding in her bag like a heartbeat.
