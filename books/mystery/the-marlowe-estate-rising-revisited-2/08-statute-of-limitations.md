## 8. Statute of Limitations

The letter had been sitting on my desk for three days before I understood what it was telling me.

Not the confession — I'd read that a hundred times by then. I mean the envelope. The postmark. The day after Edmund Marlowe's death, someone had walked into a post office somewhere in this county and mailed me a murder confession, and the thing that kept snagging in my mind like a loose thread on a vestment was not *who* but *when*. Confessions are written by people who can't hold a secret anymore. They are written at the end of something. This one was written one day after its subject died — which meant it was written at the beginning of something.

I am an archivist. Beginnings and endings are my trade. And yet it took me three days and a pot of cold coffee to ask the obvious question: what began the day after Sir Edmund Marlowe died?

The answer arrived on a Tuesday morning, in the form of a man named Pryce.

He came to the archive office without an appointment, which already marked him as unusual. The people who visit St. Aldric's archive fall into two categories: genealogists with laminated folders and undergraduates who've confused us with the county record office. Neither type wears a suit that fits that well. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, with the particular pallor of a man who spends his professional life under fluorescent lighting rather than God's own.

"Mr. Halloran." He said my name like he'd checked the spelling twice. "Dai Pryce. I'm a solicitor. Ashcombe and Pryce, in Wexham."

"Is this about the Marlowe papers?" We'd received a trickle of enquiries since the funeral. Executors wanting inventories, mostly. "I should say upfront that the estate's records haven't been formally transferred—"

"It's not about the papers." He sat down without being asked, which I noted, because people who sit down without being asked are people who intend to stay. "It's about a filing. A probate challenge was lodged with the chancery division eleven days ago — the day after Sir Edmund's funeral, actually. Contesting the validity of the Marlowe inheritance. All of it. Going back to 1947."

I kept my face still. Seventy years of stillness, professionally speaking.

"A challenge to a seventy-year-old grant," I said. "That would be... unusual."

"It would be impossible," Pryce agreed pleasantly, "except for one thing. Fraud unravels everything. If you can show the original grant was obtained by fraud, there's no limitation period worth the paper it's printed on. The courts have been very clear about that since *Lazarus Estates*." He smiled thinly. "Appropriate name, don't you think?"

"I'm afraid I don't follow."

"No," he said. "But you will. That's why I'm here, Mr. Halloran. Because whoever filed that challenge cited documents. Documents held in ecclesiastical archives. Yours, specifically." He opened his briefcase — good leather, scuffed at the corners, a working briefcase rather than a display one — and laid a single sheet on my desk. A photocopy, poor quality, of a page I recognized instantly.

My own finding aid. The catalogue entry for the Marlowe deposit collection, the one I'd compiled six years ago when the last trustee's papers came across from the diocesan strongroom. Someone had annotated it. In the margin, next to the entry for the sealed crypt records, in ballpoint, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*Ask the archivist. He'll have read them by now.*

"'He,'" I said. My voice sounded distant to me. "'By now.' Your filer knows me?"

"My client does, apparently. Or believes they do." Pryce folded his hands. "Here's the situation, and I'll be plain because I suspect you value plainness. The challenge has been filed. It names the 1947 grant of probate, the conveyance of the Marlowe estate following the flood, and certain subsequent transactions. If my client can produce evidence of fraud within the window the court allows — and that window is short; the respondent's counsel is already moving to strike on procedural grounds — then everything downstream of 1947 becomes contestable. The estate. The title. The trusts. Everything the Marlowe name has touched for three generations."

"And if they can't produce it in time?"

"Then it's over. Not paused — over. Struck out with prejudice, most likely, and no second filing permitted. After that, the fraud — if there was a fraud — sits behind a wall no one can legally breach. Whatever happened in 1947 stays buried under whatever happened in 1947." He looked around my office, at the shelves, the acid-free boxes, the little brass plaque on the door that said HALLORAN, T. — ARCHIVIST, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been sympathy. "Eleven days, Mr. Halloran. From yesterday. Ten now, by the time I finish this sentence."

I looked at the annotated finding aid for a long moment. Then I said the thing I probably shouldn't have said, the thing that tells you everything about how far gone I already was:

"Why come to me? If your client wants those documents, why warn the archivist? I could refuse you access. I could report this to the dean."

"Yes," Pryce said. "You could. But you won't, and my client knows you won't, because my client included a second message." He slid another sheet across the desk. This one wasn't a photocopy. It was a photograph, printed on glossy stock, of a handwritten page.

I knew the handwriting. Of course I did. I'd spent a week with it, measuring its slant against the cathedral's visitor books, against the old service registers, against anything with a date and a signature. The confession letter. But this was a different page — a fragment, half a paragraph, and where the confession had been allusive, circling its secret like a moth around glass, this fragment was direct.

*The ledger is real. The dates are on the walls of the church where they were paid for. Tell Halloran the clock is the point. He will understand what must be done before it runs out.*

"He will understand," I repeated.

"That's you," Pryce said unnecessarily.

"I gathered." I set the photograph down carefully, aligning its edge with the edge of the desk, because my hands wanted something orderly to do. "Your client sent me a riddle inside a deadline. Do they imagine I work well under pressure?"

"I imagine," Pryce said, standing, "that your client imagines you're the only person in this county who can read what needs reading. Eleven days. Ten and three-quarters." At the door he paused. "One more thing. The challenge was filed anonymously — through counsel, properly constituted, but the applicant's identity is sealed pending the evidentiary hearing. Even I don't know who my ultimate client is. I take instructions through an intermediary." His mouth twisted, the first honest expression I'd seen on him. "In thirty years of practice, Mr. Halloran, I have never taken instructions through an intermediary. Whoever this is, they're frightened. Frightened people pay very well, and frightened people also lie. Keep that in mind."

And he was gone, leaving the smell of expensive cologne and the sound of the cloister bells marking the hour, ten days and some odd minutes until midnight on the seventeenth.

* * *

I did what I always do when the world becomes illegible: I made a list.

It's a habit from my training, from Dr. Fenwick at the School of Advanced Study, who used to say that panic is just information without structure. So I took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote at the top, in my neatest hand: *WHAT IS TRUE.* Underneath:

1. Edmund Marlowe died twelve days ago. Natural causes, per the certificate.
2. One day after his death, someone posted me a confession describing a staged flood, a false burial, and a stolen identity — crimes dating to 1947.
3. One day after his death, someone filed a legal challenge to the entire Marlowe inheritance, citing fraud.
4. Both events reference documents connected to this archive. Both reference me by name or implication.
5. The confession's author and the challenge's filer may be the same person. Or the confession may exist to legitimize the challenge. Or the challenge may exist to force the confession into the open. I drew arrows between these possibilities until the page looked like a spider's wedding.

Then, underneath, a second heading: *WHO BENEFITS IF THE CLOCK RUNS OUT.*

That list was shorter. Whoever held the tainted inheritance — Vivian Marlowe-Ashe, presumably, though the estate's structure was a maze of trusts I hadn't begun to map — benefited enormously from silence. Eleven days of silence and the past became legally inert forever. Which meant the clock wasn't running against the truth. It was running against everyone who wanted the truth kept down.

Unless. Unless the filer's evidence was thin, and the deadline was theater — pressure designed to make someone, anyone, hand over the missing pieces before a court could dismiss the whole enterprise. Blackmail wears many costumes. Sometimes it wears a barrister's gown.

I sat with that thought for a long time while the light moved across the floor of the archive office, the way it has moved across that floor for six hundred years, indifferent to deadlines.

* * *

Vivian Marlowe-Ashe received me the next afternoon at Marlowe House, or what remained of it — the 1950s replacement, all pale brick and tall windows, built on the high ground after the flood took the original. She was smaller than I remembered, or perhaps I had simply learned to look more carefully since our first meeting. Her sitting room smelled of lilies and wood polish. On the mantelpiece stood photographs of men in uniform and women in pearls, and I found myself studying them with new eyes now, wondering which faces belonged to whom, whether any of them was the man called Alistair Marlowe or only the man who wore his name.

"You've been busy," she said, pouring tea without asking whether I wanted any. "Mr. Pryce telephoned my solicitor yesterday. Apparently there's a challenge to the estate. Apparently it cites fraud." She handed me the cup. Her hand was perfectly steady. "Apparently, Mr. Halloran, you know something about it."

"Mrs. Marlowe-Ashe, I received a visitor, not a briefing. I know almost nothing."

"Almost nothing is such a useful quantity." She settled into her chair. "Do you play chess, Mr. Halloran?"

"I don't."

"Pity. You'd be good at it. You have the temperament — patient, obsessive, slightly bloodless." She smiled to take the sting out, or to put one in; with her I still couldn't tell. "Let me tell you what I know, since we're exchanging almost-nothings. My brother is eleven days dead. Within twenty-four hours of his funeral, a stranger files a claim of fraud against my family going back to the Flood — capital F, Mr. Halloran, the way this family always says it, as if it were scripture. And the claim's timing means it dies aborning unless evidence appears within days. Evidence nobody has produced in seventy years." She sipped her tea. "So either this person has something new, or they want me to believe they do. And either way, they chose to announce themselves through you. Through the *archivist*. Why is that, do you suppose?"

"Because the confession named me."

"The confession." She said the word lightly, tasting it. "Yes. Edmund mentioned you'd received an unusual letter. He found it funny, actually. He said, 'Someone's finally writing to Theo instead of to us.'" For a moment — half a second, no more — her composure flickered, and underneath it I saw something raw and exhausted, the face of a woman who had been holding a door shut for fifty years and had just heard a knock. "Edmund laughed about a great many things in his last months. He'd become almost giddy. Did you know that? People assumed it was health. It wasn't."

"What was it?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and I braced myself for the slip, the crack, the tell. Instead she set down her cup with a small precise click and said, "That, Mr. Halloran, is exactly the sort of question you should be asking someone other than me. You have ten days. I'd spend them wisely."

At the door, as I was leaving, she added, without turning around: "Whatever you find — and you will find it, you're the type — remember that my family has survived on what this house chooses not to say. Be careful whose silence you break. Some of them are load-bearing."

* * *

That night I went back to the confession for the eleventh time, and this time I read it not as a puzzle but as a timetable.

*They think the water closed over everything,* the unknown hand had written, in that careful, elderly slant. *Water closes nothing. Water only moves things and sets them down somewhere new. I have watched the seventeenth of June approach every year for longer than you have been alive, Mr. Halloran, and this year it arrives carrying more than it ever has. When it passes, the door shuts for good. What is done in darkness and left unchallenged becomes, in the law's cold arithmetic, as if it never needed forgiving.*

The seventeenth of June. Ten days away. I checked the statute citations Pryce had left — the filer's procedural schedule was public record once lodged — and there it was: the evidentiary deadline, midnight, June the seventeenth. The confession's author had known the date before the filing existed. Had known it, perhaps, before Edmund died.

Which raised a question I wrote in my notebook and underlined twice:

*How do you know a deadline that hasn't been set yet — unless you're the one setting it?*

I sat in the archive after hours, the lamps low, the cathedral breathing its night sounds above me — stone ticking as it cooled, a door somewhere, the eternal small weather of an old building. And I turned the question over, and over, and each turn made the picture less comfortable.

If the author set the deadline, the confession wasn't a confession at all. It was a summons. Someone had spent decades preparing this moment — assembling the legal instrument, choosing the date, mailing the letter timed to the hour — and they had chosen me as their instrument of retrieval. *Tell Halloran the clock is the point.* Not: tell Halloran the truth. Tell him the *clock*.

Curiosity had carried me this far. Curiosity was easy; curiosity was a game played in warm rooms with catalogues and tea. But standing there in the darkening archive, I felt the thing shift underneath me, the way ice shifts underfoot when you realize the pond is deeper than you thought. This was no longer a question of what happened in 1947. It was a question of what was about to happen in eleven days, to living people, with me at the hinge of it. Someone had killed — the confession said poison, said the doctor's note, said *he did not die of his heart* — and someone else intended to drag that killing into the light before the law's door closed, and both of them were using me, and one of them might kill again rather than let the clock run out.

I thought of Edith Carrow's father, the sexton, drowned a year after the flood. I thought of Tomas Reeve, buried under another man's name for seventy years. I thought of the dean's smooth warning — *some stones are better left unturned, Mr. Halloran* — and how easily that warning sat beside a 1947 sealing order signed by his predecessor.

Because that was the thread I couldn't stop pulling now, the one that had been worrying at me since Pryce left. The crypt was sealed by order of Dean Harrowgate in June 1947 — the same month as the flood, the same month as the burial. Sealed fast, sealed unusually, sealed with a structural survey no one could produce. And the cathedral, my cathedral, the institution that had given a friendless boy a life among ordered papers and predictable bells — the cathedral had locked that door and thrown away the key and called it engineering.

Ten days. Somewhere in this building, or in the diocesan registry at Wexham, sat the answer to why a dean in 1947 had moved so quickly and so thoroughly. Trustee signatures. Counter-signatures. And churches, like everyone else, keep receipts.

I got my coat. I told the night verger I'd forgotten a personal item in the office, which was even true — the truth makes the best cover, another thing this case was teaching me — and I let myself back into the archive stacks, toward the shelf where the deans' private correspondence slept in boxes nobody had opened in decades.

Box 1947-C was where I'd left it. The dust on top was undisturbed.

But as I lifted it down, my torch caught the floor at the end of the aisle — a smear in the grey film of dust, the size and shape of a shoe print, pointing toward the strongroom door.

Somebody had been in the archive recently. Somebody who knew exactly which boxes mattered.

And the strongroom, when I reached it and tried the handle, was unlocked.
