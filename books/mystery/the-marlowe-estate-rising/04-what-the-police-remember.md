## 4. What the Police Remember

Detective Sorrel Vance had been awake for thirty-one hours when the lawyer finally sat down across from her, and she was grateful for it. Tiredness made her honest.

The interview room at the Kestrel Falls substation smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet, and the man from the county had left a box of evidence on the table between them like a centerpiece: one wool coat, charcoal gray; one scarf, oxblood; both tagged and bagged and photographed against the dead man's chest in the greenhouse.

Vance didn't open the folder in front of her. She'd read it twice already, once at the scene and once in her car with the dome light on, and she wanted to watch Quintana's face while she talked instead of watching paper.

"Ulyana Quintana," Vance said. "Estate attorney. Twelve years in probate. No disciplinary history, no complaints, no arrests." She let that sit for a second. "Very clean file. Almost like somebody swept it."

"I keep my nose clean." The lawyer's voice was low, even, unhurried. She sat with her hands folded on the table like a woman waiting for a bus, not a woman being questioned about a murder victim wearing her clothes.

"That's not what I asked."

"You didn't ask anything yet, Detective. You made a statement about my file."

Vance smiled without warmth and pulled out the chair opposite her. Up close, Quintana was smaller than she looked in the courtroom sketches Vance had dug up—compact, dark-haired, gray at the temples, with the kind of stillness that most people mistook for calm and Vance recognized as something else entirely. Predators were still too. So were snipers. So, once upon a time, had been Vance herself, before the job wore the edges off her patience.

She set a recorder on the table. "Interview with Ulyana Quintana, regarding the death of Thomas Wren, found this morning on the Marlowe Estate property. You're not under arrest. You're free to leave whenever you want." A beat. "But you won't."

Quintana's eyebrows lifted a fraction of an inch. "No?"

"No. Because you came here to find out what we know, and you'll sit here all day if that's what it takes." Vance hit record, said the date and time and names for the machine, then leaned back. "So let's save each other some time. Tell me about the coat."

"It was stolen from my room at the estate. Along with the scarf. I reported the loss to Mrs. Adeyemi yesterday morning—"

"At what time?"

"Around nine. Before breakfast."

"We'll verify that." Vance slid a photograph across the table. Thomas Wren, forty-one years old by the coroner's initial estimate, lying among the broken pots of the greenhouse with his eyes open and his throat bruised in a way the photographer had caught in merciless detail. He was wearing Quintana's coat. The oxblood scarf was knotted at his neck, neat as a gift. "Do you know him?"

"No."

"Look again."

"I did look. I don't know him." Quintana studied the photo the way she probably studied contracts—thoroughly, without flinching, cataloging rather than reacting. "His hands are wrong for a laborer. Soft. But there's dirt under the nails, so he'd been outside recently, or someone put him there after. The coat fits him poorly in the shoulders. Whoever dressed him either didn't know my measurements or didn't care, because I'm two inches shorter than that coat hangs on him." She pushed the photo back. "That's all I can tell you, Detective. Someone is framing me. I assumed you'd have figured that part out already—it's the only interesting thing about me in this whole story."

Vance held her gaze for a long moment. Then she opened the folder she'd been keeping closed, and turned it around, and watched the stillness in the other woman's face change for the first time.

Three photographs. Three different towns. Three different case numbers.

"Hollis Grant," Vance said. "Fifty-eight. Fell down his own cellar stairs in Ashford, three years ago. Ruled accidental. Except his daughter kept calling the state police saying her father didn't drink, and somebody kept making those calls go away, and six months later his will surfaced leaving everything to a caregiver nobody in the family had ever heard of." She tapped the second photo. "Marguerite Doyle. Sixty-three. Drowned in two feet of bathtub water in Corben County, eighteen months ago. Ruled accidental. Her nephew contested the estate, hired an independent pathologist on his own dime, and got told the exhumation request was denied for—get this—administrative reasons." Tap. "Peter Lasko. Seventy-one. Carbon monoxide in a garage with a car that hadn't been driven in a week, four months ago. Ruled accidental. His housekeeper inherited, and when she tried to sell the house, the sale was blocked by a trust nobody could produce paperwork for."

Vance closed the folder. "Three accidents. Three estates. Three inheritances redirected. And in every single file, in every single county, there's one name that shows up in the margins. Not as a suspect. Not even as a witness. As the *attorney of record*."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the hall, a phone rang three times and stopped.

"You've been busy," Vance said.

Quintana looked at the photographs for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice hadn't changed at all, which was itself an answer. "You've done your homework."

"I do everyone's homework. It's the job."

"No." Quintana shook her head slowly. "This isn't homework. Homework is a DMV check and a credit pull. This—" she touched the edge of the folder, almost gently, "—this took weeks. You pulled files from three counties. You tracked inheritance chains. You noticed a pattern that three separate police departments missed or ignored." Her eyes came up. "You knew about these cases before you ever heard my name attached to Marlowe Estate. Didn't you?"

Vance said nothing.

"So the question isn't whether I'm a suspect in Thomas Wren's death," Quintana went on, soft and precise. "You brought me in here to confirm something you already believe. You think I'm a fixer. You think I help people die conveniently and get paid for it, and you've been hunting me for months, and Marlowe Estate just handed you your collar." She folded her hands again. "Ask me the real question, Detective. I'm tired too."

For a moment—one long, dangerous moment—Vance considered it. Considered telling the truth in a recorded interview, which was a thing cops learned early never to do.

Then she did it anyway, because she was tired, and because tired people are honest, and because some part of her had been waiting months for this chair to be occupied.

"The real question," Vance said, "is how three old people died badly in three different counties, and the only person who noticed was *you*. Not the responding officers. Not the coroners who signed the certificates. Not the detectives who caught the calls and cleared them in a week. You. A probate lawyer with no forensic training, sitting in the middle of every single one of those estates with a front-row seat." She leaned forward. "And every time, the official finding stood, and the family went home, and the file got stamped closed—and then quietly, off the record, something changed. The caregiver in Ashford got reported to adult protective services by an anonymous letter with legal citations in it. The nephew in Corben County received a certified package of medical records he had no legal right to have. The trust blocking Peter Lasko's house sale got challenged by a filing so clean it made our county attorney weep with joy." She straightened. "Somebody solved those cases. Somebody who wasn't the police. And that somebody signed nothing, testified nowhere, and vanished like smoke every single time."

"And that bothers you," Quintana said.

"*Bothers* me." Vance laughed, and it came out ugly. "You want to know what bothers me? Hollis Grant's daughter called my department eleven times. Eleven. I read the call log myself last month. Eleven calls over five months, and every one of them got logged as 'unfounded complaint' by a detective who spent forty minutes on the scene and never came back. Marguerite Doyle's autopsy was signed off by a coroner who didn't examine the body—he initialed the findings of a deputy half his age who was on his third shift without sleep. Peter Lasko's CO reading came from a meter that failed calibration the following Tuesday and everybody knew it and nobody cared." She spread her hands. "The system looked at those three deaths and shrugged. And then some ghost in a good coat walked through the wreckage, fixed what she could fix, and disappeared without so much as a press release. And now I'm sitting across from the ghost, and she's wearing my prime suspect's face, and I don't know which one of us should be more afraid."

Quintana was quiet for a while. When she answered, something in her voice had shifted—not softer, exactly. More careful. Like a hand testing ice.

"You resent it," she said. "Not me. Them. The departments that missed it."

"I resent that you were right and they were lazy and nobody will ever say so out loud," Vance said. "I resent that justice in this county apparently requires a civilian with a law degree and a taste for breaking into records rooms, because the men and women with badges couldn't be bothered. Yes. I resent it. I resent it like a splinter." She pointed at the folder. "Because I've read those files until I have them memorized, and you know what I found? You were right. All three times. Whatever happened in those houses wasn't accident, and whatever you did about it was more than anyone with a badge managed, and if you'd come to any of us—if you'd walked into my office with what you had—we could have done it *right*. Warrants. Exhumations. Charges. Instead you played vigilante with a briefcase, and now a man is dead in a greenhouse wearing your clothes, and I have to spend my morning deciding whether the only competent investigator I've met in ten years is a murderer."

"I didn't kill Thomas Wren."

"I believe you believe that."

"I didn't kill him," Quintana repeated, and for the first time her voice carried weight behind it, iron under the calm. "I've spent twelve years reading wills, Detective. Do you know what a will is? It's the last honest sentence a person gets to say. Everything else in their life is negotiation and noise, but the will is the truth they finally commit to paper. I have watched families tear themselves apart over money and I have watched them do it over *forgiveness*, and I have never—not once—hurt anyone to get to the truth. I find the truth because nobody else will look for it. That's not a confession. That's a job description."

Vance studied her. Thirty-one hours awake, and somewhere in the fog of it, a thought was assembling itself that she didn't like and couldn't shake:

*If she's telling the truth, then whoever killed Thomas Wren knows about the three cases. Knows enough to weaponize them. Knows enough to dress a corpse in her clothes and stage a frame that only works if the framer understands exactly who Ulyana Quintana is and what she does.*

Which meant the killer wasn't random. Which meant the killer had done homework of their own.

Which meant, possibly, that Sorrel Vance wasn't the only hunter who'd been tracking the ghost.

"Here's what happens now," Vance said aloud, because the thought was too big to carry silently. "You're released pending investigation. You stay reachable. You don't leave the county. And you don't go near that estate's records room, its mausoleum, or its patriarch's study, because if you contaminate so much as a fingerprint over there, I will arrest you for obstruction so fast your head spins. Are we clear?"

"Perfectly." Quintana rose, collected her coat from the back of the chair, and paused at the door. For a moment she looked almost—almost—like a person instead of a locked room.

"For what it's worth, Detective," she said, "the Lasko meter calibration report was buried in a maintenance log nobody reads. Page forty-one. If you're going to resent me, at least resent me accurately."

The door clicked shut behind her.

Vance sat alone in the interview room for a long time, staring at the recorder's red light, listening to the tape hiss. Then she pulled the Lasko file toward her, flipped to page forty-one, and felt the splinter sink deeper.

Her desk phone buzzed. Dispatch. She picked it up, expecting another canvass update from the estate.

"Sergeant Vance? We've got a records request came through on the system an hour ago. Somebody pulled the full case file on the Marlowe girl—the 2014 suicide. The daughter. Signed out under your mentor's authorization code."

Vance went cold.

"Whose request?"

"That's the thing," dispatch said. "It's flagged as yours."
