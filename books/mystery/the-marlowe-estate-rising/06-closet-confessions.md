## 6. Closet Confessions

The coat was mine.

I stood in front of the guest room wardrobe at seven in the morning, three days after they pulled a dead man out of the greenhouse wearing my wool overcoat and my grey scarf, and I made myself look at the empty hanger where both of them had lived for as long as I'd been coming to Marlowe Estate. The hanger was still there. It was one of those slim velvet ones I'd bought in bulk years ago because they don't slip, and it sat in the row of identical hangers like a missing tooth.

Detective Vance had taken the coat and scarf into evidence on Tuesday. She'd taken them with a kind of ceremony, holding them up by the shoulders so the whole household could see, so *I* could see, her eyes flat and satisfied over the collar. "Recognize these?" she'd asked, and I said yes, they were mine, and she'd written that down as if it were a confession.

What she hadn't asked — what nobody had asked, including me until this morning — was *when* they were taken from me.

Because here was the thing I'd been turning over all night like a stone with something underneath it: I hadn't missed the coat. I own eleven coats; I notice when one goes missing the way you notice when a book is out of place on your own shelf. And the last time I had definitely seen the wool coat and the grey scarf together was the evening of October ninth, when I'd hung them in this closet after a long day of probate paperwork and driven home to my apartment across town.

Which meant someone had taken them out of this room sometime between October ninth and the murder, and I had never noticed.

I sat down on the edge of the guest bed, which was made with hospital corners by Mrs. Adeyemi's staff even though no guest had slept in it since before the patriarch died, and I let that fact sit in my chest for a while.

For ten years I had built a life on not being noticed. It wasn't a trick exactly; it was more like a discipline. I dressed in colors that didn't photograph well. I spoke softly and asked questions that sounded like small talk. I arrived early and left late and stood near walls. Executors' attorneys are furniture — everyone knows this, everyone accepts it, and I had turned that acceptance into an instrument. Three times now I had stood in a room full of grieving people and watched the truth move through them like weather, and three times I had written down what I saw and passed it quietly to people who could act on it, and three times the police had ruled what I'd helped expose as accident or misadventure and the world had gone on, and I had gone back to being furniture.

Furniture doesn't get murdered. Furniture doesn't get framed. Furniture has never once, in ten years, felt the specific vertigo of realizing that someone had been standing inside its private space, touching its things, studying it.

Someone had been in this room. Not once. For weeks.

* * *

I started with the room itself, because rooms confess if you interrogate them patiently enough.

The guest room sat at the end of the east wing, second floor, past the linen closet and the room that had been the daughter's — Cressida Marlowe's — before she died, which had been kept shut for a decade and opened only by Mrs. Adeyemi, who dusted it every Thursday. My room was the farthest from the main staircase. To reach it, you came up the back stairs by the kitchen, walked forty feet of corridor past two doors that were always locked, and arrived at a door that had no lock of its own.

That last detail I noted with a fresh, unpleasant clarity. No lock. A brass privacy latch on the inside, the flimsy kind that yields to a butter knife, and nothing else. When Mrs. Adeyemi had shown me to the room during my first stay, eighteen months ago, I'd thought nothing of it. Estate houses didn't lock their guest rooms; guests were family-adjacent, trusted, temporary.

Now I knelt at the latch and looked at it closely for the first time. The brass around the thumb-turn was worn bright, polished by use. The rest of the hardware in the room — hinges, handle, window latches — carried the dull patina of things touched rarely. But the latch gleamed.

Somebody used this latch often. Somebody going in and out, again and again, and each time pulling the door shut behind them so the latch caught and the room looked undisturbed.

I checked the windows next. Second floor, sash windows, painted shut on the outside frame except — I ran my thumbnail along the seam — except the one over the garden wall, where the paint line was scored clean, recently cut with a blade. The sill outside bore a faint crescent of scuff in the moss. Someone had come and gone through the window too, or at least had prepared to.

And then the closet floor. I pulled everything out — my suitcase, my spare shoes, the box of files I kept under the hanging rail — and looked at the boards. Dust everywhere except a swept-clean channel leading from the door to the closet, wide enough for a person to walk without leaving prints. Swept regularly. Swept *recently*, because the channel's edges were sharp, not softened by new fall.

Whoever it was hadn't broken in. They had a key or a routine, and they had maintained the fiction of an untouched room with real care, over weeks. This wasn't opportunism. This was preparation. Someone had been curating me — learning my habits, my sizes, my schedule — long before anyone knew a will would name me executor, long before there was any reason to frame me at all.

Unless the reason predated the will. Unless the reason was ten years old.

I put the files back and sat very still for a moment, listening to the house. Marlowe Estate made sounds the way old ships do — settling timbers, ticking pipes, the distant percussion of Mrs. Adeyemi's kitchen. Somewhere below, the eldest son, Edmund, was arguing on the phone in a voice pitched deliberately loud enough to be overheard. Somewhere in the garden, the groundskeeper — Tam, the boy with no surname — was running the leaf blower, a steady industrial drone that would cover the sound of almost anything.

Almost anything except a question asked directly. So I went downstairs to ask some.

* * *

Mrs. Adeyemi found me first, which was either luck or surveillance, and lately I was no longer confident about the difference.

She was in the morning room arranging lilies — white ones, funeral stock, ordered in absurd quantity since the greenhouse — and she didn't look up when I came in. "You slept badly," she said. "There's chamomile in the blue tin."

"I need to ask you about the guest room."

That got her eyes up. She was sixty-something, built like a keel, with hands that had worked for this family longer than I'd been alive, and she had the particular stillness of servants who have learned that most questions are traps. "What about it?"

"Who cleans it?"

"The same as always. Rosa does the east wing Tuesdays and Fridays." She stripped a leaf off a stem with a quick twist. "Why."

"Has Rosa mentioned anything odd? Things moved. A window open. Anything."

"Rosa mentions nothing. Rosa is paid to mention nothing, same as the rest of us." But her hands had stopped moving, and I catalogued that. "Is something missing? Because if something of yours is missing, Miss Quintana, you should say so plainly, and not go making the girls feel accused."

"Two things," I said. "A coat and a scarf. Taken sometime after October ninth."

Something crossed her face then — not guilt exactly, but recognition, the flicker of a person connecting a fact they'd been sitting on. She resumed stripping leaves with more force than the lilies deserved.

"You knew," I said. "Or you suspected."

"I know that coat," she said finally. "Grey wool, horn buttons. You wore it every cold day last winter, and you hung it in that closet, and I noticed it because—" She stopped. Set the stems down. "Because I noticed it was gone in November. Before the frost. I thought you'd taken it home and forgotten to bring it back, and I said nothing, because saying things is how trouble starts in this house."

"November," I repeated. The word landed in my stomach like a dropped coin. "You're certain?"

"Certain enough. I remember because I told myself to buy you a scarf for Christmas and then I didn't, because it seemed presumptuous." Her jaw tightened. "And then a dead man wears it into the greenhouse, and now the detective asks me did I see anyone near your room, and what am I supposed to say? That I noticed a coat missing two months ago and thought it was laundry? She'll write it down and make it mean whatever she wants it to mean."

"She'll want to know who had access."

"Everyone has access. That's the answer, and it's also no answer." She turned on me fully then, and for a moment the servant mask slipped and I saw the woman underneath, tired and frightened and fiercely loyal to something I couldn't yet name. "Miss Quintana. Listen to me. In thirty-one years in this house I have learned one thing above all others: when something bad happens at Marlowe, it did not start when it happened. It started long before. Sometimes years before. You're the one who finds things out — the staff talk, you know they talk, they say you have a way of hearing. So hear this: whoever took your coat didn't take it in October. They took it when they took the *room*. Ask yourself why a stranger needed a room in this house for months."

"And how would they get one?"

"That," said Mrs. Adeyemi, "is a better question than the one the detective is asking." She picked up the lilies again, conversation over, and added, without looking at me: "Edmund keeps the keys. All of them. Even the ones he says he lost."

* * *

Edmund Marlowe received me in his study with the warmth of a man granting an audience he resented. He was fifty-three, red-faced, and had spent the week since the murder performing grief for the greenhouse victim — a man named Corliss, it emerged, a former estate handyman whom nobody in the family admitted knowing well — while radiating the suspicion that I was somehow the cause of all of it.

"You want to search my study," he said.

"I want to see the key board. It's a formality."

"Nothing about you is a formality, Ms. Quintana. You arrive, my father dies, a man turns up in your clothes, and the detective treats this house like a crime scene. Forgive me if I don't leap to assist."

"Your father died before I ever set foot here."

"My father died of a heart attack in his sleep, and you've spent every visit since acting like it was a puzzle you intended to solve." He poured himself something amber at eleven in the morning. "Vance already went through my things. Twice. With a warrant the second time, which I suppose makes it legal."

"Then the key board won't surprise her. Or me."

He glared, but he led me down the hall to a narrow cupboard beside the estate office, and inside hung the board: rows of hooks, rows of keys, each tagged in faded ink. GARDEN DOOR. CHAPEL. EAST WING — LINEN. EAST WING — CRESSIDA'S ROOM. And at the bottom, on a hook whose tag read GUEST RM (E), a single key.

Except the hook was empty.

"There was a key there," Edmund said slowly, following my gaze. "There's always—"

"When did you last use it?" I asked.

"I don't use guest keys. Guests—" He stopped. I watched him do the arithmetic, the same arithmetic I'd done upstairs, and watched the color change in his face. "My father kept a spare set," he said. "In his desk. After the funeral I boxed up his study myself. If a spare went into storage—"

"Would you know if someone took it out of storage?"

"No," he said, and then, quieter, with the first honest note I'd heard from him: "No. Nobody would. That's rather the point of storage, isn't it?" He looked at the empty hook a moment longer, and then at me, and something shifted behind the bluster — not trust, but a shared, unwelcome realization. "Ms. Quintana. Whoever wore that coat into my greenhouse knew this house. Knew it well enough to keep a room, keep a schedule, keep a secret. That's not a burglar. That's family. Or close enough that it makes no difference."

It was the smartest thing Edmund Marlowe had ever said to me, and I hated him a little for having thought of it first.

* * *

I ended the day where I probably should have started it: in the corridor outside Cressida Marlowe's room.

The door was shut, as it had been shut for ten years. Mrs. Adeyemi dusted it Thursdays; today was Wednesday. The carpet in front of it was worn in a way that didn't match a room visited once a week — the wear ran along the wall opposite, in the shadow of the linen closet's jut, a strip of flattened pile exactly the width of a person standing still.

Someone had waited here. More than once. Watching the corridor, or watching my door forty feet away, or simply standing outside a dead girl's room the way people stand outside places that hold them.

I pressed my palm flat against Cressida's door. Cold panel, brass knob, and — faint, at knee height, on the paint — a smudge of green. Sap, maybe. Or the particular grime of a gardener's hands.

Tam. The groundskeeper with no surname, twenty-ish, quiet, who had found the body, who had wept at it in a way that struck me at the time as disproportionate, who moved through the estate like someone navigating by memory rather than sight. Who had known the greenhouse was unlocked. Who had known, perhaps, a great many things.

Or who was being set up as neatly as I was. Two scapegoats were better than one.

I took my hand off the door. Behind me, at the far end of the corridor, the back stairs creaked — one step, then silence, the disciplined silence of someone who had meant to take none. By the time I turned, the stairwell was empty, and the only sound in the east wing was the tick of pipes and, somewhere below, faint and steady, the drone of a leaf blower working ground it had already cleared.

That night I packed a bag, took the files out from under the closet rail, and wedged a sliver of matchstick into the latch of my door at a height only I would notice. Then I sat up with the lamp off and watched the corridor through the gap in the curtains, and understood, finally and completely, what had changed.

For ten years I had watched other people. Now something in this house had been watching me — patient, methodical, intimate with my habits — and the invisibility I had worn like armor had turned out to be exactly that: a shell, thin enough for anyone determined to see through, and useless against a hand reaching in to take what it wanted.

At 2:14 in the morning, the matchstick fell to the floor of the empty corridor, and footsteps went past my door toward Cressida's room, unhurried, familiar, and light.
