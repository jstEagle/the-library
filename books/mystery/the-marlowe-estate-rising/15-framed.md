## 15. Framed

The knock came at six in the morning, and it was not a knock so much as a verdict.

Ulyana had been awake since four—she'd slept badly in the guest room that no longer felt like hers, the room where someone had spent weeks handling her clothes—and when the pounding started on the front door of the dower cottage where she'd been staying, she knew before her feet touched the floor. Nobody knocked like that for good news.

She opened the door to two uniformed officers and behind them, on the gravel drive, Detective Sorrel Vance, arms crossed against the cold, her breath steaming.

"Ms. Quintana." Vance's voice was flat as slate. "We have a warrant for your vehicle."

"My car?" Ulyana kept her hands visible, her voice level. Ten years of reading rooms had taught her that the first thirty seconds set the terms. "It's parked around back."

"We know where it is," Vance said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They walked her around the side of the cottage, past the boxwood hedge, to where her gray sedan sat under a skin of morning frost. One of the uniforms was already standing beside it, gloved, not touching. Vance stopped ten feet away and let Ulyana look.

The driver's door was ajar. Not forced—the lock was untouched, the frame unmarked. Someone had opened it with a key or a slim jim or simply the knowledge that Ulyana had stopped locking it three days ago because there was nothing left in it worth stealing.

Inside, on the passenger seat, laid out with the neatness of a place setting, were two objects.

A document, folded once, cream paper, wax seal cracked. Even from the driveway Ulyana recognized the hand—the same spidery, deliberate script as the impossible will she'd read in the library a week ago. The second will. The real will. The one she'd found in Celia Marlowe's music box forty-eight hours earlier and then, God help her, could not find again last night when she'd gone back for it.

And beside it, small and dark and gleaming wet even now, a cufflink. Silver, monogrammed *E.M.*—Edmund Marlowe, the greenhouse victim—with a crust of brown-black blood dried into its hinge.

"Whose car is registered to this address?" Vance asked, though she knew. She'd known for days. "Yours. Rented under your name. Driven by you alone." She nodded at the uniformed officer. "Open it."

The officer bagged the cufflink first, photographed everything, lifted the will with an evidence sleeve. Ulyana watched the wax seal catch the early light and felt something cold slide down her spine—not fear exactly, though fear was in there, but recognition. This was choreography. Whoever arranged those objects on the seat hadn't been hiding them; they'd been *displaying* them. For the first officer through the door. For the press, if it came to that. For her.

"You want to tell me how your fingerprints got on that will?" Vance said quietly, close enough that only Ulyana could hear.

"I touched it because I found it," Ulyana said. "Two nights ago. In Celia Marlowe's music box, in the east wing. I read it. I put it back. When I went back for it yesterday evening, it was gone."

"Convenient."

"It's true."

Vance studied her, and Ulyana saw the war behind her eyes—the detective who'd spent a week being stonewalled by her own department, who'd made the uneasy offer of alliance on the mausoleum steps just last night, now standing here watching the evidence assemble itself around the one person she'd chosen to trust. *I told you what I find,* Vance had said. *You stay out of my crime scenes.*

"I stayed out of your crime scenes," Ulyana said, reading her thoughts. "This isn't one. This is a stage set."

"That's exactly what someone would say," Vance said, "if they'd planted evidence in their own car."

* * *

They didn't arrest her. That was the strange part. Vance read her the caution anyway, formally, by the book, and then said, "You're not in custody. But you're not leaving the county either, Ms. Quintana, and if you think about running, I'll remember every favor anyone ever did me and spend them all at once."

"I don't run," Ulyana said. "Running is for people with somewhere else to be."

But inside, where Vance couldn't see, panic was doing arithmetic. Because the will in the evidence bag wasn't just evidence—it was the thing she'd promised to protect. It named Tomas sole heir. It accused Edmund Marlowe—by which everyone would read *the dead patriarch*—of murdering his own daughter. And now it lived in a police evidence locker, entered into a case file controlled by a department that had already burned one archive to erase the past. She had carried the truth out of the music box and straight into the wolf's mouth.

And worse: whoever took it from her room had known exactly where she'd hidden it. Which meant her room. Her things. Again. The violation from weeks ago—the stolen coat, the scarf, the borrowed clothes on a corpse—had never actually ended. Someone had been living inside her life the whole time, patient as dust, waiting for her to find the will so they could take it back and turn it into a noose.

She thought about the guest room. The closet with its careful rows. The way Mrs. Adeyemi had looked at her yesterday, mid-sentence, before the phone call shut her mouth like a drawer.

Someone in this house was working for a dead man.

Or for a man who refused to die.

* * *

By nine she was back at the estate, because fugitive or not, investigator always, and because there was one person she needed to see before the household heard the news and the walls went up.

She found Tomas in the potting shed, pretending to repot ferns. His hands were shaking. He'd heard.

"They're saying you killed him," he said without looking up. "Mr. Edmund. The man in the greenhouse. They're saying you killed him and wrote yourself into the will."

"The will doesn't benefit me. It names you."

"I know what it names." He set down the trowel. His jaw was tight, his eyes red-rimmed. "My mother's will. My mother's handwriting. You told me—you showed me—" He stopped. "And now it's gone and it's in your car and I'm supposed to believe you didn't—"

"Tomas." She said his name the way she'd learned to say it over the past week, the boy's name, not the groundskeeper's, and something in him flinched and steadied at once. "If I wanted to steal that will, why would I have shown it to you? Why would I have told *anyone*? The moment I opened that music box, I became the most dangerous person in this house to whoever wants the old story to stay buried. Taking it out of my hands and putting it in my car isn't theft. It's a message."

"What message?"

"*We know where you sleep.*" She let that sit. "Whoever did this has access to my room, my car, and enough patience to wait weeks between moves. That's not a burglar. That's family."

Tomas went very still. "You think it's one of them. Mr. Alistair? Cousin Renata?"

"I think Alistair wants me gone and hasn't got the imagination for theater like this. I think Renata cries beautifully and counts everything. And I think neither of them could have gotten into a locked wing at two in the morning without waking Mrs. Adeyemi, who sleeps light and guards this house like a border." Ulyana paused. "Which leaves the person who doesn't need to sneak anywhere. The person whose death makes every door in this house open for him."

"You keep saying that." Tomas's voice dropped. "In the notes you left me. *If the patriarch is alive.* You keep saying it like it's possible."

"Your grandfather faked a stroke once before, in '09, to test which of his children would visit. Mrs. Adeyemi told me. He called it *culling the calendar*—finding out who truly loved him by letting them mourn him early." She looked at the boy—no, the young man, twenty-two now, with his mother's chin and his grandfather's stubbornness written all over him like a warning label. "A man like that doesn't stop playing. He just raises the stakes."

"And the stakes are you hanging for murder."

"And you inheriting nothing," Ulyana said. "Don't flatter yourself out of the equation. If that will is discredited—if it looks forged, planted, tainted by association with me—then the old probate stands, the estate goes to Alistair, and the one person who knows the truth about your mother's death becomes a convicted felon nobody believes." She held his gaze. "So we get it back. Or we prove it's genuine. Either way, we move today, because today is the day they expect me to run."

* * *

She spent the middle of the day doing the thing she was best at and worst at right now: being invisible. The household had heard by ten—news traveled through Marlowe Estate faster than heat through iron—and the change in how people looked at her was total and immediate. The cook wouldn't meet her eye. Renata gave her a wide berth in the corridor and a smile in the drawing room, which was worse. Alistair stood in the study doorway as she passed and said, loudly, to no one, "I always said we should never have hired outside counsel," and Ulyana catalogued it—*deflection, public positioning, building the record*—and kept walking.

Mrs. Adeyemi was the only one who looked at her directly. Found her in the linen room at noon, folding nothing, just standing among the shelves.

"They searched your car," the housekeeper said.

"They did."

"And found the will."

"And found the will."

Mrs. Adeyemi's hands were knotted together in her apron. She was seventy-one years old and had served this house for fifty years, and Ulyana had watched her almost speak twice and swallow it twice, and she understood now that the third time would decide everything—not the investigation, maybe, but the woman.

"He came to me," Mrs. Adeyemi said suddenly, barely above a whisper. "After. After the funeral that wasn't. He stood in my pantry at midnight wearing a beard and a coat I'd packed for him myself, and he gave me an envelope, and he said, *You buried my daughter's truth once, Adeyemi. Bury mine too, and you'll die in a warm bed.*"

Ulyana's breath stopped. "He's alive."

"He was." The housekeeper's eyes were wet and furious. "Six weeks ago. I've said nothing because I've said nothing for fifty years and it's a habit older than my conscience. But that boy—" Her voice broke and reassembled itself. "That boy hangs laundry like his mother used to. And I will not watch them do to him what they did to her."

"Then help me," Ulyana said. "Not with words. With access. Who comes and goes at night? Whose keys open the east wing?"

"No one's keys." Mrs. Adeyemi leaned closer, and the smell of lavender and starch and half a century of silence came off her like a tide. "There's a door in the chapel vestry. Old servants' passage. It's been bricked on the plans since 1987, but bricks are just bricks to a man who owns the mason. He's been coming and going through it since before he died, Ms. Quintana. I've heard him singing. He sings when he thinks he's won."

* * *

At four o'clock, Ulyana's phone buzzed with a text from a number she'd saved under *S.V. — DO NOT CALL FIRST.*

*Evidence log leaked. Will flagged as "suspected forgery pending analysis." Analysis assigned to Dr. Pell, county examiner. Same signature as the autopsy you've been asking about. You have until the analysis comes back — 72 hrs, maybe less. Meet me where we talked last night. Come clean-handed. — V*

Ulyana stared at the screen in the gathering dusk of the linen room, and the cold clarity of the whole design finally assembled itself in her mind, beautiful and terrible, like a cathedral built entirely of traps.

Dr. Pell. The coroner whose signature on Celia Marlowe's autopsy had looked wrong. The man tied to the firm that had sent her the threatening legal notice. Now holding the physical proof of the patriarch's guilt in his gloved hands, with seventy-two hours to declare it fake.

They weren't trying to convict her of murder. That was almost incidental—a bonus, a pressure valve. What they were really doing was buying time. Keeping the second will locked in evidentiary limbo long enough to destroy its credibility, long enough for the old probate to finalize, long enough for a man who'd been dead for three months to walk out of a bricked-up passage one final time and become, legally and forever, a memory.

Unless someone got to the will first. Unless someone proved it genuine. Unless—

Her phone buzzed again.

*Change of plan,* Vance wrote. *Pell just signed the will out of evidence. Personal authorization. Says he's transporting it to his lab tonight. Alone.*

Ulyana closed her eyes. A dead man's will, in a live man's car, driving toward a lab that answered to a ghost.

She picked up her coat—her own coat, bought new that morning, because she would never again trust the ones already hanging in her closet—and went to find the keys to a car nobody had searched yet: Mrs. Adeyemi's ancient blue hatchback, parked behind the kitchen garden, where invisible people kept their vehicles.

Seventy-two hours had just become one night.
