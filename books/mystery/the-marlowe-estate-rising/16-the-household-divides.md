## 16. The Household Divides

### Miles Marlowe

Miles Marlowe had not slept in the east wing in eleven years, and he was not going to start now, not even for a funeral, not even for this.

He stood at the long window of the blue drawing room and watched Ulyana Quintana cross the courtyard below, coat collar up against the drizzle, moving with that unhurried lawyer's stride that made him want to throw something. The woman walked through Marlowe Estate like she owned it. Like she'd always owned it. Like his father's death was a filing matter and the greenhouse corpse was a scheduling conflict.

"She has to go," he said.

Behind him, his cousin Priya didn't look up from her phone. "You've said that four times since breakfast."

"Because it's true four times." He turned. Priya sat curled in the corner of the good sofa—the one their grandmother had slipcovered twice to hide the wine stains—wearing yesterday's sweater and the expression of someone assembling a case file in her head. She'd been doing that all week. Assembling. Cataloguing. Asking the staff questions they were too loyal or too frightened to answer properly.

"Miles." She set the phone down. "A man died wearing her clothes."

"A man died wearing *stolen* clothes. There's a difference, and the difference is that she's either the luckiest frame-job target in history or the best liar I've ever met, and I grew up with my father."

"So let's find out which."

"How?" He heard his own voice crack and hated it. "By letting her dig through Mum's things? By letting her interview the staff like they're suspects? Priya, that woman has been here five days and already Cook won't look at me, and Tam—" He stopped. Saying the groundskeeper's name felt like stepping on ice he wasn't sure would hold.

"Tam's gone," Priya said quietly. "You noticed that too, then."

Of course he'd noticed. The boy mowed the south lawn like clockwork; you could set a watch by the drone of the mower. This morning the mower sat silent under its tarp and the boy's room above the garden shed stood open and empty, bed stripped, kettle still warm. Warm. Which meant he'd left within the hour, which meant he'd heard something in the night, or seen something, or been warned.

"He ran," Miles said. "People don't run from lawyers unless—"

"People run from lots of things." Priya stood, and for a moment she looked so much like Aunt Celia—like the daughter who died—that Miles had to look away. "He ran from something. Or toward something. Either way, throwing Ulyana Quintana out doesn't bring him back, and it doesn't tell us who put a dead man in her scarf."

"It tells us whose side we're on."

"And whose side is that, Miles? Dad's? Dad's dead. Dad's possibly—" She caught herself. He watched her catch herself, watched the sentence die behind her teeth, and understood that his cousin knew something he didn't. Again. Everyone in this house knew something he didn't. He was the eldest son of Marlowe Estate and he was the last person consulted on anything, including, apparently, whether his own father was actually—

"Don't," he said. "Don't finish that sentence."

"I wasn't going to finish it."

"You were."

She held his gaze, and there was no cruelty in it, only a terrible patience. "Then finish it yourself. You're the one who insisted on the sealed casket. You're the one who told the coroner's office we'd handle everything privately. You signed those papers, Miles. So ask yourself why you did that. Ask yourself what you were protecting."

"I was protecting the family."

"From what?"

And Miles Marlowe, forty-six years old, heir to nothing he wanted, discovered he did not have an answer. He had a signature on a cremation authorization he couldn't remember deciding to sign, and a memory of that week that came back to him in fragments—his father's study smelling of cigar smoke two days after the body was found, though the study had been locked; Mrs. Adeyemi crying in the pantry over something that wasn't grief; a phone call from a number he'd never saved, a voice saying *the family appreciates your discretion* in an accent he almost recognized.

"The lawyer stays," he said finally, because it was easier than the truth. "For now. But if she finds out whatever you clearly already know, Priya—if she finds it before I do—we're finished, all of us. You understand that. Whatever this house is after that, it isn't a family."

Priya picked her phone back up. "It stopped being one ten years ago," she said. "The rest of us are just catching up."

### Priya Okafor-Marlowe

Priya had learned surveillance from her mother, who had learned it from surviving three marriages, and the first rule was: people tell you everything if you stop asking questions and start being furniture.

So she was furniture now, folded into the window seat of the morning room with a novel she wasn't reading, while Mrs. Adeyemi dusted shelves that did not need dusting for the third time that day.

"You'll wear the varnish off," Priya said mildly.

Mrs. Adeyemi's cloth paused. Resumed. "Dust comes back, miss. That's its nature."

"That's a very philosophical thing to say about dust."

"I'm a very philosophical woman about dust." But the cloth had stopped again, and the housekeeper was looking at the window—at the gravel drive, at the empty spot where the groundskeeper's bicycle usually leaned—and her face did something complicated and grief-shaped.

"He took the footpath," Priya said. "Didn't he. Not the road. The footpath goes past the old chapel."

Mrs. Adeyemi turned fully then, and Priya saw fear move through her like weather. "Miss, I'd thank you not to—"

"To what? Notice things? Everyone in this house is so busy not noticing things we could wallpaper the place with it." Priya closed the unread book. "Mrs. Adeyemi. Ten years ago my cousin died in that boathouse and everyone said suicide and everyone moved on except nobody moved on, they just went quiet, and now there's a dead stranger in the greenhouse wearing a lawyer's coat and Tam is gone, and you are dusting the same shelf like it owes you money. I'm not asking you to betray anyone. I'm asking you to tell me what you're afraid of, because I promise you I'm already afraid of it too."

For a long moment the only sound was the rain starting again against the glass, soft as fingers testing a lock.

Then Mrs. Adeyemi said, "There was a phone call. Night before last. After the police left."

Priya kept very still. Furniture. Furniture.

"A voice I hadn't heard in ten years," the housekeeper said, "and hoped never to hear again. It said—" Her hand pressed flat against the shelf, steadying itself against the wood. "It said the boy was never supposed to be part of this. It said if I valued him, I'd make sure he stayed a gardener and nothing more. And then it said something else. Something about Miss Celia's music box."

The rain filled the silence.

"What about it?" Priya asked.

"That it should have burned with everything else." Mrs. Adeyemi looked at her then, and her eyes were wet and furious and ancient with loyalty worn down to the wire. "Miss Priya, I have served this family thirty-one years. I buried my husband in their churchyard. I helped raise you and I helped raise that boy and I loved Miss Celia like the daughter I didn't have, and I am telling you—as God is my witness—that whatever killed her is still in this house. It never left. It's just been waiting for someone to give it a reason."

She gathered her cloths and sprays and left the room with the straight-backed dignity of a woman who had just handed over the last of her ammunition and meant to regret it privately.

Priya sat motionless in the window seat, heart hammering, and thought: *the music box.* She'd seen it once, years ago, in Celia's old room—a chipped wooden thing that played four bars of some lullaby before winding down. It had been in the inventory when the family boxed up the dead girl's belongings. It had been in the inventory, and then it had not been anywhere at all.

Her phone buzzed. A number with no name attached, text message, six words:

**STOP DIGGING OR HE DIES.**

She stared at it until the letters swam. Then, hands not quite steady, she screenshotted it, forwarded it to Ulyana Quintana, and typed the only reply the unknown sender would ever get from her:

*Come and try.*

### Tam

Tam had never been good at being invisible, which was ironic, because invisibility had been his whole job description for ten years.

Keep your head down. Mow the lines straight. Call the son *sir* and the housekeeper *ma'am* and never, ever ask why a boy with no surname got a cottage and a wage and a rule that he never entered the main house after dark. He'd broken that rule exactly once, age nine, chasing a cat, and Mrs. Adeyemi had found him in the upstairs corridor outside a door with a faded ribbon on the handle, and she'd gone white as bone and carried him out under her arm like contraband and made him swear on her wedding ring never again.

He'd kept the oath. Until this week.

Now he crouched in the dripping green dark of the old chapel ruin half a mile past the estate wall, soaked through, phone dead in his pocket because he'd pulled the battery—he'd read somewhere you could be tracked, and after what he'd found in the greenhouse he believed anything. His hands wouldn't stop shaking. He kept seeing it: the dead man's face, slack and wrong, and the coat—*her* coat, the lawyer's grey wool coat he'd seen hanging on her shoulders Monday—and pinned inside the collar, half hidden, a photograph. Old, creased, water-stained. A young woman laughing on the boathouse dock.

His mother.

He'd torn it free and run, and he'd been running ever since, and now the rain was coming down hard enough to drown the world and he had nowhere to go, because every direction led back to Marlowe Estate, and Marlowe Estate was the one place on earth he could not return to. Not anymore. Not when whoever killed that man in the greenhouse had known about the photograph, which meant they knew about *him*, which meant the whole careful architecture of his small hidden life—gardener, orphan, nobody—had been built by someone, maintained by someone, and could be demolished by the same someone whenever it became useful.

A twig snapped behind him.

Tam spun, back hitting the cold chapel stone, fists up uselessly, and a torch beam found him and held.

"Easy," said Mrs. Adeyemi's voice, low and fierce. She stepped into the ruined doorway, hood back, seventy years old and breathing hard from the footpath, holding a thermos like a weapon. "Sit down before you fall down, boy."

"You shouldn't be here." His voice cracked on it. "They said—they called you, they *threatened*—"

"They threatened a lot of people over a lot of years and I let them, because I was afraid, and being afraid is how they keep you." She lowered herself onto the fallen pew with a grunt, patted the rotted wood beside her. "Your mother used to sit right there. Did you know that? Before you were born. She'd sneak out here with her guitar and play where her father couldn't hear."

Tam didn't sit. "You knew her."

"I changed her nappies, child. Now sit down and drink this tea, because you can't outrun what's coming and neither can I, and I am done—*done*—letting other people decide what happens to you." She unscrewed the thermos cap, steam rising between them in the cold air, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "But we have to be quick, and we have to be smart, because there's a man in this story who isn't dead, Tam. However impossible it sounds, however mad—I saw him. Three weeks ago. Walking the north ridge at dusk like a ghost with a walking stick. And I took money to forget it, and I'm giving the money back tomorrow, and if you want to live past Sunday you're going to help me give something else back too."

The rain hammered the chapel roof. Somewhere far off, down in the valley, a dog began to bark, and did not stop.

Tam sat down beside her and took the cup.

### Ulyana

Ulyana read Priya's forwarded message twice, standing at the guest-room window with the lights off, watching the dark shape of the estate wall dissolve into rain.

*Stop digging or he dies.*

They had her address. They had her habits. They had, apparently, a boy she'd spoken to exactly three times, and a housekeeper, and a household full of people who had spent a decade perfecting the art of not seeing, and now the art was failing everywhere at once.

Her phone lit again. Vance.

*"Archive fire was arson. Accelerant through the records stacks. Also—your car's been towed to the station impound. Don't ask why over text. Come in tomorrow, 8 a.m., alone."*

Ulyana typed back: *Define alone.*

The reply came fast: *Alone meaning I don't trust my own building. Meet me at the diner on Route 9 instead. 7 a.m. Bring everything.*

She set the phone face-down on the sill and stood a moment longer in the dark, listening to the house around her—the tick of old pipes, a door closing somewhere below, footsteps that might have been anyone's. For fifteen years she had made a profession of other people's houses, reading them the way sailors read weather, and always from the outside, always with an exit mapped. Tonight, for the first time, she caught herself thinking not *how do I get out of here* but *who in this house is awake right now, and are they safe.*

That was new. That was dangerous. That was exactly the lever someone was pulling.

Down the corridor, faintly, she heard the front door open and close—soft, controlled, deliberate—and then Mrs. Adeyemi's tread on the stairs, damp and hurried, and beneath it, almost lost in the rain:

Another set of footsteps. Lighter. Younger. Coming up the service stair that nobody used.

Ulyana turned off her phone screen entirely, crossed the room without a sound, and waited in the dark beside the door as the handle, slowly, began to turn.
