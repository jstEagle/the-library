## 15. The Envelope

Freya didn't open it that night.

She told herself it was strategy — that documents this dangerous deserved daylight, fresh coffee, a clear head. But standing in her kitchen at two in the morning with the envelope flat on the counter under the stove light, she admitted the truth: she was afraid of what Ruth Okafor had to say to her.

The envelope had traveled from Ruth's house in Cedar Falls to Freya's desk in Alderbrook inside a padded mailer, and even now, three days later, it sat where she'd left it, beneath a stack of closing binders, weighted down like something that might otherwise drift away. Heavy cream paper. Ruth's handwriting on the front — *For Freya. When you're ready to lose something.* No stamp, no postmark. Ruth had driven it here herself, Freya realized. Had sat in her car outside the office at some hour, walked it in, and left without a word to anyone. Or had handed it to the night cleaning crew with instructions and a tip generous enough to buy silence. Either way: Ruth had come back to the building she'd sworn never to enter again, and Freya had let the envelope sit under binders for three days while a clock burned down toward Friday.

*Ready to lose something.* She'd spent the week cataloguing what she might lose. Her license, if the tampering charges stuck. Her practice, her name, the careful reputation she'd built one immaculate filing at a time. Possibly her freedom, given the way Sheriff Dunmore looked at her across the courthouse steps yesterday — a long, unhurried look, the look of a man checking the fit of a suit he intended to wear soon.

But there was a loss she hadn't put on the list until now, and it was the one that kept her hand off the envelope. The possibility that Ruth — Ruth, who taught her that a file was a promise made to the future, who could recite probate code the way other people recited psalms — was the one who'd altered the copies. That the person who built Freya's entire professional conscience had also, quietly, over years, bent it out of shape.

You could survive an enemy. Freya had spent two weeks learning how heavy an ally's failure could be.

She made coffee she didn't want. She sat down. She picked up the envelope, and it was lighter than she expected, which somehow made it worse.

She opened it.

* * *

Three things slid out onto the counter.

The first was a will. Four pages, stapled, executed October 9, 2019. *Last Will and Testament of Daniel A. Vane.* Freya knew this document — she'd pulled the archived copy twice already this week, had compared its filing stamp against county records, had found the forgery so clean it took her a full evening to prove it was one. But this version was different in ways she registered before she consciously read a word. The paper stock was heavier. The staple was brass, not steel. And the signature block carried the embossed seal of Okafor & Villalobos — the old firm letterhead, the one they'd retired when Freya took over — pressed deep enough to feel with a fingertip.

This was the original. Not a copy, not a conformed duplicate. The instrument itself, signed by Daniel Vane in blue ink that had faded to gray at the flourishes, witnessed by two names Freya didn't recognize yet, and drafted — the notation in the lower corner said so in Ruth's small, exact hand — *O.O., atty., 10/2/19.*

She read it standing up, coffee going cold beside her.

It was not complicated. Daniel Vane had not been a man of elaborate testamentary imagination. He left the boathouse and its parcel to his brother Adrian — a bone tossed, Freya thought, to keep the peace. He left his voting shares in Vane Holdings to a trust for his daughter. And in Article IV, in language so plain it read almost like an apology, he did the thing that had gotten people hurt:

*To Marisol Quintero, in recognition of services rendered and truths held, I leave the sum of $250,000 and my written assurance, appended hereto as Exhibit B, that she bore no responsibility whatsoever for the events of August 14, 2019.*

Exhibit B was clipped to the back. One page, typed, signed by Daniel Vane. A statement. Not quite a confession — Daniel hadn't had the courage for that, even dead — but close enough. It named the night. It named the boathouse. It said, in Daniel's careful lawyer-proofed phrasing, that Marisol Quintero was present on the night of his brother's death — *the death of councilman Daniel Vane being widely misreported, per my instruction, as accidental drowning, the decedent referenced herein being myself, the survivor being my brother Adrian* — and that whatever she saw, she saw because Adrian wanted her to see it, and that she should not be punished for it.

Freya read it twice. The contortions of it — a man writing his own death notice into his own will, describing himself as both decedent and author — made her chest ache. Daniel Vane had known he was going to die. Had known how, or at least feared how, and had done the only thing a man like him knew how to do: he'd paid someone to remember the truth, and hidden the payment in paperwork nobody would ever be allowed to read.

Except someone had read it. Someone had made sure no court ever would.

The second item was the affidavit. Two pages, notarized in Cedar Falls, dated eleven months ago. Ruth Okafor's signature at the bottom, steady as a metronome.

*I, Ruth A. Okafor, being duly sworn, depose and state:* Freya read the numbered paragraphs slowly, the way Ruth had taught her to read anything that mattered — once for content, once for structure, once for what wasn't said.

Paragraph one: Ruth drafted the will of Daniel A. Vane in October 2019, including Exhibit B, at his specific instruction.

Paragraph two: On November 3, 2019 — six weeks after Daniel's death, three weeks after the sheriff's report closed the case as accidental drowning — Marisol Quintero came to Ruth's office unannounced, terrified, carrying a copy of the will she said Adrian Vane's attorney had shown her as a threat. *He told me,* the quoted statement ran, *that if I ever spoke about the boathouse, the will would disappear and I would be nothing but a liar with no proof. Then he smiled and said the will was already disappearing.*

Paragraph three: Ruth checked the probate file. The original will — the one in Freya's hands — had been removed from the records vault. In its place stood a forged copy, altered to remove Article IV and Exhibit B entirely, bearing a filing stamp that did not match county equipment. Ruth had gone to the county clerk, her friend of twenty years, and been told, gently, apologetically, behind a half-closed door, that the vault log showed no discrepancy and perhaps Mrs. Okafor should let sleeping dogs lie.

Paragraph four: Ruth made a decision. She could not restore the original without exposing Marisol to exactly the danger Adrian had promised. So instead she did something she described, in her own affidavit voice, as *an act I am not proud of and do not ask to be forgiven for.* She altered the copies herself. Eleven files over three years — every record touching the Vane name, every document that might lead an investigator from the forged filing stamp back to the missing original — she edited, re-dated, re-stamped. She used Freya's login credentials, which she still possessed from the transition year, because a trail pointing at a retired woman in Cedar Falls would bring men like Adrian Vane to Cedar Falls, and a trail pointing at her former partner would bring them somewhere Ruth could watch them arrive.

*I chose her,* paragraph seven said simply, *because she is stronger than I am, and because I believed — correctly, I now fear — that she would eventually find the alterations and follow them to the truth. This was cowardice dressed as strategy. I offer it as evidence anyway.*

Freya set the affidavit down. Her hands were not quite steady.

Eleven files. Three years. Her own login stamps, her own name threaded through a falsified paper trail like dye through water. For two weeks she had assumed an enemy inside her firm — a mole, a hacker, Camille Vane's long arm reaching into her cabinets. She had checked her staff's access logs. She had changed her passwords twice. She had lain awake composing resignation letters she hoped she'd never need.

And all of it was Ruth. All of it was the person she loved most in the professional world, sitting in a Cedar Falls kitchen, deliberately building a case against her — against *Freya* — because it was the only shelter she could think of.

*Ready to lose something.* There it was.

* * *

The third item was a note. One page, handwritten, undated. Ruth's handwriting had always been a contradiction — a woman of ferocious precision whose letters leaned like they were walking into wind.

*Freya.*

*If you're reading this, you found the grave, or you found Marisol, or you found me, and in any of those cases you are past the point where I can protect you by keeping quiet, which was always the fiction I was selling myself.*

*I altered your files. I used your credentials. I made you look guilty because I couldn't bear to make myself look findable. I have rehearsed a dozen justifications for this and none of them survive contact with your face, so I won't offer them. Here is what is true:*

*Daniel Vane was murdered by his brother. Marisol Quintero saw it. The sheriff's office buried it, the county clerk helped bury it, and Camille Vane — who I believe orchestrated more than any of us can prove — paid for the burial in ways I have only begun to map. I had the one document that could undo it, and I hid it, first to save Marisol, and then, if I'm honest, to save myself, because by the second year I understood that returning it would cost me everything and I discovered I valued my quiet life more than I valued the truth. That discovery was the worst thing I have ever learned about myself, and I have been a lawyer for forty-one years.*

*You will want to hate me. Do it quickly and then stop, because hating me costs you days you don't have. The will is valid. The affidavit is notarized and admissible. File them together, publicly, loudly, somewhere Adrian Vane cannot reach the record before the world reads it. Probate is slow everywhere except when it matters — then it is lightning, if you insist on it.*

*I lost my nerve in 2019 and I have been losing it incrementally ever since. You haven't lost yours yet. That is the whole difference between us, and it is not a difference in character, only in timing. Be kinder to yourself about this than I have been.*

*Ruth.*

*P.S. — The camera at the boathouse feeds to a server in Reno. I know because I put it there in June, before I lost my nerve a third time. If you've found it, use it. If you haven't, it's mounted in the northeast joist above the fuel shelf.*

Freya read the postscript three times.

Ruth had installed the camera. The camera Freya had found Tuesday night, still recording, still streaming — the one she'd assumed was Adrian's preparation for Friday's murder, the stage-lighting for a killing. Ruth had put it there in June. Which meant either Ruth had anticipated the murder five months early — had known, somehow, what the letters claimed to predict — or the camera had been repurposed, its feed hijacked, its unblinking eye turned from watchdog to witness.

Or both. Ruth watching the boathouse since June, and someone else learning about the camera and leaving it running because a recording device is useful to murderers too.

Freya thought about the cleaned patch of floor. The fresh rope coiled on the workbench. The way the boathouse had felt like a theater between performances. A stage doesn't care who's watching. It only requires an audience to exist at all.

She sat down properly for the first time in hours, at the kitchen table, in the chair with the wobbly leg she'd never fixed, and she let herself feel it — all of it, in sequence, the way grief insists on being felt. The betrayal first, hot and specific: *she used my name, she built her wall out of my career.* Then the fear underneath it: *and it worked, and I would have done the same, and we both know it.* Then, arriving last and staying longest, something that wasn't forgiveness yet but was at least its cousin: pity, and love, and the terrible recognition that Ruth Okafor, the most incorruptible person Freya had ever known, had been corrupted not by money or ambition but by exhaustion — by four years of being the only person holding a grenade and knowing that setting it down would kill someone.

Even allies bend. That was the lesson of the week, of the year, maybe of the profession. Power didn't need to break people. It just needed to wait until they were tired.

Freya wiped her face with the heel of her hand, got a fresh cup of coffee, and went to work.

* * *

By four a.m. the kitchen table had become a war room. The original will lay flat under the good lamp. The affidavit sat squared beside it. Ruth's note she kept separate, reading it again each time she assembled another piece of the picture, because it contained operational intelligence disguised as confession.

The camera feed. She opened her laptop and pulled up the stream she'd cloned Tuesday night. Still live. Timestamp ticking. The boathouse interior, dim and patient, the rope still coiled, the cleaned patch of floor still clean. Nobody had been back. But the feed existed, and it ran through Reno, and Ruth had set it up — which meant Ruth controlled the account, or had, in June. Freya composed an email to her, deleted it, and called instead. Voicemail. She tried the landline. Voicemail again, Ruth's recorded voice crisp and unhurried: *You've reached Ruth Okafor. Leave a message after the tone, and if this is about a deadline, the answer is no.*

"Ruth," Freya said, "it's Freya. I opened it. I'm not ready to forgive you, but I'm ready to use you. Call me."

She hung up and looked at the will again. Article IV. Exhibit B. A quarter million dollars and a written assurance, sitting in a kitchen drawer for four years while a woman named Marisol Quintero lived under a stranger's name two counties away, waiting for a lie to finish becoming history.

File them publicly. Probate is lightning, if you insist on it.

There was exactly one venue in Alderbrook where insisting would mean something — where two hundred of the town's movers, holders, and beneficiaries would be gathered in one room on Friday night, cameras present, press invited, silence impossible. The Vane estate. The gala. Camille's annual autumn party, the social event that doubled as the family's annual audit of who owed whom.

Serving a will at a gala. Freya almost laughed. It was the kind of move Ruth would have called theatrical and then, privately, admired.

But there was a problem, and the problem had a name, and the name was Elias Thorne. Her client. The nervous stranger who'd paid double, who'd watched the Vane estate from a parked car, who'd received letters predicting a murder scheduled for Friday night at the boathouse. Everything she now held — the will, the affidavit, the plan crystallizing in her head — pointed at the same conclusion Elias had hired her to prevent. If she filed publicly, she forced the issue into the open. If the letters were real, forcing the issue might be exactly what saved the target's life. Murder needs darkness; probate is floodlight.

Or — and the thought arrived cold and unwelcome — forcing the issue might be what triggered it. A cornered man with a plan and a schedule doesn't abandon the schedule. He moves it up.

She needed to talk to Elias. Tonight, if possible. Now, ideally. She needed to tell him what she'd found and watch his face when she did, because there were things about Elias Thorne that had never quite added up — the cash retainer, the detailed knowledge of a crime that hadn't happened, the way he watched the Vane estate not like a private investigator but like a man looking for someone specific in the windows.

Her phone lit up on the table.

Not Ruth. Not Elias. An unknown local number, calling at ten minutes past four in the morning, which in Freya's experience was either an emergency or a trap, and lately in Alderbrook those had stopped being different categories.

She answered. "Villalobos."

A pause. Breathing. Then a voice she recognized from two counties over, pitched low and fast, frayed at the edges.

"Ms. Villalobos. It's Marisol." A sound that might have been a laugh or its opposite. "Someone just slipped a note under my door with my real name on it. And below the name, it says: *Friday. Boathouse. Bring the testimony or we bring you.*"

Outside, the sky over Alderbrook was still dark, but somewhere past the ridge, Friday was already on its way.
