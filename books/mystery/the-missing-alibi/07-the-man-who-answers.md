## 7. The Man Who Answers

The dispatch board at 3 a.m. was a constellation of small lights, each one a car moving through the dark like a slow pulse. I had watched it for eleven years. I knew its rhythms the way a sailor knows weather — which drivers idled at the all-night diner on Corliss, which ones took the long way around the harbor to stretch their fares, which lights flickered when they should have held steady.

And I knew, now, which light didn't belong.

The phantom calls had started four months ago. I'd found the first one by accident, auditing my own log for a fare dispute: a call placed at 2:47 a.m. on a Tuesday, routed through the emergency intake line, logged under my operator ID. I hadn't made it. I'd been sitting right there, ten feet from the console, drinking bad coffee and listening to Denny Ruiz complain about a flat. The call had come from somewhere inside my voice-print window — the ninety-second gap the system assigned to my authentication token — and it had lasted eleven seconds. Long enough to say an address. Long enough to say *Greywater Point*.

I hadn't understood it then. I understood it now, sitting in the dark with the board glowing and a legal pad full of dates, because the calls had a shape. They always came on nights when the fog rolled in off the water and the drivers got chatty and the board went quiet. They always came from the same cluster of prefixes — the payphones along the coastal route, the ones the city kept promising to tear out and never did. And they always, always fell within my window.

Someone with system access. Someone who knew the dispatch software well enough to borrow my token. Someone who had been standing at a payphone on the coast road, watching the fog, dialing into my nights for four months.

Not to report anything. The calls never generated a dispatch. The system logged the intake and then the line went dead — eleven seconds of open air, like someone holding a phone up to the sea.

Like someone listening.

I pulled the prefix records and laid them out on the pad. The Kettleman Road payphone. The one outside the shuttered bait shop at Miller's Landing. The rusted unit at the pull-off above Greywater Point itself. Three phones, strung along nine miles of coast road like beads on a wire, and the calls rotated between them in a pattern I could almost hear — Kettleman, Miller's, the Point, Kettleman again, like a man walking a circuit in the dark. Like a man keeping watch.

I marked the timestamps against the tide tables I'd started keeping since the woman in my cardigan. Every call had come within an hour of low tide.

He wasn't just watching. He was keeping an appointment with the water.

I sat with that for a long time, the heater ticking, the board breathing its small lights. Then I did the thing I'd been circling for two days the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

I picked up the headset and dialed the Kettleman Road number.

* * *

It rang four times. On the fifth, someone lifted the receiver, and the line filled with the sound of the coast — wind, the far-off hiss of water, the hollow acoustic of a phone booth with its glass long since kicked out.

I said nothing. Neither did he.

The silence had a texture. Most wrong numbers hang up. Most prank callers breathe heavy, or giggle, or wait three seconds and test you with a word. This silence was patient. It had weight and intention. It was the silence of a man who had dialed a thousand numbers in his head and finally heard one ring true.

"You're up late," I said.

The wind moved through the receiver. Then, quietly, a voice — low, unhurried, with a faint roughness at the edges, like a recording played too many times. "So are you."

My hand was steady on the headset. My heart was not. "This is a city line. That's a payphone on Kettleman Road. You've called into it — or near it — forty-one times in four months. Always in my voice-print window. Always near low tide. You want to tell me why?"

Another silence, but a different one now — pleased, almost. I could hear him settling, the creak of the booth's frame or his own shoulders. "Forty-three," he said. "You missed two. The ones from the pull-off. You always miss the ones closest to you."

"You've been listening to my shift."

"Every night you work." No shame in it. No menace, either, and that was worse. He said it the way you'd say *I read your letters*, with the tenderness of long habit. "You talk to the drivers when you think the channel's closed. You tell Denny his daughter's recital is Thursday because you remembered it from a fare he mentioned in March. You tell Ruiz to take Harbor Road home because the bridge lights bother his eyes and he's too proud to say so. You notice things, Ivo. You've always noticed things."

My mouth had gone dry. "Ivo," I repeated. "I never gave you my name."

The wind again. And then, softly, almost gently, he began to quote.

"*I watch you sleep some mornings and I think: he has my father's stubbornness and my mother's habit of listening at doors. He will hear everything and say nothing, and it will keep him alive and cost him everything.*"

The letters. My mother's letters — the ones I'd found folded in the writing desk with their unsent stamps and their browned edges, the ones I'd read four times and then locked in my glovebox because I couldn't bear to have them in the house. He was quoting them from memory. Word for word.

"Those are hers," I said. My voice came out flat, which was a miracle. "You've read them."

"I've *had* them." A pause. "Some of them. She wrote more than you found. She wrote for twenty years. Did you know that? A letter every year on my birthday, sealed and never sent, stacked in that desk like sediment. I used to think that was love. Now I think it was bookkeeping. She was keeping a record of a debt she never intended to pay."

"Silas."

The name landed between us and neither of us moved. Down the line I heard him exhale — a long, controlled breath, the sound of a man hearing his own name spoken aloud for the first time in a very long while.

"You found the letters," he said. "The fourth one. The one about the flats."

"She named a meeting. June fourteenth, 1979, the tide flats below the house. She never went."

"No." The word cracked, just slightly, the first hairline fracture in all that patience. "She didn't. I waited. I was seventeen. I stood out there until the water came up to my knees and my legs went numb, and then I stood there another hour because I kept thinking — she's coming. She wrote it down. People don't write things down unless they mean them." He laughed, a small dry sound with nothing in it. "You'd have gone. That's the difference. You'd have gone."

"How do you know what I'd have done?"

"Because I know you." And here his voice changed — warmed, the way a room warms when someone lights a lamp in it. "You sleep with the radio on low so the house doesn't feel empty, but you turn it off before you're fully asleep because silence means the night is honest. You re-lock the door twice and check the stove with your palm, not your eyes. You keep your mother's letters in the glovebox of the car because you can't stand them in the house but you can't stand to be far from them either. You've read them four times. You cry on the third reading, every time, at the same line — the one where she says she chose the estate over the child because the estate was the only thing that was ever *hers*, and she hated herself so much she couldn't even write the word *sorry*."

The heater ticked. The board glowed. I could not speak.

"I've listened to your nights for four months," Silas said. "I know your voice the way you know the tide tables. And I need you to understand something, Ivo, because it matters for what comes next. I never once thought about hurting you. Not once. You're the only person in that house's whole rotten ledger I never put on a list."

"Then what do you want?"

"What she owed." Quiet. Absolute. "What the trustees strangled out of her, what the lawyers buried in probate, what they paid for with three men who signed their names to a lie and one woman who let them. I want the family to answer for what it did. All of it. Every name on every page." A pause, and when he spoke again there was something almost like apology in it. "Including yours."

"I haven't done anything."

"You have her name. You have the house. You have the letters she wrote *you* — don't look so surprised, I know she wrote to you too, I've read the carbon in the trustee's file. You got the version with love in it. I got the version with a date and a tide table and no one showing up." The wind rose, and for a moment his voice was all weather. "You're the last name on the list, Ivo. I've been trying to find a way to make that not be true for four months. That's why I called. That's why I listened. I was looking for a reason."

"Did you find one?"

The silence stretched so long I checked the connection light. Then, low: "Ask your detective about the trustees. Ask her who's still alive. Ask her before the next low tide." A breath. "Because after that, I stop looking."

The line went dead — not hung up, just gone, the way the eleven-second calls always went, like the sea taking back what it lent.

I sat in the dark with the headset in my hand and my mother's words still ringing in my skull, and I understood, with a horror that had nothing to do with fear, that the man who knew me best in this world was the one who intended to erase me — and that I had less than two days to find out what the last living trustee knew before the tide came in to collect.

I picked up the phone again and dialed Maren Kolvek's number, and for once I didn't care what she suspected.

She was going to have to drive fast.
