## 5. Heirs and Apparents

There is a particular kind of smile that rich people wear when they are being investigated, and I have spent enough of my life on the receiving end of it to know it like an old scar. It isn't warmth. It's a wall painted to look like a door. You knock, they open, and you find yourself standing in a hallway that goes nowhere — beautifully lit, carpeted in something expensive, going absolutely nowhere.

I spent Wednesday walking that hallway three times.

The exhumation had been Charles Halloway's idea. That much was public record: he'd petitioned the court for the reinterment, paid for the new granite, hired the caterers for the memorial. A grandson honoring his grandfather's memory with a proper ceremony. The town thought it was touching. I thought it was the single strangest thing a man could do — spend money digging up a grave he had every reason to believe was occupied.

Unless he knew it wasn't.

* * *

Charles received me at eleven in the morning at Halloway House, the big gray Victorian on Founders' Hill that the town postcards never quite capture — they photograph it from below, so you can't see how the paint peels at the north face, or how the gutters sag like tired eyelids. Wealth, I've learned, is mostly maintenance deferred.

He met me on the porch himself, which surprised me. I'd expected a housekeeper and a phone call. Instead there he was: mid-forties, silver coming in early at the temples like his barber was charging him extra for dignity, wearing a sweater that probably cost more than my monthly rent and looked like he'd bought it to apologize for the rest of the outfit.

"Mr. Amberdale." He shook my hand with both of his, the way politicians do, or men who want something. "Iris Vale said you might come by. She speaks very highly of you."

"Iris Vale should learn to speak less highly of people she's known for less than a week."

That got a laugh out of him, quick and genuine, and I noted it down in the mental ledger I keep — genuine laugh, first data point, possibly a man without guile, possibly a man who has practiced. "Can I offer you coffee? It's terrible, but it's hot."

"Terrible and hot describes most of my mornings. Yes."

We sat in a sunroom that had been a sunroom since before sunrooms were ironic, surrounded by furniture that had been sat on by four generations of Halloways and bore the upholstery stains to prove it. The coffee was, as promised, terrible. Charles drank his like a man doing penance.

"You funded the exhumation," I said, after the appropriate amount of small talk about the weather, which in our town means complaining about it. "May I ask why?"

"Because it was time." He set the cup down. "Grandfather founded this town. He gave it the library, the hospital wing, the school auditorium with his name carved over the door. And for thirty years he's been lying under a flat stone nobody visits while the family argues about who's supposed to maintain the plot. Margaret wouldn't return calls. Vane billed us for 'estate administration' that seemed to consist of sending letters to each other. I thought — someone should do this properly. Someone should put him back with honor."

"It's a lovely sentiment."

"But?" He heard it. Good. Most heirs don't hear the *but*. They're raised in houses where the *but* gets swallowed by the help.

"But you spent a considerable sum to move a body you'd never verified was there. Most families visit the grave first. Lay flowers. Say a few words to the headstone. You went straight to the lawyers and the permits."

Charles looked into his coffee for a long moment. When he looked up, his eyes were steady, and I'll give him this — whatever else he was, he wasn't a coward.

"My father died believing his father was in that ground," he said quietly. "My mother died believing it too. I'm the last one left, Mr. Amberdale. If there was ever a chance — even one percent — that something was wrong down there, I wanted to be the one holding the shovel when we found out. Not because I wanted it to be wrong. Because if it *was*, and I found out later, after another thirty years of flowers and lies—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Because I couldn't have lived with it. That's all."

It was a good answer. It was such a good answer that it made me more suspicious, not less. In my experience, the truth tends to come out of people crooked, halting, embarrassed. The polished answers belong to the rehearsed ones.

"And your alibi," I said, because subtlety has never been my strong suit, "for the night someone emptied that coffin. Tomas Reed remembers rain. Late October, thirty years back — no. Forgive me. The recent night. The Tuesday."

Something flickered across his face. Not guilt. Something more like exhaustion, the look of a man who has been asked the same question by Brandt's officers twice already and finds the third asking tedious rather than frightening.

"I was at the Hartwell benefit until midnight. Two hundred witnesses, an open bar, and a speech I gave about wetland conservation that at least six people filmed on their phones. Then I drove home alone, which I understand is inconvenient, and slept. My car's transponder logged me through the toll gate at 12:41 a.m." He spread his hands. "You're welcome to check all of it."

"I will."

"I'd be disappointed if you didn't."

* * *

Margaret Halloway did not receive me at all.

She received me on her front step, in a cardigan the color of wet bark, with the door half-closed behind her like a woman who intended to keep her escape route open. Her house was in the older part of town, small and stubborn, a garden gone slightly wild in the way gardens go when the person tending them has decided the world can wait.

"No," she said, before I'd finished saying hello.

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were going to ask about Edmund. Everyone asks about Edmund now. Thirty years of silence and suddenly the whole town has discovered an interest in my uncle." She crossed her arms. "Ask anyway. I enjoy watching people waste their time."

"The exhumation. Did you support it?"

"I found out about it from a notice in the *Gazette*. Like everyone else." Her mouth did something bitter. "Charles didn't call. Vane didn't call. I read about my own uncle's second funeral in the paper between the produce ads and the church listings. So no, Detective—"

"Retired."

"—*Detective*, I didn't support it. I would have opposed it, if opposing things in this family accomplished anything, which it doesn't."

"Thirty years of estrangement is a long time," I said. "What was the quarrel?"

For a moment I thought she'd close the door. Her hand actually moved toward it. But then something in her — some old stubbornness, the same grain that kept the garden wild and the house unpainted — held her where she stood.

"The quarrel was money," she said. "It's always money. My father — Edmund's brother — believed the estate owed him. Believed Edmund had promised him a share of something and then died conveniently before signing anything. He fought Vane for years. Letters, filings, one memorable incident involving a process server and a fishing boat. It ate him alive. By the end he wasn't fighting for the money anymore. He was fighting because stopping would mean Edmund had won even dead." She looked past me, down the street, at nothing. "He died still fighting. I inherited the fight and I threw it in the river. Best decision I ever made."

"And yet you stayed in town."

"Where else would I go? This town is a coat I can't take off." Her eyes came back to mine, sharp and sudden. "You think one of us took him. Out of the coffin. Don't you. That's why you're making the rounds."

"I think somebody knows something."

"Somebody always knows something. That's not knowledge, that's just weather." She stepped back, hand on the door. "Here's what I know, since you came all this way. Charles is a decent man playing at purpose because he's never needed one before. Vane is a snake who has worn the same suit since the Nixon administration. And I am a bitter old woman who reads too much and trusts no one, least of all family." The door began to swing. "If you find out who moved my uncle, tell him he should have visited me while he was up and about. I'd have made tea."

The door closed. I stood on the step a moment, listening to the garden, and thought: *that's the only person in this family who hasn't smiled at me once.*

Which either made her innocent, or made her the best liar of the lot.

* * *

Prescott Vane received me in an office that smelled of leather and old decisions.

The law firm of Vane & Associates occupied the top floor of the Merchants' Building downtown, and Vane occupied the corner office the way a spider occupies the center of a web — not ostentatiously, but with the absolute confidence of something that had built the web itself and knew every strand. He was older than I expected, seventy at least, with a face like a folded map and hands that lay perfectly still on the desk blotter, fingers laced, as though they had long ago agreed never to gesture again.

"Mr. Amberdale." His voice was dry and unhurried, a voice that billed by the quarter hour even in conversation. "I wondered when you'd come. Sit, please."

"You wondered."

"I make it a practice to wonder about everything. It's cheaper than surprise." He unfolded his hands just far enough to indicate the chair. "You're consulting for Coroner Vale. Off the record, I understand. Which means everything you learn is worth exactly nothing in a courtroom, and everything I say to you is equally worthless. A refreshing arrangement. Please, ask me anything."

Most people, when they grant you permission like that, are bluffing. I decided to test the bluff.

"The estate. Who controls it?"

"I do. Have for thirty-one years. Edmund named me executor two weeks before his death — a detail I mention because conspiracy-minded individuals sometimes find it sinister, when in fact it was simple prudence. He trusted me. I have endeavored to deserve it."

"What does the estate consist of?"

"Property holdings, a portfolio managed conservatively, the usual philanthropic remainder interests. The library trust. The hospital wing endowment. Modest annuities to various family members, including Margaret, who cashes hers and burns the accompanying letter, I assume, given her correspondence style." A faint crease appeared around his eyes, which I took to be his version of laughter. "And the bookshop lease. Yours, I believe."

"My shop?"

"Amberdale's Books sits on land owned by the Halloway estate. Your lease predates my tenure — signed by Edmund himself, I believe, shortly before he died. Curious man, Edmund. Full of last-minute arrangements." He watched me with mild, patient eyes. "Does that trouble you, Mr. Amberdale?"

"A little. I dislike learning I've been living inside someone else's plan."

"We all live inside someone else's plan. The fortunate ones get to choose whose." He refolded his hands. "Now. You came here to ask whether I emptied that coffin, so let's dispense with the scenic route. On the night in question I was at home with my wife. She has advanced rheumatoid arthritis, sleeps poorly, and requires assistance several times nightly. I was awake with her, off and on, until dawn. My household staff will confirm the general shape of it, though not the hours — I dismissed them years ago except for a cleaner who comes Tuesdays. So my alibi is my wife's suffering, which I offer without pride and with complete accuracy."

It was the first alibi of the day that hadn't been engineered to impress. It was also, I noticed, completely unverifiable by anyone but a sleeping woman and a man with every motive to lie.

"Why does the estate keep paying for the mausoleum's upkeep?" I asked. "Halloway's buried in the ground. The crypt above has stood empty for thirty years."

Vane regarded me for a long moment. Outside his window, the town went about its business, unaware it was being surveyed from above like a model village.

"Cemeteries are full of structures maintained for reasons no one remembers," he said finally. "Sentiment. Obligation. Habit. Take your pick." He rose, signaling the audience was over, and walked me to the door with the unhurried gait of a man who had never once hurried anywhere in his life. At the threshold he paused.

"One piece of advice, gratis. You strike me as a man who reads closely. Read the bequest documents sometime — the ones from 1974. Edmund's generosity was extensive that year. Almost extravagant. Men are rarely generous without reason, and rarely that generous without a deadline." He opened the door. "Good afternoon, Mr. Amberdale. Do give my regards to Miss Vale. And to your bookshop. I hear the rent is very reasonable."

* * *

I walked back across town in the late afternoon light, doing what I always do after a day of interviews: laying the pieces out on the table of my skull and looking for the shape they refuse to make.

Three heirs. Three smiles — well, two smiles and Margaret's closed door, which is its own kind of smile, the kind a fortress makes.

Charles, who spent money to open a grave and answered every question like a man reciting from a script he'd written himself and almost believed. Genuine grief, or genuine performance; the two sound identical over terrible coffee.

Margaret, who wanted nothing, claimed nothing, and hated everyone equally — the safest position on any chessboard, and therefore the one I trusted least. People who want nothing are usually lying about wanting nothing.

And Vane, who controlled everything, admitted to nothing, and had sent me out the door with a gift wrapped in a warning: *read the 1974 bequest documents.* Either it was a slip — the old spider losing a thread — or it was bait, laid precisely where a retired detective who owned a bookshop couldn't resist putting his hands on it.

Every piece smiling. Every piece with an alibi polished smooth as river stone. And somewhere beneath the board, a hole in the ground the exact size and shape of Edmund Halloway, and a newspaper dated 1974 folded where his heart should have been.

I reached the shop as the streetlights came on. Through the glass I could see the evening settling over the shelves, the dust motes turning gold, all those thousands of spines standing quiet in their rows — including, somewhere in the local history section, the donation catalogues from the Halloway bequest, shelved and forgotten for fifty years.

Vane had told me to read them.

Which meant either he was helping me —

or he already knew what I'd find, and wanted to watch me find it.

I unlocked the door and went in.
