## 8. The Year That Wasn't

The county records office smelled of dust, toner, and the particular despair of institutions that have outlived their purpose. I'd spent thirty years in rooms like it — the municipal annex, the evidence lockup, the basement of the Hall of Records where the town kept everything it didn't want to look at. The architecture of forgetting, I used to call it. Every town builds one. Ours just had better shelving.

I signed the visitor log with a name that was almost mine and took the stairs to the second floor, where vital records lived in gray boxes arranged by a filing logic that had died sometime during the Ford administration. The clerk — a young man with the complexion of someone who'd never been outdoors voluntarily — barely glanced up from his phone.

"Vital records," I said. "Deaths. 1974."

"Family member?"

"Researcher."

"Genealogy's on Thursdays."

"It's not genealogy. It's history."

He looked at me the way young men look at old men who still believe in distinctions. Then he waved a hand at the shelves. "Boxes are alphabetical by surname. You want H. Bring a pencil. No pens."

I brought a pencil. I'd been bringing pencils to rooms like this since before he was born, back when the rule was no pens because pens could be altered and pencils could not — a rule that assumed the past was worth protecting from revision. There was a time I'd have found that touching. Now I just found it accurate.

The Halloway box was thicker than I expected. Founders accumulate paper the way ships accumulate barnacles — charters, land grants, the endless ceremonial correspondence of a man who liked seeing his own name in print. Edmund Halloway had liked it very much. Half the box was proclamations and plaques, the paper equivalent of the bronze statue that stood in the square with pigeons colonizing his shoulders.

I found the death certificate in a folder near the bottom, filed under a date I already knew by heart: October 14, 1974. The day the town's benefactor died, and the day — I now suspected — that the town's benefactor did nothing of the kind.

I'd come for confirmation, not revelation. That's the honest way to put it, and I've found that honesty with myself is a discipline I have to practice daily, like stretching, or it atrophies. I'd spent the night before with the ledger spread across my kitchen table, the 1974 catalogue's coded columns transcribed onto legal pads, and by two in the morning I'd stopped decoding and started staring. Because the payments didn't stop in 1974. They *changed*. Before October, the entries were disbursements — Halloway paying out, generously, lavishly, the philanthropy that had bought him his statue. After October, the same accounts, the same handwriting, but the direction reversed. Money flowing *in*. Quietly. In amounts calibrated to be invisible.

A dead man's accounts don't receive deposits. A dead man's accounts don't do anything. Which meant either someone was forging entries in a dead man's books for decades — possible, but it would require a forger with the patience of a monk and access to a bookshop's oldest inventory — or the dead man had never been dead at all.

So: confirmation. I wanted the certificate to tell me something was wrong. I wanted the paper to corroborate the arithmetic. I wanted the mystery to stay the shape it had been, a shape I understood — a body missing from a grave, a ledger hidden in plain sight, heirs who wanted evidence destroyed. A mystery with edges.

The certificate did not cooperate.

It was, on its face, unremarkable. Edmund A. Halloway, age sixty-one, cause of death listed as *myocardial infarction*, attending physician one Dr. Aldous Wren. The signature was a quick, confident slash — the signature of a man who had signed many certificates and expected to sign many more. There was a secondary signature, the county registrar's, and the embossed seal that made it all official, that made death itself a matter of paperwork.

I sat with it for a long time. The fluorescent light buzzed overhead with the persistence of an insect. Somewhere below, the clerk's phone played something with a heavy bass line, muffled, like a heartbeat through a wall.

Then I saw it. Not on the certificate — on the *date*. October 14, 1974, was a Monday.

I know that because I checked it twice, first in the box itself — there was a newspaper in the folder, folded to the obituary page, the same edition as the one folded in the coffin, which was either a remarkable coincidence or a signature, someone's habit of leaving receipts — and then again on my own calendar, counting back through the decades on my fingers like a schoolboy. Monday, October 14, 1974.

And the certificate was *filed* — the registrar's stamp said so — on October 15. The day after.

Here's the thing about death certificates that thirty years of investigation had burned into me: they are slow. A man dies on a Saturday, the doctor is called, the family grieves, the paperwork takes days, sometimes a week. A death on a Monday, filed Tuesday, certified and sealed — that was not grief. That was *schedule*. That was a man who died on a timetable, or appeared to.

I photocopied the certificate with the copier in the corner, feeding it quarters like a slot machine that paid out in dread. Then I went looking for Dr. Aldous Wren.

* * *

Finding a doctor from 1974 in a county records office is a matter of following the paper trail of his leaving, because everyone leaves a trail, even the ones who leave quietly. Especially the ones who leave quietly. In my experience the loudest departures — the lawsuits, the scandals, the dramatic resignations — are almost always honest. It's the silent exits you have to worry about. People who vanish from a town without selling their house, without forwarding addresses, without a farewell party: those are the ones who were either running from something or running toward something, and either way they were running *from here*.

Wren's trail was in the medical licensing records, which the county kept in a set of ledgers even older than the vital records — actual bound volumes, spines cracked, pages the color of weak tea. I found him easily. Aldous Wren, M.D., licensed to practice in the county in 1958. And then, in the volume for 1974, a single line that stopped my breath:

*Wren, Aldous — license surrendered, November 1974. Reason: relocation out of state.*

Surrendered. Not lapsed, not expired, not revoked. Surrendered — which is what a doctor does when he never intends to practice again, or when he intends to practice somewhere he'd rather not be traced to. And the date. November 1974. One month after Edmund Halloway died. One month after Wren signed the certificate that put a dead man — or a living one — into the ground.

A week, I'd told myself on the drive over. I'd built the whole morning on a hunch that the doctor had left within a week of the death, and the records had given me a month, and somehow the month was worse. A week could be grief. A week could be coincidence, could be a doctor following a wife to a new posting, could be anything human. But a month — a month was a *settlement*. A month was the time it takes to arrange things, to move money, to establish that the departure was unremarkable, to let the town's attention slide off you like rain off wax. Wren hadn't fled. Wren had been *paid to exit*, cleanly and on schedule, the way you pay a contractor.

I stood in the aisle between the ledger shelves and felt the floor of the mystery tilt beneath me.

Because here's what I had, up to that morning: a stolen fortune, a faked exhumation, heirs destroying evidence, a ledger in code. A crime with a body count of zero and a villain I could imagine — Vane, probably, or one of the family, someone greedy and present. That mystery had a shape. It had edges. You could hold it.

But Wren's signature on a certificate filed with suspicious speed, Wren's license surrendered a month later like a man cashing his final check — that didn't fit the shape. That fit a different shape entirely. That fit a story where the death itself was the crime, where the empty coffin wasn't a recent desecration but a *thirty-year-old vacancy*, where the heirs hadn't destroyed evidence of a theft but evidence of a *fraud* — and the fraud wasn't financial.

The fraud was the funeral.

I walked out of the records office into October sunlight and had to stand on the steps for a while, one hand on the rail, because the vertigo was genuine. Not the metaphorical kind. The physical kind, the kind where the world's geometry stops agreeing with your inner ear. The town spread out below the steps — the square, the statue, the library with Halloway's name carved over the door, the hospital wing, the school auditorium, the park with its bandstand. Every building I could see had been touched by Edmund Halloway's generosity. And if the generosity was a laundry, and the death was a fiction, then the whole town — the whole beloved, gossiped-about, statue-and-bunting town — was built on top of a lie with a marble lid.

I thought of Iris. I thought of what she'd said in my shop, over the cheap wine, her badge already gone: *I took an oath to the dead, Dominic. Nobody told me the dead could lie.*

They could. They could lie for thirty years, and they could lie in embossed seal and registrar's stamp, and they could lie in a bronze statue with pigeons on its shoulders.

* * *

I drove to the cemetery because it was the only place I could think of where the vertigo might settle, which tells you something about how far gone I was — a man seeking equilibrium in a graveyard. Tomas Reed was in his shed, sharpening a spade with the unhurried strokes of a man who has outlived hurry. He looked up when I came in and nodded, as if he'd been expecting me since Tuesday.

"You've got the look," he said.

"What look is that?"

"The look of a man who found out the ground isn't solid." He set the spade against the wall. "Sit down, Mr. Amberdale. You're swaying."

I sat on an upturned bucket and told him. Not everything — I kept the ledger to myself, kept the coded entries to myself — but the certificate, the dates, Wren's surrender. He listened with his head cocked, like a bird, and when I finished he was quiet for a long moment, working the whetstone along a folding knife that had no business being that sharp.

"October of '74," he said finally. "I wasn't keeper here then. My predecessor was — old man Sorrel. But I heard the stories, when I took over in '79. There was talk."

"About the funeral?"

"About the *closed* casket." He said it the way you'd say *the unswept floor* — a small professional scandal, the kind that gets remembered in a trade for decades. "Halloway was the founder. The whole town wanted to look at him one last time. And the family said no. Closed casket, quick service, rain that day besides. Sorrel said the coffin was heavier than it should've been for a man Halloway's size, but lighter than it should've been for what was inside it, if you follow me. He never said it straight out. He just said, 'That box was lying, Tomas. I could tell by the way it sat on the boards.'"

"Six men carry a coffin," I said. "They know its weight."

"Six men know," Reed agreed. "And six men say nothing. That's the arithmetic of a town this size, Mr. Amberdale. Everybody knows and nobody knows, and the knowing cancels out."

I looked at him. "You're telling me the whole town might have suspected."

"I'm telling you the whole town *didn't want to know*." He sheathed the knife. "Halloway built the library. Halloway built the hospital wing. A man doesn't look too hard at the coffin of the man who fixed his mother's roof. And the ones who might have looked —" he tapped his own chest, then gestured vaguely toward town — "were paid not to. Sorrel told me that much before he died. He said the family came around every year, the first week of October. Not the lawyer. A different man. Quiet. And every year there was an envelope, and every year Sorrel looked the other way, and every year the ground stayed the way the family wanted it."

"The first week of October." The anniversary. The anniversary of a death that might not have happened. "Reed — the man with the ring. The one who paid you to look away, thirty years ago. Was it the same night? October?"

"Rainy night in October, yes."

The vertigo came back, but different now — steadier, colder. Because I'd been assuming the empty coffin was recent. Weeks old, months at most. The heirs, panicking over the ledger's discovery, digging up the founder to destroy whatever evidence the grave held. That was the story I'd been telling myself.

But if the coffin had been empty since 1974 — if the exhumation hadn't *revealed* the emptiness so much as *scheduled* it, if the whole reinterment had been arranged by someone who *knew* — then the crime wasn't panic. The crime was maintenance. Thirty years of maintenance. Annual envelopes, annual silence, an annual lie renewed each October like a subscription.

And that meant Charles Halloway hadn't funded an exhumation out of grief or greed. He'd funded it because someone had finally decided the lie was too expensive to keep renewing — or because someone had finally decided it was time to let the town see the empty box and *misread* it. To let us all stand at the graveside and gasp at a desecration that was actually a confession, thirty years deferred.

I stood up. My knees complained. Reed watched me with the mild patience of a man who buries people for a living and has opinions about the living that he keeps to himself.

"Mr. Amberdale," he said, as I reached the shed door. "Sorrel told me one more thing, before he went. He said the quiet man with the envelope — he saw him once in daylight, years later, coming out of the county building. And he said, 'Tomas, that man walks like he owns the ground, and I mean that literal.'"

"Did Sorrel know his name?"

"Never did." Reed picked up the whetstone again. "But he said the man had a way of nodding at you, like a judge. Like he'd already decided how your case was going to come out."

* * *

I didn't go home. I drove to the square and sat in my car across from the statue, engine off, watching the pigeons do their slow patrol of Edmund Halloway's bronze shoulders. The light was going amber and long, the way it does in October, and the shadows of the trees lay across the square like bars.

I took out my phone and called Iris. She picked up on the second ring, and just hearing her voice — tired, guarded, but there — settled something in my chest that the records office had unsettled.

"Tell me," she said. No greeting. She'd learned my habits fast.

"The death certificate was filed the next day. Signed by a doctor who surrendered his license a month later and vanished. The casket was closed at the funeral, and the gravekeeper's predecessor swore it sat wrong. And the grave was paid for, every October, for thirty years." I watched a pigeon take off from the statue's head. "Iris — I don't think we're investigating a theft. I think we're investigating a resurrection in reverse. I think Edmund Halloway never died."

Silence on the line. Long enough that I checked the screen to see if the call had dropped.

"Say that again," she said.

"He ran. He faked his death in 1974 to get out from under something — the blackmail, the skim, whatever came first — and the town buried an empty box and called it a founder. And everyone who kept the secret was paid, and everyone who suspected chose not to know, and the ledger in my back room isn't a record of his crimes. It's a record of his *alibi*. Thirty years of entries proving a dead man was still spending."

Another silence, and then a sound that might have been a laugh, if a laugh could be frightened. "Dominic. Do you understand what you're saying? If he's alive — if he was alive —"

"Then he's ninety-one," I said. "And someone has been keeping him buried in name all this time. And someone just spent a great deal of money making sure that when the town finally opened that coffin, it would look like a crime that started last month instead of a lie that started in 1974."

I hung up and sat in the gathering dark, watching the statue. The streetlights came on, one by one, and the bronze face of Edmund Halloway caught the light and held it, serene and certain, the face of a man who had been dead for thirty years and knew exactly how it felt.

Tomorrow, I decided, I would go see Margaret Halloway. Because if anyone alive knew what her uncle had run from — and who had helped him run — it would be the one heir who never came to the memorial, never signed the family statements, never once, in thirty years, looked at that statue.

The pigeons had gone to roost. The square was empty. And somewhere under my feet, under the town, under thirty years of October envelopes, the truth was still down there — alive, old, and waiting.
