## 13. The Old Man in the Dark

The mausoleum of Edmund Halloway stood at the crest of the cemetery's oldest hill like a man who'd insisted on the best seat at his own funeral. Granite columns, a bronze door gone green at the seams, and carved above the lintel in letters that had probably cost more than my entire inventory: *EDMUND HALLOWAY — 1931–1974 — FOUNDER. BENEFACTOR. FATHER OF THIS TOWN.*

Father of this town. I'd spent six days learning what kind of father keeps a ledger of everyone he's robbed.

I'd come at dusk on purpose. Cemeteries have rhythms, like bookshops — the mourners thin out after four, the grounds crew leaves by five, and by six you've got nothing but wind, crows, and the particular silence of a place where everybody present is either dead or lying about why they're there. I'd spent enough of my life in both categories to feel at home.

The cipher from the ledger had been simple once Iris cracked the substitution key over cheap wine two nights ago: *the founder's rest, beneath the founder's stone, where the water never reaches.* Beneath the stone. I'd assumed it meant the grave. The grave was empty. Empty things don't hide other things.

But mausoleums have floors, and floors have weight-bearing logic, and Halloway had been rich enough in 1931 to build himself a house for eternity with a basement.

The bronze door wasn't locked. That was my first surprise. My second was the smell — not the mineral cold of old marble I expected, but something faintly alive. Lamp oil. Wool. And underneath it, impossibly, tea.

*Where the water never reaches.* The floor inside was a grid of marble slabs, each engraved with a name — Halloways going back three generations, their dates neat as ledger entries. I knelt by the founder's own slab, the one directly under his name in the lintel, and pressed the corners. The third corner gave with a click that sounded, in that silence, like a gunshot.

The slab lifted on hidden hinges. Stairs went down into blackness, and up those stairs, faint but unmistakable, came the smell of steeping tea.

Someone was home.

I want to tell you I felt brave in that moment. What I actually felt was the specific dread of a man who has spent thirty years reading mysteries and has just realized he's walked into chapter thirteen. I took out my phone, considered the light, considered what it would announce, and left it in my pocket. Old habits. Old fool. Same thing, some days.

I went down.

* * *

The stair turned twice and opened into a room that should not have existed beneath a town's most sacred monument. It was dry — the water line of thirty years of damp stopped neatly at the stairwell, some old drainage trick — and it was furnished. A cot with an army blanket. A table. Shelves of canned goods with labels faded to ghosts. A kerosene lamp turned low. Books, dozens of them, stacked in careful towers. And in a chair by the table, wrapped in a cardigan the color of wet ash, sat an old man holding a teacup in both hands like it was the last warm thing in the world.

He didn't startle. That frightened me more than shouting would have. He simply turned his face toward the stairs — toward the sound, not the sight; his eyes were milky, cataract-thick — and said, in a voice worn smooth as river stone:

"You're late. I expected Vane's man hours ago."

"I'm not Vane's man," I said.

"No." He tilted his head, listening to me breathe, or maybe to something else entirely. "Vane's men smell of cigarettes and hurry. You smell of paper and grief. Sit down, whoever you are. The chair's been waiting thirty years for company."

I sat. Up close he was ruinous and magnificent at once — ninety-one if he was a day, hands spotted and trembling slightly around the cup, but a jaw still cut from granite, the same jaw that glowered down from the portrait in the town hall. I'd seen that portrait every week of my life in this town. I was looking at its original.

"Edmund Halloway," I said.

"Was," he agreed. "Edmund Halloway died in 1974. Very well attended funeral. Lovely eulogy. I read about it in the papers Vane brings me." He smiled, and it was the saddest arithmetic I've ever seen on a human face. "You have the advantage. Who are you?"

"Amberdale. Dominic Amberdale. I keep the bookshop on—"

"The bequest shop." Something moved behind the milk-white eyes. "My books. Are they selling?"

"Some of them. The rare ones I keep in glass."

"Good. Glass is honest. It lets you look without letting you touch." He drank his tea. "You found the catalogue, then. That's how you found me."

"The catalogue found me. It's been shelved in my storeroom since before I owned the shop. Thirty years I dusted a confession and called it inventory."

He laughed — a dry, delighted sound, genuinely delighted, and I hated how much I liked him already. "That's the best joke anyone's told me since Nixon resigned. Tell me, Mr. Amberdale-who-smells-of-paper: did you decode all of it? Or just enough to be dangerous?"

"All of it. The payments. The families. The skim from the mill contracts, the road bonds, the school fund. Thirty years of this town's money moving through your name into seven founding households, with Vane as the plumbing."

"Yes." He set down the cup. His hands were very still now, which somehow made them worse. "All true. Every entry. Would it help if I wept? I can manage a fair imitation. I've had practice weeping at my own grave."

"Why?" It came out rougher than I intended. "You had everything. The town named streets after you. Children. Why steal from people who loved you?"

"Because love is a currency too, Mr. Amberdale, and I learned early that it inflates." He leaned back. "You know how I started? A hardware store. One store. Then the war came and a man who supplies shovels and nails becomes a man who supplies everything, and everything has margins, and margins have appetites. By 1950 I wasn't a merchant anymore. I was a circulation system. Money came in from the county, the state, the federal projects — and I decided which neighborhoods it reached and which it bypassed. And here is the thing no one tells you about being a circulation system." He tapped his own chest. "You stop feeling like a heart. You start feeling like a pump. Mechanical. Necessary. Unloved."

"So you took your cut."

"I took my cut because everyone else was taking theirs. The councilmen, the assessors, the bank. I was merely the only one honest enough to write it down." He gestured at the ceiling, toward the town above us, sleeping under streetlights he'd paid for with its own stolen money. "Half those founding families you're circling — they didn't invent anything. They cashed checks. I invented the checking account."

"And then someone found out."

His stillness changed quality. Fear has a texture; I've interrogated enough men to know it when I hear a chair creak differently.

"In 1974," he said quietly, "a young auditor from the state came through. Bright boy. Polite. He asked questions in the order that frightened me most — the order that showed he already knew the answers. And he didn't want money. Men who want money you can pay. This boy wanted a partnership. Permanent. He wanted the machine, not a payout." He paused. "And when I refused him, he explained, very courteously, what would happen to my name, my family, and my liberty if certain documents reached the attorney general. He'd kept copies, you see. Of everything. From the beginning. He'd been building his file since before I knew he existed."

"Vane."

"He was twenty-six years old. He'd taken a position at the firm that handled my estate planning, and he had spent four years reading my life like a menu." The old man's voice didn't rise, but the room got colder anyway. "So I did the only thing left. If I stayed, he owned me forever, and through me, the town. If I died — on paper — the file became worthless. Dead men can't be blackmailed. Dead men's crimes die with them, statute by statute, year by year. So I died. The doctor who signed the certificate owed me more than he could repay in ten lifetimes, and he left town a week later, as arranged. The coffin was weighted. The newspaper was—" he almost smiled, "—vanity. I wanted to read my own obituaries one last time. Vane put it in as a joke. He has always had a sense of humor like a knife in a gift box."

"And then he kept paying you."

"To stay dead. Monthly, for thirty years. Cash, delivered by men who never spoke. Because a living Halloway is the one weapon that can destroy him — and a bored, lonely, half-blind old man under a mausoleum is a weapon he can watch." He spread his ruined hands. "He was right to watch. Do you know what thirty years of being a ghost does to a man, Mr. Amberdale? It makes him want, just once, to haunt somebody."

* * *

We sat a while after that. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above us, wind worried the bronze door, and I thought about Iris, who by now would have noticed I hadn't called, and who would be doing the arithmetic of my silences with that coroner's precision she pretended wasn't tenderness.

"Why now?" I finally asked. "Why did the coffin get emptied? Vane's kept you fed and hidden for thirty years. What changed?"

"Charles." Bitterness aged the word. "My grandson funded the reinterment — sentimentality, the papers said. But Charles has been asking questions for years. Questions about the estate, about where the money goes, about why Grandfather's philanthropy never quite balanced. And Vane panicked. Not of Charles — Charles is harmless, a decent boy with an accountant's suspicions. Vane panicked about what Charles might dig up. So he moved first. Opened the grave, removed the weighting, planted the newspaper as a mystery — a mystery pointed away from himself. Let the town chew on resurrection. Let the police chase ghosts." He shook his head slowly. "It was elegant. It's always elegant. That's how I know it's him."

"Except it brought me," I said. "And it brought Iris Vale."

"Ah." The old man turned his blind face toward me with terrible accuracy. "The coroner. The young woman who refused to hush it. Vane speaks of her the way generals speak of weather." He was quiet a moment. "And you care for her."

"That's not relevant."

"Everything is relevant. That's the first lesson of theft and the last lesson of love, and I mastered one and flunked the other." He folded his hands. "Mr. Amberdale. I'm going to ask you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you answer. Everything you decoded — the ledger, the families, the file Vane keeps — it dies with me unless it surfaces while I'm alive to confirm it. Statutes have run out for most of it, yes. But the fraud on the town, the current arrangements, Brandt's retainer — that's alive, and I am the only witness who can hang it on him. I am, in the vulgar phrase, the body of evidence. Literally. The one body this town has been missing."

"Then come up," I said. "Come out. Testify. I'll stand beside you. Iris will—"

"No."

One word, quiet as the closing of the slab above us.

"They'll carry me out on a stretcher," he said, "into cameras and microphones, and I'll be ninety-one years of scandal wearing pajamas. The town that named streets after me will learn its father was a thief, and worse — a coward who hid under his own monument while other men did time for smaller sins. Margaret will be destroyed. Charles will spend his inheritance on lawyers defending a corpse's good name against a living ghost. And Vane—" he smiled thinly, "—Vane gets exactly what he wants: a spectacle so loud that no one hears the confession in it. No, Mr. Amberdale. I have been dead for thirty years. I've gotten rather good at it. I intend to finish."

"You'll die down here."

"I'll die somewhere. Down here, I die as a rumor instead of a headline. There are worse fates. I've read about most of them." He reached out, and his hand found my sleeve with surprising accuracy — he'd mapped the room by sound, by draft, by thirty years of darkness. "Listen to me. You want the truth. Take it. Take every word I've given you tonight. Write it down, memorize it, tattoo it inside your eyelids if you must. But leave me my grave. It's the only property I have left that's honestly mine."

"And Vane? When he figures out I've been down here?"

Something ancient moved across the blind face. Pity, maybe. Or its twin.

"Then you'll understand why I stayed," he said softly. "Go home, Mr. Amberdale. Go home by the light, and don't come back alone again. And give my regards to the young woman — tell her the tea's better than her wine. I heard about the wine. Sound carries, in these hills. It always has. It's how I've listened to this town for thirty years — its funerals, its festivals, its little lies drifting down through the stone." He released my sleeve and picked up his cup again, a king abdicating back into exile. "Now go. Before I change my mind and decide I'd like company forever."

I climbed the stairs with my heart going like a trapped bird. At the top I paused, hand on the slab.

"Edmund," I said — using his name for the first time, and hearing how strange it sounded, addressing a dead man. "The town deserves the truth. Even if it breaks its heart."

From the dark below, calm as a ledger line:

"Then you'd best make sure, Mr. Amberdale, that the truth doesn't break yours first. Vane knows I'm missing forty minutes of tea. He counts, you know. He has always counted."

I sealed the slab, climbed into the night air, and stood a long moment among the headstones, breathing cold and free and guilty. Behind me, under six feet of honored marble, a living man poured another cup of tea for no one.

In my pocket, my phone lit up. Iris.

*I've been calling. Where are you?*

I looked at the words a long time. Then I typed the only answer I had:

*At the founder's rest. Iris — he's alive. And I think Vane already knows I know.*

I hit send, and the screen went dark, and somewhere down the hill, at the cemetery gate, a car door closed without hurry — the unhurried click of a man who counts.
