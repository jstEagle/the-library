## 21. The Shop on Amberdale Lane

The sign went up on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays are the day towns forgive things.

I know this now. I've lived here long enough — or briefly enough, depending on how you count — to understand that Harrow's Bend operates on a liturgical calendar nobody wrote down. Mondays are for grudges. Wednesdays are for gossip dressed as concern. But Tuesday, Tuesday is when the town decides it's ready to stop being ashamed of itself for a week. It was a Tuesday when they buried Edmund Halloway properly, with the whole town watching and half of them crying for reasons they couldn't have explained under oath. And it was a Tuesday, three weeks after that, when Dominic Amberdale climbed a ladder outside his own shop with a screwdriver in his teeth and hung a new sign over the old one.

**AMBERDALE & VALE**, it said, in letters that had cost him an argument and me a bottle of wine.

"You spelled my name right," I called up from the sidewalk.

"Don't sound so surprised."

"I'm a coroner. Surprise died in me years ago. I'm just noting it for the record."

He tightened the last screw, came down the ladder with the exaggerated care of a man who'd been shot at more recently than either of us liked to mention, and stood beside me to look at his handiwork. His handiwork being, technically, our handiwork, though he still flinched slightly every time someone said *partners* out loud, as if the word were a diagnosis.

"It's crooked," he said.

"It's level."

"It's crooked by a degree and a half."

"Then it matches everything else in your life."

He almost smiled. With Dominic, that's a parade. I've learned to read the micro-expressions the way I used to read autopsy reports — the small anomalies that tell you everything the surface won't admit. The corner of his mouth lifting a quarter inch: contentment. The shoulders dropping from their permanent defensive hunch: safety, unfamiliar and therefore suspicious. The eyes going soft when they landed on me before he could stop them: well. That one I'd stopped needing a chart for.

"Open tomorrow?" he asked.

"Open today," I said. "It's past noon. If we wait until tomorrow, the town will have invented three reasons why we didn't."

*

Here is what I want to put on the record, since apparently I'm keeping one now:

Three weeks ago I arrested my own police chief on the steps of a derelict mill while Edmund Halloway lay dying in the back of an ambulance and Prescott Vane sat handcuffed to a radiator reciting his confession like a man reading his own obituary and finding it flattering. Three weeks ago Dominic Amberdale traded thirty years of stolen history for thirty minutes of recorded truth, and the town got its fortune back, and two heirs who'd dug up a grave for nothing walked free because a dying man asked a favor of a detective who'd spent his whole career refusing to ask any himself.

And three weeks ago, standing in the cold with state police radios crackling around us, Dominic looked at me across the wreckage of everything and said, "Your badge survived."

"My badge," I said, "is the only thing that did."

That wasn't true, and he knew it wasn't true, and the fact that neither of us said what *was* true — that's its own kind of record. We're working on it. Slowly. The way he does everything, which is to say like a man defusing something.

Brandt's trial starts in the spring. Vane pled guilty to avoid the spectacle; his confession runs four hours and names eleven founding families, and District Attorney's office has been very polite about the fact that I'm the one who found the proof, and very careful about the fact that I found it while technically suspended for obstruction. There was a hearing. There was a letter of reprimand. There was a sentence in my file that reads, in full: *Officer Vale exercised independent judgment during a period of administrative confusion.* Administrative confusion. That's what we're calling a police chief on a criminal's retainer since 1987. I've decided to find it funny rather than infuriating, mostly because the alternative is screaming, and I do enough of that in the shower.

Dominic never got his name cleared in any formal sense, because there was nothing formally against him. He was never a suspect. He was never anything official at all — a retired detective consulting off the books, a bookshop owner who happened to be in the wrong cemeteries at the right times. The town has decided he's a hero anyway. The town, I've learned, doesn't need paperwork. It needs a story, and it got one: the empty coffin, the hidden ledger, the old man in the crypt, the crooked lawyer, the corrupt chief, and the two of them at the mill with the rain coming down. By now the story has grown details that never happened. Someone swears Dominic disarmed Vane personally. Someone else says Halloway's last words were a blessing on the town. In the version told at the diner, I arrive at the mill on horseback.

I've stopped correcting people. A coroner learns early that the living will believe whatever comforts them, and the dead don't mind.

*

The shop reopened on a Wednesday, actually, because Dominic insisted the sign needed a full day to "settle," which I understood immediately to mean *he* needed a full day to settle. But let him have his rituals. A man who spent twenty years pulling bodies out of rivers taught me that ritual is how the sane survive the insane.

The morning crowd was small and merciless. Mrs. Okafor from the bakery arrived with cinnamon rolls and opinions about the shelving. Tomas Reed came in leaning on a cane he didn't need, bought a paperback western he'd already read twice, and stayed forty minutes telling anyone who'd listen about the rainy night in 1994, editing his role in events with each retelling until by the third version he had essentially solved the case himself and merely permitted us to take the credit. Charles Halloway appeared at the door, hesitated like a man approaching a dog that had bitten him, and finally came in to buy a book of poetry — actual poetry, Whitman, which he paid for in cash and carried out without meeting anyone's eyes. Margaret sent flowers with a card that said only: *For the window shelf. She would have approved.* No signature necessary. Everyone in Harrow's Bend knew whose taste Margaret was invoking, and everyone understood that the late Mrs. Amberdale had become, posthumously, the town's arbiter of all things decent.

That was the part I hadn't expected to be hard. Not the notoriety, not the reprimand, not even Brandt's lawyers sniffing around my report looking for procedural sins. The hard part was Elena.

Elena Amberdale, dead eleven years, present everywhere. Her photograph still on the desk by the register — Dominic had moved it once, early on, and then moved it back, and I'd said nothing, because what was there to say? Her reading glasses still in the drawer by the till. Her marginalia still in half the stock, little pencil stars next to passages she loved, and I'd caught myself, twice now, buying a book and finding her handwriting inside, and standing there in the aisle feeling like I'd interrupted a conversation between two people, one of whom was dust and the other of whom was currently ringing up my purchase.

"She wouldn't mind," Dominic said, the first time he saw me holding one of her books like it might go off.

"You don't know that."

"I knew her better than God did." He took it from my hands gently, turned to the starred page, and read a line aloud — Mary Oliver, something about paying attention — then handed it back. "She'd mind if I stopped selling them. Books are meant to be held by strangers. That was her whole theology."

So I bought the Oliver. It's on my shelf now. My shelf — the one by the front window, the one Margaret's flowers sit beneath, the one Dominic cleared without being asked and then pretended he hadn't. Two weeks ago it held nothing but a lamp. Now it holds the Oliver, a battered anatomy atlas he found somewhere and refused to explain, and a framed copy of my letter of reprimand, which I put there myself as a joke and which he refused to let me take down.

"Best review you ever got," he said. "*Exercised independent judgment.* Frame it. Frame everything anyone gives you in writing. It's the only kind that lasts."

*

Weeks pass the way weeks do in small towns after a scandal: slowly, then all at once, then not at all. The grand jury convenes. The historical society quietly removes Edmund Halloway's portrait from the library and just as quietly puts it back, because whatever else he was, he was ninety-one years old and he came home to be buried, and Harrow's Bend can hold a grudge and a hymn in the same hand. The recovered money sits in escrow while lawyers divide it like vultures arguing over etiquette. Margaret announces she'll endow the bookshop the moment the courts release the funds, and Dominic announces, loudly and repeatedly, that he doesn't want it, and everyone ignores him, because that's how endowments work.

And I keep my badge. That's the sentence I turn over at night, the one I still don't entirely trust. I keep my badge. Chief — interim Chief, a tired, honest woman named Delgado who took the job the way you'd take custody of a haunted house — handed it back to me herself, no ceremony, no speech. Just set it on my desk and said, "Don't make me regret the letter." Which is Delgado for *thank you*, and I've chosen to hear it that way.

Some mornings I stand at the window shelf with my coffee before opening the shop and look out at Amberdale Lane, and I think about how I came to this town eighteen months ago because it was small and quiet and far from the city morgue where I'd learned that death in bulk stops meaning anything. I wanted a place where the dead were few and known by name. I got a place where the dead were few and *lying about it*, but still — known by name. Edmund Halloway is in the ground now, truly, under a stone that says only EDMUND HALLOWAY, 1933–2025, because Margaret refused to put FOUNDER on it. "He was a man," she said. "Bury him like one."

I was there when they lowered the box. So was Dominic. He wore a suit that fit him badly and held his hat in both hands like a man at a funeral for someone he'd failed, though of course he hadn't failed; he'd done the one thing nobody else in eighty years had thought to do, which was to open the coffin and look inside. That's the whole profession, really. That's mine too. Everybody else prays over the box. Somebody has to check.

Afterward, at the graveside, with the crowd dispersing and the gravediggers waiting with their professional patience, Dominic said, "You know what I keep thinking?"

"Tell me."

"He waited thirty years down there in the dark, listening to us walk over his head. Every memorial. Every wreath. Every schoolchild tour." He put his hat on. "And the whole time, the truth was six feet under everybody's feet, and nobody thought to dig."

"Somebody did," I said.

"Somebody did," he agreed, and looked at me the way he does when he means more than he says, which is always, and we left it there, because we're still learning the grammar of each other and neither of us wants to conjugate too fast.

*

Which brings me to yesterday. Or rather, to this morning, because the thing happened yesterday and I'm only now sitting still long enough to write it down, and yes, I write things down now — Dominic's habit, infectious as a cold. He keeps case notebooks the way other men keep whiskey. I've started keeping one too. Mine is neater.

Yesterday was the shop's first full ordinary day. No crowd, no ceremony, no reporters — the press had already moved on to a sinkhole in the next county, the way press does. Just rain on the windows, the smell of old paper, and the particular silence of a place that has decided to be happy and isn't sure yet whether happiness requires vigilance.

Dominic was in the back doing inventory. I was at the counter with the day's receipts and a coffee going cold, and the bell over the door rang — that small brass bell Elena must have chosen, because it rings a half-tone flat, imperfectly, like everything else in this shop that I've come to love for its flaws.

A stranger came in.

I want to describe him accurately, because accuracy is the only prayer I know. Middle-aged, maybe fifty. Raincoat, dark, unremarkable. Face the kind you forget while looking at it — I mean that forensically; I've stood over faces for a living and this one was built to slide off memory. He browsed. He was good at browsing; he had the practiced aimlessness of a man who has entered many rooms pretending to want things. He drifted through history, through mystery — paused, I noticed, at the shelf where Dominic keeps the town's local histories, the ones with Halloway's name in every index — and then he came to the counter carrying nothing.

"No luck?" I asked.

"Just looking," he said. And then, setting something down on the counter between us with the smooth economy of a magician's palm: "But I'll leave this. For the owner. It looked like something he'd appreciate."

It was a book. An old one, cloth-bound, spine sun-faded, title worn to ghosts: *A Catalogue of Rare and Curious Volumes*. And I felt it happen in my chest before my brain caught up — that cold drop, the body knowing first, the way it knows at a scene before the report confirms it. Because I have seen that catalogue before. I saw its twin in a coffin, folded around a newspaper. I watched its code come apart under lamplight and cheap wine, entry by entry, payment by payment, the whole rotten beautiful history of a town spilling out of what looked like an inventory list.

"Where did you get this?" I asked.

"A friend," he said. "Who no longer needs it."

And he smiled — a small, correct smile, calibrated to the millimeter — and turned, and the bell rang flat behind him, and through the rain-streaked glass I watched him walk up Amberdale Lane without hurrying, without looking back, the way a man walks when he knows exactly how much time he has and exactly how much he needs.

I stood there with my hand on the book, not picking it up. Training says don't touch evidence. Instinct says pick it up, open it, *know*. I compromised, the way I've learned to: I photographed it where it lay, front cover, back cover, the counter around it, the door, and then I called out, casual as Sunday,

"Dominic? You'll want to see this."

He came from the back wiping his hands on a rag, saw my face, and stopped. Twenty-two years a detective; he read the room in a second. His eyes went to the book, and I watched the color change in his face the way weather changes over water.

"That's not possible," he said. "Vane's copy burned. The original is in evidence."

"This isn't either of those."

He picked it up — gloves, of course, he'd produced gloves from somewhere, the man keeps nitrile gloves in a bookshop the way other men keep breath mints — and opened it to the middle, where the columns of titles began, and I watched him find what I'd already seen from across the counter: that the entries weren't quite entries. That the prices weren't quite prices. That the catalogue was a catalogue the way a locked room is a room.

"Another ledger," he said quietly.

"Another town," I said. "There's a bookmark."

There was. A slip of heavy paper, laid into the gutter near the back. Dominic drew it out with two gloved fingers and we read it together, standing under the flat brass bell, while the rain kept up its patient work on the windows and the shop around us held its breath.

It was typed. One line, centered, no signature:

*HARROW'S BEND WAS NOT THE FIRST. ASK MILLBROOK ABOUT THE DROWNING IN '89. THE LEDGER IS YOURS NOW. SO IS THE QUESTION.*

Below it, a string of characters in a cipher I recognized — the same substitution scheme, the same telltale rhythm, the same hand that had hidden a fortune inside a gift for forty years.

Dominic read it twice. Then he did something I'd never seen him do. He laughed — short, low, incredulous, the laugh of a man who has just been handed a bill he thought he'd finished paying.

"They're inviting us," he said.

"Or daring us."

"Same thing, at our age." He set the book down on the counter between the receipts and my cold coffee, square in the middle of the ordinary life we'd spent three weeks building, and looked at me over the top of his glasses. "Well, Vale. Your badge. Your call."

Outside, the rain eased. The bell hung silent. And on the counter, open to the cipher, the second ledger waited — patient as a grave, patient as a founder in a crypt, patient as the truth, which turns out never to stay buried anywhere, in any town, no matter how carefully you shelve it.

I reached for my notebook.
