## 4. Cipher

The diary stopped making sense on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day my mother used to say the universe did its filing—nothing dramatic, just paperwork and small betrayals.

I'd been at it for six nights by then. My apartment had entered the state I privately called "case weather": dishes migrating to the windowsill, the good lamp angled over the kitchen table like a searchlight, a constellation of sticky notes colonizing every vertical surface. My neighbor Mrs. Okonjo—no relation to anyone in Hollowbrook, though after chapter five of this story I'd learn to flinch at that surname—had taken to leaving casseroles outside my door with the resigned air of a woman feeding a stray cat that occasionally solved murders.

"Marchetti," she'd said through the door on night four, "you have that look again."

"What look?"

"The one where you forget bread exists."

She wasn't wrong. There was something about Ivy Solano's handwriting that made food seem beside the point. The early entries were ordinary in the way that makes ordinary devastating: coffee at the diner, complaints about the microfilm reader at the county archive, a running feud with her landlady's cat. She wrote the way people talk when they think no one is listening—quick, funny, a little sharp. By page forty I had a crush on a woman who'd been missing for thirty years, which was either dedication or a warning sign.

Then, three weeks past the date the whole town agreed she'd vanished, the entries changed.

Not dramatically. That was the horror of it. No scrawled warnings, no torn pages. Just a slow dissolution, like a radio signal drifting out of range. On October 14th, 1995, Ivy Solano wrote four lines about rain and a man named D.M. who "asked the wrong question at the wrong meeting." On October 15th, the sentences began substituting letters. By October 17th, it was all cipher—and not a simple one. Not a Caesar shift, not a keyword square, nothing my usual bag of tricks could crack. The substitution pattern shifted *within* entries, sometimes within words, as if the key itself were alive.

I did what I always do when a case gets its teeth into me. I made tea I didn't drink and talked to an empty room.

"Okay, Ivy," I said, pinning a photocopy of the October 16th entry above the table. "You're an archivist. You catalog things for a living. If you build a cipher, you build it *systematically*. So what's your system?"

The entry stared back, smug as a locked door.

Here's what nobody tells you about codebreaking: ninety percent of it is staring. The glamorous ten percent—the breakthrough, the click—is just staring that finally pays rent. I stared. I transcribed. I built frequency tables that told me the letter distribution was too flat for English, then too spiky, then flat again. Whatever keyed the substitutions, it wasn't a static text.

On night six, somewhere around two in the morning, I noticed the margins.

They'd been there all along, of course. Faint pencil marks in the outer edges of the final entries—small hooked symbols, slashes with tails, little stacked strokes like bird tracks in snow. I'd catalogued them mentally as decoration, or maybe Ivy testing pen pressure. But at two a.m., sleep-deprived and tea-poisoned, I looked at them properly for the first time.

And my whole body went cold.

I knew those marks. Not from any textbook. Not from any archive. I knew them the way you know a lullaby—somewhere below memory, in the place where childhood lives.

I sat very still, the way you do when a ghost walks through the room, afraid that moving might confirm it.

Then I got up, went to the closet, and pulled down the box I only open about once a year. Cardboard soft with age, smelling of cedar and old paper. Inside: my mother's linguistics notebooks, forty pounds of them, the life's work of Dr. Elena Marchetti, field linguist, notation obsessive, woman who died believing her work would matter to someone someday.

My mother spent her career documenting endangered languages, mostly in the Pacific Northwest, and she invented her own transcription system for phonemes that didn't fit the International Phonetic Alphabet's tidy boxes. She called it her "shorthand for sounds nobody writes down." Hooked slashes for glottalized consonants. Stacked strokes for tonal contours. Little bird-track marks for stress patterns. She'd taught me the basics when I was nine, sitting cross-legged on the floor of her study while she said, "Language is just a code everyone agreed to stop noticing, Wrennie. Crack one and you can hear what people are really saying."

I hadn't thought about that sentence in twenty years. Now it was standing in my kitchen, wearing Ivy Solano's handwriting.

Because the marginal marks weren't decoration. They were Elena Marchetti's notation system, applied to the diary's own text—a pronunciation key, or something like one, marking how certain syllables should be *stressed*, and the stress pattern was the shifting key to the cipher. Ivy hadn't just built a code. She'd built it using a system published exactly once, in a monograph so obscure that my mother used to joke it had a readership of eleven, seven of whom were related to her.

I stood in my kitchen holding my dead mother's notebook next to a dead-or-missing woman's diary, and I laughed—one short, cracked sound that startled even me.

"You knew each other," I said to the empty room. "How the hell did you know each other?"

* * *

Sleep was not going to happen, so I did the next best thing: I made a timeline.

My mother's academic life was well documented, because academics document themselves compulsively. I pulled up her CV from the university's archived website—she'd died in 2011, and the department had left her page up like a small monument. Scanning the conference presentations, the visiting lectureships, the summer field schools.

1994: Guest lecture series, Pacific Coast Consortium of Historical Societies. Topic: "Applied Linguistic Methods for Archival Decipherment."

Historical societies. Plural. A lecture circuit.

I dug further, into the Wayback Machine and a digitized newsletter from the consortium, and found it: a schedule fragment listing the spring 1995 session venues. Third on the list, dated April 22nd, 1995:

**Hollowbrook County Historical Society — guest lecture, Dr. E. Marchetti**

April. Six months before Ivy Solano disappeared. My mother had stood in a room in Hollowbrook and taught a roomful of local history buffs how to decipher archival texts using her notation system.

And Ivy—an archivist, a woman whose job was literally old documents, a woman bright enough to build a shifting substitution cipher—had almost certainly been in that room.

I tried to picture it. My mother at a podium, probably underdressed for the occasion, probably making a joke that half the audience didn't get. And in the third row, maybe, a twenty-four-year-old archivist leaning forward, taking notes in the margins of a notebook, learning a system for hiding meaning inside ordinary language.

Learning it so well that five months later, when someone started watching her at work, she could write down everything she knew in a way that looked like nonsense.

"She gave you the tool," I said aloud, to both of them, to the kitchen. "She handed you the lockpick and never knew what door you'd use it on."

Grief does strange arithmetic. For twenty years I'd kept my mother's death in one column and my cases in another, the way you keep church and state separate. Her notebooks were relics; my casework was machinery. Now the columns had collapsed into each other. The thing she loved—cracking codes, hearing what people really said—was the exact thing keeping a missing woman's voice alive on my kitchen table. Every symbol I decoded was a conversation between two women who'd never meet, mediated by me, their reluctant translator.

I put my head down on the table, on top of the photocopied entry, and let myself cry for exactly four minutes. I timed it. Grief with a deadline—that was another thing she taught me, though probably not on purpose.

Then I got back to work.

* * *

Decoding with the right key is a different sport entirely. It's not staring anymore; it's reading, slowly, the way you read a foreign language you're almost fluent in—catching the shape of sentences before you've parsed every word.

The stress-notation system worked like this: the marginal marks indicated which syllables of the plaintext carried emphasis, and the emphasized syllable's position determined the substitution offset for the following word. Shift within shift. Elegant, paranoid, and completely Ivy—built by someone who understood that the safest cipher is one that looks like handwriting tics.

By dawn on night eight, I had the first full decoded passage. I want to reproduce it here exactly, because some sentences deserve to be preserved verbatim:

*"He came to the archive again Tuesday. Same gray car, same cologne, asks the same friendly questions about the consolidation files. Friendly like a trap is friendly. I've started copying everything. If something happens to me, it's in the foundation."*

I read it three times. *It's in the foundation.* Foundation of what? A building? An argument? An alibi?

The next decoded entry, dated October 19th:

*"D.M. says they know about the copies. He's scared. He should be—I'm more scared, but he should be too. Ruth has the envelope. Trust Ruth. Trust no one with a Vance smile."*

Ruth. I wrote the name on a sticky note and stuck it to the window, where the morning light turned it briefly transparent. The librarian. Ruth Ellery, who had hesitated a beat too long before agreeing with the official story, who had slipped me a photocopied page of council minutes like a contraband cigarette. Trust Ruth. Well. That tracked.

And D.M.—Daniel Merrick, if the founding-family math held. Merrick's son, scared, helping. Another note on the window.

But it was the last fully decodable entry, dated October 21st—two days before the diary dissolved entirely into cipher—that stopped my heart and restarted it in a different time zone:

*"If you're reading this, you learned the system from her. The linguist. Tell her thank you, wherever she is. Tell her a stranger is alive because of a Tuesday afternoon in April."*

I sat back. The apartment was gray-blue with early light. Somewhere below, Mrs. Okonjo was putting out her recycling, and the whole ordinary world was making its morning noises, utterly unaware that on my kitchen table a woman who vanished in 1995 had just spoken directly—to me, specifically, across thirty years, through my mother's handwriting.

*A stranger is alive because of a Tuesday afternoon in April.*

Alive. Present tense. In 1995, Ivy believed whoever decoded this would find her breathing. Either she'd been wrong, or—

Or the diary's final entries, the ones still locked, the ones written in cipher so dense it took the stress-notation system plus everything I had—they weren't written by Ivy at all. The handwriting matched. The ink matched. But the rhythm didn't; I'd been living inside this woman's prose for a week, and the last entries moved differently, like a song played by someone who'd studied the original but wasn't the singer.

Someone else had kept writing. Someone who knew the system. Someone keeping the diary alive so the town would keep assuming a runaway, not a body.

Which meant the last entries weren't a message from Ivy.

They were a message *about* her. From whoever was deciding, day by day, whether she stayed hidden—or stayed buried.

I reached for my phone to photograph the page, and my hand was shaking hard enough that the first attempt came out blurred. I steadied it against the teapot and took the picture anyway.

Outside, Hollowbrook was two hundred miles away, holding its secrets. Inside, my mother's ghost and a missing archivist had conspired to make this case mine in a way no case had ever been. Three cold cases had taught me how strangers die. This one was teaching me how a stranger lived—maybe still lives—and every decoded line felt less like investigation and more like inheritance.

I brewed actual tea, drank it while it was hot for once, and opened my laptop.

Time to find out what else happened in Hollowbrook in April 1995—and who else attended a Tuesday afternoon lecture given by a woman with a readership of eleven.

Because Ivy had trusted the system to one person. And the system had just led it home to me.
