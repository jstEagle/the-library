## 1. The Second Will

The train from the city took four hours to reach Grayhollow, and for three of them Mirabel Hartwell read nothing at all. He watched the valley open up outside the window — orchards gone feral, a river the color of old pewter, and then the town itself, rising out of the mist like something that had been waiting.

He had read every file on Edmund Voss twice on the way north. Founder of Grayhollow. Timber and rail money. Drowned in the river behind his own estate thirty-one years ago, at sixty-two, in a boating accident witnessed by five respected citizens. The town had built him a statue within the year. There was a school named after him, a park, an annual festival with paper lanterns floated down the same stretch of water that had supposedly taken him.

Thirty years of grief, commemorated annually, by a town that had never found his body.

Mirabel closed the folder. Bodies not recovered happened. Rivers kept what they caught. It was none of his business why a town loved its dead founder so lavishly; his business was the will.

The Voss house stood above the river on a shelf of stone, all gables and dark shingle, too large for the family that remained in it. A woman was waiting on the steps when the car pulled up — mid-fifties, silver hair cut short and severe, a coat worth more than most of the houses visible from the drive.

"Mirabel Hartwell," she said, extending a hand before he'd fully climbed out. "Cordelia Voss-Ashford. Edmund's granddaughter. We're so grateful you came personally."

"Counselor Ashford." He shook it. Her grip was dry and firm and held exactly one second longer than courtesy required. "Your letter said the family preferred an outside executor's attorney."

"We prefer competence." She smiled without warmth. "Grayhollow is a small place, Mr. Hartwell. Small places have small loyalties. We wanted someone with none."

*Someone who can't be bought,* he thought, and didn't say. He'd heard variations of that sentence in thirty years of practice, usually from people about to try buying him anyway.

Inside, the house smelled of cold ash and lemon polish. The great room had been arranged like a courtroom: a long table, chairs for the family on one side, a leather folio at the head where the will waited under a brass weight. Four people sat along the far side — Cordelia's brother, a heavy man with a lawyer's pallor; two cousins whose names Mirabel had already forgotten; and an elderly aunt who wept quietly into a handkerchief that looked, he noted professionally, entirely unused.

He took his seat, broke the seal on the public testament, and read aloud.

It was, on its face, unremarkable. The estate — house, lands, timber rights, the trust structures that had made the Voss name synonymous with Grayhollow itself — divided among the surviving descendants in shares carefully engineered to keep the whole intact. Charitable bequests: the school, the historical society, the church roof. A final paragraph directing that his papers be archived and his legend, in the florid language of 1974, *kept faithfully for the generations to come.*

Mirabel had read ten thousand wills. This one was smooth as river stone. Too smooth, some part of him murmured, and he set the thought aside with the folder.

"There's a codicil," Cordelia said, when he finished. "You'll want to sign the closing certificate, and then—"

"Actually," Mirabel said, "before signatures. I'm required to inventory the testator's document safe. Standard procedure when I hold letters testamentary. Where is it?"

Something moved through the room. Not quite a flinch. More like the air changing pressure.

"The safe was emptied years ago," Cordelia said pleasantly. "Family papers went to the archive."

"All of them?"

"Mr. Hartwell." Her brother leaned forward, smiling the smile of a man accustomed to being obeyed by institutions. "We've retained you to execute a straightforward testament. The reading is done. If you'll just—"

But Mirabel had already seen it. On the sideboard behind Cordelia's chair, half-hidden under a linen runner someone had laid over it carelessly — or not carelessly — a flat tin document box, government gray, the kind sold in stationery stores in the early seventies. Stenciled on the lid in faded block letters: **E.V. — PERSONAL — DO NOT OPEN UNTIL**.

The stencil ended there, worn away, as if the rest of the instruction had been deliberately scraped off.

"That box," Mirabel said. "Is that part of the estate?"

"It's nothing," Cordelia said.

"It's on the inventory photographs your own letter enclosed." He had studied those photographs for three hours on the train. He'd assumed the tin was a sewing kit. "Photograph eleven, sideboard, east wall. I'd like it opened now, please, in the presence of the family."

"Mr. Hartwell—"

"Or I can note its existence and my request to open it in my case file, which becomes discoverable the moment anyone contests anything. Which someone will. Families like yours always contest." He kept his voice mild, almost apologetic. It was the voice that had won him every case he'd ever touched, and he knew it, and he enjoyed knowing it, and he would have been lying if he claimed otherwise.

The silence lasted long enough for the weeping aunt to blow her nose.

Cordelia rose, lifted the runner, and set the box on the table between them. "It's probably empty," she said. "Father kept odds and ends in it. Fishing licenses."

Mirabel put on his glasses. The lock was a simple wafer tumbler, older than he was, and it gave to his pick in under a minute because he had never met a lock he respected more than his own curiosity.

Inside, on top, lay a folded newspaper clipping — the Grayhollow Gazette, thirty-one years old, the headline announcing FOUNDER LOST TO RIVER, TOWN MOURNS. Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, a second document.

It was sealed with wax. Real wax, black, stamped with a signet he didn't recognize — not the Voss oak-and-anchor from the public testament but a plain initial: **J.O.**

And across the face of the envelope, in a hand that trembled only slightly, was written:

*The true last will and testament of Edmund Ambrose Voss.*

Dated fourteen years ago.

Fourteen years after Edmund Voss drowned.

Mirabel did not touch it again. He photographed it instead, front and back, with the small camera he carried for exactly this kind of moment, and then he set it down on the table with the gentleness of a man handling something that might still be alive.

"Well," he said.

"Give it here," Cordelia's brother said, reaching.

"No." Mirabel covered it with one hand, lightly. "This is a testamentary instrument. Dated subsequent to the death of the testator, which makes it either a fraud, a forgery, or the single most interesting document in the history of this state's probate law. In any of those cases, it does not leave my custody until a court tells me whose it is."

"You don't understand what you're holding," Cordelia said. Her voice had changed. The pleasantness was gone; underneath it was something older and harder, bedrock showing through topsoil. "That family has suffered enough. That *town* has suffered enough. Edmund Voss built everything you can see from these windows. Whatever fever dream is written on that paper, it isn't worth tearing down thirty years of—"

"Of what?" Mirabel asked. "Grief?"

Nobody answered. The weeping aunt had stopped weeping. All four of them were looking at the envelope the way people look at a snake they'd believed was a rope.

"I'll need a private hour with the document," Mirabel said. "Then I'll tell you what happens next."

"You could burn it," Cordelia said quietly. "No one would ever know. You'd be paid very well for the inconvenience of forgetting. Everyone in this room would consider it a kindness — to the town, to the dead, to yourself."

Mirabel looked at her for a long moment. He thought about his office in the city, the wall of framed closing certificates, thirty years without a loose end. He thought about how much he loved that wall.

"I don't forget things," he said. "It's the only talent I have."

* * *

They gave him the east study and left him alone, which told him they were either confident or frightened, and he suspected the difference mattered less than they hoped.

He broke the black seal with a scalpel from his travel kit, photographing each stage. Inside were six pages, closely written, in the same trembling hand. And beneath them, a smaller slip of paper, unsigned:

*If anything happens to me, give this to J.O. Only J.O. No one else can be trusted. No one else remembers.*

Mirabel began to read.

The handwriting deteriorated page by page. The first page was controlled, almost legalistic — *I, Edmund Ambrose Voss, being of sound mind, do declare the following* — and then it stopped being a will at all. It became something else. A confession wearing a will's clothing. He read phrases as he skimmed: *the fire was not an accident.* *I paid for the silence of good men.* *I did not drown; I simply let the river take my name.* *What I left behind in that house on Calder Street—*

On Calder Street. Mirabel knew Grayhollow's streets from the plat maps. Calder Street was in the mill district. It had burned, according to the archive summary he'd skimmed on the train, some months before Voss died. One fatality. A woman. The fire had been ruled accidental.

The last page named no heirs. Instead it named a debt, and a beneficiary described only by initials — **J.O.** — and a single line that Mirabel read three times:

*I leave everything to the one who lost everything, and I ask the court's forgiveness, which I do not expect to receive.*

He sat back. Outside the study window, the river ran silver and indifferent toward the town, past the boathouse ruins, past the statue in the square that he could just see the shoulder of, arm raised forever toward some future the founder hadn't lived to see.

Except he had lived. Fourteen more years, somewhere close enough to write in a hand going soft with age. Somewhere close enough to know about a fire on Calder Street. Close enough to be afraid of someone — *no one else can be trusted* — and to hide his true testament inside a tin box in his own house, under a scraped-away instruction, waiting for initials that matched no heir Mirabel had ever heard of.

J.O.

He wrote the letters on his legal pad and stared at them. Then he drew a circle around them, because that was what he did with problems he intended to solve.

A floorboard creaked in the hall. When he turned, the study door stood open an inch, and through the gap he saw Cordelia's hand withdraw from the frame — slowly, the way you pull your hand back from a door you've decided, for now, not to open.

Mirabel slid the confession into his briefcase, locked it, and kept the key in his waistcoat pocket, against his heart, like a man arming himself.

Tomorrow, he decided, he would visit the town archive. Somebody in Grayhollow would know who J.O. was.

Somebody had always known.
