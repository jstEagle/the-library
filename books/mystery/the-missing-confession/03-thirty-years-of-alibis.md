## 3. Thirty Years of Alibis

The Grayhollow Historical Society kept its records in the basement of the old mill, and the basement smelled the way all small-town archives smell: dust, mildew, and the faint chemical ghost of decades-old mimeograph ink. Mirabel Hartwell had spent enough of his life in rooms like this to find the smell almost comforting. Almost.

He'd requested everything. That was his method — not brilliance, not intuition, just the patient, unglamorous appetite for paper. The night Edmund Voss drowned, someone had written things down. Someone always did. And people who lied on paper rarely remembered, thirty years later, exactly what they'd written.

The clerk — a nervous young man named Denny who kept glancing at the stairs as if expecting to be summoned away — wheeled out four boxes and a single bound volume.

"Everything from that year," Denny said. "Fire department logs, council minutes, the *Grayhollow Gazette*. And the coroner's file." He hesitated. "Mr. Hartwell, I should say — Councilman Pike called this morning. Asked what materials you'd requested."

Mirabel looked up from the first box. "And what did you tell him?"

"That you'd asked for everything." Denny's ears went pink. "Was that wrong?"

"No," Mirabel said. "That was exactly right."

Let Pike know. Let him watch Mirabel read. A man with nothing to hide doesn't call ahead; a man with something to hide wants you to know he's watching, because sometimes that's enough to make you stop.

It wouldn't be enough. It had never once been enough, in twenty-two years of practice, and Mirabel had built a career — a reputation, a name that traveled three counties — on the simple fact that he did not stop.

* * *

The coroner's file was thin. That was the first thing wrong with it.

A drowning death — a founding father, no less, the man whose bronze likeness stood in Voss Square — should have generated an avalanche: witness statements, scene photographs, water reports, an autopsy or at least an external examination by someone qualified. What Mirabel found instead was a folder barely an inch thick, its contents typed on mismatched machines, as if assembled over several days rather than one terrible night.

He laid the five witness statements side by side across the folding table and felt the particular quickening in his chest that he had learned, long ago, to trust more than any other feeling. Not joy. Not quite. Closer to hunger.

**Statement One — Harlan Voss, son of the deceased.**

> *"I last saw my father at approximately nine-fifteen in the evening, on the dock behind the boathouse. He said he wished to be alone. At approximately ten o'clock I observed his rowboat adrift on the lake. I searched the shoreline until midnight. The authorities were notified at approximately six the following morning."*

**Statement Two — Aldous Pike Sr., family friend.**

> *"I departed the Voss property at approximately nine-thirty. As I drove past the boathouse I observed Mr. Voss standing on the dock. This would have been approximately nine-forty-five. I did not see him again."*

**Statement Three — Margaret Cole, housekeeper.**

> *"Mr. Voss took his supper at eight. He seemed in good spirits. He told me he was going down to the water to think. I heard the boat go out around nine-thirty. In the morning the boat was found empty near the reeds."*

**Statement Four — Dr. Edwin Marsh, attending physician.**

> *"I was summoned at approximately six-fifteen the following morning. I examined the shoreline and pronounced the death consistent with accidental drowning. Mr. Voss had been in declining health; I consider the outcome unsurprising."*

**Statement Five — Ruth Ellery, neighbor.**

> *"I saw the light in the boathouse burning late, as it often did. I retired at ten. I heard nothing unusual in the night."*

Five statements. Five witnesses. And every single one of them — Mirabel checked twice, then a third time with a ruler held against the page like some kind of madman — signed before the same notary. T. Grieves, Notary Public. Between 8:02 and 9:11 in the morning, the day after the drowning.

Within seventy minutes, five separate people had walked into the same office and given statements that dovetailed like carpentry.

He sat back. The bare bulb overhead buzzed. Somewhere above him, the town went about its morning, buying coffee, gossiping about the empty coffin, saying the name Voss with the careful hush people reserve for either saints or scandals.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that made the hunger curdle into something colder.

The statements didn't just agree. They agreed *uselessly*. Each witness supplied exactly one detail — Harlan saw the boat adrift; Pike Sr. saw Voss alive at 9:45; Margaret heard the boat launch; Marsh confirmed the body; Ruth confirmed the light. No overlaps. No contradictions. No two witnesses placing themselves in the same place at the same time, which is what honest witnesses do, messily, inevitably, because reality is redundant and lies are efficient.

Real testimony is a tangled net. This was five clean threads, each cut to length.

And they'd all been taken within the same hour — not gathered over days, as grief-stricken witnesses are, but produced overnight, polished, and stamped. Somebody had written these statements. All five of them. Probably sitting at one desk, probably before dawn, while the lake lay flat and gray and a legend was being born.

Mirabel pulled the coroner's report itself. Cause of death: drowning. Manner: accidental. Basis of finding: *witness statements and circumstances.* No body had ever been recovered — noted, almost apologetically, in a single line. *Presumed whole remains unrecovered; interment conducted per family request.*

Per family request. They'd buried an empty coffin and the county had signed off on it because the family asked nicely and the family was rich.

He copied everything. He photographed everything. Then he sat for a long moment with the ledger page from the coffin — the rotted scrap he now carried in a plastic sleeve like a relic — and set it beside the statements.

The handwriting on the ledger fragment was cramped, hurried, left-slanting. The handwriting on the witness statements was five different hands, but the *phrasing* was one voice. *Approximately.* Everyone said *approximately.* Five people, one verbal tic.

Somebody had dictated the truth's replacement, and five people had memorized their lines.

* * *

He worked through the afternoon in the archive's reading room, cross-referencing the *Gazette*. The coverage was loving and immediate — the special edition came out two days after the drowning, faster than any small paper had any business printing, full of elegiac prose about the founder "taken by the waters he loved." There was a photograph of the memorial service: black-clad mourners, bowed heads, the five witnesses arranged in the front row like a jury foregone.

Mirabel studied the faces. Harlan Voss, thirty years old, jaw tight, eyes not on the coffin but on the crowd — counting it, maybe, or checking for gaps. Pike Sr. beside him, hand on the young man's shoulder, wearing the expression of a stage manager watching his production open. Margaret Cole stared straight ahead with a face like a locked door. Marsh looked ill. Ruth Ellery looked, if anything, frightened.

Frightened. At her friend's funeral. Mirabel marked the photo with a slip of paper and moved on.

The fire department log for that autumn made him stop breathing for a moment.

Three weeks before the drowning, a structure fire at the Okafor property on Mill Road. Two entries. First entry: response, suppression, casualties — *one adult female, deceased.* Second entry, dated eleven days later: *re-inspection at owner's request. Origin determined accidental (wiring). File closed at request of County Assessor's office.*

At the request of the assessor's office. Fire investigations don't close at the request of assessors. Fire investigations close when they're finished, or when somebody pays for them to finish.

Edmund Voss hadn't simply drowned. Edmund Voss had run — from something, toward something — and three weeks after a woman burned to death in a house whose investigation was strangled in its crib, the great man walked onto a dock and walked out of history.

Mirabel wrote the connection in his notebook in his smallest, most controlled hand, because when his hands wanted to shake he made them write smaller instead.

*Voss → fire → Okafor → closed file → "drowning" → five statements → one author.*

Then he underlined *one author* twice, and a third time for luck.

* * *

It was fully dark when he left the mill. The parking lot behind the building held three cars: his rental, Denny's hatchback, and nothing else. The streetlights along Water Street threw orange cones of light that didn't quite reach each other, so that walking to his car meant passing through little pockets of darkness, like swimming between islands.

He noticed the passenger door unlocked before he noticed anything else. He always locked it. Twenty-two years of habit; he locked rental cars the way other men checked for wallets.

On the driver's seat, centered with a precision that was somehow worse than disorder, lay a folded piece of cream stationery.

He didn't touch it immediately. He stood in the cold and looked at it through the glass, and he was aware — distantly, academically — that his heart rate had climbed and that his mouth had gone dry, and he catalogued these facts the way he catalogued everything, because cataloguing was how he kept the world at arm's length.

Then he opened the door, picked up the note with a handkerchief, and read it under the dome light.

> *Mr. Hartwell —*
>
> *Grayhollow has buried its dead twice now. We would prefer not to do it a third time.*
>
> *Some foundations hold towns up. Undermine them and everyone living in the house goes down together. Ask yourself whether you came here to serve an estate or to burn one.*
>
> *There is still time to file your report and go home.*
>
> *— A friend of the town*

No signature. Cream stock, expensive. The penmanship was confident, old-fashioned, the loops of a man educated by tutors or by very good schools.

Mirabel stood there a long moment, the note trembling very slightly in the handkerchiefed grip, and examined himself with the same ruthless attention he gave documents.

He expected to feel fear. He identified something adjacent to it, but not the same — a hot, singing brightness behind the sternum, closer to what he imagined gamblers felt, or detectives in novels he found unrealistic. *They've noticed me,* he thought. *Which means I'm close. Which means somewhere in those five tidy statements is the thread that, pulled hard enough, unravels the whole sweater.*

And underneath that, quieter, less welcome: *someone drove to the archive lot tonight, knowing which car was mine, knowing I'd walk out alone in the dark.* Which meant he wasn't hunting alone anymore. The prey had turned its head.

He photographed the note where it lay, bagged it, and drove back to the Hartwell House Inn with his headlights carving the fog off the lake, running the sentence over and over like a stone in the shoe:

*Buried its dead twice.*

Twice. As if the empty coffin were only the second burial. As if there had been another one, somewhere, that nobody had ever counted.

He parked, sat in the ticking-cool silence of the rental, and made himself finish the thought, because finishing thoughts was the only discipline he'd never broken.

If Voss never drowned — if the coffin had always been empty — then somewhere, sometime, Edmund Voss had actually died. Really died. And someone had buried him.

And whoever wrote tonight's note knew exactly where.

In his room, Mirabel pinned the five statements to the wall above the desk, connected them with red thread like a conspiracy corkboard he'd always considered a cliché, and stood looking at the center of the web — the blank space where the sixth statement should have been.

The one from the person who wasn't a witness at all.

The one from whoever had actually seen Edmund Voss last — not on a dock, not in a boat, but wherever the man had really gone, and whatever had really happened to him there.

Somewhere in Grayhollow, that person had signed nothing, said nothing, and stayed silent for thirty years.

Tomorrow, Mirabel decided, he would find out who kept this town's secrets on paper. Every town had one. A keeper of records. A quiet room where the true ledger pages ended up.

He fell asleep with the lamp still burning and the red thread casting thin shadows like cracks across the wall.
