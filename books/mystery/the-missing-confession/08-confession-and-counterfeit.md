## 8. Confession and Counterfeit

**Mirabel**

The dictaphone tape had been June's idea — the archive's oldest machine, its reel still turning in the dark after Pike left with his threats hanging in the air like smoke. But it was the drawer that mattered now.

After the small fire, after the fire marshal's report that blamed "faulty wiring" in a building with no faulty wiring in forty years, June had finally opened the locked drawer she'd guarded since before I knew her name meant anything.

She did it at my kitchen table, not in the archive. "If they're going to burn something," she said, "let them burn the wrong room."

Inside: a oilcloth packet, a leather ledger swollen with damp, and an envelope sealed with wax gone the color of old blood. The initials pressed into the wax were E.V.

"Edmund Voss," I said.

"Edmund Voss," June agreed. "Written over eleven years. He couldn't say it all at once. Guilt comes out of a man like water through limestone — slow, and along strange channels."

I broke the seal with a letter opener older than both of us, and Edmund Voss began to speak from beyond a grave that had never held him.

* * *

*June*

I watched Mirabel read. I'd imagined this moment for thirty years — rehearsed it in the archive's back room, in the dark, in the years when I still believed someone would come who couldn't be bought. In my imagination the reader was always taller, always more certain, always less afraid than the man hunched over my table with his sleeves rolled up and his hands not quite steady.

He read aloud, because he said he needed to hear it to believe it.

*"I did not drown on the ninth of October. I stood in the trees above the river and watched five people I trusted swear me into death, and I let them, because the alternative was the gallows. The fire on Marsh Lane was mine. My lamp, my carelessness, my cowardice when I ran instead of going back in. A woman died whose name I will write here every day until my hand fails: Adaeze Okafor."*

My mother's name. Spoken in a stranger's kitchen by a stranger's voice, thirty-one years late.

Mirabel looked up at me. He didn't say he was sorry. Everyone says sorry; sorry is the cheapest word in any language. Instead he kept reading, and I understood that this was his version of respect — the work itself, done properly, all the way through.

*"June Okafor survived. She was six years old. I have watched her from a distance I did not deserve. When she took the archivist's post I nearly wrote to her a hundred times. I am writing instead what she may one day need: everything. Let this stand as my confession, and let whoever holds it judge whether I am worth the mercy of being remembered truly."*

"He never sent it," I said. "He never gave it to me. I found it after — after the boathouse."

"After someone killed him," Mirabel said quietly.

Yes. After that.

* * *

*Mirabel*

The confession ran to ninety pages, and by page forty I understood that Edmund Voss had been a better penitent than a man. He named the Five. He named the notary. He described, in damning detail, how the alibi had been constructed — each witness coached within an inch of their lives, statements signed in a single sitting at Hollis & Crane's office while Voss's hat and coat dried by the stove.

But the confession ended in 1989. And the second will was dated 1994.

"The will isn't his," June said. She'd gone very still. "Read the last entry again."

*"Someone knows I am alive. Someone has always known. I begin to think the fire was not the worst thing I did — trusting these people was."*

I spread the second will beside the confession and made myself look at it the way I'd looked at a thousand documents across a thousand conference tables. The paper was right for 1994. The ink was right. But handwriting is a biography, and a dying man's hand tells the truth about him even when his mouth lies.

Voss's confession was written in a hand that deteriorated year over year — the letters tightening, then loosening, a tremor arriving around 1991 like an unwelcome guest. The will's signature was firm. Confident. A dead-steady line under a name belonging to a man whose hands shook too badly to button his own coat by then.

"It's forged," I said. "Traced, probably, from an earlier signature. And the beneficiary clause—" I read it again. The estate, in perpetuity, to be administered by "the surviving trustees of the Grayhollow Compact." Not a person. A structure. A machine for holding the town's wealth forever, no matter who lived or died.

"One of the Five," June said. "One of them killed him and used his death to lock the estate closed."

"Not one of them." I set down the page. "Look at the trustee language. It mirrors the Compact's founding charter almost verbatim — but there are two phrases lifted from documents only one family would have had access to. Aldous Pike's father, Marcus Pike, was the Compact's solicitor. He drafted the original charter. He notarized the alibi statements."

June's face went gray. "And Aldous inherited the practice. The files. Everything."

"And the job." I thought of Pike in his beautiful office, offering me money with the ease of a man pouring coffee. *Finish what our fathers started, Mirabel. That's all this has ever been.* I'd taken it as rhetoric. It was a job description.

Marcus Pike had discovered Voss alive — perhaps blackmailed him, perhaps simply decided a living founder was a liability to the legend that paid all their bills. He'd forged the will, killed Voss sometime around 1994, buried him under the boathouse floor where the man had actually been living those last hidden years, and let the empty coffin keep its secret. And when Marcus died, Aldous hadn't learned the truth. He'd learned the *arrangement*. He'd spent twenty years maintaining it — the threatening notes, the archive fire, the pressure on anyone who pulled a thread.

"He'll know we have this," June said.

"He already knows we have something. That's why he came to your archive with his little speech about civic stability." I gathered the pages with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be. "What he doesn't know is that we can prove which parts are true and which parts are his father's forgery. The confession convicts the legend. The handwriting convicts the Pikes. Together—"

"Together we end Grayhollow as it exists." June said it without flinching. I was beginning to learn that flinching wasn't in her.

"There's a cost," I said. "Testifying means putting yourself in a courtroom in front of the last man standing between this town and its truth. It means your name in every paper. It means—" I stopped, because the next part was mine to carry, not hers. "It means I can't protect you the way I'd want to. The law protects process, not people. I've known that my whole career and pretended otherwise."

June reached across the table and turned the confession to its first page. To her mother's name, written daily by a guilty man until his hand failed.

"I've been testifying my whole life," she said. "Just never out loud. Give me the pen."

* * *

*June*

We worked until three in the morning. Mirabel built the chain of custody like a man laying bricks — photographing every page, logging every timestamp, calling a forensic document examiner in the state capital who owed him a favor and waking her up to collect on it. I watched him work and understood something I'd gotten wrong about him since the day he walked into my archive with his careful questions and his lawyer's patience.

I'd thought he wanted the truth the way collectors want rare things. To own. To close a case and shelve it.

But he handled my mother's name like it was live current. He read Voss's description of the fire twice and then had to get up and stand at the window for a minute, and when he came back he didn't apologize or explain. He just sat down and kept working. That's when I decided.

"Aldous will come for the originals," I told him. "Not copies. He needs the confession gone entirely, because the handwriting analysis only works if the source survives. Burn the confession and the will stands unchallenged forever."

"So we give him somewhere to come."

Mirabel smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a man who had lost cases before — small ones, early ones — and had learned exactly how much losing could teach you.

"The archive has a second copy room," I said. "Behind the map cabinets. Fireproof door, 1962 construction, the kind of overbuilding they don't do anymore. We put the real confession there. We put a decoy in the main room — aged paper, facsimile ink, good enough in the dark and in a panic."

"And the dictaphone stays running."

"The dictaphone stays running."

He looked at me across the table, at the wreckage of cold tea and photocopied pages, and asked the question gently, the way you ask someone if they're sure they want to jump.

"June. Once he takes the bait, there's no walking it back. You'll be the woman who brought down the founding families. Some people in this town will never forgive you for it."

"Some people in this town burned my house down when I was six," I said. "I've been waiting thirty-one years to forgive *them*. It hasn't happened yet. I'm done waiting."

Outside, far off toward the river, a car slowed on the road past the archive — slowed, and idled, and moved on. We both heard it. Neither of us spoke.

Then my phone lit up on the table. Unknown number. One line of text:

*You should ask Mr. Hartwell what happened to the last person who helped him.*

I showed Mirabel. He read it, and I watched him arrive at the same place I already was — not fear, exactly. Something colder and more useful.

"He's not warning us off," Mirabel said. "He's telling us the clock started tonight."

* * *

**Mirabel**

At dawn I drove past the archive and saw what I expected: nothing. No broken glass, no ash. Pike was patient. He'd waited twenty years; he'd wait another week for the right night, the right weather, the right absence of witnesses.

Which meant we had a week to make sure the right night never came — or that when it did, everything he touched recorded him doing it.

I called the forensic examiner back. I called the dementia ward at St. Ambrose, where Ruth Calloway still murmured about the man in the boathouse to anyone who'd listen. I called a locksmith I trusted more than most judges.

And underneath it all, one thought kept surfacing like a body in deep water:

Ruth had seen the man in the boathouse. Alive, near the end. Which meant someone else had seen him too — someone who'd needed him dead before Ruth's babbling ever found the right ears.

Pike's father was dead. But Pike had grown up in a house where the boathouse was a thing you didn't walk past after dark.

He'd known where the bones were because he'd helped put them there.

I turned the car toward St. Ambrose. If Ruth Calloway had one more fragment left in her, I intended to hear it before Aldous Pike decided she'd said enough.
