## 9. Elena's Hands

The missal had come in from a parish two counties over, and it was dying.

Elena could tell that from across the workbench—the spine slumped like an old man's shoulders, the boards warped with damp, the leather gone the color of weak tea. Someone had loved it once and then stopped, and then someone else had stored it badly for forty years, and now it was her problem, which meant it was her privilege, because there was no difference between those two things if you did this work long enough.

She tied back her hair, put on the cotton gloves, and opened the first aid kit of her trade: bone folder, wheat starch paste, Japanese tissue thin as breath. The shop was quiet at this hour. Morning light came through the cathedral-facing window in a long gold bar that moved, over the course of a day, from her left elbow to her right, so that she could tell time by where it fell on her hands. She liked that. She liked work that kept time honestly.

Her name—her current name, the one on the lease and the tax forms and the little brass plate by the door—was Elena Ruiz. Bookbinder. Eleven years at Saint Aldhelm's Cathedral shop. Quiet woman, keeps to herself, excellent with her hands. That was the whole biography anyone had ever needed from her, and she had made sure of it, because a biography is a kind of binding too, and hers had been constructed to hold.

She opened the missal to its broken point, somewhere past the Canon of the Mass, and eased the text block free of the boards with the patience of someone defusing something. Dust rose, sweet and papery. She breathed it in. Old books smelled like every hand that had ever held them, and she had always found that comforting—a book could not lie about being touched.

The flower fell out at folio 112.

It dropped onto the felt of her bench with no sound at all, and she stared at it for a full ten seconds before she let herself understand what she was looking at. A pressed flower. Small, five-petaled, white gone ivory gone brown at the edges, flattened to a whisper of itself. It had been placed deliberately—not lost, not slipped in by accident between pages, but *placed*, centered on the page, stem pointing down toward the gutter of the book like an arrow.

Folio 112. She read the rubricated heading without wanting to. *Of the Burial of the Dead.* And beneath it, in the old ordering of the mass, the prayers said when the body is carried down—and the page's marginal note, added by some long-dead hand in faded iron-gall ink: *the crypt below the north stair.*

Elena did not move for a long moment. Her own heartbeat sounded loud in the quiet shop, absurdly loud, like a knock at a door.

She knew whose flower this was.

Not how she knew—there was no label, no signature, flowers don't carry names—but she knew the way you know a voice on the phone after eleven years of silence. Marisol Ferro used to press flowers in the cathedral missals. It was a game she played, a small rebellion, tucking them into the service books she was supposed to be polishing, marking pages that meant something to her, and Elena had found three of them over the years they'd known each other and never told anyone, because telling anyone would have meant explaining why two girls who barely shared a language were leaving each other messages in prayer books.

Marisol spoke Portuguese; Elena, then, had spoken Spanish, and their common tongue was English, broken on both sides, and they had been seventeen and nineteen respectively and had communicated mostly through objects. A ribbon. A chocolate wrapper folded into a crane. Flowers pressed flat in books, marking pages, meaning *I saw this, I thought of you, we are both here.*

This one marked the crypt.

*The crypt below the north stair.* The sealed crypt. The one that had been shut since before Elena arrived, the one nobody talked about except as a fire story—*there was a fire, years ago, nothing valuable lost, better sealed*—the one that had been closed up tight the year before Marisol walked out of the cathedral grounds on a Tuesday evening in October and never came back.

Eleven years. Elena had counted them the way other people count money or sins.

She looked at the flower again and thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water: *someone has just found this book and sent it to me.* Because parish books didn't wander. The shop repaired what the diocese sent, and the diocese sent what the parishes surrendered, and this missal had come from a parish two counties over that would have no reason on earth to know what it was holding. Unless someone had routed it here. Unless someone wanted it in her hands.

Or unless it was coincidence, said the smaller, saner voice, the one she had trained like a guard dog for eleven years. Books traveled. Flowers stayed pressed for a century sometimes. Not everything was a message. Not everything was about you.

But her hands, above the bench, were not steady, and her hands were the one part of herself she trusted completely, so she paid attention to them.

She lifted the flower with tweezers and set it on acid-free tissue, and she did not throw it away, and she noted, with distant interest, that she could not have thrown it away any more than she could have thrown away a finger.

* * *

The bell above the shop door rang at half past eleven.

Elena knew the sound of every regular: the postman's shove, Mrs. Okafor's careful push, the students who flung it like they were robbing the place. This was none of those. This was the soft, unhurried ring of a man who has never needed to hurry anywhere in his life, and she knew before she turned around who it would be, and she was right, and knowing did not help.

Edmund Locke stood in the doorway with light behind him, and even after eleven years of living in the close, even after all the cassocks and the retirements and the slow silvering of him, he still looked faintly like a photograph of a verger rather than a man. Tall, straight-backed, white hair combed with geometric precision, cardigan the color of oatmeal. His face arranged itself around warmth the way other faces arranged themselves around weather.

"Elena." He said her name correctly, gently, the way he always did, and she hated that she noticed, every single time, that he pronounced it perfectly. He had learned. He had made a study of her name the way she had made a study of bindings. "Forgive the intrusion. I know you don't like to be disturbed at your bench."

"Mr. Locke." She set down the bone folder. "It's no trouble."

"It's always trouble," he said pleasantly, "which is why I try to make my troubles worth your while." He drifted along the shelves the way he always did, fingertips trailing the spines without quite touching them, a man conducting an orchestra only he could hear. "How is the missal?"

The question landed wrong. It landed wrong by a millimeter, the way a knife lands wrong when the person holding it knows exactly what they're doing. She had not mentioned the missal to anyone. The intake slip sat in the office drawer. There was no reason on earth for Edmund Locke to know what was open on her bench.

"The Missal," she said, keeping her voice level, keeping her accent where she had trained it to sit, in the middle distance between nowhere and nowhere. "It's a sad case. Damp damage. It'll take weeks."

"Weeks," he agreed, nodding, as though she'd confirmed something on a schedule he already possessed. He came to stand at the end of her bench, close enough to see the work, far enough to be polite, and his eyes went over the opened text block, the exposed sewing, the flower lying on its tissue—and paused there, just for a breath, just long enough.

"You found something in it," he said. Not a question.

"A pressed flower." She heard her own voice say it calmly and marveled, distantly, at the craftsmanship involved. "People do that. Tuck things into service books. I've found receipts, hair ribbons, once a whole letter from 1911." She smiled, small and professional. "It goes in the report as provenance."

"Mm." He picked up the tissue corner, delicately, with two fingers, and looked at the flower the way a jeweler looks at a stone he is deciding whether to buy. "White. Jasmine, isn't it? Or mock orange. They grow along the cloister wall." He set it down with the same care he'd taken it up. "The cloister wall was Marisol Ferro's favorite spot, I'm told. She used to sit there on her breaks."

And there it was. The name, laid on the bench between them like a scalpel on a tray.

Elena had rehearsed this moment for eleven years. Not consciously—she had never allowed herself to sit down and *practice*, because practicing felt like admitting—but it had happened anyway, in the shower, on the bus, in the gray minutes before sleep. *What will you do when someone says her name to you? What will your face do?* And the answer, drilled in by repetition, was: *nothing. Your face will do nothing. You are a woman hearing about a stranger.*

"That poor girl," Locke said softly. "You remember. You were working here, weren't you, when it happened?"

"I started the following spring," Elena said. Which was true. Which was the truth she had been given, wrapped and labeled and certified by people whose business was making truths. "Everyone talked about it, of course. Terrible thing. But I didn't know her."

"No?" He tilted his head, and the light moved on his face, and for one unguarded instant—half a second, maybe less—something looked out of his eyes that was not warm at all. It wasn't menace, exactly. It was more patient than menace. It was the look of a man checking a lock he installed himself. "I thought perhaps you had. You were both young women in the same building. Girls notice each other."

"We passed in corridors." She turned back to the bench, took up the wheat starch paste, began to work a strip of repair tissue along a torn margin, letting her hands answer for her. Her hands were perfect. Her hands had never once betrayed her. "I'm sorry. I wish I could help. It must be strange, after all these years, having it all stirred up again."

"Strange," he echoed. "Yes. Strange is the word." He watched her work for a moment in silence, and the silence had texture, had weight, had a blade in it somewhere that she couldn't see but could feel against her skin. "They've brought in an archivist, you know. From outside. Vasquez. Very thorough man, by all accounts. Thoroughness is a virtue, I suppose. In archives." A pause, measured to the millimeter. "Though I find thorough men often mistake noise for signal. They dig everywhere, and so they find everything, and none of it means anything."

"Some of it means something," Elena said, to the missal, to the paste, to the page about burial.

"Oh, undoubtedly. Some of it." He smiled, and she caught it in the window glass, the reflection of it, warm and paternal and absolutely false. "Well. I won't keep you. The missal is in the best hands in the county." He moved toward the door, unhurried, and then stopped with his hand on the frame, and did not turn around, and said, in the same pleasant tone:

"They're saying now she may have kept a diary. Isn't that remarkable? A whole interior life, written down, and us none the wiser all these years." He tapped the doorframe twice, lightly, a man knocking on wood out of habit rather than superstition. "If you ever come across anything like that—anything at all—you'd tell someone. Wouldn't you, Elena? It's what she would have wanted. The truth coming out. After so long."

He left before she could answer, which was its own kind of courtesy, and the bell rang behind him, soft and unhurried, and the shop was quiet again.

* * *

She sat at the bench for a long time without moving.

Then she locked the door, turned the sign, and went to the back room where she kept the materials, and knelt, and pulled out the third drawer, the one beneath the linen presses, and lifted out the false bottom she had built into it herself, with her own hands, nine years ago, in a single night of work so careful that even she could hardly find the seam.

Inside was a letter. Unopened. Addressed in handwriting she had not seen in eleven years and could still draw from memory with her eyes closed, because she had spent the first two years drawing it from memory with her eyes closed, every night, until the doctors at the clinic suggested this was not helping.

*Elena* — nothing else on the envelope, no surname, because Marisol hadn't known her real surname either. Nobody had. That had been the entire point.

She held it over the sink and burned it there, finally, eleven years late, because a man with oatmeal-colored cardigans and perfect pronunciation had just stood at her bench and told her, in the language of courtesies, that the bargain was being audited. *If you ever come across anything like that—anything at all—you'd tell someone.* Meaning: I know what you have. Meaning: I have always known what you have. Meaning: the price of your name is going up.

The paper caught and curled and went orange and then black, and she ran the tap, and washed the ash down, and stood with her wet hands braced on the sink edge, breathing.

She thought about the night it had all been decided, the night she barely let herself think about, the rectory study smelling of beeswax and brandy, the man from the diocese sliding papers across the desk—new name, new history, new references, a whole self manufactured inside the cathedral's own machinery—and Father Anselm's tired voice saying *you saw nothing, you heard nothing, and in exchange you get to exist,* and her own nineteen-year-old hand signing, because grief makes cowards of everyone and she had been grieving and terrified and seventeen-going-on-eighteen and alone in a country that was not hers.

*In exchange you get to exist.* Eleven years of existing. A shop, a craft, a name stitched together so well that even she sometimes forgot there had been another one underneath.

The flower lay on its tissue on the bench in the next room, marking nothing now, waiting.

Elena dried her hands. She thought about Marisol pressing jasmine from the cloister wall into a missal, marking the page about burial, marking the crypt, sending a message forward through eleven years to whoever was careful enough to find it—because that was what Marisol did, that was what Marisol had always done, she left messages in objects for the one person she believed would look.

And she thought about Silas Vasquez, the thorough man from outside, digging everywhere, finding everything, and about the diary, and about the fact that Edmund Locke had known about the missal before she'd said a word, which meant Locke was watching the shipments, which meant Locke was watching *her*, which meant the clock she had pretended for eleven years wasn't running was running.

There was a copy machine in the office. There was a diary in an evidence bag somewhere in this city with entries that kept writing after the girl was gone.

Elena untied her apron, hung it on its hook, and stood in the doorway of her small, carefully constructed life, listening to the cathedral bells begin to ring noon.

She had practiced lying for eleven years. She was very good at it.

She was about to find out whether she remembered how to tell the truth.
