## 17. Below the Cathedral

**Silas**

The crypt door had three locks, and Elena had the keys to all of them.

She stood at the top of the stair with the ring in her hand, not turning it. The passage behind us was black as a closed book. Above us, through the floor of the ambulatory, the cathedral breathed its night sounds—timber settling, stone drinking the damp, the great clock in the tower ticking off the minutes like a patient accountant.

"Silas." Her voice was barely a sound at all. "Once we go down, there's no version of this where we come back up the same."

"I know."

"Do you?" She looked at me then, and in the dark her face was mostly eyes. "Because I've been down here before. Eleven years ago. And I came back up and I was someone else for the rest of my life. That's what it costs to know what's under this building. I'm asking you one last time if you understand the exchange rate."

I thought about my archive upstairs. Every case catalogued, every file closed, every truth shelved in its proper place where anyone could find it if they cared to look. I thought about how much of what I'd shelved over eleven years had been arranged by other hands—Locke's hands, diocesan hands—and how the order I'd been so proud of was itself a kind of lie.

"I understand," I said. "Open it."

The first lock turned with a click that seemed to travel down the stair ahead of us, announcing our arrival. The second lock was older, iron, and fought her. The third was new—a padlock, brass, installed within the last year. Elena went very still when she saw it.

"That wasn't here in March," she whispered.

No. It hadn't been. Which meant Locke had been maintaining his crypt the way he maintained everything else: quietly, faithfully, without ever being asked.

Elena's hands shook on the hasp. I put mine over hers—not to steady her, exactly, but because I couldn't think of anything else to do with them—and together we pulled it open. The padlock swung free like a small hanged thing.

Cold air rose out of the stairwell. It smelled of stone and standing water and something else beneath both, something organic and old, the smell of a place that had been holding its breath for a decade.

"Flashlights," I said. "Stay behind me."

"You don't know the way."

"Then beside me."

* * *

**Elena**

Eleven years ago I had come down these stairs carrying a lantern and a lie, and Edmund Locke had walked behind me the whole way, humming Vespers under his breath, and I had believed—I had needed to believe—that whatever waited below was only dust and forgotten coffins.

Now I came down carrying a flashlight and the truth, and nobody was humming.

The stairs were narrow, cut into the living rock under the north transept, and they turned twice before they let us out into the dark. My flashlight found the walls first: niches, dozens of them, some still holding their lead coffins, some empty, their occupants moved or lost or—my stomach turned—never buried at all. Water beaded on the stone and ran in slow channels toward a drain somewhere we couldn't see. The air was ten degrees colder than the church above, and it moved against my face like something exhaling.

Silas swept his light across the floor. Flagstones, cracked, furred with moss near the far wall where moisture gathered. And then his light stopped moving.

"Elena."

I knew before I raised my own beam. Some part of me had known since the news story ran, since I read Marisol's name in print for the first time in eleven years and felt the bargain start to rot inside me. I had known, and I had burned the letter anyway, and now the knowing was going to be made into a thing with bones.

She lay in an alcove at the east end of the crypt—the oldest part, the part the fire report had never mentioned. Not in a coffin. Not thrown. Arranged.

Her remains had been laid out with terrible care: arms folded across the chest, head resting on a folded cloth gone grey with age, feet aligned, knees straightened. Someone had spent hours down here in the dark doing this. Someone had come back, year after year, to keep the arrangement true—to brush away the dust, to replace the cloth when it rotted. There were fresh flowers at her head. White ones. Not yet wilted.

"Oh God," I said. "Oh God, she's been tended."

Silas crouched at the edge of the alcove. He didn't touch anything. His flashlight hand was steady; his voice wasn't. "This isn't concealment. This is—" He stopped. Started again. "He kept her. Like a reliquary."

"She was nineteen," I said, though he knew it, though everyone knew it. It seemed important to say out loud, in this room, where no one had said it in eleven years. "She used to sing in the volunteer choir even though she couldn't carry a tune. She laughed too loud during the quiet parts of services. Father Anselm told her twice to leave and she came back the next Sunday and he gave up." My voice was doing something strange, climbing and breaking at once. "She wanted to be an archivist. Did you know that? She asked me once how you get a job like yours."

Silas looked at me. In the flashlight light his face was wet.

"No," he said quietly. "I didn't know that."

And then his beam slid past the alcove, past Marisol, and stopped on the second shape.

* * *

**Silas**

There was a second skeleton.

It lay deeper in the alcove, further back, half-hidden by a shelf of rock—as if whoever had arranged Marisol with such devotion had given this one less ceremony, or perhaps had simply run out of room. Older. Much older. The bones had the yellowed density of things that had been in the ground—or out of it—for decades. The skull was intact. There was a lead coffin lid propped against the wall behind it, pried loose, its inscription corroded but legible where my light caught it:

*ANNA MARIA RUIZ DE LA CRUZ · 1931–1968*

Ruiz.

I heard Elena make a sound behind me that wasn't quite a word.

"Read it again," she said.

I read it again. "Anna Maria Ruiz de la Cruz. Died 1968."

"My name," Elena said. "My real name is Ana Ruiz. He gave me—" Her flashlight beam wavered against the wall. "He built my false identity out of a dead woman from his own crypt. A woman who died down here fifty years ago. He didn't invent a person for me. He recycled one."

The cold in the crypt got suddenly much colder, or I did. Because I understood what I was looking at, and understanding arrived the way bad news always does—all at once, already complete. Anna Maria Ruiz de la Cruz had died in this crypt in 1968, and no death record existed, and no burial record, and the crypt had been sealed after a fire that never happened, and Edmund Locke had been a young verger then, twenty-two years old, learning the trade.

Marisol Ferro had vanished in 2014. Fifty-six years later. Same alcove. Same careful hands.

"This wasn't twice," I said. "This was a practice."

Behind the second skeleton, wedged into a niche where the rock shelved inward, sat a wooden box. It was plain, unvarnished, the kind of box a carpenter makes for himself—but the wood was dark with handling, with decades of being taken down and put back. A brass plate on the lid read, in engraved letters gone soft with age: *THE RECORDS.*

"Don't," Elena said.

I opened it.

* * *

**Elena**

He opened it, because of course he did. He was the archivist. The box was labeled. I could have told him what was inside and saved him the minute of hope that it might be something else, but some part of me wanted him to see it raw, the way I'd seen it at nineteen, kneeling on this same floor while Edmund Locke held the lantern and explained, in his gentle voice, what loyalty required.

Folders. Dozens of them, tied with cotton tape, each one labeled in the same neat ecclesiastical hand I would come to know as well as my own. Dates going back before I was born. I watched Silas lift the first folder into his light, and I watched his face do what faces do when the ground moves.

"What is this," he said, though it wasn't a question.

"Deaths," I said. "All of them. Everyone who ever died down here, or disappeared from here, or had to be dealt with. He keeps records, Silas. That's the worst part—he's just like you. Everything documented. Dates, names, circumstances. He calls it stewardship." I heard my own voice go flat and distant, the way it went sometimes when the eleven years pressed too hard. "He showed me the whole box the night he gave me my new name. He said, *the cathedral cannot survive on innocence, Elena. Someone must carry the weight, and the record must be kept, or the sacrifice means nothing.* He cried a little when he said it. That's the part I've never managed to tell anyone. He cried."

Silas had the folders spread now, careful as communion, reading by flashlight. I heard him breathe names. 1968. 1974. Two entries in the eighties. One in 1996—a groundskeeper, *fell*, the word in quotation marks in Silas's low murmur, because Silas could hear the irony across thirty years. And then 2014, and the folder with MARISSA FERRO written on the tab in fresh ink, and Silas went silent for a long time.

When he spoke again his voice had changed. It had the quality I'd started to recognize in him over these weeks—the archivist's voice, the one that came out when feeling got too large to hold any other way.

"There's a letter in here. Dated three days after she disappeared. In Locke's hand. It says—" He stopped. Read it again, silently. Then aloud, flat, letting the words do their own damage: "*The girl understood too much and too late. She has been received into the greater keeping of the church. May God forgive the necessity.*"

"The greater keeping," I repeated.

"He believes it." Silas set the letter down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you handle something that might detonate. "That's what I couldn't see until tonight. All those interviews, all that charm—he wasn't performing. He means every word. He thinks he's the faithful one. He thinks we're the ones betraying the cathedral by dragging this into the light."

"Does it matter? What he thinks?"

"It matters," Silas said, "because men who believe they're protecting something don't run. They defend."

Above us—faint, filtered through six feet of stone and the flagstones of the transept—came a sound.

We both froze.

It might have been the building settling. Cathedrals settle; I'd lived inside this one's sounds for eleven years and knew most of them. But this was footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. Crossing the ambulatory above us, moving from the north side toward the transept steps, and then stopping directly overhead, in the exact spot where the sealed door began its descent.

Silas clicked off his flashlight. So did I. The dark came down on us like a lid, total, pressurized, filled with the sound of our own breathing and the smell of the box of records open between us like a wound.

The footsteps didn't move on.

After a long moment, from above, muffled almost to nothing but unmistakable—unmistakable because I had heard it every Sunday of my childhood and every working day of my stolen life—came a voice, pitched low, warm, patient, the voice of a man who had all the time in the world and knew exactly who was listening:

"Silas. Elena. I'd hoped we might do this together."

A pause. The soft metallic sound of something heavy being set down on stone.

"But I see you've found the records. So perhaps it's better this way. Stay where you are. I'll be down in a moment—and then we can finally talk about what loyalty costs."

The bolt of the outer door began to turn.
