## 2. Fifteen Years of Ash

The dead man's name was Emil Brandt, and he had been paid to die before he ever did.

Gideon found the first thread of it on Tuesday morning, in a rented office two blocks from the county records annex, with a cup of coffee gone cold at his elbow and three years of Brandt's financial life spread across the desk like an autopsy. He worked under a name that wasn't his—Gideon always worked under names that weren't his—but the work itself was honest in a way almost nothing else was. Numbers didn't lie. They only waited.

Brandt had been a mid-tier accountant at Corrigan & Lowe, salary modest, debts modest, a life so deliberately unremarkable that Gideon might have skimmed past it if he hadn't been looking for the reason someone had dressed a corpse in his clothes.

There. A consulting fee, four hundred dollars a month, paid by a company called Meridian Archival Services. The payments began fourteen months ago and stopped eleven days before Brandt drowned in his own bathtub wearing Gideon's charcoal overcoat.

Meridian Archival Services had no offices, no website, no employees. It had a registered agent, and the registered agent had a filing address, and the filing address was a mail drop six blocks from the Halvorsen warehouse.

Gideon sat very still.

The coffee cooled another degree. Outside the window, a delivery truck idled, its diesel rattle steady as a pulse. He read the line item again, then again, the way he read everything—once for content, once for structure, once to burn it into the place behind his eyes where nothing ever faded. Meridian. Four hundred dollars. Fourteen months. Five thousand six hundred dollars to buy an accountant's silence about something worth far more than that.

He knew what it was worth. He had stood in the smoke of it when he was nine years old.

* * *

The night of the fire came back to him the way it always did—not whole, but in pieces, like a photograph left too long in water.

His father's hand around his own, both of them walking home along the canal because the bus fare had gone up again and Tomas Reyes thought his son should learn the value of a dollar the hard way. October cold. The smell of the river and the tar paper roofs. And then the sky over the east side turned the color of an open furnace, and his father stopped walking, and said a word Gideon had never heard him say before or since.

Halvorsen Storage & Freight. His father had worked there eleven years, nights mostly, keeping the books in a ledger so old-fashioned it might have been written with a quill. Tomas Reyes trusted paper. He used to tell Gideon that numbers were the only honest witnesses, because they couldn't be threatened and they couldn't forget.

They watched the roof collapse from the footbridge. His father's grip went slack. There were other men on the bridge, Gideon remembered that much—two silhouettes upstream, standing too still, watching the fire the way you'd watch a clock rather than a catastrophe. One of them raised a hand, and Gideon had always believed, with the unshakable certainty of a child, that the gesture was a wave. A greeting. As if they knew him.

Then his father ran toward the building, and Gideon ran after, and hands pulled him back—strong hands, gloved—and a voice above him said, *Not this one. He's just a boy.* And then there was smoke, and there was his father not coming out, and there were fifteen years of investigators explaining that faulty wiring near the loading dock had started it all, case closed, condolences, next file please.

The wiring explanation had never fit. Gideon had known that at nine, in the wordless way children know things, and he knew it now with thirty years of forensic training stacked behind the knowing. Wiring doesn't start in four places at once. Wiring doesn't wait until the insurance policy is eleven days old.

But the memory had a hole in it, right at the center, where the important thing was. The faces on the bridge. The voice above him. He could see the glove, brown leather, a seam split at the thumb. He could not see the face. Fifteen years of turning the memory over and the face stayed dark, like a window in a burning building.

* * *

Marcus Vane poured the whiskey without asking, which was how Marcus did everything—decisively, generously, and always one step ahead of being refused.

"Emil Brandt," Marcus said, settling into the chair across from Gideon's kitchen table. Retirement had softened him at the edges but not at the center; his eyes were still the sharpest instruments in any room. "Drowning claim. You flagged it."

"I flagged the clothes." Gideon turned his glass but didn't drink. He had told Marcus about the coat, the wine glasses set out for a suicide that hadn't happened, the note in handwriting that aped his own slant-for-slant. He had told Marcus everything, because he had always told Marcus everything. That was the architecture of their twenty years: Marcus had taught him to see, and seeing had become the only prayer Gideon knew.

"And now?"

"Now I know why Brandt died. Or half of why." He slid the printout across the table. "Meridian Archival. Five thousand six hundred dollars in hush money, ending eleven days before he drowned. The company traces back to a mail drop near the Halvorsen site."

Marcus read the page the way Gideon had taught himself to read decades ago—slowly, twice, letting it sink. When he looked up, something had moved behind his expression, some weather front far offshore.

"Halvorsen," Marcus said quietly. "Christ, Gideon. That's your father's fire."

"You know it is."

"Everyone swore that case was closed."

"Everyone swore," Gideon said, "because everyone was told to swear. Wiring doesn't ignite in four seats of origin. The adjuster who signed off on it retired eight months later to a house he couldn't afford on a state salary. And the ledger—" He stopped. His voice had developed a hairline fracture. "My father kept the warehouse books by hand. Every shipment, every invoice, every payout. If anyone ordered that fire, my father wrote down the proof of what it would cost them. And the ledger disappeared from the evidence inventory between the second week and the fourth. Nobody flagged it. Nobody ever even mentioned it again."

Marcus set down the printout. For a long moment the only sound was the refrigerator humming its one dull note.

"You want to dig up a fifteen-year-old arson," Marcus said, "nine days before the statute closes, while somebody out there is dressing corpses in your wardrobe and forging your handwriting. You understand what that means. Whoever killed Brandt isn't framing you for a drowning. They're framing you for the fire. They're closing the last open door, and they're using you to close it."

"I know."

"They know how you think, Gideon. That note wasn't a forgery by some hired penman. It was *good*. It caught your habits—the way you cross your sevens, the way you press harder on downstrokes. That takes study. That takes intimacy."

Gideon looked at his mentor across the table—at the man who had taught him observation like a liturgy, who had shown him how to walk a scene, how to hold a detail up to the light until it confessed. Who had been the first person, fifteen years ago, to put a hand on his shoulder at the funeral and say, *I'll help you find the truth of this someday, son. When you're ready.*

"I need your help," Gideon said. "You knew the original investigators. You knew the adjuster. You can get me access to the microfiche, the bank records, whatever survived the purge."

Marcus was quiet for a moment. Then he nodded, slow and grave, and reached across the table to grip Gideon's forearm the way he had at the graveside, all those years ago.

"Whatever it takes," Marcus said. "We'll bury this properly this time. Together."

* * *

That night Gideon dreamed in fragments, which was rare for him. His waking mind filed everything; only his sleeping mind dared to lose things.

He dreamed of the footbridge. Of two figures upstream, still as fence posts against a burning sky. Of a raised hand. In the dream he finally saw the face of the man who had held him back, the gloved hand, the voice saying *not this one*—

And he woke at 3 a.m. with his heart slamming and the dream already dissolving, the face sliding back into its fifteen-year darkness, leaving behind only one detail, small and terrible and new:

In the dream, the man holding him back had been crying.

Gideon sat on the edge of the bed in the dark and made himself say it aloud, because spoken things became real things. "Someone was sorry."

Someone had stood in the smoke with a child in his grip and wept for what he'd done—or for what he hadn't been able to stop. Someone close enough to the fire to grieve it. Close enough, maybe, to know exactly which ledger had burned, and whose hands had written it, and why it could never be allowed to surface.

He got up, switched on the desk lamp, and laid out Brandt's payment schedule beside the old fire report he'd pulled from archive that afternoon. Fourteen months of silence, purchased monthly. Which meant somewhere in those fourteen months, Emil Brandt had found something—a copy, a fragment, a ghost of his father's ledger—and had tried to sell it back to the people who burned it.

Which meant the ledger existed. Or had existed, in pieces, long enough to kill for twice.

Gideon picked up his pen and began, methodically, to list every place a frightened accountant might hide five thousand dollars' worth of dead men's secrets.

Nine days. Somewhere out there, the rest of the ledger was waiting—and so was the man who knew his mind well enough to wear it like a stolen coat.
