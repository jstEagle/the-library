## 9. A Room Above the Chandlery

The room above the chandlery had one window, and the window had a view of nothing useful—unless you counted the cathedral's western flank, which rose above the rooftops like a grey cliff face, its buttresses braced against the sky as though holding up something heavier than stone.

Marguerite counted it. She counted it every morning and every evening, and twice more in the hours between, because it was the only thing she allowed herself to look at for longer than a glance. Everything else she took in sideways, in slivers, the way a person drinks when they suspect the well is poisoned.

From this window, at this hour—the blue-grey hour when the harbor lamps came on before the sky gave them permission—she could see the small door in the cathedral's north wall that led down to the archive. And she could see, if she leaned her forehead against the cold glass and looked almost straight down, the narrow lane where a man in a brown coat walked home each night with his collar turned up and his head bent, as though he were apologizing to the pavement for taking up space on it.

Sebastian Joubert. The archivist. The quiet one. The one nobody saw.

She had made him, eleven years ago, out of that same invisibility—a coat, a scarf, a pair of gloves lifted from a drying rack behind the archivist's quarters while he knelt in the crypt cataloguing bones. She had worn his clothes out of the city on a night bus with her hair cut short and her name left behind like a coat of her own, hanging empty in a locked closet that only he would ever open.

She had told herself it was practical. She had told herself a great many things.

Now she watched him walk that lane with his collar up, and she thought: *I am going to destroy this man, or he is going to save me, and I no longer know which of us is doing the using.*

* * *

His name was Étienne Marchal, and he had hands like tree roots.

Marguerite remembered that first—before the lantern, before the voices, before any of it—she remembered the hands. He had been repairing the mortar joints in the lower crypt wall, and she had come down with a tray of coffee for the workmen because that was what she did in those days: she carried things. Coffee, messages, other people's secrets. A woman who carried trays went everywhere and was nowhere, and Canon Aubry liked having such a woman near, the way a man likes a clock—he never looks at it until it stops.

Étienne had taken the cup from her with those root-heavy hands and said, *You've got cold fingers, madame,* and wrapped her fingers briefly around the warm ceramic, and laughed when she flinched. He was forty-something, broad, missing half a finger to an old accident with a chisel, and he whistled while he worked—old Breton tunes, off-key, unbothered by the holiness around him. He treated the crypt like a workshop and God like a foreman who'd gone home early.

She had liked him immediately. That was the part that still cut. Eleven years later, and the guilt had organized itself into layers like sediment, and the bottom layer—the oldest, the densest—was not that she had seen him die. It was that she had liked him first.

It was the ninth of November. She knew the date the way she knew her own pulse, because it was the date printed on the death certificate that had been issued for *her*, four years afterward, borrowed from a woman who had once been Marguerite Vasseur and was now, on paper, a drowned stonemason's widow. The church had needed a body to close a file. It had used her marriage. It had used her name. It had used the fact that no one living loved her enough to ask questions.

But on the ninth of November, none of that had happened yet. On the ninth of November she was only a woman carrying an empty tray back up through the crypt's under-passage, the one the old guides called the *gouffre*—the swallow-hole—a low brick channel that drained spring water toward the harbor and was supposed to be sealed but never quite was.

She heard the voices before she understood them.

Canon Aubry's voice she knew instantly; it had the particular smoothness of a man who has never once raised it in anger because anger is for people without power. The second voice was harder, wetter, frightened—and then she placed it, and her stomach dropped through the floor of her: Étienne. Whistling Étienne. Root-handed Étienne, who had warmed her fingers.

She should have kept walking. She had rehearsed this ten thousand times since—she had built whole alternate lives in the space of that sentence, lives in which she kept walking, lives in which the tray rattled and the men froze and everything after unfolded differently, better, worse, anything but what actually happened. But her feet had stopped on their own, the way feet do, and she had stood in the dark of the drain channel with cold air moving past her ankles toward the sea, and she had listened.

There were three of them. Aubry, and two men whose names she learned only years later, from newspaper archives in another country: Rieux and Tanneguy. Restoration contractors. Aubry's hands, outside the church's hands.

They were talking about money in the way people talk about weather—flatly, wearily, as though the sums were simply falling from the sky and someone had to shovel them. Contracts for work never done. Stone ordered and never delivered, invoiced anyway. A restoration fund that flowed in from dioceses and donors and municipal grants and flowed out again into accounts with names like saints. And Étienne saying, in a voice that started reasonable and ended cracked: he'd measured the walls. He'd measured the walls and the numbers didn't match, and he wasn't accusing anyone, he just wanted it noted, he wanted it *written down somewhere*, because a mason knows when a wall is lying.

*A wall isn't lying, Marchal,* Aubry had said, gently, almost kindly. *A ledger is.*

Then the sound.

Marguerite had spent eleven years refusing to describe the sound to herself in full. She let herself have pieces of it: the wet percussion, the short silence after, the scrape of boots. She let herself have the lantern light swinging across the far mouth of the channel and catching, for one quarter of a second, the pale underside of a hand—the back of it, knuckles down, fingers curled inward like a fern before frost.

She did not let herself have the rest.

They had carried him down the channel toward the harbor end, where the ebb ran out through the culvert into the mudflats, where the tide would take him and give him back three days later to some fisherman, drowned, unfortunate, a workingman who'd had a few too many and wandered onto the flats at night. She had followed—not followed, *drifted*, pressed against the brick, one hand over her own mouth, the tray still hooked over her other arm like an absurd piece of stage furniture—and she had seen enough to know two things with absolute certainty.

That Étienne Marchal was dead. And that Marguerite Vasseur, if she ever said so aloud, would join him.

She had run. Not that night—she had finished her shift, smiled at the sacristan, gone home, packed one bag with the mechanical calm of a woman watching herself from a great height. She had run the next morning, and she had run cleverly, because fear had made her clever: she had taken the archivist's clothes, because the archivist was a ghost even among ghosts, a man whose comings and goings no one marked, and a woman dressed as nobody could pass through a police cordon like smoke. She had left the city before Étienne's body surfaced, and she had left her name behind in a cathedral closet, folded in a brown coat, waiting.

Insurance, she had called it. Proof that she could become someone else if cornered.

She had never imagined anyone else would find it.

* * *

The knock came at seven, as it always did. Madame Guérin from below, with bread and the day's gossip, delivered through a door opened exactly the width of a forearm.

"You missed the excitement," the old woman said, pressing the loaf into Marguerite's hands. "Dead man in the harbor. Pulled him out by the fish market this morning. Drowned, they say." She sniffed. "Third one in what, five years? This town drowns more people than the sea does."

"Does it," Marguerite said, and made her voice a flat, bored thing.

"The strangest part—" Madame Guérin lowered her voice as though the harbor itself might be listening—"they say he was wearing fine clothes. Good wool coat. Scarf. Not a fisherman's kit at all. And there's a rumor—" she leaned closer, breath like yesterday's coffee— "that the coat belongs to the cathedral archivist. The quiet one. Joubert."

The loaf went heavy in Marguerite's arms. Heavy as a stone, heavy as a body.

"They're saying he reported it himself, can you imagine. Walked down to the quay and identified his own coat off a corpse. Either the poor man's being framed or—" she laughed, a dry rattle— "or the quiet ones are never as quiet as they look."

"No," Marguerite said. "They never are."

She closed the door and stood with the bread against her chest and did not move for a long time.

Someone had found the cache. After eleven years. Someone had gone into that closet—or been sent into it—and taken the coat and the scarf and put them on a dead man and rolled him into the harbor, and every thread of that sentence pointed back to one person. Canon Aubry had spent a decade hunting a letter, hunting a witness, hunting the loose thread named Marguerite Vasseur, and now he had found a coat that smelled of her and put it on a corpse and dropped it at the feet of the one man in Saint-Aubin who had spent years quietly writing letters about accidental deaths.

He wasn't framing Joubert. Or rather—he was, but that was secondary. What he was doing was *baiting*. He had hung a piece of her history on a drowned stranger and cast it into the water, and now he was watching to see which way the current moved. If the archivist reacted like an innocent man, fine. If the archivist reacted like a man who recognized something—if he started digging toward the closet, toward the missing years, toward the razored page in the burial register—then the bait had been taken, and the trail would lead Aubry straight to whatever remained of Marguerite Vasseur.

And Sebastian Joubert, walking home with his collar up, had no idea he was a lure.

* * *

She watched him that evening from the window, and hated herself with a new and specialized precision.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not make come out clean. Sebastian Joubert was, as far as she could tell from eleven months of watching, the only honest institution in that entire building. He had written letters—she had read copies, obtained at cost and risk, from a journalist in Nantes—to the police, to the diocese, to the prefecture, laying out the deaths of Rieux and Tanneguy and the others with archival patience, footnoted, cross-referenced, ignored. He had done it knowing it would cost him. He had done it with no hope of reward, out of what appeared to be simple, stubborn decency, the kind she had stopped believing in sometime around the ninth of November, eleven years ago.

And she was going to use him.

She already had. The note in the confessional—*You wore my name for eleven years*—had been a mistake dressed as a warning. She had told herself she was protecting him, steering him away, and perhaps some of that was true. But underneath, in the honest dark beneath the honest dark, she had known what she was really doing: she was testing whether he would dig. Whether the handwriting would hook him. Whether the archivist who catalogued everything would begin cataloguing *her*. Because if he dug, he would find the letter. Her letter. The one she had written in the first terrible week, naming Aubry, describing the channel, the lantern, the hand—sealed, addressed, and never sent, because sending it meant dying and keeping it meant living, and she had chosen living with a cowardice she had carried ever since like a stone in her chest.

The letter was still in Saint-Aubin. Hidden where only an archivist would ever think to look, because she had hidden it *as* an archivist, in the one place she knew from the inside: misfiled. Deliberately, lovingly misfiled, in a box of correspondence that no sane researcher would ever request. Eleven years it had waited. It was the only true testimony that existed, and it was buried in the building of the man it accused, and the only person alive who could find it without tearing the archive apart was currently walking home in a stolen coat, being hunted by a killer who thought the coat was a trap.

If she used him, he might die. Aubry had killed a mason for measuring walls; he would kill an archivist for reading them.

If she didn't use him, the letter stayed buried, Étienne stayed a drunkard's accident, and Aubry went on shoveling money out of the mouths of saints until the day he died peacefully in a bed she would never be able to look at without seeing a lantern swing.

And there was a third thing, the one she refused to examine at the window, the one that lived lower than guilt, warmer and more dangerous: that when she watched him bend his head to the wind in that lane, something in her chest moved that had nothing to do with strategy. That she had worn his coat for eleven years in her imagination, and somewhere in all that wearing, the man had gotten inside it too.

* * *

On the twelfth night after the drowning, she went out.

Not to the cathedral—she was not ready, her nerve was not a rope that could yet bear her weight—but to the harbor, to the mudflats at low tide, where the sea had given up Étienne's colleague's body—no. Where the sea had given up *a* body. She stood at the edge of the flats with the wind coming off the water flat and salt, and she looked at the place where the fishermen had found him, marked in her mind by a leaning mooring post, and she made herself do the thing she had avoided for eleven years.

She described the sound. All of it.

The wet percussion. The silence. The scrape of boots on brick. The lantern swinging, and the hand, and then—yes, all of it, the rest, the weight of a man dragged over stones, the small human sounds he was still making when they picked him up, the sounds stopping. She stood on the flats where the tide had returned what was sunk and she said it to herself in full sentences, like testimony, like a deposition, and when she was done she discovered she was crying, which surprised her, because she had believed the tears were used up years ago, spent on smaller griefs.

A mason had died because he wanted something written down somewhere.

And she had a room above a chandlery, and a window, and a view of a man who wrote things down for a living, and a letter rotting gently in a misfiled box, and eleven years of borrowed names, and one life—her own—that had never once, since the ninth of November, been spent on anything but survival.

Étienne had wanted it written down. She had the writing. She had, God help her, the witness.

Below the seawall, the ebb was pulling the water off the flats in long silver sheets, uncovering the mud where the truth had been planted, patient as seed. Somewhere behind her, up the hill, the cathedral lamps were going out one by one, and in the last lit window of the archive a figure was still bent over a desk, working late, unaware that he was already the most important document in the building.

Marguerite wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and the guilt in her chest turned over, the way soil turns, and became something with an edge to it.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would stop hiding behind warnings written in her own hand. Tomorrow she would give him something real—a date, a name, a place to dig—and pray to a God she had not spoken to in eleven years that using Sebastian Joubert would save them both instead of burying them in the same drawer.

She turned back toward the town, and in the lane below the seawall steps, just passing under the single lamp, collar up, head bent, walked the archivist himself—earlier than usual, walking faster than usual, and glancing back over his shoulder at the harbor with an expression she could read even at fifty meters, because she had worn that expression for eleven years.

He had found something. And he was afraid of it.

Marguerite Vasseur—dead four years on paper, alive on the flats, salt wind in her hair—started down the steps after him.
