## 13. Brannigan's Bargain

The office smelled of cold coffee and wet wool, the way it always did in November, when the harbor fog came up the hill and settled into the plaster of the police station like something that had paid rent. Inspector Declan Brannigan sat behind his desk with the door closed and the blinds half-drawn, and on the desk, squared neatly in front of him like a man arranging his own execution, were four folders.

He had taken them out of the archive an hour ago. He had not yet opened the second one.

The first folder he knew by heart. Sebastian Joubert's letters — three of them, spread across two years, each one polite to the point of pain, each one cataloguing an "accident" with the dispassionate precision of a man counting beads on a rosary. Brannigan had read them the first time with his feet on this same desk, a pen cap between his teeth, and he had written *crank* in the corner of the first page and *same crank* on the second and had not bothered with the third at all. That had been before the harbor gave up a drowned man wearing a dead archivist's coat. Before the burial register with a page razored out. Before the name Marguerite Vasseur — deceased, 2013, case closed, don't look — had begun to surface in his dreams like a body rising on the ebb.

He opened the second folder now.

It was the file on Étienne Marchal, the stonemason. Fall from scaffolding, March 2019, during the crypt restoration. Signed off by a doctor who had since retired to the coast and did not answer letters. Photographs of the body on the slab, and Brannigan found himself doing what he had not done in thirty years of police work, what he had trained himself out of doing because it was a young man's habit and a young man's mistake: he looked at the hands.

Calloused. Broad. The hands of a man who had spent his life lifting stone, the fingertips worn smooth as river pebbles, a scar across the left palm that the report did not mention and the report should have mentioned, because the report described a man who had *slipped*, and a slipping man does not have time to grip a scaffold pole hard enough to leave the marks that were there, faint white crescents in the skin of the dead fingers, four on each hand, the signature of a man holding on while someone above him pried his grip loose.

Brannigan set the photograph face down.

He had spent the morning being polite to people he despised. The mayor's office had called — not the mayor himself, never the mayor himself, but the deputy, a smooth young man named Ferrand who said *we* the way other men said *I* and meant the same three families it had always meant. Ferrand had used the phrase "unnecessary agitation" twice and "the cathedral's contribution to this city" four times, and had ended with a warmth that was worse than a threat: *We know you'll handle this with the discretion your record deserves, Inspector.*

His record. Twenty-nine years. A commendation and a suspension, in that order, and the suspension had been for exactly this — for following a thread that led upstairs. He had learned from it, or he had told himself he had learned from it, or he had simply gotten older and slower and better at pretending the thread didn't exist. And now a quiet archivist with ink-stained fingers had spent years doing, alone, in a locked room, the work Brannigan had been paid to do and had declined to do, and the archivist had been right. All of it. Every polite, meticulous, unbearable letter.

He poured the cold coffee and drank it anyway.

* * *

Joubert came at four, because Brannigan had sent for him and because Joubert was the kind of man who came when sent for, which was precisely what had made him invisible for so long and precisely what had made him dangerous now. Brannigan watched him through the blinds as he crossed the yard — a narrow, careful figure in a coat that was not his coat, because his coat was in an evidence bag, and the borrowed one hung on him the way borrowed things do, like an apology.

"Inspector." Joubert sat without being asked, hands folded, and Brannigan noticed — he noticed everything now, it was the curse of the last two weeks — that the archivist's hands were not steady. That the left one bore a fresh bandage across the knuckles. The crypt stairwell. Everyone in this city knew about the crypt stairwell by now; it was the only thing anyone talked about at the market.

"You fell," Brannigan said.

"I fell."

"On a staircase that had been inspected in September."

"On a staircase that had been inspected in September," Joubert agreed, and his voice was mild as milk, and his eyes were not mild at all. They were the eyes of a man who had stopped being afraid of the right things.

Brannigan leaned back. He had rehearsed this conversation in the car, in the shower, at three in the morning staring at the ceiling while his wife slept beside him and the foghorn counted the seconds like a patient heart. He had rehearsed an interrogation. What he had not rehearsed was the truth, and the truth was that he was about to do something that felt like stepping off a pier in the dark.

"I read your letters," he said. "All three. Properly, this time."

Joubert said nothing. He was good at nothing. Brannigan had watched him do it in the first interview — the stillness, the waiting, the terrible patience of a man who had spent a decade among documents that could not lie and had learned the habit.

"The Marchal file is wrong," Brannigan went on. "The hands are wrong. The doctor who signed it has a mortgage that was paid off the same month, and I've spent the afternoon finding that out, and I'm telling you this because if I tell it to anyone else in this building it will be in Ferrand's inbox by five o'clock and dead by five-fifteen." He pushed the folder across the desk. "You knew. Three years ago you knew, and you wrote to me, and I wrote back a form letter."

"I kept the form letter," Joubert said quietly.

Something in Brannigan's chest turned over. "Of course you did. You'd have kept it in a folder, indexed, cross-referenced."

"Under 'Correspondence, Unproductive.'"

Despite himself — despite everything — Brannigan laughed. It came out rough, like a thing disused. "You're not what I expected, Joubert."

"What did you expect?"

"A crank. A lonely man with a grudge." Brannigan looked at the window, at the fog pressing against the glass. "I got the lonely part right, maybe. But a crank doesn't wait three years. A crank wants to be right out loud. You wanted to be right in a drawer."

"Drawers keep things safe."

"Drawers keep things *buried*," Brannigan said, and heard the edge in his own voice, and did not soften it. "That's the difference between you and me, maybe. I bury things for a living too. The difference is I get paid for it and hate myself on schedule."

The silence between them was long and honest, which was rarer than either of them would have admitted.

* * *

Brannigan stood, went to the window, and stood with his back to the room, because it was easier to say the next part to the fog than to the man's face.

"Here is where we are," he said. "I believe you. Let's establish that, because I may never say it again and I'd like it on the record of this room, even if it never goes on any other record. You were right about Marchal. You were right about the others — I've read your dossier, the one you think I don't know about, the one in the green box in your cabinet under the false bottom, third shelf, behind the 1890s parish maps." He turned. Joubert's face had not moved, but something behind it had, the way a house shifts when a door opens somewhere far away. "Yes. I had a man look at your office. Months ago. Before the body. I'm not proud of it, but I don't have the luxury of pride this year."

"You could have asked."

"I could have asked," Brannigan agreed. "And you'd have shown me the drawer, and I'd have written *insufficient evidence* in the corner, and we'd both have gone back to sleep. That's what asking gets you in this city. Asking is a courtesy we extend to the dead." He came back to the desk and sat, and put both hands flat on the wood, and made himself say it. "Here's what I can't do. I can't touch Canon Aubry. Not a warrant, not a formal interview, not a question on a form. The order would come down before the ink dried, and it wouldn't come from the canon — it would come from people who've never met him and don't need to. The cathedral is a *charitable institution*, Joubert. It employs two hundred people. It has restored half the old town. The man who signs the checks signs other things too, and one of them is the career of every officer in this building, including mine, including the commissioner's, and everyone knows it and no one says it, and that silence has a name and the name is *the city*."

"And so?" Joubert asked. No anger in it. Just the question, held out flat, like a hand.

"And so," Brannigan said, "I can't build a case. But I can *not stop* one."

Joubert's head tilted, very slightly. A man listening to a lock begin to turn.

"Unofficial," Brannigan said. "Off the books. You find me proof — real proof, paper proof, the kind that doesn't have a *he said* in it anywhere — and I will move it. I know a prosecutor in Rennes who owes me and hates this city. I know a forensic accountant who took early retirement for reasons that were not her reasons. I know which records in this building get copied and which get lost, and I can make sure yours get copied. What I can't do is walk into the cathedral with a warrant, and what I *won't* do is let you go into that crypt again alone. The stairwell wasn't an accident, and we both know it, and the next one won't be a stairwell."

"You're offering to help me break the law."

"I'm offering to help you *use* it," Brannigan said. "There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and it's thin as a razor blade, and I've spent twenty-nine years on the legal side of it and I am tired, Joubert. I am tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix." He looked at his own hands on the desk, the cop's hands, the hands that had written *crank* in the margin of a true thing. "You were right and I was comfortable, and comfortable is just cowardice with better furniture. That's the bargain. You bring me the paper. I bring you the machinery. Neither of us signs anything."

For a long moment the archivist said nothing, and Brannigan had the strange sensation of being catalogued — of being a document opened, read, assessed, and *filed*, and he found, to his surprise, that he didn't mind. That there was even a comfort in it. To be read honestly by someone, after years of reading everything dishonestly, was a kind of absolution no confessional in that cathedral would ever give him.

"There is a chamber," Joubert said at last, very quietly. "Behind the ossuary wall."

Brannigan did not move. "Go on."

"I haven't been inside. The entrance is sealed — was sealed, until recently. The mortar is newer than the wall. Someone went in, and someone came out, and whatever they went in for, they took care coming back." Joubert's eyes came up. "I can get through it. I've been studying the wall for two weeks. But if I go in and find what I think is there, I'll need it to *exist*, officially, within hours. Not days. Hours. Before it can be emptied, or burned, or bricked over again with better mortar."

"And if what's there is what I think is there," Brannigan said slowly, "it isn't a case anymore. It's a detonation."

"Yes," said the archivist, in the tone of a man confirming a date in a ledger.

Brannigan sat back. Through the wall he could hear the station going about its evening — phones, boots, the radio crackle, the whole machinery of a law that worked beautifully for everything small and seized up like an old engine for everything large. He thought about Ferrand's warm voice. He thought about the crescent marks in a dead mason's fingers. He thought about a woman named Marguerite Vasseur who was dead on paper and walking around in a veil, and about a coat that had gone walking without its owner, and about the tide, which kept coming back, which always kept coming back, no matter what this city buried in front of it.

"Thursday," he said. "The canon is at the diocesan conference in Rennes until Friday noon. The cathedral will be half-staffed, the crypt unlocked for the restoration survey. If you're going through that wall, you go through it Thursday, in daylight, and I have two people within shouting distance who don't exist on any roster I'll admit to." He held out his hand across the desk, an old-fashioned gesture, a dockworker's gesture. "No signatures. No paperwork. Just this."

Joubert took it. His grip was stronger than his hands looked, and it did not shake at all.

"One more thing," Brannigan said as the archivist reached the door. "The woman. Vasseur. If she comes to you — if she's already come to you — you tell me. Not because I'll arrest her. Because if the canon's people find her first, she'll be dead by paper *and* by fact, and I've had enough of the first kind of dead to last me a career."

Joubert paused in the doorway, and the fog-light made a gray halo of him, and for a moment he looked less like an archivist than like a man standing at the edge of a country he was about to enter illegally.

"She came to me a long time ago," he said. "I just didn't know her name yet."

The door closed. Brannigan sat alone with his four folders and his cold coffee and the sound of the foghorn counting the seconds, and after a while he took out the third letter — the one he had never answered — and in the margin, beneath the old word *crank* in his own handwriting, he wrote a single word in fresh ink:

*Thursday.*

Then he locked it in the drawer, and for the first time in three years, the drawer felt less like a grave than like a seed.
