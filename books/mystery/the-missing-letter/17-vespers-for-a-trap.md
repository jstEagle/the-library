## 17. Vespers for a Trap

The lie had to be small enough to be believed and large enough to be worth killing for.

Sebastian rehearsed it all morning, standing at the catalogue desk with the ledger open before them, running the sentence through their mind the way a mason runs a thumb along a seam to find the flaw. *The letter is in Box 114, uncatalogued, misfiled among the restoration invoices of 2012.* Too neat. The canon would know Sebastian would never say something so clean by accident. It needed to look wrung out of them—half-confessed, half-buried.

They had learned, over eleven years in this archive, that people believe what they overhear far more readily than what they are told. A confession can be manufactured; an accident cannot. So Sebastian would not confess anything. They would simply fail, once, at silence.

* * *

Canon Aubry came down to the archive at half past three, as he had come every day that week, his footsteps on the stone stair announcing him long before his voice did. He had taken to wearing cologne again—the same sandalwood he'd worn the year of the first death—and Sebastian catalogued that too, the way they catalogued everything: another page in the dossier that lived now not in a drawer but behind their ribs.

"Still at it," Aubry said warmly, pausing at the foot of the stairs. His eyes went, as they always did, to the ledger, then to Sebastian's hands, then to the locked cabinet against the north wall. "You work like a man burying something, Joubert."

"Cataloguing is burial," Sebastian said, keeping their voice level, their pen moving. "Everything goes into the ground eventually. I just make sure it's labeled."

Aubry laughed—a good laugh, practiced, the laugh of a man who had charmed donors across three decades. "Vespers at six. I'll want the incense inventory before then. Father Gérard has let it slide again." He turned to go, and Sebastian felt the moment arrive the way one feels a tide turn—not by sight but by some pressure in the air.

"The 2012 invoices," Sebastian said, too quickly, and then caught themselves, as if the words had escaped rather than been released. A pause. Their pen stopped. "Never mind. It's nothing."

Aubry turned back slowly. "2012?"

"The restoration contracts. There's a discrepancy in the paper trail—a letter referenced in the mason's correspondence that isn't in its box. Box 114, if you're curious. I'll find it eventually. I always do." They bent back over the ledger as though ashamed of having spoken at all.

Silence stretched. When Sebastian dared glance up, Aubry was smiling, but the smile had gone still, the way water goes still over something deep. "You always do," the canon agreed softly. "That's what I've always valued about you."

His footsteps receded up the stair. Sebastian counted them—one, two, three, four, five, six, seven—until the door at the top closed, and only then did they allow their hands to shake.

It was done. The hook was baited with the truth itself, which was the only kind of bait that worked on men like Aubry. The letter really was in Box 114—or rather, it had been, until yesterday, when Sebastian had moved it to a place no one would think to look, and left in its stead a decoy envelope, aged with tea and handled with gloves, containing pages of innocuous parish accounts. If the canon came for it, he would come believing he was destroying the last evidence against himself. He would come during vespers, when the cathedral above would be full of voices and incense and every eye pointed toward the altar, when the archive below would be empty except for the man who had spent his whole life being invisible.

And Brannigan would be waiting.

* * *

Brannigan hated churches. He hated them the way honest men hate rooms built to make them feel small—the vaulted ceilings engineered for awe, the light falling in colored slants like arguments from a higher court. Saint-Aubin at vespers was awe weaponized, and he stood in the shadow of the north transept beneath a banner of some saint whose name he hadn't caught, feeling like a burglar in his own jurisdiction.

He had told no one where he was. That was the bargain's shape: unofficial help meant help that could be disowned. If tonight went wrong, Inspector Brannigan had been home with his wife and a bad cold. If it went right—if the canon of Saint-Aubin Cathedral walked into a trap set by the archivist he'd dismissed for years—then Brannigan would step out of the shadows with witnesses enough to make denial impossible.

He checked his watch. Five forty. The choir was filing in, boys and men in white surplices, their footsteps whispering on the stone. Candles were being lit along the rail. Somewhere below his feet, under forty feet of limestone and six centuries of burials, Sebastian Joubert was waiting in the dark beside an empty box.

Brannigan had read the archivist's letters four times now—all those careful, courteous, damning letters he'd once filed away as the obsessions of a lonely man. *Accident the first: fall from scaffolding, no witness, rope frayed at exactly the point of load.* The man wrote murder like an invoice. And Brannigan had stamped it *insufficient evidence* and returned to his burglaries and his domestic calls, while the bodies kept surfacing on the ebb tides like the sea doing the work he wouldn't.

"You should have listened to me," Joubert had said to him once, without accusation, which had made it worse.

"I'm listening now," Brannigan murmured to the saint above him. "Four years late. Try not to hold it against me."

The organ drew its first breath. The service began.

* * *

Marguerite had promised Sebastian she would stay away, and she had known when she promised it that she was lying.

She sat in the last pew of the south aisle, veiled, her hands folded around a missal she wasn't reading, one more mourner among the dozen scattered through the nave. Eleven years of practice had taught her how to be furniture: how to sit so that eyes slid off her, how to dress so that she registered as *poor woman, come to pray*, how to breathe quietly even when her heart was hammering like a fist on a door.

Below her, beneath the flagstones, beneath the crypt with its ossuary wall, Sebastian was setting the trap. Above her, Canon Aubry stood at the altar in gold vestments, his voice rising through the psalm with that warmth she remembered from before—from the night everything ended, when she had crouched behind a pillar of the crypt restoration site and watched him speak gently to a stonemason named Tomas Reyes, right up until the moment he stopped speaking gently and the rope did its work.

She had testified to no one. She had run instead, and the running had cost her her name, her marriage certificate, her face in any record that mattered. Marguerite Vasseur died in 2013 on paper, and the body that made the death certificate true belonged to a stranger pulled from the harbor, and she had carried that stranger's weight ever since—Tomas Reyes, dead twice over, once for money and once for her survival.

*I will not run tonight,* she told herself. The words had the taste of a vow.

Because here was the arithmetic she could no longer avoid: if the trap failed, Sebastian died alone in the dark below, and the canon walked free into another decade of gentle murders. If the trap succeeded without her, then her testimony would come secondhand, filtered, deniable—and Aubry's lawyers would shred it like wet paper. She was the witness every record insisted was dead. Her living body was the evidence no archive could forge.

Sebastian would be furious. She could already see their face—that controlled, wounded stillness, the way they looked when a page was torn from a register. But Sebastian had chosen the trap over safety, had chosen to be seen after a lifetime of invisibility, and Marguerite understood that choice from the inside. It was the choice she was making now, sitting in a pew in the church that had erased her, wearing a veil over the face that appeared on no living document.

The choir sang. The candles burned. Below, the trap waited; above, the spider conducted the service with lifted hands.

*Come down,* she willed him, staring at the gold figure at the altar. *Come down to your box of papers. Come see what the archivist found.*

* * *

In the archive, Sebastian stood in the dark beside Box 114 and listened to the cathedral breathe.

Sound traveled strangely down here. The vespers came through the stone as sensation more than music—a vibration in the floor, a swell and fade like a tide, the organ's bass notes felt in the teeth. Sebastian had spent thousands of evenings beneath services, and had always found it comforting: the institution overhead going about its holy business while the true records slept below. Tonight the same sounds felt like a held breath.

The decoy envelope lay in the box, positioned just slightly wrong—protruding a centimeter past the folder edge, the way a thing protrudes when someone has searched hastily and replaced it badly. Aubry would notice. Aubry noticed everything about paper; it was why he had survived so long. He would open it, find the parish accounts, feel the trap close on nothing—

No. Sebastian corrected themselves. He would find the accounts and believe he had won. Belief was the poison; panic would do the rest. A man who thought he had destroyed the last letter might still sleep badly. A man who thought he had won, and then heard Marguerite's name spoken aloud—*that* man would run for the flooded passage, and Brannigan would be waiting at the end of it.

Everything depended on Marguerite staying away. Sebastian had extracted the promise at dawn, holding both her hands in the bell tower while the gulls screamed below. *Promise me.* She had promised. And Sebastian had known, watching her eyes go somewhere else, that the promise was already ash. You cannot ask a woman who has been erased to stay erased one more night for her own good. She had spent eleven years being someone's good idea of safety.

So Sebastian had planned for her presence anyway. That was the third layer of the trap, the one Brannigan didn't know about, the one Sebastian had told no one: if Aubry came down and found not an empty archive but a witness, the confrontation would happen on ground Sebastian had chosen, with the exits mapped, with the tide table memorized, with the flooding passage timed to the minute. Spring tide tonight. High water at 9:14. The lower crypt would begin to drink the sea at half past eight, exactly as it had on other nights, other years, other bodies.

Sebastian checked their watch by feel. Six twenty-three. The psalm overhead swelled and faded.

Then, faintly, through forty feet of stone, they heard the sound they had been waiting for: footsteps on the archive stair, descending slowly, unhurried, the tread of a man who believed he had already won.

But there was something else beneath it—another sound, higher in the building, a door easing shut, a footfall on the transept flags. Two sets of footsteps. One coming down. One refusing to leave.

Sebastian pressed back into the shadow of the ossuary wall, heart slamming, and whispered into the dark, to no one, to everyone:

"Come on, then. Both of you. Let's finish the catalogue."
