## 21. Recataloguing

The archive smelled of dust and cold stone and, faintly, of salt — the sea had gotten into everything during those last days of flooding, and no amount of dehumidifying had fully driven it out. Sebastian Joubert had decided they didn't mind. It seemed fitting that the cathedral's memory should carry a trace of the tide now, after all the tide had given back.

They sat at the long oak table beneath the one window that faced east, where the morning light came in pale and honest, and they opened a fresh ledger.

Not the old one. The old ledger — the great burial register with its razored page and its century of careful entries — would remain on its shelf, corrected but not erased. That had been Brannigan's condition, delivered in his flat, tired voice two days after Canon Aubry's body was pulled from the ebb channel: *You don't rewrite what was written wrong. You write beside it.* Sebastian had agreed before he finished hearing it. It was, after all, the first rule of their profession. You never destroy a record. You amend it. You date your amendment. You sign your name.

*Your name.*

Sebastian turned the pen over in their fingers and looked at the blank first line for a long time.

For eleven years, every document in this room had passed through their hands, and not one of them — not a single accession slip, not a marginal note, not a correction in the tight, deliberate script the volunteers called *the archivist's hand* — bore anything more than initials. S.J., pressed small and neat into the corner of things, like a signature trying to make itself invisible. Anonymity had been a gift once, or had felt like one. Saint-Aubin had taken them in when they had nowhere else to be, had given them a locked room and a purpose and permission to be nobody, and Sebastian had learned to love being nobody the way some people learn to love a scar: because it proved you had survived something.

But nobody could not testify. Nobody could not stand in a courtroom and read four names aloud. Nobody had watched Marguerite Vasseur sign her statement on Tuesday with a hand that shook until she reached her own true name, and then lift her head as if a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was there had finally been set down — and nobody understood that the weight did not have to come back. Not ever again.

Sebastian wrote the date. Then, slowly, pressing hard enough that the nib sang faintly against the paper, they wrote:

*Amendment to the permanent record of Saint-Aubin Cathedral, entered by Sebastian Joubert, archivist, in full.*

It looked strange there on the line, the whole name, unabbreviated, taking up space. It looked like a door opened in a wall that had been solid for years. Sebastian found they were smiling at it, alone in the great quiet room, and let themselves smile.

Then they began to work.

* * *

The work took most of the day, and it was the best day's work of their life.

Four deaths. They gave each one its own entry, cross-referenced to the evidence file Brannigan had finally — *finally*, with a grimace that suggested the word cost him money — opened under case numbers rather than the single dismissive folder marked UNSUBSTANTIATED CORRESPONDENCE where Sebastian's letters had moldered for three years.

The first entry belonged to Henri Delacroix, the stonemason, drowned in the crypt flood of eleven years ago and recorded as an accident of worksite negligence. Sebastian wrote the truth in full: the restoration contracts, the payments routed through the Fondation Aubry, the second set of books found behind the ossuary wall, the letter — *the* letter — catalogued innocently as item 2013-047, correspondence, miscellaneous, donor files, where it had slept for a decade within arm's reach of the man who would have burned the cathedral down to find it. Sebastian had held it a hundred times without knowing. There was a grief in that, and a strange comfort too: the truth had been safe here all along, waiting in the dark among the other papers, patient as bone. Archives keep what people cannot afford to keep. That was the whole vocation, and it had never once failed them, even when they failed to look.

The second and third entries were the harder ones — Madame Ozenne, who fell from the scaffolding; young Père Léonard, whose heart supposedly gave out in the north transept. Sebastian wrote their names large, wrote the dates, wrote *homicide, established by testimony of Marguerite Vasseur (see statement, 14 March) and forensic review (Brannigan, case ref. 2024-118)*, and then sat for a while with their hands flat on the table, because these were the deaths they had carried alone through all the silent years, the dossier in the locked drawer, justice folded up and put away like linen no guest would ever use. Now the drawer stood open. Now the linen was spread on the table where anyone could see it. Inspector Brannigan had come himself yesterday to photocopy every page, and had paused at the doorway on his way out and said, gruffly, not looking at them: "You should have shouted louder, Joubert." And Sebastian had answered, "I shouted for three years. You just weren't listening yet." And Brannigan had almost smiled, which from Brannigan was a kind of absolution.

The fourth entry was the drowned stranger from the harbor, the man who had washed up wearing Sebastian's stolen coat, whose calloused hands had started all of this — the man whose death certificate, filed under Marguerite's married name, had made a living woman legally dead. Sebastian wrote his real name now: Tomas Reyer, mason's laborer, of no fixed address, hired in the spring of 2013 to move crates he was never told the contents of, killed when he asked one question too many about the second set of books. A life reduced to a disposal problem by men in vestments. Sebastian wrote every detail they knew, and then, because an archive is also a place of small mercies, they added a line no investigator required: *He deserved better than to be a fiction in another person's story. This record restores him to his own.*

When they finished the fourth entry, the light through the east window had gone gold and slanted, and their back ached, and their eyes were dry and burning, and they felt — there was no other word for it — *clean*. Like a wound finally allowed air.

* * *

In the late afternoon they went to the shelf where the old register lived and took it down, and carried it to the table, and opened it to the wound.

The razored page. The night of Henri Delacroix's death, cut out with a blade so clean that for years Sebastian had believed the loss itself was the message — someone saying, *this never happened, and I am powerful enough to make the past agree.* They had run their thumb along that cut edge a thousand times over the years, feeling the ghost of the missing paper the way you feel the socket of a pulled tooth.

Now they laid a sheet of archival repair tissue beside it, and a fresh leaf tipped in and numbered — the modern way, the honest way, nothing hidden, nothing disguised — and on that new leaf they reconstructed what the blade had removed. Burial records, reconstructed from the duplicate interment cards, the sexton's daybook, the funeral invoices in the Fondation accounts. Name by name, date by date, in the archivist's hand, each line annotated: *reconstructed 2024; original leaf removed by unknown party prior to [date]; see criminal case ref.*

And at the bottom of the reconstruction, where the original page would have closed, Sebastian added the note they had found all those months ago in a hand they had almost recognized — Marguerite's hand, a single desperate pencil line she must have slipped into the register in the hours after she saw the mason die, before they erased her: *He knows. He was there. Ask about the second key.*

She had been reaching toward this room, toward whoever kept it, for eleven years. Sebastian touched the pencil marks gently, the way you touch something fragile and beloved.

"Ask about the second key," they murmured. "I'm asking."

Below it, they wrote the amendment's closing lines, and signed them — full name, date, office — and blotted the ink, and sat back.

The register lay open on the table, its scar visible, its repair visible, its truth visible. Anyone who ever opened it would see exactly what had been done to it and exactly what had been done about it. That was the whole art, really. Not perfection. Honesty, layered honestly over damage, forever.

Somewhere above them, in the tower, a rope creaked against the wheel — the bell-ringers arriving early for Saturday practice, their voices drifting down the stairwell, ordinary and alive. The cathedral went on being a cathedral. That surprised Sebastian a little, still. They had expected, in the worst weeks, that the building itself would feel complicit, its stones soaked in what had been buried beneath them. But the stones had only ever held what people put in them, and now they held this instead: a corrected page, a named dead, a woman walking free in the nave below at this very moment, learning the building at ground level for the first time in eleven years, unhidden, unhurried, hers.

* * *

The resignation was one paragraph, and it took longer to write than the four death entries combined.

*To the Chapter of Saint-Aubin,* Sebastian began, and stopped, and began again twice, and finally simply told the truth, plainly, the way Marguerite had taught them to say things plainly:

*I came to this archive because I needed somewhere to disappear, and the cathedral was kind enough to lose me. For eleven years I have kept its records and, for much of that time, kept my silence alongside them. I am resigning both now. The silence served no one but the guilty, and I find I no longer wish to be invisible. I will remain through the end of the month to complete the transfer of the amended registers and the evidence inventory to the judicial archives. After that, my name will be on the door of nothing, and I will be glad of it.*

They read it over. It wasn't elegant. It was true, which was better.

There would be consequences, of course. The Chapter was already circling — two canons had visited separately this week with expressions of grave sympathy and questions shaped like hooks (*and how did you come to be searching behind the ossuary wall, precisely?*), and the diocesan lawyer had requested copies of everything, which meant the institution was preparing either to embrace the amendments or to bury the burier. Let them. Sebastian had spent years proving that buried things surface on the ebb after a spring tide, and institutions were only tides with slower water. Whatever came, they would meet it standing in the light, with their name attached, and that was a form of wealth they had never owned before.

They sealed the letter, addressed it to the Chapter house, and left it propped on the corner of the desk where old Père Anselme, the sacristan, would find it in the morning. Then they took one last walk through the stacks — not because anything needed doing, but because goodbye deserved to be said properly, even to rooms.

The smell of paper and stone. The drawers of photographs, the boxes of vestment inventories, the shelf of parish histories with their cracked spines. Eleven years of small, faithful, invisible labor. Sebastian had thought, once, that invisibility was safety. Then a drowned stranger had worn their coat, and a dead woman had written to them from inside their own locked world, and they had learned that hiding is only safety for the people you're hiding from.

At the door, they turned off the lamp and left the key in the lock — the second key, the one Marguerite's pencil note had whispered about, the one Sebastian had cut years ago and never told a soul about, the one that had let them into the ossuary wall when it mattered. Tomorrow the locks would be changed, per the Chapter's instructions. Tonight the archive stood unlocked for the first time in living memory, and Sebastian found that the image pleased them more than any speech: a room full of truths, door open, light off, trusting the dark to guard it now instead of a lock.

Marguerite was waiting outside the west porch, wrapped in her own coat — her own, bought new last week, the first garment in eleven years that belonged to no alias — and she fell into step beside them without a word, the way people do when the words are already settled between them.

"Well?" she asked, as they came down the steps into the blue evening.

"It's done. All of it. Signed."

"And how does it feel? Being seen?"

Sebastian considered. The bells had begun above them, practice ringing, ragged and joyful. Down the hill, beyond the roofs, the harbor lay exposed at low tide, the mudflats gleaming wide and silver-brown in the last light, all the sea's hidden things lying out in the open air until morning.

"Like the tide going out," Sebastian said. "Everything I've been keeping under me, all at once, in plain view. And it turns out—" they laughed, quietly, surprised by it— "it turns out nothing sinks."

Marguerite squeezed their arm. "Come on, then," she said. "Low tide's at dawn tomorrow. I want to show you where I used to walk, back when I was dead. I want to walk it with you while we're both alive."

They went down the hill together toward the lights of the town, and behind them the cathedral bells rang on, calling no one in particular, everyone at once.
