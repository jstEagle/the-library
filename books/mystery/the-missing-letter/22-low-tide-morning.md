## 22. Low Tide, Morning

The mudflats at dawn were not the place the town imagined when it thought of Saint-Aubin's coastline. The postcards stopped at the harbor wall, at the pastel houses and the masts swaying like metronomes against a blue that never quite existed. Beyond the wall, when the sea withdrew—and in spring tides it withdrew for nearly a kilometer—the world became something else entirely: a vast brown plain stitched with silver channels, smelling of salt and sulfur and old stone, littered with the sea's honest debris. Rope. Oyster shells. The ribs of boats that had died before anyone now living was born.

Sebastian Joubert stood at the harbor steps at six in the morning, three weeks after everything, and watched Marguerite Vasseur walk down onto the flats ahead of them.

She did not wait to see if they followed. That was new. For eleven years she had been a woman who always waited—waited in doorways, waited for crowds, waited until a street was empty before crossing it. Now she walked out onto the exposed seabed with her boots sinking slightly into the wet sand, her coat open despite the cold, her hair uncovered for the first time since Sebastian had known her, and she moved as though the whole flat gray morning belonged to her.

Perhaps it did. She had paid more for it than anyone.

Sebastian followed, taking the stone steps carefully. Their ribs still ached from the crypt—not badly, not anymore, but enough to remind them each morning that water had almost taken them, that they had coughed half the harbor back up onto the stones while Brannigan's officers dragged Canon Aubry's body from the ebb channel with grappling hooks. Enough to remind them that they had chosen, in the last possible moment, to reach for another person instead of the ladder rung. They had thought about that choice often since. It was the only part of those hours Sebastian would not have traded.

"You're walking slowly," Marguerite called back, without turning. "For an archivist, you have no sense of urgency about anything."

"I have a profound sense of urgency," Sebastian said, catching up. "I simply file it properly."

She laughed—a real laugh, unguarded, the kind of sound that must have been dangerous for her once, the kind she had spent a decade teaching herself not to make. Hearing it still did something to Sebastian's chest that no document could adequately describe. They had tried, one sleepless night, to describe it, and had produced three drafts and abandoned all of them.

The flats stretched out ahead, gleaming under a sky the color of pewter just beginning to warm at its eastern edge. Out here the cathedral was visible behind them, its spire catching the first light, and Sebastian found they could look at it now without the old reflex of deference. Three weeks ago they had resigned. Not dramatically—they had never done anything dramatically—but with a letter placed in the diocesan office, a set of keys returned, and a final afternoon spent walking the archive aisles, touching the spines of registers they had catalogued for nineteen years. The new archivist, a nervous young woman named Odile who kept asking Sebastian questions by email, had already re-shelved forty volumes incorrectly. Sebastian had decided, with effort, to let this go.

"Look." Marguerite crouched suddenly, pointing. In the mud at her feet lay a small object, dark and encrusted. She worked it free with both hands and turned it over: a clay pipe bowl, ancient, its stem long rotted away, glazed with two centuries of sediment. "The sea keeps giving things back."

"It does," Sebastian said quietly. "It took me a while to understand that it wasn't hiding anything. It was only ever holding things for us."

They walked on along one of the channels, where the water ran shallow and clear over ribbed sand. This was the channel. They both knew it without saying so. The ebb channel that ran beneath the old sea gate into the lower crypt—the passage Aubry had used twice, maybe three times, to move what he needed moved, trusting the tide to do the work of erasure. The tide tables had told the story all along; Sebastian had seen it too late for the drowned stonemason, but not too late for the truth. Every body the sea had been given, the sea had eventually returned, surfacing on an ebb after a spring tide, patient as any archivist, refusing to keep what it was owed.

Now the case files were open again. All four. Brannigan had called it, in his gravel voice, *the largest correction of record in the history of this police force*, and had said it looking directly at Sebastian, which for Brannigan was roughly equivalent to a medal ceremony.

"Brannigan called again yesterday," Sebastian said. "He wants me to consult on the cold cases. Formally. Paid, even."

"Will you?"

"I don't know." Sebastian watched a heron stalk the shallows ahead, motionless as a carved thing. "I spent nineteen years believing that the most useful thing I could be was invisible. I'm not sure I know how to be visible yet. I keep drafting versions of myself the way I used to draft catalogue entries, and none of them feel finished."

Marguerite stopped walking. She turned to face them fully, and the low light caught her face—the face that had been photographed for a death certificate, that had lived under three false names, that had watched Sebastian across a confessional screen wearing a veil because even then she could not risk being seen. Eleven years of erasure, and here she stood in open daylight on a public shore, unhidden.

"Sebastian." She said their name the way she had begun to say it lately—carefully, as though setting something fragile down. "You catalogued three murders in a drawer for years because no one would listen. You got hit on the head, nearly drowned, and burned your whole quiet life down rather than let a lie stand in the record. You are the most visible man I have ever met. You were just the only one who couldn't see yourself."

Sebastian had no answer to that. They looked out at the flats instead—at the immense revealed floor of the sea, at the worm casts and the shell beds and the far-off glitter where the water waited to come home—and felt something settle in them that might have been peace, or might have been its foundation being poured.

* * *

They walked as far as the old wreck.

It was a boat skeleton everyone in Saint-Aubin knew—the *Marie-Louise*, some said, though no record confirmed it, a fishing vessel broken on the flats sixty or seventy years ago and left where she fell. Her ribs rose from the mud like the carcass of something enormous, black with age, furred with weed. At high tide she disappeared entirely. At low tide she stood in the middle of the emptiness like a monument to everything the water takes and gives back on its own schedule.

Marguerite sat on a fallen timber and pulled off one boot, shaking out sand. Sebastian sat beside her. For a while neither spoke. The silence between them had changed quality over these three weeks; it no longer had the texture of two people concealing things, but of two people who had finally emptied themselves of secrets and discovered that what remained was enough to fill a life.

"Odile emailed me," Sebastian said at last. "She found something odd in the restoration ledgers. A payment from 2011 she can't reconcile. She asked if I'd come look at the file." A pause. "She signed it 'with respect,' which I think is how young people say 'help.'"

"Are you going to?"

"I think I am." Sebastian turned a shell over in their fingers, a limpet worn smooth as a coin. "Not as an archivist. As a consultant. Someone whose name is on the work." They hesitated, then said the rest of it, the part they had been turning over for days. "Aubry is dead. The contracts are being investigated. But there will be other Aubrys. There always are—institutions attract them the way shipwrecks attract barnacles. And someone has to be the person who notices. I'm apparently constitutionally incapable of not noticing."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said," Marguerite said dryly.

"I've said better."

"You haven't. You said 'your handwriting matches the ledger note' in a voice like a man discovering fire, and then you didn't speak to me for two days because you were frightened." She smiled, though, and leaned her shoulder against theirs. "I liked it anyway. Being someone's fire."

Sebastian looked at her. There was a question they had been carrying for weeks now, and it was not the question people might expect—not *will you stay*, not *what comes next*—but something smaller and heavier. They had rehearsed it in the archive after resigning, standing among the shelves, and had failed to say it aloud every time.

"When you wrote the letter," they said. "The original one. The one naming him. You addressed it to the diocesan tribunal. Why not the police?"

Marguerite was quiet for a moment. A gull cried somewhere out over the water, absurd and lonely.

"Because I was twenty-six and terrified, and the tribunal had a name I recognized from the parish bulletin, and the police had a desk where a sergeant once laughed at my mother." She drew a breath. "And because I thought the Church was permanent. I thought if I gave the truth to something permanent, it would be safe. That was the whole logic of my life, Sebastian. Give everything to what looks permanent, and hope permanence protects you." She looked up at the wreck's black ribs. "The sea taught me otherwise. The sea is the most permanent thing here, and it changes its mind twice a day."

"So what do you trust now?"

"This." She gestured—at the flats, at the wreck, at Sebastian, at herself, at the whole exposed and shifting morning. "Things that show themselves. Things that survive being seen. Mudflats don't pretend to be anything. They're just what's left when the water tells the truth."

Sebastian reached over and took her hand. Her fingers were cold from the morning air, and they closed around theirs with the deliberate grip of a woman who had decided, finally and completely, to hold on.

"I have a proposal," Sebastian said. "Not a romantic one. Well—" they paused, reconsidered, felt heat climb their neck, "—possibly a romantic one. But it begins administratively."

"Of course it does."

"There's a house on the rue des Salines. Two rooms above a chandler's shop—the same building, actually, where you—" they stopped, corrected, "—where you used to watch the harbor. It's available. The landlady is eighty-three and mostly interested in whether tenants can fix gutters. I can fix gutters. And it's four hundred meters from the archive, if I take the consultancy, and six hundred meters from the harbor, if you—" He faltered. "If you want to stay. Here. With me. Under your own name, in a town that knows it, with a door that has your name on it if you want one, or doesn't, if you'd rather. No false names. No borrowed coats. Nothing in the record that isn't true."

Marguerite did not answer immediately. She looked out at the water waiting at the edge of the world, and Sebastian watched her profile and understood that she was doing what she always did—calculating exits, counting risks, measuring the distance to cover. But then something in her face loosened, and she turned back, and her eyes were bright and wet and entirely unafraid.

"The gutter thing," she said, "is genuinely persuasive."

"I thought you'd find it so."

"And the name on the door." She swallowed. "Vasseur. My name. Painted on glass, where anyone walking past could read it."

"If you want it."

"I want it." She said it fast, as though the words might escape if she gave them time. Then, slower: "Eleven years I've practiced introducing myself with other women's names. Sylvie. Anne-Cécile. Madame Roux, God rest her, wherever she actually is. Do you know what I did yesterday? I went to the mairie. To the civil records office. I sat in the chair and I said, 'My name is Marguerite Vasseur, and I'm legally dead, and I'd like to discuss that.' And the clerk—a girl of maybe twenty-four—she didn't laugh, she didn't flinch, she just opened a drawer and said, 'Yes, we've been expecting you. Brannigan sent the paperwork Tuesday.'" Marguerite laughed, shaky. "Eleven years of terror, and the resurrection took a form and a signature. I walked out of there holding a temporary certificate, and I stood on the steps and I read my own name four times, and then I cried in front of a war memorial, which felt appropriate."

Sebastian brought her hand to their lips and pressed them to her knuckles, an old gesture, almost liturgical, and meant it as a kind of vespers—evening prayer said at the wrong hour, gratitude arriving late but arriving.

"Then we'll get the glass painted," they said. "Good glass. Thick. The kind that lasts."

* * *

The tide turned while they sat in the wreck's shadow.

It did not announce itself. That was the thing about the sea Sebastian had learned in these weeks of studying it—it never announced anything. It simply reversed, silently, at its appointed hour, and the distant glitter at the edge of the flats began, imperceptibly, to widen. A channel that had run thin and clear began to run fuller. Somewhere beyond sight, the water was already climbing the sea wall, filling the harbor, sliding toward the old sea gate and the flooded passage below the crypt where a canon of the Church had drowned in the channel he had used as a grave. Brannigan had ruled it accidental. Sebastian had reviewed the finding and, after long thought, signed off on it. Some accidents, they had written in their consultation note, are the sea keeping its own accounts.

"We should go back," Marguerite said, watching the water come. "Before it cuts us off from the steps."

"In a minute." Sebastian did not move. "I want to watch it take the flats back. I've spent my whole life on the other side of this—watching things get buried. I want to watch the covering-up happen honestly, for once. Just water doing what water does. No one underneath it. No one hidden."

Marguerite rested her head against their shoulder. Together they watched the sea return: the channels fattening, the sandbars dissolving one by one, the wreck's ribs going dark at the waterline as the tide climbed them rib by rib, patient, thorough, indifferent to everything except its own ancient arithmetic. The heron lifted off and beat away toward the marshes. The light strengthened. Somewhere behind them the town was waking—bells soon, bread soon, Odile's next confused email soon—but here there was only the two of them and the water coming home over ground that held nothing now but shells and rope and the honest bones of an old boat.

"No false names," Marguerite said softly. It was not a question. It was a vow, or the draft of one, to be finalized later over gutters and painted glass and whatever ordinary, documented, unremarkable days they could build from here.

"No false names," Sebastian agreed.

They gathered themselves and walked back across the shining mud, side by side, leaving two lines of footprints that the rising water was already finding, already smoothing, already forgiving—while behind them, quiet and total and true to its own schedule, the tide went out.
