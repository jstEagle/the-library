## 4. The Case File Nobody Logged

The attic of the Gilded Heron Hotel was, in Ivo Montrose's private opinion, the most honest room in the building.

Every other room lied a little. The lobby lied about its grandeur — the marble had been painted on by a man named Tibbet in 1931, and if you looked closely at the reception desk you could see where his brush had run out of enthusiasm halfway down the third column. The suites lied about their comfort, the restaurant lied about its lobster, and the guests, God bless every one of them, lied about nearly everything: their names, their reasons, their marriages, their allergies. But the attic lied about nothing. It was a room of leftovers, and leftovers do not perform.

She went up on Thursday morning, because the Historical Society's ceremony was now nine days away and Mrs. Fairweather had sent a second letter, this one on thicker paper, which in Ivo's experience meant the writer was either wealthier or more anxious, and in Mrs. Fairweather's case it was reliably both. The letter requested access to the hotel's "archival materials pertaining to the Parr era" for a display table. Ivo had replied that she would see what could be found, which was a concierge's way of saying *I have not the faintest idea, but I will die before admitting it.*

So: the attic.

She climbed the narrow stair behind the linen room, the one guests were never told about, and let herself in with the brass key she kept on the same ring as her master keys, though it opened only this door and, in a sense, the past hundred years.

Dust hung in the grey light from the round window like a held breath. Racks of out-of-season uniforms. A chaise with one arm chewed by a dog that had been dead before Ivo was born. Hatboxes, ledgers, a taxidermied heron with an expression of permanent professional disappointment that Ivo had always found kinship in.

She had been up here perhaps two hundred times in eleven years. She knew the room the way she knew the lobby — by its habits, its silences, its small betrayals. Which was precisely why, forty minutes into her search of the Parr-era boxes, she stopped moving entirely, and stood in the dust with her hand flat on a cardboard flap, listening to the room the way one listens to a guest who has said something slightly wrong.

*Something had been moved.*

Not recently. Not carelessly. But moved.

* * *

Let it be said plainly: Ivo Montrose did not consider this a talent. She considered it a discipline, the way a pianist considers scales. Anyone could do it. Almost no one bothered.

She knew the attic's geography because she had, over eleven quiet years, memorized it — not as a project, never as a project, but the way water memorizes the shape of its channel. The Parr boxes sat on the third shelf of the oak unit against the north wall, under the window, because the window leaked in a driving east wind and the Parr material had seemed the least precious thing to sacrifice. There were seven boxes. She knew their order: photographs, correspondence, menus and inventories, the 1920s guest ledgers (which she had catalogued herself, in pencil, in a hand so small it could be read only by candlelight or regret), miscellaneous ephemera, the hotel's own founding papers, and a box marked MISC. — DAMP that contained, as far as she had ever determined, nothing but the smell of 1974.

The boxes were now in a different order. Correspondence before photographs. Damp before ephemera. Someone had gone through them and put them back *almost* correctly — correctly enough for Mrs. Tibbet the housekeeper, correctly enough for a fire inspector, correctly enough for anyone in the world except the person who had put them in that order in the first place.

Ivo stood very still and felt the particular cold that comes not from the fen wind but from the realization that one's private country has been visited.

She did not touch anything further. She took out her notebook — the working notebook, not the ledger, the ledger was sacred and lived in her room in a locked drawer inside another locked drawer, a nesting arrangement she was fully aware said something about her — and wrote the date, the time, and: *boxes reordered. Correspondence/photographs transposed. Damp box moved from end to middle. By someone who knew the order existed.*

That last clause was the one that made her sit down on the chaise, carefully, avoiding the dog-chewed arm, and think.

Because there were perhaps four people alive who knew the boxes had an order. Herself. Mrs. Tibbet, who dusted the shelves and had never in her life opened a box on purpose. Old Mr. Crake, the owner, who had not climbed these stairs since his hip went and his interest in anything went with it. And — she counted, and recounted, and arrived at four — no one else.

Unless the someone had not needed to know the order. Unless the someone had simply been careful, the way she was careful, and had reconstructed it afterward from dust lines and shelf shadows. Which was, she admitted, exactly what she herself would have done.

Which meant the someone was like her.

That thought she did not enjoy.

* * *

She began, then, to do what she did: to read the room as a text.

The dust on the third shelf had been disturbed in a band roughly the depth of a box — one box lifted, set down, replaced. But the band was freshest at the *ends* of the shelf, which meant more than one box had been out at a time, laid side by side somewhere. Where? She looked down. The floorboards in front of the shelf showed four faint scuff-arcs, the marks of a box dragged rather than lifted — heavy, then. Full. And the scuffs led, in a shy diagonal, toward the far corner behind the heron, where an old school desk sat under a sheet.

The sheet had been folded back. Not removed — folded, in a neat square, the way Ivo herself folded sheets when she was working and wanted them out of the way but retrievable.

She lifted it.

On the desk's scarred surface, arranged with a tidiness that was almost an apology, lay a folder of the grey-blue cardboard that police stations had used between the wars, the kind with the brass fastener and the typed label gone the brown of weak tea: **WEXLEY FEN CONSTABULARY — PARR, ALDOUS T. — MISSING PERSON — 1924 — CLOSED.**

Ivo did not touch it for a full minute.

She looked at it the way she had once looked at a guest's luggage and known before opening it that the man was not a widower at all, that the second toothbrush was hope rather than grief. The folder sat there with the patient confidence of a thing that has been waiting. It was not filed among the boxes. It was *displayed*. On a desk. Under a folded sheet. In an attic no one came to.

It had been left for her. Or it had been left by someone who worked here often enough that leaving it felt safe. Or — and this was the possibility that walked up her spine on small cold feet — it had been left by someone who wanted it found by *someone*, and did not much care who.

She put on the white cotton gloves she kept in her coat pocket for handling old paper — she was a professional, whatever her profession officially was — and opened the fastener with the care of a surgeon and the excitement of a child at a locked pantry.

* * *

The file was thin. That was the first wrong thing. A missing-person file on the town's founding father, a man whose name was on the fountain, the school, the commemorative ale, and one entire side of the war memorial — and the file held eleven pages.

Eleven pages for a man who walked out of the Gilded Heron — her hotel, her own lobby, the very carpet she vacuumed — on the evening of the ninth of March, 1924, and was never reliably seen again.

She read it twice, standing, then sat on the school desk's bench — it held her weight with a protest — and read it a third time, slowly, letting the pages speak.

The report itself was competent and dull. Constable E. R. Mowbray, whose handwriting was a brave campaign against legibility, recorded that Mr. Aldous Parr, aged 61, proprietor of the Gilded Heron Hotel and substantial landholder of the fen, had failed to keep a dinner appointment with his solicitor on 9 March 1924. That his hat and coat were missing from the hotel. That his bedroom on the second floor — *her* second floor, the corridor she walked every night with her lamp — showed no disturbance. That the fen drains had been walked, the river dragged, the railway stations canvassed. That Mrs. Parr, née Adeline, was "distressed but composed." That no body was recovered and no evidence of foul play established, and that after seven months the case was closed as *presumed drowned, misadventure*.

Seven months of searching, eleven pages. Ivo had written longer reports on guests who stole towels.

The second wrong thing was the inquest summary, which was a single sheet, and which stated that the coroner had returned the drowning verdict after hearing from four witnesses. Four. For the richest man in the fen. She had seen the Historical Society's own published account, which named nine witnesses and a jury of twelve. Someone had thinned this file. Someone had thinned it a long time ago — the paper of the surviving pages was uniformly aged, no modern insertions — but the *thinning* was the story. The file had been edited the way a guest edits his story at the desk: not by lying, exactly, but by deciding what you would not be told.

And then there was the third wrong thing. The one that turned the attic cold.

The annotations.

In the margins, in pencil — modern pencil, a mechanical one, the graphite bright and unfoxed against the yellowed page — someone had been in conversation with this file for years. Not once. The hand was consistent but the *pressure* varied, the way a hand varies across seasons of a life. Some notes were years old, the pencil gone grey and soft-edged. Some were recent enough that Ivo suspected she could have smudged them with a thumb.

Beside the line recording that Parr's coat and hat were missing: *But his umbrella remained. Who walks into a fen in March without an umbrella?*

Beside the note that the bedroom showed no disturbance: *No disturbance — or tidied? Check the chambermaid's statement. It's not in this copy. It's not in ANY copy I've found.*

Beside *presumed drowned*: *You don't drown a man who owns the river.*

And on the final page, beside the coroner's verdict, in pencil pressed so hard it had embossed the paper, three words that Ivo read four times:

*He was moved.*

Not *he moved*. Not *he was murdered*, even. *He was moved.* As though the writer had progressed past the question of death and arrived at the question of location. As though for this person the body was not a mystery but a *misfiled document*.

Ivo sat in the dust and the grey light and felt the attic tilt very slightly, the way the Heron's old floors did when a heavy guest walked above.

* * *

She did what she always did with a fact that frightened her: she made tea of it, metaphorically, and took it in small sips. She catalogued the annotations in her notebook — every one, transcribed, with her guess at their relative age. She noted the mechanical pencil, 0.7 millimetre, HB lead, a common enough tool. She noted that the annotator wrote a small, upright, unornamented hand with a distinctive flat-topped *t* — the *t* crossed with a single economical stroke, like a man signing off a ledger.

She noted that she had seen that flat-topped *t* somewhere before, and could not place it, and that this failure annoyed her more than the annotations themselves.

And then she noted the last thing, the thing she had saved for last because she already knew what it would be, the way you save the guest's bill till morning when you know it will be disputed.

The file had no accession number. No stamp of the county records office. No trace in the hotel's own inventory — she had catalogued the Parr boxes herself, in pencil, in her tiny hand, and there was no police file on her list. This document did not belong to the attic. It had been brought here, kept here, worked over here — and hidden here, in the one room in the building that nobody visited, behind a folded sheet, in the blind spot of an entire town.

Somebody had been studying the death — the disappearance, the *moving* — of Aldous Parr for years, in secret, in her attic, under her nose, in her country, while she stood at her desk noticing which guests lied about their allergies.

The invisible watcher, it turned out, had been watched all along. She sat with that a while. It did not sit comfortably. It sat the way a wet coat sits.

* * *

There was one more page, tucked at the back, and she almost missed it because it was not police paper at all but a sheet from a hotel memorandum pad — *her* hotel's memorandum pad, the ones still stacked in the back office, cream-coloured with the heron crest at the top. On it, in the flat-topped pencil hand, a single line:

*The ledgers will show it. They always show it. — Ask the attic.*

Ivo read it three times. *The ledgers will show it.* Which ledgers? The guest ledgers of 1924, presumably — the ones in the box on the third shelf, the ones she had catalogued and, she realized with a slow cold turn, had never actually *read*, not properly, not line by line, because cataloguing a thing and reading a thing are different acts and she had confused them, she, Ivo Montrose, who never confused anything, who noticed everything except, apparently, the one thing happening six feet above her office for years.

*They always show it.* The confidence of it. The intimacy. Whoever wrote this had read the ledgers. Had found something in them. Had left a note to themselves — or a note to whoever came next — in an attic they treated as a private study.

And *ask the attic*, which was either a joke, a prayer, or an instruction, and Ivo could not tell which, and the not-telling itched like a wool collar.

She repacked the file. She did not put it back under the sheet. She thought about that for a long moment, standing with the grey-blue folder in her gloved hands, weighing caution against curiosity the way she weighed a guest's luggage against the luggage tag. Caution said: put it back, say nothing, watch who comes and goes. Curiosity said: the ceremony is in nine days, the coffin is in the ground, and someone in this hotel has been holding a conversation with a dead constable's paperwork for years.

She took the file. She wrapped it in a dust cloth, carried it down the narrow stair past the linen room — nodding to the linen room's steam and Mrs. Tibbet's back, saying nothing — and locked it in the drawer inside the drawer, next to her ledger, where it lay against the leather like a cold hand.

Then she went down to the desk, put on her professional face, and began the real work. Because the file had told her two things, and only one of them was about Aldous Parr.

The first was that in March of 1924, the Gilded Heron's guest ledgers recorded every soul who slept under this roof in the week the founder vanished — and that someone believed those pages would show *it*, whatever *it* was, and had said so in writing, and had left the saying where only she would find it. Or where only she would *bother* to find it.

The second was that she had an annotator. A person with a mechanical pencil, a flat-topped *t*, the patience of years, and access to her attic. A person who was either an ally she hadn't met or an enemy she'd been serving breakfast.

She pulled the 1924 ledger box toward her across the reception desk, blew off the dust, and opened the first volume to March.

The handwriting of the old registrar was beautiful, looping, confident — and on the page for the ninth of March, 1924, one entry had been written in different ink entirely. Darker. Newer. Added, she was almost certain, long after the rest.

A guest's name, registered the very night Aldous Parr disappeared — a name that had no business being there, in ink that had no business being that colour.

Ivo reached for her magnifying glass, and her heart, which had been quiet for eleven years, began — quietly, professionally, and with enormous pleasure — to race.
