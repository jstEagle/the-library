## 8. The Parr Inheritance

Camille Parr had learned to cry on command at the age of seven, and she had never once needed the skill so badly as she did now, standing in the Gilded Heron's morning room with a cup of tea going cold in her hands while her aunt Geraldine rehearsed grief in the corner like an actress who hadn't been given enough stage time.

"Empty," Geraldine said for the fourth time, pressing a lace handkerchief to her eyes. "The coffin of my great-great-grandfather, *empty*. Do you understand what that means, Camille? It means we are a family of *frauds*. It means every plaque in this town is a lie. It means—" she drew a shuddering breath, "—it means I shall have to cancel the luncheon."

"The luncheon will proceed," Camille said.

Geraldine lowered the handkerchief and gave her the look she'd been perfecting since 1987, the one that said *you are a disappointment to four generations of Parr women.* "The luncheon was to celebrate the founder's remains being laid in state. What exactly do you propose we celebrate instead?"

"A mystery," Camille said, and smiled, because smiling was the other thing she'd learned young. "People adore a mystery. We'll lean into it. *The Founder Who Vanished.* There's a book in it. Possibly a documentary. Geraldine, we could sell tickets."

Her aunt stared at her with the particular horror of a woman discovering that her niece has inherited all of the family's ruthlessness and none of its sentimentality. Then, grudgingly: "There *could* be a commemorative tea towel."

"There could absolutely be a commemorative tea towel."

It was, Camille reflected as Geraldine swept off to telephone the printer, remarkable how quickly grief became logistics in this family. The exhumation had been three days ago. Three days since the coffin lid came up out of the fen-soaked earth and revealed nothing but a century of darkness and a pillow no one had slept on. Three days since the vicar's hands shook so badly he nearly dropped the service book, and the coroner — smooth, quick Mr. Ellery, who'd had his statement drafted before the crowd dispersed — announced that the matter would be "investigated thoroughly."

Camille did not believe in thorough investigations. She believed in what she'd been taught at her grandmother's knee, which was that the Parr fortune rested on one story and one story only: that Aldous Parr, founder, benefactor, pillar of Wexley Fen, had died in 1924 and been buried with honor, and that everything his descendants touched thereafter — the estate, the mills, the land grants, and yes, the Gilded Heron Hotel itself, deeded to the family trust in perpetuity — flowed legitimately from that death.

Take away the body, and you took away the deed. Take away the deed, and you took away everything.

She set down her cold tea and went upstairs to find the codicil.

* * *

The documents lived in the hotel's strong room, behind a door that looked like a broom cupboard and weighed as much as a small car. Camille had the key on a chain around her neck, where it sat against her sternum like a small cold heart. She let herself in, switched on the single bulb, and pulled the Parr papers from their acid-free box with the reverence of a priest handling relics and the practicality of a woman checking her bank balance.

There it was. The codicil to Aldous Parr's will, dated March 1924 — eleven days before he vanished, which was itself a detail Camille tried very hard not to think about at two in the morning.

She had read it perhaps fifty times in her life. Her grandmother had made her read it aloud at fourteen, sitting in this same room, and had watched her face carefully while she did. Camille had understood even then that she was being tested, though she hadn't known for what.

*"And should my remains at any time be found to be absent from their consecrated place,"* she read now, her voice flat in the little room, *"then the whole of my estate, real and personal, including the lands, the mills, and the house called the Gilded Heron, shall revert not to my issue but to the party named in the sealed annex, whose identity my executors shall confirm by private mark."*

The sealed annex. The private mark. And the name — the name that had been redacted. Not blacked out carelessly, the way governments redact things, but *removed*, the paper scraped or cut and replaced with a neat rectangle of matching stock, in every copy, in every transcription, in every certified abstract that had ever left the solicitors' offices. Camille had once spent a fortnight in the county archives with a raking lamp, looking for indentations, ghost-ink, anything. She'd found nothing. Whoever had done the redacting had done it well, and had done it early, and had apparently done it everywhere.

Which meant someone in 1924 had known exactly what was coming.

She stood there in the bulb-light, the codicil trembling slightly in her hands, and admitted to herself what she had been refusing to admit for three days: that the empty coffin was not an accident of history. That someone had opened that grave before — or never closed it properly at all — and that the codicil, with its careful contingency, its *should my remains be found to be absent*, read less like prudence and more like prophecy.

Aldous Parr had known he might disappear. He had written it into his will. And then he had.

* * *

"You look terrible," said Nell Ashcombe, when Camille found her in the hotel lounge that evening, surrounded by a snowdrift of photocopied parish records.

"I've been reading my own inheritance," Camille said. "It's like reading your own autopsy."

Nell looked up. She was twenty-six, freckled, chronically underfunded, and possessed of the kind of obsessive genealogical focus that Camille privately classified as either a gift or a disorder. She also, Camille had noticed over the past week, missed nothing — which made her either a useful ally or a dangerous one, and Camille hadn't yet decided which.

"Can I ask you something professional?" Camille said, sitting down across from her. "Hypothetically. If a name were removed from a legal document in, say, 1924, and removed consistently from every copy held by every office in the county — how would that be done?"

Nell's eyebrows went up. "That's not hypothetical. That's specific."

"Humor me."

"Well." Nell pushed her glasses up her nose, warming visibly to the problem. "You'd need control of the original and the authority to order copies destroyed or amended. Solicitors could do it — they hold the originals and certify the copies. Or the executors. But here's the thing—" she tapped the table, "—certified copies go to *beneficiaries*. Land registries. Banks. If someone amended them all, then either every beneficiary agreed, or the beneficiaries never saw the originals in the first place."

"And if the beneficiary was, say, a fourteen-year-old girl in 1924?"

"Then whoever controlled the paperwork controlled the estate until she came of age." Nell paused. "Camille. Whose document is this?"

Camille hesitated. Every instinct bred into her by four generations of Parr women said *say nothing, reveal nothing, smile and pour the tea.* But the empty coffin had changed the arithmetic. If the body stayed lost, the codicil activated, and some unknown name stepped into her family's shoes — and she would walk into that blind, trusting a century-old lie, unless she found allies who thought in records rather than legends.

So she took the folded copy of the codicil from her handbag and laid it on the table between the photocopied parishes.

Nell read it twice. Camille watched her lips move slightly on the second pass. When Nell looked up, her expression had gone through surprise and come out the other side into something like awe.

"This is why the church register matters," Nell said quietly. "This is why there's a parish that isn't in any record. Someone built a whole erasure. A church, a burial, a name — all of it, gone. Not lost. *Removed.*"

"My family's entire fortune," Camille said, "rests on a legend. And the legend has just failed to produce a body." She laughed, and it came out bitterer than she intended. "Do you know what my grandmother used to say? *We are Parrs. We do not dig.* I always thought it was about dignity. Now I think it was an instruction."

Nell was quiet for a moment. Then: "There's something else. Something I wasn't going to show anyone yet." She dug into her bag and produced a photograph — old, foxed at the corners, the kind of thing that surfaced in estate sales. It showed a stone crypt, arched, half-lit, with a flight of steps going down into darkness. On the back, in faded pencil: *St. Brannoc's-under-the-Fen, lower vault.*

"That's not the parish church," Camille said slowly. "That's the ruin. Above the fen. But there's no crypt marked in any survey—"

"No," Nell agreed. "There isn't."

They looked at each other across the table, and Camille felt the strange vertigo of alliance forming — two women, neither of whom fully trusted the other, both holding half a map.

"If the codicil activates," Camille said, "someone inherits everything. A name nobody's seen in a hundred years. Which means somewhere out there is a person who has spent their whole life waiting for that coffin to come up empty."

"You think they arranged it?"

"I think," Camille said, "that my family wrote a will with a trapdoor in it, and somebody has finally found the handle."

* * *

She didn't sleep that night. She sat in the suite that had been in her family's keeping for a hundred years — the oldest suite, the founder's suite, currently lent to the Historical Society's ceremony and smelling faintly of another woman's lavender — and she listened to the Gilded Heron breathe.

Old hotels breathed. She'd known that since childhood: the pipes ticking, the floors settling, the wind working at the window frames. But tonight the breathing sounded different. Tonight it sounded like the building was listening back.

At half past two, she heard footsteps in the corridor. Slow, unhurried, passing her door without pausing. She knew the staff's walks — she'd grown up cataloguing them the way other children collected stamps. This one she didn't know, or rather, she knew it and couldn't place it, which was worse.

She got up and looked through the peephole. The corridor was empty. But at the far end, near the service stair, the night porter stood motionless under the wall sconce, facing her door. Not approaching. Just standing. Watching. The way a man stands when he is memorizing something.

When she looked again ten minutes later, he was gone.

Camille returned to bed and lay staring at the ceiling, doing the sums she'd been avoiding. An empty coffin. A redacted name. A sealed annex. A porter who watched doors. And somewhere in this hotel, she was almost certain, a man who had studied the Parr case the way scholars study scripture — patiently, obsessively, waiting a hundred years for the text to yield.

In the morning, she decided, she would find Ivo Montrose. The concierge. The quiet woman with the sharp eyes whom everyone overlooked and nobody fooled. If anyone in Wexley Fen could tell her who was walking her corridors at night, it was her.

But when Camille came down at dawn, the concierge's desk was unattended, and on the blotter, squared neatly to the edge, lay a single sheet of hotel stationery. On it, in a hand she didn't recognize, were written five words:

*Ask her about the annex.*

Camille stood very still, the lobby clock ticking behind her, and understood that whatever game was being played at the Gilded Heron, she had just been dealt into it — by someone who already knew her hand.
