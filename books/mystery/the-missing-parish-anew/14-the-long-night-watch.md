## 14. The Long Night Watch

There is a particular hour at the Gilded Heron when the building stops pretending to be awake. Somewhere between one and two in the morning, the radiators give up their ticking, the lift settles into its shaft like a tired horse into straw, and even the fen outside goes quiet in a way that is less silence than held breath. I had spent eleven years learning that hour from the inside of my small office behind the front desk, and I could have told you — had anyone thought to ask, which no one ever did — exactly how long it lasted on a Tuesday in November.

That night I was not in my office. That night I was sitting in the linen cupboard across from Room 9 with a thermos of coffee gone bitter and a view through the gap where the door hinge had been repaired badly in 1987 by a handyman whose name I also knew, because I know everything about this hotel, which is precisely the problem.

Because someone else knew it too.

* * *

Let me begin earlier in the evening, because a stakeout, like a good sentence, should be built properly or not at all.

At nine o'clock I had done something I had never done in eleven years of service: I had left my ledger out. Not hidden in the drawer with its false bottom — yes, false bottom; a concierge who keeps a private book of solved mysteries and does not keep it under a false bottom deserves whatever happens to her — but lying on the desk itself, closed, its brass clasp undone, its cover facing the ceiling like a bared throat.

I had turned the page to the most recent entry before leaving it. The entry concerning a certain person's comings and goings. The entry I had written knowing — hoping — it would be read.

Then I had gone upstairs to my room, turned off my light at the usual hour, and waited for the hotel to believe I was asleep.

The Gilded Heron believes things easily. It is an old building with old habits, and one of those habits is me: Ivo Montrose, invisible as the wallpaper, reliable as the boiler. Everyone in Wexley Fen assumed I went to bed at ten and rose at six, because that was the story I had spent a decade telling with my punctual footsteps and my drawn curtains. Nobody watches the wallpaper. That had always been my great advantage.

It had stopped being an advantage three days ago, when I found the note tucked inside my ledger quoting my own private observations back to me, word for word, in handwriting so careful it might have been printed.

*You notice everything, Miss Montrose. But you write it down. And what is written down can be read.*

So tonight the wallpaper was watching back.

* * *

At half past eleven, the corridor outside Room 9 did what it always did: nothing, beautifully. The carpet swallowed footsteps. The sconces burned their low amber. Room 9 belonged to Mr. Ambrose Teale, retired police archivist, a man who slept — I had observed — with the television on, volume at two, tuned to a channel that broadcast only static after midnight. He said it helped him sleep. I suspected it was a habit from his working years, when silence meant something had gone wrong.

I understood the impulse better than he knew.

My perch in the linen cupboard was not comfortable, and I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in this account. I had wedged myself between a shelf of folded bath sheets and the hot-water tank, which ticked like a patient clock, and I had brought a cushion stolen from the window seat in the Blue Suite, and still my left foot had gone to sleep by midnight and my right was considering following. The coffee was an act of optimism. The gap in the doorframe showed me perhaps four feet of corridor: the stretch between Rooms 8 and 10, the corner of the fire extinguisher case, and the edge of the stairwell door.

It was, I reflected, rather like fishing. One sat in the cold. One waited. One told oneself stories about the nobility of patience while one's feet died quietly beneath one.

I had done a great deal of waiting in my life. Waiting for guests to reveal themselves. Waiting for the town to remember I existed. This was different. This was waiting with my heart going like a wren in a box, because for the first time the thing I was waiting for might be waiting for me.

* * *

At twenty past one, the stairwell door opened.

I will be honest: my first reaction was disappointment so sharp it was almost physical, because the figure that came through it was Nell Fairweather, the genealogist, in her dressing gown and wool socks, carrying her laptop against her chest like a shield. She padded to Room 12 — her own room — hesitated, then knocked very softly on the door of Room 9 instead.

Teale answered in his cardigan, static hissing behind him. They spoke for perhaps ninety seconds. I caught fragments. *"…the land registry office opens at nine…"* and *"…if it's a second grave, it'll show in the tithe maps…"* and Nell saying, with the fierce brightness she used when she was frightened, *"I'm not stopping. I just wanted you to know where I'd be."*

They were planning tomorrow. They were being careful with each other. Something in my chest went soft and painful watching it — two people who had arrived at my hotel as strangers, becoming allies over a hundred-year-old crime, while I crouched among the bath sheets unable to warn them that the danger was closer than either of them guessed.

Closer than I had guessed, until recently.

Nell went back to her room. Teale stood in his doorway a moment longer, looking up and down the corridor with the flat professional sweep of a man who had once done this for a living, and then he saw it.

I saw him see it.

His gaze stopped on the linen cupboard door. Held. Two seconds. Three. Then he shook his head almost imperceptibly — *no, of course not, don't be foolish, Harold* — and went inside, and the lock clicked, and I let out a breath I had been holding since the coroner's too-quick composure at the graveside.

But I filed it away, because that is what I do. *Teale checks corners. Teale trusts his instincts except when they frighten him.* A man like that could be saved by information. I made a note to give him some.

* * *

At quarter to two, the stairwell door opened again, and this time nobody knocked on any doors.

I heard the footsteps before I saw anything — soft ones, unhurried, with a rhythm I almost recognized and couldn't place, which bothered me more than the footsteps themselves. I know every tread in this hotel. I know Mrs. Petchey the cook walks on her heels, and Dr. Lomax walks on his toes because he fancies himself quiet, and Camille Parr walks like she is owed the carpet personally. These steps were none of these. They were *practiced* at being none of these, which is a skill, and skills are learned, and learned skills leave traces.

A shape crossed my four feet of corridor.

I saw what I later described to Inspector Reeve as "a silhouette," and what I privately called, in the privacy of my own skull, *a person-shaped absence*. Tall-ish. Moving with the particular economy of someone who has walked this corridor many times in the dark and knows exactly which floorboards to forgive. The sconce light caught them only at the edges: the line of a shoulder, the fall of a sleeve, and then —

— the hand.

The hand came up as the figure passed the extinguisher case, and in it, briefly lit, was a key. Not a room key. Room keys at the Gilded Heron are heavy brass fobs shaped like herons, charming and jingly and impossible to use silently. This was a plain steel key on a plain ring, and I knew it the way I know my own teeth, because I had inventoried them a hundred times: the master key. One of three. One kept in the safe behind my desk, one in the manager's office, and one —

One that had gone missing from the night porter's board eleven days ago, according to the log I kept of the log, because I am the sort of person who keeps a log of the log, and thank God for it, because everyone had told me I was being fussy.

The figure paused at Room 6. The empty room. The room I had chosen — no, the room *they* had chosen, because I had arranged for Room 6 to be left vacant, its door unlocked, its lamp lit, my ledger moved from my office desk to the bedside table there at half past nine, positioned exactly as I position it every night in my own quarters, clasp open, page turned to the entry about *them*.

Bait. I had baited a trap with the only thing I owned that mattered, and now I watched a hand turn the handle of Room 6 without a sound, without a fumbled second attempt, and slip inside, and close the door with the care of a parent tucking in a child.

Hunter and hunted. I had spent two weeks feeling the eyes on my back, reading my words, walking my corridors. And now here I was in a cupboard with my heart hammering, and there *they* were, in the room I had prepared, doing exactly what I wanted.

It should have felt like victory.

It felt like standing on a frozen fen, listening to the ice talk.

* * *

Here is the difficulty with catching someone, which no one tells you because everyone who knows it is either a policeman or a criminal: the moment of capture is the moment you are closest to them. All my life I had solved mysteries at a distance — observed, deduced, concluded, and then arranged for the truth to reach whoever needed it without my name attached. Distance was safety. Distance was invisibility. Invisibility had been my armor for fifty-one years, and I had put it on so long ago I had forgotten it was armor and thought it was skin.

If I stepped out of that cupboard now, I would see a face. A face meant a name. A name meant that whatever happened next happened to *me*, out loud, in the world, where people could see.

And if I stayed in the cupboard, I would learn nothing except that my trap worked, and the person would walk out again, and tomorrow they would know the ledger had been moved, and they would know why, and the last thin advantage I had — that they believed me merely observant rather than *hunting* — would burn like the paper it was written on.

I thought about Vane. Harold Vane, dead in the ruined church, arranged like a photograph from a 1924 file. He had known things. He had known them alone, quietly, without telling anyone, because he was a proud man and an archivist and archivists believe knowledge is safest kept. The killer had studied the original investigation. So had I. The difference between us, I had told myself, was that I cared about people and they cared about paper.

Vane had probably told himself something similar about his killer, right up until the end.

I got my dead foot under me. I stood up in the linen cupboard, among the bath towels, in the dark, and I made the choice that would end my old life one way or the other, though I did not yet know which way.

I reached for the door.

* * *

What happened next took eleven seconds, and I have replayed all of them so many times since that they have worn grooves in me like a record played too often.

I opened the cupboard door. The corridor was empty — the figure had gone into Room 6 and the door was shut, and I had perhaps a minute, perhaps less, before they finished photographing or copying or simply *reading* my ledger and slipped away the way they had come.

I crossed the corridor. My plan — such as it was, assembled in the space of four footsteps — was simple: I would not confront. Confrontation was for people with witnesses. I would do what I had always done. I would *observe*. I would press my ear to the door of Room 6, learn what I could — a voice, a breath, the scratch of a pen, the click of a camera — and then I would go down to the desk, telephone Inspector Reeve at home, where I knew from the directory that he ate supper at seven and took his tea at ten and lay awake until midnight worrying about the exhumation, and I would say, out loud, in my own voice, to another human being:

*Ivo Montrose. I have something for you.*

Being seen. On purpose. As a choice.

I got my ear to the door of Room 6, and through it I heard — nothing. No pen. No camera. No breathing that I could distinguish from the radiator's tick. And then, from directly behind me, from the direction of the stairwell, soft as a closing book:

"Miss Montrose."

I did not scream. I want that noted. Fifty-one years of composure held, barely, and I turned around slowly with my hands folded, because if I was going to be seen, I was going to be seen *composed*, damn it all.

The corridor was empty.

The voice had come from nowhere and everywhere, the way voices do in old hotels, and I understood in one cold drop that I had been played. Room 6 was the decoy. The figure I had watched was meant to be seen. The real visit to my ledger had happened hours ago, or would happen later, or was happening right now somewhere I was not — and the message of that voice, gentle and unhurried and horribly familiar in a way I still could not place, was not *I caught you*.

It was *I know you're hunting now. Good. Now we're both playing.*

"Room 6," the voice said, nearer, or I dreamed it nearer, and the sconce at the end of the corridor dimmed as if the wiring itself were holding its breath. "You should check the bedside table. I've left you something. You leave such interesting things for me — it seemed rude."

And then the stairwell door sighed shut, and the corridor was mine again, and my legs informed me they had opinions about all this.

I did not go into Room 6 immediately. I stood in the corridor of my own hotel, in my dressing gown, shaking, and I made myself count to sixty, because panic is a guest too and must be checked out politely. Then I went in.

On the bedside table, squared neatly beside my ledger — untouched, I checked first, untouched, thank God, untouched — lay a single sheet of paper, typed, in the same careful print as the note before.

It said:

*Chapter fourteen. You're ahead of schedule.*

And beneath it, as if the writer could not resist, as if grievance leaked out of them the way ink leaks through cheap paper:

*You think you set a trap tonight. Tomorrow, ask yourself who chose which room you'd watch. Ask who told the genealogist to knock on Teale's door where you could hear. I have read your ledger, Miss Montrose. Every page. I know how you think — it's how I learned to think. Sleep well.*

I stood there a long time. Long enough for the radiator to tick forty times. Long enough for the coffee in my thermos, wherever I'd abandoned it, to go fully cold.

Then I picked up my ledger, clasped it shut, and carried it against my chest the way Nell carried her laptop, and I went downstairs to the front desk, and I lifted the telephone, and I dialed Inspector Reeve's home number from memory, because of course I knew it from memory.

He answered on the fourth ring, awake, unsurprised, and when I said, "Inspector, this is Ivo Montrose, and I need to report a crime," my voice did not shake at all.

What I did not tell him — what I sat turning over afterward in the small hours, in my office, with the ledger locked in the safe and my back to the wall — was the thing that had finally surfaced as I listened to that voice in the corridor. The reason it had sounded familiar. The reason my skin had crawled before my mind caught up.

I had heard it every evening for eleven years.

*"Goodnight, Miss Montrose. Quiet night?"*

The night porter's greeting, at the change of shift, at seven o'clock, five hundred nights a year.

And the master key had gone missing from the porter's board.
