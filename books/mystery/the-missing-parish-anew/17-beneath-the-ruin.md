## 17. Beneath the Ruin

The key to the crypt was not a key at all. It was a hymn.

That was what Nell had discovered in the land records, cross-referenced against the constable's photograph and Camille's family papers: the entrance to the lower vault beneath the ruined parish of St. Aldhelm's was sealed by a lock whose combination had been set to the opening bars of a psalm — the one carved, worn nearly smooth by weather, into the lintel above the church door. *I will lift up mine eyes unto the hills.* Seven words, seven numbers, if you knew which letters to count and which to skip.

"Your founder had a sense of humor," Nell said, crouched at the base of the collapsed vestry with her torch between her teeth. "Or a sense of paranoia. Possibly both."

"He was a man who built a town on a fen," Camille said. "He must have understood that everything solid is only water pretending."

Ivo stood a little apart from them, as she always did, and watched them work with the particular pleasure she took in watching competent people be competent. It was nearly dawn. They had come out under a sky the color of pewter, across the fen path where the reeds stood stiff with frost, and now the three of them were gathered at the mouth of a ruin that every map for a hundred years had insisted did not exist.

She had dressed for invisibility, out of habit — dark coat, dark scarf, boots that made no sound on stone — and then, halfway down the path, had caught herself and laughed aloud, startling a heron up out of the shallows. There was no point in it anymore. The porter had read her ledger. He knew her thoughts better than she'd written them. Invisibility was a garment she had already lost; she might as well walk in the open.

"You find something funny?" Camille asked.

"I was remembering," Ivo said, "that I once solved the matter of Mrs. Pettibone's missing wedding ring by noticing that her cat had learned to open drawers. I thought that was the strangest case I would ever work." She looked at the ruined archway, at the black gap beneath it. "I was wrong."

Nell spat out the torch. "Got it. The psalm numbers. Stand back."

There was a grinding, a groan of old iron, and then a breath of air came up out of the ground — cold and mineral and very old, like the smell of a cellar in a house that no longer stands. All three of them went quiet. Even the fen seemed to hold its breath.

"Ladies first?" said Nell.

"After you," said Ivo. "You found the door. And besides, if there's anything down there that bites, I'd rather it started on someone younger than me."

* * *

The stairs were cut into the living rock of the fen's gravel spine, and they descended in a spiral so tight that Ivo's shoulder brushed the wall with every step. Someone had built this carefully, secretly, and long before the church above it — the masonry was older, cruder, marked with masons' marks that Nell stopped to photograph with hands that would not quite steady.

"Aldous Parr didn't dig this," Nell murmured. "He found it. This is pre-Norman. Maybe older. The church was built on top of something that was here first."

"Like everything else in Wexley Fen," Camille said. "We build our fine houses over other people's graves and call it founding."

They came down into a chamber that the torchlight could not finish describing. It was round, perhaps thirty feet across, its vaulted ceiling lost in shadow above them, and around its walls ran niches — dozens of them, empty, their ledges furred with pale dust except where the dust had been disturbed in long-ago sweeps. In the center of the floor stood a stone table, an altar or a bier, and on it lay a coffin.

Not the coffin from the exhumation. That one had been polished oak with brass fittings, made to be seen by mourners. This one was plain elm, unadorned, the kind of box a carpenter makes quickly and quietly and without asking questions. It had been opened. The lid leaned against the table's side like a door left ajar.

"Oh," said Camille softly. It was not a word so much as an exhalation.

Ivo approached the way she approached everything — slowly, reading. She read the dust on the floor: footprints, old ones, two sets, one large and one small, coming and going many times but never recently. She read the niches: all empty, save one, far along the wall, where a small wooden box sat with a nameplate too corroded to read. She read the coffin itself: the grain of the elm, the hand-forged nails, and inside —

Inside lay a skeleton, articulated, complete, dressed in the rotted remnants of a fine wool suit. On the skull's left temple was a healed fracture, an old injury long survived. And tucked beneath the crossed bones of the right hand, preserved by the dry cold of the vault, was a folded paper.

"Don't touch it yet," Ivo said, when Nell reached out. "Photograph it first. Everything. Then we'll see what he wanted us to know."

"He?" Camille asked.

"The constable," Ivo said. "This is his work. Look." She indicated the floor with her torch. "Two sets of prints, repeated visits over years. A man who kept coming back to check on what he'd done. And the coffin is elm because he couldn't afford oak, or wouldn't risk ordering it — but he laid the body straight, folded the hands, put the paper where a prayer book would go. Whatever else he was, he was decent about it. He gave Parr a burial, even if it had to be a secret one."

Camille had gone very still. In the torchlight her face had the look of a woman watching a house burn from across a river — unable to help, unable to look away, aware that everything she owned was inside.

"That's my great-great-grandfather," she said. "That's him. That's actually him." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"Yes," Ivo said gently. "And I think, when we read that paper, we're going to learn why he's been lying here for a century while the whole town mourned an empty box."

* * *

It took Nell twenty minutes to photograph everything to her own exacting standard, and Ivo used the time to walk the chamber's circumference, counting niches, reading the faint ghosts of inscriptions. Forty-one niches. Thirty-nine empty. One holding the small box. One — near the spiral stair — holding nothing but a candle stub fused to the ledge by decades of wax, and beside it, scratched into the stone in a careful clerk's hand:

*Forgive me. I did what was asked and more than was asked. God sees the difference.*

"Here," Ivo said. "Come and read this."

The three of them stood before the scratched confession like pilgrims at a shrine. It was Nell who finally said, "Asked? Asked by whom?"

"By Aldous Parr himself, I should think," Ivo said. "Or arranged before he died. Think it through. The constable didn't steal a body. Nobody steals a founder's corpse. But a man who knows he's dying — really dying, and knows his death will trigger a codicil, an inheritance, a war among his heirs — a man like that might arrange for his own disappearance. Might ask one trusted officer to bury him properly, quietly, below the parish everyone thinks has vanished, and let the legend do the rest." She turned to Camille. "What exactly does your codicil say happens if the body is never found?"

Camille recited it as though she'd been repeating it in her sleep, which Ivo suspected she had. "*Should the remains of Aldous Parr never be recovered, the estate shall pass entire to the lawful heir named herein,*" — and there, of course, the redaction, the black bar that appeared in every copy — "*whose claim the family acknowledges in perpetuity.*"

"In perpetuity," Ivo repeated. "Which means the family has known, all along, that there was another heir. An acknowledged heir. A second family." She watched Camille's face. "You didn't know."

"We knew there was a redaction. We assumed it was shame — some scandal, some illegitimacy they wanted hidden. We assumed the clause was a formality, a legal courtesy that would never activate." Camille's laugh was thin. "A hundred years of Parr pride, and the whole fortune was sitting on a promise we were too squeamish to read."

Nell had, meanwhile, eased the paper from beneath the skeletal fingers with tweezers and a reverence she usually reserved for rare maps. She carried it to the flat of the stone table and unfolded it under three torches held close.

The handwriting was small, upright, clerk-neat. The same hand as the scratch on the wall.

*If you are reading this, then the truth has outlived us all, and I am glad of it.*

*I, Thomas Ellery, constable of Wexley Fen, do set down what I did and why. In the winter of 1924, Mr. Aldous Parr sent for me. He was dying — the doctors gave him a month, though he told no one, for he wished to settle his affairs without pity or interference. His affairs included a wife, a son, and a daughter born to Miss Ruth Callow, the schoolmistress, whom he loved and could not marry and would not abandon. The codicil names that daughter's line. He feared that if his death were known, the family would destroy the document and the child's claim with it.*

*So we made a plan together, he and I. He would vanish. I would report what I was instructed to report. The town would grieve, the legend would grow, and the codicil would stand unchallenged, because a missing man cannot be declared dead without a body, and without death there can be no probate — only waiting. He thought that was the cleverest part. "Let them wait," he said. "Waiting is the interest paid on a secret."*

*I buried him here myself, in the old place beneath the old church, which his grandfather showed him when he was a boy. I have visited every year on the anniversary. I have kept the ledger entries true and let the copies lie, because he asked it, and because I judged — rightly or wrongly, God judge me — that a living girl's inheritance was worth more than a dead town's certainty.*

*His daughter's name was Miriam. If any of her blood reads this: you were not forgotten. You were protected. Forgive the manner of it.*

*Thomas Ellery*

Nobody spoke for a long time. The torches hummed. Somewhere far above, water dripped through the ruined roof with the patience of centuries.

Then Camille sat down on the edge of the stone table — sat down, heedless of the dust and the dignity of a hundred years of Parr propriety, right beside the ancestor whose fortune she had inherited — and began, quite unexpectedly, to cry.

Ivo did not touch her. She had spent a lifetime learning that some griefs are private even in company. But she stood close, and after a while she said, "Miriam Callow. Nell — the schoolmistress. Can you find her line?"

"Already thinking." Nell was scribbling. "Callow... there are Callows in the parish registers up to the thirties, then they thin out. If Miriam married, changed name..." She looked up, and Ivo saw the genealogist's mind working like a loom. "But Ivo — think. The porter. The night porter. He's studied this file his whole life. He told you his grandfather was the redacted heir. Which means—"

"Which means his grandfather was Miriam's son, or grandson," Ivo finished quietly. "And everything he's done — Vane, the exhumation, the fire he's surely planning — he's done to claim a promise that was always real. He doesn't need to kill anyone. The paper says it all. The law will honor the codicil." She shook her head slowly. "He doesn't want justice. He wants the whole estate, and he wants no witnesses to how he got it."

The drip of water. The cold. Camille wiping her face with the heel of her hand like a child.

"We should take the letter up," Nell said. "To the police, to the solicitors—"

"No," Ivo said.

Both women turned.

"Not yet. Listen to me, both of you. The moment this letter leaves this room, it exists in the world — and the porter will know within hours, because he has proven again and again that he knows things before we do. Right now he believes the crypt is his secret, that we've come down here and found bones and guesses. Guesses he can dismiss, evidence he can burn. But this—" she touched the paper lightly, "—this is conviction. If he learns it exists while it's still down here, he'll seal us in with it and burn the ruin above, and no one will dig us out for a generation. If it leaves safely, in daylight, with witnesses, with the police already informed and waiting — then it convicts him instead." She met their eyes in turn. "We go up together. We say nothing. Tonight, at the hotel, I spring the trap I've been building since the night my ledger was read. Tomorrow morning, this letter goes to Inspector Hale with three signatures on the chain of custody. One night. That's all I'm asking."

"One night," Camille echoed. She looked back at the coffin, at the man who had engineered his own erasure to protect a daughter the town never knew. "One night more. He waited a hundred years. I suppose he can wait until breakfast."

They climbed the spiral stair in single file, Ivo last, and at the top she paused and looked back down into the dark — at the forty-one niches, the plain elm coffin, the small corroded box in its lonely niche, and the candle stub burned down to nothing by a faithful man's yearly visits.

"Rest easy, Mr. Parr," she murmured. "You're found."

Then she climbed out into the grey morning, and pulled the stone closed behind them, and heard it seat home with a sound like a book shutting.

It was only when they reached the fen path, and she saw the figure standing motionless among the reeds a quarter mile off — a slight man in a porter's dark uniform, watching them with the stillness of a heron watching water — that she understood, with a lurch of cold in her stomach, that the trap had a second door, and that all night long, they had been walking through it.
