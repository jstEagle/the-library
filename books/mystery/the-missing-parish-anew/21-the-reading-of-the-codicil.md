## 21. The Reading of the Codicil

The solicitor's office in Wexley Fen smelled of beeswax and old paper, and Camille Parr had spent forty years of her life in rooms that smelled exactly like this one, waiting for men in grey suits to tell her what her name was worth.

She had promised herself she would not cry. She had promised Ivo Montrose she would not cry, and Ivo had a way of knowing when you broke a promise, even across a room, even without looking up from whatever she was writing in that ledger of hers. So Camille sat with her spine straight and her hands folded in her lap, and she watched Mr. Pellowe — the third Pellowe to handle the Parr estate, a small damp man with a magnificent pair of spectacles — arrange the documents on the table before her.

The room was not crowded. That had been her doing. There was no reason, she had decided, for the reading of the codicil to be a spectacle. Wexley Fen had had quite enough spectacle for one autumn: the exhumation, the empty coffin, the fire at the ruined parish, the arrest of a man whose face she had passed a hundred times in the Gilded Heron's corridor without ever once seeing him. The town had gorged itself on mystery. She would not feed it dessert.

So it was a small gathering. Mr. Pellowe. Herself. Ivo Montrose, seated by the window with her notebook closed on her knee, invited — at Camille's insistence — because the woman had earned a seat at any table in this town she cared to sit at. And one other.

Margaret Fenwick sat as far from Camille as the room allowed, which was not very far, and held her teacup the way a person holds something they are not entirely sure they've been given permission to keep.

She was the porter's sister. That was the phrase everyone used, though it did her a disservice. She was also the great-granddaughter of the redacted name — the heir written out of every copy of the codicil, the child of Aldous Parr's unacknowledged second family, the line that had waited a hundred years in the margins of other people's documents. Her brother had wanted what the margins owed him, and he had wanted it badly enough to kill for it, or at least to arrange for a man to die and for three women to burn in a crypt for it. Margaret, by every account Ivo had quietly assembled and every account the police had confirmed, had not known. Had not spoken to her brother in eleven years. Had, in fact, changed her name to escape the family entirely, which was, Camille thought, the most Parr thing she could have done.

"Right," said Mr. Pellowe, in the tone of a man about to dive into cold water. "The codicil."

He read it aloud. Camille had read it herself a dozen times since the police had released the estate documents — read the original, unredacted version that had surfaced with the constable's effects, and the mutilated copies that had sat in this office for a century, every mention of the heir's name struck through in that careful, deliberate ink. Hearing it in Pellowe's dry voice made it stranger, not plainer. *In the event that the body of Aldous Parr is never recovered, the estate shall pass in its entirety to* — and here Pellowe paused, because the name was no longer a redaction, it was a name, written plainly in the restored text: *Thomas Parr-Fenwick, of the second family, and his heirs lawfully begotten.*

"Heirs lawfully begotten," Pellowe said, and set the page down. "That would be Mrs. Fenwick. And, had he lived and been convicted — which he has been, of course, of the murder of Mr. Vane and of the attempted — well. The law is clear on what conviction does to a claim. But the conviction does not extinguish the *line*, Mrs. Parr, only the man. The estate is divided. The principal holdings — the house, the land, the shares — remain with you, as the recognized descendant of the first family, subject to the terms we discussed. A portion of the liquid estate, and the interest in the Gilded Heron Hotel, passes to Mrs. Fenwick as the heir of the second."

The teacup rattled very slightly against its saucer.

"I don't want the hotel," Margaret said.

"You may not want it," Pellowe said, with the mild patience of a man who had watched people want and not-want things across three generations, "but it is yours. One-eighth interest, to be precise. Your brother's actions, I'm afraid, have made the whole business public, and the court was not inclined to reward concealment with more concealment. You may sell your share, of course. There would be buyers."

Camille watched the woman's face. She had spent her life learning to read faces in solicitors' offices — the flicker of calculation, the flinch at a number, the small cruel satisfactions. Margaret Fenwick's face did none of those things. It did something rarer. It looked *afraid of the gift*, the way a stray looks at an open hand.

"My brother studied that hotel," Margaret said quietly. "For years. I thought it was — I don't know what I thought. A hobby. He was a quiet man. He read everything. He used to say the building remembered things people didn't." She looked down at her tea. "He was talking about the crypt. He was talking about it the whole time, and I sent him a Christmas card every year and never once asked what he was reading."

The room was quiet. Through the window Camille could see the fen, silver and flat in the pale morning, and the far smudge of the ruined parish, its burned roofline black against the sky.

"The hotel remembers things," Ivo said.

It was the first thing she had said in twenty minutes, and Camille nearly laughed at the sheer Ivo-ness of it — the concierge, correcting the record even in a solicitor's office. Ivo unfolded her hands.

"Mrs. Fenwick. If you keep your share — and I'd advise you to think before you sell, because this town has a habit of undervaluing things and regretting it a hundred years later — you should know that the hotel's staff will be told the truth. All of it. The good and the bad. I have spent my career keeping secrets for other people, and I have recently learned the cost of that." She looked at Camille, briefly, and Camille held the look, because they had an understanding now that had been paid for in fire and a dead man in a ruined church. "Your family's name is in the ledgers. It has been in the ledgers since 1924, under the ink. I don't intend to let anyone alter it again."

Margaret looked at her for a long moment. "And the staff? Will they — I'm the sister of a murderer. They'll know."

"They'll know you're the sister of a man who was caught," Ivo said, "because a woman he'd never met refused to stay quiet. In my experience, staff remember that sort of thing longer than they remember the other."

Camille did laugh then, just a little, and Margaret's mouth twitched, which Camille counted as the first victory of the morning.

"The remaining terms," Pellowe said, shuffling pages with the enthusiasm of a man nearing the end of a very long sermon. "Mrs. Parr. The conditions attached to your portion. You wished them read into the record."

"I did."

She had written them herself, three drafts, on hotel stationery, late at night, with the fen fog pressing against the window. She had expected the writing of them to feel like loss. It had not. It had felt like setting down something heavy she had been carrying on behalf of a dead man who had never once carried it himself.

"First," she said, and her voice was steadier than she'd expected. "The parish records. All of them — the registers, the maps, the constable's file, the ledgers from the Gilded Heron, everything recovered from the crypt and the estate archive. They go to the town. To the Wexley Fen Historical Society, to be held in common, catalogued properly, and open to any person who walks in off the street. Including," she added, glancing at Ivo, "genealogists. Especially genealogists. The town has spent a century being lied to by paper. It can spend the next century being told the truth by it."

"Second," said Pellowe, reading. "The ruined parish church and its grounds are to be conveyed to the town as a memorial site, with the crypt sealed as a grave — a proper grave, marked, with both families' names upon it. The remains of Aldous Parr to be reinterred there, with the names of Thomas Parr-Fenwick and his descendants added to the stone."

Margaret's breath caught. Camille had expected that. She had cried herself, the night she'd written it — not for Aldous, whom she had come to think of as a legend built on a lie, but for the hundred years of Sundays the second family had sat in some other church, in some other town, singing hymns to a founder's name they didn't know was theirs.

"Third." Camille took this one herself, because it was the one that mattered. "The Parr Trust is to be reconstituted. Not as an inheritance — I've had enough of inheritance — but as a maintenance fund. The churchyard, the records, the school in the town that bears the family name, and the wages of a part-time archivist. The position is to be offered first to Miss Nell Ashcombe, who found the second grave when every adult in this county was content to leave it buried."

"She'll say yes," Ivo said. "She's already started cataloguing. She's been doing it in the hotel lounge for a week. She thinks nobody's noticed."

"Everyone's noticed," Camille said. "She's very loud with the card files."

And there it was — the gentle humor of the thing, surfacing through the solemnity like a fish through fen water. Even Margaret smiled. Even Pellowe, who Camille suspected had not smiled since the Coronation, permitted himself a small crease at the corner of his mouth.

"Fourth," Pellowe read. "The Gilded Heron Hotel. The one-eighth interest passing to Mrs. Fenwick notwithstanding, the controlling interest remains with Mrs. Parr, subject to the following direction: that the hotel's oldest suite, Suite One, be designated in perpetuity as the archive and consulting room of the hotel's concierge, and that the concierge holding that position be granted authority over all matters of the hotel's history, records, and — " Pellowe paused, adjusted his spectacles, and read again, as though the words might have rearranged themselves. " — *and discreet inquiries arising therefrom.*"

"That's not a standard legal phrase," Margaret said.

"No," Ivo said. "It isn't."

Camille turned in her chair. Ivo was looking at the window, at the fen, with the expression of a woman who had spent her whole life being the person nobody looked at and had only recently, painfully, learned to bear being seen. Camille had watched her stand in a burning churchyard and refuse to run. She had watched her read a ledger aloud to a police inspector and say *my name is on the first page* as though it were a confession, when it had been the proudest sentence Camille had ever heard anyone say.

"I'm told," Camille said, "that there was once a sign in the back office that said *no cases*. I'm told it's been taken down. I'd like to make sure it stays down. Legally. Permanently. It seemed the sort of thing a codicil is for."

Ivo said nothing for a moment. Then she opened the notebook on her knee, and Camille saw — just a glimpse, before the cover closed again — that the first page was no longer blank. Something was pinned or pasted there, a folded paper, a closed case.

"You'll want a salary provision," Ivo said. "And a budget. I've drafted one."

"Of course you have," Camille said, and this time she did laugh, properly, and found that her eyes were wet after all, and decided that Ivo Montrose, of all people, could be trusted to pretend not to notice.

* * *

The signing took an hour. Signatures, witnesses, Pellowe's small dry voice intoning *and in witness whereof* over and over like a tide coming in. When it was done, the three of them — Camille, Ivo, Margaret — stood on the solicitor's steps in the thin cold sun, and the town went by, and the town looked. That was the thing Camille noticed. People looked. At Margaret, walking down the high street with her share of a stolen-and-returned inheritance in her handbag; they looked, and some of them nodded, and one woman — the constable's daughter, ninety years old and sharp as a tack, wrapped in a coat the size of a small tent — stopped and took Margaret's arm and said, loudly enough for the whole street, "You've got his chin, you know. The founder's. I always wondered who'd got it."

Margaret walked on with her chin up. It was, Camille thought, a fine chin. It had waited long enough to be seen.

At the corner, the constable's daughter pressed something into Margaret's hand — a small photograph, Camille saw, though not what was on it — and said something too quiet for the street, and the two of them stood together for a moment, the last of the old secret and the first of the new truth, and then went their separate ways, and the town closed over them like water, gently, without a wound.

* * *

That evening Camille walked out to the ruin. She had told no one, which was unlike her, and told Ivo, which was like her — a note left at the desk, *gone to the parish, back by dark, don't send a search party unless it's genuinely interesting.* The reply, in pencil, in that small precise hand: *Define genuinely interesting.*

The church was a skeleton now, the fire having finished what a century of neglect had started. But the town had been out in force with ladders and lime wash and that stubborn Wexley energy that Camille had once dismissed as smallness and now understood as the only thing that had ever held this place together. The walls stood. The east window, glassless, framed the fen going gold and grey with evening. And in the churchyard, under a new stone not yet fully weathered, the grave.

*ALDOUS PARR, 1849–1924, AND THOMAS PARR-FENWICK, 1891–1946. ONE PARISH. TWO FAMILIES. AT REST.*

Nell had argued for a longer inscription. Camille had overruled her. Some truths were better as foundations than as monuments.

She stood at the stone for a long while, and she found she had nothing to say to the dead, which was itself a kind of peace. Her family had spent a century speaking for Aldous Parr — inventing his virtues, curating his legend, building an empire on a coffin they'd never once opened. Let him be quiet now. Let him be a name on a stone shared with a brother nobody had known he had, in a parish that was, at last, on the map.

When she turned to go, Ivo was standing by the lych-gate.

"You walked out," Camille said.

"I took the long way." Ivo came up the path, hands in her coat pockets, and stopped beside her at the grave. For a while neither of them spoke. The fen made its small evening noises. Somewhere a heron — the actual bird, not the hotel — lifted off the water with a sound like a sail filling.

"I've been thinking," Ivo said, "about what inheritance is. I had it down as a dirty word. Everything anyone inherited in this story was a lie or a debt."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's a job." She looked at the stone. "Somebody has to keep the records. Somebody has to answer the door when a stranger arrives with a question. Somebody has to notice." She glanced at Camille, and there was the ghost of the old dry humor. "You've just hired somebody to notice, permanently. Legally. I hope you know what you've done."

"I've been told it's not a standard legal phrase."

"It's going to be. Nell's already using it. She's ordered a stamp."

Camille laughed, and the sound went out over the fen, and she thought: *this is what it was supposed to be all along.* Not the fortune, not the name on the school, not the legend. This — a town that knew its own story, a grave with both names on it, a woman with a notebook walking beside her because she chose to be there and not because she was paid to be invisible.

They walked back along the fen path as the light failed. At the edge of the hotel grounds, Ivo stopped.

"One more thing," she said. "The archive. Suite One. I've begun the catalogue, and there's a box I haven't opened. It came from the solicitor's office this afternoon — papers found behind the safe when they moved it. Dated 1924. Addressed to the constable, never delivered." She paused, and in the dark her voice was very quiet. "The handwriting on the envelope is Aldous Parr's own. And it isn't addressed to the constable at all. That's just where it was sent to be hidden. The name inside — the name it's really for —"

She held up the envelope, pale in the dusk, unopened.

"— is mine."
