## 3. A Ledger With Teeth

Detective Ibarra arrived at the archive at nine in the morning, which told me two things: she was thorough, and she wanted me uncomfortable. Nobody is comfortable in an archive at nine in the morning. The light comes through the clerestory windows at an angle that makes every dust mote legible, and the smell of old binding glue and dehumidifier gets into your clothes. It is a room that remembers everything and forgives nothing. She walked into it like a woman entering a confessional she intended to run herself.

"Señor Reyes." She didn't shake hands. Detectives on television shake hands; real ones keep their hands free for notebooks. "Thank you for making time."

"I work here," I said. "Making time is most of the job."

She looked around at the shelves — the parish registers going back to 1841, the maps, the boxes of correspondence tied with cotton tape, the finding aids I had written in my own hand over eleven years — and I watched her do what detectives do, which is read a room as a biography of its occupant.

"You catalogued all this yourself?"

"Most of it. The crypt inventory was mine too."

"That's why we're talking." She set her notebook on the reading table, opened it flat, and did not sit. "Walk me through 2009."

I had known this question was coming since the stones came out of the coffin yesterday afternoon, and I had spent part of the night — after Marisol left, after I'd washed two glasses instead of one out of habit and stood holding the second like an idiot — rehearsing my answer. That's the trouble with rehearsal: it makes you sound rehearsed.

"In 2009 the diocese ordered a full condition survey of the crypt," I said. "Moisture damage, structural settling, that sort of thing. I spent three weeks down there with a clipboard and a humidity meter, itemizing every interment, every coffin plate, every niche."

"And your records were accurate?"

"They were accurate when I wrote them."

"When you wrote them." She let that hang. "The survey took three weeks. Your log shows you entered the crypt on the fourteenth of June and didn't sign out again until the twenty-ninth. Except—" she turned a page, and I confess my stomach dropped before she even said it— "there's a gap. The night of the twenty-second. You went in at six-forty in the evening. There's no exit time. No entry for the following day until noon."

"One hour," I said. "One missing hour, sixteen years ago."

"Sixteen years is not long in this building, I'm told. Is it long in yours?"

"It's long enough to forget an evening."

"Forget it completely? You?" She glanced at the shelf nearest her, where my finding aid for the 1918 influenza registers sat spine-out, cross-referenced in three places. "Forgive me, Señor Reyes, but you don't strike me as a man who loses an hour. You strike me as a man who invoices himself for fifteen-minute increments."

It was a fair hit. I have been accused — by Marisol, affectionately; by my late partner Tomás, less so — of being a man who keeps receipts against his own life. When Tomás died I found I had catalogued our years together more thoroughly than I had lived them: ticket stubs, restaurant bills, a museum map from Oaxaca with his handwriting in the margin. Grief, for an archivist, is just another accession without a deed of gift.

"The twenty-second of June, 2009," I said slowly. "There was a storm that week. A pipe leaked in the north transept and I was moving boxes half the night. I may simply have forgotten to sign out."

"You may have." She wrote something. The scratch of her pen was very loud. "Or you may have been doing something in that crypt you didn't want logged."

"Such as?"

"Such as emptying a coffin."

The sentence landed in the room and just stayed there, the way heavy things do. Somewhere above us the cathedral bells rang the quarter hour, indifferent as ever.

"Detective," I said, "if I had emptied Emiliano Vidal's coffin in 2009, I would have needed to move roughly forty kilos of remains out of a sealed crypt beneath a working cathedral, past a sexton, past the night watchman, past—" I gestured at myself— "me. And then I would have needed to fill the coffin with river stones, which means a trip to a riverbed, which means transport, which means witnesses. All to wait sixteen years for a court order nobody could have predicted. Does that sound like planning to you, or does it sound like a novel?"

"It sounds like something," she said. "Novels are also made of things people hide." She turned another page. "Tell me about the transfer forms."

And there it was. The thing I had been sitting on all night like a man sitting on a lit stove and smiling about it.

Every object moved within the cathedral's holdings requires a transfer form — one copy filed, one retained. In 2009, during the survey, several coffin plates were removed for cleaning and reattached. Standard practice. I had filed the forms myself. I knew them the way you know your own signature, because my own signature was on them.

Except one.

I had found it again two nights ago, after the exhumation, when I pulled the 2009 file to prepare for exactly this conversation. Form 2009-114: authorization to remove the Vidal coffin plate for conservation. My name printed in the header. My signature at the bottom — and it *was* my signature, or close enough that no expert short of a forensic document examiner would gainsay it. But I had never signed it. I remembered the plate coming off, remembered the conservator who did the work, remembered filing the batch on a Friday afternoon. I did not remember signing that particular form, because I hadn't. The slant was wrong. The R in Reyes curled the wrong way, a small telltale curl, like a smile on a face that shouldn't be smiling.

Someone had signed my name. Sixteen years ago. To a form authorizing the handling of Emiliano Vidal's coffin.

I want to be honest about what I did next, because honesty is the whole currency of this story and I was already spending it badly. Ibarra asked me if all the transfer forms bore my signature, and I said yes, all of them, routine. I said it smoothly. I am fifty-three years old and I lied to a police detective with the fluency of a much younger man, and some part of me stood back and observed the performance with professional admiration and personal horror.

Why did I lie? Because a signed form I never signed is either evidence of a conspiracy or evidence against me, and I did not yet know which, and I am enough of an archivist to know that you do not surrender a document to someone whose filing system you don't trust. That is the charitable version. The uncharitable version is that I was afraid. Both versions are true. They usually are.

"Everything checks out so far," Ibarra said, closing her notebook, and the mildness of it frightened me more than an accusation would have. "You understand you're a material witness."

"I understand."

"And potentially more than that. An empty coffin, an archivist with a gap in his own log and a signature on every relevant paper. If I were writing this report cold, Señor Reyes, you'd be my first chapter."

"If you were writing it cold," I said, "you'd be wrong. But I take your point."

She paused at the door. "One more thing. The sexton, old Eusebio — he was there in '09, wasn't he? He'd have held the keys."

"Eusebio has held the keys since before I was born."

"Mm. He wasn't at the exhumation yesterday. Sick?"

"He doesn't miss burials. Or unburials. He'll have been ill."

"We've been unable to reach him." She said it lightly, the way you mention weather, and left.

* * *

After she was gone I sat for a while among my shelves and did the arithmetic of fear.

Here is what an archive teaches you, if you stay long enough: every document is a testimony given by someone who cannot be cross-examined. Paper says what paper says. It does not care about your intentions, your griefs, your missing hour. I had built my life on the assumption that the record was the truth's best friend. Now the record was wearing my signature like a stolen coat, and I understood — with the slow, cold clarity of a man watching frost climb a window — that whoever had done this had done it inside my own system. Not despite my meticulousness. Through it.

A forger who signs your name once is a criminal. A forger who signs your name inside a catalogue you built, on a form filed in a sequence you designed, dated to a night you cannot account for — that is not a criminal. That is a curator. Someone had curated me. Had selected, from sixteen years of my diligent life, exactly the documents that would make me look guilty, and had planted one more, and had waited.

Waited for what? For the centennial, presumably. For the exhumation. Which meant the empty coffin and the forged signature were parts of a single design, and the design predated the court order, which meant someone had known — or arranged — that the coffin would be opened.

I pulled Form 2009-114 again and looked at the signature under the loupe for the better part of an hour. Whoever wrote it had practiced. The pen pressure matched mine. Only the curl of the R betrayed it, and only barely — the kind of error a forger leaves deliberately, sometimes, as a private joke, or the kind they leave accidentally, as a fingerprint of habit. I photographed it with my phone, then photographed the photograph, which is the sort of thing I do, and locked the original in the fire safe where I keep the deeds.

Then I did what I always do when the present becomes unbearable: I went backward.

If someone had gone to the trouble of forging my hand in 2009, they had gone to the trouble of knowing the crypt intimately in 2009. And if they knew the crypt that well, they had likely read my inventory — every entry, every annotation. Which meant that anything odd I had noted in 2009 was now known to them too.

I retrieved my own crypt ledger, the working copy, and turned to the Vidal entries. There, in my younger handwriting, beside the record of the coffin plate removal, was a note I had entirely forgotten:

*Plate engraving re-cut? Letterforms inconsistent w/ others of period. Query conservator.*

I had noticed, sixteen years ago, that the engraving on Emiliano Vidal's coffin plate looked wrong — newer than it claimed to be, or older than it pretended, the letterforms a costume that didn't quite fit. I had written a query. And somewhere in the intervening years, the query had gone unanswered, the page had gone unread — by everyone, perhaps, except the one person who could not afford to have it read.

Whoever filled that coffin with river stones had also, at some point, replaced or recut the plate on the lid. And I had caught it in passing, the way you catch a wrong note in a hymn, and written it down, and forgotten.

My own meticulousness had armed the killer twice over: once with a missing hour, once with a warning.

I sat in the green-shaded lamp light with the ledger open in front of me and felt the peculiar vertigo of a man who realizes the map he trusts has been redrawn in his own hand. Somewhere in this building — in these registers, these files, these boxes tied with cotton tape — was the person who had done it, or the trail they had left, and they knew the terrain better than I did, because part of the terrain was me.

Outside, the afternoon bell rang. Below my feet, under three feet of stone, the crypt waited with its river stones and its recut plate and whatever else it had been keeping quiet for a hundred years.

I closed the ledger, locked it away, and went to find everything the parish had ever written about Emiliano Vidal — because if his coffin plate was lying, I wanted to know how long the rest of him had been lying too.

I should have checked my pigeonhole first. But that, as Ibarra might say, is the next chapter's business.
