## 7. The Sexton's Last Entry

The sexton died the way old men in San Isidro are supposed to die: in his sleep, in his own bed, with a rosary draped over the headboard like a decoration he'd forgotten to take down. Natural causes, said the doctor who signed the certificate. A heart that had simply decided, after eighty-one years, that it had carried enough.

I wanted to believe it. I want that on the record, in whatever archive eventually holds the account of these weeks: I wanted to believe it the way you want to believe the tide will come back in, the way you want to believe the rain when it promises.

But wanting is not a method. Records are a method. And so, two days after they buried old Eusebio Lanz in the plot he had dug for forty years and never once occupied himself, I did what I always do when a man dies and leaves questions behind me.

I went looking for his paperwork.

* * *

Eusebio had kept an office the size of a generous confessional, wedged between the tool shed and the north wall of the cemetery, and in thirty years of walking that cemetery I had never once been invited inside. He was a private man in the way of men who spend their lives among the dead and the grieving — he had seen what people do with information, and he had decided early that he would be a vault. The sexton knows where everyone is buried. Think about that. In a town like San Isidro, that is not a job. It is a hostage situation that lasts a lifetime.

The doctor had sealed the office with a strip of tape, pending the family's sorting — though Eusebio had no family anyone could name, which in this town meant the parish would inherit, which meant the deacon would inherit, which meant that whatever was in that room was about to become church property, catalogued by me, filed by me, and thereby made safe forever.

Or made safe from everyone except the person who wanted it unsafe.

I stood outside the tape for a long moment, listening to the wind move through the cypress rows. Detective Ibarra had told me — not ordered, exactly, but told me in the way a detective tells you something that is an order wearing a cardigan — to stay out of the sexton's death. Natural causes, he'd said. The man was eighty-one. His heart was the size of a walnut and had been for a decade. There is no case, Reyes. Go home. Sleep. Stop finding conspiracies in filing cabinets.

But here is what Ibarra did not know, because I had not told him, because I was not yet sure it was true: Eusebio Lanz had died the same night Ibarra's quarry trail went cold. The same night, within hours, of the detective asking around town about who might have known that the old river quarry off the Camino Bajo had closed in 1968. And Eusebio Lanz, sexton of San Isidro for forty years, was the single most geographically intimate man I have ever met. He knew this town's ground the way I knew its paper. Every grave, every stratum, every place the water table rose and fell. If anyone alive could have carried a coffin's worth of river stones up from a quarry that had been closed for half a century without anyone noticing — it was the man whose job was moving earth and never being noticed doing it.

I am not saying Eusebio filled the coffin. I am saying Eusebio knew who did. And I am saying that men who know things in San Isidro have a way of dying with tidy hearts.

I peeled back the tape at the corner. I told myself it was a temporary trespass, that I would restore everything to the millimeter, that my hands — trained by thirty years of handling other people's most fragile legacies — would leave less trace than the tape itself. This is the kind of lie archivists tell. We are very good at it. It is how we sleep.

* * *

The office smelled of lime and machine oil and, underneath it, the particular sweetness of old paper kept too long in a damp room — the smell I have loved and hated in roughly equal measure my entire life. A cot in the corner, military-tight. A sink with a mug upside down on the rim. And shelves. God, the shelves. Eusebio had been a record-keeper too, in his way. Burial registers, plot maps drawn in a draftsman's hand that could not have been his own, decades of interment permits rubber-banded by year.

I started with the obvious: the current register, the one that lived on his desk. It was exactly what it should have been, complete and dull, every entry in his crabbed, patient hand. I checked the 1970s volumes next — the decade that mattered, the decade of the quarry's slow death and the diocese's various quiet rearrangements — and found them equally unremarkable. Too unremarkable. A man who kept everything else this faithfully does not keep the most interesting decade of his tenure this blandly.

I was crouched at the bottom shelf, running my fingers along the spines the way a blind man reads, when I felt it: the shelf board sat a finger's width too high. Not by accident. There was a lip of wood beneath it, a false bottom, shallow as a Bible's hollow.

Inside: a notebook. Cheap, spiral-bound, the kind sold at the farmacia for schoolchildren, its cover soft with handling. And beneath the notebook, a small tin — the kind that once held throat lozenges — containing a single key, brass, unlabeled, and a photograph I did not look at long, because looking at it made my chest hurt, and I had work to do. It showed a much younger Eusebio standing beside a much younger priest, both of them squinting into a sun that no longer exists, in front of a crypt door I knew intimately.

I sat on the cot — I know, I know, contamination, but my legs had made an executive decision — and I opened the notebook.

* * *

The early pages were exactly what you'd hope a sexton's private book would be: not records but complaints. The mower pulls left. Padre Anselmo wants the east path widened, again. Water bill. A recipe for liniment, copied from somebody's wife. Forty years of a life, kept in pencil, in a hand that grew shakier by the year the way handwriting does when it is honest.

Then, near the middle, the entries changed.

The date was March 1974. The handwriting was the same — Eusebio had been sexton then too, already fifteen years in — but the tone was different. Tighter. As if he were pressing the pencil hard enough to make the words permanent, or to punish the paper.

*List of graves moved, spring '74. By order of the parish council. As follows:*

*V. Emiliano — moved to crypt, upper niche. Remains transferred by R. and myself. Witnessed by Fr. Anselmo.*

*R. Ocampo — moved to row 9. Reason: water.*

*Infant, surname withheld — moved to row 12. Reason: water.*

*Two more, unmarked — consolidated. Reason: water.*

*All done in one week. All done at night. Padre says the diocese wants the crypt full for the anniversary. Padre says no one will ask.*

*But I am asking. I am the one who dug. I am the one who carried him up the stairs, and I am telling this book what I will not say aloud: the man in the crypt was lighter than a man should be who lived seventy years. Lighter than the stones we put back down.*

*I have written it. I will not write it again.*

I read that passage four times. Then I sat very still and listened to my own breathing, which had gone shallow and quick, the breathing of a man standing at the edge of something.

*Lighter than the stones we put back down.*

He had known. In 1974, Eusebio Lanz had dug Emiliano Vidal's grave, carried up whatever was in it, and understood — at least suspected — that the founder's coffin had been filled with quarry stones even then. Fifty years before the centennial exhumation opened it before the whole town and broke the mayor's face on live television, the sexton had known, and had done what men like Eusebio do with unbearable knowledge: he had written it down, sealed it away, and said nothing for half a century.

And then, fifty years later, someone had opened the coffin anyway. Which meant either that the notebook's secret had finally leaked — or that someone else had always known, and had been waiting for the one moment when the truth would be dragged into the light on their own terms.

I turned the pages after that entry. There were only a few. A line about the anniversary preparations, 1975. A line about the priest's death, 1979, and a strange sentence — *he asked me to forgive him at the end and I said God would have to do the arithmetic* — that I read twice and then had to put the book in my lap for a moment. Then decades of nothing but mower repairs and water bills, the notebook's one great secret buried under forty years of trivia, exactly the way a secret survives.

The last entry was dated the night he died.

*The detective came by. Asked about the quarry. I told him what I tell everyone: the quarry closed before my time. That is a lie and I think he knew it was a lie and I think he was kind about it.*

*After he left I sat a while. Someone has been in my office. Not the detective. Someone who left everything exact. I know my own dust.*

*Tomorrow I will decide what to do with the book.*

There was no tomorrow. There was only the tin, and the key, and me, sitting in a dead man's office holding the answer to everything and understanding, with a cold that started in my hands and climbed, that the last living person who could corroborate what I held had died with his heart full and his book unfinished.

* * *

I should have gone straight to Ibarra. I know that. In the account I will one day write of all this — the account I am, in a sense, writing now — I will note that the correct procedure was to seal the scene, call the detective, and let the chain of custody do its slow holy work.

Instead I did what I had been doing for weeks, what someone had trained me to do without my noticing: I examined the evidence myself.

The notebook's pages told a story beyond their words. The early pages — the complaints, the liniment — were handled the way you'd expect: soft paper, edges furred, a faint general grime of a life's handling. The 1974 pages were different; the pencil had been pressed so hard the paper had embossed, and those pages had been turned rarely, reverently, over fifty years.

But the last pages — the ones written the week he died — those had been turned recently. Recently and often. And here is the thing that stopped my breath, the thing I checked three times with a loupe I carry the way other men carry worry stones:

There were no prints in the recent grime. No finger marks. No smudges of the natural oils that every human hand leaves on paper, marks that no one, however careful, fully avoids. Instead the pages bore a pattern I know better than my own signature: two faint, parallel pressure lines at the outer corners of each leaf, and a whisper of lint fiber caught in the spiral binding. White cotton lint.

Archival gloves.

I wear those gloves eight hours a day. I know exactly how they grip a page — thumb and forefinger at the corners, the two soft pressure lines, the way they drag slightly on old paper and shed a fiber if the pair is new. Someone had sat in this office — or taken the notebook elsewhere and returned it, which is worse — and had read Eusebio's last entries with the same care I would give a fourteenth-century folio. Had handled a dead man's confession the way a professional handles evidence.

Not a burglar. Burglars leave fingerprints and broken things and take what's valuable. Nothing here was taken. Nothing here was broken. Everything here had been *read*.

And then put back. On the shelf. Under the false bottom. Exactly where Eusebio had hidden it.

Why? Why read a confession and leave it for the next man to find?

Because it wasn't for the next man. It was for *me*. I understood it then, sitting on the cot with the notebook open on my knees, the same cold recognition that had been building in me since the anonymous note in my pigeonhole, since the transfer form bearing my signature in ink I had never touched. Everything I had found in this case, I had found because someone wanted me to find it. The notes. The altered registers. The trail through the decades. And now the sexton's book — left where only I would look, handled with the reverence of my own profession, as if to say: *I know how you read. Here is your next page.*

The killer was not hiding the truth from me.

The killer was curating it.

* * *

I put the notebook back. I want that recorded too, because it will look like madness in the trial transcripts, and I want the madness explained: I put it back because the moment I removed it, the person who had already read it would know I had found it, and I was not yet ready to be known. I photographed every page with my phone, both sides, scale reference against a coin, the whole liturgy of documentation performed in a dead man's office by a man whose hands were not quite steady. Then I restored the false bottom, the tape, the dust. I left the office the way a careful reader leaves a book: as though he had never been there.

Outside, the cypress rows had gone blue with evening. Somewhere down the hill the town was lighting up for the novena, and I could hear the bells — the same bells that had rung for the founder's empty coffin, for the sexton's sudden heart, for every lie this town had ever told itself at volume.

One thing more, before I left. I stood at Eusebio's grave — row three, the plot he'd dug for himself decades ago and joked about in the way sextons joke, which is to say without smiling — and I looked at the fresh-turned earth and thought about what he'd written. *Tomorrow I will decide what to do with the book.*

He had decided nothing. Or he had decided, and someone had decided for him. The doctor called it a heart. Ibarra would call it a heart. The certificate would say it forever.

But I had held the notebook that man had hidden for fifty years, and I had seen the cotton lint in the binding, and I knew now — with the certainty that archivists are granted once in a career and pay for forever — that the person who had read Eusebio's last entry wore white cotton gloves, worked inside these walls, and had been writing my investigation for me, page by page, like a patient author waiting for his archivist to catch up to the ending.

I walked down through the graves as the bells rang seven.

And at the cemetery gate, half-buried in the notice case where the parish posts its announcements — Mass schedules, novena intentions, the small civic scripture of San Isidro — was a fresh sheet of paper, typed, unsigned, folded once.

I opened it with gloved hands. My own gloves. I had started carrying them everywhere.

It was a single line, and it was in my own catalogue's voice, the voice only one other person alive had ever read:

*You found the book. Now find what the key opens.*
