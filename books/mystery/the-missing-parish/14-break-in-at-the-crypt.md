## 14. Break-In at the Crypt

I have a theory about archives, which I have never said aloud because it sounds like the kind of thing a man says right before people stop inviting him to lunch: an archive is not a building full of paper. It is a building full of promises. Every register, every folio, every marginal scrawl is somebody's promise that a thing happened, that a person existed, that a debt was recorded and could someday be paid. Burn an archive and you don't destroy paper. You break ten thousand promises at once, and the smoke smells like every funeral the town ever pretended to grieve.

I was thinking about this, God help me, at half past eleven on a Tuesday night, standing in the crypt corridor with my hands on fire.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me do this properly, in order, the way I do everything. Order is the only prayer I've ever been any good at.

* * *

It began, as these things now always began, with Marisol.

She had taken to calling me in the evenings — not to talk about the case, or not only about the case, but to read me passages from the conservation manuals she'd studied in Madrid, the ones with sentences so dry they could be used as kindling, and to make them funny, which is a talent I don't have and admire without reservation. That night she was telling me about a professor who wept over a water-damaged antiphonal, and I was laughing into the phone in my kitchen with a cup of tea going cold beside me, when I heard the second line click.

The rectory line. The one that rings down in the crypt office.

Nobody calls that line after eight. Nobody calls that line at all, really; it exists mostly so that the diocesan accountant can leave me messages about lighting invoices, and even he has the decency to do it during business hours. The phone rang four times, stopped, and then — this is the detail I would give Ibarra later, the one that made him finally look at me like a colleague instead of a suspect with a nice vocabulary — it rang again. Two rings. Stop. Two rings. Stop.

Like someone testing whether the line was being answered. Or like a signal.

"Marisol," I said. "I have to go."

"Felix—"

"The crypt line just rang twice and hung up. Twice."

There was a pause, and then her voice came through flat and fast, the conservator's voice, the one she used for triage. "Go. Don't touch anything with your bare hands. Call Ibarra from the car."

I did call Ibarra from the car. I want that on the record, since so much of my record has been forged. I called him twice from the Fiesta, both times getting the desk, both times being told the detective would be paged. The drive from my apartment to the cathedral takes six minutes at that hour; the town empties out after the ten o'clock news like a tide going out. I remember the streets being wet — it had rained around nine, a brief theatrical rain, the kind this town does to remind itself it has a river somewhere — and I remember thinking, with the particular stupidity of a man who has read too many of his own case files, that wet streets were good. Wet streets meant witnesses. Wet streets meant the world was paying attention.

The cathedral square was empty. The floodlights on the west face threw the buttresses into long black teeth. And from the side door, the one that opens onto the stair down to the crypt, there was a smell.

I have catalogued the contents of that crypt down to the brass fittings on the coffin handles. I know what stone smells like when it sweats in summer, what forty years of candle wax smells like, what the deodorizer the sextons use smells like (pine, aggressively, and always too much). This was none of those. This was paper burning. Old paper, rag-linen paper, the kind that doesn't burn so much as it *grieves* — slow, sullen, giving up its ink in black curls.

I did not call anyone else. I want to say that was bravery. It was not. It was the oldest instinct I have, older than sense, older than self-preservation, probably older than my conscience: the instinct of a man who hears a book screaming and goes toward it.

* * *

The crypt office is a small room off the main corridor, past the niche wall where the sexton's tools used to hang. It holds the working registers — not the originals, which live in my archive upstairs under lock and humidity control, but the duplicates, the day-copies, the ones the parish kept at hand for consultations. Burials, transfers, plot assignments, a hundred and forty years of them. I had spent the previous week copying them, page by page, on the good copier in the parish office, because I had stopped trusting the originals and had decided that if the truth was going to be burned, it would at least burn twice, once for the killer and once for me.

I had hidden my copies. I will not say where. Not here, not yet.

The door to the office was open six inches, and the light coming through the gap was the wrong color — not electric white but a shivering orange, the color of a thing eating.

I should have waited. Every procedural instinct, every true-crime documentary, every sensible voice — Marisol's voice, specifically, saying *call Ibarra from the car*, which I had done, technically, the way you technically keep a promise when you hang up before the person finishes answering — all of it said wait in the square, in the light, where the wet stones could witness you.

I opened the door.

The room was full of smoke and a man's silhouette. He was kneeling at the far wall, where the lower shelves of duplicate registers ran back into the dark, and he had a metal bucket beside him and something in his hand — a bottle, I think, though I never saw it clearly — and the bottom shelf was already alight. Not blazing. Smoldering, the way archives burn when someone has done this before and knows that fire is a slow tool, that you feed a shelf of registers a little at a time and the paper does the rest, and the smoke stays low, and the stone room keeps the noise in.

He turned. I saw — and this is the part I have gone over four hundred times, and I will go over it four hundred more — I saw nothing. A hood, a shape, a height I'd swear to and then swear I couldn't. He was average in every dimension, the way a man is who has practiced being no one. But as he rose, his hand came down on the burning shelf, almost gently, the way you'd quiet a dog, and he said — and the smoke made the voice strange, flattened it, but the *cadence* was a voice I knew from a hundred parish meetings, a hundred sermons, a hundred small courteous exchanges over the coffee urn —

He said: "You shouldn't have come down, Felix."

Not a threat. A regret. That's what has kept me up since. Not *stop* or *you're too late*, but the tone a man uses when he's sorry you've made something necessary.

Then he moved. Not toward me — toward the door behind him, the old maintenance passage that runs up under the sacristy, the one I catalogued in 2009 and have never once seen anyone use. He went through it and pulled it shut, and I heard a bolt, and I understood, in the half-second before the smoke reached me properly, that he had not run away from the fire. He had run away from *me*, and he had left the fire to keep me company.

The lower shelf was going. The registers there — 1912 through 1948, the heart of it, the years when a drifter became a founder, the years I needed most — were curling at their edges like leaves deciding to be ash. And wedged at the back of the shelf, half behind the 1931 volume, was the folio.

I knew it before I touched it. Every archivist knows the shape of the thing he has catalogued. It was the transfer ledger, 1974, the year the sexton's notebook said the graves were moved. The year everything turned. I had noted it in my own hand a decade ago: *water damage, lower shelf, poor condition, deaccession candidate.* I had signed off on its slow death myself, and now it was dying faster, and my hands went into the fire.

I don't remember deciding. I remember the heat, and I remember the smell of my own skin, which is a smell I will not describe because you have all of it in you already, everyone does; you learn it once as a child on a stove and you carry it forever. I remember pulling the folio out from under the burning volumes, and it came away trailing flame along one edge, and I beat it against the floor, and then against my own chest, which was stupid, and then I got it into the metal bucket the man had left — his bucket, his instrument, turned to my purpose, which felt like the first fair exchange in this whole case — and I put the lid on and starved it.

Then I sat down on the floor of my crypt, in the smoke, holding a bucket, with my hands burning, and I did the only liturgy I know. I took inventory. One folio, 1974 transfer ledger, edge-charred, perhaps a third of the entries legible. Everything else on the lower shelf: gone. The upper shelves, I noticed distantly, were untouched. He had burned selectively. He had burned *exactly* the years.

That was the thought that made me laugh, alone in the smoke, a laugh I'm not proud of. Even his arson was archival. Even his fire had a finding aid.

* * *

Ibarra arrived to find me on the back steps of the cathedral, bucket between my knees, hands wrapped in the emergency blanket Marisol keeps in my car — she had arrived four minutes behind me, having ignored my *I have to go* with the efficiency of a woman who has decided which of my instincts are trustworthy and which are not, and had driven straight over, and had found me, and had not scolded me, which was somehow worse.

The paramedics wanted to take me to the hospital. Ibarra told them to wait. He crouched down in front of me, and for a long moment he just looked at the bucket.

"Show me," he said.

I lifted the lid. The smoke smell rose up between us like a ghost introducing itself. He looked at the charred edge of the folio, at the legible entries beneath the burn line, and then he did something he had never done in all our interviews. He took out his phone and photographed it — not as evidence, not with the case number and the placard, but quickly, privately, the way a man photographs something he intends to think about at home.

"Describe the voice," he said.

"I told you. I couldn't—"

"You told me you *couldn't* see him. Describe the voice. The cadence. The words. Exactly."

I closed my eyes. The smoke was still in my sinuses; the whole night was in my sinuses. "He said, *You shouldn't have come down, Felix.*" I opened my eyes. "Regretful. Like a host apologizing for the weather. And Ibarra — he bolted the passage door. He knew the bolt was there. That passage hasn't been opened since I catalogued it in 2009. I wrote it up as *sealed, unused, key unaccounted for.*"

Ibarra stood up. He looked at the cathedral — the black teeth of it against the wet sky — and then at my hands, wrapped white, already blistering in the shapes of the folio's edges.

"Six weeks ago," he said, "I had a folder with your name on it. Motive, opportunity, access. It was a good folder. I liked that folder." He put it away, the folder, metaphorically; I watched him do it, the way you watch a man close a drawer. "Then the sexton dies the same night I start looking at him. Then your archive starts feeding you documents like a trained dog. And now someone burns exactly 1912 to 1948 and bolts a door only the 2009 catalogue would know about." He crouched again, so we were eye to eye, and his voice dropped to something without any officialdom in it at all. "You know what I keep coming back to, Reyes? Whoever this is, he's not cleaning up after himself. He's *editing*. And editors only work on manuscripts they intend to publish."

"The inheritance," I said. "The estate passes to the parish if the bloodline ends. Whoever holds the parish holds everything. And there's a sealed letter from 1974 that says—"

"I know what the letter says. I read your notes. Yes." He almost smiled. "I read your notes, Reyes. Do you understand what that means? Six weeks ago I'd have called that a confession of obsession."

"What does it mean now?"

He looked at my hands again. "It means you burned yourself for a ledger, and men don't do that for forgeries. It means you called me from the car before you went in, which is the first thing you've done in this case that makes sense." He stood, and offered me his arm, and I took it, which cost me something, and cost him something too, I think. "Come on. Hospital. Statements in the morning. And Reyes — from tonight, you're not my suspect. You're my archivist. God help us both."

* * *

They bandaged my hands at the hospital, both of them, to the wrists, and sent me home at three in the morning with painkillers and a lecture. Marisol drove. Neither of us spoke for the first ten minutes, and then, at the long light by the quarry road — the quarry road, I noticed, because I notice everything now whether I want to or not — she said:

"You went in after it."

"The whole town's founder is a fiction and someone was burning the proof—"

"You went in *after it*, Felix." She pulled the car over. She took my bandaged hand, the left one, as carefully as she handles vellum, the way she handles the things she has decided the world cannot afford to lose. "I restore things for a living. I know what it costs to go into a fire for paper. I also know that paper doesn't hold you at night." Her voice did something I had not heard in it before. "Next time, you wait in the square. You let the paper burn and you stay whole. Promise me."

And here is the thing I have learned about promises: they are the archive of the living. Every one you make is a record that will someday be checked.

"I promise," I said, and I meant it, and I already knew, with the certainty of a man who has catalogued his own character and found it wanting in exactly one column, that I would break it.

* * *

The folio sat on my kitchen table at dawn, propped open with saltcellars, drying under Marisol's instructions — she talked me through the stabilization by phone at six a.m., her voice rough with no sleep, precise as scripture. Charred at the fore-edge. Water-and-smoke damage across the upper third. But the middle register, the entries for the autumn of 1974, had survived, and I read them with my bandaged hands flat on the table like a man steadying himself against weather.

Plot transfers. Nine of them, October through December, 1974. Signed by the sexton. Countersigned — and here my heart did the thing hearts do in the last chapter of the cheap novels I pretend not to read — countersigned by the parish administrator of the day, in a hand I knew.

I have spent six weeks with that hand. It is in the sermon drafts, the baptismal margins, the centennial correspondence. It is a small, controlled, beautiful hand, the hand of a man who learned penmanship as an act of devotion. And the deacon is fifty-eight years old. In 1974, he would have been a boy of ten.

But the countersignature was not his. It was the man who trained him — the old parish administrator, dead thirty years, the one whose protégé he was, whose *name*, if the sealed letter was true, he inherited along with everything else. The lie had a paper trail, and the paper trail had a teacher, and the teacher's signature was sitting on my kitchen table in a folio that should be ash.

The last legible entry was dated December 1974, and it was not a plot transfer at all. It was a note, in the sexton's hand, squeezed into the margin where margin notes go to be forgotten:

*Admin says the boy is to learn the registers. Says the name must have a keeper. Says every name needs a keeper or it dies. God forgive what we keep.*

I sat very still. Outside, San Isidro was waking up — the bakery van, the bells, the ordinary machinery of a town built on a name that belonged to a dead boy. My hands throbbed in their bandages, keeping time.

Every name needs a keeper.

The deacon hadn't inherited a fortune. He'd inherited a *job*. And somewhere in the last six weeks, he had decided I was applying for the position.

The phone rang. The crypt line — they'd reconnected it at the rectory while the office was taped off. Two rings. Stop. Two rings. Stop.

I looked at my bandaged hands, and at the folio, and at the phone, and I did not answer it.

But I wrote down the time.
