## 16. Confession Booth

The deacon found me at the coffee machine on the morning after the fire, which was either providence or surveillance, and by that point in my life I had stopped pretending I could tell the difference.

"Felix Reyes," he said, as if we hadn't been sharing a building for eleven years. "You look terrible."

"I saved a folio from a fire with my bare hands last night. This is what terrible looks like when it's trying to be dignified."

He laughed — a warm, rounded laugh, the kind you'd trust with your grandmother — and poured two cups of the parish's industrial-grade coffee, handing me one like we were old friends instead of two men circling each other across a crime scene. Ambrose Salas had always been good at this. Goodness, I mean. The performance of it. He visited the sick, remembered birthdays, carried an elderly woman's groceries up three flights every Thursday for years until she died and he carried her casket too. If you'd asked anyone in San Isidro to name the most decent man in town, they'd have said the deacon before they finished breathing.

That was the problem, of course. I'd spent weeks learning that everything true about Emiliano Vidal had been buried under everything performed, and here was his heir, performing decency at me over burnt coffee.

"The hands," he said, nodding at my bandages. Both wrapped now. Marisol had done them, her fingers careful and furious at the same time, and I had thought: *this is what I almost traded away for paper.* Then I'd thought: *and I would do it again,* and hated myself a little for both thoughts.

"Second-degree burns," I said. "The doctor says I'll keep the fingers."

"A miracle."

"A fire extinguisher."

He laughed again. And then, gently, the way a man sets down something fragile: "I heard the detective cleared you. Finally. That must be a relief."

"It is."

"And yet." He sipped. "You don't look relieved. You look like a man who's still reading."

I looked at him. He smiled back, open-faced, patient, a shepherd's smile. Somewhere behind my sternum, the sexton's notebook sat in its hiding place, and the granddaughter's affidavit sat in another one, and the locket — God, the locket — sat in a third, and all three hiding places were lies I was now maintaining in real time against a man who had killed at least once and possibly twice and who was currently offering me friendship the way other men offer a knife handle-first.

*Lie smoothly,* I told myself. *For the first time in your life.*

It turned out I didn't need telling.

* * *

Here is what you should understand about me, in case the trial transcripts ever get read aloud to schoolchildren or whoever inherits this mess: I have never been a good liar. It's not virtue. It's architecture. A man who has spent thirty years among records learns that every document is a promise, that a false entry is a small murder of the future — some researcher in 2080 will believe what I write today — and so I built myself into a creature incapable of casual falsehood the way other men are incapable of casual cruelty. My partner used to tease me about it. *Felix, if I ask if these pants make me look fat, lie to me just once, as an experiment.* I never managed it. He died four years ago and I have regretted every honest thing I ever said to him, because honesty is cheap when the person hearing it loves you anyway.

So when Salas said, "Walk with me? The garden needs an opinion," and I said yes, and he began, within four minutes, to probe — I discovered that lying is not hard at all. It's just cataloguing. You file the truth somewhere else and hand over a forgery, and if you've spent your life studying forgery, yours comes out beautifully aged, correct ink, correct tremor of hesitation.

The garden opinion was fiction; there is no garden, only a courtyard with three dying rosemary bushes and a bench dedicated to a bishop nobody remembers. We sat. The morning was doing its best, all pale gold and birdsong, the kind of morning that makes you believe towns are innocent places.

"You know," Salas said, "when I heard someone broke into the crypt office, my first thought was: thank God Felix wasn't hurt. My second thought was—" he paused, masterfully, "—whoever did it knew exactly where the duplicate registers were kept. Not guessed. Knew."

"That narrows it to everyone who works here."

"Or everyone who's been *shown* here." He turned to me, and his eyes were kind and bottomless. "You've been showing people things, haven't you, Felix. Since the exhumation. The conservator. The detective. I see you three in the archive at hours when the archive should be empty."

There it was. Not an accusation — an invitation. Confess to me, the eyes said. Bring it to the church. Bring it to *me*. I am the parish's memory now, just as you are its archivist, and memories belong together.

"Marisol needed the light tables," I said. "Ibarra has a warrant covering the case files. Neither of those is a secret."

"No," he agreed softly. "Neither is. What I wonder about is the things that aren't files yet. The things a man carries in his head." He touched my bandaged hand, briefly, a benediction. "Whatever you found in that fire, whatever you're carrying — you know the seal of confession runs both directions, metaphorically speaking. I can't repeat what you tell me. Not to Ibarra. Not to the prosecutor. Not to anyone. That's not a trap, Felix. That's sanctuary."

And God help me, it was beautiful. It was the exact thing a man like me — isolated, grieving, professionally alone for decades — would ache to hear. For one full second I wanted to set down the whole weight of it: the notebook, the letter from 1974, the woman in the next county flinching at her own telephone, the locket with the hair inside it, the name that wasn't Vidal's. I wanted a co-carrier. I wanted absolution-adjacent friendship so badly my teeth ached.

Which is precisely how he'd designed it. He had studied me the way I studied documents. He knew the lonely archivist would rather burn than break confidence, but might, just might, be lured into *sharing* — and a shared secret is a shared noose.

"Sanctuary," I repeated. I let my voice go tired, let my shoulders drop a half-inch, gave him the performance of a man cracking. "Ambrose, I'm going to tell you something because I trust you, and because I can't carry it alone anymore."

His breath changed. Barely. A man less trained than I am in reading documents — and a face is a document, ink and fiber and watermark — would have missed it entirely.

"When I pulled the folio out of the fire," I said, "it was already ruined. Charred through. I burned my hands on ash." I shook my head, ashamed, a liar to the bone now and hating how natural the shame felt. "I didn't save anything. I just couldn't admit it. Ibarra thinks I have evidence. I have nothing. I'm terrified he's going to figure out I let him believe otherwise, and it'll bury me."

Silence. The rosemary smelled like a funeral. Then Salas exhaled, and the relief in it was so faint, so controlled, that only a man who had spent weeks learning this particular reader could have caught it — and I had.

"Oh, Felix." He put his arm around my shoulders, actually around them, and I sat inside the embrace of a murderer and felt my skin crawl and my heart stay perfectly level, and thought: *so this is what he does. This is how he's done it for fifty years. He holds you while he checks your pockets.* "You poor man. You have nothing?"

"Nothing but burns and suspicion."

"Then listen to me." His voice dropped into the register priests reserve for dying parishioners. "Whatever you think you've found — drop it. Let the trial come. Let the lawyers argue about stones. Some truths are load-bearing, Felix. Pull them out and the whole church comes down on the people underneath. The founder made this town. Whatever he was, the town is real. The schools are real. The endowments are real. Isn't a living parish worth more than a dead man's honest name?"

There it was. The motive, offered freely, wrapped in theology. I could have kissed him for his arrogance.

"I don't know," I said, and let my voice waver. "I need time."

"Of course." He squeezed my shoulder and stood, brushing off his cassock, decent to the last inch. "Take all the time you need. But Felix —" he paused at the courtyard door, backlit, framed like a saint in bad glass — "the conservator. Marisol. She's talented. Dedicated. She reminds me of you." A beat. "Be careful who you show things to. Archives attract the wrong kind of attention sometimes. I'd hate for her to get hurt in ours."

And he was gone, leaving me on the bench with my forged confession dissolving in my mouth like sugar in rain.

* * *

Marisol met me at the conservation lab at noon, and I told her all of it — the probing, the offer of sanctuary, the threat dressed as concern — while she worked a scalpel along the edge of a document sleeve with the concentration of a surgeon and the temper of one too.

"He threatened me," she said flatly.

"He warned you. In deacon."

"In *deacon*." She snorted. "That's not a warning, Felix, that's a countdown. He knows I dated the forgeries. He knows I can date the rest." She put down the scalpel and looked at me, and her eyes were bright and afraid and angrier than afraid, which is my favorite combination in a human being. "Say the word and I take everything to Ibarra tonight. The ink analysis, the portrait, all of it."

"Not yet."

"*Not yet?* Felix—"

"If we go to Ibarra now, Salas becomes a suspect with a lawyer and a parish full of witnesses who love him, and the locket goes into an evidence locker where his friends in the diocese can reach it, and the trial becomes a he-said-she-said between a beloved deacon and an archivist who already admitted he lied about the fire." I held up my bandaged hands. "We get one shot at this. One. It has to be airtight, and it has to happen on ground he thinks is holy."

She stared at me. "You're talking about the crypt."

"I'm talking about making him come to us."

"And until then? Until your one perfect shot? He thinks I'm a loose end with a scalpel."

"Then we make sure he doesn't think it for long." I took her hand — carefully, mindful of the instruments, mindful of everything, the way I am with anything irreplaceable. "Tomorrow you're going to announce, loudly, in the refectory, that the ink analysis came back inconclusive. That the forgeries can't be dated. That you're sorry, but the samples degraded."

"Lying," she said. "In public. As a professional conservator."

"Yes."

A long pause. Then, slowly, terribly, she smiled — the same expression, I realized, that must have been on my own face in the courtyard. "God," she said. "It *is* easy, isn't it?"

"It's disgusting how easy it is."

"That's what worries me about you, Reyes." She went back to her scalpel, but her voice followed me toward the door, light and lethal. "You're getting good at this. And men who get good at lying usually forget which drawer they filed the truth in."

I promised her I wouldn't. I even believed it, mostly.

* * *

That night I couldn't sleep, which was standard operating procedure by then, so I sat in my kitchen with the granddaughter's affidavit and read it again, though I knew it by heart, the way you reread a letter from someone you love who is far away and frightened. *My grandfather was buried in unconsecrated ground because the church refused him. Ask the river bend past the old quarry. Ask for the stone with no name.*

The quarry. Ibarra's quarry, closed in 1968, source of the stones in the coffin. Everything in this case ran through that same stretch of river like water through a channel cut a century ago.

And tomorrow, per the plan Marisol and I had begun sketching on the back of a conservation report, I would need to start moving pieces toward that river without Salas seeing the board. Which meant permits. Which meant paperwork. Which meant — I understood, with a sinking, familiar dread — that the next set of documents I created would pass through county offices where a deacon with fifty years of friendships and one very motivated interest could read them over some clerk's shoulder.

I had lied to a murderer's face and gotten away with it. I had recruited the woman I loved into the lying. And now I was about to build a paper trail — my native element, my cathedral, the one place in the world I had always been safe — knowing that every sheet of it was also a message I was sending him, whether I meant to or not.

I got out a fresh pad and wrote, in my best archival hand, a single line:

*He reads everything. So write him something worth reading.*

Then I crossed it out, because even my private notes were starting to sound like bait, and went to bed, and dreamed of the river bend, and woke at dawn with my hands throbbing and one sentence fully formed in my head, the way sentences sometimes arrive uninvited:

*The locket should go back where it belongs.*

I lay staring at the ceiling, understanding that the plan had just written itself — that I had, in my sleep, committed to announcing publicly that I intended to return the locket to the crypt before trial, and that when I did, somewhere across town, a decent-seeming man would smile and begin preparing a fire.

I got up and started drafting the announcement. It felt, I confess, wonderful.
