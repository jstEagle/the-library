## 21. Testimony in Ink

The courthouse in San Isidro had been built the same decade as the crypt, by the same masons, with the same conviction that limestone and gravity were sufficient arguments against time. Standing on its steps that October morning, watching the town file past me into the gallery, I understood for the first time that they'd been wrong. Time had gotten into everything. It was in the walls, in the ledgers, in the name we all said like a prayer. And now it was going to be cross-examined.

Ibarra met me at the top of the stairs. He looked like a man who had slept in his car and won the argument anyway.

"Nervous?" he asked.

"I've spent my whole life being believed about documents," I said. "It's strange to have to earn it out loud."

"Save it for the stand." He glanced at the crowd — the mayor, whose face had finally stopped collapsing and settled into something like relief; Marisol's colleagues from the conservation lab; three generations of Vidals-by-marriage who didn't know yet that they weren't. "Whatever happens in there, remember: you're not the one on trial."

"That's what worries me," I said. "For six months I *was* the one on trial. At least then everyone's expectations were clear."

He almost smiled. Then he did the thing he does instead of blessing people, which is to straighten their collar without asking permission, and pushed open the door.

* * *

The courtroom smelled of floor wax and old paper, which under other circumstances would have felt like home. Judge Ondoy presided — a woman who had once, I knew from the parish records I probably shouldn't have read, been baptized by Father Emiliano Vidal himself, or by the man wearing his name, depending on which century you trusted. She did not recuse herself. She said only: "We will proceed carefully," in a tone that suggested she intended to weigh every word of the next several days on the same scale she used for everything else, and that the scale was older than any of us.

The prosecutor, Attorney Villanueva, opened with the coffin. She was good. She held up the photograph of river stones arranged in a human shape and let the image do its terrible arithmetic: someone had knelt in this crypt, sometime in the last hundred years, and built a man out of geology.

"Someone," she said, "who knew the crypt. Who knew the records. Who knew exactly how long a lie could sleep before anyone checked on it."

Every head in the gallery turned toward me with the smooth synchrony of a congregation. I kept my hands flat on my knees and thought about Marisol, sitting two rows back, and about the locket sealed in an evidence bag in Villanueva's case cart, and about the fact that fear, properly catalogued, is just information you haven't filed yet.

* * *

They called me on the second day.

Walking to the stand felt like walking down the crypt stairs — that same drop in temperature, that same sense of descending into a room where the truth had been stored badly for a very long time. I took the oath with my hand on a Bible that, I noted professionally, bore a 1911 imprint from the same press as our oldest registers. Everything in San Isidro was related to everything else. That had always been the problem.

Villanueva began gently, the way you begin restoring a document — at the edges, where the damage is least frightening.

"Mr. Reyes, describe your role at the cathedral."

"Archivist. I catalogue, preserve, and control access to the parish records. Baptismal registers, burial ledgers, land deeds held in trust, correspondence. Roughly forty thousand items."

"And in 2009, you personally catalogued the crypt?"

"I did. Every niche, every coffin plate, every inscription. It took eleven weeks." I heard my own voice go slightly reverent and corrected course. "It's the kind of work you do slowly or not at all."

"Why so slow?"

"Because a record isn't just paper. It's a claim. Every entry says *this happened, to this person, on this date*, and if you transcribe carelessly, you're not making a mistake — you're forging. You're putting words in a dead man's mouth." I paused. "I take forgery seriously."

Villanueva let that sit. Then she introduced Exhibit A: the reconstructed register.

I watched the jury look at it the way people look at a reliquary. They couldn't read the Latin marginalia or date the watermarks, but they could see the burn scar along one edge — the folio I'd pulled from the fire in the crypt office, the one that cost me the skin on both palms — and they could see, laid beside it, the pages Felix Reyes had spent six weeks rebuilding: baptismal entries in matching ink across three decades, all altered in favor of a man named Emiliano Vidal, each correction photographed, dated, and cross-referenced against the quarry manifests and the sexton's notebook and the granddaughter's affidavit.

"Who made these alterations?"

"Not one hand. Three hands, over roughly thirty years. But the same ink formula throughout — iron gall with a specific chloride content, which Marisol Quintero can speak to better than I can. The point is continuity. Someone taught someone else the recipe. This wasn't a crime. It was a maintenance program."

"A maintenance program," the judge repeated.

"Yes, Your Honor. The way you maintain a roof. Regularly, across generations, by people raised to believe the house matters more than the weather."

* * *

Cross-examination belonged to Salas's defense counsel, a careful, expensive man named Ferrer who had the archivist's worst quality — he liked documents more than people — and none of the archivist's saving grace, which is shame about it.

"Mr. Reyes. You admit you hid evidence during your first interview with Detective Ibarra."

"I failed to volunteer a transfer form bearing my signature," I said. "A signature I did not write. Which is precisely why I hesitated — because in my profession, an unexplained signature in your own hand is a hanging offense. I've since provided exemplars, and the court's document examiner has confirmed the forgery."

"You'll forgive me if I note that confirmation came rather late."

"In my experience, Mr. Ferrer, most confirmations come late. That's why we keep records — so the truth doesn't depend on anyone's punctuality."

Something flickered in the gallery. I hadn't meant it as a line. It was simply the truest sentence I owned.

Ferrer moved on to the missing hour — the one unaccounted-for hour in my 2009 catalogue, the gap I'd confessed to Ibarra in that first terrible interview. He walked me through it twice, looking for the seam. There wasn't one, because there never had been; Ambrose Salas had cut that page out of the ledger himself, decades before I ever opened it, and had counted on the archivist's honesty to make the absence look like guilt.

"One final question," Ferrer said. "You've described a conspiracy spanning a century. Forgers, a murdered sexton, an abducted conservator, a deacon of this parish. Isn't it more reasonable to conclude that a grieving, isolated man with unusual access constructed an elaborate story to explain away his own negligence?"

I considered the question honestly, because it deserved that much.

"Six months ago, I would have agreed with you," I said. "Then I found the granddaughter. Then I found the locket. Then someone burned forty years of duplicate registers rather than let them be read. Negligence doesn't burn anything, Mr. Ferrer. Only inheritance does."

* * *

The affidavit came in on the third day, read aloud in a voice made thin by distance and age. Elena Vidal-Roque, seventy-eight years old, of the next county, sworn statement: her grandfather had died at nineteen, before the town existed; the man her grandmother married afterward had taken his name, his fortune, and eventually his grave; the locket in evidence contained a lock of her grandfather's hair, identified by the engraving on its reverse in her mother's hand.

When the prosecutor read the engraving aloud — *E.V., from M., wait for me* — I heard Marisol breathe in behind me. Some documents you catalogue. Some documents catalogue you.

And then the chain of custody, item by item, witness by witness: the locket lifted from unconsecrated ground; logged, sealed, photographed; stolen back and ransomed; recovered from the crypt floor the night Deacon Ambrose Salas knelt in the dark expecting an empty room and found instead an archivist, a detective, and a door locking behind him. Ibarra testified with the flat competence of a man describing plumbing. Marisol testified about ink and varnish and the second face beneath Vidal's portrait, and when Ferrer asked her whether her relationship with me compromised her objectivity, she said, "No. It improved it. He used to think archives were safer than people. I disabused him." The gallery laughed, briefly, like weather clearing.

Salas sat through all of it in his gray suit without his collar, hands folded, face composed into something between penitence and patience. He watched me the way he had watched me at the exhumation — not with hatred. With *custody*. As if I were a document he had misfiled and was waiting to retrieve.

* * *

On the fifth day, Ferrer asked for a recess, and when court resumed, he rose and did the thing nobody expected, though I suppose I should have: he folded.

"The defense wishes to enter a plea," he said. "The accused will plead guilty to obstruction, evidence tampering, and the falsification of historical records. In exchange, the remaining charges—" and here his voice did something complicated around the word *remaining*, because the sexton's death had been ruled natural and the kidnapping charge was the one with teeth— "will be resolved by agreement."

The courtroom made a sound I'd never heard in it before — half exhalation, half protest. Villanueva objected on principle; the judge overruled her on arithmetic; a trial is a gamble, and Salas had just counted the cards.

But I understood, standing there, what was really happening. It wasn't cowardice. It was the last act of a curator. A plea bargain would seal the transcript's worst passages — the motive testimony, the inheritance, the drifter and the dead boy and the lock of hair. The founder's myth would survive the way myths survive: not by being proven, but by never quite being disproven in public. Ambrose Salas would go to prison for tampering, and Emiliano Vidal would keep his statue, his feast day, his name on the school and the plaza and the baptismal font, because the full account would live only in a sealed plea agreement and in the memory of a few hundred people who could be counted on, eventually, to doubt themselves.

He was archiving the lie one last time. Filing it where no one would read it.

I caught his eye across the room. He inclined his head — barely, the acknowledgment of one professional to another — and mouthed something I chose not to read.

* * *

Afterward, on the courthouse steps, the mayor shook my hand too hard and said the town would "move forward together," which is what towns say when they've decided to keep the stone. Reporters asked me whether justice had been served. I said the law had been served, and that those were related but distinct institutions, and they printed only the first half.

Marisol found me at the bottom of the steps. She took my burned hand — mostly healed now, new skin pink and tender as a fresh watermark — and turned it over in hers.

"So it's finished," she said.

"The trial is." I looked up at the cathedral visible over the rooftops, its tower holding up the late afternoon the way it had held up a hundred years of afternoons. "The record isn't. Sealed agreements, suppressed testimony, a statue that still gets flowers every feast day. Half the truth went back in the ground today, Marisol. Formally. With paperwork."

"Then what do we do?"

I had the answer ready, because I'd had it ready since the night I locked a deacon in a crypt: I'd written it out, dated it, and signed it, because a record isn't real until someone puts their name to it.

"We do what archivists do," I said. "We keep the other copy."

That evening I climbed to the archive with a blank folio under my arm, and I began at the beginning — the centennial order, the torn page, the stones — writing the account that no plea agreement could reach. And when I reached the last section, the part about who stood beside me through the fire and the ransom and the long reconstruction, I found I already knew the final entry. It was a name.
