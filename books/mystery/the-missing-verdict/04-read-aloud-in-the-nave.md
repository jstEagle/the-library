## 4. Read Aloud in the Nave

Xanthe had spent ten years perfecting the art of being overlooked. He knew exactly how to stand in a doorway so that people's eyes slid past him like water off oiled wood. It was a useful skill for an archivist, and an even more useful one for a man cataloguing secrets that could unmake half the town.

So it was with some professional satisfaction that he pinned the notice to the cathedral door on Monday morning and watched the town gather around it like gulls around a dropped sardine.

**PUBLIC READING OF SEALED DOCUMENTS — SATURDAY, NOON — THE CATHEDRAL NAVE. ALL ARE WELCOME.**

"All are welcome" was doing rather a lot of work in that sentence. What he meant, of course, was *you are welcome, gentlemen of the ledger, you who signed your names in the margins of a verdict you bought; come and see what your signatures have purchased.*

He'd written the notice himself, in his best round hand, the one he used for exhibit labels. Mara had read it over his shoulder and snorted.

"You've made entrapment look like a parish announcement."

"That," Xanthe said, "is the entire point. Nobody guards against a parish announcement."

She was quieter after that. She'd been quieter since the night outside the archive, since the car that had swerved toward her and thought better of it at the last moment — or hadn't thought better of it, and been called off by whatever hand held its leash. She stood now at the edge of his desk, arms crossed, looking at the notice as though it might bite her.

"They'll come," she said. "You know that. And they'll bring more than regret."

"I'm counting on it." He straightened the pin. "A trap only springs if the animal walks in. I'm simply making the cheese very legible."

Mara almost smiled. Almost. "My mother used to say you could hear my father's verdicts from three pews back without a microphone. That he believed justice should be spoken where everyone could catch it, like a cold."

"A sensible man."

"He was a dramatic man who happened to be right." She looked up at the vaulted dark above them, where the morning light was just beginning to find the windows. "Saturday, then."

"Saturday," Xanthe agreed, and did not say: *and if this goes wrong, we will both be filed away under closed cases.* Some things were kinder left unsaid. Most things, actually. He was learning that.

* * *

Mara had not slept properly in eleven days, which she understood was roughly how long it took a life to be dismantled and reassembled around a different shape.

Her father had been dead for ten years. She had buried him — no, that wasn't true, that was the whole rotten heart of it — she had *mourned* him, with no body, with a verdict of accidental drowning that nobody in town quite believed but everybody agreed not to examine, because examining it would have meant asking why a judge who had spent thirty years being scrupulously honest had, in his final months, signed rulings that made the founding families richer and the river cleaner-looking and the poor of Harrowgate measurably worse off.

Now the police sergeant kept saying *your mother*, in that careful voice, and Mara kept hearing it wrong.

Because the woman sitting across from her in the vestry on Saturday morning, wrapped in a gray traveling coat, hands folded like a woman waiting for a train, was Judge Amara Voss. Alive. Ten years older, hair gone white at the temples, but unmistakably, impossibly, her mother.

"You're angry," her mother said.

"I'm furious," Mara said. "I'm also relieved, and grieving, and I don't know what order those go in."

"The order doesn't matter. They all get their turn." Her mother's voice was steady, but her hands weren't. Mara watched them and understood: the steadiness was a verdict her mother delivered daily, over and over, to herself. "I want you to know that leaving you was the hardest ruling I ever handed down. And I have handed down some appalling ones."

"I know." Mara's throat tightened. "I read them. All of them. Xanthe let me."

"He has a gift for knowing what people need before they ask." A pause, and something wry moved through her mother's tired face. "It's slightly infuriating."

"It really is," Mara said, and felt, absurdly, like laughing, there in the vestry with the killers assembling outside like weather.

The cathedral bells began. Noon.

* * *

The nave filled the way a wound fills with blood — slowly at first, then all at once.

Xanthe counted them from the shadow of the pulpit: the aldermen, three of them, seated together with the studied casualness of men who had rehearsed looking unconcerned. The harbormaster. Old Mrs. Calloway, who owned half the riverfront and none of the guilt. And near the back, standing, two men who had never once set foot in a church service in all the years Xanthe had catalogued the attendance rolls — the same two whose faces Mara had described, twice, in careful detail, while he wrote down every word.

They had come. Of course they had come. You did not leave a sealed document unread when your name might be in it. Guilt was, Xanthe reflected, the most reliable form of attendance ever devised.

He stepped into the light. The murmuring died.

"My name is Xanthe Ishikawa," he said, "and for eleven years I have been the keeper of this cathedral's records. Which means I am the keeper of everything this town preferred to forget." He let that sit. Somewhere in the third pew, an alderman shifted. "Ten years ago, this court recorded the accidental death of Judge Amara Voss. The file was sealed. Sealed files are how towns sleep at night. Today we wake up."

He opened the misfiled hymnal — hymn 214, *Abide With Me*, which he suspected the judge had chosen deliberately, as a private joke aimed at whoever finally found it — and lifted out the folded pages.

And read.

He read the verdict she had been coerced into signing, and the letter she had left behind it, naming each signature in the margins, each payment, each threat delivered in the pleasant tones men use when they believe themselves untouchable. His voice carried the way voices do in old stone buildings, as if the walls themselves had been waiting a decade to repeat it.

He got as far as the third name when the back doors banged open.

The two men came fast, and they came stupid, because fear makes even professionals stupid, and the taller one shouted something about slander and stopped documents, and that was the moment — precisely that moment — when the crypt door beneath the east transept swung open, and a woman in a gray coat climbed the steps into the light of her own memorial.

The sound the congregation made was not a gasp. It was something older than gasping. It was the sound a town makes when the floor of its history gives way.

Judge Amara Voss stood in the nave and said, clearly, in the voice her daughter remembered from three pews back:

"I presided here for thirty years. I know how a court is opened. This one is open. Sergeant — those two men first. Then the aldermen can stay seated. We'll go in order of guilt, oldest first."

* * *

Afterward — after the arrests, after the statements, after the alderman who wept and the harbormaster who didn't — Mara found Xanthe in the archive, feeding papers into the small iron stove he used to take the winter chill off the reading room.

"What are you burning?" she asked, though she already knew.

"The rest of it." He fed another folder to the flames. Debts and discretions, small sins and survivable ones, the private wreckage of people who had done nothing worth destroying them for. "The verdict goes to the court tonight, officially, formally, reversed and resealed in daylight. Everything else—" he nodded at the fire "—was never mine to keep. I built a life out of other people's secrets. Turns out it's a very quiet life, and very cold."

Mara sat down on the reading table, which would have scandalized him a month ago and now seemed entirely correct. "And what will you build instead?"

He considered the fire. "Something said aloud," he said. "I'm told it's healthier."

"It is," said Mara Voss, daughter of a judge who was alive, witness to a crime that was solved, keeper of one last name — the last name on the ledger, the one she had recognized in her father's old clerk's handwriting, the one she had not yet told anyone, because it was written in ink she knew, in a hand she'd grown up watching sign her birthday cards.

She watched the flames a moment longer.

"Xanthe," she said quietly, "there's one more page."
