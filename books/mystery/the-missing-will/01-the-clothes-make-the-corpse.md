## 1. The Clothes Make the Corpse

The thing nobody tells you about true crime podcasting is that ninety percent of it is a woman alone in a closet, apologizing to a microphone for the sound of her own swallowing.

"—and that concludes our brief history of the Harrow Bay cannery," I said, leaning into the mic in my converted coat closet, where the foam panels I'd glued to the walls in 2019 were beginning to peel like sunburned skin. "Next week, we'll look at the great oyster wars of 1961, which, I promise you, involved more violence and considerably more profanity than the word 'oyster' suggests. Until then, I'm Lena Sterling, and remember: the past doesn't haunt us. It just files itself alphabetically and waits."

I hit stop, played it back, and winced at the click my jaw makes when I talk too long. Twenty-two years of recording and I still hadn't fixed it. Some flaws you keep because they're the only proof you were ever young.

The episode was filler, honestly. *Harrow Bay: A History in Small Crimes* had been running on local lore for three weeks while I waited for something real. Cannery strikes. The lighthouse keeper who embezzled the lamp oil. Charming stuff. The kind of episodes that get downloaded by insomniacs and middle school teachers, not by anyone who might know something. I'd built my name — such as it was, a modest regional name, the kind of name that gets you free chowder and dirty looks at the same dinner — on the 2007 Pell case, and I'd been chasing a follow-up worthy of it ever since. Harrow Bay is a small town with a long memory and exactly one murder anyone talks about, and I'd promised myself I'd never be the kind of podcaster who pokes a wound just to hear the sound it makes.

That's the irony, in case you missed it. Hold onto it. It gets heavier.

My phone buzzed against the desk at 4:47 in the afternoon, which is a time of day when nothing good has ever happened to anyone. The screen said RUIZ.

Detective Amara Ruiz and I had a relationship best described as professional détente. She'd been a source back when I was a stringer for the *Coastal Register*, feeding me just enough to keep me useful and never enough to get her named in a lawsuit. She'd retired from talking to me around the time I'd retired from the paper, but we still exchanged pleasantries at the farmer's market, two women circling each other like dogs who'd agreed on a fence line.

I answered. "Ruiz. To what do I owe the—"

"Don't come out here." Her voice was flat, cop-flat, the voice she used when she was deciding something. "I'm calling to tell you not to come to the marsh road. There's a scene. There's press coming. Stay at your house."

"Amara," I said, already standing, already reaching for my boots. "You have never once in your life called me to tell me not to do something. You call me to tell me you told me not to do something, later, in front of witnesses."

A long pause. Wind on her end, and gulls, and that low marsh hush like the land itself breathing. "Lena. I'm serious."

That was the second strange thing. She never used my first name unless she was scared.

"Is it Okafor?" I asked, and I don't know why that name came out of my mouth. It had been sitting in my chest for thirty years, the way certain names do in a town like this — Elias Okafor, convicted of murdering his business partner in 1994, the town's one great wound, the case every kid in Harrow Bay learned about the way kids learn about the boogeyman, except our boogeyman was in Souza State Correctional and had been for three decades.

The silence on the line went on so long I checked whether the call had dropped.

"Stay home, Lena," she said, and hung up.

I want it on the record that I am a grown woman with a mortgage and a filing system, and that I considered, for a full ninety seconds, doing what I was told. I made tea. I sat down. I looked at the foam panel peeling off the closet wall. Then I put on my boots, because I have never once in my life been a person who stays home when a detective calls me at 4:47 on a Tuesday, and because some animal part of my brain had already done the arithmetic: a body on the marsh road, and Ruiz scared enough to use my first name, and the name *Okafor* sitting in my mouth like a stone I'd swallowed as a teenager.

The marsh road is ten minutes from my house if you drive like a person who grew up here, which is to say fast on the straights and slow where the frost heaves have made the asphalt lie about itself. I parked behind the second cruiser, past the sawhorse with its flapping POLICE LINE tape, and I was still twenty feet out when I saw Ruiz walking toward me with her hands in her pockets and her face doing something complicated.

"I told you to stay home," she said.

"You did. I've decided to interpret that as a professional courtesy." I looked past her shoulder. "Who is it?"

She stepped sideways, which was its own kind of answer, and let me see.

They'd left the body where it lay, half in the sea grass at the road's shoulder, and the tide-light made everything the color of old pewter. A man, middle-aged, on his back, eyes open to a sky he clearly hadn't expected to be the last thing. And I did what any person does first — I looked at his face, looking for the story there, and found nothing, a stranger's face, weathered and ordinary.

It was the second thing I looked at that stopped my heart the way a hand stops a clock.

The clothes.

Corduroy jacket, rust-brown, with the elbow worn pale. Gray henley underneath. Dark trousers. And on the left breast of the jacket, a small embroidered patch — a heron in flight, stitched in cream thread.

I knew that jacket. Not the way you know a jacket you've seen. I knew it the way you know a sentence you've typed, a paragraph you've filed, a statement you've read so many times at nineteen years old that it lives in your hands like a piano piece. Corduroy jacket, rust-brown, heron patch. Gray henley. Dark trousers. *The man was wearing a rust-brown corduroy jacket with a bird on the pocket, and gray under it, and dark pants.*

The words from the 1994 witness statement, the one I'd photocopied and cross-referenced and archived in a banker's box labeled **OKAFOR, E. — WITNESS MATERIALS**, the box that had followed me through three apartments and a divorce and now sat in my hallway closet between tax records and Christmas lights.

"Lena." Ruiz's voice, from very far away. "Lena. Sit down before you fall down. You've gone gray."

"That jacket," I said. My mouth was working fine; it was the rest of me that had disconnected. "That's the jacket from the statement."

"What statement?"

"The witness statement. From '94. The one that put Elias Okafor away." I heard my own voice go thin and precise, the voice I use when I'm frightened, which is the voice I use when I'm right. "Amara. That's not a man dressed in those clothes. That's a man dressed *as* the description. That outfit exists in one place in the world, and it's in a sworn statement, and I have the only copy that isn't in a courthouse basement."

Ruiz looked at me for a long moment, and then she did something I'd never seen her do in twenty years of knowing her. She glanced over her shoulder — at her own crime scene, at her own people — before she spoke.

"Come here," she said. "Don't touch anything. And Lena — what I'm about to show you stays between us until I say otherwise, or I will personally end your career, your podcast, and possibly your life."

She led me to the edge of the tarp they'd set up, close enough to see what the photographer had already captured: the dead man's hands, and in his right hand, clutched with the desperate grip of a man who knew he was carrying something that mattered, the corner of a manila envelope. Empty. Whatever had been inside it was gone.

"Victim's Danny Okafor," Ruiz said quietly. "Elias's brother. Came back to town three weeks ago after thirty years away. Nobody knew him, nobody saw him, and this morning a jogger found him on the marsh road wearing" — her jaw tightened — "wearing a costume, Lena. A costume of a description from a thirty-year-old witness statement. Somebody dressed this man up as the evidence that convicted his brother, and then killed him."

The marsh breathed around us. Somewhere behind the tape, a tech laughed at something, the ordinary sound of people doing their jobs around the dead.

"Why show me?" I asked. "You could've kept me out. You tried to keep me out."

Ruiz looked at the body, then at me, and her face did the complicated thing again, the thing that had made her call me, the thing she'd been carrying since before the phone rang.

"Because the witness statement you're talking about," she said. "The original, the one in the courthouse file? I pulled it an hour ago. It's signed. And the signature belongs to Marisol Vane."

The world did not stop. That's the thing nobody tells you. The gulls kept screaming, the tide kept coming, the techs kept working, and somewhere in my chest a door that had been closed for thirty years swung open on a dark room, and everything I had ever filed so carefully, alphabetized and cross-referenced and safe, rearranged itself in a single breath into something that was no longer a story I was telling.

It was a story I was in.

"Marisol Vane," I said, and my voice was steady, God help me, steady as a headstone. "That's my mother."

* * *

I drove home with the windows down because the car suddenly felt too small for my body. The marsh road unspooled behind me and I kept checking the mirror like the day itself might be following.

By the time I pulled into my driveway, the light was going copper, and my mailbox was hanging open. I hadn't left it open.

Inside, on top of the flyers for gutter cleaning, was an envelope with no stamp and no return address. My name on the front in block capitals. Inside, a single sheet, and on it, in the same careful block letters, eight words:

**STOP DIGGING. SOME THINGS ARE BURIED FOR A REASON.**

I stood in my driveway holding the note while the first porch light on my street came on, and I thought about a rust-brown corduroy jacket folded in a banker's box in my hallway closet, and a woman across town who taught me to read, who taught me to file, who taught me that the truth was the only thing worth keeping — and who had signed a statement in 1994 that had just walked out of my archive and onto a marsh road, wearing a dead man's body.

I went inside. I locked the door. And I did the only thing I knew how to do with dread that size.

I started a new folder, and at the top of it, in my neatest hand, I wrote: **THE MISSING WILL.**
