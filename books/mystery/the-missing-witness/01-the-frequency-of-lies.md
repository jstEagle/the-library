## 1. The Frequency of Lies

The depot at three in the morning had a sound Rosalind Whitlock thought of as the city breathing in its sleep. Radiators ticking. The coffee machine's dying gurgle. Somewhere below, the garage doors rolling up for the four o'clock changeover, letting in a wedge of cold air that smelled of diesel and wet asphalt. He sat in the dispatch chair with his headset on and his feet on an overturned milk crate, watching the board light up like a slow constellation—car twelve on Delancey, car seven idling outside the bus terminal, car nineteen not answering again, which was typical of Reyes, who treated his radio as a suggestion rather than a tool.

"Seven, you copy? Fare waiting at the terminal, north side."

"Copy, Dispatch." Marta's voice came through crisp even though she wasn't on shift yet—no, that was Okafor. Marta wouldn't be in until six. Rosalind made a note anyway, out of habit, because habits were the architecture that held his nights together.

He'd been doing this job eleven years. Long enough that the city's rhythms lived in him like a second pulse: the last trains emptying their tired cargo at half past midnight, the bars flushing their loud ones at two, the hospitals spitting grief onto the curb around visiting hours' end, and then the long gray trough between three and five when the only calls were insomniacs, nurses, and people who shouldn't be driving. He knew the city by its voices before he ever knew it by its streets.

That was the thing nobody understood about dispatching. Everyone assumed it was about addresses. It wasn't. It was about listening. People lied to cab drivers constantly—not big lies, mostly. *I'm just coming from work* when they were coming from somewhere they didn't want named. *My wife's expecting me* when there was no wife, or no expectation. *I've never been to this neighborhood* while giving directions to it from memory. The lies rose off callers like heat shimmer off summer pavement, and after enough years, Rosalind couldn't not hear them. It wasn't a skill he'd cultivated. It was more like tinnitus—a frequency he'd been tuned to so long that silence itself sounded wrong.

He pulled the log toward him and pretended to review it, though what he was actually doing was what he did most slow nights: turning over the three deaths, the way other men turned over worry beads.

The first had been Desmond Achebe, sixty-one, found at the bottom of his own staircase on Halsey Street fourteen months ago. Fell while intoxicated, said the report. But Rosalind had dispatched Achebe home from the airport nine days before he died, and the man who'd called for the cab had spoken with the careful, over-enunciated clarity of someone fighting hard to seem sober—and then, forty seconds into the call, had asked Rosalind to repeat the fare estimate twice, because his working memory was already going soft. Early-stage something. A man like that didn't navigate stairs drunk; a man like that held the banister with both hands and descended like a child. And the address he'd given—the house on Halsey—wasn't his own. It belonged to his nephew, who stood to inherit the actual house, the good one, if Desmond died before the paperwork went through. Rosalind had written it all down in one of his notebooks, addressed it to the precinct, and mailed it anonymously. Six weeks later: arrest. He'd read about it over somebody else's shoulder on the bus and felt nothing so much as a door closing quietly.

The second was Lorraine Pettibone. Carbon monoxide, ruled accidental—a faulty heater in her garden apartment. But Rosalind had taken her booking call herself, three days prior, and she'd mentioned—chattering the way lonely people chatter to dispatchers, because dispatchers are the last strangers who have to listen—that her landlord had been "so helpful," coming down twice that week to "check the heater." Nobody checks a heater twice in one week unless they're making sure of something. And her voice had carried that particular bright brittleness Rosalind had learned to recognize as fear wearing a costume of gratitude. He'd sent another anonymous letter. That one took longer; the boyfriend was careful. But careful men slip in small ways, and eventually a warrant turned up what the heater was supposed to explain away.

The third was the one he still dreamed about, though he'd never admit that to anyone, least of all himself. Tomas Wren, thirty-four, drowned in the reservoir. Accidental, said the coroner—cramped while swimming at night. But Wren had called for a cab to the reservoir at eleven p.m., and the voice on the line hadn't been afraid of water. It had been afraid of the person who'd be waiting when he got there. Rosalind could still hear it: the way Wren had paused too long before saying *just me tonight*, the way he'd spelled his destination street name unnecessarily, the way people do when they want a record to exist. *I want this on the record,* he'd almost seemed to say. *Somebody should know where I went.* Rosalind had caught the call at 11:04 and heard about the body at dawn, and he had known—not suspected, known—with the flat certainty of arithmetic. The brother-in-law. It was always family, or nearly-family. People killed for proximity, for inheritance, for the unbearable intimacy of being known.

Three letters. Three arrests, eventually, though never traced back to him. The papers never learned a dispatcher had handed them the thread; the detectives who worked the cases assumed some guilty conscience in the family had written the letters, and in a way that was true. Rosalind's conscience had been guilty of knowing things since he was old enough to know them.

He kept the letters untraceable. Plain paper, bought with cash, block capitals, mailed from different neighborhoods. He kept himself untraceable the same way—one-bedroom apartment under a dentist's office, no social media, no photographs, a name so common it dissolved into the phone book. Night shift, because night shift meant fewer eyes, and because the city told its truths at night. He had built a life the way you build a soundproof room: deliberately, plank by plank, until nothing got in and nothing got out.

Which was why the feeling he'd had lately—this thin, papery sensation, like wallpaper beginning to lift at a corner—bothered him so much.

It had started small. A man at the all-night diner on Fulton had looked at him two beats too long last Tuesday, then looked away with the studied blankness of someone who'd been told to look. Then the mail: his utility bill had arrived opened and resealed, the glue smeared, and maybe that was nothing, maybe the postal service was just the postal service. Maybe.

And then there was Elias Vane.

Rosalind turned the name over in his mind the way you'd turn a stone found in your shoe. Vane had died eleven days ago—heart failure, eighty-one years old, alone in the big house on Corliss Hill. Rosalind had read the obituary in the paper's back pages, three paragraphs, dignified and vague. Estranged mentor, the obituary said, which was a strange phrase for a newspaper to use about anyone. What it meant, Rosalind knew, was that Vane had once mattered enormously to someone and then stopped mattering, or been stopped.

Vane had been his teacher, once. Not formally—nothing so clean. When Rosalind was young and broke and working the switchboard at a smaller company across town, Vane had come in sometimes, an old man with a hearing aid he conspicuously didn't need, asking questions about the routes, the drivers, the patterns of the night. He'd bought Rosalind coffee exactly twice and talked about listening the way other men talked about fishing or God. *Most people hear,* Vane had said, stirring his coffee without drinking it. *Very few listen. And the ones who can listen—well. They're dangerous, aren't they.* Rosalind had been twenty-three and flattered. It had taken him years to understand that the sentence had been a warning, or a threat, or possibly both, and by then Vane had drifted out of his life as gradually as he'd drifted in, leaving behind nothing but the habit of listening and a low, persistent hum of unease that Rosalind had never managed to name.

They hadn't spoken in six years. Rosalind hadn't gone to the funeral. He'd told himself it was because funerals were exposure—crowds, names spoken aloud, faces catalogued. That was true. But underneath the truth was another truth, which was that he hadn't wanted to see the coffin closed, because some part of him still needed Vane to be listening somewhere, the way you need the lighthouse to keep turning even after your ship has come in.

The board blinked. Car twelve, done with a fare, requesting position. Rosalind acknowledged it in two words and let the silence come back.

At 3:47, the switchboard lit.

He glanced at the caller ID out of pure reflex, the way you glance at any screen that demands your attention, and then he looked again, properly, because the display showed a number he recognized—not from memory, but from the system itself. The routing software flagged it automatically, a little amber tag that popped beside the incoming line: **NUMBER NOT IN SERVICE**.

That happened sometimes. Spoofed calls, telemarketers running number generators, kids with apps. Usually the tag came with a garbage string of digits. This one didn't. This one displayed a full, valid-looking local number, formatted correctly, complete with area code—and Rosalind knew, with the certainty of a man who had stared at this same console for eleven years, that the software only tagged numbers that way when the exchange itself had been decommissioned. Not blocked. Not spoofed, probably. Retired. A number that belonged to a dead line.

He let it ring twice. Professional habit. Never answer on the first ring; it unsettles callers, makes them feel watched.

Then he keyed the line. "City Cab, this is Dispatch."

Static first. The deep, oceanic static of an open line, the kind you get from a handset lying on a table, or a connection routed through equipment older than the building it sat in. And beneath the static—faint, but unmistakable to a man whose entire life was frequencies—a voice. Not speaking. Breathing. Slow, deliberate breaths, the kind a person takes when they are listening as carefully as Rosalind was.

"Caller, do you need a cab?" His voice came out level. He was proud of that, later.

The breathing continued. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Then, so quietly he would have missed it if he hadn't spent a lifetime leaning into silences, the voice spoke.

"You always did answer on the second ring."

Male. Old. Dry as paper. And beneath the age and the distortion, threaded through it like wire through cable, something achingly familiar—a cadence Rosalind knew, a rhythm of speech he had heard across a diner table twenty years ago, delivered over untouched coffee.

His hand stayed steady on the console. His heart did not.

"This line is out of service," Rosalind said. "Who is this?"

A sound that might have been a laugh, filtered through distance and dead equipment. "You know who. You've always known. That's your whole gift, isn't it, Rosalind? Knowing." A pause, filled with static, like snow falling on a dead channel. "I'm sorry I couldn't tell you sooner. There wasn't time, and then there was too much. I've written it all down instead. You'll understand when—" The voice thinned, frayed, and for a moment Rosalind thought the connection had dropped. Then it returned, closer somehow, clearer, as if the speaker had leaned into the mouthpiece of a telephone that had no business carrying anything at all.

"—when the letter comes. Watch for the letter. And Rosalind—" the old cadence caught, stumbled, recovered, "—they're not finished. Whatever you think is buried, it isn't. Count the heirs. Count what's left. One of them is going to need a fourth funeral, and I couldn't—I couldn't stop it from in here."

"In here," Rosalind repeated. "Where is in here?"

But the line had already begun to die, the static rising like water over the voice, and the last thing he heard, faint and distinct and terrible, was not words at all. It was humming. Four notes, descending, an old tune he hadn't heard since he was small enough to be hummed to sleep by someone whose face he could no longer retrieve—someone the records said had left, or vanished, or never existed at all, depending on which lie you preferred.

Then the dial tone. Clean, modern, immediate. As if the dead line had never connected at all.

Rosalind sat with his hand on the dead key for a long moment. Around him the depot breathed its three a.m. breath: radiators, coffee machine, the garage doors far below. On the board, car twelve rolled north through the empty streets, carrying some stranger home through the dark, oblivious.

He pulled the call log toward him and wrote down the number by hand, digit by digit, although the screen had already cleared it. Then he wrote the date, the time, and the single word *line retired*, and underlined it twice, because underlining was how he told himself a thing was real.

Elias Vane was dead. Rosalind had seen the obituary. Eleven days cold.

And yet a voice with Vane's cadence had just told him to watch the mail.

Outside, the sky over the rail yards had begun its slow bruise toward gray. Rosalind finished his shift the way he finished every shift—log signed, board cleared, headset hung on its hook with a click that sounded, this morning, unusually final. He walked out into the cold end of the night, past the row of sleeping cabs with their roofs glowing faintly yellow under the sodium lights, and he did not yet know that three blocks from his apartment, in a metal mailbox with a broken latch, a letter was already waiting for him—postmarked yesterday, written in a dead man's hand, naming a murder no one had reported and warning of one still to come.

He only knew that the frequency had changed. After eleven years of listening to the city lie, something had finally started listening back.
