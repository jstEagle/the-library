## 5. Night Routes

The depot at three in the morning had a sound Rosalind Whitlock had never been able to describe to anyone who hadn't sat inside it: the low electrical hum of the switchboard, the tick of the wall clock that ran four minutes fast because nobody had ever bothered to fix it, and beneath it all, the radio traffic of the city's insomnia—drivers calling in fares, drunks calling for rides, the occasional voice so calm it made his ear twitch the way a dog's does at a frequency too high for human comfort.

He had the ledger open under the dispatch console, hidden in plain sight the way he hid everything. That was the trick of invisibility, he'd learned early: not concealment but ordinariness. A man reading paperwork at his desk was nothing. A man reading *that* paperwork was nothing, as long as no one looked closely enough to see what it was.

The ledger lay open to the page he'd been staring at for twenty minutes. The Quiet Account. Payments, monthly, stretching back further than he'd yet been able to read without his chest tightening. And beside each payment date, in Vane's small, precise hand—the handwriting from the confession letter, unmistakable now that he'd seen it—a notation. Not an address. Not a name. A route number.

Rosalind knew every route number in this city the way priests know psalms.

Route 14 ran the river district. Route 9 cut through the old shipping quarter where the Vane fortune had supposedly been made. Route 22 ended at St. Ambrose Cemetery.

And on eleven dates across thirty years—eleven dates circled in the ledger with a single thin line, like a fisherman marking a chart—cabs from this depot had been dispatched to 44 Marlow Street.

His childhood address.

He'd told himself, driving home after the storage locker, that it might be coincidence. Eleven coincidences, spaced at intervals of two to four years, each one falling within seventy-two hours of a ledger payment. He'd told himself that the way you tell yourself the floor is solid when you're standing over a basement you've never been allowed into.

Tonight he was going to stop telling himself things.

* * *

The cab logs lived in the archive room, forty years of them, bound in green canvas spines that shed dust like dandruff when you pulled them off the shelf. Nobody came down here. The company had digitized everything after 2004, and before that, well—paper was for lawsuits, and nobody sued a taxi company. The room smelled of cold concrete and toner and time.

Rosalind carried down a legal pad, a pencil, and the ledger wrapped in a canvas mail sack, as if he were transporting something mundane. In a sense he was. Mundanity was his armor.

He started with 1987, the earliest year the depot's records still held. The log books then were handwritten by whichever dispatcher drew the graveyard shift, entries in a dozen different slants and pressures: *Cab 31, dispatched 0240, Marlow St., fare paid cash, driver R. Okafor.*

Marlow Street. 1987. Two years before the death at the heart of whatever Elias Vane had spent his life filing away.

He worked backward through the decade, then forward through the next, and the pattern assembled itself the way patterns always did for him—not all at once but in accretion, detail by detail, until suddenly there was a shape in the fog and he couldn't unsee it.

Every circled date in the ledger corresponded to a dispatch to Marlow Street. But not just any dispatch. Each one was logged as a pickup or drop-off at the house itself—his mother's house, the narrow brick row house with the iron railing he used to hang from like a gymnast—and each one was followed within the week by a longer run: airport runs, out-of-town charters, fares logged to passengers whose names repeated across decades in ways that made his neck prickle. A man named Grieves. A woman who signed only *M. Halloran.* And once, in 1996, in a dispatcher's careful cursive: *V., E.*

Elias Vane himself, riding a company cab to Marlow Street at 1:15 in the morning, six days after a Quiet Account payment cleared.

Rosalind set the pencil down. His hands were steady—that was the terrible part. Thirty years of listening to liars had taught his body to go still when the truth arrived, the way the air goes still before weather changes.

The payments weren't hush money flowing outward. They were maintenance. Someone had been paying Vane—or Vane had been paying someone—to keep a watch on a house where a boy used to live. Eleven visits across thirty years. Checking on him. Or checking on the story. Making sure the witness stayed quiet, stayed gone, stayed *nobody.*

*They didn't forget me,* he thought. *They audited me.*

The clock upstairs ticked its lying four minutes ahead. Somewhere above him, a driver keyed up asking for a fare to the bus station, and Rosalind heard himself answer automatically—"Copy, Marta'll assign it"—before he realized he'd spoken aloud to an empty room.

* * *

Marta Reyes found him at the end of shift, which was how she found everyone; she had a supervisor's instinct for who was bleeding quietly.

"Whitlock." She dropped into the chair beside his desk, a paper cup of vending machine coffee balanced on her knee like it was a sleeping infant she'd learned to carry. "You've been wrong all night."

"I've been working."

"You've been *downstairs* all night. Nobody goes downstairs unless they're hiding something or looking for something, and you don't hide anything, you're the most annoyingly transparent man in this building." She sipped. "So you're looking for something."

Rosalind closed the ledger drawer with his hip, casual, practiced. "Old logs. Audit stuff."

"We're not audited. We're barely regulated. I checked once, out of spite." Marta had worked the day desk for nineteen years and covered nights when the schedule fell apart, which meant she'd covered his shifts often enough to know his rhythms better than anyone alive. She knew he took his breaks at odd intervals. She knew he never ate at the desk. She knew, though she'd never said so, that he could tell when a caller was lying about their destination, and that he'd once talked a jumper off the Harrow Street bridge by keeping her on the line for a fake fare quote for forty minutes until the patrol car arrived.

She was, in the strictest sense, the closest thing he had to a colleague. Which was exactly why he'd kept her at arm's length for two decades.

"Elias Vane," she said.

The name landed between them like a coin dropped on marble.

"What about him?"

"Your face did a thing just now. When I said his name." She turned the coffee cup slowly. "I drove past the funeral home that day. Big turnout. Old money." A pause, calibrated, the way she calibrated everything. "You went. I saw the notice in your hand last week. You don't read obituaries, Rosalind. You told me once they're the most lied-to section of the newspaper."

He almost smiled. Almost. "I said the police blotter was."

"You said both." She set the cup down. "Look. I'm not going to ask what you're doing, because you'll do that thing where you give me a technically true answer that tells me nothing, and I'll want to throw the coffee at you."

"That thing," Rosalind said.

"That thing." Her voice lost its dryness. What replaced it was quieter, and it unsettled him more than the ledger had. "But I'm going to say one thing, and then I'm going to let you get back to whatever it is. Twenty-two years ago, when I transferred here from the Eastside depot, my first week, a man called in drunk, said he needed a cab to the river. You took the call. You kept him talking about the fare zones for an hour. You saved his life and you never told anyone, and I only know because he came back sober a year later and asked for you by name, and you pretended not to remember." She stood, retrieving her cup. "I have watched you spend twenty years taking care of people who will never know your name. So whatever you're looking for downstairs—you're not doing it alone anymore. That's not a question. That's a weather report."

She walked back toward the day desk, and Rosalind sat very still, feeling something in his chest that he hadn't felt since he was eight years old and a kind stranger named Elias Vane had knelt down to his eye level and said, *You're safe now. No one will ever find you here.*

Safe. Hidden. Audited.

He opened the drawer again and looked at the last entry he'd copied onto his legal pad, the one he'd been avoiding since midnight:

**March 2011 — Cab 17, dispatched 0312, Marlow St. Passenger: J.V. Fare logged, no return trip.**

Julian Vane. Standing outside the house where Rosalind grew up, at three in the morning, fourteen years ago. The same Julian Vane who had shaken his hand at the funeral with warm, dry fingers and said, *Elias spoke of you. The dispatcher. He admired your discretion.*

Discretion. There was the word for it. Everyone in this story had been paid for their discretion—the operator, the lawyer, whoever M. Halloran was, the dead man who'd written a confession he'd mailed one day too late to matter.

And now the eldest heir knew exactly who Rosalind Whitlock was, had known, perhaps, since the day Elias died—if not long before.

Rosalind flipped the legal pad to a clean page and wrote, in the neat block letters he used for anything that might someday be evidence:

**JULIAN VANE — MARLOW ST., 3:12 AM, MARCH 2011. ASK HIM WHY.**

Outside, the sky over the garage was going the color of dishwater, first light bleeding into the last of the night routes. His shift was over. For the first time in thirty years, Rosalind Whitlock intended to spend his daylight somewhere he wasn't supposed to be.
