## 8. What the Tide Returned

The boatyard smelled of diesel and salt and the particular rust of things left too long in sea air, and Danny Okafor had spent twenty-five years convincing himself that smell was enough — that a man could live a whole life inside the ordinary, patching outboards and scraping hulls, and never once have to open the door at the back of his skull where October 1999 still lived with the light on.

Then the lawyer came back.

Danny saw him from the top of the gantry, standing at the yard gate in his city coat, looking wrong the way a heron looks wrong standing in a car park. Jonah Hartwell. The name had gone around Greywater Bay like a stone skipped across still water — Marguerite Voss's executor, asking questions nobody asked anymore. Old Mrs. Trelawny at the post office had told him so over the counter, loud enough for the whole queue. Rosa's care home had mentioned a visitor. Even the chippy had an opinion.

Danny climbed down the ladder slowly, wiping his hands on a rag that had been black since the Carter administration, and decided — in the last three seconds he would ever have of not deciding — to walk over anyway.

"You're the boat mechanic." Hartwell said it like a question he already knew the answer to.

"I'm the boat mechanic." Danny looked past him at the water, flat and grey as sheet metal. "And you're the man asking questions in a town where we don't."

"Mrs. Okafor—"

"Danny. Nobody calls me Mrs. Okafor except tax people." He tossed the rag onto a coil of rope. "You want something. Everyone who comes here wants something. Fuel, or a fix, or a story."

"Actually," Hartwell said, "I wanted to look at your boats."

Danny laughed before he could stop it, a short ugly sound. "That's a new one."

* * *

He let the lawyer walk the rows with him because refusing would have been worse. Refusing was a signal. In Greywater Bay you learned early that the only safe answer to anything was boredom, delivered convincingly, and Danny had been delivering it for a quarter century. He pointed out the crabbers, the one pleasure cruiser some fool kept moored through winter, the skiff with the soft patch in her stern he'd been meaning to glass over since spring.

Hartwell stopped at the last boat in the row, an old twenty-two-footer under a tarp, and didn't touch it, just stood there looking at it the way men look at graves.

"That's the *Kestrel*," Danny said.

"Is it."

"She belonged to Aldous Voss. Then Edmund. Then nobody." Danny heard his own voice go flat and careful, each word set down like a full glass on a moving table. "She's been sitting here since I took over the yard. Rent's paid a year ahead, always has been, by a solicitor's account nobody will tell me about. I don't ask. She doesn't leak. Everybody's happy."

"Who pays now?"

"Same account. Same amount. Every year, like clockwork." Danny met his eyes finally. "You'd know about clockwork. You're the executor. Maybe it's you paying."

"It isn't."

"No," Danny said. "I didn't think it was."

The wind came off the bay and pushed at the tarp, and beneath it the *Kestrel* shifted on her cradle with a groan like an old man turning over in bed, and Danny thought: *twenty-five years, and she's still waiting for someone to ask.*

"Walk with me," he said, because if he was going to do this — and God help him, some part of him had already done it, had been doing it since the lawyer first appeared at the gate — he wasn't going to do it standing next to that boat.

* * *

They walked the sea wall toward the point, past the war memorial with its four names and its fifth, newer line — EDMUND VOSS, 1971–1999 — that half the town still touched with two fingers when they passed, the way you'd touch a lintel for luck. Danny had never once touched it. He couldn't explain why, even to himself. It felt like touching a lie with your bare hand.

"You interviewed everyone," Danny said. "The harbourmaster. The vicar. The Trelawny woman. They all gave you the same story."

"They gave me the same story," Hartwell agreed. "Word for word. Same pauses. Same little shrug at the same moment. Twenty-five years of rehearsal."

"And what did you make of that?"

"I've done this work long enough to know there are two kinds of towns. Towns that forget, and towns that agree." He walked a few steps. "Greywater Bay agrees."

Danny stopped at the wall and put both hands on the cold granite and looked down at the water below, the way he used to as a boy, when the tide dropped and the rocks showed their teeth. Below the cliffs. That was the thing about this town — everything important had happened below the cliffs, in the dark, in the cold, and everything above them had happened ever since.

"There were teeth," he said.

Hartwell went very still. Danny felt it without looking — the change in the air, the way a room changes when a dog stops wagging.

"The body they pulled out of the water," Danny said. "Two days after. Off Gull Rock, tangled in the kelp. I was sixteen. My foster mother—" His voice caught on the word and he hated it, hated that after all these years it still caught. "Rosa. Rosa Voss, she was the housekeeper up at the house, and she took in strays, kids the county didn't know what to do with, and I was one of them. I was on the recovery boat because old Sam Petrie needed hands and paid in fish and I'd have worked for nothing to be near the water. So I was there. When they brought him up."

"What did you see?"

"I saw a dead man wearing Edmund Voss's ring." Danny turned the words over like stones, choosing which to throw. "Signet ring, gold, the Voss crest on it. Everyone on that boat knew the ring. You couldn't grow up in this town without knowing that ring; Aldous wore his to church like a threat. And the ring fit. That's what got me, even then, even at sixteen — the ring fit the finger. Whoever planned it knew Edmund's size, or Edmund gave it up himself, or—" He stopped. Breathed. "But the teeth."

"The teeth," Hartwell repeated quietly.

"The coroner identified him partly on dental records. Edmund's records. From the family dentist in Ashcombe." Danny laughed, and there was no humour in it, only the old cold. "Except I'd seen Edmund Voss's smile a hundred times. He used to come down to the yard in the summers, before everything, before the trouble, and he had this gap — front tooth, left side, chipped when he was a kid falling off the wall right there." He tapped the granite under his hand. "Whole town knew that gap. Kids used to copy it. And the man on the boat had a full set. Perfect teeth. Not a chip, not a gap. I was sixteen and I saw it and I said it — I said, *that's not Mr. Edmund, he's got a broken tooth* — and Sam Petrie put his hand on my shoulder like it weighed a hundred pounds and said, *Danny. Look at me. Look at the water. Tell me what you see.*"

"And what did you see?"

"Water," Danny said. "I saw water. And I understood, being sixteen and smarter than anyone gave me credit for, that Sam wasn't asking about my eyes. He was asking about my life. Because the Voss household fed me. Clothed me. Kept me out of the system that had already chewed through two placements before Rosa said *enough, this one stays*. Because half the crew on that boat owed their roofs to the same house. Because the alternative to agreeing was a boys' home in Ashcombe and everybody knew what those were like." He spread his hands. "So I looked at the water. And when the coroner's man came, I said I hadn't seen anything unusual. And Sam squeezed my shoulder, and that was that."

The tide was coming in below them, filling the channels between the rocks, erasing the gaps one by one. Danny watched it work and thought: *that's all any of us ever did. Fill the gaps. Smooth it over. Let the water close.*

"Whose body was it?" Hartwell asked.

"If I knew that, I'd have slept better for twenty-five years." Danny shook his head. "There was talk, later, quiet talk, that it might have been a sailor lost off the Portuguese trawlers that autumn. Unclaimed. Nobody local missing. A body that needed a grave more than a name needed a body. But I don't know. All I know is what I saw, and what I said, and what it cost."

"Did it cost you?"

Danny looked at him then, really looked, and saw the lawyer wasn't being cruel — he genuinely didn't understand yet, this man with his retainer cash and his safety deposit boxes, how much a lie weighs when you carry it from sixteen years old.

"You carry it," Danny said. "That's the cost. You carry it into every job, every friendship, every night you can't sleep in October. You carry it when you take over the boatyard and find the *Kestrel* in your care and realize somebody, somewhere, is still paying rent on a dead man's boat, which means somewhere, somehow, the dead man isn't. You carry it until a man in a city coat walks through your gate and asks to look at your boats, and you understand that the carrying is over, one way or the other, because either you tell him or you spend the rest of your life watching the door."

* * *

They ended up in the yard office, a shed that smelled of two-stroke and instant coffee, because Danny found he couldn't say the rest of it standing in the open. Some things needed walls.

"Rosa followed him that night," he said. "Down the cliff path. I know because she told me — not then, not for years, but she told me, when she started to get frightened of losing her memory, like she was putting things in safe places before the bank closed. Edmund went down to the water and there was a boat. He chose to go. Whatever else is true, whatever anybody tells you — he chose it. He was running from his father, and the whole household helped him run, and when it was done, Aldous—" Danny paused, choosing. "Aldous didn't rage. That was the frightening part. He sat at the head of the table the next morning and said, *my son is dead, and no one in this family will ever say otherwise*, and you could hear that it wasn't grief talking. It was strategy. Edmund had done something with company papers — faked evidence, Rosa said, made it look like theft, so that if he ever surfaced he'd be a criminal and no one would believe a word he said. Aldous knew. And instead of turning his son in, he buried him. Because a dead son who stays dead is worth more than a living son in prison, and because—" Danny's jaw tightened. "Because hunting a ghost for ten years, which is what the ledger says he did, that's not a father grieving. That's a man who cannot tolerate losing."

"You know about the ledger."

"I know about everything you know, Mr. Hartwell, and a few things you don't, because I grew up in that house and you grew up in a city." He leaned forward. "Celeste gave you the ledger. Fine. Read it. But understand what you're holding. That family didn't kill Edmund Voss. That family *built* him a death, brick by brick, out of love and terror and Aldous's iron will, and then they lived inside it for twenty-five years, and every single person who repeats that polished story to you — the harbourmaster, the vicar, old Trelawny — every one of them is keeping a roof over someone. That's what this town is. It's not a conspiracy. It's a family, the big kind, the kind you don't get born into but get carried by."

"Then help me end it," Hartwell said. "The partner who went to prison — Marcus Bell — he confessed to a murder that never happened. He died in prison for a drowning that was a doorway. His daughter is sitting on a letter he wrote her. There's a man alive somewhere who let all that happen rather than come home."

"Do you think I don't know?" Danny's voice cracked and he let it. "Marcus Bell coached my football team. He drove me to matches when nobody else would drive anywhere with me. He went to prison protecting this — protecting *Edmund*, maybe, or protecting Rosa, or protecting the whole rotten tender arrangement — and I helped bury the truth with a lie about teeth when I was sixteen years old. You think I haven't counted that? Every year in October, when this whole town raises a glass to a man who isn't dead, I stand in the corner of the Anchor with my drink untouched doing arithmetic. Marcus Bell's years. My silence. It doesn't balance. It will never balance."

"Then help me balance it."

"No." Danny said it fast, before the other thing in him could win. "No. Listen to me. There's a dinner coming. Six weeks. The anniversary, the ritual, the whole town in its Sunday grief, and you're planning to stand up in the middle of it with your papers and your proof and tear the roof off everyone's house at once. And I'm telling you as someone who has lived under that roof his whole life — you don't know what's up there. Aldous is dead, fine. But someone is still paying rent on the *Kestrel*. Someone has kept that account alive for twenty-five years without missing a payment, and whoever it is wanted Edmund forgotten, or wanted him *staying* forgotten, and I promise you they are watching you the way I'm watching you right now. Except they have more money than I have and fewer scruples."

"So you want me to stop."

Danny looked at his hands. Big hands, scarred, good with engines, useless at this. "I want you to be careful," he said. "I want you to wait until after the dinner. Do it quietly, legally, in a courtroom where nobody can burn anything down. Don't do it in front of two hundred grieving people and a family that's been rehearsing a lie since before I could drive." He stood, because sitting felt too much like surrender. "I've told you about the teeth. That's more than I've told anyone in twenty-five years, and it's going to cost me, and I know it, and I told you anyway, because Marcus Bell deserves better than a lie, and because Rosa's getting old and I want her to hear the truth spoken while she can still hold it. But I'm begging you — *begging* you — don't force it at that dinner. Whatever you think you know, you don't know who else knows. And whoever stopped writing to Marguerite eighteen months ago—"

Hartwell's face changed. Just slightly. Just enough.

"—you know about the letters," Danny said slowly. "Of course you do. You've found the letters." He laughed, hollow. "Then you already understand. Somebody out there cut a thread that ran thirty years between a brother and a sister, and they did it recently, and they did it deliberately, and they did it while you were being hired. That's not grief, Mr. Hartwell. That's not a family keeping a secret out of love. That's maintenance. Somebody is maintaining the lie like I maintain these boats — regularly, professionally, against wear."

The wind rattled the office door in its frame. Out in the yard, under its tarp, the *Kestrel* groaned against her cradle.

"One more thing," Danny said, and his mouth had gone dry, because he'd sworn this part to himself he'd never say aloud, and here it was coming up anyway like floodwater. "The night Edmund went down the cliff path — Rosa followed him, yes. But she wasn't the only one who saw. I was on the cliff path myself that night. Sixteen, sneaking a smoke, hiding from curfew. I saw two figures come back up. Not one. Rosa — and someone else, smaller, walking behind her, and I've spent twenty-five years telling myself it was one of the Voss cousins, anyone, anyone at all." He met Hartwell's eyes. "But I've been having dreams lately, since you came to town. And in the dreams, the second figure turns around, and it's got Julian Voss's face on it. And Julian was fourteen that year. And Julian is the one who keeps the estate books now. And Julian is the one whose signature, if you pull the harbour trust records, has renewed the *Kestrel*'s mooring fee every single year since Aldous died."

Hartwell stood very still. "You're saying the boy who may have walked Rosa home from the cliff that night grew up to become the lie's accountant."

"I'm saying," Danny said, "that in this town, the truth doesn't stay buried. It gets *inherited*. And somebody just inherited it with a sale to developers pending and a dead uncle who won't stay legally dead — and Mr. Hartwell, whatever you do, whatever you decide about that dinner — I'd start watching what Julian Voss does next. Because I burned a boat once, fixing one careless. And I know exactly how a fire starts when a man needs a problem to disappear."

Outside, far down the bay, a gull screamed once and quit, and the tide kept coming in over the rocks, patient as paperwork, closing the gaps.
