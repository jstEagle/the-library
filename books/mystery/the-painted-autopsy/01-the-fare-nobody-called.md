## 1. The Fare Nobody Called

The radio at Grayhaven Taxi & Livery had a voice like a man reading his own eulogy, and at eleven minutes past midnight on a Tuesday in October, it said: "Casper, you got anything moving near the cannery road?"

Casper Sterling leaned back in the swivel chair that had been squeaking since the Clinton administration and considered the board in front of him. Four cars out. Two of them were Denny and Louie, who between them had logged maybe nine fares all shift and were currently parked behind the Harbor Light diner arguing about baseball through the dispatch channel with their mics keyed open, which was against company policy and also the closest thing either of them had to a social life.

"Negative," Casper said. "It's dead out there."

"It's always dead," said the voice, which belonged to Ruthie Pell, who ran the answering service three doors down and had opinions about everything from the mayor to the correct way to fold a fitted sheet. "You know what's not dead? My sciatica. You want a fare, come rub my back."

"I'm on the clock, Ruthie."

"So's my pain."

He clicked off before she could develop the bit further. The office of Grayhaven Taxi & Livery was a room the size of a generous closet, wedged between a nail salon and a bail bondsman on Cutter Street, and it contained: one desk, one swivel chair, one radio rig older than most of the drivers, a corkboard with a map of Grayhaven so sun-faded that the harbor appeared to have moved inland sometime around 2009, and a coffee maker that produced a liquid Casper had privately classified as a controlled substance.

The night shift ran from ten to six. On good nights — Friday nights, payday nights, nights when the ferries ran late — the radio never stopped, and Casper was less a dispatcher than an air traffic controller for drunks, which he'd once described to Daniel as "the only job where you get paid to be disappointed in strangers." Daniel had laughed at that. That had been — Casper did the math and then stopped doing the math, because the answer was four years ago, and four years ago felt like a different country, one with friendlier customs officials.

Tonight was not a good night. Tonight the city of Grayhaven had gone quiet the way it did when the season turned, when the tourists packed up their windbreakers and their grievances and left the port to its own devices. The fog was coming in off the water in long gray ropes, wrapping the cranes at the container terminal, softening the sodium lights down at Pier 9 until they looked like candles in a church nobody attended anymore.

At 12:40, Louie picked up a fare outside the Anchor Bar — a big man in a fish-company jacket who wanted to go to the trailer park on the dunes and spent the whole ride telling Louie that the herring were finished, done, cooked, and that anyone who said otherwise was selling something. Casper heard none of this directly, but Louie summarized it afterward in the manner of a war correspondent: "Herring's done, Casper. Cooked. Guy says otherwise is selling something."

"Noted for the record."

"You should write that down. That's economics."

Denny got a call at 1:15 from the woman on Marsh Lane who called every Tuesday and Thursday night after her show ended — she needed a ride to her sister's in Bellhaven at six a.m., standing appointment, twelve years running, and every week she asked if it would be Denny, and every week Denny said "it's whoever's on, Mrs. Okonkwo," and every week she said, "Make it Denny," and somehow it always was. Casper had stopped investigating this years ago. Some mysteries you leave alone because solving them might ruin them.

By two o'clock the board was empty again. Casper poured himself a cup of the coffee-substance and stood at the window watching fog eat Cutter Street. Across the way, the bail bondsman's neon sign buzzed and flickered — GUARANTEED FREEDOM, it said, or had said once; now half the letters were dead and it read G A R N E D E O , which Casper thought was honestly more accurate.

His phone sat on the desk beside the logbook, screen dark.

He had not looked at it since six o'clock yesterday, when his niece-by-marriage — no, that wasn't right, Daniel had never married, so she was nothing by marriage, she was just Priya, Daniel's neighbor, the one who actually showed up for people — had texted him: *Service is Saturday, 10 a.m., Hollis & Crane. Wearing the blue suit? He liked the blue suit.*

Casper owned a blue suit. It was hanging in his closet right now, dry-cleaned, bagged, ready, like a prop for a play he'd decided not to audition for.

The service had been this morning. Ten a.m. He knew this because he'd been awake for it, lying in bed staring at the ceiling of his apartment above the laundromat on Vane Street, listening to the dryers thump below like slow hearts, doing the arithmetic of distance: Hollis & Crane was eleven minutes away by car, fourteen if the drawbridge was up. He could have been there in eleven minutes. He had instead gotten up, made eggs, burned the eggs, eaten them anyway, and driven to work early, which Ruthie had noted with surprise, because in nineteen years Casper Sterling had never once been early.

"You sick?" she'd asked.

"No."

"Huh," she'd said, in the tone of a woman filing something away for later.

The ruling had come down Wednesday. Accidental drowning. That was the phrase the *Grayhaven Courier* used, and the phrase Detective Brandt used on the phone when Casper called, and the phrase everyone at the garage used when they thought he wasn't listening, delivered in the careful hushed voices people use around grief, like grief was a sleeping dog. *Accidental drowning.* Daniel Sterling, forty-one years old, found at the base of the old ferry slip off Kestrel Point with water in his lungs and no marks on him worth mentioning, and the tide had taken his satchel, his phone, his glasses — Daniel had worn glasses since he was nine — and the police had looked at the smooth cold arithmetic of it and shrugged the shrug of men with caseloads.

*Accidental drowning.* As if Daniel had simply walked into the sea the way he walked into everything else, too fast, too curious, too sure the world would make room for him.

They hadn't spoken in four years. Not properly. There had been a text at Christmas — Daniel's, always Daniel's, because Casper's pride was load-bearing and Daniel's forgiveness was bottomless, and the asymmetry of that had embarrassed Casper so much he'd answered fewer and fewer of the texts until eventually they stopped coming, and then, last month, the last one came:

*Found something at the works. Big. Call me. Please, Cas. I miss your dumb voice.*

And Casper had read it at 2 a.m. on the night shift, thumb hovering over the screen, and told himself he'd call tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, and then a Tuesday came when the call didn't matter anymore, because a jogger found Daniel at the ferry slip and the tide was already arranging things.

*Please, Cas. I miss your dumb voice.*

Casper set the coffee down harder than he meant to. It slopped onto the logbook, blurring the 11:40 entry from last night into a gray bloom. He blotted it with a napkin from the Anchor Bar — HERRING FESTIVAL 2016, they never run out — and told himself the tightness in his chest was the coffee, which was fair, because the coffee could plausibly kill a man.

At 3:20 the radio crackled: "Dispatch, Car 3, I got a live one at the Anchor. Wants the paint factory."

Casper frowned at the board. Car 3 was Denny. "Nobody goes to the paint factory, Denny. It's condemned."

"That's what I told him. He says he knows. Says he wants to look at it." A pause, the sound of Denny covering the mic and having a brief negotiation with reality. "He's paying cash and he's not drunk, Casper. Well. He's drunk, but he's drunk polite."

"The Kestrel Works?"

"Yeah. Old gate on the harbor side."

Casper looked at the map on the corkboard, at the smudge of streets north of Pier 9 where the Kestrel Paint Works sat behind its chain-link and its NO TRESPASSING signs going soft with rust. He hadn't thought about the place in years. Nobody had. It had been dying since the fire back in — '98? '99? — and it sat there now like a whale skeleton, all ribs and rust, and the city had agreed, collectively and without discussion, to stop looking at it.

"Log it," Casper said. "One-way. Tell him we don't do returns from condemned property."

"He knows," Denny said. "That's the weird part. Guy tipped me twenty bucks and said, 'Tell your dispatcher thanks for the ride home.' How's he know you?"

"How would I know?" But Casper wrote it down anyway, because writing things down was the whole religion of dispatching, and because the fare bothered him in a small way he couldn't locate, like a pebble in a boot. *Thanks for the ride home.* He'd never dispatched a car to the Kestrel Works in his life.

Except — and here the pebble shifted — except he checked. Habit, mostly; boredom, partly; and under both, some current he didn't name, he pulled the bound logbooks from the shelf, last month first, flipping back through the carbon-paper ghosts of fares. Drunks to the dunes. Nurses to the hospital at shift change. Mrs. Okonkwo, Tuesdays and Thursdays. And there, three weeks ago, an entry in his own handwriting: *Car 2, 11:52 p.m., Anchor Bar to Kestrel Pt. Rd., fare declined, comp'd per management.* He remembered it now — a quiet man in a raincoat who'd changed his mind halfway there and asked to be taken back. Comp'd, because arguing with sad men at midnight wasn't in the job description.

Two runs to a dead factory in three weeks, in a city where nobody went to the dead factory.

Casper closed the book and made a note to himself to stop noticing things at 3 a.m., because noticing things at 3 a.m. was how a man talked himself into believing in patterns, and patterns at 3 a.m. were usually just loneliness wearing a costume.

***

The rest of the shift passed the way the tide passes — imperceptibly, and then all at once. At 4:40 a bachelorette party required three cars to the casino boat and screamed directly into the radio for six consecutive minutes, during which Casper learned more than he wished to know about a person named Jess. At 5:15 Ruthie called to say the sciatica had spread to her hip and that if he loved her he'd bring donuts, and he did, because loving Ruthie was easier than arguing with her. At 5:50 Denny brought Car 3 in smelling of cigarettes and confession.

"Guy at the factory," Denny said, while they ran the day's paperwork. "Weird duck. Stood at the fence just looking at the place. Didn't even get out of the cab at first. Then he paid me and said—" Denny squinted, retrieving it. "'Somebody ought to say goodbye.' Then he wandered off toward the harbor side. I watched till he went around the corner. You want me worried, Casper? Because I'm a little worried."

"It's Grayhaven, Den. Everybody's saying goodbye to something."

Denny considered this, nodded slowly as though it were profound, and drove home.

At six, Casper signed the log, killed the radio, and stepped out into the fog, which had thickened into something almost solid, the streetlights hanging in it like lanterns on a sunken ship. His own car — a fifteen-year-old sedan the color of wet cardboard — started on the second try, which was a personal best.

He should go straight home. Home was eight minutes north, over the canal, past the laundromat, up the stairs to the apartment where the blue suit hung in its bag like an accusation. Eight minutes, and then sleep, and then the weekend, and then Monday, and then the rest of a life arranged so carefully that he'd never have to think about any of it.

Instead he found himself driving south, along the harbor road, wipers ticking at a fog that wasn't rain, and it wasn't until he saw the glow of Hollis & Crane Funeral Home — warm yellow windows, brass lamps burning late out of some old-fashioned courtesy to the bereaved — that he understood what he'd done.

He pulled over. He didn't mean to. His hands did it the way hands do.

Through the fog and across the wet lawn, he could see the parlor's front room, and in it, the shapes: folding chairs in rows, a lectern, flowers going soft at the edges, and at the far end, on the stand, the box. From the car he couldn't see inside the box, obviously, but he knew what was in it, or believed he did, and the believing was the thing that made his chest do what it was doing. Forty-one years old. Found at the ferry slip. Water in his lungs. *Accidental.*

There was a light on in the side office, and a figure moving behind frosted glass — old Hollis himself, probably, or his daughter, closing out the day's ledger of departures. Everyone else had gone home hours ago. The viewing had ended at eight. Which meant the room Casper was looking at was empty except for Daniel, and had been empty since eight o'clock, and would be empty until the crematorium van came in the morning, and Casper had skipped the one appointment in his life that mattered, and now he was sitting across the street from his brother's body at one in the morning like a burglar casing a house he used to live in.

*You missed it,* he thought. *You had eleven minutes and a full tank of gas and you missed it.*

Daniel would have forgiven him. That was the unbearable part. Daniel forgave the way the tide came in — completely, without being asked, whether or not anybody stood on the shore to watch. Casper could picture it exactly: Daniel finding out his brother hadn't come, and making the face, the small rueful smile, the one-sided shrug — *that's Casper* — and meaning no cruelty by it, and meaning, underneath, all the cruelty in the world.

*I miss your dumb voice.*

"Damn it, Danny," Casper said aloud, to the fog, to the windshield, to nobody. His breath fogged the glass and erased the funeral home and gave it back.

He sat there for a long time. Long enough that the figure behind the frosted glass left and the building went dark except for the brass lamps, which apparently burned all night, little flames for whoever came grieving at the wrong hours. Long enough that his engine ticked cool and the fog began to bead and run down the glass like the windows were crying on his behalf, which he appreciated, since he seemed to be out of network.

He thought about going in. He actually opened the door. Cold air came in off the harbor and with it the smell — salt, diesel, creosote, the smell of Grayhaven, the smell of every summer of their childhood, the smell of the ferry slip where they used to fish for nothing with hand lines and catch it. Daniel, ten years old, holding up a sculpin, delighted: *Look, Cas, it's so ugly it's beautiful.* Daniel at thirty-five, at their mother's funeral, gripping Casper's arm in the same parlor across the street: *We've still got each other, right? Say it, you idiot.* And Casper had said it, and meant it, and then let four years happen anyway, one unanswered text at a time, because pride is patient and grief is lazy and there is never a day so ordinary it can't be the last one.

He closed the door. He drove home.

That was the guilt he carried up the stairs past the dark laundromat, past the sleeping dryers: not that he'd skipped a service. Services were for other people. The guilt was smaller and sharper than that — it was that he'd sat across the street and couldn't make himself walk eleven minutes' worth of lawn to be in a room with his brother, because some cowardly accountant in his chest kept insisting the debt was already settled, that four years of silence had priced him out of the mourning, that he had no standing. No standing. As if love were a court and he'd failed to appear.

He hung his keys on the hook. He did not hang up the blue suit; taking it out of the bag felt suddenly obscene, a costume for a role forever vacated.

The mail was stacked on the counter where the carrier pushed it through the slot — flyers, a water bill, a postcard from a roofing company. And beneath it all, white envelope, typed address, no return:

**CASPER STERLING.**

No stamp art. No handwriting. Just his name in courier type, and a postmark he'd look at twice, later, when everything had changed: dated the day after they'd put his brother in the ground.

He stood in his kitchen at 7:40 in the morning, fog still on the windows, and opened it.
