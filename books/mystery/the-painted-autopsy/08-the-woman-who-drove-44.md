## 8. The Woman Who Drove 44

Marisol Vega did not want to be found, which in Grayhaven meant she had done a competent job of it. The city was small enough that everyone knew where everyone lived, and large enough — in the way of all port towns, which grow sideways and inward rather than up — that nobody knew where anybody actually *was*.

It took Casper three days and the better part of two paychecks' worth of coffee to find her.

He started where any dispatcher would: with the old rosters. Grayhaven Taxi & Livery kept paper records going back to 1979, because Old Man Ferro, who'd founded the company, believed computers were "a fad with a plug," and his son Ricky, who ran it now, believed his father had been right about everything except the cigarettes. The rosters lived in cardboard boxes in the back office, stacked like sedimentary rock, each layer a year of the city's nights. Casper spent his lunch breaks kneeling on the cold tile, blowing dust off pages, sneezing in a way that made Ricky holler from the front desk that he'd better not be dying back there because they couldn't afford to replace him.

*Vega, M. Driver 44. Hired March 1996.*

Driver 44. He remembered her, vaguely, or remembered the voice — a woman's voice on the night channel, low and unhurried, the kind of dispatcher's voice that could talk a drunk out of throwing up in the back seat without ever raising her volume. She'd left before Casper came on full-time. He'd been maybe nineteen, riding along with Daniel some weekends when their mother still hoped proximity might fix whatever was broken between the brothers. He remembered Daniel saying once, *That Vega woman, she sees everything. She'll be running this place inside five years.*

She hadn't. Inside five years she was gone entirely, and no one at the company would say where.

Ricky claimed not to remember her. This was a lie so lazy it was almost charming — Ricky remembered every driver who'd ever worked for him, mostly by what they'd broken. When Casper pressed, Ricky polished the counter with a rag that had never once cleaned anything and said, "She quit. People quit, Casper. It's a whole thing people do."

"Nobody quits dispatch," Casper said. "You don't quit dispatch. Dispatch quits you."

Ricky stopped polishing. For a moment something moved behind his face — not fear exactly, but the cousin of it, the relative you see at funerals. "Look," he said quietly. "My dad got a call about her, back then. From somebody downtown. Asked why we were asking questions about a run she took. My dad decided the healthy response was to lose her file." He set down the rag. "I'm losing it again right now, as far as I know. You understand me?"

Casper understood him. It was the first time anyone had confirmed, out loud, that the questions themselves were dangerous — that somewhere, someone listened for them the way Casper listened to his radio, patient, professional, always on.

* * *

Finding her took a fisherman, a church bulletin, and a lucky guess about bingo.

The fisherman was Tully Okafor, who drove nights for the company twenty years ago and now sold bait and gossip off a cooler at the north pier, both items fresher than anything at the supermarket. Tully remembered Marisol. Everybody remembered Marisol. "Best dispatcher this town ever heard," he said, scaling a mackerel with the absent violence of long practice. "Left sudden. Left *wrong*, if you follow me. One day she's on the board, next day her locker's cleaned out and there's a cake nobody eats."

"Where'd she go?"

Tully shrugged with his whole body. "People say downstate. People say she married a dentist. People say a lot of things." He looked at Casper over the mackerel. "You're Daniel's brother."

"I'm Daniel's brother."

"Daniel asked me the same question, eight months back." Tully dropped the fish into a bag like a verdict. "I gave him the same answer I'm giving you. St. Brigid's. Thursday nights. If she still goes, she goes there, and if she goes there, she wins at bingo, because that woman does not lose at bingo."

St. Brigid's was a squat brick church in the Saltmarsh district, its steeple leaning slightly eastward as if craning to watch the harbor it no longer trusted. The Thursday bingo crowd was elderly, ruthless, and armed with daubers in colors that suggested a crime scene. Casper stood in the back hall among folding tables and the smell of burnt coffee, feeling conspicuous in a way he hadn't since high school, scanning faces.

He found her at the prize table. She was perhaps sixty now, silver threaded through dark hair pulled back tight, wearing a cardigan the color of a school bus. She was counting out cash prizes with the crisp authority of a bank teller and correcting the priest's arithmetic without looking up from the ledger. When she finished, she caught Casper watching her, and her eyes did something he recognized from his own mirror: the quick, tired assessment of a person deciding whether a stranger is trouble.

"You're not here for bingo," she said. Not a question.

"No, ma'am."

"Then either you're selling something, grieving something, or asking about 1998." She untied her cardigan apron with deliberate calm. "And you've got the face of a man doing all three. Coffee's terrible but it's free. Walk with me."

* * *

They sat in the church basement kitchen at a table scarred by forty years of potlucks. Marisol poured two cups of coffee that tasted, as promised, like punishment, and wrapped her hands around hers without drinking.

"Casper Sterling," she said. "Dispatch, night shift. You took over my old chair, more or less. You run a clean board."

"You know my logs?"

"Honey, I taught half this town how to keep logs. Of course I know your logs. Cleanest board in three counties." She finally sipped, grimaced, continued anyway. "Which is why I'll talk to you and not to a reporter, and why I didn't talk to your brother, God rest him, though I've regretted that every day since he went in the water."

Casper's chest tightened. "You talked to Daniel?"

"He called me. Eight months ago. Wanted to know about a run on October eleventh, 1998 — eleven-forty p.m., pickup at Kestrel Paint Works, no billing, signed out under a driver ID that wasn't in the book." She recited it flatly, from memory, and Casper felt the hair rise on his arms. Twenty-five years, and she carried the number like a stone in her pocket. "I told him what I told no one else. Then I told him to stop digging, and he laughed at me. He had his father's laugh. You have it too, a little, when you think something's funny and hate yourself for it."

"What happened that night?" Casper asked. "The run."

Marisol turned her cup slowly on the table, aligning the handle with the table edge, then with herself, then with nothing.

"The call came in at eleven-thirty-something. No name. A man's voice, very calm, very used to being obeyed. Said there'd been an accident at the paint works, somebody hurt, needed a car to the harbor fast and quiet — no meter, no log entry. That happens sometimes, in this business. Owners call. You take care of owners." Her jaw tightened. "But then the driver comes back — this kid, Danny Przekop, sweet boy, dead of lung cancer now, so I can say it — and he's white as the plates. Says he drove a passenger from the loading dock to the ferry landing. Man in his thirties, well-dressed, sitting very straight, hands folded like he was at church. And in the back seat with him, wrapped in a tarp, something heavy. Something that smelled like solvent and salt water."

The kitchen hummed around them — refrigerator, radiator, the distant squeak of bingo chairs upstairs. Casper realized he was holding his breath and made himself breathe.

"The passenger paid in cash. Triple fare. Told Danny, 'You drove tonight for Mr. Kestrel, and Mr. Kestrel thanks you.' Then he walked down the ferry ramp and was gone." She looked up. "Wendell Kestrel. The heir. The golden boy. Two weeks later the whole city's crying about his boating accident — poor Wendell, lost at sea, such a tragedy, the family holds the biggest funeral Grayhaven ever saw, closed casket, gorgeous flowers."

"And you knew it was him in that cab."

"I knew the man in the cab was alive, breathing, and carrying something dead." She said it simply, without drama, which made it worse. "I logged the run anyway. Quietly. In my own book, not the company's. Because I was raised that you write down what actually happened, even if nobody reads it for twenty-five years. Somebody has to hold the true version or the false one wins by default." She smiled, thin and tired. "Your brother understood that. It's why he died, I think. He held the true version too hard."

"Mr. Kestrel," Casper said. "The passenger said it himself. But Kestrel's dead."

Marisol set down her cup. "Is he? They never found a body, Casper. You know that? Twenty-five years, no body, no wreckage worth the name. The family buried a coffin full of good intentions and everybody agreed to stop looking, because looking made people uncomfortable, and uncomfortable people ask about solvent drums and dockworkers who fall off piers." She leaned forward. "The Kestrel Paint Works shut down in '99. Officially. But I drive past it sometimes — I live over in Saltmarsh, I take the long way, old habit — and there are lights in that building. Low ones. Amber, near the ground floor. And smoke from a chimney that's supposed to be cold. Somebody lives in there. Somebody who keeps the furnaces warm."

"The one who keeps the paint," Casper said quietly.

Marisol went still. "Where did you hear that?"

"A letter. Anonymous. It confessed to killing my brother and named three men — Vey, Roark, Sallow. And a fourth it wouldn't name. Just called him that."

For a long moment Marisol studied him, and Casper watched her decide something — watched the decision move through her like weather. Then she reached into her cardigan pocket and produced a small notebook, cloth-covered, soft-cornered, ancient. She didn't hand it over. She just laid it on the table between them, her hand resting on top.

"My own book. Every odd run I ever logged, every owner's favor, every quiet ride, October '96 through June '99. Names, times, plate numbers I memorized off cars idling at the loading dock." Her voice wavered, just once. "I kept it in case somebody someday asked the right question. Your brother asked the right question and I got scared, and I sent him away, and he went in the water. So." She pushed it across the table an inch. "I'm not getting scared twice. But you listen to me, Casper Sterling."

"Yes, ma'am."

"Wendell Kestrel is not a ghost and he is not a victim. Whatever's in that factory, whatever he's been keeping warm all these years — the men in that letter are afraid of him, and men like Aldous Vey are only afraid of two things in this world: the tide, and witnesses." She stood, buttoning her cardigan with steady hands. "The city will tell you he drowned. The city tells itself stories it can live with — that's how a place survives, same as a person. Belonging here means knowing which stories we've all agreed to forget." She picked up the bingo cash box. "You want to belong to Grayhaven? Start remembering."

At the door she paused, back to him.

"One more thing. The amber lights — they come on around ten most nights. But last week, the night after your brother's funeral—" she turned, and her eyes were wet and furious, "—they burned until dawn."

* * *

Casper walked out of St. Brigid's into a night that smelled of rain coming off the water, the notebook heavy in his jacket pocket against his ribs like a second heartbeat. Up the hill, the city glowed its ordinary glow — taverns, porch lights, the hospital's green cross. And far off, past the shipyards, dark against the darker sky, the old Kestrel Paint Works crouched over the harbor with its broken windows and its cold chimneys.

Except, he saw now, squinting through the drizzle, they weren't all cold.

One chimney breathed a thin gray ribbon into the night, and low on the ground floor, faint but unmistakable, an amber light flickered on — patient as a pilot flame, burning for someone who had been dead for twenty-five years and was evidently keeping late hours about it.

Casper stood in the rain a long moment, the notebook against his heart, and made a decision he would spend the rest of this story surviving.

Tomorrow, he would go and knock on a dead man's door.

But tonight, walking back to his car, he found a fresh key-scratch down the driver's side door, deep and deliberate — and tucked under the wiper, a note in block letters:

**SHE KEPT A BOOK TOO. NOW YOU BOTH DID. WE KNOW WHERE SHE BINGOS.**

Casper looked up the hill toward St. Brigid's, where the lights were going out one by one, and understood that finding Marisol had not widened the map of the conspiracy.

It had put a target on the only ally he had.
