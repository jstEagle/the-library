## 11. The Letter Writer Speaks

The dead-drop instructions arrived on a Tuesday, folded inside a hymnal that had been left — by whom, God only knew — on the seat of cab 12, the oldest car in the Grayhaven Taxi & Livery fleet, the one nobody drove because the heater howled like a man being reasonable about it.

Casper found the hymnal at the start of his shift, opened it to the ribbon marker, and read the single sheet tucked there twice before his hands stopped shaking enough to read it a third time.

*St. Brendan's. Thursday. 9:40 p.m. Confession is heard until ten. Come alone. Come without your phone. Come, and I will tell you what the water wouldn't.*

No signature. No threat. Just the faint chemical smell of something — solvent, maybe, or the memory of it — clinging to the paper like a ghost clinging to a coat it used to wear.

He told no one. Not Brandt, who had earned the truth about the fire at the records annex and had given him, in return, a look that said *I believe you and I can't help you, which is the most honest thing anyone's said to me in years.* Not Marisol, who had already given him more than the city ever had. He sat with it the way he sat with everything now: alone, in the amber light of the dispatch office, listening to the radio crackle with the small, ordinary emergencies of a small, ordinary city.

"Dispatch, this is 44, I've got a fare at the Anchor wanting a ride to the casino, over."

"Copy, 44. Tell him the casino takes his money either way."

It was the kind of joke that kept a man sane at three in the morning. Casper made it and felt nothing, because his whole self was already in St. Brendan's, sitting in a wooden box, waiting for a voice.

* * *

Thursday came gray and salt-winded. Casper arrived at the church at 9:31, left his phone in the cab under the floor mat like a man hiding evidence from himself, and stood on the steps of St. Brendan's feeling like the world's least qualified penitent.

He hadn't been inside a church since Daniel's funeral — the one he'd skipped. He'd stood across the street instead, under the awning of the florist's, watching the mourners file in, counting them out of spite and then, when the count climbed past sixty, out of something closer to awe. Sixty people had come to bury a man Casper hadn't spoken to in four years. It had made him feel small in a way he hadn't known how to name.

Now he knew the name for it. It was *too late.*

Inside, the church was dim and smelled of candle wax and old stone and, faintly, of the harbor, because everything in Grayhaven eventually smelled of the harbor. A single votive rack burned near the side altar, and a cleaning woman — or perhaps an angel with a mop — worked her way up the far aisle with the unhurried rhythm of someone who had outlasted every priest in the building.

The confessional stood against the north wall, an old dark-wood booth with a carved lintel Casper couldn't read in the gloom. Two doors. Two curtains. The kind of furniture built for secrets, back when secrets had better manners.

He took the penitent's side. The kneeler creaked under him. Through the lattice screen between the two halves of the booth, he could see nothing — just the suggestion of a shape, a stillness, a presence that breathed.

Then the shape spoke.

"You came alone." The voice was old and dry and careful, like paper being unfolded in a quiet room. "Thank you. I wasn't certain you would. I wasn't certain I would."

"You wrote the letter." Casper kept his voice low, matching the room. "The first one. The one that said you killed my brother."

A pause. Then, softly, with the terrible precision of a man who had spent a lifetime choosing his words and had finally stopped: "I wrote that I killed him. I did not write that I drowned him. There is a distinction, Mr. Sterling, and I have had twenty-five years to become an expert in distinctions, and I am here tonight to tell you that they are all lies. Every distinction. Every careful phrase. They are the bricks I built my house out of, and the house is coming down, and I would like, before it does, to say one sentence without a distinction in it."

"Say it, then."

The shape in the other booth shifted. Casper heard the small sounds of an old man arranging himself — joints, fabric, breath.

"I falsified the autopsy of Tomas Reyes."

The name dropped into the booth like a stone into a well. Casper had read it in Daniel's notebook, had traced it through harbor records and payroll ledgers, had said it aloud to Marisol in a diner booth at two in the morning. But hearing it in a confessional, from the mouth of the man who had signed the death certificate — that was different. That was the difference between reading about a wound and watching it being made.

"Tell me," Casper said. "All of it. Slowly."

* * *

"He was twenty-six," Sallow said. "Tomas Reyes. Twenty-six years old, a dockworker, a man who sent money home to his mother in Ondarria every month and bought his boots one size too large because he'd heard they lasted longer that way. He was not supposed to be on the night pier. He was not supposed to see the drums going into the water. But the tide was wrong that night, and the schedule moved, and he was there, and four young men with a scheme and a boatload of industrial solvent had a decision to make."

The lattice screen threw thin bars of shadow across Casper's hands. He gripped the kneeler's edge.

"They killed him." Not a question. He needed to hear it said anyway. Needed it in the air, in the church, under the eyes of whatever saints kept watch over Grayhaven, a city that had agreed long ago to look at the water and see only water.

"They killed him," Sallow said. "Aldous Vey decided it before anyone else had finished being afraid. That was always his gift — deciding while other men were still trembling. Emil Roark held the flashlight. The fourth man —" a breath, ragged, "— the fourth man did what Aldous told him, because he had loved Aldous since they were boys, and love, Mr. Sterling, is the most exploitable substance on earth. And I —"

The voice stopped. Casper waited. Somewhere in the church, the cleaning woman's mop knocked gently against a pew.

"I was the medical examiner on call. Twenty-nine years old. I had a wife and a mortgage and a career that existed entirely at the pleasure of men like Aldous Vey, and I looked at a dead boy on a steel table and I wrote *accidental drowning, consistent with marine mishap,* and I signed my name, and my hand did not shake. That is the part I have never been able to forgive. Not the lie. The steadiness."

Casper thought of the letter in his jacket pocket, the one he'd carried for weeks now like a coal he couldn't put down. *I killed your brother.* Typed. Steady.

"Daniel," he said.

"Daniel." Sallow said the name the way a man says the name of a place he'd promised himself he'd never return to. "Twenty-five years later. He came to my office with a photocopy of the Reyes file and a face like your face, Mr. Sterling, and he asked me one question: *Was it the water?* And I looked at him and I saw Tomas Reyes, and I saw myself at twenty-nine, and I said, *Yes. It was the water.* And he thanked me. He *thanked* me. He was polite about it. And he left, and three days later he was in the harbor, and I was asked — politely, the way everything in this city is done politely — to confirm the findings."

"You signed it again."

"I signed it again." The old voice cracked on the words, a dry branch giving way. "And that night I sat at my desk and I typed a letter to you, because you were the only person in Grayhaven who might not be bought, might not be frightened, might not be polite. Because I am a coward, Mr. Sterling, and cowards delegate. I sent you to do what I could not."

The confession booth had never held a confession like this, Casper thought. Or maybe it had. Maybe this was the only kind it had ever held — the kind that arrived too late, the kind that traded truth for nothing, because the truth was all the penitent had left in his pockets.

"Why the letters?" Casper asked. "Why now, after twenty-five years? Why me?"

"Because I am dying." Sallow said it without drama, the way a man reports the weather. "The doctor tells me months. And I have spent my life signing documents that said the dead died honestly, and I will not let the last document under my name be a lie. I sent the letters to force it into the open. I sent them to *you* because Daniel trusted you, even after four years of silence. He told me that. The night he came to my office. He said, *My brother sees everything. He's a dispatcher — he knows where everybody in this city is at all hours. He just doesn't know he's looking.*"

Casper's throat closed. He had spent four years believing Daniel had written him off — the resentment had been mutual, tidy, well-maintained, like a fence between properties. And all the while Daniel had been telling strangers that his brother saw everything.

"He also said," Sallow went on, gentler now, "that you never called him on his birthday, but that you always knew when it was. He found that funny. He said it was the most Sterling thing about you — keeping track without touching."

"Stop," Casper said, and the word came out rougher than he intended. The shape in the other booth went still, and let him have his silence, and after a while the silence turned from unbearable to merely heavy, which was as much as anyone could ask of it.

* * *

"There's something else," Sallow said at last. "I didn't come here only to unburden myself. Unburdening is for the priest and the grave. I came to arm you."

A soft clink in the dark. Something passed through the gap beneath the lattice screen — a small object, wrapped in a scrap of cloth. Casper picked it up. Heavy for its size. Cold.

He unwrapped it. A key. Old brass, worn smooth, with a paper tag tied to it in careful, spidery handwriting: *Kestrel Works — Locker 9.*

"There's a locker room in the old paint works, ground floor, east wing, behind the loading office," Sallow said. "The lockers were never emptied when the plant closed. Number nine was mine, once — the men kept their street clothes there, their private things. What's in it now is twenty-five years of the truth. Photographs. Ledgers. The documents Aldous thought were destroyed in the fire." A pause. "The first fire. There has since been a second, at the annex, which I assume you know about, since you seem to be the only man in Grayhaven who notices anything."

"The fourth man," Casper said. "The one who keeps the paint. Marisol Vega told me the name. Wendell Kestrel. She says he's dead."

"Wendell Kestrel is the most alive dead man in Grayhaven," Sallow said, and there was something in his voice now that might have been pity, or fear, or the strange tenderness the guilty feel toward the secrets they've kept longest. "The fire in '98 — the one that closed the plant — it was real. It was meant for him. Aldous needed the works to burn for the insurance, and Wendell was supposed to be inside it, and the solvent did what solvent does. But he did not die, Mr. Sterling. He crawled out of that fire the way a man crawls out of his own life, and he went into the walls of the place, and he has been there ever since. Keeping the paint. Keeping everything. He is the reason the ledgers survived. He is the reason *anything* survived. And he is — I say this with love, because I knew him when his face was whole — he is not a forgiving man."

Casper turned the key over in his palm. It felt less like metal than like a verdict.

"Why give it to me and not the police?"

"Because I gave twenty-five years to the police, and look what they grew." The old voice was tired now, running down like a clock. "Because Detective Brandt is a good woman inside a bad machine, and the machine grinds good women into paperwork. Because the courts in this city are Aldous Vey's second home. No, Mr. Sterling. The law will come, if it comes at all, only after the truth is already loose — the way the tide comes after the moon has already decided. You are going to be the moon."

"That's a lot to put on a taxi dispatcher."

"You underestimate yourself. Daniel didn't." A dry sound that might have been a laugh. "And neither do I. I have watched you for weeks. You pull logbooks at four in the morning. You find women who vanished from the industry. You sit outside funeral parlors counting mourners. You are your brother's brother, whether you ever admitted it to him or not."

Casper looked down at the key. "The sluice gates," he said. "The undercroft. Daniel's body was moved through it, wasn't it. The accident scene was staged above."

The silence from the other booth went on long enough that Casper thought the old man might have fainted, or fled, or simply dissolved, confession complete, into the candle smoke.

"You've seen the tide tables," Sallow said finally. "Then you know more than the police ever did, and you should know this too: the water in that undercroft does not keep the schedule printed on the chart. Not anymore. Not since the gates were left the way he left them. If you go in there, go in at the lowest tide of the month, and go in knowing that the man who keeps the paint knows every pipe and every passage, and that his feelings about the Vey family are —" the pause stretched, "— *complicated.*"

"Is that a warning or an invitation?"

"It is a confession," Sallow said. "Everything I have is a confession, now. That's all I have left in the inventory." The booth creaked; the old man was rising. "One more thing, Mr. Sterling, and then you should go, because the cleaning woman finishes the north aisle at ten, and she has seen enough strange men in this church to last her."

"What is it?"

"Your brother's last words to me, as he left my office. I have carried them three months, and I find I cannot carry them into the ground." The voice dropped to almost nothing. "He said, *Tell Casper the water's fine.* He was smiling when he said it. He knew I'd lie. He knew, and he went anyway, and he left a joke for you, because that's what brothers do — they leave jokes where the apologies should go."

Casper sat very still. Somewhere behind his ribs, something that had been clenched for weeks — for years, if he was honest, for four years of birthday silence — let go by a single degree. Not forgiveness. Not yet. But the first loosening. The knot acknowledging that it was, in fact, a knot.

"Tell him thank you," Casper said. "When you see him. Whenever that is. Tell him the water wasn't fine, but I'm going to find out what was."

* * *

The other door opened. Footsteps, slow and deliberate, moved up the north aisle — an old man in a dark coat, hat pulled low, walking with the unhurried dignity of a man who had just put down the heaviest thing he owned. He did not look back. At the font he stopped, dipped two fingers, and made a sign of the cross so automatic and so worn that it looked less like devotion than like an old scar.

Then he was gone into the fog off the harbor, and Casper was alone in the church with a brass key, a paper tag, and the words *the water's fine* ringing in him like a struck bell.

The cleaning woman appeared at the end of the aisle, mop in hand, and regarded him with the unimpressed calm of someone who had heard everything and believed roughly half of it.

"Booth's out of order till Father Mateo oils the hinge," she said. "You were never here."

"No, ma'am," Casper agreed, and meant it in every direction.

Outside, the fog had come in off the water thick as wool. The streetlamps of Grayhaven glowed in it like the last embers of something. Casper walked to his cab, lifted the floor mat, and looked at his phone sitting there dark and obedient, full of calls he hadn't made and warnings he hadn't given.

Locker 9. The lowest tide of the month.

He checked the tide chart folded in his glovebox — he'd been carrying it for a week now, the way other men carry photographs — and felt his stomach drop and settle, the way it did on the bridge when the wind came hard off the bay.

The lowest tide of the month was tomorrow night.

And the fog rolling in off the harbor was the kind of fog, the old dispatchers used to say, that the water sends ahead when it wants company.
