## 14. The Tide Table

The tide table for Grayhaven Harbor was pinned to the corkboard above the dispatch desk, right between a faded photo of the 2003 company softball team and a takeout menu for a Chinese restaurant that had burned down in 2011. Nobody had updated it since March. Nobody ever did. The tides didn't care about our schedules, so we'd stopped pretending they should.

I'd walked past that piece of paper four thousand times. Maybe more. I'd used it exactly once, years ago, when a fare wanted to know if he could launch his kayak off Pier Six without looking like an idiot stuck in the mud.

At two in the morning on a Tuesday, with the radio quiet and the coffee gone bitter in the pot, I took it down and spread it flat on the desk like it was a map to buried treasure.

Because that's what it was. I just hadn't known what was buried.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't make you stupid, but it makes you obsessive, and obsession looks a lot like stupidity from the outside. For three days after finding Daniel's notebook — three days of reading *If I go in the water, it wasn't the water* until the words wore grooves in me — I had been doing what I do best, which is nothing dramatic at all. I'd been keeping records.

Dispatchers keep records the way monks kept psalms. Every run, every address, every odd request logged in a hand that gets neater the stranger the job. And Daniel, God rest him, had been a dispatcher too, before he quit and went strange. He'd learned the same trade. He thought in logs.

So I'd been re-reading his notebook not as a diary but as a dispatch log, and somewhere around page forty, a pattern surfaced that I'd been too raw to see before.

Daniel had visited the Kestrel Paint Works eleven times in the last six months of his life. Eleven visits, each noted with a time. And the times weren't random. They clustered. Nine of the eleven fell within an hour of low tide.

I sat with that for a long time while the coffee went from bitter to geological.

A man investigating a cover-up doesn't visit a site at low tide because he likes the view. He visits at low tide because that's the only time he can get where he needs to go. Which meant there was a place beneath the paint works that spent most of its day underwater.

I pulled up the building plans next — or what passed for them. The city archives had a set of survey drawings from 1974, scanned by some clerk who clearly hated his job, because half the pages were upside down. But one sheet showed the foundation level, and there it was, hatched in blue ink like a wound: a long vaulted chamber running the length of the factory's river side. Labeled, in a draftsman's careful hand, *UNDERCROFT — TIDE-VENTED — NO STORAGE OF SOLVENTS (SEE ORD. 88-C)*.

An ordinance against storing solvents there. Which, in my experience, is the municipal equivalent of a sign saying *please store solvents here.*

* * *

"You look like a man doing math," Marisol said when I called her that afternoon. She'd answered on the second ring, which told me she'd been waiting as much as I had. "Bad math or good math?"

"Math that adds up to my brother being somewhere he shouldn't have been."

"Those are usually the interesting sums." A pause, the sound of a cigarette being lit on the other end. "Tell me."

"Daniel went into the paint works at low tide. Repeatedly. There's an undercroft under the building that floods twice a day. It's been illegal to store solvents down there since before any of this started, which means it's exactly where you'd store solvents." I heard my own voice go flat and procedural, the dispatcher's trick for keeping your hands steady. "Marisol, the coroner's report says Daniel drowned in the river off Pier Four. Shallow water. But his lungs — Sallow's report said saltwater drowning, brackish, consistent with the harbor."

"And?"

"And the river at Pier Four isn't brackish. It's upstream of the mixing zone. I checked. I called the state environmental office and asked about salinity readings like a concerned citizen, and the nice man on the phone read me numbers for twenty minutes." I'd also asked him about the weather and his mother's hip, because that's how you get a government employee to like you. "Brackish water means tidal mixing. It means the water came from downriver. From the industrial reach. From right below the paint works."

Silence on the line. Then, quietly: "They moved him."

"They moved him. Through the undercroft, out at high tide, or in at high tide and out through the pier — either way, the scene at Pier Four was staged. The body was placed. And the only way to prove it is to walk where he walked." I looked at the tide table. "Low tide tomorrow is 4:17 in the morning. The window is maybe ninety minutes before the water starts coming back."

"Casper." Her voice changed, lost its wryness. "You're describing walking into the place where three men killed your brother, alone, in the dark, on a schedule."

"I'm describing it very calmly, aren't I? That's the training."

"It's not funny."

"It's a little funny. Dark-funny. Funny like a funeral." I rubbed my eyes. "Look. If I go to Brandt, she has to open a case, and the moment she opens a case, Vey knows, and whatever's down there gets cleaned out or sealed up behind lawyers until we're all dead. If I go myself, I come back with something she can't unsee. Photographs. Physical evidence. Something with a serial number on it."

"And if someone's waiting for you?"

"Then I'll be polite. I'm very good with difficult fares."

She laughed despite herself, a short unhappy sound. "That's what I'm afraid of. You joke exactly like Daniel did."

That landed harder than any warning could have. I let it sit between us like a third person on the call.

"Marisol," I said finally. "He wrote *finish it.* I'm not going to finish it by filing a complaint. I'm going to finish it by going down there and seeing what they hid, with my own eyes, before the tide takes its turn again."

Another drag of the cigarette. "Then take the tide table seriously," she said. "Not the city's. Take the real one. The harbor master's office posts corrected tables — Roark's office. The city printouts run ten, fifteen minutes off sometimes. Down there, fifteen minutes is the difference between a door and a wall of water."

"Emil Roark's office," I said. "You're suggesting I get my tide data from one of the men who might drown me with it."

"I'm suggesting the harbor doesn't lie even when the harbormaster does. Water keeps better books than people do." She stubbed out the cigarette audibly. "Check the posted table against the NOAA feed online. Cross-reference. Trust the water, not the man. And Casper — tell someone where you're going. Not the police. Someone. Me. Write it down, leave it somewhere. If you don't come out, I want to know where to point the divers."

"I will."

"I mean it. I buried enough of that year already. I'm not burying you too."

We hung up, and I sat in the quiet dispatch office with the tide table glowing under the desk lamp, and I did the thing she said. I wrote it all down — the address, the time, the plan — folded it into an envelope marked *MARISOL VEGA*, and left it with Nadia at the day desk, who accepted it without questions because Nadia had worked nights in Grayhaven long enough to know that questions were a luxury item.

* * *

I didn't sleep much. That's not a confession; it's a logistics report. I lay in bed and ran the plan like a route sheet, over and over, the way I'd rehearse a complicated pickup: enter the yard from the drainage cut on the north fence — Daniel's notebook had sketched it, a gap where the chain-link had rusted through, and I trusted Daniel's maps the way sailors trust lighthouses built by dead men. Down the loading ramp. Into the undercroft before the water bottomed out. Ninety minutes. Flashlight, spare flashlight, phone charged and recording, Daniel's notebook in a ziplock bag inside my jacket. Work gloves, because twenty-five-year-old solvents leave things behind you don't want on your skin.

And I thought about Daniel the whole time, which was the point, though I wouldn't have admitted it.

We'd fought, near the end. Stupid fights, dispatcher fights — he thought I'd gotten small, content with the booth and the radio and the same forty streets; I thought he'd gotten grand, chasing ghosts through other people's ledgers instead of living his own life. The last thing I said to him, three weeks before he went in the water, was *"not everything is a conspiracy, Danny. Sometimes a drunk guy falls off a pier because he's drunk."*

Sometimes a drunk guy does fall off a pier because he's drunk. And sometimes a man is carried to a pier after drowning in a flooded room beneath a paint factory, because he found a ledger that proved three respectable men let a dockworker die in 1998 and paid to keep it quiet ever since.

I got up at three and made coffee and checked the cross-referenced tide data one more time, because checking things is how I pray.

NOAA said low water at 4:09. The harbor office posting — photographed from across the street, because I wasn't ready to shake Emil Roark's hand yet, or rather, I wasn't sure what my face would do — said 4:22. I split the difference and gave myself a margin. Enter at 3:45. Out by 5:30, no exceptions, no matter what I found. The water would not negotiate. Water never reads the room.

* * *

Grayhaven at four in the morning is the city with its makeup off. The streetlights buzz. The gulls are silent for once, which somehow makes everything lonelier, like the birds know something. I drove my own car partway, then walked the last half mile along the rail spur, because a taxi idling outside a condemned factory is a confession in itself, and I'd had enough confessions delivered to me lately to know how they felt.

The Kestrel Paint Works rose up against the gray sky like a cathedral that had lost its congregation. Windows boarded. The great painted sign on the river wall — KESTREL QUALITY COATINGS SINCE 1921 — flaked down to bare metal in long streaks, so that from a distance it looked like the building itself was molting, shedding its name.

The north fence gap was exactly where Daniel's pencil had said. Rust flakes came away on my gloves as I slipped through, and I stood a moment in the weeds of the loading yard, listening.

Water. Somewhere below, water moving through stone, patient as bookkeeping.

The loading ramp descended into darkness on the river side, a wide concrete throat with a heavy door standing open at the bottom — open, I noticed, not forced, its hinges oiled. Somebody maintained this door. Somebody came and went.

My flashlight found the tide marks on the ramp wall first. Bands of dark stain, layered like tree rings, climbing eight feet up the concrete. High water line. Below them, the concrete glistened, still wet, still breathing harbor.

I checked my watch. 3:52. Ninety-eight minutes, give or take, before the sea started collecting its rent.

I stood at the top of that wet ramp with the cold air rising out of the dark below — air that smelled of salt and solvent and old stone — and I understood, with a clarity that felt almost gentle, that I wasn't afraid. Or rather, I was afraid the way you're afraid on a first date: aware that everything mattered, unwilling to let it stop you.

"Danny," I said quietly, to nobody, to the water, to the whole rotten beautiful city. "I'm coming down. Try to have kept your receipts."

I started down the ramp, and the dark closed over me like a held breath —

— and at the bottom, in the beam of my flashlight, glinting on the wet concrete just inside the door, was a fresh set of footprints, leading in.

Someone was already here.
