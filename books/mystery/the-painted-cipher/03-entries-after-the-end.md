## 3. Entries After the End

The second package arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day the universe had apparently chosen for delivering things that ruined Orsen Okonkwo's sleep.

The first package—the seller's water-stained volume—had come on a Thursday, and he'd spent the weekend with it the way a man spends a weekend with a letter from a dead friend. The second came in the same plain brown wrapping, same careful tape, same absence of a return address. It was sitting in his building's lobby when he came down at seven a.m., and Mrs. Adeyemi, who held court by the mailboxes with the authority of a woman who had lived in the building since the earth cooled, watched him pick it up with open interest.

"Another one of your mystery boxes," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Research materials," Orsen said.

"For that radio show where you talk about dead people."

"Podcast. And I talk about *how* they died, Mrs. Adeyemi. There's a difference."

"There is not," she said comfortably, "and you know it." She handed him a small envelope that had come for him, then added, as he turned to go: "Whoever is sending you boxes, they know where you sleep. Think about that one before you drink your coffee."

Orsen thought about it. He thought about it all the way up the stairs, and then, because he was who he was, he opened the package anyway.

Inside, wrapped in acid-free tissue paper with the fussiness of someone who understood archival practice, was a second diary. Smaller. Thicker. The cover was a deep blue cloth, unwatermarked, unburned, un-drowned. It looked like it had been cared for. The first diary had looked like it had survived something; this one looked like it had been *kept*.

He turned to the first page and his hands went still.

The date was March 4, 2001.

Ada Nwosu had disappeared on June 17, 1997. Her body—what there was of it—had been recovered from the lagoon eleven days later, formally identified by dental records that Orsen had once spent three months trying and failing to obtain. Her mother had wept at a closed casket. The city had moved on with the efficiency of water finding its level.

And here, in handwriting that made something in Orsen's chest go tight and cold, was an entry dated four years after the end.

*The okra at the market today was disgraceful. I told the woman so and she told me the disgrace was mine for arriving at noon like a tourist. She's right. I bought it anyway. Some arguments you lose on purpose because the winning would cost you more than the okra is worth.*

Orsen set the diary down on his kitchen table, walked to the window, and looked out at the street for a long moment. Mrs. Adeyemi's voice in his head: *they know where you sleep.*

Then he made coffee, because he was who he was, and he sat down to read.

* * *

The handwriting was the first problem, and it was the problem he was best equipped to solve.

Orsen had spent two years of his life, early on, doing document verification for a newspaper that paid him badly and taught him well. He knew what a forged hand looked like: the tremor of a forger thinking too hard, the telltale uniformity where a real hand varies, the way a copied signature gets the shape right and the rhythm wrong. Rhythm was the thing you couldn't fake. A hand moves the way a voice speaks, and it carries the same fingerprints of habit.

He spent the morning with the second diary open beside a folder of photocopies he'd begged, borrowed, and in one case mildly stolen from the city archives: Ada Nwosu's personnel file from the Records Department, her handwritten index cards from the 1994 municipal survey, a postcard she'd sent to a colleague from a conference in Jos. He laid them out on the table like a spread of tarot cards and compared.

The *a* was the same—open-topped, slightly drunk on its left side, the same in every sample. The *f* crossed with a long, impatient stroke that sometimes clipped the next letter. The *N* in *Nwosu* leaned forward like it was in a hurry to get somewhere, which, he thought grimly, it turned out to be.

And the rhythm. He read a page of the 2001 diary aloud, quietly, and heard the same voice he'd heard in the 1997 entries—the dry, precise, faintly amused voice of a woman who catalogued the world because the world kept misfiling itself. The okra entry wasn't the kind of thing a forger invented. A forger wrote *evidence*. This was a woman complaining about okra. Nobody forges okra.

He sat back. His coffee had gone cold. The light through the window had moved from one side of the table to the other, and he had read perhaps forty pages without once standing up.

Either this was the most brilliant forgery he had ever encountered—executed by someone who understood not just Ada Nwosu's letterforms but her *jokes*, her private shorthand, her habit of underlining a word twice when she didn't believe it—or Ada Nwosu had never died at all.

*Everyone swears she's dead,* he wrote in his notes, and then crossed it out, because it wasn't true. Everyone *believed* she was dead. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole story.

* * *

He called Tunde at eleven.

"Tell me you're calling about the season finale numbers," Tunde said. "Tell me the finale did well and you're calling to celebrate and nothing else is happening."

"The finale did well."

"Orsen."

"Very well. Top of the charts. Everyone loved it."

"*Orsen.*"

"There's a second diary," Orsen said.

There was a silence on the line long enough that Orsen checked whether the call had dropped. Then Tunde sighed, and Orsen heard him set down whatever he was drinking, which meant he was settling in, which meant he'd already surrendered.

"Of course there is. There's always a second diary. There's a second diary in every one of your cases. You're like a magnet for second diaries."

"This one is dated after she died."

"Everything is dated after she died, Orsen. She died in 1997. The *Bible* is dated after she died."

Orsen read him the okra entry. He heard Tunde's breathing change.

"Okay," Tunde said finally. "Okay. That's—okay. Who sent it?"

"Anonymous. Same as the first one. Same wrapping, same tape. Whoever it is, they're careful."

"Or it's the same person who sold you the first one, and they're reeling you in like a fish, and you are *thrashing*, Orsen, you are thrashing beautifully."

"I'm aware of the possibility."

"You are not. You are never aware of the possibility. That's what I'm for. I'm the awareness department. So let me do my job: someone sends you a diary of a dead woman, then sends you a *second* diary proving she's not dead. What do they want?"

Orsen looked at the blue cloth cover on his table. "I don't know yet."

"Money?"

"They didn't ask for any."

"Then they want something else, and when people want something else, they usually want you to do something. Think about it. Call me back when you've thought about it. And Orsen—don't read it in the bath."

"I've read things in the bath exactly once."

"You dropped a first-edition into the water and then cried about it for a week. I was there. I helped you dry the pages with a hair dryer. Don't read it in the bath."

Orsen hung up, smiling, and then stopped smiling, because Mrs. Adeyemi's voice came back: *they know where you sleep.* He got up and checked the door chain, which was a thing he had started doing recently and hated that he had started doing.

* * *

The 2001 diary was not, strictly, a diary. It was a ledger wearing a diary's clothes.

The early entries were domestic—weather, market complaints, a neighbor's goat that had developed what Ada described as "a philosophical opposition to fences"—but threaded through them, the way a thread runs through cloth, were references to paintings.

*The woman at the studio says the varnish on the Adoration is beyond saving, but she says that about everything. Underneath the dirt there is a sky the color of forgiveness. I checked the back. The frame remembers.*

Orsen read that three times. *The frame remembers.* He went back to the first diary—the drowned one, the 1997 one, the one that had ended on June 17 with a final entry that read, in full: *If I am not careful, I will be an accident. I have been careful. I have been so careful. Tomorrow I will be more careful than careful.*

And now, four years later, in a different hand at a different time, the same phrase: *the frame remembers.*

He spread the diaries side by side and began to hunt. It took him most of the afternoon. Ada had a habit—he'd noticed it in the first volume, filed it as a quirk—of referring to paintings by partial descriptions rather than names. *The Adoration. The blue madonna. The one with the wrong saint.* In the 1997 diary, these had seemed like the habits of an archivist who spent her days among art records. In the 2001 diary, they had a different texture. They appeared at the ends of entries, after the okra and the goat and the weather, like a signature at the bottom of a letter. Like messages that weren't for her.

*The frame remembers. The third one is the honest one. Count the candles.*

He wrote the phrases on index cards and pinned them above his desk, and by evening his wall looked like the wall of a man who was either solving a mystery or losing his mind, and Orsen had long ago made peace with the ambiguity.

The code, if it was a code, was elegant. He was almost sure it was a code. *The Adoration*—he'd found a reference in the 1997 diary to an Adoration being restored at a studio, a restoration studio Ada had visited on records business. *The blue madonna* appeared twice in the second diary, once beside a date, once beside what looked like a street name abbreviated to three letters. *Count the candles* sat underlined twice—Ada's double underline, the one she used for things she didn't believe, or things she needed herself to believe.

He made tea. He drank the tea without tasting it. He stood at the wall with his arms folded like a general reviewing a losing battle, and he thought: *she's alive, she's alive, she's been alive for years, writing about okra and goats and paintings, hiding in plain sentences.*

And then he thought the other thought, the one that had been waiting patiently at the back of his skull all day, the way unpleasant thoughts do.

*If she's alive, then someone went to enormous trouble to make the world believe she drowned. Someone forged a death certificate. Someone filled a casket. And someone, four years later, is sending her diaries to a true-crime podcaster—diaries that, if they're real, are the most dangerous documents in this city.*

Nobody mails dynamite out of kindness.

* * *

He worked until two in the morning, which was a habit Tunde had tried to break and had given up on with the weary grace of a man accepting a climate. Around one, the code began to give.

It gave the way codes do—not all at once, but in seams. *The third one is the honest one.* He'd assumed *one* meant painting. But cross-referencing the 1997 diary, he found Ada had written about three accidents—not the three Orsen had built his career on, but three smaller ones, three deaths in the months before her disappearance, each one officially ruled misadventure, each one involving someone who had signed or witnessed or handled the evidence logs she'd been cataloguing. The third of those men had been the one who'd raised questions. The third one had asked for a transfer. The third one had died in a stairwell.

*The third one is the honest one.*

Orsen sat very still in his dark kitchen. Somewhere down the street a generator coughed and settled into its overnight hum. On his table, the blue diary lay open to a page dated April 2001, and at the bottom of that page, in that dry, precise, faintly amused hand, was a line he had read a dozen times that day and only now heard properly:

*If he is reading this, then I suppose I finally trust someone. God help us both.*

He read it again. *If he is reading this.*

The diary hadn't been addressed to him. It hadn't been addressed to anyone. But it had been *sent* to him—wrapped in acid-free tissue by someone who understood archival practice, left in his lobby by someone who knew his building. Ada hadn't sent it. Ada, if she was alive, had spent four years learning to be invisible, and invisible people did not mail their souls to podcasters.

Which meant someone else had the diaries. Someone who knew where Ada was, or had been. Someone who wanted Orsen reading.

Orsen got up and checked the door chain again, and this time he also checked the window latch, and then he sat back down and laughed at himself—a short, unamused sound in the empty flat—because the truth was that a latch had never once stopped anyone, and they both knew it.

He picked up his phone to call Tunde, saw the time, and put it down. Some conversations deserved daylight.

Instead he opened the diary to the last written entry—dated June 2001, the most recent, the ink still confident—and read it one final time before bed.

*The blue madonna goes to the chapel on Friday. He will understand. He always was a man who read to the end of the sentence. Count the candles, and then count the years, and then forgive me for what the counting costs you.*

Orsen turned off the lamp and lay in the dark, listening to the generator hum, doing what he did best and worst: reading to the end of the sentence.

*He.* Someone Ada trusted, four years into a life she'd built out of nothing. Someone who was now handing her words to a stranger with a microphone.

In the morning, he would start looking for a deconsecrated chapel that kept a blue madonna. In the morning, he would count candles.

But lying in the dark, one thought kept turning over like a coin that wouldn't settle: whoever had mailed him the diary wanted him to find what Ada had hidden. And whoever wanted him to find it, Orsen was almost certain, was not Ada—

which meant that somewhere out there, a second person knew she was alive.

And Orsen had a terrible, professional, unshakable feeling that the killer who had followed the first diary was about to discover he had competition.
