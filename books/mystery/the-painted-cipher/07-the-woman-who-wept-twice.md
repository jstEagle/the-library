## 7. The Woman Who Wept Twice

Grace Nwosu lived in a bungalow in Surulere that looked like it had been arguing with the rain for thirty years and winning on points. The zinc roof had been patched so many times it resembled a quilt, and the front gate — blue paint flaking to reveal an older, more hopeful green beneath — announced my arrival with a squeal that could wake the dead.

Which was, I suppose, the point of the visit.

I had rehearsed the whole thing on the drive over. I was good at this part, or I used to be. The gentle knock. The sympathetic tilt of the head. The careful questions dressed up as small talk until the small talk got tired and went home and the real questions came out. Twenty years of true-crime work teaches you how to sit with grieving people without making it worse, and for most of those twenty years I told myself that was a kindness. Lately I'd begun to suspect it was a skill, and skills are what you sharpen when you intend to use them.

The woman who opened the door was smaller than I expected, wrapped in a wrapper the color of ripe pawpaw, her hair covered in a neat black tie. Her face did something complicated when she saw me — a flicker of recognition, because everyone in Nigeria recognized a microphone even without the microphone — followed by something that looked almost like relief, which surprised me, and then a door closing behind her eyes, which did not.

"You're the podcast man," she said. "Orsen Okonkwo."

"Yes, ma. Thank you for seeing me."

"I didn't see you," she said. "I opened the door. Come inside before the mosquitoes file a report."

Her sitting room smelled of camphor and old upholstery and, faintly, of eucalyptus, the kind of smell that means someone has been burning leaves against fever. A wall of photographs held court above the sofa: children I didn't recognize, grandchildren by the look of their school uniforms, a stern man in a graduation gown who was probably a late husband. And there, third from the left, slightly crooked as if it had been straightened a thousand times and always drifted back, a portrait of Ada Nwosu at maybe twenty-two, in a graduation gown of her own, smiling the way people smile when they don't yet know what's coming.

I'd seen that photograph a hundred times. It was the one every news outlet had run in 1997, the one that opened my very first episode about the case. Seeing it in situ — hanging in a mother's living room, dusted regularly, angled toward the best chair — rearranged something in my chest.

"She hated that picture," Grace said, following my eyes. "She said the photographer made her look like she was being arrested." She paused. "Sit. I'll bring water. You'll drink it whether you want it or not; that is how hospitality works in this house."

* * *

The water came in a glass older than both of us, and Grace sat across from me with her hands folded in her lap like a woman preparing to hear a diagnosis. I started gently, the way I'd planned. Her daughter's work at the National Archives. How meticulous she'd been. How the case had never left me, how some cases get into your blood like that, how sorry I was to reopen anything.

Grace listened with the patience of a woman who had heard every version of sympathy the country could produce.

"You want to know if I believe she's dead," she said at last.

I set down the glass. "Ma—"

"Everyone dances around it. The journalists dance. The police danced. Even the priest dances, and priests are supposed to be past dancing." Her voice was level, almost conversational, which somehow made it worse. "You're the first one in twenty-six years to just come and sit in my house and drink my water and wait for me to say it myself. So. Ask your questions properly, Mr. Okonkwo, and I will decide which ones deserve answers."

I asked about Ada's habits. Her routines. Whether she'd seemed frightened in the weeks before she vanished. Whether she'd mentioned anyone at the archives — a supervisor, a visitor, a man from outside. Grace answered some questions fully and others with a smooth, practiced vagueness that I recognized, because I'd spent two decades listening to people construct sentences they'd built before, sentences with load-bearing walls already tested.

It was the grief itself that gave her away. Not its absence — its *choreography*.

When she spoke about the flood, about the basement where the diary was found, her hands trembled and her voice cracked and she reached for a handkerchief that was already in her lap, positioned, I noticed, within easy reach before I arrived. That grief was real. But it was also *worn*, the way a path gets worn — trodden so many times it had stopped being wild. And then there were moments when the grief simply wasn't there at all: when I mentioned the closed casket, she recited the details flatly, without touching her handkerchief, like a woman reading from a document she'd signed long ago and no longer agreed with.

And once — only once — when I said the words *"if she were alive,"* Grace Nwosu looked at the portrait on her wall, and her expression did not break. It *settled*. Like a house after a storm that has already passed.

"Tell me about the funeral," I said softly.

"What is there to tell? Rain. Many people. The commander sent representatives — very respectful, very generous." Something moved behind her eyes at the word *commander*, quick as a fish under water. "The casket was closed. They said the water... you understand."

"The body was never identified by family?"

"They showed me photographs." She said it quickly. Too quickly. "It was her coat. Her bag was found nearby. What else was there?"

What else was there indeed. Except that the coroner who signed the death certificate — Dr. Emeka Bassey, whose name had surfaced twice now in my research, whose signature sat at the bottom of three other deaths I'd proven were murders — had signed off on an identification that no next of kin ever confirmed in person. And except that Grace Nwosu had just described her daughter's funeral with the emotional temperature of a weather report.

I should have pushed. Twenty years of instinct said push, lean in, find the seam. But I looked at this old woman in her pawpaw-colored wrapper, alone in a house full of photographs of people who visited, presumably, less than they should, and I thought about what it costs to be the mother of a dead girl for twenty-six years, and I asked instead:

"Do you still keep her room?"

Something flickered across her face — alarm, calculation, decision — fast enough that I nearly missed it.

"No," she said. "I turned it into a sewing room years ago. A person cannot live in a museum."

But her eyes went, for half a second, to a door at the end of the hallway. A door that was closed. In a house where every other door stood open to the afternoon air.

* * *

We sat in silence for a while after that, and it was a strange silence, companionable and hostile at once, like sharing a bench with someone you're suing. Grace refilled my water without asking. Outside, a hawker's voice rose and fell, selling bread that nobody bought in this heat.

"My daughter," Grace said finally, "was a very tidy girl. Everything in its place. When she was small, she arranged her crayons by color, and if you moved one — even her father, even by accident — she would know. She would stand there and look at the box and say, 'Mummy, something has been touched.'" She smiled at the memory, and the smile was real, unguarded, and it hurt to look at. "The archivists at the National Archives, they loved her for this same thing. She could look at a shelf of ten thousand files and know which one had been moved."

"That's what made her good at her job," I said carefully.

"That is what made her dangerous," Grace said, "at her job."

The sentence landed in the room and stayed there. I didn't move. Neither did she. Somewhere in the walls, a lizard clicked its tongue at both of us.

"Mrs. Nwosu," I said, "when I was leaving, I was going to ask you the question I ask every family in every case I've worked. So let me ask it now, while we're both being honest, since honesty seems to be contagious today." I leaned forward. "Is there anything you've never told the police? Anything at all — a letter, a phone call, a feeling, a dream — that you kept back because telling it would have changed everything?"

And here is the thing about twenty years of interviewing people: you learn to watch hands. Faces lie; faces are trained liars, social animals, professionals. Hands are amateurs. Hands tell the truth or they hide, and Grace Nwosu's hands — folded, calm, grandmotherly all through our conversation — moved. Just slightly. Toward the pocket of her wrapper. And then stopped, caught themselves, returned to her lap with the studied innocence of a child near a broken vase.

"There is nothing," she said.

"Okay," I said, and stood, and thanked her, and meant it.

At the door, with the mosquito-dark evening coming down and the blue-green gate squealing somewhere in my future, she touched my sleeve. Her grip was stronger than her size suggested.

"Mr. Okonkwo. You have made a career of digging up what people buried." Her voice was quiet. "Do you know why people bury things?"

"To hide them," I said.

"No." She shook her head slowly. "To *plant* them. Some things are buried so that something else can live." She released my sleeve. "Go home. Eat something. Stop digging."

* * *

I got as far as the car. I sat in the driver's seat with the engine off and replayed the whole visit, and the longer I replayed it, the worse — or better, depending on your appetite for ruin — it got.

The rehearsed grief. The flat recitation of the funeral. The glance at the closed door. The hand moving toward the wrapper pocket. *Stop digging.* People tell you to stop digging when you're digging in the wrong place. Nobody says it when you're digging in empty ground.

I drove four streets away, parked under a mango tree, and called Tunde.

"Let me guess," he said. "You need me to pull something at eight p.m. on a Friday, and it's morally gray."

"It's a records request. Burial permits, 1997, Lagos mainland. And I need you to find out if Grace Nwosu ever filed a change of address, forwarded mail, anything—"

"Orsen." His voice did the thing it does when he's deciding whether to fight me or fold. "The teaser episode dropped Tuesday. We're at four hundred thousand downloads. Do you know what that means? It means half the country is listening, and the other half is the country's lawyers. If you're about to tell me the mother knew—"

"I'm not telling you anything yet. I'm telling you a hand moved."

"A hand moved," he repeated. "Great. Wonderful. I went to university for this." A pause, and then, quieter, because Tunde is many things but heartless isn't one of them: "If the mother knew — Orsen, think about what that means. Think about what a woman would have to be protecting for twenty-six years to sit on a truth like that. Whatever she's carrying, it isn't a secret. It's a *load-bearing wall*. You start pulling at it, the whole house comes down, and she's standing under it."

"I know," I said.

"And you're going to do it anyway."

I watched a fruit bat negotiate with the mango tree and lose. "I'm going to do it anyway."

He sighed the sigh of a man updating his résumé in his head. "Records by Monday. Go home. Eat something — actually eat, don't stand over the sink like a criminal."

* * *

I went home. I did not eat.

Instead I spread my notes across the kitchen table — the photocopies, the timeline, the coroner's signature traced and retraced on three separate death certificates — and I laid the new information over it like a transparency sheet, and the pattern that emerged made my tea go cold in its mug untouched.

Because here was the thing I hadn't let myself think in the parking lot, the thing that had been waiting patiently for the dark to say it out loud:

Grace Nwosu hadn't lied to me tonight. Not once, not really. Every answer she'd given was technically true, deflected rather than false, and that kind of truthfulness takes *practice*. Twenty-six years of practice. Which meant she hadn't learned it last week. She'd learned it in 1997, in the immediate aftermath, when the police came asking their questions and a mother made a choice — fast, permanent, irrevocable — about which version of events she could live inside.

And there was one more thing. Small. Almost nothing. But I'd spent a career on almost-nothings.

When she'd spoken of Commander Eze's representatives at the funeral — *very respectful, very generous* — her mouth had said gratitude. But her voice had done something I've only heard a handful of times in twenty years, usually from witnesses testifying with a gun-shaped absence in the room. Her voice had gone *careful*. The way yours goes careful when you're choosing words with someone standing behind your shoulder, even — especially — when that shoulder belongs to a memory.

A mother doesn't sound careful about a man who sent flowers to her daughter's funeral.

A mother sounds careful about a man she believes might send something else.

I sat there until two in the morning, turning it over, and around the time the neighborhood generator finally surrendered and the street went truly dark, I admitted it plainly, the way you admit things at two in the morning because no one else is awake to argue:

Ada Nwosu might be alive. And her mother had known for twenty-six years. Had grieved in public and known in private, had worn the widow's garments of a living daughter, had buried an empty casket and tended an open wound, all so that somewhere — in another name, another town, another life — her tidy, dangerous, meticulous girl could keep arranging the world and noticing what had been touched.

Every instinct I had said this was the biggest story of my career.

Every other instinct I had — the ones I liked better — said it was the biggest *burden* of my career, and that Grace Nwosu's plea wasn't a denial at all. It was a warning from a woman who had made this exact bargain long ago and knew precisely what it cost.

Stop digging, she'd said.

I opened my laptop instead. And that was when I saw it — an email, timestamped 11:47 p.m., sitting in my inbox with a subject line of six characters that I recognized instantly, because I'd spent three nights teaching myself the cipher they came from:

**K-7-F-R-A-M-E.**

No sender name. No message body. Just the subject line, and beneath it, a single attached image: a gallery listing for a small exhibition in Lekki, opening Saturday. One painting in the show had a title that meant nothing to anyone else in the world.

Ada Nwosu had just broken twenty-six years of silence.

And she'd chosen to break it to *me*.
