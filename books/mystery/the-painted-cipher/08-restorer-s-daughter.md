## 8. Restorer's Daughter

The town was called Ibeno, which Ada learned meant something like *the place where the water forgives*, though the woman who told her this—a fish seller with a laugh like a dropped crate of bottles—admitted she might be inventing the etymology entirely.

"It sounds better that way," the woman said, weighing snapper with hands that had done this for forty years and would do it for forty more. "A place where the water forgives. Who wouldn't want to live there?"

Ada paid for her fish and thought: someone running from the water. Someone who knew exactly what it did not forgive.

She had been Adaeze Umeh for eleven months now. The name had come to her on a bus, half-remembered from a secondary school classmate she'd liked but never known well—a girl who chewed her pen and drew horses in the margins of her notebooks. It felt strange to wear another girl's name like a borrowed dress, but strangeness was the point. Strangeness kept her awake. Awake kept her alive.

*Alive,* she reminded herself, was still an achievement. She had to keep score of these things now.

* * *

Madam Felicia Ojo's restoration studio sat at the end of a lane that smelled of salt and turpentine, behind a house whose paint had given up arguing with the sea decades ago. The sign above the door read F. OJO — FINE ART CONSERVATION in letters so faded they were practically a secret. Ada had found it the way she found everything those days: by walking until her feet made the decision for her.

"You have steady hands?" Madam Felicia had asked on that first day, not looking up from the canvas she was cleaning—a seascape drowning under sixty years of yellowed varnish.

"Yes."

"Can you sit still for six hours?"

"I can sit still for longer than that," Ada said, and only afterward realized how it sounded. Like a confession. Like a woman who had practiced being motionless somewhere very dark.

Madam Felicia looked up then. She was seventy if she was a day, small as a folded chair, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-threaded hair and eyes that had spent a lifetime looking at paintings closely enough to catch their lies.

"Hm," she said. "Come back Monday. Bring nothing. If you steal from me I will know before you reach the road."

It was not, strictly speaking, an offer of employment. But Ada came back Monday anyway, and Madam Felicia pretended not to be surprised, which was its own kind of welcome.

The work saved her. That was the plain truth of it, and Ada was a plain-truth woman when no one was listening. There was something in the labor—the cotton swabs, the solvents, the slow resurrection of a face beneath grime—that demanded exactly what hiding demanded: patience, precision, and the willingness to look at damaged things without flinching. She learned to consolidate flaking paint with a brush of three hairs. She learned that a canvas's tears were called *losses*, as if the painting were grieving. She learned to line a weakened canvas onto new fabric, pressing it flat with a warm iron, giving old wounds a fresh backing to hold them together.

"Some paintings want to be dramatic," Madam Felicia said one afternoon, watching Ada coax a water stain out of a portrait of some colonial administrator's wife. "They crack everywhere, they flake, they demand attention. Others—" she tapped the portrait's frame "—this one has been through a flood, two moves, and a decade in a leaky storeroom, and it held. Quiet strength. You can always tell which ones survived by keeping quiet."

Ada's swab paused for half a second. Then continued.

"You're getting good at faces," Madam Felicia added. "Better than me at your age, and I was good. You look at them like you're apologizing to them."

"I'm not."

"No," the old woman agreed mildly. "But you look like you are. It's fine. Guilt makes careful restorers." She said it the way she said everything, as if it were about paint.

* * *

The ledger began in February, in a notebook Ada bought from the stationery shop beside the church—the same shop, she noted with a private, bitter humor, that sold the schoolchildren their exercise books. She kept it inside a hollowed space beneath a floorboard in her rented room, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp.

Not a diary. She'd sworn off diaries; diaries were how you got caught, diaries were evidence, diaries were the reason she was alive at all. This was different. This was accounting.

The first entry:

**Mr. Okpara (records clerk). Fell from scaffolding, January. He covered for me twice. He laughed at my terrible handwriting jokes.**

The second:

**Mrs. Danjuma (registry). Car accident on the Enugu road, March. She once gave me her umbrella and walked home in the rain herself.**

She wrote each name the day she heard the news, in the smallest handwriting she could manage, as if smallness might shrink the weight. And then, because she was who she was—an archivist down to the marrow, even buried, even renamed—she dated everything, cross-referenced everything, and left margins wide enough for corrections.

Because here was the thing she could not stop noticing, the thing that sat in her chest like swallowed glass: the deaths were not random. They were *sequential*. Each one followed the last the way footprints follow footsteps, each one a person who had touched the same documents she had touched, seen the same numbers, signed the same registers. The men from Eze's circle were tidying. And the tidy-up was moving outward from the center—from her—in concentric rings.

Which meant every ring they cleaned bought her time. Which meant every ring they cleaned was paid for in someone else's blood.

*I am spending other people's lives,* she wrote one night, and then crossed it out, because it wasn't quite true and untruths had no place in a ledger. She rewrote it: *I am surviving on credit. Someone is paying the interest.*

* * *

The radio in the studio was Madam Felicia's concession to modernity, tuned permanently to a station that played highlife between the news bulletins. Ada had learned to dread the bulletins the way farmers dread certain clouds.

"—authorities say the fire, which claimed the life of retired police sergeant Bala Musa, appears to have started from a faulty generator—"

"That's three this year," Madam Felicia remarked, not looking up from her easel. "Accidents. All men who worked in government offices, all retired, all—" she waved her brush vaguely, "—quiet ones. The quiet ones die strangely in this country. The loud ones live forever."

Ada kept her breathing even. Under her hands, a seventeenth-century Madonna watched her with painted eyes that had seen four hundred years of wars and looked, frankly, unsurprised by this one too.

"You think it's suspicious?" Ada asked. Casual. A student's question.

"I think," Madam Felicia said, "that when a fisherman drowns, I ask what the water was doing. When three fishermen drown in one season, I ask what the water wants." She mixed a little ochre into her palette. "And then I mind my business, because I am old and the water is big. You should learn to mind your business too, Adaeze. You ask questions like a person who used to get answers."

Ada laughed—she'd gotten good at laughing on cue, a skill she wished she could unlearn—and changed the subject to varnish removal rates.

That night she added a fourth name to the ledger. Sergeant Bala Musa. She remembered his face: a heavy, kind man who had once, absurdly, brought puff-puff to the records office on Fridays. In the margin next to his name she wrote: *He knew about the second logbook. He never said so, but he knew.*

Then, because the ledger demanded honesty even when she wanted mercy: *Four. Four people. One year. And I am here learning to clean paintings while they bury him.*

She pressed the notebook flat under her palm for a long moment, as if she could hold the names still, keep them from multiplying. Then she hid it again beneath the floorboard, next to the oilcloth packet of copied documents she had carried out of Lagos sewn into the lining of a coat she no longer wore but could not burn.

* * *

Spring came to Ibeno the way it comes to coastal towns: not arriving so much as intensifying. The rains thickened. The lane outside the studio grew green moss between its stones. Madam Felicia's cat, a disreputable orange creature named Bishop—he had been found, allegedly, asleep in a church collection basket—took to sleeping in the drying cabinet, which was against approximately nine rules of conservation, and got away with it because he was warm and the studio was cold.

Ada was restoring a small landscape—a river scene, ironically—when the letter came.

It arrived with the morning post, among bills and a circular from a fishing cooperative. A plain envelope, her assumed name written in a hand she didn't recognize, postmarked Lagos.

Her whole body went cold before her mind caught up. Nobody in Lagos knew the name Adaeze Umeh. Nobody in Lagos knew Ibeno existed except—

She opened it with fingers that wanted to shake and refused to.

Inside was a newspaper clipping. No note. Just the clipping, from a Lagos paper, dated three weeks prior. A short item, buried on an inside page:

**FORMER CORONER'S ASSISTANT DIES IN BOATING ACCIDENT**

She read it twice. Chike Odum. She knew the name. Of course she knew the name. He had been junior staff at the coroner's office in '97—young, nervous, helpful in the way of people who haven't yet learned what help costs. He had pulled a file for her once without logging it. She had thanked him. She had promised him nothing would happen.

In the ledger, that night, she wrote his name fifth. Her hand was steady. She noticed that her hand was steady and hated it—hated the calm professionalism of it, the archivist's composure applied to a list of the dead.

Below his name she wrote: *He helped me. I promised him. I broke it.*

And then, because five names in fourteen months formed a pattern and patterns pointed somewhere, she wrote the thing she had been circling for months without landing on:

*They are working backward. From me, outward. When they run out of witnesses, they will start looking for the witness who doesn't exist. They will start looking for a dead woman who isn't dead.*

She sat with that sentence for a long time. Bishop the cat came and sat on the notebook, which was either comfort or censorship.

* * *

"You've gone quiet," Madam Felicia said in June, squinting at a gilded frame she was re-arming with gold leaf. "Quieter. You were quiet when you came, like a heron, but now you're quiet like a heron that's seen the gun."

"Do herons see guns?"

"The smart ones do. The others make nice trophies." She smoothed a leaf of gold with a soft brush, delicate as a heartbeat. "There was a man asking after you. At the market."

The room did not tilt. Ada had trained it not to. But something behind her ribs went very still, the way a painting goes still under examination light.

"Asking after me."

"After the quiet girl who works for the old restorer. Young man. Polite. Said he was from an insurance company, checking on apprenticeships." Madam Felicia snorted. "Insurance companies don't check on apprenticeships. I told him you were my niece and terrible at your job and he went away."

"Why did you say niece?"

"Because 'stranger who appeared last year with no history' invites more questions than 'niece.'" The old woman finally looked up, and her eyes over the reading glasses were not vague at all. "I was married to a man who ran from things, Adaeze. Before he was my husband. I know what a person looks like when they count the exits. I've never asked you your business and I'm not asking now. But I'll tell you what I told him: this town forgets strangers slowly and protects its own quickly. So be from here. Be somebody's niece. Be the girl who fixes the Madonna. It's harder to make a person disappear when everyone knows her face."

Ada stood in the turpentine-smelling light of the studio, holding a swab, and felt something crack gently in her chest—not painfully, the way ice cracks on a river in early thaw. For eleven months she had been a ghost practicing to be furniture. Madam Felicia had just offered her the opposite trick: not invisibility, but *belonging*. A different armor.

"Thank you," Ada said.

"Don't thank me. Finish the river. The client wants it by Friday and the sky is still muddy."

* * *

But that night, alone in her room with rain hammering the tin roof like applause for something terrible, Ada took out the ledger and did not write a sixth name. Instead she turned to a blank page and, in her smallest hand, began a different kind of entry.

*If they find me, they find the copies. The copies are the proof. The proof is useless unless someone is looking for it. No one is looking, because everyone believes I am dead, and the dead cannot testify.*

She stared at the words. The rain applauded. Somewhere out in the dark water, the sea that forgave nothing went on with its work.

*Sooner or later,* she wrote, *someone is going to ask the right question about a dead archivist. And when they do, I need them to find the trail before Eze finds me. I need to leave signs. Coded ones. In places a certain kind of person would look.*

She knew exactly what kind of person. She had been that kind of person once: the sort who read marginalia, who checked verso sides of canvases, who believed that everything hidden was also, in its way, an address.

Ada closed the ledger. Beneath the floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, the truth waited with the patience of all archived things.

Outside, far off down the coast, a dog barked at something—or someone—moving along the beach road in the rain.
