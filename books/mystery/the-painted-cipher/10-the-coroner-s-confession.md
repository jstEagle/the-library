## 10. The Coroner's Confession

The dying man lived in a bungalow at the end of a street that smelled of rain and frying plantain, in a part of the city where the gutters worked and the neighbors still greeted each other by name. Orsen knew this because he'd spent three days learning everything there was to know about Dr. Emmanuel Bassey, retired coroner, before knocking on his door.

What he knew could be arranged into a short, sad list. Forty-one years of service. A wife who had died in 2011. A son in Canada who called on Sundays. A diagnosis delivered eight months ago with the kind of word that ends sentences—*pancreatic*—and a prognosis measured now in weeks, maybe days, if the way the curtains stayed drawn all morning meant anything.

And one more thing: a signature. The same steady, looping *E. Bassey* on four death certificates Orsen had photographed and laid out across his kitchen table like a hand of very bad cards.

Chukwuemeka Okafor-Obi. Drowned, 1999.
Ngozi Adeyemi-Solanke. Fall from height, 2003.
Peter Umeh. Carbon monoxide, 2008.
Ada Nwosu. Fire, 1997.

Three accidents. One disappearance. Four signatures, identical down to the angle of the crossbar on the *t*. Orsen had spent two nights with a magnifying lamp and a bottle of Star Lager he never opened, comparing them, and the conclusion sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone: nobody's handwriting was that consistent. Not over eleven years. Not through tremors, or age, or the small daily betrayals of being human. These signatures were copies. Practiced copies. Which meant either Dr. Bassey had signed things he shouldn't have—or someone else had learned to be him.

He knocked.

The door opened on the third try, after a long silence during which Orsen imagined the man inside deciding whether he had the strength for whatever came next. What came next was a tall, hollowed-out figure in an embroidered robe gone soft with washing, holding himself upright against the doorframe with one hand and regarding Orsen with eyes that were yellowed but not yet dim.

"You're the podcast man," Dr. Bassey said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "My son listens to you. He thinks you're a troublemaker." A pause, and something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but a relative of one. "He's right."

"Dr. Bassey. My name is Orsen Okonkwo. I'd like ten minutes of your time."

"I have less than that left in most ways." But he stepped back from the door, which was its own kind of answer. "Come in before the neighbors see you. Mrs. Adichie next door has been praying for my soul since March, and she'll take your visit as evidence her prayers are working. I'd hate to disappoint her."

* * *

The living room was clean in the way rooms are clean when someone has stopped caring about cleanliness but hired help hasn't. A fan turned overhead with a rhythmic patience. On the walls hung photographs: a younger Bassey in graduation robes; Bassey and his wife squinting into some long-gone sun; Bassey shaking hands with dignitaries, including—one photograph, slightly crooked, as if it had been straightened many times and always drifted back—a man Orsen recognized instantly from a hundred gala photographs and foundation brochures.

Commander Daniel Eze, thirty years younger, full of medals and confidence, gripping Bassey's hand while both men smiled for a camera that captured nothing of what passed between them.

Bassey saw him looking. "You noticed that already. Of course you did. That's why you're here." He lowered himself into an armchair with the care of a man handling his own body like borrowed equipment. "Sit down, Mr. Okonkwo. Ask me what you came to ask. I'm too tired for the dance."

Orsen sat. He had prepared questions on the drive over—eleven of them, ordered, refined—but sitting in that room, facing that man, he found himself setting the list aside. Some interviews were interrogations. This one felt more like last rites.

"The four death certificates," Orsen said. "Okafor-Obi. Solanke. Umeh. And Ada Nwosu. Your signature is on all of them. But it isn't your handwriting, is it? It's a copy. Someone practiced your signature until they could produce it perfectly, and then they used it for years."

Bassey was quiet for a long moment. The fan turned. Somewhere outside, a radio played highlife music at the volume of a man who wanted company but not conversation.

"You've done your homework," the old man said finally. "Better than the police ever did. Better than the journalists who came sniffing and left when I gave them nothing." He looked at his own hands, which lay in his lap like sleeping birds. "Do you know what a coroner actually is, Mr. Okonkwo? Everyone thinks we find truth. We don't. We *certify* it. There's a difference. Truth is what happened. Certification is what gets written down. In a good country, those are the same thing." He coughed, wet and long, and waved off Orsen's half-rise. "In our country, they haven't been the same thing since before independence."

"Did you falsify the reports?"

"Yes." No hesitation. No flinch. Just the flat delivery of a man who had rehearsed this confession alone, in this room, for months, waiting for someone worth giving it to. "All three. Okafor-Obi didn't drown drunk in his bathtub—he drowned because a man held him under it, and I wrote 'accidental' because Commander Eze's aide-de-camp visited my office on a Tuesday and left an envelope that weighed more than paper should. Solanke fell because she was pushed, and I wrote 'fall' because by then I understood how the world worked and my wife was sick and my son needed school fees and—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "There is no version of this where I come out well, Mr. Okonkwo. I want you to understand that. Whatever you write about me, whatever your listeners think—I was not trapped. I was *bought*, and I stayed bought, and I told myself stories about survival until I believed them. That is the whole ugly architecture of it."

Orsen felt the recorder in his pocket like a coal against his thigh. He hadn't asked permission yet. He would. But first—

"And Ada Nwosu?"

Something changed in the old man's face. It was subtle—the way weather changes, a pressure shift you feel before you can name it—and Orsen, who had interviewed killers and liars and grieving mothers, recognized it immediately. This was the one thing that hurt differently from the others.

"That," Bassey said slowly, "is the strangest file of my career. And I have been trying to make sense of it for twenty-seven years."

* * *

He talked for a long time. Orsen got the recorder out, asked permission, was granted it with a tired wave—"Say what you like about me, young man. I'm past shame; it's a luxury of the healthy"—and let the tape run while the fan turned and the light through the curtains went from gray to gold.

"In 1997, I get a call. Body coming in from a fire in Yaba—female, mid-twenties, identified as an archivist named Ada Nwosu. Standard procedure. Except the call doesn't come from the fire service. It comes from a police liaison, and the liaison says the family has already identified her, paperwork is handled, I just need to sign off. Fast-tracked. Closed casket, closed case." Bassey's hands tightened on the armrests. "I've been doing this long enough to know what fast-tracked means. It means someone important wants it done. So I say fine, send the body."

"It never arrived," Orsen said.

"It never arrived." Bassey laughed, and it cost him—it bent him forward and turned into coughing, and when he straightened there were tears standing in the yellowed eyes, though whether from the coughing or something else, Orsen couldn't tell. "I waited two days. Two days, calling the morgue, calling the liaison, getting transferred and disconnected and told the transport was delayed. And then the certificate comes back to me for countersignature—already filled out, already stamped, cause of death: smoke inhalation, remains unrecovered due to fire damage—and my signature is already on it. Perfect. Flawless. Better than mine, honestly. Whoever did it had practiced." He shook his head slowly. "Do you understand what I'm telling you, Mr. Okonkwo? For three years, someone had been signing my name. I found out because they used it *without asking*. They didn't need me anymore. They'd made a copy of me."

"A protector," Orsen said quietly. "Someone inside Eze's circle who got to the paperwork first."

"Someone with access. Someone who knew the system so well they could move a body that didn't exist through channels that ask no questions." Bassey looked at him directly. "I falsified three reports because I was a coward with debts. But that fourth signature—that wasn't cowardice. That was *mercy*. Somebody in that machine looked at a frightened girl and decided she deserved to live, and they were inside enough, clever enough, to fake her death so cleanly that even I believed it for a week. Even me. I checked, you know. Afterward. Quietly. The fire report exists. The identification exists. Everything exists, all of it perfect, all of it wrong. You cannot forge a whole death alone. You need people. You need access. You need—" he paused, searching, "—you need love, I think. Or something near enough to love that it will commit crimes for you."

Orsen sat with that. Outside, the highlife music had stopped, and the street sounds rushed in to fill the space—children, traffic, a generator stuttering awake somewhere. The ordinary world, going on around a confession that would unmake part of it.

"There's something else," Bassey said. "Since we're emptying the cupboard." He reached into the pocket of his robe and drew out an envelope, old and soft-cornered, and held it without offering it. "A week after the Nwosu certificate came through, this was left at my office. No note, no name. Inside—" he tipped it, and a single sheet slid halfway out, "—a photograph of a young woman. Alive. Standing at what looked like a bus station, hair cut short, wearing clothes she clearly wasn't used to. On the back, one line of writing." He turned it over, read it from memory rather than look again. "*She is dead. Let her stay dead.*"

Orsen's breath caught. "You kept it all these years?"

"I kept it because I didn't know what else to do with it. Reporting it meant admitting I'd signed a false certificate. Ignoring it meant—" he shrugged, a small, exhausted motion, "—it meant leaving a girl alive somewhere, which seemed, on balance, the better sin." He held the envelope out now, properly. "Take it. I have no use for secrets where I'm going. And take my advice, which is free and therefore worthless: whoever sent that photograph protected her once. Find out if they're still protecting her. Because if Commander Eze learns she's alive—and Mr. Okonkwo, from the sound of your voice on that podcast of yours, he may already suspect it—then the only question that matters is whether her guardian angel is still on duty, or whether he died, or retired, or was buried in one of those nice tidy accidents I certified."

* * *

Orsen walked out into the late afternoon with the envelope inside his jacket, against his chest, like a second heartbeat. At the gate he paused and looked back. Through the window, Dr. Bassey sat in his armchair in the gold light, and he raised one hand—not a wave exactly, more a salute between soldiers of the same losing war.

Orsen raised his hand back.

Then he was moving, fast, dialing Tunde before he'd even reached the car. "Tunde. It's real. She's alive—or she was. Someone staged her death from inside Eze's circle, someone with access to the whole machinery, and they left a photograph at the coroner's office a week later like a signature on their work. I've got it. I've got proof the death was theater."

Tunde's voice came through tinny and tight. "Orsen—slow down. Who? Who inside?"

"That's the question, isn't it?" Orsen slid behind the wheel, and the envelope crackled softly against his ribs. "Whoever it was knew the coroner's office, knew the police liaison chain, knew how to make a person vanish into paperwork. Someone close enough to Eze to protect a witness from him. And here's the thing that's keeping me up—" he started the engine, "—the diary. The continuing diary. Ada writes about hiding, about fear, about watching the news for names. But she never once writes about being *alone* in those first months. Never mentions how she survived, who moved her, who taught her to disappear. It's a hole in the record shaped exactly like a person."

He pulled away from the curb, the city opening up before him in its evening noise and color, and the thought assembled itself fully for the first time, complete and cold:

Ada hadn't escaped alone. Someone had built her exit—and that someone had been standing beside Daniel Eze the entire time.

Which meant the protector had a name. And Orsen had a very short list of everyone who'd had access in 1997—and every name on it was still alive, still respectable, and still, as far as the world knew, loyal to the commander.

Including one man whose job, back then, had been handling the commander's most delicate correspondence.

Silas.
