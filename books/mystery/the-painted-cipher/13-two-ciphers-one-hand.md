## 13. Two Ciphers, One Hand

The message arrived the way all important things in Orsen's life seemed to arrive lately: at two in the morning, disguised as something mundane.

He'd been asleep on the couch again, laptop open on his chest, an episode of a rival true-crime podcast playing at low volume—professional jealousy, he told himself, was just research with worse lighting. His phone buzzed against the floor where it had slipped. He almost didn't look.

The email was from an address that was nothing but numbers and a letter: **gallery-listings@** some domain he didn't recognize. The subject line read *New Acquisitions — West African Modernists*. And the body was a single paragraph of gallery-speak so aggressively boring that Orsen nearly deleted it on reflex.

*This month we are pleased to present three works newly conserved: a market scene in the manner of Enwonwu; a study of egrets over water; and a portrait of a woman reading, oil on board, unsigned, frame original.*

He stared at it for a long moment. Then he sat up so fast the laptop slid off his chest and hit the cushion, and the rival podcaster's soothing voice said, *"—and that's when police found the second body—"*

"Shut up," Orsen told him, and opened Ada Nwosu's diary to page one hundred and twelve.

He'd been staring at that entry for nine days. It was the only one in the continuation volume written entirely in what he'd come to think of as her cipher-hand—not code exactly, not substitution, but a private shorthand, the kind a meticulous archivist develops when she wants to be able to reread her own notes but no one else to. Dates compressed into fractions. Names reduced to initials and brushstrokes. He'd transcribed it three times, filled two notebooks, and gotten nowhere, because the key wasn't linguistic. He'd been looking for words.

It was paintings. Of course it was paintings. The whole diary was paintings—the chapel, the frames, the studio watermark. She hadn't hidden her message in language. She'd hidden it in a catalogue.

*A market scene.* Entry 112 began with a sketch of awnings, striped, angled steeply—*awnings at forty degrees, seven stalls, the third from the left sells fish.* He checked his notes from the restoration studio records Tunde had pulled: the studio had conserved, in its last documented year of operation, a market scene. Provenance unknown. Location after 2003: *deaccessioned, sold privately.*

But that wasn't the message. The message was in what she'd done to it. In the diary, the market scene's description contained four errors—a painter who knew her craft wouldn't make them. The awnings were wrong by ten degrees. There were six stalls, not seven. The fish stall was fifth, not third. And the portrait mentioned in the email—*a woman reading*—didn't appear in the diary's version of the scene at all.

Errors were how archivists signed their work. Errors were flags. *Look here. This is not what it seems.*

Orsen got out a fresh notebook, drew the market scene as the email described it, and overlaid the diary's version. Where they diverged, he wrote the letters the diary assigned to each element in her cipher-key—which he now realized wasn't a key at all but simply her own filing system, the one she'd used in the evidence room, which he'd found reproduced in the front of the first diary under the heading *Shelf Logic*.

A-W-N-I-N-G-S-T-E-N-D-E-G-R-E-E-S-F-I-S-H-P-O-R-T-R-A-I-T.

He rearranged them according to the shelf logic—an old archival sorting rule, third-from-left, descending—and the sentence assembled itself like bones finding their joints:

*NIGHT MARKET. THURSDAY. THE WOMAN READING WILL BE THERE. COME ALONE. DO NOT WRITE MY NAME.*

Orsen Okonkwo, a man who had spent fifteen years making other people's secrets loud, put both hands flat on the table and breathed until his heart stopped trying to escape through his ribs.

Then he did what any sensible paranoid man would do. He called Tunde.

"It's two-forty in the morning," Tunde said, by way of hello.

"She contacted me."

Silence. Then the sound of Tunde sitting up in bed, springs complaining. "Contacted you how."

"A fake gallery listing. Ciphered through the diary. Thursday, night market." Orsen was already pacing, the phone wedged against his shoulder. "Tunde, she's alive, she's watching me, and she wants to meet."

"Okay. Okay." A pause. "You hear how that sounds, right? Like the exact thing a trap sounds like?"

"I've thought about that."

"You've thought about it for four seconds."

"Three," Orsen admitted. "But listen—if Eze's people had her, we'd know. They don't do quiet. Every witness who got close ended up at the bottom of a stairwell or under a car. If they had Ada, there'd be a fourth accident by now. This is her. The cipher uses her own shelf logic, Tunde. Nobody alive knows that system except someone who kept the diaries."

Another silence, longer. When Tunde spoke again, his voice had gone soft in the way it did when he was scared for someone and too proud to say so directly. "Thursday. Come alone. You're going to go alone, aren't you."

"I'm going to go alone."

"You're going to go alone *and tell me everything afterward*, is what you're going to do. And you're going to wear something that doesn't scream 'podcast host,' which rules out literally every jacket you own."

*

Thursday came gray and slow, and Orsen spent it doing the thing he hated most, which was waiting. He cleaned an apartment that was already clean. He re-read the diary entries around 112 until he could recite them. He ate half a bowl of pepper soup and forgot about the other half. At six he changed shirts four times, settled on the least interesting one he owned, and then—because Tunde's voice was in his head—put on a cap he'd bought at a petrol station in 2019 and never worn.

The night market was in the old quarter, spilling down a street that had been pedestrian since before cars were common, lit by strings of bulbs that turned everything the color of weak tea. Fish smoke. Fried plantain. A boy selling phone chargers from a tray strapped to his neck. Music from three competing speakers, none of them cooperating.

He arrived early, which was a mistake, because arriving early meant standing still, and standing still meant being visible, and being visible was the one skill Orsen had never managed to unlearn. He bought a skewer he didn't want to have something to do with his hands. He drifted past fabric stalls. He looked, casually he hoped, for a woman reading.

At nine-fifteen, near the far end where the market thinned into residential dark, he saw her.

She was seated on a low stool beside a stall of secondhand books, holding a paperback open, and she was very good—or she would have been, if anyone actually read like that, eyes fixed and unmoving, turning pages at metronome intervals without appearing to absorb a word. Her headwrap was deep blue. Her face, turned away from the nearest bulb, was half-shadow by design. She wore gloves. In this heat, she wore gloves.

Orsen's mouth went dry. Fifteen years of imagining this moment—of narrating it, honestly, in draft episodes he'd never aired—and now that it was here, every rehearsed line evaporated. What came out was:

"That book is upside down."

The pages stopped turning.

For a moment he thought she'd bolt. Then, slowly, she looked at the book, registered his point, and closed it with the deliberate dignity of someone conceding a small defeat in a larger war. "Nine days," she said. Her voice was lower than he'd imagined, roughened at the edges, a voice that hadn't been used much in company. "Nine days you sat on that entry. I started to think I'd picked the wrong podcast."

"I picked it apart for nine days. There's a difference."

"There usually is, with you." She stood. Up close she was smaller than the photographs, or maybe the photographs had been of a girl and this was what time did instead. "Walk with me. Don't stop walking, don't stand anywhere longer than a minute, and don't say my name."

They walked. The market carried them along, anonymous in the way crowds are generous to people who need it.

"You've read my mother's name in my file," she said. "You've read my school records, my employment history, my death certificate. You know more about who I was than most people ever learn about anybody. So understand what I'm asking when I say: never say my name aloud. Not in your apartment. Not to your producer. Not into a microphone, not into a pillow, not even as a joke. The moment my name exists in the air near you, you become a door, and doors get opened."

"I understand."

"Do you? Because your entire career is opening doors. You're good at it. It's genuinely impressive—I've listened to all of it, even the season where you cried on air about the ferry case, which, for the record, was the right call emotionally and the wrong call professionally." Something moved at the corner of her mouth that might once have been a smile. "I need you to be bad at your job. Just once. Just for me."

They passed the fish stall—fifth from the left, Orsen noted absurdly, just as the diary said—and she steered them toward the thinning edge of the light.

"Here are my terms," she said. "One: you find the final painting. The diary points to it; you've probably worked out which museum by now, or you will within a week. Inside its frame is everything—original logs, and a recording I made in 1997 that I have spent twenty-six years hoping someone would never have to hear. Two: you do not publish anything about me. Not a teaser, not a hint, not a clever little episode title that makes your listeners feel smart. Three—" she stopped, finally, at the mouth of a side street where the bulbs gave out and the dark was honest, "—three, when you have the proof, you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone. Not the police. Not the press. Me. I decide how it gets used. I have paid for that decision in ways you cannot imagine, and I am not surrendering it now."

Orsen looked at her. Half her face was shadow, and the half he could see was calm in the way of people who have practiced calm until it became load-bearing.

"And if I say no?" he asked. "If I say the public has a right—"

"Then I vanish again, deeper this time, and you'll spend the rest of your career wondering if the woman in the diary died because of your download numbers." No anger in it. Just arithmetic. "I'm not threatening you, Mr. Okonkwo. I'm telling you what the price list looks like. You of all people should respect a price list."

He thought of Grace Nwosu's face, rehearsed grief and real grief fighting for the same muscles. He thought of the coroner's wet cough and forged signature. He thought of his own empty apartment and the strange, humiliating truth that the closest thing he had to a friend was a producer who yelled at him about jackets.

"Agreed," he said. "All three terms."

Something in her shoulders let down half an inch. It was the smallest movement he'd ever seen mean so much.

"Thank you," she said, and meant it, and then—because she was, underneath everything, an archivist, a woman who believed in completeness of record—she added, "For what it's worth: I didn't contact you because I trust you. I contacted you because you're the only person reckless enough and careful enough to find the painting, and because Eze's people were already following you, which means you were going to lead them somewhere eventually. Better it be somewhere I choose."

The cold went through Orsen slowly, like water finding the seam in a boot.

"Following me," he repeated.

"For eleven days. A man. Gray jacket, changes hats. He's not here tonight—" she paused, and her half-lit face tilted, and Orsen watched the calm crack down the middle, "—or he wasn't supposed to be. Why are your bag straps hanging loose?"

Orsen looked down. His satchel—the one he'd carried to the coroner's house, to the gala, to every dead end and live wire of the last month—hung open. The main zipper, which he kept meticulously closed, a habit from a childhood of crowded buses, gaped like a mouth mid-sentence. And clipped inside the lining, small and matte black and utterly indifferent, was a plastic rectangle the size of a matchbox with a pinprick of green light.

He didn't remember buying it. He didn't remember anyone giving it to him. Which meant it had been placed during one of the moments he'd stopped being careful—hanging on the back of a chair at the archive, the coat check at the foundation gala, the hour his apartment had been searched by professionals who took nothing.

Nothing taken. Nothing given back, either. Except this.

"He knows you're here," Ada said quietly. All the calm was gone now, stripped down to something faster underneath. "Wherever that thing reports to—he's known since you left your street. How long does it take to drive here?"

"Twelve minutes," Orsen heard himself say. "Fifteen in traffic."

"Then we have less than that." She was already moving, pulling him by the sleeve into the side street, her voice dropping to a rapid whisper. "Listen to me. You're going to walk out the north end, alone, fast, like nothing's wrong. You're going to drop that tracker in a delivery truck, any truck, the farther the better. And you are never, *never*, going to carry that bag again."

"The terms still stand?" he asked, stupidly, desperately, because it was the only sentence he had.

She glanced back at him, half her face already swallowed by the dark, and for one instant he saw not the careful stranger but the girl from the 1997 photograph, terrified and ferocious at once.

"They stand," she said. "But Mr. Okonkwo—you just taught me the most important lesson of your entire career."

"What's that?"

"That the man who exposes everyone's hidden life can't even keep his own bag shut." She pressed something into his palm—a folded scrap of paper, gone before he could look at it. "Go. Now. And whatever happens next—remember you brought him here, and I stayed anyway."

Then the dark took her, and Orsen Okonkwo ran north through the night market with a traitor in his hand and the sound, behind him, of a motorcycle engine turning onto a street where no motorcycles were allowed.
