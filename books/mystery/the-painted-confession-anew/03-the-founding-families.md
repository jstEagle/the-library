## 3. The Founding Families

There is a particular kind of silence in a parish register office, and I have come to believe it is not the absence of sound but the presence of listening. The ledgers sit in their cases like sleeping animals, and if you work among them long enough—as I have, four years now—you begin to feel that they are not asleep at all. They are waiting for someone to ask the right question.

I had been asking the wrong one. For two days after finding Elowen Pryce's diary among Peter Voss's effects, I had been asking *how*—how the entries continued past her disappearance, how the handwriting could be verified and still be impossible, how a dead woman kept writing. It was a good question. It was also, I now understand, a decoy. The diary itself was the answer to a different question entirely: *who*. Who had needed her gone badly enough that thirty years later, an old organist was still paying for it.

So I did what archivists do when the documents confuse them. I made a map.

Not of places—of people. I spread four sheets of acid-free paper across my worktable and began, with the patient tedium that is both my profession and my only reliable social skill, to chart the founding families of St. Aldhelm's.

The Ashcombes first. Their name appears in the cornerstone ledger from 1887, and then again and again with the regularity of a heartbeat: roof repairs in 1903, the lychgate in 1921, the restoration fund through two wars. By 1994—the year everything stopped making sense—the family fortune had narrowed to a single surviving line: Edmund Ashcombe, land agent, treasurer of the Fabric Committee, a man whose signature I had seen so often on donation receipts that I could reproduce it from memory, down to the little flourish he added to the crossbar of his *d*, like a man doffing his hat.

Then the Hales. Three generations of clergy, which in a parish like this amounts to aristocracy with better Latin. Reverend Alistair Hale held the living in 1994—a broad, comfortable man by all accounts, beloved, the sort of vicar whose sermons ran long because no one ever dared check his watch. And there was a son. Julian Hale. His name surfaced in the records only glancingly: a confirmation photograph, a mention in the parish magazine as having "returned to assist his father's ministry," a single line in the Fabric Committee minutes noting his attendance. I wrote his name on my map and drew a small circle around it, the way you'd mark a spot where the ground felt hollow underfoot.

And finally the Vosses—which gave me pause, because I had assumed Voss was simply the organist's surname, unremarkable, unconnected. But the registers told another story. The Vosses had given the organ. All of it: the case, the pipes, the endowment for the tuner. In 1994 the family consisted of Margaret Voss, widowed, and her daughter Ruth—and Ruth, according to a baptismal record I nearly missed, had married a Pryce. Which made Peter Voss, Ruth's son, first cousin to a woman who had vanished without a trace and whose diary he had apparently spent his last years keeping in a locked drawer beneath his music scores.

I sat back and looked at what I'd drawn. Three families, their lines converging on 1994 like roads funneling into a tunnel. And at the mouth of the tunnel, a fourth name, written in pencil because I hadn't yet earned the right to ink it: *Elowen Pryce — domestic?*

The question mark bothered me. So I went looking for her employment, and found it scattered across the archives like breadcrumbs someone had dropped in haste. The Ashcombe household accounts, 1991–1994: wages paid to "E. Pryce, general help." The vestry cleaning rota, same years: "Miss Pryce, Thursdays." A letter of reference in Reverend Hale's own hand, praising her discretion—"a quality," he wrote, "of real value in one who moves through private rooms."

I read that sentence three times. *Discretion.* In most references it means she didn't gossip. In this one, written by a man whose son attended meetings about the cathedral's fabric and finances, it sounded almost like a job description. Or a warning.

Elowen hadn't worked for the founding families. She had worked *between* them—a thread passing through all three needles, present in the Ashcombe kitchen, the Hale rectory, the Voss house, the vestry, the sacristy. If anyone in St. Aldhelm's parish knew where every body was buried, metaphorically speaking, it was the woman who dusted around them.

And then they sealed the crypt, and she vanished, and everyone agreed these two facts were unrelated.

Everyone agreed very quickly, as it happens. That was the thing I noticed next, once I started reading the sealing documents properly instead of cataloguing them. The order to seal came eleven days after Elowen Pryce was declared missing. Eleven days—inquest pending, police inquiry open, a young woman's whereabouts unknown. And yet the paperwork moved through the diocesan channels with the smoothness of oiled machinery. Four signatures authorized it. Edmund Ashcombe. Alistair Hale. Margaret Voss. And a fourth, a solicitor named Garreth Mowbray, acting for "the combined trustees."

Four signatures. Three families. One missing servant girl whom none of them, in any deposition I could find, had ever been formally asked about.

I want to be fair to them. I have spent four years among these records, and fairness is a discipline like any other. Grief makes people strange; fear makes them fast. Perhaps they sealed the crypt because a death had happened there—Peter Voss's father had fallen down those same stairs in 1989, the first "accident"—and grief-bewildered families close doors. It happens.

But here is what an archivist knows that a grieving family does not: paper remembers its own hesitations. And there were no hesitations anywhere in this file. No marginal query from the diocesan registrar. No second draft. The sealing authorization reads like a document written once, correctly, by people who already knew exactly what they wanted and why. You can fake grief. You cannot fake the absence of doubt.

* * *

That night I took the diary home, which I am aware is against about six regulations, and read it again—not for the impossible dates this time, but for the woman between the lines.

The early entries are ordinary and rather lovely. Elowen Pryce wrote the way some people breathe, without effort or audience. She described the Ashcombe kitchen ("Mrs. Ashcombe counts the sugar cubes, I counted nine going in and eight coming out, and said nothing, which is my whole art"). She described the rectory ("the Reverend prays beautifully and listens badly"). She described the cathedral itself with the unsentimental tenderness of someone who cleaned it: "The nave at six in the morning holds the light like a cup held out. I mop toward the east window. It seems right to walk toward something while working."

But by the spring of 1994, the entries change. They shorten. They develop a habit of ending mid-thought, as though she'd heard a floorboard. And the margins—those coded annotations I'd noticed before, little clusters of letters and numbers that looked like nothing—begin to appear more frequently, always beside entries about the families.

One entry from March 1994 I copied out entire, because it would not leave me alone:

> *Mr. Ashcombe was shouting in the study again tonight. I only brought the coffee, but the door was ajar and the house is old and carries voices the way the church carries singing. He said the man from the council "would be dealt with" and Mr. Hale's son said something low I couldn't catch, and then Mr. Ashcombe laughed—not a nice laugh, the kind that has teeth in it—and said, "Between the four of us, nothing leaves this parish." I set down the coffee and left before my hands shook. Some things you are not meant to hear. The trouble is, once heard, they live in you like a stone in a shoe.*

> *[margin: 4-11-3 / H-s / W-n]*

*Nothing leaves this parish.* I set the diary down and looked at my map, pinned above the worktable, three family names converging on a tunnel. Between the four of us. Four signatures on the sealing order. Four names, one organism.

I had been hired, four years ago, to keep their records. Catalogue, conserve, index. A quiet outsider with quiet hands. And I had been good at it—good enough that DI Marsh once told me, in the tone of a man conceding a chess game he didn't know he was playing, that I noticed things the police budget couldn't afford to notice. I had solved three small deaths the parish preferred to call accidents, quietly, without credit, because credit in St. Aldhelm's is a currency the founding families mint themselves.

It had never occurred to me until tonight that my competence might be visible. That the same organism which sealed a crypt eleven days after a girl disappeared might keep watch on the archive the way I keep watch on it. That "discretion of real value in one who moves through private rooms" might describe me too.

I looked at the margin code again. *4-11-3.* Four signatures? Eleventh day? Third family? Or a date—April eleventh? I had cracked harder ciphers than this, but every cipher I'd broken before belonged to strangers. This one belonged to a household. To the parish. Possibly, if the handwriting analysis held, to a woman who had been dead for thirty years and was still, somehow, writing.

Outside my flat, the cathedral clock struck eleven, and I realized with a small cold start that I had left the map pinned in the archive workroom, face up, names showing.

Tomorrow I would be in early. Earlier than anyone.

Probably.
