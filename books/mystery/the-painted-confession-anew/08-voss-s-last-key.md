## 8. Voss's Last Key

The keyring had been sitting in an evidence bag in my office for eleven days, and I confess I'd been avoiding it the way one avoids a letter from the tax office—knowing it contains nothing good and knowing, too, that it will not open itself.

Peter Voss's keys. Four of them, plus a fob shaped like a treble clef that had lost its argument with gravity sometime around the Blair government. Three were ordinary: the vestry door, the organ loft, his flat on Chandler's Row. The fourth was brass, older than the others, unmarked except for a small stamped cross near the bow—and a patina of disuse that told me it hadn't turned a lock in years.

I laid them out on my desk under the lamp, in a neat row, because order is what I do when I am frightened. It is also what I do when I am not frightened. There is a reason people call archivists cold.

Here is what I knew by then: Peter Voss had fallen down the crypt stairs on the fourth of March, and everyone from the coroner to the flower guild had accepted it as one more instance of an elderly man and a steep staircase failing to negotiate. But I had spent three years of my life quietly proving that St. Aldhelm's had a habit of producing fatal accidents at a rate no actuarial table could love. A scaffolder in 1987. A verger in 1991. A stonemason in 1993. All falls. All men who had, in some small way, asked inconvenient questions about the founding families or their money. I had solved those three in silence, filed my findings where only I would ever look, and told myself that competence was its own reward, which is the sort of lie you tell yourself when you have no one to tell the truth to.

And now a fourth accident, thirty-one years after the third, in a crypt that had been sealed since 1994.

Sealed crypts do not require keys. Sealed crypts are not descended by elderly organists on Tuesday afternoons. Unless, of course, someone has a key.

I took the brass key down to the north transept that evening, after the last of the volunteers had gone, because there is a door behind the reredos—a narrow thing, painted the same grey as the stone so that your eye slides off it—that I had catalogued as "secondary access, disused" without ever once testing the claim. Archivists are supposed to be thorough. We are also supposed to survive our employers, and sometimes those aims conflict.

The key fit. Of course it did. It turned with the reluctant groan of metal that had been waiting decades for exactly this indignity, and the door swung inward onto a spiral stair hugging the wall like something ashamed of itself.

I stood at the top for a long moment, listening. The cathedral does its evening sounds—the tick of cooling stone, a pigeon relocating somewhere in the clerestory, the great silence underneath all of it that old buildings wear like a coat. Nothing else. I went down.

* * *

The stair let out into the crypt's undercroft, below the sealed main entrance, in a low vaulted chamber that the 1994 sealing order had apparently considered beneath the dignity of being bricked up. Which was either carelessness or confidence, and having met the families who signed that order, I would have bet on confidence.

There was a smell down there—dust and candle grease and something faintly organic, the way old cloth smells when it has been folded too long. My torch found stacked chairs, a broken processional cross, and then, against the far wall, a wooden crate with P.V. stencilled on the side in fresh marker pen.

Fresh. As in recent. As in Peter Voss had carried things down here within weeks of his death.

I crouched and opened it, and I will admit my hands were not steady, because by then I had done the arithmetic I should have done days ago. Elowen Pryce, vanished 1994. Peter Voss, born 1958. Pryce was her maiden name; her brother Thomas had married a Voss. I had the parish registers; I had simply never put the two lines of the family tree next to each other, because why would I? Nobody had told me to look.

Peter Voss wasn't investigating Elowen Pryce. He was her nephew. He had grown up with a story about an aunt who ran away, or drowned, or whatever version the family had been fed, and somewhere in his last years he had started pulling threads. And someone had killed him for it—not to protect a secret about her death, but to prevent proof of her life.

That distinction matters. You kill to hide a murder. You also kill to keep a survivor hidden, if enough people's respectability depends on her staying dead.

The crate held, first, letters. Bundles of them, tied with green garden twine, addressed in a hand I recognized before I consciously did—because I had spent weeks inside that diary, and handwriting is like a voice; you know it across a crowded room. Elowen's hand. Letters dated 1994 through 1996, then thinning out, then one every few years, the most recent postmarked fourteen months ago. Addressed to Chandler's Row. She had written to him. She had been alive and writing to him while the parish lit candles for the repose of her soul.

I did not read them all. I read enough. Enough to see she never gave an address, never a surname beyond the one she'd shed, only reassurances—I'm well, don't look for me, please stop looking—and, in the later ones, a tenderness that made me feel like a trespasser in my own archive.

Beneath the letters: a cloth, folded into a square the size of a hymnal, brown at its centre with a stain that thirty years had turned the colour of strong tea but which I did not need a forensic team to identify. Blood. Kept. Deliberately kept, the way you keep evidence when you believe no one will ever believe you.

And beneath that, a single sheet of paper, folded twice, soft as cloth itself from handling.

A list of names.

Seven names, in Elowen's writing. Not the founding families—those I knew by heart now, Ashcombe, Hale, Voss, the whole civic pantheon. These were smaller names. A gardener. A choir mother. Two vergers. People who had been on the cathedral payroll in 1994 and had each, I realized as I read the annotations beside them, received something: a payment, a reference, a quiet word that secured a silence. Beside each name, a date and a figure. It was, essentially, an invoice. Thirty years of hush money, itemized in a young woman's careful hand, because she had overheard every arrangement through those whispering arches and written it all down before she ran.

She hadn't just survived. She'd built a bomb and left it under the floor.

I sat down on a broken chair in a sealed crypt under a cathedral full of dead benefactors and laughed, once, which echoed in a way I did not enjoy.

* * *

I want to say I was careful after that. I photographed everything, replaced the papers exactly as found—or nearly exactly; I am honest with myself if no one else—locked the door, and went home via the long route like a woman in a thriller, checking reflections in shop windows. I even texted DI Marsh, who has graduated in my esteem from "man who dismissed me" to "man who grudgingly admits I exist," a message reading only: *Found it. Crypt secondary stair. Come tomorrow, bring warrant.*

He replied at 11:40 p.m.: *Tomorrow early. Don't go back down alone.* Which was reassuring, right up until I noticed he hadn't asked what *it* was, and I lay awake deciding whether that meant trust or whether I was becoming paranoid, and concluded that after four deaths and a staged drowning, paranoia was less a flaw than a professional qualification.

What I did not anticipate was the morning.

St. Aldhelm's does not close. In thirty years it has closed for two royal funerals and a boiler explosion, and the boiler explosion was arguably more mourned. So when I arrived at nine to find the west doors locked, a laminated sign taped to the glass—*CLOSED TODAY FOR ESSENTIAL MAINTENANCE, APOLOGIES*—and no maintenance van, no workmen, no vicar, I understood the message perfectly.

It was not a sign. It was a sentence, and I was its object.

Someone knew what I'd found. Someone had been down that secondary stair recently enough to know the crate was disturbed, or had watched me carry my torch past the reredos, or—less flattering to me, more likely—had simply been watching the archive the way I watch it, and had finally noticed what I was building. For weeks I'd had the uneasy sense of a second reader in my records, another set of eyes moving through the same files, patient as mine. I'd told myself it was Marta, whose cleaning cart appeared in corridors I'd just left, though Marta's eyes, when they met mine, held something other than menace. Something almost like hope, which is a strange thing to find in the face of a woman who has never spoken to you.

Whoever it was, they had closed my cathedral. They had taken a building that has stood open through wars and scandals and one memorable incident involving the Ashcombe memorial fountain, and shut it like a hand closing over a coin.

I stood on the steps in the drizzle and thought about the list in my bag—no, not in my bag; I had learned that much. The list was in a place no one would think to look, which I will not record here, because I have finally absorbed the lesson of this entire affair: write nothing down that you cannot afford to be read.

My phone buzzed. Marsh: *Just heard the cathedral's shut. That's not maintenance. Where are you?*

I looked up at the tower, grey against grey, at the windows where the saints kept their painted faces turned politely away. Somewhere inside, in the dark above the crypt, the whispering gallery hung silent, holding whatever it heard next.

*I'm outside,* I typed. *And I think we're out of tomorrows.*

The doors, at that moment, unlocked—from the inside.
