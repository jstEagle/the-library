## 9. Confession in the Crypt

There is a particular kind of forgery that requires no skill at all, only patience. You steep new paper in old tea, you press it between boards until it forgets its own age, and then—and this is the part they never show you in the films—you wait for a man's vanity to do the rest.

I had been waiting three days.

The forged page sat in the exhibition case in the north transept, labeled with the cheerful bureaucratic honesty of a working archive: *Elowen Pryce diary — final entry (facsimile), pending authentication.* Pending authentication. That was the bait. A real archivist would have said *facsimile* and meant it. I said it and meant a trap. The page named him—Julian Hale-Ashcombe, benefactor, pillar of the parish, the man whose family money had poured the crypt floor and whose father's signature had sealed it—in Elowen's reconstructed hand: *He came for me tonight. He knows about Tom. He said the families protect their own. If anything happens to me, look to J.H.*

I had written it by lamplight in Marta's supply cupboard, my hand shaking so badly on the first attempt that I burned the paper in the boiler and started again. Marta watched me from the doorway, cloth in hand, saying nothing. She never says anything. But when I finished the second attempt she took it from me, held it to the light like a jeweler, and nodded once—the nod of a woman who had spent thirty years learning to read documents she was never allowed to touch.

"You knew him," I said. "In 1994."

Her eyes went to the corner where the cleaning trolley stood, as if the answer might be filed there. Then she mouthed one word. *He sang.*

Of course he did. Julian Hale-Ashcombe, choirboy, golden voice, photographed every Christmas in the parish newsletter with his arm around his father's shoulder. And Elowen Pryce, organist's assistant, who would have stood in the gallery above while he sang below, close enough to hear everything—including, perhaps, what he whispered between verses.

The cathedral closed early that evening. "Structural inspection," said the notice taped to the great doors, in handwriting I recognized as Julian's own—he signs his donations with the same flourished H that hangs over the west porch. Structural inspection. Thirty years ago the structure inspected was a woman who knew too much, and the remedy was a sealed door and a missing person file nobody opened twice.

At six o'clock I let myself into the crypt by the secondary stair, Voss's key cold in my pocket like a stone I'd swallowed. I arranged the scene the way you arrange evidence for a jury: diary open on the ledge beside the crypt door, lamp lit, my chair angled so that anyone descending would see me before they saw the dark behind me. Then I did the hardest thing an archivist can do.

I waited without reading anything.

Footsteps came at half past seven. Not hurried. Julian Hale-Ashcombe has never hurried anywhere in his life; hurry is for people who fear being caught. He came down the spiral stair with a torch in one hand and something heavier in the other, and he stopped at the bottom and shone the light full in my face.

"Miss Zhou." His voice was warm. It had always been warm; warmth was how the parish forgave him things. "You shouldn't be down here alone. It isn't safe."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. It hasn't been since 1994."

He moved the torch to the diary. I watched him read his own initials in a dead woman's hand, watched something behind his face rearrange itself—the benefactor folding away, and underneath, the boy from the choir gallery, the one who had learned young that some people are furniture and furniture doesn't testify.

"That's a fake," he said.

"Probably," I said. "But you're going to tell me which parts are true."

He laughed. It echoed beautifully; this building flatters every voice, even guilty ones. "You've been reading too much marginalia, Miss Zhou. Coded scribbles. Old grief. There's nothing down here but bones and your imagination."

"Then why are you holding a crowbar?"

He looked at it as if surprised to find it in his hand. "For the seal," he said smoothly. "In case you'd damaged it."

"The seal was cut three days ago. By someone with Voss's spare key." I stood up. My legs were not entirely convinced about standing, but standing is mostly a decision. "Peter Voss wasn't investigating Elowen, Mr. Hale-Ashcombe. He was her nephew. He wanted proof she was alive. And on the fourteenth of March, he asked you whether you remembered her. And you pushed him down these stairs."

The torch beam wavered. Just once. Just enough.

"He fell," Julian said.

"People say that a lot in this parish. It's practically the liturgy." I took a step toward him, toward the crypt door, because the acoustics needed my voice at the right place—I had tested it with Marta two nights before, whispering from the door while she stood in the niche above and confirmed she could hear me counting backward from ten. "Your father sealed this crypt to bury a whistleblower's name. Your family wrote the alibis—all those identical sentences, all that rehearsed wool. And Elowen heard you planning it through that gallery up there, and she chose to die on paper rather than die in fact, and she paid thirty years for the choice. All I want is the truth. Say it here. This room keeps secrets better than any archive I've ever worked in."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I publish the page, authenticated or not, and let the parish do what parishes do."

Something went out of his face then—not the mask, the thing under the mask. He set down the torch so the light lay between us like a border, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register men use when they believe no one living will ever repeat it.

"You want the truth? Here it is. Her brother was going to the papers about the building funds—your precious Reverend Hale's restoration fund, half of it never touched a brick. Tom Pryce was going to burn it all down, and Father said the families don't survive scandal, we survive by *closing doors*. So we closed one." He smiled, and it was the smile from the newsletter. "She was supposed to be a warning. A frightened girl who ran away. But she fought, and there was blood, and Father handled it, Father always handled it—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Voss was worse. Coming back after all these years, digging, asking about her like she mattered. She didn't matter. None of them matter. This building matters. I built half of it."

"You buried the other half," I said.

And then, because terror and triumph apparently share a staircase, I heard it: footsteps overhead, deliberate, unhurried, crossing the gallery above the crypt. Julian heard them too. He looked up, and for the first time in thirty years of closing doors, Julian Hale-Ashcombe heard a door opening.

"Mr. Hale-Ashcombe." DI Marsh's voice came down through the arches, carried on stone the way it had carried Elowen's whispers, Voss's last questions, every confession this building had ever swallowed and kept. "This is Detective Inspector Marsh. I've been in this niche for forty minutes, and the architecture has been taking notes the whole time. Everything you said just now is admissible, recorded, and witnessed. Please put down the crowbar."

He didn't put down the crowbar. For one long moment the torchlight trembled between us and I thought—quite calmly, which surprised me—that this was how archivists died, defending paperwork in a hole in the ground. Then Marta stepped out of the dark of the secondary stair behind him, and she was holding not a weapon but a rag and a bottle of brass polish, the tools of thirty years' invisibility, and she looked at Julian Hale-Ashcombe with eyes he suddenly, horribly recognized.

He made a sound I will hear for the rest of my life. Not a word. A recognition.

"She's dead," he whispered. "You're dead. I *saw*—"

Marta set the polish down on the crypt step, very gently, the way she sets down everything, and spoke aloud for what I suspect was only the second time in thirty years.

"My name," she said, and the arches carried it up through the whole dark spine of the cathedral, "is Elowen Pryce."

Above us, boots were already on the stairs.

* * *

They took him up through the main stair, past the exhibition case, past the forged page that had done its work and could now retire honestly into my files labeled *fabricated evidence, justified, see attached statement.* Marsh shook my hand at the top of the steps with the expression of a man revising thirty years of opinion in real time.

"The recording," he said. "The arches. How did you know it would carry?"

"I didn't know," I said. "I listened. It's the same job, really. Everything in this building is a document. Most people just never learn to read the walls."

Below us, faintly, Elowen was still standing at the foot of the stairs, looking up at the vaulting as though seeing it for the first time—as though hearing, perhaps, what it had kept for her all these years. When I came back down, she was touching the crypt door, the sealed one, the one that had cost her everything.

"It opens tomorrow," she said. Not a question. A sentence she had been drafting for thirty years.

"Yes," I said. "First thing. I'll bring the inventory sheets."

She almost smiled. "You'll catalog my grave?"

"Somebody should," I said. "It's had thirty years of misfiling."

And that was when we both heard it—a small sound from inside the sealed crypt, beyond the door, soft as paper settling in an old drawer. Something shifting in the dark. Something that had been waiting, patient as any archive, to be found.
