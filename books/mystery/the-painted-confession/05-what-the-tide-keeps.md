## 5. What the Tide Keeps

The harbormaster's office still stood at the end of the seawall, though it hadn't been a working office in years. The sign — SALTMEDE HARBOUR AUTHORITY, the E long since flaked away so it read SALT M D — hung by one bolt, swinging in a wind that came off the water with the smell of iron and old rope. Below it, a smaller sign had been screwed over the door at some point: CORLISS. TIDE TABLES & GENERAL INQUIRIES.

General inquiries. I could have laughed. I'd been making general inquiries for two days and getting nothing but doors that closed like mouths.

I knocked anyway.

"Open," said a voice from inside, gravel over gravel.

The room smelled of diesel and tea gone cold. Charts papered the walls, most of them yellowed past legibility, and a tide clock on the far wall ticked through its slow lunar pantomime. A man sat behind a desk built for someone twice his size, or perhaps he had shrunk into it over the years the way driftwood shrinks — Corliss, presumably, though he was nothing like I'd imagined. I'd pictured a watchman: narrow, nervous, eyes that skittered. This man was broad as a bollard, with forearms mapped by old rope burns and a face weathered down to its useful features. His eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and they did not skitter anywhere. They settled on me and stayed, patient as ballast.

"You're the one asking after Lena Oduya," he said.

Not *good morning*. Not *who's asking*. He'd been expecting me, or someone shaped like me.

"Word travels," I said.

"In Saltmere, word doesn't travel. Word sits where it's put." He gestured at the chair across from him — a captain's chair, brass-studded, absurdly formal for a shack full of dead charts. "Sit. You'll want to write this down."

"I don't need to."

Something moved behind his eyes then — not quite surprise, more like a man testing a floorboard and finding it solid when he'd expected rot. "Photographic memory," he said. "That's what they say about you. The insurance man."

"They say a lot of things about me."

"They say you never forget a page. Never forget a face. Never forget what someone told you, word for word, ten years later." He poured tea from a thermos into two enamel mugs, pushed one across the desk. It was hot. He'd made it recently, which meant he'd known I was coming before I'd decided myself. "Then you'd best be careful what you hear, Mr. Kavanagh. Some things are heavier than they look."

"I've carried heavy things before."

"Aye." He drank. Set the mug down with a click that sounded final. "Well. Ask your questions, then. I've got nothing but time and tide, and the tide keeps better schedule."

* * *

He talked for nearly an hour, and I let him, because the first rule of interviewing is that people tell you who they are in how they tell you what happened. And Corliss told it like scripture. Like he'd rehearsed it in front of a mirror, or worse, like someone had written it out for him and he'd learned it by heart until the heart part wore smooth.

"Lena came down that night around eleven," he said. "Wanted the skiff. I told her the water was wrong — spring tide coming, wind backing round to the northeast. She said she'd be back by two. I said the pier was Meridian property and I couldn't hand out boats after hours. She said—" and here he paused, and the pause was exactly as long as a pause should be, "—she said she'd take her chances."

"What did she look like?"

He blinked. Of all the questions, that one landed off-script. "Look like?"

"That night. At the office. Was she frightened? Wet? Carrying anything?"

"Carrying her bag. Paint box, brushes. She always had it." His thumb moved along the rim of his mug, a small circular motion, like a man polishing something invisible. "Frightened — no. Determined. There's a difference, and you see it enough times in this job. Frightened people look behind them. Determined people look ahead. She looked ahead the whole way down the pier."

"And you gave her the boat."

"I gave her the boat." He said it flatly, without inflection, the way a man recites a line he's said so many times it has lost all texture. "And she went out toward the point, and the wind backed round early, and that was that. They found the skiff on the flats below Gull Rock three days later. Found her the day after."

"The official finding was accidental drowning. Capsized in a squall."

"That's the finding."

"That's not what I asked."

For the first time, the patience in his face flickered — just at the edges, like a lamp in wind. "It's what I know, Mr. Kavanagh. I watched her go out. I didn't watch what happened after. Nobody did. That's the sea for you. It takes what it takes and it doesn't leave witnesses."

*Except it does,* I thought. *The sea leaves everything. That's the whole problem with the sea.*

But I said none of it. Instead I asked about Meridian Freight, and here — here was the strange thing — he opened up like a lock.

* * *

"Meridian bought the pier in '04," he said. "Lease, then freehold. They ran coastal freight out of Saltmere — fish meal, scrap, container parts. Brought maybe forty jobs. For a town that was dying on its feet, forty jobs is forty sermons." He turned the tide clock's dial absently, though it needed no turning. "They were good employers, near enough. Paid on time. Fixed the pilings. Put lights on the walkway that actually worked."

"And the town loved them for it."

"The town needed them for it. Love's a different animal." He looked out the salt-fogged window at the harbor, gray under a sky the color of dishwater. "You want to understand Saltmere, understand this: we were a cannery town, and the canneries died, and Meridian was the only thing that floated in to fill the hole. When a drowning man gets thrown a plank, he doesn't ask whose ship it fell off."

"When did Lena start painting their buildings?"

His thumb stopped moving. Just for a second. Then resumed. "She painted everything. Walls, boats, the chandlery, the fish market. Woman couldn't leave a flat surface alone. Meridian commissioned her for the warehouse end wall — big harbor scene, ships coming home. Everyone thought it was grand."

"Did they pay her well?"

"Fair, I'd say."

"Would you say she was happy that year? Her last year?"

Now he set the mug down entirely, and leaned back, and studied me with those colorless eyes, and I understood that we had arrived at the place where the script ended — or rather, where the script had anticipated me and prepared a response.

"She was an artist," he said slowly. "Artists aren't happy. They're *occupied*. She was occupied." A beat. "If you're asking whether something was eating at her — I wouldn't know. People don't bring their troubles to the harbormaster. They bring them to the priest, or the bottle, or nobody."

"But you were here that night. You handed her the boat. You were the last person in Saltmere to speak with her."

"Yes."

"And in fifteen years, no one has ever asked you what she said."

"No one's ever asked me anything." He smiled, and it was the strangest smile I'd seen in Saltmere — not warm, not cold, but *placed*, like a buoy marking a channel. "Until you."

* * *

I walked the seawall afterward, because walking is how I think, and because I wanted to put distance between myself and that office before I examined what I'd collected there.

Here is what troubled me, and I want to be precise about it, because precision was the only inheritance my mother left me: Corliss had given me too much, too easily, too cleanly. Every other door in Saltmere had shut in my face. The landlady stopped mid-sentence when her son walked in. The publican suddenly remembered an appointment. The woman at the post office developed a sudden fascination with sorting mail that had already been sorted. But the harbormaster — the man who had physically handed Lena Oduya the boat she drowned from — talked for an hour without resistance.

People who have something to hide refuse. People who have something to protect deflect. People who have been *coached* perform.

Corliss performed. The pauses were right. The grief was right. The details were right — the paint box, the wind backing northeast, the skiff at Gull Rock. All of it verifiable, probably, against the inquest file. That was the craft of it. A lie built entirely out of true things is the only kind worth building, and whoever built this one knew the file cold.

But performance leaks. It leaked in three places.

First: he said she went out "toward the point." The inquest — the version I'd reconstructed from memory, from the file with its altered pages — placed the wreck at Gull Rock, which is northwest. The point is south. A man who genuinely watched a boat leave would have said the direction without thinking. A man reciting a report says the destination, because reports record destinations.

Second: he said the wind "backed round early." Backing means shifting counterclockwise. On that date, fifteen years ago, the recorded wind veered — clockwise — according to the weather log I'd memorized along with everything else in the claim file. Small thing. Invisible thing. Unless you were me.

Third, and this was the one that sat in my chest like swallowed stone: he never once said her name with weight. Fifteen years, last man to see her alive, and every mention of *Lena Oduya* came out level, load-bearing, rehearsed. Grief has grain in it, like wood. You can sand grief smooth but the grain still shows under light. His had no grain at all. Either fifteen years had worn it perfectly flat —

— or there had never been any grief to wear.

* * *

I stopped at the rail where the seawall curved toward the old pier. Meridian's pier, once. Now a rusted skeleton with gulls disputing the crossbeams, the warehouse at its root boarded up, Lena's mural somewhere on its far wall behind plywood and padlock. The tide was going out, peeling back from the mudflats in long silver sheets, exposing the ribs of old wrecks and the black teeth of pilings. Things the water kept, offered up briefly, twice a day, to anyone patient enough to look.

A figure stood at the pier's end. Small, still, hooded against the wind. Watching the water.

I watched him for a while, and he either didn't notice me or didn't care, and there was something in the absolute quality of his stillness that reminded me of Corliss's smile — placed, deliberate, meant to be seen. Or meant to seem unseen while being seen. In a town that rehearsed its silence, even loneliness might be theater.

When I looked again five minutes later, the figure was gone. No boat had left the pier. No footprints led back along the mud — the ground was too soft; there should have been prints. There were none.

I stood a long time at the rail, doing what I do, which is remembering. The tide clock in Corliss's office, ticking its slow circle. The mug set down with that final click. *Some things are heavier than they look.* The wind log. The point versus Gull Rock. The letter in my coat pocket, the second one, with its fragment of painted image and its promise: *the rest is where she left it.*

Where she left it. Not where the sea took it. Where she *left* it — deliberate, active, chosen. Someone had written that sentence knowing exactly what it meant. And someone in Saltmere was feeding me a version of the night designed to close the case before I opened it: poor girl, bad weather, sad accident, go home.

The trouble with feeding a man with a photographic memory is that he keeps everything. Including the inconsistencies. Especially the inconsistencies.

The tide kept sliding out, further than I'd yet seen it go, uncovering a long dark shape in the mud below the pier — a hull, maybe, or a chest, or just a rock wearing the sea's black varnish. Tomorrow it would be covered again. Everything here worked that way: surfaced, submerged, surfaced.

Corliss had told me his story like a man setting a table for guests he expected. Which raised the only question that mattered:

Who had cooked the meal?

Behind me, on the seawall, someone had chalked a small mark on the granite at hip height — fresh, rain-smudged at the edges, no more than a day old. A key. Drawn in white, simple as a child's drawing, pointing down the pier toward the water.

I photographed it. Then I stood in the wind and understood, with the particular chill of a man who realizes the trap has been around his ankle since the moment he stepped off the bus: I wasn't investigating Saltmere.

Saltmere was investigating me.
