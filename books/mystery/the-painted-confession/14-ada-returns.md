## 14. Ada Returns

The knock came at six in the morning, and Vesper knew before he opened the door that it would change the shape of everything.

Not because of the sound. The sound was ordinary — three raps, knuckles not fist, the knock of someone raised to be polite even when furious. He knew because of what his memory had been doing all night: sorting, cross-referencing, refusing to sleep. Somewhere around four a.m. it had surfaced a detail from the boarding house register he'd glanced at two days ago and filed without thinking — a name signed in a hand that slanted left, that pressed hard enough to dent the page beneath.

*Ada Oduya. Room 4.*

She had been under his roof since Tuesday.

He opened the door and found a woman built like a verdict. Mid-forties, tall, gray threading her hair in a way that suggested she'd stopped fighting it around the time her sister died. She held a canvas satchel in one hand and a photograph in the other, and she looked at him the way auditors look at men who say they've never made a mistake.

"You're the investigator," she said. It wasn't a question. "You've been in my sister's cannery. You photographed the walls. And you didn't call me."

"I didn't know I should."

"You should have known the moment you saw the paintings." She stepped past him into the room without being invited, set the satchel down on the table, and turned. "Nobody photographs Lena's work like that. Like evidence. Like it *matters*. Tourists take pictures because it's pretty. You took pictures because you were counting."

Vesper closed the door. "You've been watching me."

"I've been *following* you." She said it without shame. "Since the first letter arrived at my apartment in Oakland. Same hand as the ones you got, I'd bet. Same paper. It said: *He's coming to Saltmere. Watch what he does with what she left.* So I bought a ticket, and I watched, and what you did with what she left was stand in the dark with a camera for four hours like a man praying."

"That's roughly what it was."

Something flickered across her face — not softness, exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman who had also spent four hours in the dark with her sister's ghosts.

"Sit down," she said, and then, catching herself, "sorry. I don't — people say I do that. Arrange rooms like I'm entitled to them."

"You are entitled to this one," Vesper said. "You've paid for it since Tuesday."

*

Ada had not slept well either, though her insomnia had a different architecture than his. His was made of images — the murals repeating behind his eyes, crates and coins and keys, the ledger assembling itself line by line like a sentence being dictated. Hers was made of a single sentence, fifteen years old, spoken by a police sergeant in a doorway while rain came down sideways: *Your sister went out at night alone, ma'am. These things happen.*

She had carried that sentence across the country and back twice. She had carried it through a divorce, through two jobs she was too good for, through a storage unit full of Lena's canvases that she couldn't bear to open and couldn't bear to sell. And then a letter had come in handwriting that stopped her heart on the sidewalk outside her own building, and she had understood that the sentence was not a conclusion. It was a wall. And walls are built by people, and people can be made to talk.

"You decode it yet?" she asked. "The pigment code. The objects."

"Mostly. Names, amounts, dates. Forty-one payments over three years. Corliss took the largest single sum — eleven thousand — but he wasn't the only one. The harbormaster's office, two police officers, the town clerk, the priest —" he watched her face for the flinch and found it — "half of Saltmere, Ada. The murals aren't a memorial. They're a *record*. She painted the bribery into the harbor scenes where nobody would look for it, and she did it in plain sight for three years, waiting for someone with eyes."

"And then she drowned."

"And then she drowned."

Ada turned away from him and stood at the window, where the gray morning was lifting off the water. When she spoke again her voice had changed — flatter, more controlled, the voice of a woman who had rehearsed this part on two airplanes and a bus.

"There's something you don't have yet. Something she left me."

*

*Ada, three weeks before the water took her, sat on the floor of her studio with her back against the drying rack and wrote a letter she never mailed.*

*Ada —*

*If you're reading this, I've been stupid or I've been brave, and knowing me it's both. There's a man at the cannery, the foreman, Dez Calloway. He's the only one in this whole salt-rotted town who isn't for sale. Meridian's been running loads over the limit off the old pier and paying everyone to look at the horizon, and I've been painting it — all of it, every name, every envelope — into the walls where they walk past it every day and see nothing but pretty boats. Dez is helping me. There's a chest. When it's done, when it's safe, I'm sending copies everywhere — the county paper, the insurance commission, the Coast Guard, everyone. And then this town can hate me openly, which will at least be more honest than the way they hate me now.*

*If something happens to me, it wasn't the tide. The tide doesn't take women who grew up swimming in it. Find Dez. Trust Dez. And don't trust anyone who was kind to me, because kindness is what they bought.*

*Your sister,*

*Lena*

Ada recited it from memory, standing at the window, and Vesper wrote nothing down because he would never need to. When she finished, the room was very quiet.

"Dez Calloway," Vesper said. "The foreman."

"Dead," Ada said. "That's what the town says. Left after the drowning, drank himself to death up the coast somewhere. There's no grave. There's no record. There's nothing, which is what I've spent fifteen years being told there was." She turned to face him. "But the letter says he helped her. The letter says *trust* him. And the man who wrote your confessions — Corliss — he was the pier watchman, wasn't he. He watched. He didn't act. Lena always said the town was full of watchers."

"Corliss didn't write those letters alone," Vesper said. "There's a second hand under his. Someone taught him what to confess. I've been chasing it through the town records — the penmanship, the phrasing, the way the letters use words like *ledger* and *witness* like a man quoting documents he's memorized." He paused. "A foreman who managed crews for fifteen years would have written a thousand work orders. His hand would be everywhere."

Ada was already moving, pulling the satchel open, and Vesper felt the strange vertigo of watching someone else do the thing he did — the compulsive, unglamorous labor of proof — and do it well.

"I pulled the cannery's employment filings before I left Oakland," she said. "State archive, microfilm, the works. Lena's name was on the 1989 crew roster as a muralist — commissioned, would you believe it, by Meridian Freight itself. They paid her to paint their walls." Her mouth twisted. "And three rows down, same roster —"

She laid a photocopied page on the table between them, and Vesper looked at the signature block and felt the ground tilt.

*Foreman: D. Calloway.*

The hand was angular, left-slanting, pressed hard enough to dent a page beneath. The same hand that had signed the boarding house register. The same hand that hovered, ghost-like, beneath every letter Corliss had ever sent him.

"He's alive," Vesper said. "He's been here. He signed the register at this boarding house the same week I did."

*

*Dez Calloway had been alive for a long time, and it had not gotten easier.*

*He kept the lighthouse at Gray's Point, forty minutes up the coast, and he kept it the way he did everything now — carefully, quietly, with the constant low hum of a man listening for footsteps. Fifteen years of listening. He'd gotten good at it. He'd gotten so good at it that he'd nearly convinced himself the footsteps were imaginary, that the chest in the pilings had rusted to nothing, that Lena Oduya's voice had finally stopped saying his name every night at three a.m.*

*Then the letters started coming back to him as consequences. The investigator in town. The photographs. The cannery. And last night, Corliss on his doorstep, soaked in rain and terror, saying: he decoded it, Dez. He decoded all of it. He knows about the second hand. He's coming for you next.*

*Dez had made coffee he didn't drink and stood at the lighthouse window watching the sea do its slow, patient arithmetic, and he had understood that the fifteen years were over. Not because he was ready. Because the tide, as the letters kept saying, was going to give up what it took.*

*He just hadn't expected it to give it up this fast. Or to find him first.*

*

"You're not going to the police," Ada said.

It was not a question either. Vesper was learning that about her — the Oduya sisters shared a grammar of certainty.

"Half the police took the money," he said. "And Hale from Meridian arrived two days ago offering me a consulting fee to close an old file. If I walk into the station with what we have, it goes into a drawer by nightfall."

"So what's the method, investigator? Because I've watched you for a week and your method is *wait*. Photograph, decode, wait. Lena waited too. She waited until she was sure, and the tide took her while she was being careful." Ada's voice cracked on the last word and she hated it, he could see her hating it, and she kept going anyway. "I'm not built for waiting."

"No," Vesper said. "You're built for the other thing. The thing I can't do."

"Which is?"

"Walk up to a stranger's door and be believed." He held up a hand before she could argue. "I have a memory that makes people uncomfortable. I recite things and they look at me like a machine that's learned to talk. But you — you're her sister. You have her chin, her hands, her fury. When you stand in front of this town and say *Lena Oduya was murdered and I can prove it*, they will believe you in a way they will never believe me. I'm the evidence. You're the witness. It only works if we're both standing there."

Ada looked at him for a long moment. The morning light came through the window and caught the gray in her hair, and something in her posture shifted — not softening, exactly. Reloading.

"Lena's letter said don't trust anyone who was kind to me," she said. "You haven't been kind to me. You've been useful. That I can work with."

"High praise."

"Don't fish for it." She was already repacking the satchel, fast and precise. "Where do we find him?"

"The letters point to the coast north of town. Corliss mentioned — twice, in different letters, which means it matters — a light that burns all night at Gray's Point. The keeper there is a recluse who came fifteen years ago and never gives his name to visitors. And the hand on your crew roster matches the second hand in the confessions." Vesper took his coat off the hook. "Dez Calloway is keeping a lighthouse."

Ada stopped packing. For a moment she was fifteen again, or twenty-five, or any of the ages at which her sister had called her at strange hours with a scheme in her voice, and the grief of it went through her like cold water.

"Then let's not keep him in the dark," she said. "He's had enough of that."

They went down the stairs together, past the landlady's door, which was shut, though Vesper noted the shadow of feet beneath it — Mrs. Pell listening, as she always listened, and this time perhaps deciding which side of the ledger she stood on. Out into the street, where the tide was going out further than it had in weeks, exposing the flats below the old pier in long silver sheets, and the whole town seemed to pause and look at the water.

"Three days," Vesper said, checking the almanac folded in his pocket. "Spring tide. The lowest ebb in six months. Whatever Lena hid out there, we'll be able to reach it."

Ada looked at the flats, then at him, and nodded once — the nod of a partnership being signed, not in ink, but in purpose.

"Then we'd better find Dez Calloway first," she said. "Because if we can reach it, so can Meridian."

They walked north along the coast road, the lighthouse waiting at the end of it like a sentence neither of them had finished reading.
