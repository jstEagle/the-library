## 6. The Anniversary Invitation

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like a joke. Tuesdays are for errands, for dentist appointments, for the small bureaucratic indignities of an ordinary life. Nobody stages a revelation on a Tuesday. And yet there it was, wedged between a water bill and a flyer for a gutter-cleaning service, sitting on my doormat like a swan in a flock of pigeons.

Heavy cream stock. Deckled edges. My name embossed in copperplate script that had been pressed into the paper by an actual hand, with an actual die, the old way. I've received exactly two invitations like this in my life — one to a publisher's gala I skipped, one to a wedding I regretted skipping — and both of them had been addressed by machines. This was different. This was *personal*.

I stood in my doorway in yesterday's shirt, holding it, and I didn't open it for a full minute. That's the thing nobody tells you about being hunted: the worst part isn't the moment you see the predator. It's the moment you realize you've been carrying the bait around all along, and you have to decide whether to bite.

I bit. Of course I bit. I'm a podcaster. Biting is the whole job.

*Mr. Bram Marchetti requests the pleasure of your company at the Hollis Legacy Dinner, commemorating thirty years of the Aldous Hollis Foundation for the Arts. Saturday, the ninth of November. Seven o'clock. The Hollis House, Blackwater Point.*

R.S.V.P.

And beneath the printed card, on a separate slip of paper in the same elegant hand, a handwritten line:

*You've been working so hard on this story. It would be a shame to miss the ending.*

I sat down on my own front steps. The morning was cold enough to see my breath, and the street was doing its usual Tuesday pantomime — the mail carrier two doors down, the woman with the terrier, the kid on the electric scooter who I was fairly sure was dealing something. All of it utterly indifferent to the fact that someone had just slipped a hand into my life through the mail slot and patted me on the head.

Because I recognized the handwriting.

Not from anything recent. From 1994. From a letter in the original case file — the one Detective Ferran had let me photograph page by page in a conference room that smelled like burnt coffee and institutional despair. A letter from a "concerned citizen" that had helped steer the investigation toward Danny Voss in the first place. A letter that three separate document examiners had quietly flagged as probable forgery, and that the prosecution had quietly ignored, because it pointed where everyone already wanted to point.

The letter had been signed *A Friend of the Gallery*. The hand was fluid, confident, with a distinctive flourish on the *F* that looped back through itself like a noose.

The same hand had just invited me to dinner.

* * *

I did what I always do when I can't think straight: I made a timeline. It's a trick I learned from Ferran, actually, though I'd never tell her that. Get the facts on paper, in order, and let the shape of the thing emerge. Facts don't lie. People lie, documents lie, memories lie, but a timeline just sits there being true at you until you're ready to look.

I spread it across my kitchen table. The murders — three of them, thirty years ago, at the Hollis Gallery during a private exhibition. The confession of Danny Voss, twenty-six years old, a handyman with a ninth-grade education and a lawyer he'd never met before arraignment. The recantation, eighteen months later, delivered in a letter the courts ruled inadmissible. The death, eleven years into a life sentence, of a heart attack that a decent prison doctor might have caught two years earlier. The case closed, sealed, and shelved.

And now: the riverside body wearing my jacket. The staging that quoted my unpublished outline beat for beat. The break-in at my studio where nothing was stolen and everything was read. And now this.

The invitation wasn't from the Hollis family. I checked. I called the number on the Foundation's public listing and got a cheerful woman named Dot who confirmed that yes, the Legacy Dinner was real, yes, it was the thirtieth anniversary, and yes, invitations had gone out — but to a curated list of about two hundred donors, trustees, and civic luminaries. She checked my name twice. She checked it a third time. "I don't see a Marchetti," she said, with the mild apology of someone who deals with disappointed people all day. "But these things always have room. Are you perhaps affiliated with a donor?"

"I'm affiliated with the truth," I said, and immediately hated myself for it, and hung up before she could respond.

So the invitation was real, but my invitation was a forgery. Someone had gotten hold of the design, the stock, the engraver's die or a very good facsimile, and put my name on a list I was never meant to be on. Which meant the killer had access not just to my files and my home, but to the Hollis family's inner machinery. Or was *part* of the Hollis family's inner machinery.

I sat with that for a long time, watching the steam come off my coffee in the cold kitchen, and I noticed something happening in my chest that I didn't like and couldn't stop.

I was *excited*.

That's the shameful confession at the center of this whole story, so I may as well put it here, in the middle, where it belongs: when I understood that the killer was toying with me — that I wasn't a bystander to this thing but a character in it, maybe even the co-lead — some animal part of my brain lit up like a marquee. Thirty years I've chased other people's endings. I've built a career, a modestly comfortable one, on standing at the edge of other families' craters and describing the shape of the hole. And here was someone offering me a seat at the table. A role. An *ending*, hand-delivered, engraved.

The better part of me — the part that had spent a sleepless week looking at Danny Voss's prison letters, at his sister's red-rimmed patience, at the photograph of a man who died recanting into a void — the better part of me knew exactly what that excitement was worth. It was worth exactly what the Hollis family had paid for it. Nothing. Less than nothing. Excitement is the sugar they coat the trap with.

I called Odile Ferran.

* * *

She came over instead of having me come in, which told me everything about where I officially stood. Detectives don't make house calls to witnesses. They make house calls to suspects they're being polite to.

She sat across from my kitchen table with the timeline spread out between us like a chessboard, and she didn't touch her coffee. Ferran has a way of not touching things. I've seen her stand in a room full of evidence for an hour without letting her coat brush a chair.

"You got mail," she said.

"I got mail."

"Through the slot, or delivered?"

"Through the slot. Regular carrier. Whoever sent it wanted it to look like ordinary post."

"Which means they've watched you receive mail." She said it without drama, the way she says everything, like she's reading a weather report for a hurricane. "Or they know your carrier's schedule. Either way, they've spent time on your street."

I slid the invitation across to her, along with my photograph of the 1994 letter. She put on reading glasses — I'd never seen her in reading glasses, and there was something almost indecent about the glimpse of ordinary humanity — and compared them in silence for a long moment.

"The F," she said finally.

"The F."

"Same hand?"

"I'd bet the show on it. Get your examiners to confirm, but yes. The person who forged the letter that buried Danny Voss is the person who invited me to dinner. Thirty years apart, same penmanship. People change their faces, Detective. They almost never change their hand."

Ferran set the documents down and looked at me with an expression I was starting to learn how to read. It wasn't skepticism. It was the look of someone recalculating.

"Here's my problem," she said. "Everything you just said is plausible. And none of it is evidence. A forged invitation isn't a crime I can charge. A similarity in handwriting between a thirty-year-old letter and a party invitation isn't a case. What I have is a dead man in your jacket, a second scene staged from your drafts, a break-in where the thief read your files instead of stealing them, and a podcaster who keeps showing up at every edge of the investigation like a bad penny." She paused. "You see how that reads from where I sit."

"I see how it reads. I'd write it that way myself. It's a great episode."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"Marchetti." She took the glasses off. "There's a version of this where you're the smartest man in the room, and there's a version where you're the most useful man in the room, and the killer has decided which one you are. I need you to tell me honestly: do you have any idea who has this kind of access? To your drafts, your home, your mail, and the Hollis family's stationery?"

I thought about lying to her. I want that on the record — I thought about it. But Ferran had shown me the shape of her own honesty, back at the riverside, when she told me the victim's clothes matched my missing ones before the press had a word of it. She'd given me a truth that made her job harder. I owed her the same coin.

"No," I said. "And that's what frightens me. I've spent three weeks pulling at this case and I can't find the killer's face anywhere in it. Which means either they're very good, or they were never in the files to begin with. They were in the *house*. The Hollis house. Someone who grew up inside it, who knows the case from the inside, who has the stationery and the access and the patience." I turned the invitation over. On the back, faintly, in pencil — so faint I'd missed it until that morning — someone had written a number. "And I think they want me to come to the dinner."

Ferran leaned forward. "What's the number?"

"Twelve," I said. "Just the numeral. Pencil, light pressure, erased and rewritten at least once — you can see the ghost of an eleven under it." I'd spent an hour with a loupe on it. "You know what the original case file had? Twelve pieces of physical evidence logged. Twelve. And you know what my episode outline had? Twelve beats. I structured the whole season on the twelve exhibits. The killer read the outline. The killer is telling me the dinner is the twelfth beat."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside, the terrier woman went by, and the kid on the scooter, and the ordinary world went on being ordinary, and I felt the floor of my life tilt a few degrees.

"Or," Ferran said carefully, "the number twelve means something else entirely, and you're finding the pattern you brought with you."

"That's fair. That's absolutely fair. But here's the thing, Detective." I stood up, because sitting felt wrong, because my body wanted to pace and I let it. "Whether the pattern is real or I'm projecting it, the killer and I are now doing the same thing. We're both reading this case. We're both editing it. They're staging scenes from my drafts; I'm building timelines from their staging. At some point — and I think that point is the dinner — we're going to be in the same room, looking at the same documents, and one of us is going to be right about how it ends."

"And the other?"

"The other is going to be dead, or arrested, or both. That's usually how my stories go."

Ferran stood too, and for a moment we just looked at each other across the wreckage of my kitchen table, two people who had both realized, in our different dialects, that the case had stopped being a case and become a contest.

"I can't stop you from going," she said. "The invitation isn't evidence, you're not under any order, and frankly, if I tried to keep you away, you'd climb in through a window. But if you go, you go wired. You go with a channel to me open the entire time. And you go knowing that if you see the killer and you don't tell me — if you try to be the hero of your own episode — I will arrest you on what I have, and what I have is enough to ruin you. Are we understood?"

"Perfectly."

"I mean it, Marchetti."

"Odile." I used her first name for the first time, and I watched it land like a stone in still water. "I've spent my whole career telling other people's endings. This is the first time I've been inside one. I have no interest in dying in the third act. I want to *see* it."

She held my gaze for a long three seconds, then nodded once, picked up her coat, and left. At the door she paused.

"The RSVP," she said. "Don't send it."

"Why not?"

"Because whoever sent this knows you'll say yes. The invitation isn't the trap." She pulled the door open onto the cold bright street. "The trap is finding out what you do next."

* * *

I didn't send the RSVP. I did something better, or worse — I've stopped being able to tell the difference.

I spent the evening with the case file photographs spread out around me, all twelve exhibits, and the forged letter from 1994 propped up where I could see it, and the invitation beside it, twin documents in the same beautiful hand, thirty years apart. And I wrote a reply. Not on the cream stock. On plain paper, in my own terrible scrawl, the handwriting of a man who types for a living.

*Thank you for the invitation. I wouldn't miss the ending for the world. — B.M.*

I didn't mail it. I photographed it, and then I burned it in the sink, and then I posted the photograph — just the photograph, no caption, no context — as the final frame of a short teaser I released at midnight, unlisted, unsearchable, findable only by someone who knew exactly where to look.

If the killer was reading my files, they'd read this too.

If I was right about the dinner — about the twelfth beat, about the ending being staged for an audience of one — then somewhere in the Hollis house, or the Foundation offices, or wherever that elegant hand kept its pens and its patience, someone was going to open their files tonight and find my answer.

And if I was wrong — if I was just a man shouting into a void, projecting patterns onto pencil marks, walking willingly into a room full of people who'd buried a handyman and a detective's conscience and thirty years of silence — then I'd be at Blackwater Point on the ninth of November, in a room with two hundred donors and one murderer, wearing my own clothes for the first time in a month, hoping like hell that the ending I'd been invited to wasn't mine.

The teaser went up at 12:04 a.m.

At 12:31, my phone buzzed once on the kitchen table.

I looked at the screen. A message, from a number with no name attached, no history, no trace. Six words.

*Wear the gray suit. I'll be watching.*

I stood in my cold kitchen, heart going like a trip-hammer, and I did the math I'd been avoiding for three weeks. Whoever this was, they weren't just in my files.

They'd been in my closet.
