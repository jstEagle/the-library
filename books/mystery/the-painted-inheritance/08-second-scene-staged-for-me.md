## 8. Second Scene, Staged for Me

The call came in at 4:17 a.m., and Detective Odile Ferran was standing over the body by 5:40, coffee untouched, gloves on, watching the crime scene techs work around a dead man who had been arranged like furniture.

The victim was Tomas Reyes, forty-six, a freelance art handler who had once — briefly, in the summer of 1993 — done contract work for the Hollis Gallery. Ferran had pulled that thread herself, three days ago, from a payroll stub buried in the original case file. She had not yet told anyone she'd found it. Which meant the killer had found it first.

"He was killed somewhere else," said Dr. Anselm Okafor, crouched beside the body, his voice flat with the particular weariness of a man on his fourth night scene of the month. "Blunt force trauma to the occipital, post-mortem lividity inconsistent with the position. He was moved here after death. Hours, at least."

"Here" was the decommissioned freight annex behind the old Meridian Cold Storage building, a brick cavern two blocks from where the Hollis Gallery had stood before it became a wine bar and then a rubble lot and then a condo development with a plaque nobody read. The killer had chosen the space with care. The annex had a loading dock, a partition wall, and — this was the detail that made the back of Ferran's neck prickle — a single hanging bulb, its cord shortened so the light fell exactly where the body lay, exactly the way witnesses had described the light falling in the gallery's back room thirty years ago.

Reyes lay on his side, one arm folded beneath him, the other extended toward the partition as if reaching for something just out of frame. His clothes were neat. His shoes were polished. Someone had combed his hair.

Ferran walked the perimeter slowly. She had learned, over nineteen years, to let a scene speak before she interrogated it. This one spoke in a borrowed voice. Every element was quotation. The angle of the arm matched the position of Aldous Hollis's private secretary, Genevieve Marsh, as she'd been found in 1994. The single bulb reproduced the testimony of the night watchman down to the wattage — forty, he'd said, dim enough that he'd needed a moment to recognize what he was looking at. Even the dust on the floor had been disturbed in patterns suggesting the killer had staged a struggle that never happened, correcting the record of a struggle the original investigators had wrongly inferred.

*Correcting.* The word arrived uninvited and stayed.

She crouched near the victim's outstretched hand. Beneath it, half-hidden by the shadow of his own fingers, lay a Polaroid.

She photographed it in place, four angles, then lifted it with a cotton-gloved thumb and turned it toward the hanging light.

It was a photograph of a front door. A blue door, paint peeling along the lower rail, a brass knocker shaped like an acorn, a doormat that said WELCOME in letters worn nearly to illegibility. A door Ferran had stood outside of eleven days ago, waiting for Bram Marchetti to answer, while he took his time doing whatever podcasters did before answering doors.

She knew the door because she'd been to it twice more since.

The Polaroid was recent. The light suggested evening. There was no date stamp — Polaroids didn't have them — but the film stock was current production, and when the lab ran it they would find what Ferran already knew: it had been taken within the last week, from the sidewalk, straight on, patient, the way you photograph a house you intend to return to.

On the white border beneath the image, in small, precise block capitals, someone had written:

**SCENE TWO. CORRECTED.**

* * *

By nine a.m. the annex had been processed, Reyes's identity confirmed through the art handlers' union, and Ferran was sitting in her unmarked car outside Marchetti's building, watching the blue door, feeling like a woman in one of her own surveillance photographs.

She should have called him. Procedure said notify persons connected to the scene promptly; the Polaroid made him connected in a way that was rapidly becoming central. But she sat there instead, hands around a cooling cup, and thought about the shape of the case as it now stood.

One: a murder staged to reproduce an unpublished document — Marchetti's episode outline, which existed, as far as anyone knew, only in his cloud storage and his head.

Two: a second murder, staged to reproduce not the outline but the *original case file itself*, including details that had never been public. The wattage of the bulb. The corrected positioning of the body. The payroll connection between Reyes and the Hollis Gallery, which lived on page sixty-one of a box that had been sealed for a decade until Ferran herself had requested it six weeks ago.

Three: a Polaroid of Marchetti's front door left at the scene like a signature, or an accusation, or an invitation.

Either Bram Marchetti was the most elaborate serial killer she'd encountered in her career — a man who murdered strangers to decorate a podcast narrative — or he was being hunted by someone who knew his files, his home, and his habits better than she did. And the ugly truth, the truth she'd been circling since the riverside, was that both possibilities fit the evidence equally well. That was the craft of whoever was doing this. They were building a case against Marchetti the way a prosecutor builds one: patiently, cumulatively, each scene adding a plank.

Or Marchetti was building it himself, sloppily on purpose, trusting that no one would look too hard at a celebrity podcaster with a perfect record of closed cases.

She got out of the car and knocked on the blue door.

He answered faster this time. He looked bad — not performatively bad, not the groomed exhaustion of a man who wanted to be seen suffering, but genuinely wrung out, three days of stubble and eyes that had the glassy overbrightness of someone who hadn't slept properly since the riverside. He was holding a mug of something black.

"You found another one," he said. Not a question. He'd seen it on the scanner feeds, or heard from a source, or — she noted the thought without letting it show — he'd known already.

"Tomas Reyes," she said. "Art handler. Worked a summer contract at the Hollis Gallery in '93."

"I don't know that name."

"No," she said. "You wouldn't. It wasn't in your outline."

Something flickered across his face — relief, she thought, and then immediately guilt about the relief, which was interesting. A guilty man would have pretended recognition. Marchetti looked like a man grateful to be excluded from a club he feared he belonged to.

"But here's what was in my file and nowhere else," Ferran continued. "The annex. The bulb. The position of the body matching Genevieve Marsh. And a photograph of your front door."

His hand tightened on the mug. She watched the knuckles go pale.

"My door."

"Taken recently. From the street." She held up the Polaroid in an evidence sleeve. "Do you want to tell me who has access to your cloud drafts?"

"I've changed the passwords twice. I wiped the shared drive. I—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Detective, I need you to understand something. Whoever this is isn't hacking me. Hacking is distant. This person is *close*. They've been in my studio. They know what jacket I owned. They're photographing my door at night, which means they've stood on my sidewalk, calm, taking their time, and I never saw them." He laughed, a short unhappy sound. "I make a living noticing things. I noticed nothing."

"That's either very frightening for you," Ferran said, "or very convenient."

He met her eyes. "You don't believe me."

"I believe the evidence is being arranged for my benefit," she said. "And I haven't decided by whom. Those are different things, Mr. Marchetti, and right now the second one is my job."

She handed him a card with a number written on the back — her personal cell, a breach of protocol she'd tell herself later was investigative necessity. "If anyone contacts you, if anything arrives, if you find so much as a note under this door, you call me before you touch it. Before you *narrate* it. Understood?"

"Understood."

"And Marchetti." She paused on the top step. "Don't go to the Hollis dinner."

He blinked. "What dinner?"

Which was how, standing on his own doorstep in the gray morning light, Bram Marchetti learned that an engraved invitation had been sent to him — or rather, that Ferran had learned of one, because the Hollis family's event planner, subpoenaed casually as part of the widening background check, had listed the guest list, and a plus-one invitation in Marchetti's name had gone out ten days ago to an address that wasn't his.

Someone had RSVP'd for him. Someone had accepted on his behalf.

She watched the color leave his face and thought: *either the best actor I've ever seen, or a man who has just understood how deep the water is.*

* * *

Back at the precinct, the walls closed in the way they always did — slowly, then all at once.

Lieutenant Vasquez called her into the glass office at noon. On his desk: a printout of the Polaroid, a copy of Marchetti's published episode list, and a memo from the DA's office that used the phrase *media personality* three times.

"Talk to me," Vasquez said.

"Two scenes, both quotations. First quotes Marchetti's unpublished draft. Second quotes the sealed case file. Both times, evidence points gently toward Marchetti — his clothes, his documents, now his front door photographed at the scene." She laid it out flat, no adjectives. "My read is we're watching someone construct a frame. My problem is I can't prove the direction of construction."

"Because?"

"Because the staging is flawless, and flawless staging requires either intimate knowledge of the original investigation or access to the man whose name keeps appearing in it. Possibly both." She hesitated. "There's also the timing question. The Hollis file was requested by me six weeks ago. The killings started five weeks ago. Either the requester leaked, or the request triggered something."

Vasquez tapped the memo. "The DA wants Marchetti interviewed formally. On record. Today."

"He'll lawyer up, and we'll lose the one cooperative thread we have."

"The DA wants—"

"I heard what the DA wants." She kept her voice level. "Give me seventy-two hours. If I can't produce a better suspect profile than 'famous podcaster with a flair for the dramatic,' you can have him."

Vasquez looked at her for a long moment. Odile Ferran had a reputation for two things: closing cases, and never asking for favors. Seventy-two hours was a favor.

"Seventy-two," he said. "Then it's out of my hands."

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing the thing she trusted more than informants: reading.

The original case file, page by page, again, but this time reading it the way the killer seemed to be reading it — as a text with errors. And once she adopted that lens, the errors leapt out. The original investigation had been sloppy in specific, almost thematic ways. The back room struggle that forensics never supported. The timeline that put Danny Voss at the gallery twenty minutes before any witness placed him. The confession, taken after nine hours of interrogation without counsel, that contradicted the physical evidence in four places and was accepted anyway.

The killer wasn't just reenacting the murders. The killer was *revising* them. Each new scene fixed a mistake. The staged struggle in the annex had been physically coherent — lividity consistent, drag marks absent — as if to say: *this is how it should have looked. This is the true version.*

People didn't revise cases out of abstract principle. People revised cases they loved. Or cases they'd grown up inside.

She pulled the personnel files next. Original lead detective: Raymond Cutler, retired 1997, decorated, deceased — no, wait. Not deceased. She checked twice. Cutler was alive, eighty-one, in a care facility out past the county line, dying slowly of something the intake forms spelled wrong. The file noted a commendation for the Voss arrest and, tucked behind it, a routine expense approval that had always struck previous reviewers as unremarkable: a consulting payment from Hollis Family Trust, dated three weeks after the confession, categorized as *historical advisory services.*

Thirty years ago, nobody had blinked at a rich family paying a retired-adjacent detective for "advisory services." Tonight, with a Polaroid in her pocket and a clock running, it looked exactly like what it probably was.

Ferran wrote the name on her board, drew a line from Cutler to the Hollis Trust, and then — because the pattern demanded symmetry — drew a line from the Hollis Trust to the anniversary dinner, circled the date, and stepped back.

Two weeks. The dinner was in two weeks. And every scene the killer staged was counting down toward something, each correction more ambitious than the last, like a musician rehearsing toward a performance date.

Her phone buzzed. A text from the lab: the Polaroid's film batch traced to a camera sold locally; purchase paid cash; but the store's security footage showed the buyer's build, coat, and gait — and the timestamp.

She opened the attachment. Stood very still.

The figure on the footage was slight, hooded, face angled away. Unidentifiable. But the timestamp was 11:52 p.m. on the night of the riverside murder — and twelve minutes earlier, at 11:40 p.m., Bram Marchetti's phone, per the records she'd quietly pulled, had pinged the tower two blocks from his apartment.

An alibi with a twelve-minute hole. Small enough to ignore, if you liked him. Precise enough to hang him, if you didn't.

Ferran set down the phone and looked at her board: Marchetti in the center, lines radiating outward, and every line she drew to clear him seemed, from certain angles, to tighten instead.

Somewhere out there, someone was editing this case toward an ending. And she was beginning to suspect that when she finally saw the finished version, she would find she had been written into it all along — not as the detective who solved it, but as the detective who arrested the wrong man, right on schedule, right on cue.

The board stared back at her. In the corner, beneath the circled date of the anniversary dinner, she added the only line she was sure of:

*The killer doesn't want me to catch him. The killer wants me to catch Marchetti.*

Her cell rang. Unknown number. She answered.

A voice, low and unhurried, said: "Detective Ferran. You're reading the file wrong. Page sixty-one isn't the clue. It's the apology."
