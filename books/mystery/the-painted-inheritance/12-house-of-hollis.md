## 12. House of Hollis

There are two ways into a house like the Hollises'. The front door is made of money and lawyers, and it does not open for podcasters. The side door is charity.

Every old family keeps a charity the way other people keep a dog — something to feed, something to love them back unconditionally, something that makes the neighbors say what good people they are. The Hollises had theirs: the Aldous Hollis Foundation for the Arts, which for thirty years had been funding scholarships, restorations, and an annual winter gala at the Meridian Hotel that drew half the city's moneyed class and all of its photographers. Tickets were five hundred dollars a plate. My producer's expense account was not going to survive it, but my conscience had already stopped itemizing.

I bought one under the name Daniel Marsh, which felt less like an alias than a small apology — Danny Voss deserved to get into rooms his killers never let him enter, even if only as a borrowed name on a borrowed ticket.

The invitation said *Black Tie. In Celebration of a Legacy.* Legacy is a word rich people use instead of *body count.*

* * *

The Meridian's ballroom was built in an era when the city believed it would outgrow New York, all gilt cornices and ceilings painted with allegories nobody looked up at anymore. Someone had hung the Hollis collection through it — actual paintings, loaned from the gallery, each with a little brass card bearing a price dressed up as a provenance. I recognized three of them from the case file photographs. One of them, a moody harbor scene by a Dutch hand whose name I won't pretend to spell, had been hanging in the gallery's back room the night of the murders. It had been cleaned since. Paintings clean easier than rooms do.

I took a champagne flute I didn't drink from and did what I do best, which is listen.

You'd be amazed how much people tell you when you're holding a camera-shaped absence where your attention should be. I wore no recorder — nothing that could be found on me later, nothing that could turn me from guest to evidence — so I kept it all in my head, arranging names and glances the way I arrange episode beats. A string quartet sawed away at something baroque. Waiters moved through the crowd like a tide. And at the center of it, arranged around a portrait of the patriarch himself — Aldous Hollis in oils, jaw like a cliff face, eyes that had been painted kind by someone who wanted to keep getting commissions — stood the family.

Start with the widow. Vivienne Hollis was seventy-something and wore her age the way some women wear couture: deliberately, expensively, as armor. She was brittle in the way porcelain is brittle — beautiful until touched, then catastrophic. I watched her hold court near the portrait, accepting condolences for a legacy rather than a man, because Aldous had been dead eleven years and everyone had long since run out of things to say about him except *wonderful* and *visionary*, both of which are words mourners use when they're afraid of the alternative. Her hands trembled slightly around her glass. Not grief-trembling. Something closer to vigilance. She kept her back to the harbor painting. All night, she never once faced it.

Then the heir. Marcus Hollis, fifty-one, Aldous's only son, tall in the way of men who peaked at twenty-five and have been leaning on their height ever since. He had the family jaw and none of the family discipline; I clocked the flush in his cheeks before I clocked anything else, the too-bright eyes, the way he visited the bar four times in the first hour and came back lighter each time, drinking with the desperate efficiency of a man managing a deficit. He laughed loudly at jokes that weren't funny. He gripped the shoulders of younger men a half-second too long. When a woman in emerald asked him about the gallery — *your father's wonderful gallery, Marcus* — his smile stayed on his face while everything behind it went dark, like a house with the lights cut.

"The gallery's in excellent hands," he said. "Always has been."

His wife was standing six feet away, and she heard it too.

And there she was. Sable Hollis.

I want to describe her fairly, which is difficult, because fairness requires admitting that my first thought was *she doesn't belong here,* and that thought is exactly the trap this woman has spent her married life inside. She was perhaps forty, dark-haired, dressed in black that wasn't mourning but might have been mistaken for it, standing slightly outside the family constellation the way a moon stands outside a planet — held by gravity, defined by distance. While Marcus performed, Sable watched. While Vivienne received, Sable observed. She had the particular stillness of someone who has learned that in this house, noticing things is safer than saying them, and more useful.

She was also, I noticed, watching me.

Not obviously. Not the way security watches, or the way hostesses count heads. She watched me the way I watch people — cataloguing, patient, faintly amused. I turned toward the harbor painting and gave it a connoisseur's squint, and when I glanced back, she was still looking, and she raised her glass maybe half an inch. A toast, or a challenge. With this family, I suspected there was no difference.

* * *

I worked the room for two hours. Here's the map I carried out:

Vivienne Hollis rules nothing and controls everything, a dowager operating through suggestion and silence, and her silence has a specific shape — it's the silence of someone who knows exactly what happened in 1994 and has decided the price of speaking was higher than the price of staying quiet. At one point a foundation board member mentioned the gallery's thirtieth-anniversary retrospective being planned for spring, and Vivienne said, "We won't be doing a retrospective," in a tone that ended the sentence, the topic, and possibly the man's career. Thirty years. The math of it sat in the room like a guest nobody introduced.

Marcus drinks because he knows something, or suspects something, or was shaped by something he can't name, and the alcohol is the only tool he has for keeping it submerged. He told a story twice — once to a banker, once to a judge — about his father teaching him to sail, and both times he got to the same place in the story, the part where Aldous says *the water keeps what it takes*, and both times he stopped as if the words had a trapdoor under them. He laughed it off. Nobody laughed with him.

The rest were satellites: cousins, lawyers, a city councilman who owed somebody something, Corwin Hale himself holding court near the dessert table — silver-haired, avuncular, the family lawyer with the gentle voice of a man who talks people out of things for a living. Hale shook hands like he was handing out pardons. When he passed me, he paused.

"I don't believe we've met."

"Daniel Marsh. Foundation donor." I smiled the smile of a man with nothing behind it. "Wonderful evening."

"Wonderful," Hale agreed, and his eyes did a slow, courteous inventory of my face, my hands, my shoes, filing me somewhere. "We're so grateful for friends of the arts." He moved on, and I felt the file close behind me.

But it was Sable who found me.

* * *

It happened near the harbor painting, which I had returned to for the third time, because I'm a professional and professionals know that the thing a room avoids is the thing the room is about. The quartet had shifted to something slower. The crowd had thinned toward the dessert table. And Sable Hollis materialized at my elbow with two glasses of champagne and the expression of a woman about to conduct an experiment.

"You keep looking at that one," she said.

"It's the best thing in the room."

"It's the only honest thing in the room." She handed me a glass. "Everything else here is either lying or for sale."

"That's a bold thing to say at your own party."

"It's my husband's family's party. I'm furniture." She sipped. "Daniel Marsh, isn't it? You signed the donor book."

"Guilty."

"Mm." She looked at the painting, not at me, which I understood was a courtesy — or a technique. "You have the look of a man reading a room. Most men here are performing in one."

"And which are you?"

"Stage crew." Almost a smile. "I've been married into this family nine years, Mr. Marsh. Do you know how you survive that? You become a student of it. I read everything about these people. Everything I can find." Now she turned, and her eyes were level and dark and entirely awake. "Which is why I know I haven't seen you at any of these things before, and why you stand like a man taking notes he'll need later, and why you flinched — just barely, just for a second — when the councilman said the word *anniversary*."

The quartet played. Somewhere across the room, Marcus laughed too loudly at nothing.

"I don't know what you think—"

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You don't. That's the problem. Neither do I. That's why I'm talking to you instead of reporting you to Corwin Hale, who notices everything and forgets nothing, and who would have you identified before the coffee course." She set down her untouched glass on a passing tray. "Whatever you are — journalist, investigator, ghost — you came here about the same thing I've spent nine years quietly trying to understand. So here's my offer, and it expires when the dessert is cleared: find me tomorrow. Not at the house. There's a café on Delancey, the one with the green awning, at ten o'clock. If you're not there, I'll decide you were nothing, and we'll both go on smiling."

She stepped back into the light of the party, became Mrs. Marcus Hollis again, smooth and decorative and harmless, and drifted toward her husband's elbow just as he reached for another glass.

I stood alone with the harbor painting and my heart going like a drumline.

Because here's what I hadn't told her, and what I couldn't stop thinking: the harbor scene hanging above us had hung in the gallery's back room in 1994. It had been there the night two people died and a handyman was fitted for a crime he didn't commit. And in thirty years, according to every record I'd read, it had never once been displayed publicly — the family kept it in storage, listed as *unavailable for exhibition*, year after year after year.

Until now. Until the anniversary season. Until tonight, when someone in this smiling house had taken it out of the dark and hung it in the brightest room in the city, over a brass card with a clean new provenance, like bait.

Or like a message.

Across the ballroom, Vivienne Hollis caught her daughter-in-law's eye, and something passed between them — not warmth, not warning, but recognition, the silent traffic of two women who had both learned to read this family in the dark. Then Vivienne looked past Sable, past the crowd, directly at me, and her trembling hands went still.

She knew the face. Not mine — Danny Marsh's. The name. For one second, the widow of Aldous Hollis looked at me the way people look at a grave they thought had finished filling in.

Then she smiled, and raised her glass to me across the room, and drank.

I set down my champagne, untouched, and walked out through the side door of the House of Hollis with a rendezvous at ten o'clock and the growing, electrifying suspicion that I hadn't gone undercover at all.

Someone in that room had been expecting me.
