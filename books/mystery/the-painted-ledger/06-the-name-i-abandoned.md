## 6. The Name I Abandoned

The letters sat on Ivo's desk for three days before they could bring themselves to say the name aloud.

Not read it—reading was easy, reading was silent, and silence had been Ivo's first language. But to speak it, in the stone hush of the archive, with only the breathing of old paper for company—that was another act entirely. The name lay on the page in careful, old-fashioned script, the letters formed the way schoolchildren in another century had been taught, each one a small act of obedience: *Querido Ivo*—no. Not Ivo.

*Querido Ivette.*

Ivo Villalobos had been Ivo for thirty-one years. Ivo of the cathedral archive, Ivo of the gray cardigans and the grayer manner, Ivo whom the vergers nodded to and the canons forgot to invite and the restoration crews learned, within a week, to stop asking questions of. Ivo was a shape worn smooth by use, a name like a coat that had grown into the skin. But the letters did not say Ivo. The letters said *Ivette*—the name given at a christening in a village church in the hills above the river, in a parish that no longer kept records because the parish no longer existed, in a village called San Remei that appeared on no map printed after the war.

Nobody alive called Ivo that. Nobody alive *could* call Ivo that, because the list of people who had known it was a list of the dead.

Except, evidently, one.

* * *

Ivo had built the invisibility the way other people built houses—one deliberate stone at a time, over years, with a patience that looked like temperament but was really architecture. It had begun with the voice. The village accent had gone first, sanded away in the orphanage in the provincial capital, where a girl with hills in her vowels was a girl who invited questions, and questions were dangerous. Then the name, changed at sixteen by a clerk who did not look up from his ledger—Ivo, watching the pen move, understanding even then that a ledger could erase a person with one stroke, and that the same instrument, turned in the hand, could keep one. That was the day the archive chose Ivo, though it took another decade for Ivo to choose the archive back.

Because here was the thing no one understood about people who made themselves invisible: they did not do it out of shyness. They did it because they had once seen, with total clarity, what happened to people who were *visible*. Who had names. Who were on lists.

The night the families of San Remei vanished, Ivo had been nine years old, and it had been raining, and Ivo had been awake, and Ivo had said nothing.

* * *

The rain came back to Ivo now, the way it always did, uninvited, in the hour before dawn when the archive's oil lamps burned low and the cathedral above creaked like a ship. Ivo sat with the two letters under the lamp and did not read them again and read them again anyway, and the rain began.

San Remei, October, a lifetime ago. The village had been a scatter of stone houses around a spring, terraced orchards going gold, a church with a bell that rang flat because the bell had a crack nobody could afford to fix. Ivo's father mended shoes and read too many newspapers; Ivo's mother kept hens and sang off-key and had a laugh that could be heard from the spring to the churchyard. They had a name for their daughter that they used softly, at night, the way you'd use a secret: *Ivette*. It meant something in the old speech—Ivo had never learned what. The old speech had gone the way of the bell, the way of everything.

The war had been a rumor for years, the way weather is a rumor until it arrives. Men from the valley came and went. Lists were spoken of in lowered voices—lists of names, of who was red, who was rich, who had said what in the tavern in 'thirty-six. Ivo's father burned his newspapers one night in the bread oven, all of them, two years of them, and the house smelled of ink and ash for days, and he said to Ivo's mother, in the old speech, words Ivo was not meant to understand and did not then, but understood later, terribly, from the inside: *They are selling names now. Neighbors are the currency.*

Ivo was nine. Ivo did not know what currency was. Ivo knew that the hens had stopped laying because of the cold, and that Father Anselm—no, not Anselm, not yet, that was later, that was the cathedral, that was the man who would come to matter—no. In San Remei the priest was old Father Berenguer, who had a cough and a kind face and a ledger of baptisms that he hid inside the lining of the vestment chest, and who taught Ivo, in the last autumn, how to grind pigment with a stone muller because Ivo liked to watch him touch up the saints on the church walls, liked the smell of linseed and lime, liked the way a painted eye could follow you and yet keep all its secrets.

*They can't arrest what they can't see,* Father Berenguer said once, tapping Ivo's nose with a paint-blackened finger. *Remember that, little one. The saints survive because they stay on the wall and keep their mouths shut.*

Ivo remembered. Ivo was still remembering, sixty years later, under a lamp, with two letters that would not keep their mouths shut.

* * *

The night itself. Ivo had rehearsed not-remembering it for so long that remembering it felt like trespass.

It had rained. Dogs had been barking since dusk, that flat, continuous barking that is not alarm but dread, the sound dogs make when they have smelled something they cannot name. Ivo had been awake in the loft above the workshop, listening to the rain on the tiles, listening to the parents downstairs not sleeping—the particular silence of two people lying awake side by side, which is louder than snoring, louder than talking.

The trucks came at two in the morning. Ivo knew the hour because the church bell had struck twice and then, instead of falling silent, seemed to hold its breath.

Lights in the street. Voices—not many, and low, the voices of men doing paperwork, men reading from lists. A name called. The Ferreiro family's door. Then the Costa family's. Then, after a silence that Ivo's whole body refused, the knock—not even a knock, a polite double tap—on the door below the loft.

Ivo's father opened it. Ivo heard him do it. Heard him say, in his careful valley Spanish, *We have done nothing.* Heard the paper voice answer, *The list says otherwise.* Heard Ivo's mother, unbelievably, laugh—that laugh, that laugh that carried from the spring to the churchyard—except that this time it broke in the middle, and the breaking of it was the worst sound Ivo had ever heard, worse than what came after, which was mostly quiet: boots on stone, the hens startled into one brief outcry, the truck doors, the engines.

And Ivo—nine years old, in the loft, with a view through the shutter slats of the wet street and the lanterns and the line of neighbors being walked to the trucks, the Ferreiros holding hands, old Marta's mother in her nightdress with a shawl over her head like a woman going to early Mass—said nothing.

That was the whole of it. That was the act. Ivo said nothing. Did not cry out, did not run down, did not throw a shoe, a stone, a word. Lay flat on the boards in the dark with a hand pressed over Ivo's own mouth—physically pressed, Ivo remembered the taste of the palm, the salt of it—and watched, and breathed through the fingers, and survived.

The trucks left. The rain went on. And in the morning the village had holes in it the way the frescoes had holes, and Ivo walked down through the wet grass to the empty houses with the doors standing open and the rain coming in, and stood in the Ferreiros' kitchen looking at a bowl with a spoon still in it, and understood, at nine, with the total and permanent clarity that only children and the condemned are granted: *they could not see me, and that is why I am alive. My whole life from this moment is built on not being seen.*

Ivo's parents were on the trucks. Ivo must say that plainly, because Ivo spent sixty years saying it in every way except plainly. The parents were taken. Ivo was not spared *instead of* them—there was no arithmetic like that, the trucks did not trade. Ivo was simply not found, and they were simply taken, and the difference between Ivo and the Ferreiro children, who had also been in a loft, who had also been awake—this Ivo learned much later, from Marta, in a single sentence delivered like a nail—was that the Ferreiro children had cried out. A small thing. A child's thing. The most human thing in the world.

Ivo had not done the human thing. Ivo had done the surviving thing. And the two, it turned out, were not the same thing at all, and the space between them was where a life could be hidden, the way a ledger could be hidden under paint.

* * *

The lamp guttered. Ivo came back to the present the way one surfaces from deep water, in stages, the letters resolving out of the dark.

*Ivette.* The confessor had written it twice now, once in each letter, in the salutation and—this was the detail that had kept Ivo awake—in the closing of the first. *Your name was the last thing they erased, and the first thing I kept.* Signed, with a child's formality, *Ivo*—the new name, the coat, worn by another hand like a stolen garment. Whoever wrote this knew both names. Knew the old one and knew the new one, and knew the exact seam where one had been stitched over the other.

That was a short list. Ivo had kept it short the way other people kept their savings. The orphanage clerk: dead. Father Berenguer: dead, and his ledger burned with the vestment chest, or so the diocese said. Marta, if she lived—Marta would be past eighty now, if she lived, somewhere in the hills, if the hills still held anyone. And here, in the city, in this cathedral, the handful of people who had known Ivo for decades: the canons, who knew nothing; the vergers, who knew less; Father Anselm, who knew a great deal and said little, and had never once, in thirty years, asked Ivo a personal question, which Ivo had always taken for kindness and now, under this lamp, in this rain, began to take for something else.

And Tomás.

Ivo set the letters down and pressed the heels of both hands against both eyes until the dark went orange.

Tomás, who had come to the cathedral two years after Ivo, a boy of seventeen with burn-scarred hands and a stammer he kept like a secret, who had learned bookbinding from the old brothers and never spoke of where he'd learned to endure pain. Tomás, who ate lunch with Ivo every day for forty years and had never once asked about the past, and who had said, only once, on a winter evening years ago, over wine in the bindery, with the presses ticking as they cooled: *We are the same kind, you and I. The kind that was not found.* And Ivo had laughed—had actually laughed—and said, *Everyone was not found, Tomás. Half the country was not found.* And Tomás had smiled his gentle, ruined smile and said nothing, and refilled the glass, and the moment had passed the way such moments pass, and Ivo had spent forty years not thinking about it.

The confessor knew the name. The confessor knew the village. The confessor wrote with an old-fashioned, schooled hand—Tomás's hand, the bindery hand, the hand that lettered spines in gold. The confessor knew that the crypt had been sealed in 1963, knew that the ledger existed, knew where Ivo's desk was, knew the exact weight of silence Ivo could carry.

*No,* Ivo told the empty archive. *Not him. Not Tomás.*

The archive, being an archive, kept the objection without agreeing to it.

* * *

Morning came gray and thin. Ivo did what Ivo always did with unbearable things: made a record. A clean sheet of archive paper, the good rag stock, and in pencil, in the small exact hand that had catalogued forty thousand documents without once being noticed doing it, a heading: *Persons aware of birth name, San Remei parish, 1934–1943.* The list was seven lines long. Four lines were struck through with the quiet finality of a death notice. Three remained: Marta, if she lived; Anselm; Tomás.

Ivo looked at the three lines for a long time.

Then, because the archivist's instinct outruns every other instinct the way water outruns stone, Ivo took the two letters down to the conservation room, where the light was north light, the only honest light, and laid them under the raking lamp, and saw what the lamp showed: the faint impression of a previous draft beneath the first letter's salutation, pressed through from a sheet written on top of it and removed. Ivo angled the light, breathed on the page the way one breathes on old glass, and read the ghost of the abandoned words.

The confessor had begun the letter differently the first time. Not *Querido Ivette.*

*Querida alma gemela.*

*Dear twin soul.*

Ivo stood in the north light for a long moment, the paper trembling very slightly under one finger, and felt the ground of forty years tilt.

There was only one person in the world who had ever used that phrase. A boy in the bindery, forty years ago, over cooling presses and cheap wine: *We are the same kind, you and I*—no. Before that. Before the cathedral. A boy in the orphanage yard, the first winter, two children with no names worth having, and the boy with the burned hands had said, in the hills' accent he too would later sand away: *They didn't find us, Ivette. That makes us twin souls. The dead gave us to each other.*

Ivo had told no one. Ivo had never told anyone. It had been a thing between two children in the snow, spoken once and buried, and it was not in any ledger, and it was not on any list, and it could not be known by any living person except the one who had said it.

Ivo put on the coat. Ivo took the letters, and the pencil list, and locked the archive with hands that were, Ivo noted with the detached professionalism of a lifetime's training, perfectly steady.

Down the cloister, past the bindery, where the smell of glue and leather came out through the door along with the small rhythmic sound of a bone folder working a fold—Tomás at his bench, forty years of mornings, the burned hands moving with their gentle certainty. Ivo stopped in the doorway without meaning to. Tomás looked up, and smiled the gentle, ruined smile, and said, *Cold morning. I'll make the wine hot at lunch, shall I?*

*Yes,* Ivo said. *Yes, that would be good.*

And walked on, toward Anselm's rooms, because Anselm had been the diocese's man in 1963, and Anselm had signed the order sealing the crypt, and if anyone alive knew what was buried beneath the frescoes—and who had carried a ledger out of a burning parish and kept it for sixty years—it would be the old priest who had begged Ivo, only last week, with a hand on Ivo's sleeve, to let the dead stay dead.

Ivo had thought, at the time, that it was piety.

Walking the cloister now, with *twin soul* burning in the pocket like a coal, Ivo understood that it had been a warning.
