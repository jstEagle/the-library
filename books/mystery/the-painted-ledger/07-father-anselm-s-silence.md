## 7. Father Anselm's Silence

The bell for None rang while Ivo was still standing in the crypt doorway, and the sound came down the stairwell like water finding a crack—filling the dark, pooling around their ankles, rising. Ivo counted the strokes out of habit. Twelve, though it was mid-afternoon; the old bell had never learned to tell time properly, only to repeat itself, and there was something in that stubbornness Ivo had always found comforting. Today it sounded like an accusation.

They climbed back into the light.

The sacristy corridor smelled of beeswax and old stone, of the particular dust that accumulates in buildings where people have been praying for six hundred years—a dust with a liturgy of its own. Ivo walked it slowly, photograph still burning in their pocket like a coal. The scratch on the inner door. The door that opened only from inside. They had turned the image over in their mind all morning the way one turns a stone over to see what lives beneath, and every rotation had brought them closer to the same conclusion: they could not carry this alone, and there was exactly one person alive who might know why the diocese had welded shut a door that was already locked.

Father Anselm kept his office at the end of the corridor, past the vestry, in a room that had once been a penitential cell and had never quite stopped being one. The door stood open. It always stood open. Anselm said a closed door invited the devil's privacy, though Ivo suspected the truth was simpler—that after sixty years of hearing confessions, the old priest could not bear to be on the wrong side of one.

He was at his desk, mending a breviary whose spine had given up. His hands, when he worked, were beautiful—large, scarred, unhurried, the hands of a man who had spent a lifetime holding broken things together long enough for them to forget they were broken. He did not look up.

"You've been in the crypt," he said.

Ivo stopped in the doorway. The corridor's hush pressed against their back like a hand between the shoulder blades.

"The seal is intact," Ivo said. "Externally."

Now he looked up. His eyes were pale as watered milk and just as clear, and they held Ivo the way the archive held its documents—completely, without judgment, without mercy. "Sit down, child."

"I'm fifty-one years old."

"Sit down anyway."

Ivo sat. The chair was hard oak, older than both of them, and it received them with the indifference of furniture that has heard everything. On the desk between them lay the ruined breviary, its threads trailing like severed tendons. Anselm set down his needle. He folded his hands over the book, and Ivo watched him arrange his face into the expression he wore for hard conversations—the one that looked like patience but was actually fortification.

"Tell me what you saw."

"A scratch," Ivo said. "On the inside of the inner door. Fresh—days old, perhaps weeks. The outer seal hasn't been touched since 1963, if the wax is original, and it is; I checked the impression against the diocesan register. Which means either someone entered before the sealing and never left—" They heard their own voice flatten into the archivist's register, the safe voice, the voice that dealt in facts because facts could not bleed. "Or the door opens from the inside, Father. And someone has been opening it."

Anselm said nothing. The silence went on so long that Ivo began to hear the building around them: the tick of the sacristy clock two rooms away, the settling groan of stone in the afternoon heat, somewhere below, the faint continuous whisper of the nave where tourists moved through the restored light of the frescoes, admiring saints whose faces had been repainted last spring.

"You knew," Ivo said. Not a question. The archivist again, cataloguing.

"I knew."

"When?"

The old priest exhaled through his nose, a sound like wind moving through a ruin. "I have known about that door since I was younger than you are now. Longer than you have been alive, child, I have known that the crypt beneath our Lady chapel does not open from outside. That was the point of it. That was the whole design." He rose, slowly, one hand braced on the desk, and crossed to the narrow window that looked out onto the cloister garden. He stood with his back to Ivo, which was itself an answer; Anselm was a man who faced things, and turning away cost him visibly. "You want the history. Very well. You are owed the history, God help me, because you will dig it up with or without my blessing, and I would rather you dug with a shovel than with your bare hands."

* * *

What he told them took an hour, and he told it standing at the window, in the flat voice of a man reciting a confession he had rehearsed for decades and never delivered.

The crypt had not always been sealed. Before the war it had been a burial place for the cathedral's benefactors—a respectable ossuary, dry and orderly, its niches labeled in gilt letters. During the war years, in the months when the city changed hands twice and the lists changed hands three times, it had become something else. People had hidden there. That much was in the official account, softened and sanctified: *shelter was offered to the persecuted.* What was not in the official account was how some of those people had died—not by soldiers' hands, but by suffocation, by thirst, by the simple arithmetic of too many bodies in a space built for forty and holding ninety. And what was not in any account at all, Anselm said, was the night in the winter of '43 when the inner door had been barred from the outside by men who feared discovery more than they feared damnation—and unbarred three days later, when the danger passed, to reveal what the dark had done with the ones inside.

"One of them was alive," Anselm said. "A girl of nineteen. She scratched at the door until her fingers were nothing. She survived another week in the infirmary and then she didn't. Before she died she told the prior what had happened—who had barred the door, and why, and whose names were on the list that made hiding necessary in the first place. The prior wrote it all down. Names, dates, payments. Because here is the thing no one wants carved on a memorial wall, Ivo: the men who barred that door were not strangers. They were the cathedral's own benefactors. The same names on the niches. The same names on the donor plaques in the nave. They had bought their safety during the occupation by selling their neighbors' names to whoever was buying, and when some of those neighbors came to the cathedral seeking shelter, the benefactors feared exposure more than invasion. So they locked the evidence in with the evidence."

"And the prior's record?"

"Became the ledger." Anselm still did not turn around. "The prior understood that a written accusation from a dying man would be dismissed—or worse, would bring the soldiers back down on all their heads. So he encoded it. Painted it, actually. Into the frescoes, before the restorers came after the war to make everything beautiful again. Dates in the halos. Sums in the borders. A record hidden in plain sight, in the most public room in the city, where no one would think to look because everyone looks at frescoes and no one reads them." He laughed then, a small terrible sound. "And no one has. For eighty years, no one has read them."

"But the crypt itself—"

"In 1963 a new bishop decided the scandal was a wound that would fester as long as anyone could visit it. He sealed the outer door, burned the crypt's records—all the burial registers, the niche inventories, the prior's working papers, everything that wasn't painted into plaster—and declared the space closed for structural reasons. Structural reasons." Anselm's shoulders moved, a shrug or a shudder. "The building has excellent bones. It was never the structure that was unsound."

Ivo sat very still. The photograph in their pocket seemed to grow heavier with each sentence, as though the scratch on the inner door were deepening even now, cutting through plaster and stone and sixty years of institutional silence.

"The records were burned," Ivo repeated. "All of them."

"All but one copy of everything that mattered. The paint remembers, child. Paint is patient. Paint is the only witness the Church has never managed to silence." At last he turned, and his face in the window light was older than it had been an hour ago, aged by the telling. "Now you understand why I ask what I am about to ask."

"No," Ivo said. "But I suspect."

* * *

He came back to the desk. He did not sit; he stood over Ivo with his ruined book between them, and when he spoke again his voice had lost its recitation-flatness and become, for the first time, simply an old man's voice—tired, pleading, afraid.

"Let the dead stay dead."

"Father—"

"Listen to me." His hand came down on the breviary, gently, as if calming it. "I know what you are. I have watched you for thirty years, Ivo Villalobos, and I know that you believe a thing unwitnessed is a thing unforgiven, that your whole life has been an argument with oblivion. I know why." Something moved behind his eyes—knowledge, old and carefully buried. "But hear an old man who has buried more secrets than bodies. Those people in the crypt are beyond harm. Nothing you uncover will warm them, feed them, or raise them. But the living—" He gestured vaguely upward, toward the nave, the city, the world of the breathing. "The living have built their houses on top of this. Families. Institutions. Reputations. Some of those families are innocent of everything except inheritance. Do you propose to make children answer for grandfathers? To pull down the cathedral's roof over the heads of people who pray under it in good faith?"

"The dead were also somebody's family."

"Yes." He closed his eyes. "Yes. And their family waited eighty years for the Church to do right by them, and the Church burned the paperwork instead. I am not defending it. I am telling you what it costs to defend it, because I have paid that bill every day of my priesthood. Every Mass I have said over that crypt. Every absolution I have given to men I knew—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "There are things I have carried, child, that would strip the gilding off your soul to hear. Things I did not do, and things I failed to prevent, and one or two things I did, in the name of keeping this house standing so that it could go on feeding the hungry and sheltering the frightened, which it does, which it has always done alongside the rot, the way a body carries disease and life in the same blood. If you open that door—if you read the paint, if you name the names—you will not find clean hands anywhere. Not even mine. Perhaps least of all mine."

"Then tell me yours," Ivo said quietly. "And let me decide about the rest."

For a moment—one long moment in which the sacristy clock ticked twice and the building groaned overhead—Anselm looked as though he might. His mouth opened. His hand lifted from the breviary, reached toward Ivo, hovered.

Then it withdrew.

"No," he said. "Not because I am protecting myself. Because the moment I speak, you stop being an archivist and become a witness, and witnesses can be destroyed. I have seen it. I am asking you—as the man who signed the papers that brought you to this archive when no other institution would take a war orphan with no papers at all—I am asking you to leave the crypt sealed. Let the dead keep their room. Grief has a place to live here already. Don't evict it into the daylight where it will burn."

Ivo rose. Their legs felt distant, borrowed. Thirty years of loyalty stood in the room with them, wearing a cassock, begging—and beside it, newly visible, something else had been standing all along, patient as paint: complicity. The same body. The same hands. One could not be excised from the other without killing both.

"You signed those papers," Ivo said, "because the alternative was leaving me nowhere."

"I signed them because I loved your mother's memory and hated myself, and the two feelings required the same action." He said it without flinching, and Ivo understood that this, too, was a rehearsed confession, delivered now because the hour had come when rehearsal was all that remained. "Go home, Ivo. Sleep. Burn that photograph. In the morning it will look different."

"It won't."

"No," Anselm agreed softly. "It won't. That is why I asked anyway."

Ivo walked to the door. In the threshold they stopped, because stopping was necessary, because some doors should be paused at.

"The girl in the crypt," they said, not turning. "The one who survived three days scratching at the door. Did she have a name in the prior's papers?"

Silence. Then, so quietly it barely crossed the room:

"She had a village. San Remei."

The word landed in Ivo's chest like a key dropped down a well—falling, falling, striking depths they had spent fifty years pretending were bottomless rather than full.

They walked out. Behind them, faintly, they heard Anselm begin to pray, and the prayer sounded less like devotion than like a man talking himself out of following.

That night, alone in the archive with the lamps low, Ivo spread the oldest inventory across the reading table and ran one finger down the column of acquisition dates until it stopped at a folio entered in the spring of 1944, catalogued by a prior's hand, described in seven words:

*Fresco cycle, nave — donor commissions, thirteen panels.*

Thirteen panels. Ivo had walked beneath them ten thousand times.

They fetched the ladder.
