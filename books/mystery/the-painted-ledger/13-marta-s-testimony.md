## 13. Marta's Testimony

The road to San Remei had been paved sometime in the eighties and abandoned sometime after, and now it was neither road nor field but a seam of cracked asphalt stitched with wild fennel. Ivo's rental car bottomed out twice on the potholes, and each jolt felt less like mechanical misfortune than like the earth itself objecting to the visit. They had not come back in fifty-two years. They had told themselves for decades that this was because there was nothing to come back to, and the sentence had worn smooth with handling, the way a stone does, until it seemed like a fact of geology rather than a choice.

The village announced itself the way ruins do—not all at once but in increments. First a stone wall running along the roadside, hip-high, its coping long since carried off for other walls elsewhere. Then a chimney standing in a field of broom, absurd and upright, like a man waiting for a bus that would never come. Then the bell tower, which had survived because it was the only thing in San Remei built to survive anything: squat, buttressed, its roof gone to sky, its bell gone to wherever bells go when parishes are dissolved. Ivo parked where the road ended, which was where the village began, and sat for a long moment with both hands on the wheel, listening to the engine tick as it cooled.

*You came back to finish it?* Marta had not yet said it. That was still ahead of them, an hour away, a chapter away. But Ivo already felt the shape of the accusation pressing through the windshield, the way one feels weather before it arrives.

They got out. The air smelled of fennel and hot stone and, beneath it, faintly, of ash that was no longer ash—only the memory of ash, the way a burn scar remembers fire. Ivo had prepared a professional self for this trip, the archivist's self, the one that catalogued and photographed and did not tremble. That self lasted approximately forty seconds, which was how long it took to walk from the car to the first doorless doorway and understand that they were standing in the house where someone named Costa had once lived, and that the Costa family had been on a list, and that Ivo knew whose hand had written the list, or would know soon, and that the knowing was coming for them like the potholes had come for the tires: inevitable, jarring, and of their own making.

* * *

The memorial wall stood at the village's eastern edge, where the ground dropped away toward the river. It had been built in the nineties by a committee of the dispersed—survivors and their children, pooling money and grief—and it was a modest thing, a limestone face perhaps four meters long, carved with the names of the taken. Ivo had seen photographs of it in a regional archive: rows of clean incised letters, the newest stone in a village with no new anything.

Someone had repainted one name.

The rest of the wall had weathered into a uniform grey, lichen softening the serifs, rain streaking the stone into a kind of weeping. But one name, third row from the top, second from the left, had been freshly painted over—white paint, matte, recent enough that Ivo could smell it, or believed they could. Not defaced, precisely. The letters were still legible; the paint had been applied with a fine brush, carefully, into the grooves, as if someone had re-inked the name the way a conservator re-inks a faded manuscript. As if someone had wanted it *sharper*.

Ivo took out their phone and photographed it, and their hands were steady, because photographing was what their hands did when the rest of them was coming apart. The name was *Villalobos*. Both of them. Father and mother, side by side in the stone, and the fresh paint lay over both.

They stood before it for a long time. A kestrel hung in the wind above the drop to the river. Somewhere a dog barked twice, in a village that still had dogs, and fell silent.

It was Marta's voice that broke the stillness.

"You came back to finish it?"

Ivo turned. She had come up the path from the lower houses—there were four inhabited houses now, Ivo remembered, four households of returnees and their stubborn gardens—and she was small and straight-backed and brown as a walnut, with a black headscarf and eyes that had not softened in fifty years so much as been sanded down to something harder than softness. She carried a canvas shopping bag and a stick, and she used neither to lean on; they were simply props in a performance of age that her spine refused to endorse.

"Marta," Ivo said. "Doña Marta."

"You know my name." She did not return the courtesy. "I know yours. I knew it before you were Ivo." She came closer, stopped an arm's length away, and looked at Ivo the way one looks at a painting one has known since it was new and is now checking for restorations. "You were a skinny little thing. Always standing in doorways. Always half in shadow. I used to say to my mother, that child will either be a priest or a ghost." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I see which."

"I came to ask questions," Ivo said. "About the wall. About the paint."

"Did you." Marta set her bag down on the grass with great deliberation, as though establishing a camp. "And who sent you? The diocese? The police? Or did you send yourself, Ivo Villalobos, after forty years, because someone finally wrote you a letter?"

The wind moved through the broom field with a sound like a long exhalation. Ivo felt the ground tilt—not the ground, of course, but the assumption beneath the ground, the one they had carried up the road: that they had come here as the investigator, the archivist, the one who asks. Marta was informing them, with the economy of a woman who had never wasted a word on anyone she didn't respect, that here Ivo was also a document. A record to be read.

"Someone wrote to me," Ivo admitted. "Signed with a name I haven't used since I was nine years old."

"Vil," Marta said.

The syllable went through Ivo like a needle finding the one nerve in a tooth. *Vil.* The village name. The name their mother had used in the last weeks, when tenderness and terror had made her careless with secrecy—*come here, Vil, eat something, Vil, whatever happens, be nothing, Vil.* Ivo had buried it so deep that hearing it aloud now was like hearing their own heartbeat played back on a recording: familiar, alien, proof of an interior they had forgotten they had.

"You know who wrote it," Ivo said.

"I know who *didn't*." Marta lowered herself onto the low wall beside the memorial, unhurried, arranging her skirt, settling in the manner of a woman who intended to talk and had decided the talking would happen on her terms. "Sit down. You'll want to sit down."

Ivo sat. The stone was warm.

* * *

"A boy took the ledger," Marta said, without preamble, once Ivo was settled. "The night the families were taken. You know the night. Everyone from here knows the night; the ones who aren't from here don't believe it happened, which is a kind of mercy, or an insult, depending on the day."

"I know the night," Ivo said quietly.

"You survived it by saying nothing. I've never decided if that makes you the smartest child in San Remei or the saddest. Probably both. It usually is, both." She looked out at the river. "There was another one like you. Another nothing-sayer. Older. Fourteen, maybe fifteen. He worked with his hands—his father was the wheelwright's cousin, the one who kept the accounts for the co-op. And the co-op kept accounts for other things too, if you understand me. When the priest and the notary and the mayor sat in the back room dividing up who would be sold and who would be spared, the co-op books were where they wrote it down. Sums. Dates. Initials. They thought paper was safe because paper was theirs."

"And the boy took the books."

"The boy took the book. One book. The ledger." Marta's voice flattened, became the voice of testimony, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "He was in the rafters of the co-op that night—I don't know why, boys hide in rafters, it's what they're for—and he saw them write, and he saw them seal it, and when the soldiers came for the families and the men were still drunk on their own cleverness, he climbed down and he took it. And he ran. And I helped him run, God forgive me, I gave him bread and a blanket and pointed him at the mountains, because he was a child and they were monsters, and I have spent fifty years deciding whether that makes me an accomplice or a saint, and I have concluded, like you, both."

Ivo's mouth was dry. "What did he look like."

Marta turned and looked at them fully then, and there was something in her face—not cruelty, but a kind of terrible courtesy, the courtesy of someone about to hand over a weight and wanting the recipient to be ready.

"He was tall for his age," she said. "All wrists and elbows. He had a way of walking with his shoulders drawn up, like a man perpetually expecting a blow, and a way of standing that made him seem to take up less space than he did—you'd look at a room and not see him, and then he'd speak and you'd jump. Quiet hands. Beautiful hands. A bookbinder's hands, though none of us knew that word then. He mended things. Anything with a spine and pages, he could make whole. He mended my mother's missal the week before the end. She kept it under her apron like it was gold." Marta paused. "He had a scar on his left thumb. Split it on a chisel, his father said. He used to rub it when he was thinking, thumb against forefinger, over and over, like a man telling beads."

The wind came again. Ivo did not feel it. Ivo felt nothing except a slow, widening cold, as though the river below the wall had risen through the limestone and into their chest.

Because they knew the gait. They knew the shoulders drawn up like a man perpetually expecting a blow—knew it from twenty years of watching Tomás cross the archive with his head slightly bowed, apologetic even in motion, taking up no space. They knew the hands: the beautiful, quiet, mending hands that had bound Ivo's first facsimile project, that had splinted a broken folio of the twelfth-century lectionary with a delicacy Ivo had once described, in a cataloguing note, as *surgical*. They knew the thumb. God help them, they knew the thumb—the way Tomás rubbed it against his forefinger in meetings, in the refectory, standing over the bookbinder's bench, an unconscious liturgy Ivo had noticed a hundred times and never once asked about, because Ivo had made a career, had made a *life*, of not asking about the things people carried.

"He swore something," Ivo said. It was not a question. Their voice came out level, which surprised them, and frightened them, because level was what Tomás's voice sounded like.

"He swore." Marta nodded slowly. "The night he left. He was crying, and he wasn't crying about being afraid—I'd have understood that; I'd have comforted that. He was crying about the book. He held it against his chest and he said—" her own voice caught, and she mastered it with visible irritation, "—he said, *when the law runs out, they'll think they're safe, and that's when they'll learn no one is.* Something like that. A child's phrasing. But the shape of it was exact. He said the ledger would keep, because he would keep it. He said he'd learn to keep things, to preserve things, to make things last, so that when the day came, the proof would be perfect." She looked at Ivo. "He said, *I'll become an archivist of the crime.* Those words. A boy of fourteen. *An archivist of the crime.*"

The cathedral's archive. The restricted holdings. The locked crypt, sealed in 1963, its records burned—or said to be burned. The boy who had fled with a ledger and grown into a man who bound books beside Ivo for two decades, patient, gentle, invisible in the way that Ivo was invisible, two nothings standing in doorways together, and Ivo had thought they had recognized a fellow survivor of silence. They had. They simply had never asked what the silence was *for*.

"You know him," Marta said. It was not a question either.

"I know someone," Ivo said. "I don't—"

"You know him." Marta's voice was not unkind, but it permitted no exit. "I watched your face. I'm old, not blind. You watched his hands in your mind just now. I saw you watching them." She picked up her stick, not to rise yet, but to hold, the way a judge holds a gavel. "Then let me tell you one more thing, and then you can go and do whatever it is you came to do. The name on that wall that I repainted—no. That *he* repainted. He came here in the spring. First time in fifty years. He stood where you're standing and he looked at his parents' names, and he took a brush from his pocket and he painted the grooves white so the names would read clean from the road." Her eyes glistened and did not spill. "He said to me, *I'm doing it for the ones who were sold, not for me.* And then he said, *and there's one more name that isn't on the wall that should be.* And I asked him which, and he looked at me—" Marta's hand closed on the stick, "—he looked at me the way you're looking at me now, and he said, *the one who was paid to raise what was left behind.*"

The ground did not tilt. It opened. Because Ivo had read the last page of nothing yet, had decoded only the halo-numbers of the nave, but the sentence fell into place with the terrible click of a key in a lock they had been carrying all their life without knowing: the aunt in the city, the church pension, the quiet arrangement that had fed and clothed and schooled a nine-year-old orphan with no questions asked by anyone, ever, in a country full of questions. The payment received by the person who raised what was left behind.

"His name," Ivo said. "Tell me his name."

"No," Marta said. "You tell me. Because if I say it, you'll have an excuse to doubt me. But if you say it—" she rose at last, straight-backed, terrible, "—then you'll have to live with having known it all along."

The kestrel dropped toward the river and pulled up. The dog barked again, once, and stopped. Ivo stood before the memorial wall with the fresh white paint drying in the sun over their parents' names, and the whole of the last two weeks rearranged itself behind their eyes: the letters in a hand they had told themselves they didn't recognize, the slip of ledger paper in a desk drawer that no stranger could have opened, the third letter naming Anselm—*Anselm, who burned the crypt's records, who begged me to let the dead stay dead*—and beneath it all, patient as water finding its level, the image of Tomás at his bench, head bowed, thumb moving against forefinger, mending the world one broken spine at a time.

"Tomás," Ivo said.

Marta closed her eyes, as if the name were a debt finally paid. "He told me you'd say it," she said. "He said, *if she comes, and she will come, she'll know. She's the only one who ever saw me clearly.*" She picked up her shopping bag. "He also said you'd hate him. I told him he was a fool. Love doesn't work like that." She began to walk away, then stopped, and spoke without turning. "The statute expires in nine days, Ivo. He waited fifty years for nine days. Don't you waste them."

Ivo watched her descend the path toward the lower houses, small and straight and brown as the earth she'd never left. Then they turned back to the wall, and took out their phone, and photographed the freshly painted names one last time—not for the archive, not for the police, but because their hands needed something to do that was not shaking.

Nine days. And a cathedral, and a bookbinder's bench, and a man with beautiful quiet hands who had been waiting half a century for Ivo to read the last page.

Ivo walked back to the car without looking at the village again. Some doors, once seen, could not be un-seen; and the road back was long, and every kilometer of it was a conversation Ivo did not yet know how to begin.
