## 2. Every Grave by Name

The morning after the exhumation, I did what I always do when the world misbehaves: I went to work early and pretended it hadn't.

The county morgue smells like formaldehyde and floor wax, which is a lie in itself — nothing about that room has ever been truly clean, just maintained. I signed the paperwork for an eighty-one-year-old man from Millbrook who had died the way most of us should be so lucky, in his sleep, mid-dream, with a half-eaten slice of lemon cake on the nightstand. Cause of death: cardiac arrest. Manner: natural. The universe occasionally honors its contracts.

Then I drove to Hollow Creek Cemetery, because there was one more contract I needed to check.

* * *

The cemetery sits on a hill above town like a chaperone at a school dance — present, disapproving, ultimately ignored. From its iron gate you can see all of Hollow Creek laid out below: the courthouse with its cupola, Main Street's brick facades, the skeleton of the old candleworks where the weeds have been winning a forty-year war. And dead center, visible from everywhere, the Vane mausoleum, white marble gone the color of old teeth, standing over this town the way a founder's monument should: expensively, and with secrets.

I walked the rows with a clipboard I didn't strictly need. Forty years of burials live in my head the way other people keep phone numbers. It's not a gift. It's a symptom. When your mother is the town's unofficial historian and your father died before you could disappoint him, you end up knowing things about people they'd rather you didn't. You become the keeper of dead truths. There's no plaque for it. There's just the weight.

Aldous Vane's funeral was April 14th, forty years ago. I know because my mother kept the newspaper clippings in a shoebox, and because I was eleven years old and stood at the edge of the crowd holding her hand while the entire population of Hollow Creek wept on cue.

I started at the gate and worked inward, row by row, saying names to myself like a rosary.

Row one: the old guard. Mayor Pro Tem Gus Halloran, who gave the eulogy and cried so hard he had to stop twice — buried here now, twelve years gone, under a stone that says BELOVED SERVANT OF HOLLOW CREEK. The Whitfield brothers, who owned the bank. Margaret Osei-Adjei, whose daughter Harriet would turn out to be the last living witness of anything in this town. All of them at the funeral. All of them front row.

Row two: the merchants. The Delgados, who ran the general store and closed it for three days of mourning — three days, for a man most of them had met exactly twice. The Pruitts. Old Sam Ferro, the barber, who cut Vane's hair every second Tuesday for thirty years and told anyone who'd listen that Mr. Vane had "the saddest eyes of any rich man I ever shaved." Sam's plot is unmarked; his family couldn't afford the stone after the candleworks closed and took half the town's wages with it.

Funny thing about that. The candleworks closed six months after Vane died. Nobody ever connected those facts out loud. In Hollow Creek, we don't connect dots out loud. We connect them quietly, at kitchen tables, and then we bury the string.

Row three: the workers. The men from the factory, buried in cheaper ground further from the view, because even in death Hollow Creek keeps its seating chart. And there — third from the end, granite going green with lichen — MARSH.

No first name. Just MARSH, and two dates, and between them a dash that stands for everything nobody bothered to record. Eli Marsh. Vane's business partner. The man whose name isn't in a single eulogy, though he co-founded the damn company. I remember my mother pausing at that grave once, years ago, when I was a teenager and too impatient to ask why. She'd stood there maybe a minute, her coat buttoned wrong, and then she'd said, "Come along, Bram," in the voice she used when a question would cost more than silence.

I stood there now. The wind came up the hill and moved through the pines, making that sound like the trees were being asked something.

Here's what rattles me, and I'll say it plainly since no one else is listening: Aldous Vane's coffin came up light yesterday. Not empty-empty — there was the letter, painted in a hand I haven't identified yet, nailed to the inside of the lid like a note pinned to a door. But no Vane. No bones, no dust, no wedding ring, no fillings. Forty years underground and the man simply wasn't home.

And the whole town turned out to bury him anyway. Four hundred mourners. A closed casket — of course a closed casket, caskets are always closed, that's the entire trick of them — carried by six pallbearers who are all dead now except one, and lowered into a vault that was sealed with a ceremony so solemn the minister's voice cracked on the word *resurrection*.

We wept for a man who wasn't in the box.

Or we wept for something else entirely, and used him as the excuse.

* * *

"You're talking to gravestones again."

Deputy Marisol Reyes came up the hill with two coffees and the particular walk of someone who has decided not to run but wants credit for considering it. She handed me one without asking if I wanted it. She knows me.

"I'm conducting a census."

"Of the dead."

"They're the only ones who answer honestly."

She looked at the Marsh stone, then at me, then back at the Marsh stone, doing the math she does — quick, quiet, complete. Reyes transferred here from Bakersfield four years ago and still hasn't learned the local dialect, which is the language of things left unsaid, spoken fluently by everyone born within fifty miles. Sometimes I think she stays just to watch us perform it.

"Coroner Ishikawa," she said, "you supervised an exhumation yesterday of the most famous grave in county history, found it empty except for a piece of artwork, filed a report that says, quote, 'anomalous interment conditions,' and then went home. You didn't call me. You didn't call the sheriff. You called the state lab and ordered a paint analysis." She sipped her coffee. "So I'm going to ask you a question, and I want you to hear how strange it sounds coming out of my mouth. Why does the coroner seem rattled?"

"I don't get rattled. I get methodical."

"You alphabetized the evidence bags. Twice."

"That's called rigor."

"That's called rattled with a clipboard." She nodded at the mausoleum up the hill. "Walk with me and tell me why a painted letter scares you more than a missing body does."

I walked. The gravel crunched. Somewhere below us a church bell rang nine, off-key, the way it has rung off-key since the earthquake of '89 cracked the bell and the town voted that replacing it was less important than repaving the parking lot. Hollow Creek in miniature: keep the surface smooth, let the soul ring wrong.

"A body tells you how someone died," I said. "That's my whole job. Bodies are honest. They can't help it. But a letter — a letter is chosen. Somebody sat down with a brush and made a decision about what future people would find. That's not evidence. That's *communication*. Evidence happens to you. Communication is meant for you."

"And you think it's meant for you?"

"I think it's meant for whoever dug. Which, as of yesterday, is me."

Reyes stopped at the top of row three, where the ground flattens before the mausoleum rise. She read the Marsh stone the way she reads everything — thoroughly, suspiciously, like it might be armed.

"Eli Marsh," she said. "That name was in the file box I pulled last night. You know that, right? I pulled everything touching Vane. Business licenses, land deeds, obituaries." She tapped the granite. "This guy vanishes the same week Vane dies, and the obituary for Vane doesn't mention him once. Co-founders. Same company. Same street address on the letterhead. Nothing." She looked at me. "In Bakersfield we'd call that a lead. Here, do we call it something else?"

"We call it nothing," I said. "That's the local specialty. We've been calling it nothing for forty years."

"Who's *we*, Bram?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question. Because I'm not an outsider looking in at Hollow Creek's lies. My mother's handwriting is somewhere in this story — I felt it yesterday when I saw that painted A, a cold recognition I haven't said out loud to anyone, the way you feel a fever before the thermometer confirms it. She kept the clippings. She kept the photographs. She kept a shoebox of this town's grief like it was hers to curate, and maybe it was, and maybe that's the problem.

"My mother attended that funeral," I said. "Everyone did. That's the point. Four hundred people watched a coffin go into the ground, and every single one of them believed, or said they believed, or agreed to believe. Grief like that doesn't happen spontaneously. It's organized. Somebody rang the bell and everybody came."

"People loved him."

"People loved the *story* of him. The patriarch. The builder. The man who gave the town its name on the sign and its name in the mouth." I gestured at the rows behind us, the stones marching downhill toward the living town. "Look at this. Look at who's here. The councilmen. The bankers. The merchants. The factory men. They all came. They all wept. And six months later the factory closed and half of them lost everything, and they wept about that too, and never once did anybody stand up in the church or the diner or the council chamber and say, wait a minute — the week our benefactor dies, his partner disappears and the money dries up? What are the odds?"

"Pretty good odds," Reyes said, "if the benefactor didn't die."

I didn't answer. The wind filled in for me.

* * *

I'll tell you what I actually remember about that day, forty years ago, because memory is the only archive this town keeps properly indexed.

It rained in the morning and cleared by noon, which everyone later called provident. My mother wore her navy dress and white gloves and held my hand so tightly my fingers went numb, and I remember thinking that was strange, because she hadn't liked Aldous Vane — she'd said once, mildly, over dishes, that he was "a man who counts other people's candles." But at the graveside she wept, really wept, tears cutting channels through her powder, and when I asked her why she was crying she said, "Because it's over," in a voice I couldn't parse then and have been parsing ever since.

Because it's over. Not *he's gone*. Not *what a loss*. *It's over.* Like the end of a play. Like the curtain coming down on something long and exhausting, and the audience rising, relieved, to applaud.

There was a woman near us in a black veil who didn't cry. I remember her because everyone else was crying and grief makes non-grief conspicuous. She stood very straight with her hands folded, and when the coffin descended she turned away before it reached the bottom, as if she already knew how deep it went. My mother squeezed my hand harder when she saw her. Later I learned the woman was Celeste Vane's mother — the founder's own daughter — and that she left town within the month and never came back, and that the town explained this as grief too painful to bear among familiar streets.

Grief. Always grief. This town's universal solvent. Pour it over anything and the thing dissolves.

* * *

Reyes and I reached the mausoleum. The flood damage was worse in daylight — a crack running diagonally across the facade like a scar, the foundation stones shifted, the county's orange warning tape snapping in the wind. Inside, through the door we'd left chained, the empty vault waited with its patient dark.

"The lab called at six this morning," I said. "Preliminary. The paint on the letter is oil-based, lead white and a red oxide, consistent with formulations from the late seventies. Not recent. Whoever painted that A painted it around the time Vane supposedly died, and it's been sitting in that coffin ever since."

"So it's not a prank."

"It's a message with a forty-year delay. Which means somebody expected the coffin to be opened eventually, and expected it to be opened by someone who'd understand." I looked at the crack in the marble. "Or hoped. Or — arranged."

"Arranged how?"

"Floods don't choose targets, Deputy. But maintenance records do. That mausoleum's drainage was 'inspected' fourteen times in forty years, always passed, always by the same engineering firm. The firm that's been on retainer to the Vane estate since 1971." I let that sit. "I'm not saying anything yet. I'm saying the ground gives way exactly when the statute of limitations on whatever happened forty years ago is about to expire, and I don't believe in that kind of coincidence anymore. I used to. It was easier."

Reyes was quiet for a moment. Then: "You've been walking these rows for two hours reading names off stones. Who were you looking for?"

"Everyone who was there," I said. "Because here's the thing about a town that lies to itself, Marisol. It needs keepers. Someone has to maintain the lie — water it, prune it, make sure it blooms on schedule every anniversary. And the keepers get old. And when they die—" I nodded at the rows, the hundreds of them, the whole congregation of the silent— "they take their piece of it down here with them. Every grave by name. Every name with a secret sewn into the lining. And the lie gets thinner and thinner until one spring the flood comes and the ground finally gives."

She followed my gaze down the hill, to the town, smoke rising from chimneys, the flag over the courthouse snapping bright and certain against the blue.

"So who's left?" she asked. "Who's still keeping it?"

I thought of my mother's shoebox in my closet. Of the navy dress. Of *because it's over*. Of a woman in a black veil turning away before the coffin reached bottom.

"One way to find out," I said. "The lab report on that paint comes back tomorrow. And I want to see what else was in the coffin besides the letter — the nails, the lining, the wood. If somebody built that box to be opened, they built it with instructions."

Reyes drained her coffee and dropped the cup in the bin by the gate, a clean shot, no bounce.

"You know," she said, "in Bakersfield, when a grave turns up empty, we look for the body."

"In Hollow Creek," I said, "we look for the reason there's no body. It's a much longer list."

I locked the mausoleum chain, and as I did, my eye caught the threshold stone — the one every mourner crossed forty years ago, worn smooth by four hundred pairs of shoes. And there, low in the corner, half-filled with flood silt, was a mark I'd taken for damage yesterday.

It wasn't damage.

It was a curve. Deliberate. Painted, once, long ago, in the same red-brown as the letter in the coffin — the beginning stroke of another character, mostly eroded now, mostly, but not quite, gone.

Someone had marked the door.

And I was suddenly, urgently, done walking among the dead. I wanted the lab report. I wanted the coffin's lining under magnification. I wanted to know what the rest of that hidden alphabet spelled, because I was no longer sure the letters were addressed to whoever dug.

They might be addressed to whoever *buried*.

I climbed into my car and sat for a moment with the engine off, watching the Marsh stone in the rearview mirror shrink down the hill.

*Come along, Bram,* my mother said, in the voice that cost more than silence.

Not this time, Mother. This time I'm counting the candles.
