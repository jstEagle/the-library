## 4. What Mother Kept

The boxes had been in the garage for eleven months, which is how long it takes a coroner to develop the courage to open cardboard.

I want to be clear about that: I handle death for a living. I have looked into more faces than most priests, and I have never once flinched at the work. But my mother's handwriting on a shipping label — *Bram's winter coats, maybe* — that could stop me cold in my own driveway for twenty minutes on a Tuesday evening. Grief is not rational about its targets. It will walk past a mausoleum without a shiver and then ambush you over a box of sweaters.

The flood had given me an excuse, at least. Water had gotten into the garage through a cracked sill, and three of the boxes sat in a tide line of silt, their bottoms gone soft as old fruit. If I didn't sort them now, I'd be sorting slurry later. So I told myself it was practical. I told myself a lot of things that week. It was the week after the coffin came up empty, and I was looking for anything in the world that behaved according to rules.

My mother, Sana Ishikawa, had been dead since last spring — heart, in her sleep, in the house she'd refused to leave even after Dad passed, because Hollow Creek was *her* town the way blood is your blood, whether you like what it carries or not. She'd been the town's art teacher for thirty-one years. Every mural in the elementary school hallway had her fingerprints somewhere under the varnish. Every kid who ever held a brush in this county learned their grip from her. When she died, half the town came to the funeral, and people said the same sentence over and over, the way they do: *She taught me everything I know about color.*

It occurs to me now, going through her things, that nobody ever asked what she knew about paint that she hadn't taught.

* * *

The first box was exactly what the label promised. Winter coats, smelling faintly of cedar and mothballs, one sleeve still holding a movie ticket stub from 1998. The second box was kitchen things — her good serving spoons, a ceramic rooster with a chipped comb that I'd knocked off the counter when I was nine and glued back badly, and she'd kept it anyway, displayed it anyway, for forty years, so that every guest in her house saw my childhood mistake sitting next to the fine china. That's a kind of love I don't have language for. I'm a coroner. We deal in facts. That rooster was a fact, and it made me sit down on the garage floor for a while.

The third box was labeled, in her hand, simply: *Studio.*

Not studio supplies. Not paints. Just *Studio*, like the whole room she'd kept locked-ish and unremarked-upon for decades had been folded up small enough to fit in cardboard. My mother's studio had been off-limits in the way of things that aren't forbidden but are somehow never available. As a kid I'd stood in its doorway plenty of times — turpentine smell, north light, jars of brushes standing at attention like soldiers — but I'd never been invited past the threshold while she worked, and she'd always seemed to be working. Even retired, even after her hands started shaking, she went out there every morning. The summer before she died, she spent six weeks repainting the picket fence around the historical society. Nobody had asked her to. The fence had been fine.

I opened the box expecting brushes. What I got was a rag.

It was a shop rag, cotton, once white, folded into a neat square with the particular compression of something put away deliberately rather than tossed. And across its face, in strokes of dried paint — ochre, mostly, with a vein of black and a smear of something that might have been iron red — was the ghost of a letter.

I knew the shape before I let myself know it. You spend two weeks staring at a thing nailed inside a coffin lid, you start seeing it everywhere. The crossbar. The diagonal leg. The confident terminal flick at the end of the stroke where a hand lifted the brush away satisfied.

An A.

The same A that had been nailed inside Aldous Vane's coffin forty years ago, painted in ochre with a vein of black, dated by the lab to within a hair of his supposed death year. The same A that had turned my professional calm inside out and left me walking the cemetery at odd hours like a man checking whether the ground itself was lying.

And here it was again, or its twin, folded up in my dead mother's studio box like a napkin after dinner.

I did what any reasonable person does. I told myself it was nothing. Art teachers paint letters. Art teachers paint letters *constantly* — classroom alphabets, parade banners, set pieces for the spring play. My mother had painted the entire alphabet onto the kindergarten wall in 1987; I remembered the smell of the enamel coming home on her cardigan. A paint-stained rag proved nothing except that my mother had been, her whole life, exactly what everyone said she was.

Then I found the photograph, and the reasonable person in me lost the argument.

* * *

It was tucked inside a book — her old college copy of Kandinsky's *Concerning the Spiritual in Art*, spine broken, marginalia in pencil. The photo slid out from between pages 40 and 41, where she'd apparently used it as a bookmark for longer than I've been alive.

Five children, standing on the steps of the Vane house, squinting into sun that had probably set before I was born.

I recognized the house immediately. Everyone in Hollow Creek would. It's the historical society now, the one whose fence my mother had repainted unprompted, the one whose basement mural had coughed up a painted B during restoration work two days ago. In the photo it was just a big gray pile of money with a porch, and on the porch steps stood five kids arranged with the stiff formality children are forced into when adults want proof of an occasion.

Aldous Vane's five grandchildren, presumably. Or his children — the dating was fuzzy, the way old photos are fuzzy, all that era's faces slightly melted by cheap film. There was a girl front and center, maybe ten, dark-haired, holding a paintbrush the way other kids hold trophies. Beside her, a boy with a cowlick and a scowl. Behind them, three more: a tall girl with her arm slung possessively around the scowling boy's shoulders, a smaller child half-hidden at the edge of frame, and another boy at the end, grinning like he'd been told a joke the others weren't in on.

And there, kneeling at the edge of the group in a work apron, sixteen years old at most, was a girl I knew better than I knew my own face.

My mother.

Younger than I'd ever seen her. Dark hair cut bluntly at the jaw, that same level gaze she gave me whenever I lied about having done my homework — the look that said *we both know, and we'll get there*. Her hands were stained to the wrist. There were paint cans at her feet. And the girl front and center — the one with the brush, the one positioned like the star of the composition — had her free hand resting on my mother's shoulder like they were conspirators.

On the back, in pencil, in a hand that wasn't my mother's — looser, loopier, a child's cursive grown up into an adult's signature I'd seen on campaign flyers:

*The five of us + S., June 19—. A. says we'll each do our letter & he'll keep them forever.*

*A. says.* Aldous says. Aldous Vane, patriarch, founder, the man whose coffin I had personally supervised opening, the man who was not in it.

I sat in my garage until the light went blue and then went dark, holding a photograph of my mother kneeling at the feet of the Vane family like a hired hand, and I ran the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the mausoleum.

The letters matched the coffin. The lab dated the A to the year of Vane's celebrated funeral. The B surfaced in the historical society mural — a building my mother had volunteered at, painted for, maintained with a devotion that had always read as civic-mindedness and now read as something else. Something like maintenance. Like upkeep. Like a woman checking, decade after decade, that a secret was still sealed under its varnish.

And here was a photograph proving she'd been painting alongside the Vane children the very summer the letters were made, with instructions from Aldous himself that they'd each do *their* letter and he'd keep them *forever*.

Forever, as it turned out, meant until a flood cracked the mausoleum and a coroner with an inconvenient surname pried the lid.

* * *

Here's the thing about being the town's keeper of dead truths: you learn to hold two ideas in your hands at once without dropping either. The body can tell you one story and the paperwork another, and your job is to stand between them and refuse to blink. I've done it hundreds of times. I've stood in front of grieving families and told them things they didn't want true, and I've stood in front of comfortable people and told them things they'd paid to keep untrue, and I thought my hands were steady.

They were not steady that night.

Because the second idea — the one I couldn't put down — was this: my mother had known. Whatever the letters were, whatever Aldous Vane had actually died of or hadn't, my mother had stood in the middle of it with paint on her hands, and she had come home from it and raised me in this town and taught its children color and never, not once in my life, let a word of it slip. Forty years of Sundays. Forty years of casseroles for the Vane family at election time. Forty years of her telling me, when I took the coroner's job, *Good. This town needs someone honest counting its dead.*

Counting its dead. I heard it differently now.

I tried the innocent explanations one more time, the way you try a door you know is locked. Maybe she was just the art tutor. Maybe the letters were a children's game, a summer project, harmless. Maybe the rag was coincidence, the mural volunteerism coincidence, the photograph a bookmark and nothing more.

But I kept coming back to the rag's fold. Neat. Deliberate. Kept. People don't keep rags. People keep evidence, or keepsakes, and the terrible possibility I couldn't rule out was that for my mother those had been the same category. That she'd folded up that afternoon in 19— and put it away, and taken it out sometimes, alone, in her locked studio, and looked at it the way I was looking at it now.

Was she one of the painters? Was she the painter? The A in the coffin had been done by a skilled hand — controlled, sure, the stroke of someone who'd held a brush ten thousand hours. The B in the mural, same hand, I'd have sworn it under oath. And my mother's hand was the only one in this town I'd watched make letters my entire childhood, on walls and fences and parade floats, patient and exact.

The five children of the founder. Each doing *their* letter. A, B, C — I'd found two. Somewhere out there were D, E, F, letters waiting in walls and attics and grave goods, planted by children who grew into the founding families of Hollow Creek, and my mother had knelt among them with paint to her wrists.

I put the photograph back in the Kandinsky. I put the rag in an evidence bag, which felt obscene and necessary at the same time. Then I sat in the dark garage and did the thing I'd been circling all evening: I let myself miss her and suspect her in the same breath, and discovered they fit together without any gap at all. That's the cruelty nobody warns you about. Love doesn't pause for the investigation. It sits right there beside the suspicion, keeping it warm.

Tomorrow I'd have to pull the historical society's membership rolls from that era. Tomorrow I'd have to find out who the other four children were, and which of them were still alive, and which of them had attended the funeral of a man whose coffin they'd helped empty. Tomorrow Reyes would ask me questions I wasn't ready to answer honestly, and I'd have to decide, again, what kind of keeper of dead truths I was going to be.

But tonight, one more thing. Because when I'd repacked the box, my hand had found a final item wedged against the bottom flap, thin and flat, wrapped in wax paper the way my mother wrapped anything she considered precious: a single bristle brush, sable, size two, its ferrule stamped with a maker's mark, its tip stained with ochre that no amount of turpentine had ever fully freed.

The kind of brush you use for a letter small enough to hide.

And pinned to the wax paper, in my mother's handwriting — the same hand that had labeled the boxes, the same hand that had painted an alphabet across my childhood — four words that I read a dozen times before the meaning landed like a dropped coffin:

*For Bram. Eventually.*

Eventually had arrived, Mother. The flood saw to that.
