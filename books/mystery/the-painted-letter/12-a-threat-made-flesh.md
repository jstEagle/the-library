## 12. A Threat Made Flesh

I have outlived four presidents, two husbands, one very bad marriage that doesn't deserve to be counted among presidents, and every single person who stood with me at Aldous Vane's funeral forty years ago. I have earned the right to be smug about it. I intend to exercise that right daily, usually over breakfast, addressing my toast directly.

So you'll understand why it irritated me — genuinely irritated me, like a pebble in a shoe — when young Bram Ishikawa sat across from my kitchen table three days ago and asked, in that careful coroner's voice they must teach in coroner school right after *how to say 'I'm sorry for your loss' without blinking*, whether I remembered anything unusual about the funeral.

"Unusual," I repeated. "Bram, I was twenty-six years old. The whole town cried so hard the gutters overflowed. The only unusual thing was that Deacon Pruitt's toupee blew off during the eulogy and nobody had the decency to tell him."

"And the alphabet?" Bram said. "You recited it. Unprompted."

Ah. That.

I had done it too, hadn't I. Forty years of keeping my mouth shut and then an Ishikawa sits down across from me with their mother's eyes and my mouth just — opens. Like a drawer that's been stuck for decades and finally gives.

"A, B, C, D," I'd said, and then stopped myself like a woman catching a plate before it hits the floor. "E, F..." I pressed my lips together. "Old habit. We taught the children letters that year. The Vane school initiative. It was a whole thing."

It was not a whole thing. There was no Vane school initiative. But Bram had nodded the way polite people nod at old women, and I had let them believe it, because some lies are load-bearing. You don't pull them out to see what happens. You paint around them. You keep the house standing.

That was the whole art of Hollow Creek, really. Painting around things.

* * *

Let me tell you what I actually remember about that spring, since I'm apparently in a confessing mood and there's no one left alive to contradict me except a mayor and a couple of heirs who'd rather I were dead, which — well. Hold that thought.

Aldous Vane did not die of his heart. I know this because I was the one who answered the telephone at the Osei house when the call came, and the voice on the other end was Celeste's mother, Vivienne, and Vivienne Vane did not sound like a grieving daughter. She sounded like a woman reading from a script she'd rehearsed in the car. *"He's gone, Harriet. It's done. You know what has to happen now."*

Done. As though it were a quilt she'd finished binding.

There were five of us children of the founding families who knew — five of us who'd been summoned to the candleworks office the night before the funeral and shown a letter. Not a written letter. A painted one. A great looping **A** on board, still tacky at the edges, and Vivienne standing over it with wet red hands saying, "If anything happens to me, this gets found. All of them get found."

All of them. Because there wasn't just one. There was going to be a whole alphabet scattered across Hollow Creek like Easter eggs, each one a piece of the confession Vivienne had written out in her dead father's name — or rather, in the name of the man her father had killed, Eli Marsh, whose body Vivienne and her father's foreman had loaded into a coffin while the town planned a funeral for a man who was, at that very moment, alive and packing a suitcase in the next county.

Wait. Let me get this straight in my own head, because even now the order of it slides around like fish.

Vane killed Marsh — that part was true, that part was always true, the argument over the factory money, the blow that went too far. And then Vane's own death was staged — the heart attack announced, the closed casket, the ground we all wept over — so that he could run before the Marsh family's lawyers could pin the killing on him. Vivienne stayed behind to hold the town together and hold the secret shut, and she painted her confession in pieces because she couldn't bear to write it whole, and she made five of us swear on our mothers' graves to help hide the letters and never, ever speak of it.

I was twenty-six. I swore on my mother's grave. My mother was not yet in it, which should have been my first clue that I was doing it wrong.

* * *

The photograph is the thing I keep coming back to. Five children on the steps of the candleworks, summer maybe 1948, squinting into the sun: Vivienne Vane with her chin up like she already owned the building; Eli Marsh's boy Thomas grinning gap-toothed; the Calloway girl; the Pruitt boy; and me, Harriet Osei, nine years old and already suspicious of everyone in a nice dress.

Vivienne gave us each a copy the night of the swearing-in. "So you remember who you're protecting," she said. I kept mine in the bottom of my sewing box under sixty years of buttons, and I looked at it more often than I'd admit, mostly at Thomas Marsh, wondering if he ever learned what happened to his father, and whether knowing would have broken him or freed him.

Three nights ago, after Bram left, I took the photograph out and looked at it again, and I noticed something I'd never noticed, which at my age is either a miracle or a symptom. In the background, in the window of the candleworks office, there was a shape. A board. Painted. One letter, half-visible through the glass.

**A**.

She'd already started. Even then. Before her father died, before any of it — or perhaps the photograph was taken after, and time has gone soft in my head the way butter goes soft on a warm sill. Either way: she'd started. The alphabet wasn't a deathbed penance. It was a project. Something she worked on the way other women work on needlepoint, a little at a time, all her life.

And if she worked on it all her life, then the letters weren't hidden once. They were *re*-hidden. Moved. Maintained. Which meant somebody had been tending them, all these years, the way you tend a fire you're not allowed to let go out.

Which meant whoever was killing the letter-keepers — because I read the paper, I may be old but I'm not wallpaper — wasn't looking for something lost. They were looking for something *kept*.

* * *

Yesterday morning I found my back gate open.

Now, my gate sticks. It has stuck since the Ford administration. It does not open itself, and it certainly does not swing gently shut behind whatever came through it. When I saw it ajar I stood on my porch with my coffee for a long moment, doing arithmetic. Then I went inside and checked the sewing box.

The photograph was still there. The buttons were still there. Everything was still there, which frightened me considerably more than absence would have, because it meant someone had looked and judged it not worth taking yet. Or — worse, and I want you to appreciate how much worse — someone had looked and wanted me to know they'd looked. A courtesy call. A knock before the knock.

I did what any sensible woman of eighty-four would do. I locked the gate, put the photograph in my coat pocket, and called no one, because who would I call? The sheriff's department is Reyes and a part-timer. Bram Ishikawa is elbow-deep in a mausoleum. And Mayor Vane — Celeste, little Celeste, who used to sell lemonade outside my house for a nickel and always shortchanged herself rather than cheat a customer — Celeste Vane is the last living person with a reason to want an old woman quiet.

I didn't want it to be her. I want you to know that. I sat up half the night wanting it not to be her, remembering the nickel lemonade, remembering how she cried at her mother's funeral the way none of us had been allowed to cry at her grandfather's. But wanting is not evidence, and neither is lemonade, and the fact remained that every question Bram asked was a question aimed, eventually, at the Vane family, and the statute clock was running, and I was the last witness whose memory still worked.

Harriet, I told myself, you are going to be so much trouble.

I've never been trouble in my life. That was the deal, wasn't it? Keep quiet, stay small, live long. Loyalty as a pension plan. And it paid out, I'll grant it — this house, this garden, the respect of a town that thinks I'm charming. But the interest on that loan has been coming due my whole life, one sleepless night at a time, and yesterday, standing in my kitchen holding a photograph of five children who never got to be anything but accomplices, I decided the debt was paid.

I was going to tell Bram everything. Today. This morning, in fact. I had it all planned out — I'd make the tea first, because confessions go down easier with tea, and I'd start with the telephone call, *done, you know what has to happen now*, and I'd end with the window in the photograph, and I'd watch Bram's face do the thing faces do when a story finally becomes a shape.

I laid the photograph on the kitchen table next to my good teacup so I wouldn't forget it. I am eighty-four; forgetting is a professional hazard. Then I went to bed, and I slept better than I had in forty years, which I mention only because it seems unfair. You finally decide to be honest and the reward is one good night's sleep. The universe has a stingy sense of humor.

* * *

The floorboard woke me.

Third stair from the top. Everyone's house has its voice, and mine has been complaining about that stair since 1987. Nobody who lived here would step on it. Nobody who belonged here would be here at all.

I did not do anything brave. I want that on the record. I lay in my bed in the dark with my hand on the telephone and my heart going like a sewing machine, and I thought about dialing, and I thought about what I'd say — *there's someone in my house, come quickly, I'm the last witness* — and I understood, with terrible clarity, that by the time anyone came, whatever was going to happen would have happened, and that the person downstairs had counted on exactly that. Small town. Slow response. An old woman's death makes no noise at all if you let it look natural.

Natural. That was the word the whole conspiracy had lived on. Natural causes. A natural grief. A naturally closed casket. We dressed everything in nature's clothing and called it peace.

The stairs creaked their slow way up. I had time for exactly two thoughts. The first was that I should have given the photograph to Bram three days ago instead of hiding it in my coat like a coward with buttons.

The second was the alphabet. My alphabet. The one I'd taught to actual schoolchildren, back before, back when letters were innocent — and I lay there in the dark finishing it, silently, defiantly, all the way to the end, because if these were my last minutes I was going to spend them on something true:

*A, B, C, D, E, F, G.*

The door opened.

*H, I, J.*

I did not see the face. I want to say that plainly, for whoever reads this, if anyone reads this: I never saw the face, and I hope that haunts them, the way not-knowing has haunted me.

*K.*

* * *

They found me the next afternoon, which is embarrassing — a whole day in my own kitchen before anyone missed me. Reyes kicked the door in, poor thing, apologetic even in the breaking. I was at the table, head on my arms, teacup beside me, photograph gone from where I'd left it. The medical examiner said my heart. Said it kindly, even. *At her age, in her sleep, peaceful.* Natural.

But Bram Ishikawa stood in my kitchen a long time, and Bram noticed what the ME didn't, or wouldn't: the smell of smoke that didn't belong, faint as a ghost, rising from my cold fireplace. And in the ash, curled like a dead moth, the corner of a photograph — five children on some steps, burned past saving, all but one small square of sky and the edge of a window.

Through the glass of that window, half-consumed but unmistakable, a single painted letter.

Bram turned it over twice in gloved fingers, and then looked up at my body at my table, and said quietly, to me or to God or to the room, "You were going to tell me."

Yes, child. I was. I had the tea ready and everything.

And somewhere across town, whoever had stood on my third stair was already deciding which of us would be found next.
