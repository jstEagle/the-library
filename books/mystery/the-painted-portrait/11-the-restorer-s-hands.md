## 11. The Restorer's Hands

*Dictated to tape, Wellspring Hospice, Room 9, October 2003. Transcribed by hand for N. Lockridge.*

They tell me I have weeks. The doctor said it kindly, the way people say things they've said a thousand times, and I thanked him, because what else is there? So now I will do the one thing left that requires a steady hand. Not painting. Talking.

Noor Lockridge. If you are reading this, then you found me the way I knew you would — through the letters, through the file, through whatever strange gift lives in your head that lets you hold a page in your mind forever after seeing it once. Your mother had a version of it too, did you know? Not memory. Something adjacent. Iris could look at a room and know what was wrong with it. A vase moved two inches. A lie wearing the truth's clothes. It's why Margaret kept her close, and it's why Iris ran. But I'm getting ahead of myself. Dying men get ahead of themselves; we're all just trying to arrive somewhere before the end.

Let me start where it started, which is not with fire. It starts with a brush.

* * *

I came to Ashcombe House in 1974, twenty-three years old, with a portfolio of copies so good my professors had expelled me for them rather than praise me. That's the joke of forgery — the better you are, the worse your crime looks on paper. I had copied a Vermeer from a postcard at sixteen because I couldn't afford the book. By twenty I could paint in any hand that had ever lived, except my own. I didn't have one. That's not false modesty; ask anyone who knew me then. Tomas Reyes, the man of no style. A voice that could only imitate.

Margaret Ashcombe looked at my portfolio for eleven minutes — I counted, counting being another thing I do — and then she closed it and said, "You must be very lonely."

Not *you're talented*. Not *you're hired*. She looked at me the way restorers look under varnish, past the surface, into the ground layer, and she saw the thing I'd spent twenty-three years hiding: that a man who can be anyone is no one, and loneliness is the occupational disease.

Then she said, "I can fix that," and God forgive me, I believed her. Because she did. For thirty years, she did.

The gallery was real, in the beginning. Legitimate acquisitions, honest sales, a small restoration studio in the back where I worked on other people's paintings — cleaning, relining, inpainting losses. Elias Vane was there when I arrived, already the resident genius, already painting those gray coastal interiors that made his name. He taught me color. I taught him patience. We were friends. I want that on the record before anything else, because everything else will make it sound like I hated him, and I never hated him. Hating him would have been easier to live with than what I actually felt.

The first forgery wasn't planned. That's the part nobody believes, but it's true, and if you've read this far you might as well believe the true parts. A client wanted a minor Sargent study — watercolor, seaside, the kind of thing that changes hands quietly. We couldn't source one. Margaret mentioned this to me over coffee the way she mentioned everything, as weather, as nothing, and I said — flippant, showing off — "I could paint you one by Friday."

She didn't laugh. She looked at me for a long time, and I watched her decide something, and then she said, "Would you?"

And here is the confession underneath the confession, Noor: I said yes because she asked. Not for money — though the money came, and it was good, and I was poor. Not for thrill. Because Margaret Ashcombe was the first person who had ever looked at my hands and seen a self instead of an absence. When she asked *would you*, what I heard was *you matter, your gift matters, it has a purpose*. Every lonely boy dreams of being needed like that. She knew it. She built a house out of us strays precisely because we were starving for exactly that sentence.

I painted the Sargent in four days. It sold. Nobody ever doubted it. And Margaret came down to the studio that night, stood over the drying paper, and touched my shoulder, and said, "Now you're one of us."

One of us. Do you understand the machinery of that phrase? It doesn't invite you into a family. It tells you there was always a family, and you were always meant for it, and the door behind you has quietly locked. Ruth felt it. Simon felt it. Iris felt it most of all, I think, because Iris was the youngest and the sharpest and the one who eventually noticed the lock.

* * *

Over nineteen years we placed — I'll use the word *placed*, the trade word, the word that lets you sleep — forty-one works. Minor pieces, mostly. Watercolors, studies, drawings, the margins of famous careers where provenance thins out and money doesn't look too closely. Never a major canvas. Margaret had rules. Rules are how criminals forgive themselves; I know this now. Rule one: never forge a masterpiece, only its shadows. Rule two: never sell twice to the same institution. Rule three: everyone eats, everyone stays quiet, everyone is family.

Rule three was the whole engine. She paid us from the gallery, off-ledger, in envelopes she sealed herself. She paid our rents when we faltered, our medical bills, once — I'll say this plainly — Iris's tuition, and later, quietly, money toward a child she'd never met. You. There were payments marked *I.L. — dependent* for years. I did the books some winters. I saw your name before you were born, in her handwriting, in the same ink she used for the sales ledger.

So when you come to judge her — and you will, you're built for judging, it's your trade — weigh this alongside it: every dollar of that fraud fed someone. She called it patronage. The IRS would call it laundering. Both are right. That's the ugliest truth about Margaret, the one the second letter promised you: she wasn't a monster who pretended to love us. She loved us genuinely, lavishly, and the love and the crime were the same act, indistinguishable, like pigment and binder in dried oil. You cannot separate them without destroying both.

Elias couldn't live inside that. He was the only one of us who had a self before Margaret found him — he'd been somebody since childhood, a man with his own name in his own mouth — and so the family thing never quite took. He suspected early. I think he knew by 1990. He said nothing for years because he loved her too, differently, the way you love a parent who raised you even after you learn what they are. And because Delphine adored Margaret, and Elias would have burned the world before he let his daughter see it burn.

But ledgers accumulate. Envelopes accumulate. In the spring of '98, an appraiser named Corliss — you may have heard the name recently, in connection with a staircase — got curious about a Fenwick gouache we'd placed in Boston, and started pulling threads. Margaret decided the evidence had to die. All of it: the practice studies in my studio, the chemical tests, the correspondence, the duplicate ledger pages I kept against the day she turned on one of us. Forgers keep insurance too.

She asked me to burn it. She didn't order; she never ordered. She sat across from me in the studio, seventy-eight years old, hands folded, and said, "Tomas, I need you to protect the children." The children. Four adults in their forties, and one woman of fifty-one, and she said *the children*, and my eyes stung, and I said yes.

That's how it works. That's the whole trick, the entire architecture of her. She never made anyone do anything. She just told you what love required, and you did it, and afterward you believed you'd chosen.

* * *

The plan was simple and I designed it, which means the plan's failures are mine, all of them, including the one that matters.

Saturday, June 13th, 1998. The gallery closed at six. Elias was in Provence — he went every June, three weeks of light, he lived for it. The building would be empty. I came in at nine through the delivery entrance with two cans of solvent-thinned turpentine and a timer rigged from a darkroom clock. Fire in the archive room and the studio, doors shut, smoke doing most of the work. Paper burns fast when you want it to. The point was never spectacle; the point was ash. By morning, every document that could hang us would be carbon, and the insurance would rebuild the gallery, and Margaret would stand in front of the cameras with wet eyes and a coat over her shoulders, and we would all be safe inside the family forever.

I set the timers. I walked out at 9:40. I remember the streetlight outside, the smell of the harbor, the ordinary sweetness of a June night. I remember thinking: tomorrow this is over, and Monday I go back to cleaning other people's paintings, and no one ever knows.

Elias came home Tuesday instead of July.

His flight changed — a sale fell through in Arles, he told people later, he wanted his own bed. He heard the gallery had suffered a fire, and being Elias, being the man who checked on things, being the resident genius with keys to everything, he came down that first evening while the building was still smoking, while the investigators were still days away, and he went inside.

Why? I have asked myself this for five years and I have stopped pretending I don't know the answer. He went in because he knew what was supposed to burn. He went in because he understood, faster than any investigator would have, that the fire's location was a signature — archive room, studio, nowhere else, nothing valuable touched — and that signature spelled *forgery ring* in letters only family could read. He went in to see whether the duplicate ledger pages survived. Whether there was anything left to save, or to bury, or to bargain with.

And the floor gave. The old building, the water from the hoses, the burned joists above the archive — he went through into the basement and the ceiling came down after him, and he died alone in the dark under the room where I had learned to paint lies, twelve hours after I drove away believing no one would be hurt.

He wasn't supposed to be there. Write that down however you need to write it. I have written it ten thousand times on the inside of my skull. *He wasn't supposed to be there.* It is true and it is worthless, because intent is a luxury the dead don't audit. I built a fire to destroy paper and it destroyed Elias Vane, and every day since, when my hands shake too badly to hold a fork, some honest part of me says: good. Let them shake. Let them fail you at everything, Tomas. It's the least they can do.

* * *

Here is what I need you to carry out of this room, Noor, because I won't last long enough to say it twice.

First: the originals exist. The real paintings — the ones we copied, sourced over the years from estates and storage and one very bad decade of Margaret's buying — never burned. They were never in the gallery. They are in the place I chose in 1985 when I began keeping my own insurance against her, and I will tell you where before I finish this tape, and you will understand why I waited until now, because as long as I breathed, the family had a reason to let me breathe gently.

Second: the fire was arson but the death was accident, and I will swear to both in any court that will still take my oath. What happened after — that's not mine to confess. Ask the others what they swore, and when, and to whom, and notice how neatly the stories fit together, like brushwork in a single hand. Rehearsed alibis have a texture. You'll feel it. You feel everything.

Third — and put down your pen for this one, because I want you to hear it with your whole head —

There were two letters, you said, when you sat by my bed. One from me. One promising the truth was uglier.

I wrote one letter in my life about Ashcombe. The confession. My hand, my typewriter, my guilt. I did not write the second. And Noor — listen — whoever did write it knows about the originals. Knows about the ledger. Knew enough to aim you at me like a bullet down a barrel, and let you walk out of this hospice carrying everything I've given you tonight.

A person like that isn't confessing. A person like that is using you to dig up the grave so they can decide what gets buried in it.

Find out whose hand it was before you open anything else. Because I've seen that handwriting before — years ago, on gallery correspondence, on thank-you notes, on the cards Margaret sent every Christmas.

And I never forgot a hand in my life.
