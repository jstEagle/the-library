## 12. The Magistrate's Mercy

I had rehearsed the conversation on the walk over — not the words, exactly, but the weather of it. Magistrates, I reasoned, were like rare books: valuable, brittle, and best approached with clean hands and no sudden movements. I had decided Odile Ferrant would deny everything with the smooth efficiency of a woman who had been denying things for thirty-seven years, and that I would leave her chambers with nothing but the smell of her furniture polish and a fresh entry in my catalogue of Marrowgate's hypocrisies.

I was wrong in every particular, which is why I have never fully trusted my own reasoning since.

The magistrates' court sat on Wexford Street in a building that had once been a corn exchange and had the air of a place still trading in something — only now the commodity was other people's lives, bought and sold by the bushel. Ferrant's chambers were on the top floor, up a staircase worn into shallow waves by a century of anxious feet. Her clerk, a young man with the complexion of someone who had never been told bad news personally, announced me as "the archivist from St. Aldhelm's," and I heard the words travel through the door ahead of me like a cold draft.

She was standing at her window when I entered, looking down at the street. She did not turn around at once.

"Xanthe Ravensworth," she said. "I wondered how long it would take you."

"You know my name."

"I've known your name for twenty years, Mr. Ravensworth. I've known it since you took the crypt key off old Mr. Pellow's ring and nobody thought to ask whether the boy deserved it." Now she turned, and I had my first proper look at her. The photographs in the town records had prepared me for a magistrate's gravity — the grey suit, the spectacles on a chain, the hair pinned with the severity of a woman who had decided long ago that vanity was a tax she refused to pay. What they had not prepared me for was her eyes, which were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. It was the tiredness of a person who has been holding a door shut for decades and whose arms have forgotten any other position.

"You've been reading the deposit ledger," she said. It was not a question.

"I read everything. It's my profession."

"Your profession is silence, Mr. Ravensworth. Reading is merely how you earn the right to it." She gestured to a chair — not the good chair opposite her desk, but a smaller one by the wall, the chair one gives to petitioners and salesmen. I sat in it and noted, professionally, that the placement was deliberate. She wanted me to know my station. "You've had the Hale letter in your hands for — what, a fortnight? Three weeks? And in all that time you have written letters to the constabulary, requested the Marsh file twice, and visited the widow Pellow at the Fairhaven home. You've been busy. For an invisible man, you leave a remarkable trail."

I confess my first feeling was not fear. It was a kind of appalled admiration, the feeling of a craftsman seeing his own techniques used against him. I had spent two decades believing that no one watched the watcher, that the archive's locked door worked in both directions. Ferrant had just shown me a mirror.

"The Marsh file is a matter of public record," I said.

"The Marsh file is a closed case."

"Closed for good," I said, and watched her. Everyone in Marrowgate used the same phrase, in the same cadence, like a password or a prayer. I had begun to collect the repetitions the way a lepidopterist collects specimens — pinned, dated, labelled. *Constable Drewitt, 14th of October: closed for good. The parish secretary, on the telephone: closed for good, Mr. Ravensworth, I really must ask you to stop.* Now I waited to see which specimen Ferrant would provide.

She provided none. That was the first surprise.

Instead she crossed to her desk, poured two glasses of water from a jug — the water, I noticed, was already poured, the second glass already waiting, which meant she had expected me before her clerk had announced me, which meant she knew my habits better than I had credited — and sat down across from me with the glass held in both hands as though it were something that might otherwise escape.

"I am going to speak to you for perhaps four minutes," she said. "In that time I will say several things that could end my career, and one thing that could end considerably more. You will not repeat any of it. Not to your client — yes, I know about the stranger and his advance payment; Marrowgate has three banks and I know the managers of all of them — not to the tribunal that doesn't yet exist, and not to whatever notebook you keep in that coat pocket. You will listen, and then you will go back to your crypt and you will lock the door behind you, and you will stay there."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you will die of something mundane. A fall on the cathedral stairs. A chill that turns. Marrowgate is very good at mundane deaths. We've had practice."

There it was. Not denial — I had braced for denial the way one braces for a slammed door — but something far worse: confirmation, delivered with the weary courtesy of a woman discussing the weather. I found my hands were steady, which surprised me. I had expected them to shake. Perhaps it is true what they say about the archive: it teaches you to hold fragile things without trembling.

"Mrs. Ferrant," I began.

"Magistrate Ferrant, if you please. I have spent thirty years earning the prefix; you will not take it from me in four minutes." A ghost of humour crossed her face and was gone, and I understood suddenly that the humour was not a defence but a wound — that she joked the way amputees scratch, at the place where something used to be.

"Magistrate Ferrant. Your name is in a sealed confession deposited in my archive in November of 1987. It names you, Dean Vellacott, Mayor Vane, and a man recorded as deceased — Aldous Crane — as parties to a crime that had not yet been committed: the murder of the man who wrote the letter. The letter specifies the circumstances. It specifies a date. That date is now less than a month away, and the cathedral bells — which the letter names as the signal — are scheduled for maintenance that will keep them silent for exactly the required period. Someone is following the script, Mrs.— Magistrate. Someone read the letter, or someone wrote it, and either way the machinery is moving. I came here because you are named in it, and because a magistrate is sworn to the law, and because I am — " I hesitated, and the hesitation cost me, because she saw it and something in her face softened by a single degree. "I am not equipped for this. I file things. I don't fight them."

For a long moment she said nothing. The clock on her mantel ticked with the smugness of clocks in official buildings, as though time itself were on the town's payroll.

"You are not equipped," she said at last. "No. You are a man who has spent twenty years arranging the past into alphabetical order so that it can't reach out and touch you. Don't look offended; I've read your deposit ledger the way you read everything — carefully, and with an eye for what's *missing*. Do you know what's missing from it, Mr. Ravensworth? You. Twenty years of deposits, and not one of them is yours. A man who files everyone's secrets and keeps none of his own is not discreet. He is a man with no one to tell them to." She took off her spectacles and polished them on her sleeve, a small domestic gesture that seemed suddenly terribly lonely. "I know the type. I invented it."

"Then you'll understand," I said, "when I tell you I can't simply lock the door. There's a man's life attached to the deadline. Corwin Hale. Whatever he is — and I'll grant he's strange, paying triple rates to commission proof of his own murder — he came to me because the archive was the only institution in Marrowgate he believed couldn't be bought. I would like to go on deserving that."

"The archive can be bought. The archive has been bought. That is rather the point of the last thirty-seven years." She set the spectacles down and looked at me directly, and I saw that she had made some decision, and that the decision had cost her something visible. "You want the name of the crime. Very well. In the autumn of 1987 a girl named Ivy Marsh came into possession of information concerning certain gentlemen of this town — information she witnessed in a place she had no business being, and which they could not permit her to carry out of it. She was seventeen years old. She liked poetry and she was saving up for a bicycle. She attempted to convert her information into money — not greed; she wanted the bicycle, and her mother's rent, and perhaps a little power, which is a thing even seventeen-year-olds are owed a taste of. She approached the wrong man. And on the fourth of November, 1987, in a place I will not name, that man killed her."

The clock ticked. Somewhere below us, in the court, a clerk was reading charges against a man for stealing a bicycle. I remember thinking, with the cold irrelevance of a mind trained to catalogue, that the bicycle had made it into the record and the girl had not.

"The drowning was staged," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, as though someone else were filing the words for later.

"The drowning was staged," she agreed. "Poorly. The constable who certified it knew it was poorly staged, and certified it anyway, and has been dying of it slowly ever since. The report was closed — " and here, at last, the phrase came, but bent, warped, said with a bitterness that stripped it of its password-quality — "closed for good, yes, because *good* men closed it, Mr. Ravensworth, that is the joke of the phrase, that is the whole rotten joke. Not bad men. Good ones. Men with pensions and parishes and daughters. Each of us closed one small door, you see, each of us told ourselves it was one small compromise, and by the time we looked up the corridor behind us was made entirely of doors, and there was no way back to the daylight."

"And you. Which door did you close?"

She was quiet for a moment. When she answered, her voice had dropped into something almost private.

"In December of 1987 I was a junior clerk of the court, twenty-six years old, with a sick mother and a reputation I had built out of nothing but correctness. I was handed a file and asked to enter a verdict of accidental death, and I was told — not threatened, *told*, that was the elegance of it — that the file had already been approved by my superiors and that my signature was a formality. I signed. It took me four seconds. It has taken me the rest of my life." She turned her water glass a quarter-turn, aligning it with the desk's edge, and I recognised the gesture with a jolt: I did the same thing with my ledger volumes. We were both curators. She curated a verdict; I curated a crypt. "The letter you hold names me as a murderer. It is wrong about the word and right about the fact. I did not kill Ivy Marsh. I killed the truth of her, which in a court of law is the same offence, and I have served the sentence every day since, in this room, wearing this prefix, handing down honest verdicts in small cases to balance the one enormous dishonest one. Do you understand the arithmetic of it? I have sentenced four hundred petty thieves and two hundred drunkards and eleven wife-beaters, and I have done it well, and none of it weighs anything against four seconds in December of 1987."

I should have felt revulsion. I had come expecting to feel it, had packed it in advance like a lunch. Instead I felt something that frightened me considerably more: recognition. A woman who had filed away her own guilt and built a life on top of the filing cabinet. I knew that architecture. I had lived in it for twenty years.

"Why tell me this?" I asked. "You've just handed me a confession. I could take it to — "

"To whom?" The weariness in her voice was absolute. "To the constabulary? Drewitt signed the death certificate; he will recant nothing while he still draws breath, because the moment he does, his own signature hangs him. To the bishop? The dean has the bishop's ear and the bishop has the dean's debts. To the press? The *Marrowgate Advertiser* is printed on a press that Mayor Vane's family bought in 1979 and has been paying off ever since. To a tribunal, a court, a jury — Mr. Ravensworth, I *am* the court. That is what I am telling you. There is no outside. There is only the inside, at various depths, and you are currently swimming downward very fast, and the water gets colder, and the things that live down here have been down here a long time and are not pleased about visitors."

"And Hale? The man whose murder is scheduled? Is he a visitor too, or has he always lived down here?"

Something moved behind her eyes at the name — not guilt exactly, but its cousin, the guilt of the bystander.

"Corwin Hale," she said slowly, "is a name I have heard twice in thirty-seven years. The first time was in 1987, attached to a boy who left town the week Ivy Marsh died and was spoken of, when he was spoken of at all, as a cousin who couldn't be found to identify the body. The second time was three weeks ago, when a stranger checked into the Bell and Anchor under that name and began asking questions with a southern accent he can't quite hold onto. If that man has come back to Marrowgate to dig up the fourth of November, then he is not a client, Mr. Ravensworth. He is a detonator. And you are standing on the same charge."

*The cousin who couldn't be found.* I turned the phrase over and felt it click against something — the letter's uncanny precision, its knowledge of the deposit protocols, its author's certainty that the archive would hold the seal for exactly as long as it needed holding. Someone who knew the archive's habits. Someone with a reason to know them.

"You said four minutes," I said. "You've spent five. What is it you actually want from me?"

She rose then, and went to the window again, and stood with her back to me, and for a moment the magistrate fell away and I saw only a tired woman of sixty-odd looking down at a street where a girl had once saved up for a bicycle.

"I want you to drop it," she said. "I want it with every tired bone in my body, because I have seen what happens to people who pick this thread up, and the weft of it is made of funerals. I want you to take your triple fee, and your sealed letter, and your meticulous little soul, and go back to being nobody, because being nobody in Marrowgate is the only safety there has ever been, and I say that as a woman who has been somebody for thirty years and would trade all of it for one anonymous afternoon." She turned. "But I know you won't. I have read your ledger, as I said. And there is one thing I know about men who keep ledgers like yours."

"Which is?"

"Which is that eventually they find their own name in them, and then God help everyone." She came around the desk, and before I could rise she did something I did not expect: she took my hand, briefly, and pressed something into it, and her grip was stronger than her tiredness suggested. "You will be followed from this building. Walk past the corn exchange, cross at the lights, and do not look at the paper in your hand until you are inside St. Aldhelm's, where — for reasons that will become apparent — they cannot follow you. When you have read it, burn it. Not file it. *Burn* it. Some things must never enter an archive, Mr. Ravensworth, and this is one, because archives can be searched, and I am an old woman, and I am more afraid of what they would do to me than I am of dying. Remember that about me, when you write your verdict. I was not brave. I was only honest once, at the very end, and in the smallest possible denomination."

Then she rang the little bell on her desk, and the clerk opened the door, and I was dismissed — not like a petitioner, I noticed, but like something closer to an equal, which from Odile Ferrant was perhaps the largest mercy available.

* * *

I walked as instructed, past the corn exchange, across at the lights, a middle-aged man in a brown coat carrying nothing, seeing nothing, being no one. The habit of invisibility served me well that day; I felt the follower behind me the way one feels weather — a pressure at the back of the neck, a reflection that lingered a half-second too long in a shop window. He was good. He was not good enough. I had spent twenty years learning every sightline in Marrowgate, and I took him past the fishmonger's and through the lych-gate and into St. Aldhelm's, where the dark swallowed me the way it always had, and where no one, I was almost certain, could follow.

Only in the crypt, with the door locked and the lamp lit, did I open my hand.

A slip of paper, small, folded twice, written in a hand so controlled it might have been typed: a magistrate's hand, a hand that had signed four seconds of infamy and thirty years of penance. One word, in the centre of the page, underlined twice.

Not a name, at first glance. An initial and a fragment. *M.V.*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, the sentence I have carried ever since, the sentence that turned my blood to something cold and mineral:

*The letter is wrong about the fourth name. Crane is not dead, and he is not guilty. Ask instead who has a key to the close.*

I sat for a long time in the lamplight with the paper trembling — at last — in my hands. *M.V.* Merrick Vane. The mayor's brother, gone abroad these thirty years, spoken of the way one speaks of the dead. And a question that rearranged the whole architecture of my archive: who had a key to the cathedral close, to the crypt's outer door, to the corridors I had believed were mine alone?

I should have burned the paper. She had told me to burn it, and burning it would have been the wise thing, the invisible thing, the thing the man I had been for twenty years would have done without a second thought.

I filed it instead. Behind the loose brick behind the 1987 consistory records, where only I would ever think to look.

Because she was right about one thing: I had found my own name in the ledger at last — not written in ink, but written in the shape of everything I'd failed to read. And a man who has found his name in a ledger has only two choices. He can close the book.

Or he can start writing in it.

The next morning, I bought a railway timetable, and looked up the coastal line.
