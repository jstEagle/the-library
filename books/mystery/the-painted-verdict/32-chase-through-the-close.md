## 32. Chase Through the Close

There is a particular smell that clings to a man after he has nearly burned to death in a medieval crypt, and I will tell you now that no cologne in Christendom will cover it. Smoke, yes — but under the smoke something older, the smell of stone that has been heated past its patience, and under that, embarrassingly, the smell of my own fear, which I had always assumed would smell more dignified.

I came up the ventilation flue the way a cork comes out of a bottle — propelled, undignified, and trailing debris. The flue emptied behind the vestry into the grey light of four in the morning, and I lay on the cold grass of the close for a full thirty seconds doing nothing except breathing, which after the crypt seemed less like an involuntary function and more like a hard-won achievement.

Behind me, the cathedral was breathing smoke. Not yet aflame — Merrick's fire had been a patient one, set in the archive itself, among the dry paper I had spent twenty years keeping safe — but the smoke came out of every seam and lancet like the building trying to cough me up a second time.

And Hale was still inside.

No. Not inside. The bell-ringers had gone in — I had seen their torches bobbing through the smoke as I clawed my way up the flue, heard young Petch's voice cracking on a shout of *here, here, there's a man here* — and they had dragged Hale out through the west door before the first proper flames found the stair. I had watched them carry him across the grass, a limp bundle with a soot-blackened face, and I had knelt beside him in the wet grass and pressed two fingers to his throat and found the stubborn little drumbeat of a living man, and I had felt something in my chest that was either relief or the onset of a cardiac event.

Then I had looked up, and seen Merrick Vane running.

He had been at the edge of the close, at the gate into the lane, and for one moment he had done the thing that damns every careful man: he had looked back. Not at the fire. At me. He had wanted to see whether his work was finished, and in wanting to see it he had told me exactly where he was.

I got up. My left arm had a burn on it shaped, as far as I could tell through the blistering, like a page — which felt editorially appropriate — and my throat felt as though I had swallowed the archive whole. But I got up, and I ran, and I will confess here, in the privacy of my own testimony, that I did not think about it at all. Thinking was for later. Later, I would have years to think. What I had now was four o'clock in the morning, a burning cathedral at my back, and a murderer thirty yards ahead of me who believed I was dead.

That last part was the whole of my advantage, and I spent it recklessly.

* * *

The close at that hour is a corridor of sleeping stone. The canons' houses stood dark on either side, their windows reflecting the orange pulse from the cathedral, and Merrick ran up the middle of it with the flat, economical stride of a man who has walked this ground every day for twenty years and never once been seen doing it.

I had one thing he did not account for, and it was not courage. It was the map.

Twenty years an archivist teaches you that a building is only its public rooms plus its lies. I had catalogued the lies of the cathedral close along with everything else — the forgotten stair in the vestry wall, the rood-loft access, the passage behind the chapter house that the Victorian restorers had bricked halfway and then, in the manner of Victorians, abandoned out of boredom. Merrick knew the close as a steward knows it: doors, paths, the respectable skeleton of the place. I knew it as an archive knows it: the places where the record had been misfiled.

He went left, toward the gatehouse and the lane and whatever vehicle he had waiting. I cut right, through the gap between the canons' library and the old schoolroom, over a wall I had climbed exactly once before, at nineteen, for reasons I no longer remembered, and came out on the roofed walkway that runs along the back of the cloister — the pentice, the old guides called it, a sloping lead roof over the cloister walk, pitched at an angle that a sensible man would call a roof and a desperate man would call a road.

I took the road.

I will not pretend it was graceful. Lead roofing in October is slick with dew, and my burned arm made every reach a small negotiation with God, and twice I went down onto my knees hard enough to leave marks I would be examining for weeks. But the pentice runs the length of the cloister, and it runs *above* the lane-side gatehouse, and so it was that when Merrick Vane came through the cloister door into the walk below me, heading for the gate and his escape, I was directly over his head, flat on my belly on wet lead, looking down at the crown of his head like God reviewing a particularly disappointing petition.

He had a canvas bag over his shoulder. I did not need to see inside it. I had spent the last hour in a fire he had set to destroy a body — mine, and Hale's, and if the flames spread as he intended, the last physical evidence of Ivy Marsh besides the three copies I had hidden. The bag was the residue of his life in the close. The lay steward's plain clothes, the key ring, the twenty years of being nobody, which was, I realized with a strange cold clarity, the one thing he and I had genuinely had in common.

He had been invisible too. He had simply chosen what to be invisible *for*.

"Merrick," I said.

My voice came out as a ruin — smoke-scraped, barely a whisper. It should not have carried. But sound behaves strangely in the cloister at night, dropping straight down the arcade like water down a chute, and he heard it. He stopped. He looked up.

I have faced perhaps four genuinely frightening sights in my life, and I rank them now with an archivist's precision: the fire in the crypt; the wax-sealed warning in my office; the fresh mortar over Ivy Marsh's bones; and Merrick Vane's face when he saw me above him. Not rage. Rage I could have understood, could have worked with. What I saw was *recalculation* — the swift, cold arithmetic of a man discovering that his sums were wrong, and beginning, without any visible disturbance, to correct them.

"You," he said. His voice, by contrast, was perfectly calm. That was the worst of it. He had strangled a girl in a crypt, falsified a town for twenty years, locked two men in with a fire, and his voice had the mild patience of a man discussing guttering.

"Me," I agreed, and coughed, and the cough nearly folded me in half on the lead.

"You should be ash," he said, conversationally. "The flue. Of course. The flue." And then, with something that in a better man I might have called admiration: "Twenty years cataloguing the place, and you never once told anyone what you knew about it."

"I told no one anything about anything," I said. "That was the arrangement."

"And now?"

"Now I have three copies of everything, Merrick. Hidden in three places you will never find, held by three people who have instructions to post them to London if I miss a single appointment. Including this one." I let that sit. It was perhaps forty percent true, in the way that all the best archive work is forty percent true and one hundred percent effective. "You burned the cathedral for nothing. The record survived you. It will outlast you. That is the entire point of a record."

He looked at me for a long moment. Below him, at the mouth of the lane, a light came on in the gatehouse — the porter, woken by the bells, by the smoke, by the town beginning to stir. And I watched Merrick Vane understand that the gate was closing, in every sense, and watch him do the thing I should have predicted and did not, because I still, even then, thought of him as a steward and not as what he was.

He ran for the tower.

* * *

Why the tower? I have turned this over the way I turn over any misfiled document, looking for the true order of it, and the best answer I have is this: the tower was the one direction the fire had not yet reached, the one door he still held a key to, and — I believe this mattered more than tactics — it was the place where his brother's power had been most visibly built. Roscoe Vane had funded the restoration. Roscoe Vane's name was on the dedication plaque at the tower base. When the world collapsed, men like Merrick do not run from the symbol of what they are. They run *into* it.

I came off the pentice at its far end, dropped the last six feet into the chapter-house yard, and went after him through the dark north door of the tower base — unlocked, because the workmen's scaffolding for the rehanging still stood inside, and the workmen had been summoned, along with everyone else, to fight the fire.

The tower stairs are a spiral, stone, lit by arrow-slits that showed nothing but smoke-glow. I climbed. My legs informed me, in the frank language of legs, that they had been through a great deal that night and would prefer to file a grievance. I overruled them. Somewhere below, the cathedral's west door burst open and voices poured in — the fire brigade arriving at last, the whole machinery of the town that Vane had bought and bent and owned beginning, groggily, to turn over — but in the stair there was only my breathing and, one turn above me, his.

He was not running anymore. I understood why when I came out onto the ringing chamber and found him standing at the open tower door onto the parapet, the canvas bag at his feet, the whole of Marrowgate spread out below us in the pre-dawn grey — the rooftops, the market square, the river, the little lit windows of a town waking to the sight of its cathedral smoking like a censer.

The bells hung above us in the dark, fresh from the restorers' hands, silent still, waiting to be rehung and rung. The letter had said *when the bells fall silent*. The bells had fallen silent, and the deadline had passed, and nothing had happened — because the deadline had never been prophecy. It had been a schedule. And the man who had kept the schedule was standing in front of me with nowhere left on his calendar.

"There's nowhere to go," I said. I meant it as fact, not mercy. It came out as both.

He looked out at the town. "Do you know what I did for this place?" he said. "Twenty years. Every leak fixed, every problem handled, every — every *mess* cleaned up before it could stain. Roscoe got the credit and I got a room in the close and a set of keys, and it was *fine*. It was fine, because the alternative was 1987." He turned and looked at me, and for the first time his calm cracked, just at one edge, the way old plaster cracks. "You found her, didn't you. Behind the mortar."

"Yes."

"She was supposed to stay in the river." He said it simply, without cruelty, which was the cruelest way available. "That was the whole plan, and it was a *good* plan, and it worked for thirty-seven years. Do you know what undid it? A letter. One letter, filed by a sentimental old man who couldn't keep his mouth shut even in death." He shook his head. "Paper. All of it, paper. I should have burned the archive in '87."

"You should have done a great many things differently," I said. "Beginning with Ivy Marsh."

Below us, in the tower's throat, I could hear boots on the stairs. The fire brigade, or the constabulary, or both, coming up. He heard it too. I watched him weigh it — the stairs, the bag, the parapet, me — and I saw him arrive at the arithmetic of a cornered man, which is always the same arithmetic: one exit, and it is the one you fear.

He picked up the bag.

"Merrick," I said, and took a step forward, and here is the place where my testimony must become honest in the way that costs, because what happened next happened in perhaps two seconds, and I have had every hour since to examine it, and I cannot tell you — I will never be able to tell you — what I intended.

He was between me and the parapet door. The bag was heavy. He raised it — to swing it at me, I believed, to buy his two seconds for the door. I moved. I know I moved; there is a bruise on my shoulder from where I struck something, the doorframe or him or the stone. The parapet door was open. The morning was wet. The lead on the tower roof slopes, as lead does, toward the gutter and the drop.

And Merrick Vane went over the edge of the bell tower and fell the hundred feet to the close below, and did not get up.

Whether I pushed him and the memory has filed itself somewhere even I cannot reach; whether he slipped on the wet lead with a heavy bag in his hands; whether he looked at the stairs full of boots and the parapet full of me and chose the shorter drop — I have been asked this, in the weeks since, by lawyers and journalists and once, gently, by Odile Ferrant, and I have given the same answer every time.

I don't know.

I have spent my life insisting that every fact be filed in its true place, and this one fact I cannot place, and I have made my peace with that, or something adjacent to peace. What I know is this: I stood at the parapet with smoke in my lungs and my heart going like a bell, and I looked down at what lay on the close below, and I felt — I have searched for a better word and there isn't one — I felt *quiet*. Not triumph. Not horror, or not only horror. A terrible administrative quiet, the quiet of a ledger balancing, of a debt at last entered in the correct column. And I have never decided whether that quiet was my innocence or my verdict.

* * *

They took me down the tower stairs by the arms, though I did not need taking. A young constable wrapped a blanket around me that smelled of someone else's dog. Below, the firemen had the west roof and were winning, slowly, the way the truth wins — expensively, and too late for the things it was supposed to protect.

They laid Merrick under a tarpaulin on the close grass, twenty yards from the west door, twenty yards from where Hale had been carried out alive. I stood over him for a moment. Up close, with the steward's plainness stripped away by death, he looked like what he had spent twenty years pretending to be: nobody. A man with no history. The close had finally made him invisible, and it had taken a fall to do it.

"Did he say anything?" the young constable asked, notebook out, pencil trembling. "Before he — did he confess anything?"

I looked at my hands. They were burned, blackened, and steady.

"He said the archive should have burned in 1987," I said.

It was true. It was not all of it. I filed it the way I have learned to file things now — completely enough to be honest, incompletely enough to be merciful, to whom I still could not say.

Across the close, the sky was going the colour of wood ash, and the cathedral stood smoking against it, wounded but standing, and down in the market square the first bell of morning rang from St. Cuthbert's parish church — the wrong bells, the small bells, the town's ordinary timekeeping going on as if nothing had ended. And at the gatehouse, pushing through the gathering crowd of firemen and constables and neighbours in their nightclothes, I saw a figure I knew: Roscoe Vane, the mayor of Marrowgate, in an overcoat over pyjamas, his face assembled into grief before he had even reached the tarpaulin, already performing for the crowd, already beginning the long work of controlling what this night would be allowed to mean.

He looked up. He found me across the grass — the burned archivist, the dead man's witness — and for one unguarded second, before the grief settled back over his face like a lid, I saw him understand that his brother's fall had not closed the account.

It had only transferred the balance.

I had three copies of everything, hidden in three places, and a constable's sworn statement waiting in a drawer, and a tribunal that would convene at nine o'clock, and I was done, as of this morning, with letting powerful men decide what the record would say.

Roscoe Vane was walking toward me through the crowd, and dawn was coming up behind him, and I stood my ground on the wet grass of the close and waited for him to arrive.
