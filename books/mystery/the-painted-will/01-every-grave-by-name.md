## 1. Every Grave by Name

The fog had not yet lifted from Bellhaven's cemetery when Paloma Kavanagh arrived, and she was glad of it. Fog made the place honest. It hid the harbor, the rooftops, the church spire — all the things the living had built — and left only what mattered: the stones, the grass, the names.

She walked the rows the way she always did, unhurried, her boots leaving dark prints in the dew. She did not carry flowers. She carried a clipboard, though there was nothing on it that needed writing this morning, and she supposed the clipboard was a kind of habit, a prop, the way some men carried canes they didn't need. Fifteen years of carrying it had worn the handle smooth as driftwood.

She began at the north wall, as she always did, because the north wall held the oldest stones and the oldest stones held the town's first mistakes.

*Hollis, Thomas, 1931–1998.* Heart failure, certified by Dr. Ambrose Fenn before Paloma ever held the pen. She'd inherited Hollis the way she'd inherited everything — a filing cabinet full of other people's endings, each one signed, dated, and closed.

*Merrin, Ada, 1940–2004.* Pneumonia. Paloma had certified that one herself, her second winter in office. Ada Merrin had been a schoolteacher who taught three generations of Bellhaven children to read, and in the end her lungs had filled like paper soaking in water, and Paloma had written *pneumonia* in her careful, upright hand and felt nothing but the rightness of the word. That was the job. Not feeling nothing — feeling the right thing, at the right time, in the right amount. A coroner who felt too much signed wrong certificates. A coroner who felt too little signed them anyway.

She moved down the row.

*Okafor, Samuel, 1952–2011.* Cardiac event, on his boat, alone. His daughter had flown in from Vancouver and asked Paloma whether he'd suffered, and Paloma had told her the truth as the evidence gave it: probably not more than a minute or two. The daughter had wept with something close to relief, and Paloma had understood then, in a way she never quite had before, that her work was not for the dead at all. The dead were past needing anything. The certificates were for the living — small, sealed rooms the grieving could enter and stand inside without the wind getting in.

That was why order mattered. That was why she kept her files the way she kept them: cross-referenced, initialed, every page accounted for. Fifteen years, four hundred and six deaths, and not one loose thread. Other towns had scandals. Bellhaven had Paloma Kavanagh, and the difference between those two things was the whole architecture of her life.

The fog thinned as she climbed toward the upper terrace, where the newer graves stood in their tidy ranks. She passed the ones she knew best without slowing — she could have walked this cemetery blindfolded, could have recited it like a psalm — but she slowed anyway, because today was not an ordinary day, and because the grave she was walking toward deserved the courtesy of approach.

Eileen Voss would be buried here at eleven o'clock.

Paloma reached the open plot at ten minutes past seven and stood at its edge, looking down into the rectangle of dark earth. The vault liner waited at the bottom, pale gray, patient. A folding canopy stood folded against a nearby yew, and a stack of chairs sat shrink-wrapped in plastic like something perishable. The gravediggers had done their work overnight and gone; the spoil heap was covered with a tarp weighted at the corners, neat as a made bed.

Everything in its place. Everything accounted for.

Except, said a voice in her head that she did not recognize and did not like, except the manner of it.

She pushed the thought down where she pushed all such thoughts — into the same deep drawer where she kept her mother's last voicemail, unlistened-to for nine years, and the letter of resignation she'd drafted in her third year and never sent. The drawer was full and locked and it worked fine.

But the thought came back up, the way thoughts do when you're standing over an open grave.

Eileen Voss had drowned. That was what the certificate said, and Paloma had written it herself, eleven days ago, without hesitation. Found floating in the tidal pool below the breakwater at Voss Point, six-forty in the morning, by a dog walker. Cold water, cold season, seventy-nine years old. No signs of struggle. No visible trauma. The lungs had been waterlogged; the blood had told the same story the water did. Drowning, accidental. Signed, sealed, filed.

It had been the cleanest certificate she'd produced all autumn.

So why was she standing here at seven in the morning, three hours before the funeral, looking at a hole in the ground like it might look back?

She turned away from the plot and walked, because walking was what she did with questions she refused to answer. She took the long way, along the eastern fence where the sea showed through gaps in the cypress, gray and flat as hammered tin. The gulls were working the tide line. Somewhere below, the town was waking — she could see the bakery's lights, the blue flicker of the fish market, the first truck rumbling up Harbor Road.

Four hundred and six names. She began to count them off in her head, the way she did when she needed the world to be a list instead of a mystery. *Prentiss, Ruth. Calloway, Daniel. Ibarra, Marisol.* The fishermen and the teachers, the babies who hadn't lasted a week and the old men who hadn't known when to stop. Every one of them certified by her hand or her mentor's, every one of them filed in the cabinet behind her desk in strict alphabetical order, because death was chaos enough without her adding to it.

*Ibarra, Marisol,* she thought again, and stopped walking.

Marisol Ibarra had drowned too. Four years ago, off Gull Rock, in July. And Paloma remembered — now, standing in a cemetery she knew better than her own kitchen — that Ambrose Fenn had been in her office that week, uncharacteristically curious about the toxicology panel. Just curiosity. Just an old man asking questions about an old friend's case. She hadn't thought twice about it. She hadn't thought about it since.

She thought about it now, briefly, and put it away.

Ambrose was two years dead. He had taught her everything: how to hold a scalpel, how to write a report that a widow could read without breaking, how to be the one person in a town whose word no one questioned. He had signed her first certificate alongside hers, and he had signed her last-but-one, and between those two signatures lay fifteen years of trust so complete it had never once occurred to her to examine it. You didn't examine a foundation. You built on it.

The fog was burning off. By eight the sun would be on the water, and the mourners would begin arriving in their dark cars, and Aldous Voss would stand at the head of his mother's grave with his jaw set like a man holding a door shut against weather, and Marguerite would stand beside him in black silk looking like a woman who had dressed for a wedding and been disappointed, and Corwin Hale would stand slightly apart with his hands folded and his eyes moving over everyone, counting them, the way lawyers do.

Paloma would attend. She attended every burial in Bellhaven — it was policy, her own policy, established her first year: *the coroner owes the dead her presence, if nothing else.* The selectmen had called it morbid once, early on, and she had looked at the man who said it until he apologized, and the policy had never been questioned again.

She checked her watch. Eight-twelve. The certificate was still in her bag — the original, which she'd taken out of the file last night for reasons she had declined to examine, and which she intended, she told herself, simply to re-file after the service. Standard practice. Tidiness.

She found the bench beneath the big maple, the one facing the Voss plot, and sat, and laid the folder on her knee.

The certificate was one page. That was the beauty of the form — one page, and a life was accounted for. Name: Eileen Anne Voss. Date of death: October 14th. Manner: accident. Cause: drowning. She read it top to bottom the way she'd read it the day she signed it, checking each line against her memory of the body, the scene, the lab work. All of it matched. All of it was true, as far as truth went, which was exactly as far as the page required it to go.

Her pen was in her coat pocket. She took it out, uncapped it, and held it over the signature line — not to sign, the certificate was already signed, but out of some reflex, some muscle memory of finality. This is how it ends. This is what happened. File it and move on.

And there — just there, in the space between the pen hovering and the pen lowering — came the crack. Hairline. Barely a sound at all.

Because Eileen Voss had been a swimmer. Seventy-nine years old, yes, and her heart was not what it was, but Paloma had pulled the old records — routine diligence, she'd told herself at the time, though she'd never pulled records like that before — and Eileen Voss had swum the length of the hotel pool every morning until three years ago, and had walked the breakwater every evening until the week she died. A woman like that did not slip from a breakwater she'd walked five hundred times. Women like that died in beds, surrounded by pill organizers and adult children, and were buried with their shoes barely worn.

*No signs of struggle,* the certificate said. But drowning doesn't always leave struggle. Sometimes it leaves only water, and water leaves nothing.

Paloma capped the pen.

She was being ridiculous. She was a woman who believed in evidence, and the evidence was airtight, and the only thing troubling the air was a swimmer's shoes and an old man's idle questions four years ago, neither of which was evidence of anything except her own imagination working overtime on a slow month.

She stood, squared the folder, and walked back toward the open grave.

By nine-thirty the cars had begun to arrive. Paloma watched from the bench as Bellhaven assembled itself in black — the harbormaster, the school principal, half the congregation of St. Brendan's, the women from the garden society who had hated Eileen Voss and come anyway, because in a town of nine hundred people you came. The Voss family arrived last, in a car long enough to be embarrassed by the cemetery road. Aldous got out first and helped his wife. Corwin Hale emerged from the far side, smoothed his coat, and paused — just a moment — to look up at the terrace, at the bench, at Paloma.

He nodded. She nodded back. It was the nod of two professionals acknowledging each other across a room, and it should have meant nothing.

It was the undertaker's men who broke the spell, wheeling the casket up the path on its bier, and the crowd turning like water finding its level, and the minister opening his book. Paloma stayed on the bench through all of it — she was not family, not friend, only the town's witness, and witnesses watched from the edge — and when the casket went down and the first earth went on and the mourners began to drift back toward their cars and the reception at Voss House, she remained, as she always remained, until the cemetery was empty again and the only company left was the four hundred and six.

Then she opened the folder one last time, looked at her own signature — firm, upright, certain — and slid the certificate home into the file.

Drowning. Accidental. Closed.

She was halfway down the hill, the fog fully burned away now, the morning bright and ordinary, when she heard the mail truck grinding up Cemetery Road behind her, and saw young Danny Pell stop at the box by the gate, and saw him wave a thick envelope at her — *this one's yours, Ms. Kavanagh, came special* — and thought nothing of it at all.

* * *

The envelope was waiting on her desk when she returned to the office at noon, smelling of rain and old glue. No return address. Her name typed, not handwritten, on a label that had begun to peel at one corner.

She set it beside the Eileen Voss file, squared its edges, and did not open it.

Not yet. First she hung up her coat. First she logged the morning's correspondence, watered the plant Ambrose had given her the year she took the office, a stubborn fern that had outlived him. First she made tea and drank half of it standing at the window, watching the harbor do its eternal arithmetic of boats.

Only then, with the file square on her blotter and the afternoon light going long and gold across the desk, did she pick up the letter opener Ambrose had also given her — brass, engraved, *FOR THE TRUTH* — and slit the envelope open in one clean stroke.

Inside was a single sheet, handwritten, in a slanted, careful hand she almost recognized.

*Coroner Kavanagh,* it began. *You signed the wrong certificate.*

Paloma Kavanagh, who had not spilled tea or dropped a file or misfiled a name in fifteen years, sat down slowly in her chair, and read it twice, and reached for the telephone before she remembered there was no one to call — and then, instead, reached for the Eileen Voss file, and opened it, and began, for the first time in her career, to check her own work.
