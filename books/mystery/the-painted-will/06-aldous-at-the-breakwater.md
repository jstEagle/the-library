## 6. Aldous at the Breakwater

The breakwater ran out into the harbor like a stone arm pointing at something it could never reach. Paloma walked it at low tide on the fourth morning after the funeral, her boots finding the worn hollows in the granite where a century of feet had polished a path. Gulls argued over the mussel beds below. The sea did its usual work of pretending to be gentle.

Aldous Voss was at the end of it, standing where the concrete gave way to the old timber pier, his back to her, his hands jammed in the pockets of a coat too fine for the salt wind. She had called his office twice and been told he was unavailable; she had not called a third time. Some interviews you scheduled. Others you simply arrived at, the way weather arrives.

She stopped ten feet behind him and let the silence do the first part of her work. Fifteen years of talking to the bereaved had taught her that grief had a texture, and that you could read more from what a person did with silence than from anything they said to fill it.

Aldous did not turn. He said, "You came out here to ambush me."

"I came out here to talk. The ambush is incidental."

A sound escaped him — not quite a laugh, more the ghost of one, drowned halfway. He turned then, and she saw what the funeral had hidden: three days without sleep had carved him. His eyes were red-rimmed, his jaw gray with unshaved stubble, and there was a tremor in his hands that he tried to disguise by gripping the rail. He was fifty-eight and looked older, the way men did when the last parent died and the generation above them vanished all at once, leaving them exposed to the sky.

"Coroner Kavanagh," he said. "Or is it Doctor?"

"Either."

"My mother drowned." He said it flatly, the way a man reads a headline about a stranger. "That's what your certificate says. Accidental drowning. She went out at night, she slipped, the water took her. Case closed. So what is there to talk about?"

"Routine follow-up. I interview the family in every death that occurs on the water."

"You don't." He said it without heat, which made it worse. "I've lived in this town my whole life, Doctor. I know how Bellhaven works. You sign the paper, the paper goes in the drawer, and the sea keeps what it takes. Nobody walks the breakwater asking questions about a seventy-nine-year-old woman who fell in the harbor. Unless somebody's been asking you questions first."

The wind came off the water and pushed between them, carrying the smell of kelp and diesel from the fishing boats. Paloma kept her face the way she kept her files: closed, ordered, nothing out of place. But inside, the hairline crack from the cemetery morning — the one she had refused to name — shifted a fraction wider.

"Has someone been asking you questions?" she said.

"Has someone been asking *you*?"

They looked at each other, and for a moment the interview inverted, the coroner becoming the subject, and Paloma felt the peculiar vertigo of a man who has spent his life opening doors finding one opened on him. She recovered first. Practice.

"Mr. Voss. I'd like to walk through the night your mother died. Not the paperwork version. The human one."

"Why?"

"Because the paperwork version is what I write when there's nothing left to learn. I haven't written it yet."

That was not entirely true. She had written it. She had signed it. The certificate sat in her filing cabinet under V, Eileen M., dated and stamped and as final as a headstone. But something about saying it aloud here, on the stones where the old woman had presumably taken her last breath of salt air, made the signature feel less like an ending than a placeholder.

Aldous studied her for a long moment. Then he shrugged — the shrug of a man with nothing left to protect — and turned back to the water.

"She came out here most nights," he said. "Did you know that? Everyone thinks she was frail at the end. She wasn't. She was ninety percent iron. She walked this breakwater in weather that kept the fishermen ashore. Said the sea was the only thing in Bellhaven that never asked her for anything."

"Did you walk with her?"

"Sometimes." His jaw worked. "Not that night."

"Where were you that night?"

He turned on her, and for an instant there was something other than grief in his face — a flash of the temper that had made him a formidable man in the county land office, the son who had inherited the mother's iron along with the house.

"You think I don't know where this is going? You think I haven't sat through enough of my mother's dinner parties to know what it looks like when someone starts circling the inheritance?" He laughed, bitter and short. "I was home. Marguerite was home. Corwin Hale was at his office until eleven — you can ask his billing records, he bills his own breathing. Half the town saw my mother alive at six o'clock and none of them saw her again, and that includes me. There. Is that the human version you wanted?"

It was more than she'd expected, and less than she needed, and the fact that he'd offered it unasked — alibis, unprompted, like a man reciting a rehearsed defense — she filed away in the part of her mind that never stopped taking notes, even when the rest of her wanted to stop.

"I didn't come to accuse you, Mr. Voss."

"Then why does it feel like an accusation? Why does everything feel like an accusation now?" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. When he lowered it, he was older again, just a tired man on a cold pier. "I'm sorry. I'm not — I don't sleep. The house is full of her. Every room. Her glasses on the piano. Her reading in the kitchen, still open to the page. You can't grieve in a museum, Doctor, but that's what she left us. A museum with the lights off."

Paloma felt the sympathy arrive before she could vet it, warm and unwelcome. She had learned long ago to distrust her own tenderness; it had gotten her into trouble exactly twice in fifteen years, both times with men who wept well. But this was not performance. She knew performance. She had sat across from killers who cried more beautifully than widows. Aldous Voss was simply a man standing in the ruins of the only structure his life had been built on.

"Tell me about her heart," Paloma said gently. "The medication."

His head came up. "What about it?"

"She was on digoxin. That's standard for her condition. I'm confirming dosage, adherence — the ordinary things."

"Ask Marguerite. She managed the pills. She had the whole system — the organizer, the little compartments, days of the week." He said it without suspicion, without anything, and Paloma understood that Aldous Voss was a man who had never once looked closely at the machinery of his own household. Things were done *for* him. They always had been. "I just know she took them. She was religious about it. She was religious about everything."

"Except the sea?"

That almost got the laugh out of him, the real one, and for a moment his face was young — a boy on this same breakwater, forty years gone, the mother alive somewhere behind him on the shore. Then it closed again.

They stood in silence while a fishing boat came in through the harbor mouth, its wake slapping the pilings below them in slow, patient strokes. Paloma counted them without meaning to. It was a habit from the autopsy suite, counting things, giving the mind a metronome when the heart wanted to race.

"Mr. Voss. May I ask about the will?"

The change in him was immediate and total. It was as if someone had reached inside his chest and turned a valve. His shoulders squared. His eyes went flat and careful. And Paloma, watching it happen, thought: *there*. There is the thing. Whatever it was, it lived behind that door.

"Why?" he said.

"Because you're the executor's principal beneficiary and the deceased's sole surviving—"

"She had two children."

The words came out before he could stop them. She saw the instant he realized it, saw the color leave his face and return in a blotchy rush, and she watched him try to walk it back — "had. Had two. I misspoke" — and Paloma stood very still on the cold stones and let him hang himself with his own grammar.

The file was in her head already, unrolling like a chart. Eileen Voss, two children: Aldous, born 1954. Lena, born 1959. Lena Voss, died 2009, single, no issue. Death certificate signed by Dr. Ambrose Fenn. No body released to the family. She had read it three nights ago by lamplight and felt the first true vertigo of this whole affair, and she had told herself then what she told herself now: *coastal town, bodies lost at sea, it happens.* But Aldous had not said *had* like a man misremembering a date. He had said it like a man who had spent fifteen years being careful and had slipped on a wet morning.

"Your sister," Paloma said quietly. "Lena."

"She's dead."

"Is she?"

The question landed between them like a dropped glass. Aldous stared at her, and she watched him decide — she could see the decision happening, could see him weighing what his mother would have wanted against what this woman with the steady eyes already knew — and then he did the thing she had not expected. He sat down. Right there on the breakwater's edge, on the cold granite, with the harbor at his back, he sat down like a man whose legs had simply resigned.

"God," he said. "You're going to be the one who does it, aren't you. You're going to be the one who digs it all up."

"Mr. Voss—"

"Sit down, Doctor." He patted the stone beside him, an oddly courtly gesture from a man coming apart. "You'll want to sit down for this."

She sat. The granite was cold through her coat. Below them, a heron stood in the shallows with the patience of a thing that had never once been in a hurry.

"The will," Aldous said, staring at the water. "You asked about the will. Here's what you need to know about the will. Everything goes to the town. Voss House, the land, the endowment, the wharf rights — all of it, to Bellhaven, on the condition that it's accepted at the anniversary dinner. One year after her death. If nobody challenges it by then, it's sealed. Permanent. Irrevocable." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "My mother's last joke on her family. She always said the town deserved the house more than we did. Maybe she was right."

"And if someone challenges it?"

"Then it gets ugly. Litigation for years. The estate frozen. Corwin says—" He stopped.

"Corwin says what?"

"Corwin says a challenge would be catastrophic. That's the word he used. Catastrophic. He's very keen that nobody challenge it. He talks about the anniversary dinner like it's a coronation." Aldous rubbed his face. "And I keep thinking, *of course he does.* He's been the family's lawyer for thirty years. He wrote the thing. He knows every clause better than my mother did."

Paloma turned the sentence over, looking for the seam in it. "You don't trust him."

"I trust him the way you trust weather. He is what he is. He was my mother's closest advisor and mine, and he will be whoever's he needs to be next." Aldous's voice dropped. "But that's not what you're really asking, is it. You asked about Lena."

The heron struck the water and came up with nothing, and stood there again, unbothered, unembarrassed.

"My sister left in 1987," Aldous said. "She was twenty-eight. She and my mother had a fight — a real one, the kind you don't recover from. I never learned all of it. Lena wouldn't say, and my mother wouldn't say, and the house just... closed around it, the way it does. She went to the city. She sent a card the first Christmas. Then nothing." His hands were shaking again; he pressed them flat against the stone. "In 2009 we got word she'd drowned. Up the coast, some town I'd never heard of. Dr. Fenn handled the paperwork — he was our family doctor, he did everything for us, God rest him. There was no body to bury. There was never a body. My mother paid for a headstone in the cemetery plot with Lena's name on it and an empty grave underneath, and she never spoke my sister's name again for the rest of her life. Not once. Fifteen years."

"And you never questioned it?"

"Questioned what? The certificate was signed. Fenn signed it. Ambrose Fenn, who delivered me, who sat with my father while he died. You don't question a man like that. You *can't*." Aldous turned and looked at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and, beneath it all, terrified. "But you're questioning it. I can see it. Why are you questioning it, Doctor? My mother fell off this breakwater. Your own paper says so. What could Lena possibly have to do with—"

He stopped. Paloma watched the thought arrive, watched it move through his face like a shadow crossing a field: *a dead sister. a will. a challenge. a claimant.*

"No," he said. "No. That's — she's dead. She's been dead fifteen years. There's no—"

"Mr. Voss." Paloma kept her voice level, kept it kind, and hated how much the kindness cost her. "I haven't said anything. You've said everything."

He stared at her. The wind came up and flattened the water and for a moment the whole harbor went gray and hard as slate.

"I don't know what I've said," he whispered. "I don't know anything anymore. My mother is dead, my sister is dead, and in eight months the house goes to the town and that's the end of the Voss family, and maybe that's what everyone wanted. Maybe that's what *she* wanted." He was crying now, openly, without shame, a big man on a cold stone with his grief finally out of its cage. "I loved her. Whatever you think you know — I loved her, and I loved my sister, and I have spent my whole life in a house where loving people was the one thing nobody knew how to do."

Paloma sat beside him and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not be a lie or a promise, and she had built fifteen years on giving the bereaved neither.

* * *

She left him there. He asked to be left; she honored it. She walked back along the breakwater with the wind at her back and her mind moving fast and cold, sorting what she'd gathered the way she sorted evidence at a scene — by weight, by provenance, by what it cost the person who gave it up.

Aldous had given her three things, and only one of them on purpose.

First: the will. Everything to the town, sealed at the anniversary dinner, eight months out. A deadline. She had felt it the moment he said it — not as information but as pressure, the way you feel a storm front before the barometer moves. Deadlines changed people. Deadlines made careful men careless and honest men strategic. Somewhere in Bellhaven, someone was already counting the days.

Second: Corwin Hale. *He's very keen that nobody challenge it.* Aldous had said it as complaint, but Paloma heard architecture. The executor of an unchallengeable will was a man with a fortress. The executor of a contested one was a man under siege. She thought of Hale at the funeral reception, standing at the edge of the room with his wine untouched, watching her watch him, and she thought: *he bills his own breathing.* A man like that kept accounts of everything. She wondered what his accounts would show if anyone ever audited them.

Third — the one he had not meant to give: *she had two children.* Present tense, slipping into past, the tongue of a man who had never fully believed the empty grave. Either Aldous Voss knew his sister was alive, or he knew the death was wrong in some way he'd never let himself examine. And either way, he was afraid — not of her, not exactly. Of the digging. Of what the digging would cost.

Paloma reached the shore and turned once to look back. Aldous was still sitting at the end of the breakwater, small against the gray water, a man alone on the last piece of his family's world.

Eight months, she thought. The clock had been running since the night Eileen Voss went into the harbor, and Paloma had signed the certificate and let it run. She would not let it run unwatched.

She turned toward town — and stopped. Across the harbor road, parked in the shade of the chandlery, sat a gray sedan she did not recognize, and a man leaned against its fender with the studied idleness of someone who had been standing there long enough to watch her whole walk back from the breakwater.

When she looked directly at him, he tipped an invisible hat, got in the car, and drove away without hurry.

Paloma stood on the shore with the wind pulling at her coat and felt, for the first time in fifteen years of certifying the dead, that she had walked into a story already in motion — and that everyone in it knew their part except her.
