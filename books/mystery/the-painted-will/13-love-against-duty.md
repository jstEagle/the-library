## 13. Love Against Duty

### Paloma

The bookshop smelled of glue and old paper, and Paloma told herself, for the fourth time that week, that she had come in for a reason.

She had one. She'd written it down, even, on the pad she kept by her kitchen phone: *Ask about binding repairs — Fenn's case notes.* It was true. The ledgers from the old days were falling apart, spines cracked, and Nora Quill was the only bookbinder in forty miles. It was a reason. It was even a good one.

It was not the reason.

"Coroner." Nora looked up from the workbench, a bone folder in her hand, a half-bound volume open under the lamp. There was flour on her — no, not flour. Wheat paste, Paloma corrected herself. She knew the difference now. She was learning the texture of this woman's days the way she'd once learned the texture of Fenn's.

"Ms. Quill."

"You can't keep calling me that like we're strangers. You've been in here four times."

"Three."

"Four. You came in on a Sunday and pretended to be lost." Nora set down the folder. "In a town with four streets."

Paloma put the ledger on the counter. "My mentor's case notes. The bindings are failing. I'd like them repaired — properly. They're not replaceable."

"Nothing is," Nora said, and there was something under the words, some sediment of meaning that Paloma could not dredge up without revealing how much she'd already dredged elsewhere. She opened the ledger to the first page instead, and Nora bent over it, and for a moment they were both silent over the same handwriting, and Paloma thought: *You knew him. You must have. Did he hold your face in his hands the way he held mine when my mother died? Did he tell you that grief is just love with nowhere to file itself?*

"These will take a month," Nora said. "Maybe longer. I do them at night, after the shop closes. You could come by."

"You could deliver them."

"I could," Nora agreed, and did not, and they both understood that the ledgers were going to live in this shop for a very long time, and that neither of them minded.

Paloma walked home along the seawall. The tide was out and the mudflats shone like hammered pewter. She caught herself composing the evening — not planning it, exactly, but *anticipating* it, the way she had not anticipated anything since Fenn died. It frightened her, the lightness of it. Fifteen years she had built her life out of weight: files, signatures, names of the dead, the grave ledger she kept in her head. Lightness felt like a structural flaw.

Because she knew. That was the thing she carried under her coat like a warm stone. *Nora Quill is Lena Voss.* She had known since the lighthouse records, since the photograph, since the moment the woman had said *I do them at night* with hands that had once signed — what? What had Lena Voss's hands done, the night her mother died?

Paloma did not know. That was the other thing she carried. She knew the name and not the truth, and every kindness she offered was a kind of interrogation, and every kindness was also — and this was the unbearable part — real.

She stopped at the breakwater. Below her, the rocks where Eileen Voss had been found were black and slick with the retreating tide. Somewhere out there, a woman had watched her mother die and had said nothing for fifteen years, and was now binding books with wheat paste on her hands, and had asked Paloma to come by in the evening.

*What are you waiting for?* Paloma asked the water. *What are you waiting to tell me?*

The water did what water does. It kept everything.

### Nora

She should have left in March. She had a rule — fifteen years old, worn smooth as a sea stone: *no more than two years in any town, no close friends, no one who asks questions.* Bellhaven had broken the rule by being small enough to disappear in and dull enough that no one looked twice. She had been Nora Quill for three years now, longer than any of her names, and she had begun, dangerously, to like her.

And then the letter had come — no, the letter had come to *Paloma*, she was almost sure of it, and the town's weather had changed. She'd seen it in the way Aldous Voss stood at the harbor railing. In the way Corwin Hale's smile had developed a hairline fracture. And in the way the coroner kept walking into her shop with ledgers and excuses and eyes that were kind and cataloguing at once.

Nora knew that look. Ambrose Fenn had worn it too — the look of a person who files everything, who loves carefully because love is a record, and records can be subpoenaed.

Tonight Paloma was coming at seven. Nora had closed the shop early and made tea she didn't drink and arranged the workbench so that Fenn's ledgers were visible, spines freshly sewn, and then moved them so they weren't so visible, and then moved them back.

The bell over the door rang at seven exactly.

"You're punctual," Nora said.

"Deadlines," Paloma said. "Occupational habit."

"Coroners have deadlines?"

"Everyone does. Most people just don't know their date yet." Paloma took off her coat — she'd never done that before, and Nora watched her do it with a strange tightening in her chest, the way you watch a bird land on your hand. "The ledgers look good."

"They're not done."

"They look *better*. There's a difference." Paloma sat on the stool across the bench, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the shop filled with the particular silence of two people listening to each other not-talk.

Then Paloma said, "Tell me about the worst thing you ever repaired."

Nora blinked. "What?"

"Books. Everyone in a trade has a worst one. Fenn had a worst autopsy. A boy, a cliff, a bad rope. He never described it fully. He'd just say *the rope* and stop." Paloma's voice was even, but her thumb was pressing a crease into the counter's edge, and Nora saw it, and saw that Paloma knew she saw it, and that neither of them would mention it.

"A Bible," Nora said. "Water damage. A woman brought it in — her husband had drowned, and the book had gone in with him. Every page warped. The ink had bled so the psalms ran into each other." She turned a page of Fenn's ledger, smoothing it flat with her palm. "She wanted it readable again. I told her it couldn't be what it was. She said she didn't want it to be what it was. She wanted it to be *legible*. She wanted to be able to read it without the water getting in the way."

"And did you manage it?"

"Mostly. There are pages you can't save. You can only stabilize what's left and stop the damage from spreading." Nora looked up. "That's the whole trade, really. You can't undo. You can only keep it from getting worse."

Paloma held her gaze a moment too long. "Do you believe that applies to things that aren't books?"

"No," Nora said, before she could choose a safer answer. "Books are the only things I've found where that's true."

The tea went cold. Paloma stayed until nine. When she left, she paused at the door and said, "The woman with the Bible. Did she ever come back for it?"

"Every week for a year," Nora said. "Then she stopped."

"Did that mean she'd made her peace?"

Nora turned off the lamp. In the dark, the shop was all grey shapes and the smell of paste. "It meant she stopped coming back. That's all I ever know about anyone. That's the deal with this town — I get to be no one, and no one gets to know me."

"That sounds lonely," Paloma said from the doorway, and it wasn't an accusation. It was something worse. It was recognition.

### Paloma

They fell into it the way water finds a channel. Tuesday evenings, then Sundays, then an unspoken almost-every-day: walks along the seawall after the shop closed, Nora's hands still smelling of paste, Paloma's coat pockets full of things she would never show her.

Paloma had spent her adult life being the one who knew. The coroner was the town's keeper of last facts; people told her things at gravesides they'd never tell their priests. Now she was on the other side of it, and she understood for the first time what it cost — that knowing someone's secret without their consent is a small theft committed daily, and that the interest compounds.

She tried to give things back. Small truths, offered like coins.

"I was engaged once," she said on the seawall, the third week. "Marcus. He wanted children. I wanted the files. We were both very reasonable about it for two years and then he married a teacher in Portland and I certified a drowning that same week and I remember thinking the water was more honest than I'd been."

Nora walked a few steps without answering. "Do you regret it?"

"The drowning?"

"The honesty."

Paloma laughed — a real one, surprised out of her. "No. I regret the two reasonable years."

"I ran from someone," Nora said, and Paloma's pulse ticked up like a gauge. "Not a man. A family. The kind you can't divorce, only escape. I did escape. And the price was that I stopped existing, officially, and I've been — improvising a person ever since." She said it lightly, a stone skipped across deep water, and Paloma let it sink without reaching for it. *Not yet. Not until you choose to tell me. Not until I can tell you I already know.*

"Did it work?" Paloma asked. "The escape."

Nora looked out at the lighthouse, white against the dusk. "Ask me again in a month."

* * *

### Nora

She was telling the truth in pieces. That was the strategy she'd settled on, if you could call it a strategy — feed the coroner enough true things that when the whole truth came, it would arrive as a continuation and not a betrayal. It was also, she admitted on the long dark walk home, simply what she wanted: to be known by this woman in increments, so that each increment could be survived.

Because Paloma Kavanagh was not a woman you survived all at once. She was a woman you survived the way you survived winter — gradually, and with stores laid in.

"She sounds like someone I knew," Nora said one night. They were on the dune path above the lighthouse, and the wind was doing its work on both their coats. "My — a mentor. He used to say that grief is love with nowhere to file itself."

Paloma stopped walking.

"He said that?" Her voice had changed — gone careful, the way it went careful in her shop when the ledgers came up.

"He did. He was a coroner too. In a town I won't name. He taught me that the dead are owed two things: the truth and the dignity of not being made a spectacle of, and that the hard part of the job is when those two things disagree."

"Ambrose," Paloma said quietly. Not a question.

"You knew him."

"He trained me." Paloma's face was turned toward the sea and Nora could not read it, and she realized with a lurch that she had made an error — had given away too much or not enough, had revealed that she knew Fenn intimately without revealing *how*, and that Paloma was doing that filing thing behind her eyes, slotting this into a cabinet Nora couldn't see.

"He died two years ago," Nora said. "I read the notice."

"Did you go to the funeral?"

"I couldn't." *Because I was dead, and dead women don't attend funerals, and he's the one who killed me off, and he did it to save my life, and I never thanked him, and now he's in the ground and I'm the only person alive who knows what he was.* The sentence assembled itself whole in her chest and she said none of it. "I sent nothing. That's the part I regret. People should be thanked while — " She stopped.

"While they're alive," Paloma finished. "He'd have hated a fuss."

"He'd have hated it and needed it. Those were the same thing with him, I think."

Paloma turned to her then, and the wind pushed Nora's hair across her face, and Paloma reached over — hesitated, a full second, the hesitation of a woman who measures everything — and tucked it back behind Nora's ear. Her fingers were cold from the sea air. They stayed a moment longer than the task required.

"I'm glad you knew him," Paloma said. "He didn't have many people who — " She stopped herself, and Nora watched her choose a smaller sentence. "He didn't have many people."

They walked back in the dark, shoulders almost touching, and Nora thought: *She knows. She has to know. No one asks questions that precisely by accident.* And then she thought: *And she hasn't turned me in. And she keeps coming back. So either she doesn't know, or she knows and is waiting for me, and I don't know which is worse, and I don't know which is more like love.*

* * *

### Paloma

The night it almost broke open, they were in the shop and the rain was coming down like a verdict.

Nora was sewing headbands onto a rebacked volume, and Paloma was supposed to be examining the work, and instead she was watching the woman's hands and thinking about the pill organizer and the foxglove and the gray sedan she'd seen twice now idling near the harbor, and she said, without deciding to:

"What would you do if you found out someone you cared about had done something terrible? Not to you. Just — terrible. And the reasons were good. Or the reasons were bad but the person wasn't. Would it matter?"

The needle stopped.

"Why?" Nora said. "What did you find out?"

"Nothing. Hypothetically."

"Paloma." First time she'd used her name. It went through Paloma like a stitch through cloth. "You're the coroner. You don't do hypotheticals. You do certificates."

Paloma looked at the rain on the window, at the street beyond it where the gray sedan had been parked an hour ago and was parked no longer. "I found out that a man I loved did something that was both a crime and a mercy, and I can't certify it either way, and the longer I wait the more it costs, and the only person who can tell me which it was — " She stopped. *Careful. Careful.* " — is dead."

Nora set down the needle. For a long moment she looked at Paloma with an expression Paloma had no file for — grief and fear and something that looked unbearably like the urge to confess.

"Maybe," Nora said slowly, "he left something. A record. People like him — people like *us* — always leave a record. It's the one thing we can't help."

"Us," Paloma repeated.

"Bookbinders and coroners," Nora said, and smiled, and the smile didn't reach her eyes. "We're in the business of what's left."

Paloma left at ten. At the door she paused, and the question she'd been carrying for weeks rose in her throat — *Who are you, really?* — and she swallowed it, because she had finally understood that the answer would cost her this: the Tuesday evenings, the walks, the cold fingers at a temple, the whole fragile architecture of almost.

She would let Nora tell her. She had to. It was the only version of this that could survive the truth.

She was three steps down the wet street when she heard the shop bell ring behind her, and turned, and Nora was standing in the doorway with her coat half on.

"Paloma. The man you loved — the one who's dead. Ambrose." Her voice shook on the name. "Did he — did anything of his ever come to you? A letter? Anything at all?"

Paloma's heart went very still and very loud.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because I think," Nora said, and the rain came down between them like a curtain, "he may have left something with me too."
