## 19. The Night Before the Dinner

The tide was going out when I told her.

I had chosen the hour deliberately, the way I chose everything in this second life of mine — by what it concealed. Low tide exposed the bones of Bellhaven's harbor: the black ribs of old wrecks, the mussel-crusted pilings, the smell of sulfur and rot that drove even the gulls inland. No one walked the flats at dusk. No one but a bookbinder who had spent eleven years learning to be no one, and a coroner who had spent fifteen years learning to be everyone's keeper of the dead.

"I'll testify," I said. "Tomorrow. Before the dinner. I'll walk into Voss House as Lena Voss and I'll say his name in front of all of them."

Paloma didn't answer at once. She stood with her boots sinking slightly into the wet sand, her hands in the pockets of her coat, and I watched her do the thing she always did — file me. I could almost see the drawer opening behind her eyes, the card being written, the cross-reference being made. *Nora Quill, bookbinder, states intent to testify. See also: everything she has not told you.*

"You understand what it costs," she said finally.

"I've been paying the interest for eleven years. I'd like to pay the principal and be done."

"And Fenn?" Her voice was gentle, which was how I knew the question was not. "If you stand up in that room and name Corwin Hale, someone will ask how a dead woman came to be standing. Someone will ask who signed your death certificate. Ambrose Fenn's name will come out of the ground with yours."

The tide sucked at the sand between us. Far out, a heron stood on one leg in the shallows, patient as a debt.

"I know," I said. "He knew too. That's why he mailed the letter. He said —" My throat closed. I hadn't spoken of that night aloud in two years, and the words came out rusted. "He said a debt you take to the grave isn't repaid, it's just transferred. He wouldn't let me carry it. He made me promise I'd decide for myself, but he mailed the letter anyway, because he knew what I'd decide if no one made me choose."

Paloma looked at me for a long moment, and I understood that she had already opened Fenn's letters, already matched the handwriting, already grieved this in private. She was not learning anything from me tonight. She was giving me the dignity of telling her anyway.

"Then we do it in order," she said. "Not at the dinner. Before. You come to my office at eight in the morning, you give your statement, I notarize it, and we walk into Voss House together with paper instead of a speech. Paper doesn't get shouted down."

"You've thought about this."

"I've thought about nothing else for three weeks." Something moved through her face then — something I had seen only in fragments, in the pauses between her sentences, in the way her eyes rested on me a half-second longer than the conversation required. She looked out at the water. "Nora. If it goes wrong — if it goes wrong in any way — you don't run alone. That's the whole point of doing it together."

I wanted to tell her. God help me, I wanted to say *I know who you are to me, I've known since the day you came into my shop with salt-damaged hands and asked me to repair a ledger, and I have loved you in the coward's way, silently, the way I do everything* — but the words were a door I had kept locked so long I no longer trusted what was behind it. So I said, "All right. Eight o'clock."

"All right," she said.

We walked back along the flats as the light died, not touching, our shadows stretching toward each other across the sand like two people reaching in their sleep.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in the apartment above the shop with the lamp off and the window cracked to the sound of the harbor, and I made my lists the way I always did when terror came: passport, in the tin box under the floorboard. Cash, four hundred and twelve dollars. The bus that left Bellhaven at 6:40, the one I had memorized eleven years ago and never taken. I told myself I would not run. I told myself that running was the one thing Corwin Hale could never take from me again, and that if I ran now I would be giving it back to him, handing over the last thing that was mine.

At some point I must have slept, because I woke to a sound I could not name — not a sound, really, but an absence. The harbor gulls, which began their argument at first light every morning of my life in Bellhaven, were silent.

I went down the back stairs in the gray half-dark with the tin box in my hand.

The shop door was ajar. I had locked it. I always locked it; I had the paranoia of a woman who had died once already and did not care to do it twice.

Inside, the smell hit me first — glue and torn linen and the sour tang of someone else's sweat. Then the light through the front window found the wreckage. My workbench, overturned. The presses askew. Books pulled from the shelves and slit open, their spines cracked, pages fanned across the floor like the feathers of something killed. My tools were gone — the bone folders, the awls, the knives, all of it — not stolen, I understood at once, but *removed*, because knives in the hands of a dead woman make a story, and stories were Hale's profession.

The floorboard by the hearth was pried up. The tin box sat open and empty in the hole, gleaming dully.

My passport. Lena Voss's passport — the one Fenn had obtained for me, the one with my true name in it, the one that proved I existed — was gone.

I stood in the middle of eleven years of my life, scattered and slit open, and I did the arithmetic with the cold, clear part of the mind that had survived my first death. They had not come to frighten me. Frightening was cheap and Hale did not spend cheaply. They had come for the passport because a passport is a document, and documents can be amended. A dead woman's passport in the hands of the executor of the estate — a woman who died abroad, perhaps; a body never recovered, a certificate signed by a doctor now conveniently deceased — it would take Hale a single morning and a single phone call to a single obliging clerk to make my death certificate not a lie but a *record*. To make it true retroactively. To close, permanently, the one door I had ever left open.

And the note. There was a note. It was pinned to the wall above my workbench with one of my own awls — the awls that were supposed to be gone, this one left deliberately, a signature — and beneath it a sheet of my own bookbinding paper, the good rag paper I reserved for commissions, as if to say *we were in your most private drawer, we touched the things you touch*.

The dinner happens either way.

I read it twice. My hands were steady. That was the strangest part — eleven years of fear had worn my hands into something beyond shaking. I took the note down, folded it into my coat, and I did not run.

I told myself I did not run because running was what they wanted. That was part of it. But the larger part, the part I admitted only as I slipped out the back into the alley where the fog was coming in off the water like a held breath — the larger part was a woman with gray in her dark hair and a coroner's calm and a way of saying my false name as if it were a real one. Eight o'clock, she had said. You don't run alone.

I would not make her a liar.

* * *

The fog was thick enough to drink. I went the long way, the way I'd mapped in the first year of my new life and never needed until now — the fish-cannery road, the cut behind St. Brendan's, the footpath along the breakwater where Eileen Voss had walked on the last night of her life. My mother's last night. I had avoided that path for eleven years the way you avoid a stair in the dark that once broke your leg, and now I walked it in fog with my dead mother's name in my mouth like a stone.

I was three hundred yards from Paloma's office when the car came.

Gray sedan. I had seen it twice before — once outside the bookshop in the rain, once idling at the end of Quay Street with a man reading a newspaper he never turned. The umbrella leaning in the back seat had a brass handle and a familiar crest: the interlocked C and H that Hale & Associates had stamped on every legal document in Bellhaven for thirty years.

The sedan did not speed. That was the horror of it — it did not need to. It simply rolled alongside me at walking pace, window down, fog curling into the car like something being poured, and the man in the passenger seat — a soft-faced man I had never seen, which somehow made it worse; Hale's hands were always soft-faced and unseen — said, "Ms. Quill. Mr. Hale would like a word before your eight o'clock. He's very reasonable. You'll find the drive pleasant."

I did not answer. I turned and walked the other way, back toward the breakwater, back toward the water, and the car kept pace, unhurried, a shadow with headlights.

"Your shop's a shame," the soft voice said behind me, conversational, almost kind. "All those beautiful books. Mr. Hale said to tell you: the dinner happens either way. But it can happen with you at the table or without you at all. Dead women don't get to choose which."

I reached the breakwater steps. Below, the tide had turned; it was coming in now, filling the channel, erasing the flats where Paloma and I had stood. I looked at the water and I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *he doesn't need to kill me. He just needs me to be unfindable by morning. He needs the certificate and the absence to agree.*

I cut left, down the fish-cannery road, into the fog, and I ran — not away, I kept telling myself, not away, *toward* — toward the one person in Bellhaven who knew what I was and had not flinched. The fog took the car's engine sound and folded it away. My breath tore. The tin box was gone, the passport was gone, the shop was gone; everything I had built to be no one was gone, and what was left, running through the wet gray morning with my lungs on fire, was not no one at all.

I was Lena Voss, and I was running toward the light in Paloma Kavanagh's office window the way the drowned are said to run toward the surface.

I never reached it.

The alley behind the cannery was a throat of brick and shadow, and the fog made it a blind one. I heard them before I saw them — not the sedan, footfalls, two sets, closing with the unhurried confidence of men who had done this before and been paid well for it. I turned. The soft-faced man stood at the mouth of the alley with his hands in his pockets, and behind him, half in the fog, a second figure held something folded and dark.

"Mr. Hale's car is just here," the soft-faced man said. "You'll be comfortable. You'll be *found* comfortable, after — somewhere public, somewhere sad, somewhere with water. It's better this way, truly. A dead woman who stays dead is at peace. A dead woman who testifies is just a woman in a courtroom with a forged passport and a perjury charge and a family that's been looking for her for eleven years. Think of it as mercy."

"I've been dead since 2009," I said, and my voice did not shake, and I marveled at it. "You're late."

Something flickered in his soft face — respect, or its counterfeit — and then the rag came down over my mouth from behind, and the world went to chemical sweetness and gray.

My last thought, going down into the dark, was not of the passport or the will or the eleven wasted years. It was of a woman on a tidal flat at dusk saying *you don't run alone*, and of the clock above her office door, which I had glanced at a hundred times without ever reading it, and of how it would now be standing at some minute past eight, ticking, while I did not come.

*Hold the lights on,* I thought, as the fog closed over me like a page turning. *Whatever you do — hold the lights on.*

* * *

At 8:47 in the morning, Paloma Kavanagh stood in the doorway of a ransacked bookshop with the fog at her back, and I was not there to see what her face did.

I know what it must have done, because I knew her by then — knew her the way you know a shoreline you've sailed past a hundred times, by the shape of it in bad weather. She would have stood very still, the way she stood over bodies. She would have seen the slit spines and the empty tin box and the single awl left behind like a signature, and she would have read the room the way she read everything: completely, and in order, and without flinching until there was nothing left to read.

And then she would have understood — with the terrible clarity of a woman who has certified a thousand deaths and knows exactly how few hours stand between a living witness and a tidy one — that Corwin Hale was not trying to stop the testimony.

He was trying to make the paperwork true. Lena Voss, missing. Lena Voss, presumed drowned. Lena Voss, dead before the dinner could be challenged — and the clause sealed forever by ten o'clock tomorrow night, with the whole of Bellhaven raising its glasses to a lie.

She would have looked at the empty floorboard where my passport had been, and then at the fog rolling in off the harbor, and she would have said my name — my false one, my true one, both of them, she was the only person alive who carried them both now — into that empty, ruined room.

And somewhere across town, in the cold dark of a boathouse on Voss land, tied to the very posts my father's boats had once been moored to, I was waiting for her to find me — with the tide rising, and the anniversary dinner eleven hours away, and only one woman in Bellhaven who knew that a dead girl was still breathing.
