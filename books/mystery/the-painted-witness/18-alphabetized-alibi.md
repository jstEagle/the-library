## 18. Alphabetized Alibi

The evidence room at the county annex smelled of cold coffee and toner, and at eleven p.m. it belonged entirely to Priya Raman.

She had signed out twenty-three leather ledgers. They sat stacked on the steel table in front of her like a wall of small, patient headstones—*Marchetti, O., Guest Records, 2003–2004*, *2004–2005*, and on through two decades in unbroken annual volumes, each one indexed in a hand so precise it looked typeset. The defense would call them obsessive. The prosecution, if she could ever get one interested, might call them something else. Tonight she just called them what Oscar had told her to call them.

*Read my ledgers like a novel, not a file.*

She pulled the first volume toward her, cracked its spine—it gave with a sigh, like an old man settling into a chair—and began.

***

The first hour was disorienting. The entries were not what a hotel ledger was supposed to be.

Room 214, checked in under *R. Calloway*, 9:14 p.m., paid cash. Fine. Normal. But beneath it, in the same flawless hand: *RC = R. Calloway per ID; voice higher than registration tone; carried own pillow; asked twice about east wing access.* And beneath that, a small circled glyph: **V.C. — 22:40 — corridor 2 — 6 min.**

Six minutes. Vivian Cross had spent six minutes in the second-floor corridor outside Room 214 forty minutes after check-in, and Oscar Marchetti had written it down the way other men wrote down the weather.

Raman leaned back. Under the fluorescent hum, the evidence room felt suddenly crowded—not with people, but with attention. Twenty years of a man watching everything, recording everything, filing everything, and no one had ever thought to read it as anything but housekeeping.

She found the key to the system at 12:40 a.m., in the back matter of the 2005 volume: a single page titled, without irony, *Index of Persons.* It was exactly what he'd claimed in holding. Alphabetized. Every guest, every staff member, every recurring visitor assigned initials, and after the initials, in columns too neat to be accidental, cross-reference numbers pointing to dates in other volumes.

*T.B. — Beckett, Thomas — groundskeeper — see 10/04, 11/04, 03/05, 09/11, 06/19.*

*M.H. — Hale, Margaret.*

*E.H. — see restricted index.*

Restricted index. There was no restricted index in the volume. Either it had never existed or it lived somewhere else—in his head, or in whatever notebook Vivian Cross had torn her archive apart looking for.

Raman got up, stretched until her spine popped, and made herself another coffee from the machine that tasted like punishment. Then she went back to the table and started building what the ledgers alone refused to be: a timeline.

She taped butcher paper along the length of the table. Across the top she wrote the deaths—or rather, the events, because only one of them was officially a death yet. October 14th, 2004: Edmund Hale's funeral. March 2005: Gerald Hoyt, funeral director, heart attack at fifty-one, ruled natural. September 2011: a county records clerk named Dolores Wynn, retired, aneurysm in her garden. June 2019: an appraiser passing through town, found dead in his room at the Ashgrove Hotel itself, cause listed as cardiac event, file closed in nine days. And now Tom Beckett, three weeks ago, in the greenhouse, with swapped medication sitting in an evidence bag ten feet from where she worked.

Five events. Five ledgers. She opened each to the relevant season and began copying entries onto the paper, date by date, initial by initial.

By three a.m., her hand was cramping and her eyes burned, and the butcher paper had become something she had to stand back from to see properly.

Because it was a map. Not of geography—of proximity.

Around every event, like iron filings around a magnet, the same initials clustered. V.C. V.C. V.C. In the week before Gerald Hoyt's death: *V.C. — 10/03 — met G.H. at ferry office, 25 min.* Two days before: *G.H. arrived hotel unregistered — V.C. private parlor, 1 hr 50 min.* The day after the funeral director died: *V.C. — composed; took tea in lobby at usual hour; declined condolence calls (3).* Declined condolence calls. Who logs their own boss declining condolences? A man building a record. A man who understood, even then, that someday someone would read this not as gossip but as testimony.

Dolores Wynn, the records clerk—the woman who would have processed any amendment to the cemetery's burial permits. Three days before her aneurysm: *V.C. — drove to county seat herself, unusual; returned 4 hrs later; said nothing at dinner.* The day after: *V.C. — requested copy of permit file "for family archive."*

And the appraiser in 2019, the one nobody had ever connected to anything because why would they—a man valuing the hotel's art collection, a man who would have examined paintings, provenance, estate holdings. Room 306. *Checked in as D. Merritt; actual name per luggage tag: D. Merriweather, estate appraiser, Portland.* Oscar had caught the alias in real time, twenty years into his habit, and logged it without comment. Two nights later: *V.C. — 11:55 p.m. — stairwell 3 — carrying nothing up, small case coming down.* The morning after: *Guest in 306 did not come down for breakfast. Housekeeping instructed to wait until noon.*

Wait until noon. She read that line four times. *Housekeeping instructed to wait until noon.* By whom? The entry didn't say. It didn't have to. The instruction came from ownership, and ownership was V.C., and the man writing it down knew exactly what he was preserving for whoever might someday stand in a cold evidence room at three in the morning with butcher paper and bad coffee and a warrant request she couldn't yet write.

Raman stood over the map for a long time. The pattern wasn't subtle once you saw it. It was a signature written across two decades in invisible ink that only patience could develop. Vivian Cross moved toward every death like a tide moves toward a shore—unhurried, inevitable, leaving no footprints because she owned the beach.

It was beautiful. It was damning.

And it was worthless.

***

That was the word District Attorney Alan Prentiss used, anyway, at nine the next morning, standing in her office doorway with his sleeves rolled and his tie already loosened, which meant he'd been briefed and had decided to be disappointed in person.

"Walk me through it," he said, though his tone suggested he'd rather not.

She walked him through it. She'd rebuilt the butcher paper on her office wall overnight—she'd driven back to the annex at six a.m. to do it, running on four hours of sleep and a conviction that felt load-bearing. She showed him the clusters. The initials. The stairwell entry. The *wait until noon.*

Prentiss studied it the way a man studies a painting he's been told is valuable and can't personally see why. "These are a concierge's notes."

"These are contemporaneous records kept by a trained observer over twenty years."

"They're a concierge's notes," he repeated gently, the way you'd talk to someone whose relative had joined something. "Unsworn. Unwitnessed. Kept by a man currently charged with murdering Thomas Beckett, whose fingerprints are on the murder weapon, whose own notebooks read—" he tapped the photocopy of Oscar's surveillance-style entries— "like a stalker's diary. You want me to take the state's case against your defendant and rebuild it around his hobby?"

"He's not the defendant yet. Charges were dropped."

"Charges were *suspended* because the DA's office has an election in November and I don't try cases I can't win." He said it flatly, without shame; Prentiss wore his pragmatism like a badge. "Here's what I've got that's real: prints on the bottle. Motive by way of grudge—he was suspended, he was obsessed with a twenty-year-old case, Beckett talked to him the day before he died. That's a jury-friendly package, Priya. Clean. Refillable."

"It's also wrong."

"Maybe. Prove it differently." He looked at the wall again, and for a moment—one moment—something flickered behind the pragmatism. She watched him see it: the stairwell, the small case coming down, the *wait until noon.* Watched him almost believe. Then the flicker guttered out, because believing cost more than disbelieving, and Alan Prentiss had built a career on knowing the price of things. "You know what happens if I put this wall in front of a defense attorney? He calls it a conspiracy scrapbook. He puts Marchetti on the stand and walks him around the room for an afternoon—*Mr. Marchetti, you recorded the private movements of thousands of guests without their knowledge, didn't you? You kept files on this town, didn't you? Isn't it true you constructed this entire narrative yourself?*—and by four o'clock my case is confetti and yours truly is a footnote."

"Then don't put the wall up. Use it as a roadmap. The ledgers tell us where to look for real evidence—the ferry office log from October 2004, the county permit file Wynn pulled, the appraiser's report from Portland that nobody ever collected. Follow the map, get the documents, and the documents convict her."

"With what budget? With what time?" He rubbed his eyes. "Beckett's sister is suing the hotel. The board wants this closed. Half the department thinks we already had our guy and let him go out of sentiment. And the other half won't say what they think, which worries me more." He straightened his tie, decision made, door handle already turning. "Ninety days. If you bring me physical evidence linking Vivian Cross to any of it—any of it—I'll run the table. Until then, the wall comes down and the file stays open and quiet."

"Alan."

He paused.

"The night porter. Danny Ferro. He's ready to give a statement about the intercom system and about being followed in the east wing. And there's a witness—Ada Finch, the old county registrar—who hasn't been seen since Tuesday. Her car's at the ferry terminal. Her teacup was still warm."

Something shifted in his face. Not belief. Arithmetic. "Finch," he said slowly. "Missing persons has it?"

"Missing persons has a form. I have a pattern." She pointed at the wall, at the tidy clusters of initials, at twenty years of a tide going out and coming back. "Every person who ever knew what happened in October 2004 is dead, silenced, or missing. Except three: a fugitive in protective custody, a concierge you want to charge, and a woman who signs her letters V.C. When Ada Finch turns up—and she will turn up, one way or the other—you're going to wish you'd spent ninety days instead of ninety hours."

Prentiss looked at the wall a long moment. Then he left without answering, which from Alan Prentiss was nearly a concession.

***

She didn't take the wall down.

Instead, at dusk, she drove to the hotel.

The Ashgrove at evening was a different animal than the Ashgrove of her interviews—all amber light and low voices, the lobby smelling of beeswax and old roses. Oscar wasn't behind the desk. Of course he wasn't; they'd suspended him, stripped the uniform off him, turned the institution's most faithful servant out onto the pavement. But the young porter—Danny, all elbows and watchful eyes—was restocking the brochure rack, and when he saw her he came over quickly, wiping his hands on his vest.

"Detective. He said you might come." He glanced toward the archive room door, then back. "He left something for you. Said you'd know when to use it, and not before."

"What is it?"

"A ledger page." Danny lowered his voice. "He said—his words—he said, 'Tell her it's the page that isn't in any book.'"

Raman took the envelope. Inside, on a sheet of the same cream stock as the ledgers, in that immaculate hand, was a single entry dated October 14th, 2004—reconstructed, the header noted, *from memory, 2024, verbatim as originally recorded.* She read it standing under the lobby chandelier, and the lobby seemed to tilt.

*11:52 p.m. Second casket received, light. First casket removed, heavy. V.C. supervised. E.H. observed from wheelchair at service entrance, weeping. T.B. drove. G.H. assisted. Weather: rain. Witness count including self: five. Four remain.*

Four remain. Written twenty years ago, in the rain-soaked aftermath, Oscar Marchetti had already counted them down like a man marking the fuse on something. And now the arithmetic had run itself down to its final terms: himself, Margaret Hale, Vivian Cross—and Ada Finch, missing, her car at the ferry terminal, her teacup warm.

Raman folded the page into her coat, over her heart, where she kept the things she hadn't yet earned the right to file.

Outside, the fog was coming off the water in slow gray sheets, and somewhere out past the harbor lights a boat was moving through it—she could feel it the way you feel weather changing, the way Oscar Marchetti must have felt the past being dug up on the day the county crew arrived with their shovels.

She got in her car and called the ferry terminal.

"Pull your October manifests," she said when the night clerk answered. "All of them. Especially anything booked through a shell company—Crosswood Charitable Trust or anything that smells like it. And check whether a blue hatchback registered to Ada Finch paid for a crossing it never took."

A pause on the line. Paper rustling. Then the clerk's voice, careful and strange:

"Detective, funny you should call. Somebody else asked about that same manifest yesterday. Woman. Paid cash for the copies." A beat. "Signed the receipt V.C."
