## 24. The Ferry Manifest

The telephone at the front desk had a voice Oscar Marchetti had trusted for thirty years—a warm, slightly hoarse murmur that made every caller sound like they were leaning across the counter toward him. He sat behind it now in the blue hour before dawn, the lobby dark except for the single lamp he allowed himself, and dialed the first of eleven numbers from memory.

He had not slept. Sleep, he'd decided somewhere around two in the morning, was a luxury for people whose friends weren't missing.

Ada Finch's cottage had been tidy the way a stopped clock is tidy—everything in its place, nothing moving. Raman had photographed the teacup, the warm ring on the saucer, the pale rectangle on the wallpaper where the little portrait of young Edmund had hung for decades. Oscar had stood in that cottage and felt something he hadn't felt since the night Tom Beckett failed to appear at the hedge maze: the specific cold of being late.

But late was not the same as too late. That distinction had become his religion.

The ferry terminal camera had shown Ada's sedan parked in the long-term lot, driver's door unlocked, keys in the ignition—an invitation to assume she'd walked off the pier on her own accord. The county sheriff's office had been content to assume exactly that. Raman did not have the jurisdiction or, yet, the evidence to argue otherwise. What they had was a warm teacup and a missing painting, and neither of those fit into an evidence bag with a bow on it.

What they had, Oscar thought, dialing, was hospitality.

* * *

"Marchetti." The voice on the line was gravel and salt—Gus Ferreira, who ran the Harborlight Inn down in Port Aldous and had run it, like Oscar, since the days when reservations came by postcard. "You know what time it is?"

"I do. I'm sorry. I need a favor, Gus, and I need it quietly."

A pause. In thirty years, Oscar had never asked Gus for anything louder than a late checkout for a honeymooning couple. The pause stretched, then folded. "Go."

"Your marina log. Tuesday evening, the six-forty ferry to the mainland. Did anyone unusual book passage? Anyone traveling alone, older woman, no luggage to speak of?"

"You're the third person this month asking after ferry passengers," Gus said, and Oscar's blood went thin. "County man came by yesterday. Friendly fella. Wanted to know if we'd seen anything odd."

Oscar closed his eyes. "And what did you tell him?"

"Told him the truth. Nothing odd. But—" the gravel shifted, "—he didn't ask about bookings. Just walk-ups. And here's the thing, Marchetti, and I'll deny I said it if you print it anywhere: nobody walks up to my ferry anymore. Everybody books. Online, phone, whatever. So if your woman boarded, she boarded under a booking, and the county man didn't ask me about bookings because he already had the manifest. Which means he wasn't looking for her. Which means somebody told him not to be."

Oscar wrote none of this down. Some things he kept where paper couldn't be subpoenaed. "Gus. The manifest. Can you get me Tuesday's passenger list?"

"It'll cost you a dinner at your restaurant that I've heard is better than mine."

"It isn't, but I'll lie to you over it. Tonight?"

"Tonight," Gus said, and hung up.

* * *

By seven, Oscar had worked through four more numbers. This was the part of his life no ledger recorded—the web of innkeepers, desk clerks, porters, maids, and night auditors strung along three hundred miles of coast like beads on a wire, all of them invisible people who noticed everything and were noticed by no one. He had spent decades cultivating them the way other men cultivated orchards: patiently, without expectation, watering each acquaintance with small kindnesses—a remembered birthday, a comped room when a marriage collapsed, a letter of reference written in his beautiful filing-cabinet hand. He had never thought of it as building a network. He had thought of it as simply being decent to people like himself.

Now the orchard was going to feed him, or fail to.

Marta Kowalczyk, night auditor at the Sandpiper in Cape Verity, remembered a booking for Tuesday's ferry-adjacent rooms: a party of one, reserved under the name **Harrow & Associates**, paid by corporate card. Marta had laughed about it at the time—*nobody names their company Harrow unless they want to be forgotten*, she'd said—and now she repeated the joke back to Oscar with less laughter in it.

"Harrow," Oscar said. The word sat in his mouth like a coin he recognized. "Marta, does the name Cross mean anything on that reservation?"

Silence on the line. Then, carefully: "Marchetti. Why would you ask me that?"

Because Vivian Cross owned four shell companies the way other women owned hats, and Oscar had catalogued every one of them across twenty years of ledgers—**Harrow & Associates** among them, the entity that had quietly billed suite 204's perpetual "maintenance" for two decades. Because Ada Finch had not wandered onto a ferry. She had been *checked out*, the way a guest is checked out, under a booked alias, by someone who understood that the difference between kidnapping and hospitality was only paperwork.

"Because I think an old woman is being held somewhere against her will," Oscar said, "and I think the reservation was made by the same hand that's been writing this town's secrets for twenty years."

Another silence. When Marta spoke again, her voice had changed—lower, faster, the voice of a night auditor who had spent her career watching things through glass. "There was a second name on the card. Not the guest—the guarantor. Sometimes corporations put a contact. It said **V.C., care of the Ashford Hotel**."

The Ashford was Vivian's sister property, forty miles south. Oscar thanked Marta, promised her the same dinner he'd promised Gus, and sat for a long moment with his hands flat on the desk, letting the shape of it settle over him.

Ada was alive. Booked, billeted, and held—alive because a dead registrar proved nothing, but a living one, produced at the right moment, could unmake Edmund Hale's testimony entirely. Vivian hadn't killed her. Vivian had *reserved* her.

It was, Oscar thought, the most horrifying thing he'd learned yet: that even abduction, in Vivian Cross's hands, looked like impeccable service.

* * *

Raman arrived at nine, off-duty, out of uniform, carrying two coffees and the expression of a woman who had spent the night losing arguments with a district attorney. She set one coffee in front of Oscar without asking and leaned against the desk.

"The DA wants to charge you with obstruction for even asking about the ferry," she said. "I talked him down to 'aggressively unhelpful.' You're welcome."

"She boarded under a booked alias," Oscar said. "Harrow & Associates. Corporate card, guaranteed by V.C. at the Ashford. She's alive, Detective. Vivian is keeping her the way you'd keep a witness you might still need—or a hostage you definitely do."

Raman stared at him. Then she pulled out her notebook, and Oscar watched—with a small, private satisfaction he didn't bother to hide—as she wrote down everything he'd just said in her own careful hand. Two record-keepers, he thought. The town had bred them like twins.

"If she's alive," Raman said slowly, "then this stops being a murder investigation and becomes a rescue. And a rescue needs a location, and a location needs probable cause, and probable cause needs more than a shell company's initials on a credit card."

"Then we find the location."

"How? The coast is three hundred miles of coves and inns, and she could be in any of them. Or in a basement. Or—" Raman stopped herself.

"Or nowhere," Oscar finished. "No. She's close. Think like a hotelier, Detective. You don't move a fragile eighty-year-old woman forty miles on a moment's notice. You keep your assets where you can service them. Vivian doesn't do distance. Vivian does *control*. Ada is within an hour of this hotel—I'd bet the ledgers on it."

"That's not evidence either."

"No," Oscar agreed. "But it's a smaller map."

* * *

The call from Port Aldous came at noon, earlier than expected, and Gus Ferreira's voice had lost its gravel humor entirely.

"Marchetti. My dockhand pulls the manifests for the harbormaster, don't ask how. Tuesday, six-forty sailing. One passenger booked under Harrow & Associates, single occupancy, wheelchair assistance requested at boarding."

Oscar's hand tightened on the receiver. "Wheelchair assistance."

"Like I said. And Marchetti—one more thing. The return booking. There isn't one. One-way, prepaid, no return leg on file. Whoever booked that passage never planned on bringing her back herself." A beat. "You find that woman. My mother was a registrar in the old country. They were the ones who kept the truth when nobody else would."

Oscar set down the phone and stood very still in the empty lobby, the morning light coming through the windows in long dusty blades, and let himself feel it—not triumph, nothing so clean—but the terrible, human weight of what they now knew.

Ada Finch was alive. An old woman with arthritis and a painter's eye, sitting somewhere in a wheelchair she hadn't needed last week, waiting in a room that smelled of dust, while the town below went on preparing anniversary bunting. Alive because her death would be inconvenient. Alive as a card to be played against her friend Edmund Hale's courage—and Edmund, frail and terrified in protective custody, did not yet know that the price of his testimony might be an old woman's life.

That was the cruelty of it, Oscar thought. Not the coffin, not the poison, not even the murders. The cruelty was that Vivian had turned love itself into leverage. Every person who might speak was now a person who could be *spent*.

He picked up the phone again and called Raman.

"She's alive," he said, before Raman could speak. "Booked one-way, wheelchair assistance, no return leg. Held somewhere within an hour of the hotel. And Detective—we have maybe days. Once Edmund's deposition is scheduled, she has no further use for a living registrar."

On the other end of the line, Raman was quiet for a long moment. Then: "My captain won't authorize a search grid on a hunch and a ferry manifest."

"Then don't ask him," Oscar said. "Ask Danny."

"The porter?"

"He knows every corridor, every sealed door, every room this hotel and its sister properties ever pretended didn't exist. And he's invisible, Detective—invisible people see everything. If Ada Finch is within an hour of here, she's in a building. And every building on this coast keeps records." He paused, and heard his own voice drop into something older and harder than the concierge's polite murmur. "Vivian taught me something these past weeks. She taught me that a reservation is a confession. Somewhere out there, there's a check-in register with Ada Finch's alias in it, in a hand we'll recognize. We just have to find which shelf it's sleeping on."

"And if we find it," Raman said, "and it leads us to her—what then? We can't storm a house on a forged registration card."

"No," Oscar said softly, looking up through the lobby windows toward the east wing, where the afternoon light was beginning to die behind the hedge maze. "Then we do what hotel people have always done. We knock politely. And we bring our own key."

Down the corridor, faint beneath the vacuum cleaner's drone, he could hear Danny's cart rattling toward the archive room—right on schedule, right where Oscar had told him to be. The boy had asked no questions when Oscar slipped him the list of sister-property addresses that morning. He'd only nodded, the way invisible people do, and gone to work.

Somewhere on that list, Oscar thought, a register was waiting. And somewhere in this building, a phone was about to ring that Vivian Cross didn't know she'd answered.
