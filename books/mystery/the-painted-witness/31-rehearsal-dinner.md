## 31. Rehearsal Dinner

The ballroom at eleven o'clock at night was a different animal than the ballroom at any other hour. By day it was a performance—chandeliers blazing, staff gliding, guests performing the pleasant fiction of their own importance. At night, with the house lights dropped to a single amber glow and the chairs stacked in their rehearsal rows, it became what it had always been underneath: a large, patient room with very good hearing.

Oscar Marchetti stood at the service entrance with a legal pad and a pencil, and he did what he had done for thirty-one years in this building, the one thing no one had ever thought to stop him from doing. He watched. He recorded.

*Service door: hinges oiled Tuesday, opens without sound. Sightline from head table to service door: clear, forty feet, two pillars at the twenty-foot marks. Chandelier above head table: sways slightly when kitchen door opens—air current. Note.*

His pencil moved in small, tight strokes, the shorthand of a man who had spent two decades compressing other people's lives into lines of code. *V.C. prefers the third chair from the window. E.H. will be wheeled from the east corridor—ramp installed Monday, new carpet smell, they haven't let it air out.* He wrote that last one and then sat with it for a moment, the pencil still against the page, because *they* was a word that meant Vivian, and Vivian had ordered a ramp installed for a father the town believed dead, and tomorrow four hundred people would watch that father raise a glass, and every detail of it had been arranged with the loving precision of a woman planning a funeral she intended to survive.

His hands, he noticed, were not steady.

He looked at them with a kind of clinical disappointment, the way he might look at a chipped piece of stemware that had somehow gotten past his inspection. Thirty-one years of perfect stillness. He had carried trays of consommé through a wedding where the groom's father was having a heart attack in the coatroom. He had stood at this very desk while a state trooper told him his brother's car had gone off Route 9, and he had said *thank you for letting me know, sir, will there be anything else* because that was what the moment required. And now, the night before the most important evening of his life, his hands trembled like a first-week bellhop's.

*Because tomorrow you will be seen,* he told himself. *Not noticed. Seen.*

There was a difference, and the difference had kept him alive.

He walked the room. That was the rehearsal—his rehearsal, the one nobody had scheduled. He moved from the head table to the service corridor and back, counting his own footsteps, memorizing the give of the floorboard near the second pillar that would announce him to anyone who knew the building. Vivian knew the building. He had to assume she knew it the way he did, which meant he had to assume every creak in this room was a witness against him.

The wine service was staged on the long sideboard under the portraits of the founders: Edmund Hale at forty, painted with the flat confidence of a man who had never imagined being a lie. Six crystal decanters, each labeled in Vivian's own hand on cream card stock—*To the Founder, 1984–2024*—because tomorrow's toast would be poured from decanters, not bottles, so that no one could trace a single poisoned vintage to a single poisoned bottle. He had to give her this: she filed her crimes beautifully.

Danny appeared at the service door at 11:40, exactly as arranged, carrying a linen tray with the unhurried gait of a boy delivering nothing more dangerous than water glasses. He was nineteen, had worked the desk for eight months, and had the single most valuable quality Oscar had ever identified in a colleague: he was so thoroughly unremarkable that security cameras seemed to look through him. Tonight that quality was worth more than the whole security budget.

"West corridor," Danny murmured, not looking at him. "Cart's by the ice machine. Nobody's on that hall after midnight except the night audit, and she's got headphones in."

"Decanters?"

"Swapped at eleven. The real ones are in the dry storage cage, wrapped, labeled as returns." Danny's jaw was tight. "Mr. Marchetti—the ones on the sideboard now. Those are the ones she'll pour from?"

Oscar looked at the six decanters gleaming under the portrait of Edmund Hale. Tomorrow night, Vivian Cross would lift the founder's decanter with four hundred witnesses, pour her father's celebratory wine with hands that had never once shaken, and drink from his glass first—the devoted daughter, the theatrical flourish, the gesture the society pages would quote. She would drink first because she believed the glass was her father's death and not her own. She believed that because Oscar had let her believe it, because for three weeks he had fed her a performance of a frightened, suspended concierge who had surrendered his notebooks and wanted only his job back.

What she did not know was that the decanter she would pour from had been filled this afternoon with a diluted solution—enough of the compound Danny's pharmacist contact identified to alarm a toxicology screen and stop a toast cold, not enough to stop a heart. And what she did not know, because she had never in her life needed to check the work of invisible staff, was that Oscar had also switched the cream card labels. The decanter she thought held her father's toast wine held the decoy. The one she'd dismissed as the spare held the truth.

"Those are the ones," Oscar said.

Danny nodded once, swallowed, and said, "And if she doesn't drink first? If she just pours for him?"

"Then we've lost nothing but our nerve, and I never had any to begin with." He almost smiled. "Go home, Danny. Sleep. Tomorrow you're just a boy carrying water."

"Yes, sir." A pause at the door. "Mr. Marchetti? The ledger. The one in the archive room. If she finds it—if she takes the bait and comes for you instead—"

"Then she comes for me instead of the wine," Oscar said, "and that is a trade I will make every hour of every day for the rest of my life. Go."

The boy went. Oscar stood alone in the amber dark among the stacked chairs, and his heart was going like a drumline, and his hands had gone from trembling to something worse—steady now, cold steady, the way they went steady before a storm broke a window.

He had one more thing to plant.

* * *

The archive room was on the second floor, behind the unmarked door that generations of managers had pretended not to see, because the archive room was where the hotel kept the parts of itself it didn't want on display. Oscar had kept it for thirty-one years the way a priest keeps a confessional—dusted, ordered, alphabetized, and absolutely private.

Tonight he unlocked it with the key he was technically no longer authorized to carry, since the board's suspension, since Raman's arrest, since the charges had evaporated like morning fog and left him standing in the wreckage of his own reputation. The town looked at him sideways now. The board had taken his uniform and given him a title that meant *nothing*: *consultant, emeritus.* He was, officially, a man with no reason to be in this building after midnight.

He turned on the desk lamp—the green-shaded banker's lamp, older than he was—and set the decoy ledger on the center of the desk, square to the edges, because Vivian Cross would notice a crooked ledger the way he would notice a crooked painting.

He had made it himself over four nights. Real leather, aged with tea and a radiator. Real pages, real entries in his real hand—because his hand was the one thing he could not fake and did not need to. The entries were a masterpiece of the true and the lethal: accurate dates, accurate initials, accurate observations of Vivian Cross's movements across twenty years, each one cross-referenced in his private code, each one placed like a stone in a path leading exactly where he wanted her to walk. And on the cover, in gold leaf he had applied with a jeweler's brush, the label that would make her blood run cold:

*October 2004 — FINAL.*

Not a working ledger. A finished one. A testament. A book that said to anyone who read it: *I knew everything, I wrote it all down, and I sealed it.*

It was a lie in exactly one respect: the real ledger—the one with Tom Beckett's letter folded inside the back cover, the one with Ada Finch's name and the ferry manifest and the floor plan of the east wing sketched in his own code—was at that moment in a safe-deposit box at the First Coastal Bank, under a name that was not his, with instructions Raman knew and no one else did. If Oscar Marchetti died tomorrow, the book would speak. If he lived, the book would wait.

But Vivian didn't know about the deposit box. Vivian knew one thing with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life buying silence: that Oscar Marchetti filed everything, and that a man who files everything can be found through his files. She had already come for his notebooks once—had stood in this room, he was sure, had run her fingers along the spines looking for the one that mattered. He had let her find nothing. Tonight he let her find something.

He placed the ledger square on the desk. Then, with a cotton cloth, he wiped the room—not clean, never clean, but *touched*. A faint smudge of his presence on the desk edge. The lamp warm. A coffee ring he made himself with the dregs of the staff-room pot, aged twenty minutes to look like a week. The archive room would say, tomorrow, to anyone who looked: *he was here recently, and he left something behind.*

And Vivian Cross, who audited everything, who had personally searched this room eleven days ago and found it empty of the notebook that should have been there—Vivian would look at that ledger the way a hunter looks at fresh tracks. She could not leave it. A book labeled *FINAL*, in the hand of the man who had watched her whole life, sitting one day before the dinner at which she intended to close the account forever? She would come for it. And when she came for it, she would come at night, alone, because this was the one errand she could never delegate.

And the archive room, like every historic room in this hotel, was wired.

Oscar had spent two evenings in the basement with Danny, restoring the servant-bell intercom system to something better than it had been in 1952. The old copper lines ran through the walls like veins, and the central panel—the great brass-and-bakelite listening board that had once let a housekeeper summon a bellhop from four floors away—now fed a small digital recorder Danny had bought with cash and knew how to work. Room 312, where the dying bellhop had whispered his interrupted truth. The east wing. The archive room. And tomorrow, patched and live for the first time in seventy years: the ballroom.

He had already heard one confession through these walls. Vivian Cross, rehearsing a eulogy in an empty ballroom at two in the morning, her voice low and liturgical: *My father built this house on a foundation of names, and a name is worth more than the people carrying it.* He had stood in the basement in the dark with his hand flat on the panel, feeling the vibration of her voice in the brass, and he had understood then that she had been rehearsing her whole life. Tomorrow she would deliver the final draft.

He switched off the banker's lamp. In the sudden dark, the gold lettering on the decoy ledger seemed to hold the light a moment longer, the way a glass holds the last of the wine.

* * *

He did not go straight to his room. Habit—the good kind, the kind that had kept him alive—said: never leave a room the way you entered, never let your pattern be a pattern. So he took the service stairs down to the mezzanine, meaning to cross through the gallery and circle back, and that was how he came to be standing in the dark of the portrait gallery when he heard footsteps on the marble below.

He stopped. He did not breathe. He stepped back behind the case of Wedgwood that had stood in this exact spot since 1987, and he watched through the glass at the reflection in the case's far pane—a trick as old as the building, a concierge's trick, watching the world doubled in silvered glass.

Vivian Cross crossed the lobby below him at ten minutes past midnight, dressed not in her evening armor but in a gray cashmere wrap, her silver hair down, and she moved the way she always moved—like a woman who owned the floor and everything standing on it. But she did not go toward the stairs. She went to the front desk. His desk. She stood behind it for a long moment, in the dark, in the place where he had stood for thirty-one years, and she put her hand flat on the guest book—the current one, the open one—and left it there, fingers spread, as though taking its pulse. As though measuring what he had left behind in it.

Then she spoke, quietly, to the empty lobby, in the pleasant carrying voice she used for toasts.

"I know you're in the building, Oscar."

His heart did not stop. He was almost proud of that.

"You always are," she said. "That's the thing about you. Twenty years, and I never once caught you *not* watching." She turned her head slightly, and in the reflection he watched her smile at the dark lobby, gracious as a hostess. "Tomorrow night, drink the wine. Enjoy the dinner. You've earned a seat at the table. And afterward—well. We'll settle accounts. We always do, in this family."

She straightened the guest book so its edge was square to the desk, a gesture so precisely his own that for one vertiginous second Oscar felt he was looking into a mirror, and then she walked to the east corridor, unhurried, and was gone.

Oscar stayed behind the Wedgwood for a long time. Long enough for the grandfather clock to strike the quarter hour. His hands, he observed distantly, had gone perfectly still at last—still as the hands in the founder's portrait, still as a man who has stopped pretending he is invisible and has begun, instead, to aim.

She knew he was in the building. Of course she did. She had probably known since the ramp went in. It changed nothing—no, he corrected himself, walking the long way back, it changed one thing, and the one thing mattered: tomorrow she would be watching him as closely as he watched her. Two observers at the same table, each convinced the other was the prey.

The wine was swapped. The ledger was baited. The walls were listening.

Oscar reached his room, locked the door, and sat on the edge of the narrow bed in his street clothes, and only then did he let his hands shake—let them, deliberately, the way you open a fist—and he thought of Edmund Hale in his wheelchair, weeping in a rain-soaked courtyard twenty years ago, and of Tom Beckett in the greenhouse, and of Ada Finch's teacup still warm on its saucer, and he said aloud, to the dark, to the walls, to the whole patient listening house:

"Tomorrow, everyone sees."
