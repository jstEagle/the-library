## 3. Arrival at Ashcombe

The bus from Exeter let me off at a crossroads with no name on it, only a signpost whose arms had rusted to the colour of dried blood, pointing in four directions toward places that no longer existed or had never mattered. Ashcombe was down the lane to the left, according to the driver, who told me this without looking at me, which I noted and filed away as either indifference or courtesy. In my experience the two are often indistinguishable in rural England.

I had chosen the bus deliberately. Trains keep records; buses barely keep timetables. If anyone in Ashcombe were going to learn how I'd arrived, they would have to learn it from watching, and watching was what I had come to do.

The rain had been falling since Taunton, a fine grey insistence that turned the hedgerows into walls of dark green glass. By the time I walked the last mile into the village, my coat was soaked through at the shoulders and my shoes had taken on water with the resigned patience of old leather. None of it bothered me. Discomfort is information too — it tells you where your body ends and the world begins, which is a boundary worth knowing when you're about to spend weeks pretending to be someone else.

Ashcombe announced itself first by smell: woodsmoke and wet stone and, beneath both, something older and sweeter — leaf mould, perhaps, or the ghost of the incense that had soaked into the church over two centuries of Sundays. Then came the sound: water running somewhere out of sight, in a culvert or a gutter, the village's own private pulse. And then, all at once, the place itself, arranged around its church like a congregation around a coffin.

I stopped where the lane widened into something generously called a square and let my memory do what it always does, which is everything.

The church of St. Brannoc sat on a slight rise at the far end, its tower rebuilt after the fire in a paler stone that hadn't yet learned to lie convincingly about its age. The nave roof was new slate over old beams. A yew tree stood in the churchyard, vast and black in the rain, older than every grave beneath it and most of the families buried there. Around the square: a shop with a post office counter visible through fogged glass, a chapel converted to an architect's office, a row of cottages with whitewash peeling in long vertical strips like old wallpaper, and the inn — the Drowned Bell, said the hanging sign, though the bell painted on it looked more asleep than drowned.

Thirty-one buildings visible from where I stood. I counted them without deciding to, the way other people breathe. Fourteen with lights on. Three with cars under carports. One — the shop — with a man standing in the doorway beneath the awning, looking at me.

He wasn't pretending not to look. That was the first thing wrong with Ashcombe. In London, people watch you constantly and pretend they aren't; here, the man watched me openly for perhaps four seconds, then went back inside, and I understood that he had not been curious. He had been confirming.

Confirming what? That I existed. That I had come. That the thing some of them were waiting for had finally arrived, wearing a wet coat and carrying a leather satchel.

I crossed the square. My shoes left prints in the shallow standing water, each one filling behind me as I lifted my foot, so that my passage through the village erased itself step by step. I remember thinking — I still think — that it would have been nice if the whole visit could work that way. It could not. Nothing in Ashcombe would erase itself. That was rather the point of me being there.

* * *

The Drowned Bell's interior smelled of beeswax, beer, and decades of coal smoke absorbed into plaster. Low beams, brasses polished to a dull shine, a fire laid but unlit in the grate. Behind the bar stood a woman in her sixties drying a glass that was already dry.

"Maren Trelawney," she said, before I'd spoken. "You'll be wanting a room."

It was not a question, and something in its phrasing snagged against my attention like a burr in wool. *You'll be wanting.* Not *can I help you*. She had looked at me and seen a guest, the way a chess player looks at a board and sees a move.

"Ivo Galloway," I said, and gave her the name evenly, because a false name is a liability and my real one was common enough to be anonymous. "Archivist. I'm working on a survey of parish records — Devon historic documents project. Your vicarage archive, I understand, has material going back to the 1700s."

"Does it." She set down the glass. Her hands were large, capable, reddened at the knuckles — a working woman's hands, but steady, and she used them the way some people use their eyes, placing each object exactly where she meant it to be. "And the diocese sent you all this way in November."

"The diocese sends me wherever the paper is."

She studied me then, and I studied her back, and for a moment we were two people doing precisely the same job on one another, which is a rarer experience than you might think. Maren Trelawney was tall, broad through the shoulders, with iron-grey hair cut short and practical and eyes the pale washed-out blue of sea glass. There was a scar bisecting her left eyebrow, old and white. Her face did the thing that faces in small villages do: it gave away nothing while appearing to give away everything. Openness as architecture. A façade built to be read.

"Room's forty a night," she said. "Breakfast included. Supper if you want it, seven o'clock, don't be late, I cook once." She took a ledger from beneath the bar — an actual ledger, cloth-bound, its spine cracked — and opened it to a fresh page. "How long?"

"A fortnight. Perhaps three."

The pen paused above the paper. Half a second, no more. But I have spent my life measuring half-seconds; they are where people hide things. A fortnight is a holiday. Three weeks is an occupation. Somewhere behind those sea-glass eyes, a calculation completed itself, and whatever answer it reached, she wrote my name in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if the words were trying to get away from her.

"Three weeks," she repeated. "Long time, in November."

"It's a lot of paper."

"Is it." Again the flat echo, the statement dressed as agreement. She handed me a heavy iron key with a wooden fob worn smooth as river stone. "Room four, top of the stairs. Water's hot till ten. And Mr. Galloway—" she said my name carefully, tasting it, the way you test a coin between your teeth— "if you're going up to the church, mind the floor by the vestry. They never did get the boards right after the fire. You'll go through your ankle otherwise."

I thanked her. I climbed the stairs. And it was only halfway up, with her eyes between my shoulder blades like a hand pressed flat against my back, that I understood what she had done.

I had not mentioned the fire. I had not mentioned the vestry. I had said *parish records*, and she had said *after the fire* — casually, helpfully, the way any innkeeper might speak of any local history. Except that in a village where thirty years ago a beloved vicar had burned to death in his own church, the fire is not local history. It is the wound. People circle it. They speak around it the way tongues find the gap where a tooth was pulled. Nobody offers it up to a stranger inside ninety seconds unless offering it is the point.

Maren Trelawney had put the fire into the conversation deliberately, the way you drop a stone into still water to see what rises.

I unlocked room four and stood dripping just inside the door, doing what I do, which is remembering. Her exact words, replayed: *mind the floor by the vestry*. Her tone: helpful, unhurried. Her eyes during it: on mine, not on the key, not on the ledger. Watching for the flinch.

There had been no flinch. I am very good at that. But she had been watching for one, which meant she believed there might be one to see, which meant she had already decided I was not what I claimed, and was now simply gathering evidence of what I actually was.

I hung my coat on the rail by the narrow window and looked out at the square going blue with dusk. Rain still fell, silvering under the one streetlamp as it flickered on. Across the way, the shopkeeper was pulling in his awning. Further down, a light burned in an upper window of a substantial stone house set back behind gates — the Verney house, I knew from the file, though from here it was only a light like any other light, except that I found myself unable to look away from it, the way you cannot look away from a dog that is watching you.

* * *

I came down at seven for supper because declining would have been more conspicuous than attending, and found the dining room empty except for Maren, who served me lamb stew and bread without ceremony and then, instead of retreating to the bar, sat down across the table from me with a cup of tea.

Village innkeepers do not dine with guests. I registered the deviation and waited.

"Parish records," she said.

"That's right."

"We've got a chest of them up at the vicarage, I suppose. Old Tregose kept everything. Bills and baptisms and whatnot." She blew on her tea. "Course, there's a gap. Ninety-four onward, near enough five years. Boxes got water-damaged when the roof went, after." She said *the roof*, not *the fire*. Interesting. She had given me the fire freely at the door and withheld it now, and I wondered whether the first gift had been bait and this withholding was the hook, or whether she simply changed weather with her moods, as coastal people do.

"Water damage is common," I said. "Paper survives worse than people expect, if it's dried properly."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then it becomes pulp and legend."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, maybe, of a shot returned across the table. She drank her tea and looked at me over the rim, and the lamplight made her sea-glass eyes almost colourless.

"You'll want to talk to people," she said. "For your survey. Folk who know the parish."

"Inevitably."

"Edmund Verney's your man for that. Churchwarden thirty years. He knows every stone in that church and most of the bodies under it." She set down the cup with a small precise click. "He'll want to meet you. He meets everyone. It's how he keeps the parish tidy."

*Tidy.* The word sat in the air between us with its edges showing.

"And does he?" I asked. "Keep it tidy?"

"He keeps it *his*," Maren said, and rose, and collected my plate, and that was all — except that as she turned toward the kitchen she paused, half-turned back, and said, without looking at me:

"Your mother's name was Sable, wasn't it. Galloway. She lodged here, winter of '93 into '94. Housekeeping for Vicar Tregose." Now she did look at me, full-on, letting me see that she knew exactly what she had just done. "I remembered the surname off your signature in the book. Faces change. Names don't."

The fire ticked in the grate, unlit logs settling. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice and went quiet.

"My mother," I said, "was a housekeeper in Somerset. She died in 2011."

"Yes," Maren said. "I heard that too." And she carried my plate out, leaving me alone in the lamplight with a cooling teapot and the sound of rain beginning again against the windows, harder now, as though the sky had only been waiting for the conversation to end.

I sat for a long time. This is what I will admit, here at the start of things, when it is easiest to confess: I sat for a long time and felt nothing, and distrusted the nothing, because Ivo Galloway feels nothing precisely when feeling would cost him most, and the absence of grief where grief should be is not peace. It is a locked room. And I have made my living opening other people's locked rooms long enough to know that the lock on your own is always the last one to yield.

My mother had stood in this room. Or one like it — the dining room may have been rearranged, the tables moved, the wallpaper changed. But she had breathed this air, eaten at some table in this house, walked this square in rain not unlike this rain, on her way to a church that would soon be burning, carrying secrets that would buy her silence and my childhood and, eventually, her escape.

I had come to Ashcombe believing I was investigating a fire.

Sitting in that yellow lamplight with the rain closing the village off from the world like a held breath, I understood for the first time that I had been expected — not merely awaited but *anticipated*, prepared for, a seat kept warm at a table I couldn't see. Maren had known my name before I signed it. Edmund Verney would know my purpose before I stated it. Thirty years ago someone had bought my mother's silence, and tonight, in a dining room smelling of lamb and beeswax, I had been shown the receipt.

Upstairs, through the floorboards, I could hear Maren moving below the bar, and then the distinct, deliberate sound of the front door being bolted — not for the night, I thought. Just for now. Just until she was sure where I was.

I wrote nothing down. I never write anything down. I lay on the bed in my clothes and closed my eyes and let the day file itself: the rusted signpost, the fourteen lit windows, the shopkeeper's four seconds, the dry glass, the half-second pause of the pen, *mind the floor by the vestry*, *he keeps it his*, *names don't*.

And underneath it all, faint as the culvert running beneath the square, one question that had not been there when the bus let me off at the crossroads:

If everyone in Ashcombe had known I was coming —

who had sent the invitation?
