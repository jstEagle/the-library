## 8. The Second Letter's Sender

The letter had been in my pocket for three days before I let myself look at it properly.

Not read—read it I had, a dozen times, the way you might reread a ransom note. *Looked at* was different. Looked at meant paper, ink, pressure, provenance. In my trade we called it forensic reading, and it was the one discipline that never failed me, because it asked nothing of feeling. A document is a confession of its own making. Every sheet carries its biography: where it was milled, how it was stored, what hands warmed it.

I sat at the window table of my room at the inn with the second letter flattened between two saucers, the rain doing its patient work against the glass. Ashcombe rain was not London rain. London rain fell; Ashcombe rain *insisted*, as though the sky had a grievance with the village specifically and intended to pursue it through all eternity.

The paper itself first. Cream-laid stock, 90 grams if it was anything, with a faint vertical chain pattern visible when I tilted it toward the lamp. Not stationery-shop paper. Not the kind sold in packs of fifty to people writing complaints about their bins. This was the heavy, old-fashioned laid stock that cost real money per sheet and came in boxes of twenty-five, the kind bought by solicitors and vicars and people who wanted their correspondence to feel like an inheritance.

One watermark, lower right quadrant, when I held it over the bulb: a sheaf of barley above the letters **H & S**.

I wrote it down without needing to. The memory kept it whether I asked or not—that was the bargain I'd lived under since childhood, the photograph behind my eyes that developed instantly and never faded. But writing things down made them real in a way the mind's archive did not. Writing things down gave them edges.

H & S. Hodge & Sons? Harrow & Stead? I turned the name over while the kettle muttered on the dresser. Then I stopped turning it, because there was no need to speculate. There was a shop in this village that sold paper. There was always a shop in villages like this one—the kind of establishment that had sold candles in 1890 and sold phone chargers now, and had changed nothing essential in between.

I had walked past it twice already. Prewitt's, the sign said, though the paint had surrendered most of the *w*. General stores, post office counter, newspapers, and—I'd noted it absently on my first morning, the way I noted everything, filing it unasked—a small stationery section near the back, between the birthday cards and the birdseed.

* * *

Prewitt's smelled of paraffin and boiled sweets and the particular dust of a building that had decided against renovation sometime around the Coronation. The bell over the door announced me with a cheerful indifference I found almost touching.

Behind the counter stood a man built like a doorstop, sixty-odd, with a face so uniformly pink and placid that he seemed designed to put customers at ease and witnesses off their guard simultaneously. Cyril Prewitt, presumably. Maren had mentioned him once, in her flat innkeeper's way: *Cyril sees everything and says nothing, which in Ashcombe is the highest form of wisdom.*

"Morning," he said. "Bit wild out."

"It is." I moved down the aisle with the unhurried gait of a man killing time, letting my eyes do their work. Newspapers. Bread. A shelf of jams with hand-lettered labels. And there, third shelf up, past the cards: two boxes of cream-laid writing paper, £4.50 each, and beside them a single box marked down to £2 because the cellophane had torn.

I picked up the marked-down box. Turned it over.

**Hartley & Sloane. Fine Laid Papers. Est. 1847.**

The barley sheaf sat in the corner of the label like a family crest.

"This the only laid stock you carry?" I asked, holding it up.

Cyril came around the counter at the pace of a man who had never in his life been hurried by anything. "That's it. Nobody buys it, mind. Everyone's on email now, aren't they." He took the box, weighed it in his palm as though checking for contraband. "You after some?"

"I'm after information, actually." I set two pound coins on the counter—not payment, exactly. A framing device. "I'm cataloguing correspondence from the parish for the county archive. Nineties material mostly. I need to know who bought this paper, because I've got letters on it and I can't date them properly otherwise."

It was a good lie. It had the two qualities every effective lie requires: plausibility and tedium. No one volunteers enthusiasm for correspondence dating.

Cyril's pink face did something complicated. Not much. A tightening around the eyes, a fractional stillness, gone in half a second—but I caught it, because catching such things was the whole of my profession and most of my nature.

"Well," he said. "Wouldn't be able to tell you who buys what. Data protection, see."

"Of course." I smiled. "I don't need names. Just—how many boxes of this have you sold in the last month?"

He considered the ceiling. "Two," he said finally. "Sold one to Mrs. Tregenna for her Christmas round-robin—no, that were December. This year, then. Two boxes this year." He counted on fingers that were thick and clean and slightly tremulous, I noticed. Tremulous with age or with something else. "One went to the Rectory—well, you'd call it the Old Rectory now, Miss Hartnell has it. And one to—" He stopped.

The stop was the loudest thing in the shop.

"To?" I prompted gently.

"To Mr. Verney's man," he said. "Tobias Verney sent the lad down for it. Paper and envelopes." He shrugged, a shrug that wanted very much to be casual and didn't quite arrive. "Gentry and their letters, eh?"

Gentry and their letters. I thanked him, bought the damaged box anyway—it would be poor tradecraft not to—and walked out into the rain with my pulse conducting a quiet, disciplined acceleration.

Tobias Verney. The dissolute son. The man whose alibi for the night of the fire had been delivered to me with the smoothness of a story told so many times it had worn grooves, like a stair tread. The man who signed insurance claims unread, if his father's household ran the way I suspected it ran.

But wait. Wait. My mind, that cursed meticulous archive, laid out the objection before I'd crossed the street: Tobias drank. Tobias was careless. Tobias had signed things unread before. If Tobias had written me a threat, it would smell of whisky and self-pity. This letter had neither. Its menace was dry, economical, *patient*. *If you keep digging, there will be more.* More what, left deliciously unstated. That was not a drunkard's syntax. That was a man who chose each word the way a surgeon chooses instruments.

So either Tobias had written it and someone else had dictated it—

—or the purchase was cover. Send the feckless son's errand-boy for the paper, and the trail dies at a name everyone will happily believe capable of anything.

Three people, then. Three people with access, motive, and the particular composure the letter required.

Edmund Verney, who knew why I'd come before I'd told him, whose handshake had been calibrated to the half-degree.

Maren, the innkeeper, who watched too carefully and told too little, and who had been the first person in Ashcombe to say my mother's name aloud.

And Cyril Prewitt himself—who saw everything and said nothing, and who had just said a great deal more than nothing, which either made him a fool or made him very clever indeed.

I turned the damaged box over in my hands as I walked. Inside, through the torn cellophane, I could see the sheets. Cream-laid. Barley watermark. Ninety grams of quiet menace, waiting to be written on.

* * *

I spent the afternoon being seen.

This is a technique older than policing: make yourself legible, predictable, harmless. I walked the churchyard with my notebook, copying dates off gravestones with visible diligence. I bought a pint at the pub and listened to two farmers argue about drainage with the passion other men reserve for war. I let Maren refill my tea twice and asked her, idly, whether the quarry road was walkable in this weather.

She paused with the teapot suspended. One full second. Maybe a little more.

"The quarry road," she repeated.

"For the archive. There's a boundary stone up there I'm meant to record. Eighteen-tens, I think."

"Mm." She poured. The brown stream was perfectly steady. "Take the high path. The low one floods." She set the pot down and looked at me, and her eyes were the grey of the sea outside the window, and they gave away precisely nothing except the fact that they were giving away nothing. "Mind how you go, Mr. Galloway."

Mr. Galloway. Not the false name I'd given at booking. She had known who I was since the moment I walked in—or longer—and had chosen, until now, to let the fiction stand. Hearing my true name in that low Devon voice was like stepping on a stair that wasn't there.

"Thank you, Mrs.—"

"Maren," she said. "Just Maren." And she turned back to her glasses, drying a tumbler that was already dry.

I walked the high path to the quarry in the failing light, found my boundary stone, recorded it faithfully, and all the while felt the skin between my shoulder blades registering what my eyes refused to confirm: that I was being watched, or rather, that watching was happening somewhere in my vicinity, patient and unhurried, the way weather happens.

Good, I thought, walking back down in the dark. Let them watch. Let them see a dull archivist trudging home with wet boots and a notebook full of gravestone rubbings. The best place to hide a hunter is inside a man nobody fears.

I wish I could say the thought comforted me. What it actually did was remind me that hunters are also, always, prey to something.

* * *

I knew the cottage had been entered before I touched the door.

This is not mysticism; it is inventory. My room at the inn had been exchanged two days ago for a rented cottage on the lane behind the church—Maren's suggestion, offered with that same flat neutrality: *You'll want privacy for your papers, I expect.* The cottage belonged to a farmer named Dolling who wintered in Torquay. I had a system for my things. I always have a system. The towel on the rail hung with its fold facing left; I had folded it facing left, deliberately, the way I do everything, and the fold now faced right.

Someone had refolded my towel. Someone tidy. Someone careful.

I stood in the doorway a long moment, rain dripping off my coat onto the flagstones, and let the cottage tell me what had happened to it. The air was wrong—still warm, faintly, with another body's presence, though hours stale. A cup on the drainer sat a centimetre further from the tap than I'd left it. My suitcase lay at the angle I'd left it, but the zip pull pointed at four o'clock instead of seven. On the desk, my papers looked untouched, which is the most damning condition papers can present, because untouched papers mean someone lifted the top sheet, photographed or read what lay beneath, and replaced it with a forger's devotion.

I did not turn on the main light. I moved through the rooms the way water moves, silent, filling the low places, and my mind performed its terrible faithful work: reconstructing the search from its residue the way a palaeontologist builds an animal from a footprint. They had started with the suitcase—amateurs start with suitcases—found nothing but clothing and the insurance file's public summary, then worked outward. Desk. Coat pockets. The lining of my spare jacket, which they had unpicked and restitched with a competence that chilled me more than violence would have. The Bible in the bedside drawer had been opened at the Psalms and closed again. Under the mattress: nothing taken, because there was nothing there, because I am not an amateur.

What they wanted was obvious. The ledger. Or whatever they believed I carried that was worse than the ledger.

What frightened me was not that they had come. What frightened me was the *care*. This was not panic. Panic kicks doors and scatters drawers. This was a housekeeper's thoroughness, a surgeon's economy—and underneath both, unmistakably, a kind of respect. They had searched my things the way you search the belongings of someone you regard as dangerous.

I sat down on the bed that was not mine, in the dark, and made myself useful to my own fear by dissecting it.

Three names. I had narrowed the field to three, and now the field narrowed me. Edmund Verney had the power and the motive and the half-second-too-warm smile. Maren had the access—she'd steered me into this cottage as neatly as a shepherd steers a flock through a gate—and the watching eyes and the withheld name. And Cyril Prewitt had the tremor in his hands and the information he should never have given and the pink, placid face of a man who has spent thirty years seeing everything and saying nothing, and who perhaps had finally begun, in his own oblique way, to say something.

Or the three of them were one thing wearing three faces. Villages are like that. Villages keep their secrets the way a body keeps a splinter: wrapped around it, growing over it, aching only when pressed.

Outside, the rain went on insisting. Somewhere down the lane a dog began to bark—not alarmed, just conversational, telling the night about a shape that had passed it going the other way. I listened to that bark with great attention. A shape passing the other way, after ten o'clock, on the night my cottage was searched.

I did not sleep. I sat upright in the wing chair by the cold fireplace with the lights off and the damaged box of Hartley & Sloane paper on my knee, and I watched the door, and somewhere around three in the morning I understood, with the clarity that comes only to the thoroughly exhausted, that I had made a category error. I had come to Ashcombe thinking myself the investigator. But the second letter had never been a threat aimed at keeping me away.

*There will be more.*

It had been an invitation. Someone wanted me here—wanted me digging, wanted the village pressed and probed and made to sweat. The search hadn't been the work of my enemy.

It had been the work of someone afraid of what I'd find. And fear, in my experience, lives next door to guilt far more often than it lives next door to malice.

At dawn the rain thinned to a silver drifting mist, and I saw, from the bedroom window, a figure cross the churchyard below—small, quick, hooded, carrying something flat and rectangular against their chest the way you carry a thing you cannot afford to drop.

They did not look up. But they walked, I noticed, with a limp I recognised.

I had seen that limp yesterday, in Prewitt's, attached to a pair of thick, clean, trembling hands.

I dressed quickly, and went out into the wet grey morning to follow the only man in Ashcombe who had ever told me the truth by accident.
