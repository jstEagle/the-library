## 3. The Locked Crypt

The petition form was a single sheet of cream paper, and Rafael Hartwell had been staring at it for twenty minutes.

It asked for very little. *Name of applicant. Purpose of access. Duration. Signature of sponsoring officer.* She had filled in the first three lines in her small, upright hand — the same hand that had catalogued eleven thousand folios in the cathedral archive without a single cross-reference error — and then she had stopped at the fourth, because the fourth required a reason, and Rafael had not yet decided how much truth the paper could hold.

*Research into historical burial practices* was accurate. It was also a lie by omission, and she disliked lying by omission the way some people disliked touching wet wool. She crossed it out. Wrote it again. Crossed it out.

Outside the archive window, Bellwether Lane ran down toward the harbour in its usual morning shuffle: fishmongers hosing their slabs, the flower arranger — Mrs. Ives, arms full of chrysanthemums — letting herself into the north transept, and Callum Reeve, the verger, sweeping the porch steps with the unhurried strokes of a man who had been sweeping those steps for thirty years and expected to sweep them for thirty more. He looked up as he worked, and when he saw her at the window he raised a hand. She raised one back.

He had been unfailingly helpful since Odile Vasseur had walked into the archive with her envelope of cash and her impossible request. He had unlocked the vestry side rooms without being asked. He had brought her tea on a tray, unasked, twice. He had told her, cheerfully, that the crypt was locked — *has been since the restoration, love, before my time even* — and that if she wanted to get down there she'd need to go through the Dean, and that the Dean was a reasonable man, *once you got past the first no*.

Rafael had spent eleven days getting past the first no. She had written letters. She had made calls to the diocesan office, forty miles inland, and been passed between three secretaries whose voices all had the same trained gentleness of people paid to absorb frustration. She had learned that the crypt key was not, in fact, held by the diocese at large but by the Dean personally, in a cabinet in his study, and that access required his signature on the very form now sitting half-blank beneath her fountain pen.

She wrote, finally: *Investigation of burial records relevant to a missing-persons inquiry.* It had the virtue of being true. It lacked the virtue of being wise, because the words *missing-persons inquiry* would travel from the Dean's desk to the parish council to the harbourmaster to Mrs. Ives's flower-arranging circle by nightfall, and Odile Vasseur had paid Rafael specifically to be quiet.

She looked at the words a long moment. Then she left them. If the truth was going to cost her, she preferred to know the price in advance.

* * *

Dean Aldous Pryce received her in his study at two o'clock, in a room that smelled of pipe smoke and old varnish, though she noticed no pipe anywhere in it — only a brass stand, cold and ornamental, like a relic of a man he had decided to stop being.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined a prelate from central casting: vast, jolly, dismissive. Pryce was none of those things. He was slight and silver-haired, with reading glasses pushed up into his hair and a face that might once have been handsome in a scholarly way and was now simply tired in a way that seemed structural, load-bearing, as though the tiredness were holding something up.

"Miss Hartwell." He gestured her into the chair across from his desk. On the desk lay her petition form, and beside it a folder she suspected was hers in every sense that mattered — her archive employment record, her references, the letter she had written when she took the post four years ago promising, among other things, *discretion in all matters touching the parish*. "You've been busy."

"I've been doing my job."

"Your job," Pryce said, "is the archive. The registers, the folios, the — " he made a small circling gesture — "the paper. It's valuable work. It's quiet work. I chose you for it because your predecessor talked too much and you, I was assured, did not."

"And yet here I am, being talked at."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgement that she'd landed a point, filed away for later consideration. "Tell me about the Vasseur woman."

Rafael kept her face still. It was a skill she had practiced in interrogation rooms and probate hearings and one memorable afternoon in a solicitor's office in Portsmouth, and it held. "There is no Vasseur woman in my petition."

"There's a missing-persons inquiry in your petition, which is the same thing wearing a different coat. Callum mentioned a visitor. A woman asking about her brother. He has an eye for that sort of thing." Pryce removed his glasses, cleaned them on his sleeve, and did not put them back on. "So. Tell me."

Rafael weighed it. She could deny, and he would know she was lying, and the interview would curdle into something adversarial that she could not afford. Or she could give him a version — thin, partial, technically honest — and watch what he did with it.

"A woman came to me," she said. "Eleven years ago her brother disappeared. The family believes — believed — he may have died in this parish. She wants to know where he is. Or was. She wants an ending."

"And you took the case." He said it without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "An archivist. Taking cases."

"I'm an investigator who keeps an archive. It's on the sign."

"It is on the sign," Pryce conceded. "Very well. You want to see the crypt. I'm going to tell you no, and I'd like you to hear the whole of the reason before you start composing your rebuttal, because I know your type, Miss Hartwell, and you are already composing it."

Rafael, who was indeed already composing it, folded her hands and waited.

"The crypt was sealed in 1974," Pryce said. "Not out of superstition, whatever the parish gossips will tell you, and not because of anything dramatic. The restoration engineers found the vaults damp. Rising water, salt rot in the brick, structural movement where the old sea wall meets the foundations. The diocese of the day made a decision — a sensible one — to close the space, consolidate what remained down there, and leave it be. The key was given to the Dean's office and it has stayed there through five deans. Including me. I have never been down. I have never wanted to go down." He leaned back. "There is nothing down there that concerns the living. That is not a riddle. It is an answer."

"It's a very tidy answer."

"I'm a tidy man."

"Then you'll forgive a tidy question." Rafael took the folded photocopy from her bag — she had brought it on instinct, and instinct in her experience was just research that hadn't finished explaining itself — and laid it on his desk. "The burial registers list interments in the crypt up to 1974. After that, nothing. But the parish almshouse records — which are, incidentally, in disgraceful condition and should be a matter of some embarrassment to the diocese — reference *committal to the vault* for a handful of deaths in the years after the sealing. Seventeen, by my count, across four decades. Someone was still burying people down there, Dean. Someone had to open the door."

Pryce looked at the photocopy for a long time. When he looked up, something had changed in his face — not guilt, exactly. Rafael had seen guilt often enough to know its weather. This was closer to recognition. The look of a man who has heard a sound in the night and is deciding, deliberately, to have heard nothing.

"Those records are in error," he said.

"All seventeen of them?"

"Parish records are full of errors. Copying errors, transcription errors, clerks who wrote what they assumed rather than what they knew. You of all people know that. You've built a career on correcting it." He slid the photocopy back across the desk with two fingers, as though returning something slightly unclean. "I'm sorry about the Vasseur business. Genuinely. But if her brother died here, he'll be in a register, or a coroner's file, or the sea. Not in a crypt that has been locked for fifty years. I won't have the vault opened on the strength of a clerical anomaly and a private detective's suspicion."

"Archivist," Rafael said.

"Excuse me?"

"You called me a private detective. I'm an archivist who investigates." She stood, and was pleased to find her legs steady, because her pride had taken a hit somewhere in the last ninety seconds and she wanted very much not to show it. "Thank you for your time, Dean."

"Miss Hartwell." She had reached the door when he said it, and she stopped, hand on the frame. "The petition is denied. The decision won't be revisited. And — " he paused, and the pause had weight, had intention, had the careful placement of a man setting a stone in a wall — "I'd think carefully about how much of this you share with the parish. Institutions are like ships, Miss Hartwell. Everyone aboard is safer when the ballast stays where it was put."

"Is that a threat?"

"It's a metaphor. I'm told I overuse them." He picked up his pen. "Good afternoon."

* * *

She did not go back to the archive. She walked instead, out through the cloister garth where the rain had left the grass dark and shining, down past the almshouses with their mullioned windows and their geraniums, and out along the lane to where it ended at the sea wall.

The wall was old — older than the cathedral's present stonework, the masons' marks suggested, though she'd never had the chance to prove it — a long grey shoulder of granite holding back the harbour tide. At its base, where the water worried at it even on a calm day, the stones were furred with weed and barnacle, and in one place near the outfall a section had been patched with concrete that was itself now cracking, a pale scar on old bone.

She stood there in the wind and let herself be angry, because she had learned that anger was most useful when it was spent quickly and in private, like a sneeze.

*Nothing down there concerns the living.* It was the sort of sentence a man said when he had thought about what was down there more than he wanted to admit. Five deans, and none of them curious. None of them had gone down, Pryce said, and Rafael believed him — believed it the way you believe a confession, because it had the shape of one. Five deans had held a key for fifty years and none had turned it, and that was not prudence. That was an inheritance. That was a thing handed along a chain of hands, each one glad to pass it on.

And beneath that, quieter, more unwelcome: *seventeen committals to a sealed vault.* She had found the entries on Thursday, in the almshouse day-book, and she had told herself — was still telling herself — that they were transcription errors, exactly as Pryce had said, because the alternative was a door that opened for the dead but not for deans, and doors like that had keepers.

Keepers like a verger with thirty years of keys on his belt and an unfailing memory for who was ill, who was lonely, who had stopped coming to service.

She was not ready to think that thought. She set it down carefully, the way you set down something hot, and told herself she would pick it up later, when there was more evidence under it.

The tide was coming in. It came the way it always did here — without malice and without hurry, filling the harbour basin inch by patient inch, and at the base of the wall the water found the cracked concrete scar and pressed into it, and withdrew, and pressed again. Rafael watched it work. It had been working, she reflected, for fifty years. Nothing dramatic. Nothing anyone would call a threat. Just pressure, and patience, and the slow discovery of every weakness in the stone.

She turned back up the lane. Callum Reeve was at the lychgate, refilling the parish noticeboard — the flower rota, the food bank appeal, a laminated sheet about the sea wall works. He saw her and smiled his easy, unhurried smile.

"No luck with the Dean, then," he said. Not a question.

"Did you expect me to have any?"

"Wouldn't be the first no he's given." He pinned the last sheet in place and smoothed it with his thumb. "He means well. They all do, up there in the big study. But you want to know something about that crypt, Miss Hartwell?" He leaned on the lychgate, and the wind moved his white hair, and his voice dropped into the register of a man sharing a confidence he'd held for decades. "I've been verger here thirty-one years, and I never once saw that door opened. Not for a funeral. Not for an inspection. Not for anything." He smiled again, and it was a kind smile, and it reached his eyes. "Whatever's down there, nobody living has looked at it in half a century. That's not hiding, is it? That's just leaving well alone."

"No," Rafael agreed. "I suppose it isn't."

She walked on toward the archive, and it was only halfway there that she understood what had been bothering her about the conversation — what had been sitting wrong in her ear like a note slightly flat.

She had never told Callum Reeve that she wanted into the crypt.

He had told *her*. Unprompted, on her first day, cheerfully: *locked since the restoration, love, you'd need to go through the Dean.*

She stopped in the middle of the lane. Above her, gulls argued over the harbour; below, the tide kept up its patient work against the wall; and behind her, at the lychgate, Callum Reeve was watching her go with his hands folded on the gate rail, the way a man watches a visitor leave a house he has kept in perfect order for a very long time.

Rafael turned and walked on, unhurried, her face composed, while her mind ran backward through every conversation she'd had with him, cataloguing, cross-referencing, and filing — and found, filed under *pending*, a single new question, small and cold and patient as the sea:

What else had the verger told her, before she ever thought to ask?
