## 9. The Order of Drowning

The archive at Greywater Bay Cathedral kept its own hours, and none of them were mine.

I had learned this over thirty years of cataloguing its moods. The medieval deeds sulked in damp weather and shed their vellum like dandruff. The Victorian correspondence turned brittle and dramatic in summer, cracking along its folds as though fainting onto a chaise. And the twentieth-century ledgers — the ones nobody loved, bound in cheap buckram the colour of old porridge — sat in their boxes radiating the smugness of things that know they are boring and have decided to weaponize it.

It was one of these I carried to my worktable on the morning after the sexton's body was found in the font, wearing a dead man's cassock.

I will confess something here, since confession appears to be the parish's principal export: I did not sleep. I sat up in my vestry flat with the cardigan-shaped absence in my wardrobe staring at me from across the room, and somewhere around three in the morning I understood that I was not going to solve anything by lying in the dark rehearsing Detective Inspector Okafor's questions back at her in her own voice. She did it better than I did anyway. She had the advantage of not flinching.

So I came down to the archive while the cathedral was still grey and empty, made tea in the electric kettle I was technically forbidden to keep beside the manuscripts (I kept it behind the fire extinguisher, which struck me as a reasonable exchange — one hazard for another), and began to do the only thing I have ever been good at.

I read.

* * *

Let me explain what I had, because an archive is only ever as useful as its gaps, and mine had developed a gap you could drive a hearse through.

Box 1979/C, the parish ledger for the year the Blackwood bequest was founded. Pages razored out — professionally, cleanly, by someone who knew that a razor leaves less evidence than scissors and that paper cut close to the spine looks, to a casual eye, like simple loss. I had found the wound two days ago and told myself it was routine decay. Verity Marsh's visits to the archive had been logged; she had asked to see exactly this box. I had told myself that too was coincidence, because Verity was a benefactor and benefactors browse.

Two bodies later, coincidence had filed for retirement.

What remained in Box 1979/C was instructive. The ledger's index, written in the tidy hand of Mr. Prewitt, who had been verger and unofficial bookkeeper for four decades, listed entries by date and subject. And against several dates in March and April of 1979, the index still stood sentinel over pages that no longer existed:

- *14 March — Ward matter, correspondence re: certification.*
- *22 March — Bequest instrument, execution.*
- *2 April — Burial register annotation, see attached.*
- *11 April — Trust disbursements, first quarter.*

Four entries. Four amputations. Whoever had taken the pages had been careful about the ledger but careless about the index, which is the most common sin in archival crime and the reason I have spent my career telling people that indexes are where documents go to gossip.

And there was one more thing. On the verso of the page following the second excision — the paper thin enough that ink ghosted through — I could make out, when I held it under the raking light of my desk lamp and tilted it like a woman trying to read a letter upside-down across a train carriage, the tail end of a signature list. Five names, of which the last three letters of each survived like footprints at the edge of a tide.

I wrote down what I could reconstruct and then sat looking at it, feeling the particular cold that comes not from fear but from arithmetic.

Because I knew those name-endings. Every archivist knows the signatures of their institution the way a priest knows his congregation's coughs.

* * *

Here is what else I had, laid out on my table by nine o'clock like a surgeon's instruments:

Item the first: the garment inventory Okafor had shared with me — grudgingly, and only after I pointed out that she would need someone who could date a cassock by its stitching, and that withholding me was cutting off her own nose to spite her face, a phrase Father Ambrose used so often in my youth that I briefly believed it was scripture.

Item the second: the vestry flat key log. Five keys. Mine, Ambrose's, the sexton Tom Quill's, Verity Marsh's (benefactors got keys; it was one of the small corruptions we called tradition), and the spare in the safe, signed out — I discovered with a lurch — eleven days before Verity died, by the flower-arranger, Mrs. Fenwick, who had told Okafor she'd borrowed it to retrieve a vase. There was no vase logged. But that thread belonged to another hour.

Item the third, and the one that mattered: the stolen garments themselves. My grey cardigan. Tom Quill's spare cassock — no wait. Not Tom's. That was the detail that had been nagging at me all night like a loose thread, and now I pulled it.

The cassock the sexton drowned in was not Tom Quill's cassock. It was older — the cut was pre-war, the buttons bone rather than plastic, and there was a laundry mark inside the collar: *A.R.C.*, in faded indigo ink. I had catalogued the vestry's textile holdings two years ago. A.R.C. was Arthur Reginald Cobb, archivist of this parish from 1931 to 1968. Dead forty years. His cassock should have been in the memorial chest in the north vestry, locked, catalogued, and forgotten.

Which meant the killer had not simply been stealing clothes from convenient wardrobes. The killer was *selecting*. Choosing garments with provenance. Choosing clothes that remembered.

And if the clothes were chosen for what they meant, then the victims might be chosen the same way.

* * *

I spread it all out and began, in the manner of my profession, to make lists. Lists are how cowards think and how archivists pray.

Verity Marsh, drowned in the sealed crypt, wearing my cardigan. Tom Quill, drowned in the font, wearing Arthur Cobb's cassock. Two deaths. Two garments. Both garments belonging to keepers of records — one living, one dead, both of us people whose job was to hold the parish's memory.

I looked at the index ghosts again. *Ward matter. Certification. Burial register annotation.* A child, Okafor had said, placed in the parish's care in 1978, officially relocated, whose trust fund had been absorbed into the Blackwood bequest. A file thin enough to read by moonlight through both sides.

Certification. A burial register annotation. Someone had certified something about that child. Someone had annotated a grave.

I went to the burial registers.

They live in Box 1974–1985/B, and they are heavy as sins. I carried volume '79 to the table and opened it to spring, and there — 2 April, matching the mutilated index entry exactly — was an annotation in the margin beside a burial entry for an infant girl, name recorded only as *E—, ward of the parish*, interred in the paupers' row behind the north wall.

The annotation read: *Certificate received. No stone to be laid. See file.*

See file. The file that was too thin. The file that had been fed, page by page, into whatever razor had visited Box 1979/C.

But registers, thank God, are copied. The diocesan office holds duplicates, and I had spent a tedious fortnight two winters ago microfilming our duplicate set for preservation purposes, an exercise I had resented at the time and now blessed with my whole heart. I fetched the film, threaded the reader — a machine held together largely by prayer and electrical tape — and cranked through to the same page.

The duplicate margin note was identical except in one respect. It was initialled.

*A.P.*

Ambrose Pemberton.

My mentor. The man who taught me conservation-grade wheat starch paste from ordinary glue. The man who brought me soup when I had pneumonia in my second year and read me the Song of Songs "for the vocabulary." The man whose name is on my letters of appointment, whose chair I still cannot sit in without feeling like a boy borrowing his father's coat.

His initials stood beside the erasure of a child.

I put my head down on the table, carefully, avoiding the manuscripts, because even in grief one does not transfer acids to sixteenth-century vellum. Ambrose trained me well. He trained me well, and he certified a child's death, and both of these things were true at once, and I lay there on the worktable learning what it costs to hold two truths that hate each other.

Then I got up, because grief is no excuse for sloppy method, and finished the job.

* * *

The signature ghosts. Back to them. Three names' worth of final letters surviving on the verso ghost:

- *…erton*
- *…arsh*
- *…ill*

Pemberton. Marsh. Quill.

Ambrose. Verity. Tom.

I sat very still. Outside, the tide was going out with that long sighing sound the bay makes, as though the sea itself were keeping accounts.

Verity Marsh was first. Tom Quill was second. If the ghost-list ran in the order the names appeared on the original document — and every instinct I had as an archivist, every scar tissue fibre of professional pattern-recognition, said it did — then the list continued past the visible edge. Names four and five had been swallowed whole by the razor.

But a list has a logic, and the logic here was legible. These were not random parishioners. These were the five signatories of the 1979 instrument — the men and women who had certified a child dead, absorbed her inheritance, and built the bequest on the foundation of her absence. The bequest that paid for the cathedral's restoration. The bequest that funded my position, my flat, my kettle-behind-the-fire-extinguisher, my entire sheltered and scholarly life.

The killer was working down the signature page.

First Verity, who controlled the trust money. Second Tom, the sexton, keeper of the graves — the man whose register annotation had declared a living child buried. Third—

Third was *erton*.

I looked at the letters for a long time, hoping they would rearrange themselves into something kinder. They declined. Letters are honest that way; it is people who aren't.

Ambrose Pemberton was third.

* * *

I want to record, for the sake of the record — and the record is all any of us are, in the end — that my first thought was not for myself.

Okafor would have expected self-preservation. She had already noted, in her dry way, that my cardigan had been worn by the first victim, and that patterns in this case tended to rhyme. But the thought that arrived, cold and complete as a catalogue card, was: *he doesn't know.*

Ambrose didn't know he was third. He was ninety-one years old, half-deaf in the left ear, stubborn in both, living alone in the canonry with a cat named Augustine who hated everyone including Ambrose. He had watched me find the mutilated ledger and had begged me — actually begged, his voice gone small and ancient in a way I had never heard in forty years of knowing him — to let the dead stay buried.

He wasn't begging out of guilt. Or not only. He was begging because he had done the same arithmetic I had just done, decades ago, and had been waiting ever since for someone to finish the sum.

He knew the order. Of course he knew the order. He had signed the page.

And Verity was already dead, and Tom was already dead, and the third name on the list drank his tea at four o'clock sharp and left the saucer out and would not hear a word against the parish that had made him rich in comfort if not in coin.

I checked the clock. Ten past noon. Ambrose took his lunch at half twelve and his nap at one, and slept like the righteous, which after everything I now knew felt like the cruellest detail of all.

* * *

I stood in the archive doorway with my hand on the light switch and understood, with terrible clarity, what the building had become overnight. For thirty years this room had been my refuge — the one place in Greywater Bay where nothing could surprise me, where every horror was laminated between acid-free boards and labelled in pencil. Now the archive itself was counting down. Four entries razored. Two names crossed off in seawater. A signature page with my mentor's name sitting third from the top like a lit fuse.

The killer was reading the same collection I was. We were, in the most literal sense, colleagues.

And there was one more thing — one small, awful thing I had noticed and not yet said aloud, even to myself. The ghost-signatures ended at *erton, arsh, ill*. Three survivors of the razor's edge. But the index had listed *four* mutilated entries, and the fourth — *Trust disbursements, first quarter* — implied a fifth signatory. Money needs more than three hands to disappear properly. There was a fourth name, maybe a fifth, still hidden past the tear, still unaccounted for.

Someone on that list was alive besides Ambrose. Someone whose name-ending I couldn't see. Someone with a key to my flat, perhaps, and a borrowed vase that never existed.

I switched off the archive lights, climbed the worn steps into the nave, and stood a moment in the smell of candle wax and cold stone, listening to the bay breathe.

Then I went, as fast as dignity allowed, to save the third name on a drowning list — before the tide, which keeps its own ledger, entered him into it.
