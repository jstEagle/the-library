## 19. The Lock That Turns Both Ways

The crypt door closed behind me with the soft, deliberate sound of a book shutting.

I had heard that sound ten thousand times in my life—the archive's great oak doors, the vestry cupboard, the little cabinet where Father Ambrose kept the communion wine and, once, memorably, a hedgehog. A closing door in Greywater Bay was never merely a closing door. It was a sentence being punctuated. And this one, I thought, had been punctuated with a full stop.

The crypt was lit by two hurricane lamps set on the stone floor, and by them I saw her properly for the first time.

Marguerite Teale. The flower arranger. The woman who had, for eleven years, arranged lilies on the high altar and never once been late, never once been noticed, never once been asked about herself. She stood by Verity Marsh's memorial slab with her hands folded, a small, tidy woman in a cardigan the colour of weak tea, and she looked at me the way I imagine a librarian looks at a book returned after forty years overdue.

"You came," she said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped. Hoping and knowing are different animals." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, which was the worst thing about it. "You're a keeper of records, Mr. Blackwood. You don't leave an entry unfinished."

I had left her the note's reply unstamped, which I suppose was its own kind of signature. *Come read the last entry,* she had written, in the looping, patient hand I had spent three weeks tracing through returned envelopes. And I had come, against Sable Okafor's explicit instruction, at nine o'clock at night, carrying nothing but a torch, a thermos of tea I would not drink, and the reconstructed ledger wrapped in oilcloth under my arm.

"You brought it," Marguerite said, her eyes going to the bundle.

"I brought it."

"May I?"

"You may look. You may not touch." I heard my own voice and was startled by it—steady, dry, the voice I used with researchers who wanted to lick their fingers before turning a page. "House rules."

Something flickered across her face. Not anger. Recognition, maybe. The pleasure of meeting a fellow professional.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened in my chest during the next hour, because honesty is the whole point of what came after, and I have learned that honesty begins in uncomfortable places.

I liked her.

That is the shameful truth of it. She was clever and funny in a small, dry way, and she knew the parish better than I did in the ways that mattered—knew which flagstones rang hollow, knew that the yew by the north wall had been struck by lightning in 1963, knew that Verity Marsh had taken her tea with lemon and had held the parish's charitable trust like a woman holding a leash. For eleven years Marguerite Teale had stood in the nave arranging flowers over the graves of the people who had erased her, and no one—not Ambrose, not Verity, not the wardens, not me—had ever thought to wonder what she was thinking.

"I used to practice," she told me, walking me slowly along the crypt's alcoves, her lamps casting our shadows huge on the vaulted stone. "Being invisible. When I was a girl, in the first home. The matron used to say, if you're quiet as furniture, nobody throws furniture out. So I became furniture. I became *very good* furniture." She touched a memorial urn absently, the way you'd straighten a picture frame. "Eleven years here. Do you know how many conversations I've had with Verity Marsh about the flower budget? Hundreds. She'd stand there in her good coat telling me the lilies were extravagant, while wearing a coat her charity bought with my mother's grave money."

"Your mother's—"

"The land. The cottage on Marsh Lane. Sold into the trust when they declared me dead. You've read the file, I assume. You're the archivist; of course you've read the file." She said it without bitterness, which frightened me more than bitterness would have. "It's a very tidy file. My mentor—well. He was everyone's mentor, wasn't he."

"Ambrose," I said.

"Ambrose," she agreed. "He signed the certificate. I looked it up, eventually. Took me twenty years to find it, because he was *good*. He was the best. That's the tragedy of your Father Ambrose, Mr. Blackwood. Not that he was a bad man. That he was a good man who did one perfect, unforgivable thing, and then spent fifty years being kind to everyone as a kind of penance he never named." She turned to face me fully. "He was kind to you most of all. I used to watch. The way he looked at you—I thought, for years, that you must be his grandson. Some secret. And then I found out you were nobody. A foundling. He *chose* you. Do you understand what that means?"

I did. I understood it with a pain that went all the way down to the foundations.

"It means," I said carefully, "that everything I was given was bought."

"With my inheritance. Yes." She said it gently, the way you'd correct a child's arithmetic. "Your whole life, Mr. Blackwood. Your flat in the vestry. Your position. The roof over your head, the education, the—what did they call it? The family. The found family." She smiled again. "I've always thought that phrase was obscene. *Found.* As though a family were a lost umbrella."

* * *

Here is what I had prepared, in the sleepless days since her note, in the long rehearsals I conducted in the archive with only the dead for an audience.

I had prepared to agree with her.

Not entirely—not to murder, never to murder—but to the principle. Because the principle was correct and I could no longer pretend otherwise. The record was obscene. The cover-up had been real. Ambrose had signed a lie and been paid in a lifetime of reverence, and Verity Marsh had administered stolen money for four decades and called it charity, and the sexton—poor Mr. Pettifer, drowned in the font like a terrible parody of baptism—had countersigned the relocation order with a shaking hand and been given a cottage for it.

Marguerite had been robbed of a name, a childhood, a mother's grave, and forty years of her one life. The institution that did it still stood, still rang its bells, still called itself good.

So I had prepared to stand in this crypt and tell her: *You're right. It was monstrous. And I will help you finish the record—not with bodies, but with publication. Every page. Every name. Let the whole parish hear it read aloud.*

I had prepared that speech. I had rehearsed it. I believed it.

And then she said, "I've saved you for last, you know. Not because you're guilty. You're the only one who isn't. I saved you for last because you're the only one who'll understand it."

"Understand what?"

"The last entry." She moved to the far wall of the crypt, where the old ledger shelf stood—the iron one, bolted into the stone, the one the 1979 wardens had used to store the trust documents. She lifted down a slim volume. "I've been keeping one. Forty years of watching, written down. Every lie, every cheque, every Christmas the wardens toasted their own generosity. It's all here. And the last page is blank, Mr. Blackwood, because the last entry hasn't happened yet." She held the book out to me. "I wanted you to write it."

I looked at the book. I looked at her small, patient hands.

"And what," I said, "did you imagine the last entry would say?"

"That's the choice I'm offering." Her voice was still gentle, but underneath it now I could hear the other voice, the one that had lured Verity Marsh into a sealed crypt and held her head under forty years of accumulated silence. "Either it says *Elodie Blackwood, drowned in the crypt of Greywater Bay Cathedral, wearing his own cardigan—the record's final witness, erased as the record was erased*—which would be, I think, a kind of poetry. Or it says *Elodie Blackwood, archivist, unsealed the parish of Greywater Bay and let the light in.*" She tilted her head. "I'd prefer the second. I've had enough of drowning. But I've learned not to hope. Hoping and knowing are different animals."

"And if I choose the first?"

"Then I'm afraid you'll choose it lying down."

She said it without heat. That was the terrible thing. She said it the way I would report a catalogue number.

* * *

I have been asked, since, how I kept my hands steady. The answer is that I didn't, entirely; I simply had somewhere to put them.

Because while Marguerite Teale had spent eleven years becoming furniture, I had spent thirty years becoming the building. I knew this crypt the way Ambrose knew his psalter. I knew that the flagstone by the ledger shelf rang hollow. I knew that the iron shelf was not merely bolted to the wall but *counterweighted*—that the whole shelf assembly hung from a stone pivot above, balanced against a granite slab in the floor, because the 1979 wardens had wanted a strongroom and had not wanted to pay a mason. I knew it because I had catalogued the mason's unpaid invoice, and because Ambrose, in one of his lucid, guilty moods two winters ago, had shown me how it worked.

"It's the old crime's own machinery," he'd said, turning the hidden lever behind the shelf's end-bracket. "They sealed the crypt with it, that night in '79, after they moved the papers. Counterweight. You pull this, the shelf swings, the slab drops into the channel, and the door above locks itself. Nothing opens it from inside. Nothing opens it from outside without the key." He had laughed, a dry, unhappy laugh. "Clever men, doing a stupid thing, always leave clever machinery behind. And clever machinery always remembers."

I had filed that conversation away as one more piece of parish lore. I had not known, then, that I was filing ammunition.

So when Marguerite said *lying down*, I did the thing I had rehearsed for a different speech. I set the oilcloth bundle on the memorial slab, unwrapped the reconstructed ledger, and said:

"Then let's do it properly. If I'm to be the last entry, I want the record complete. Your book. My ledger. Side by side. That's the only way I'd ever consent to anything—cross-referenced."

Her eyes lit. God forgive me, her eyes *lit*, and I understood that I was not the only lonely archivist in Greywater Bay. She brought her slim volume and laid it beside mine, and for a few minutes we were simply two keepers of records comparing sources, and it was the closest thing to happiness either of us had felt in years, and I have never fully made my peace with that.

"The certificate," she said, pointing to my reconstruction. "Page four. Ambrose's signature. You've dated it the ninth of March, but it was the eleventh. I know because I was in the infirmary with fever, and the fever broke on the tenth, and the matron said, *you gave us such a fright, we'd nearly told them to prepare the paperwork.* They'd already prepared it. Two days early." Her finger trembled, just slightly, on the page. "They were so efficient at killing me that they didn't wait for me to die."

"I'll amend it," I said. "Eleventh of March. Source: the Teale testimony." And I took out my pen—the good pen, the one Ambrose gave me when I was nineteen, the pen I had used for every catalogue entry of my adult life—and I wrote it in the margin, in the space I had always reserved for corrections, because an archivist who cannot correct his own record is only a hoarder with a filing system.

Marguerite watched me write, and her face did something complicated, and I think—I have always hoped—that in that moment she might have accepted the other ending. The publication. The light.

But then she said, quietly, "There's one more source you'll need," and she turned back toward the ledger shelf to take down the original 1979 file—the true papers, the ones the wardens had sealed in the strongroom, the ones she had evidently already retrieved—and her back was to me, and her hands were on the iron shelf.

And I pulled the lever.

* * *

I want to describe it exactly, because I have described it a hundred times since, in statements and in dreams, and exactness is the only penance I know how to perform.

The lever was cold and stiff and required both hands. There was a sound like a held breath being released—stone sliding on stone, forty years of dust finding its voice. The iron shelf swung on its pivot, slow as a church door, and the granite counterweight dropped into its channel in the floor with a *boom* that I felt in my teeth, and the slab that had sealed the original crime sealed its sequel.

Marguerite spun. The lamp light caught her face, and what was on it was not fear. It was betrayal—pure, refined, the betrayal of a woman who had been betrayed so thoroughly and for so long that she had, I think, believed herself immune to it.

"You," she said.

"The mechanism's the same one," I said. My voice cracked on the second word, which I regret, but there it is. "The one they used on you. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But I couldn't—Marguerite, I couldn't let the last entry be written in blood. Not in *my* crypt. Not in anyone's. The record ends here. It ends *read*, not *buried*."

"You're locking me in with it." She looked around at the stone, at the lamps, at the two ledgers lying side by side on the slab, separated from her now by the dropped slab and the swung shelf. "With the evidence."

"Yes."

"And yourself?"

"Me?" I put my hand flat on the oilcloth bundle. "I'm the archive, Marguerite. I don't get locked away. I get *consulted*."

It was not, I admit, my finest line. But I had been awake for thirty-one hours and my heart was attempting to exit through my ribs, and I have never claimed to be brave—only thorough.

She pressed her hands against the slab and pushed, and it did not move, because clever machinery remembers its purpose. She laughed then, a short, astonished, terrible laugh. "Forty years," she said. "Forty years I planned this. And they sealed me in with my own evidence using *their own lock*. Do you know what the worst part is?"

"Tell me."

"The worst part is it's *elegant*. You'd appreciate it. I appreciate it." She slid down until she was sitting against the stone, a small tidy woman in a tea-coloured cardigan, sitting on the floor of the crypt where she had been declared dead before she ever lived. "Go on, then. Call your detective."

"I don't need to call her."

Marguerite looked up.

"Detective Inspector Okafor forbade me to come," I said. "Which means she followed me. She's been sitting outside the lychgate in an unmarked car for two hours, drinking cold coffee and composing the lecture she's going to give me. I'd estimate you have four minutes. I'd use them." I sat down on the steps, on my side of the stone, and put the two ledgers on my knees. "I'll read you the eleventh of March entry while we wait. You should hear it in your own file's voice. It's the least the record owes you."

And I read it to her. The false certificate, in full, Ambrose's signature and all, in the flat clear voice of the archive. She listened with her eyes closed, and somewhere in the middle of it she began, very quietly, to weep—not the weeping of a murderer caught, I think, but the weeping of a girl in an infirmary in 1979, hearing at last, forty years late, the official confirmation that everyone had known she was alive and had signed her out of the world anyway.

The crypt door opened above us on the fourth minute, precisely as forecast, and Sable Okafor came down the stairs with her torch and her handcuffs and an expression that promised the lecture would be delivered in instalments over many months.

She looked at Marguerite on the floor. She looked at me on the steps with two ledgers on my knees. She looked at the swung shelf, the dropped slab, the whole absurd apparatus of the parish's original sin, now repurposed.

"Blackwood," she said, "you locked her in the crypt."

"I locked the *evidence* in the crypt," I said. "She was holding it at the time."

Okafor closed her eyes briefly, in the manner of a woman consulting whatever deity oversees detectives assigned to archivists. Then she cuffed Marguerite Teale gently, helped her to her feet, and read her the caution in the voice one uses for a difficult parishioner.

Marguerite went with her. At the foot of the stairs she stopped, and turned, and looked at me over the detective's shoulder.

"The last entry," she said. "You never wrote it. The page is still blank."

"I know," I said.

"Write something true," she said. "That's all I ever wanted. Write something *true*."

And then they took her up into the lamplit nave, and I was alone in the crypt with the ledgers and the dust and the machinery, and I opened her slim black book to the final blank page, and I picked up Ambrose's pen.

I was still writing when the bells began—no, not the bells. The telephone, ringing down through the stone of the vestry above, urgent and repeated, and then footsteps on the crypt stairs that were too fast and too many for a night that should have been ending.

Sable came back down alone. Her face had changed.

"Elodie," she said. She never called me Elodie. "It's Father Ambrose's cottage. There's smoke."
