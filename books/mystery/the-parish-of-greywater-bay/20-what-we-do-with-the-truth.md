## 20. What We Do With the Truth

Sable Okafor had testified at enough hearings to know that most of them were theatre performed for an audience of paperwork. The Greywater Bay magistrates' room was small, wood-panelled, and smelled of lemon polish and old rain, and on the morning of the committal hearing of Margaret Thorne—née whatever name the parish had given her in 1978, then un-given, then given back—it was full past capacity.

She had asked for a larger room. The clerk had said there wasn't one. Sable suspected that was a lie told out of kindness: nobody in Greywater Bay wanted this heard in a big room, where it would echo.

Margaret Thorne sat in the dock with her hands folded, wearing a plain navy cardigan. Not grey. Sable had checked. There was something almost demure about her now, the flower-arranger's calm still clinging to her like the scent of lilies, and it unsettled Sable more than any rage would have. Rage you could work with. Calm was a locked door.

The charge sheet was read out. Two counts of murder—Verity Marsh, Thomas Quill, the sexton—and one count of false imprisonment, which was the legal language for what she'd done to Elodie Blackwood in the crypt before Sable had arrived with the bolt cutters and found them both standing in the dark among the coffins, the counterweight lock groaning overhead like a ship changing its mind.

Margaret pleaded not guilty to everything.

That surprised exactly no one who had been in the crypt that night, least of all Sable. She remembered the woman's voice in the dark, level as a tide table: *I'm not finished reading.* As if murder were a document and she intended to see it through to the colophon.

* * *

The first surprise came an hour in, when Margaret's barrister—a pale, apologetic man named Fitch who looked like he'd been assembled from spare solicitors—rose and said, "My client wishes to make a statement."

The magistrate frowned. "This is a committal hearing, Mr. Fitch, not a pulpit."

"With respect, sir, my client understands that perfectly." Fitch paused, and Sable watched him choose his next words the way a man chooses which wire to cut. "She wishes to read a document into the record. A historical document. She maintains it is relevant to her state of mind, and she has been advised—" here he glanced sideways at his client, and something in his face suggested the advice had been extensive and entirely ignored "—that relevance is... arguable."

"Overruled," said the magistrate.

"I'd ask the court to hear it," said Margaret.

Her voice carried. It wasn't loud. It didn't need to be. It was the voice of someone who had spent forty years being unheard and had since made a study of how to fix that.

"The court has ruled," said the magistrate, but he said it the way people say things they already regret.

"Then I'll read it anyway," said Margaret pleasantly, "and the court can decide afterward whether I was disruptive or merely thorough. I've read the rules. I catalogued them myself, once, in a former life. Section by section." She unfolded a paper from inside her cardigan. "It isn't long. It's only ever been long because of what everyone did to avoid reading it."

Two uniformed officers looked at Sable. Sable looked at the magistrate. The magistrate, a dry stick of a man named Halloway who had presumably sentenced smugglers and arsonists and at least one memorable case of sheep rustling, opened his mouth—

And then Elodie Blackwood stood up in the public gallery.

Sable had half expected him not to come. He'd spent the week since the arrest looking like a man relearning how to inhabit his own house, moving through the archive touching nothing, drinking tea he forgot about. But he came, of course he came, because Elodie's entire moral architecture was built on the principle that records exist to be read.

"Sir," Elodie said, in the mild, citation-ready voice Sable knew so well, "I'd ask the court to let her speak. I can vouch for the document's authenticity. I reconstructed it myself. Every page."

"You're the archivist," Halloway said.

"I'm the archivist," Elodie agreed. "It's what I'm for."

There was a small laugh in the gallery—not a cruel one. Even now, even here, Greywater Bay couldn't help being fond of him. Sable noted, professionally, that fondness was going to be a problem if this ever reached a jury. She also noted, less professionally, that she shared it.

Halloway rubbed his eyes. "Five minutes," he said, in the tone of a man granting five minutes to a flood. "Read your document, Miss Thorne."

* * *

She read it standing, though she didn't need to stand. She read it the way Elodie read deeds aloud when he thought no one was listening—carefully, giving each clause its weight, letting the dates do their terrible arithmetic.

A child placed in the parish's care in 1978. A trust fund, modest but real, left by a grandmother who had sold fish and saved every penny. A death certificate signed in 1979 by a young curate named Ambrose Wren, certifying the child dead of fever, though no fever existed, no grave existed, no body. A bequest founded the same year—the Blackwood Bequest—with the child's inheritance folded into it like an egg into batter. Four signatures certifying the arrangement. Four men paid, in one form or another, in restoration stone and stipends and quiet, comfortable years.

Verity Marsh's signature was first. She had administered the transfer. She had been twenty-nine years old and had needed the money herself, and had told herself, apparently for fifty years, that the child was better off without ties to a town like this.

Thomas Quill's mark was second—he'd been the sexton even then, and had dug the empty grave and kept the secret the way he kept everything, under the grass and silent.

Ambrose Wren's name was third.

And the fourth signature belonged to a man Sable had never met, because he'd had the good fortune to die in 1994: Reginald Blackwood, Elodie's adoptive father, the architect of the whole quiet machine.

Margaret read all of it. She read the returned letters too—or the list of them, nineteen letters over eleven years, sent to the parish office and returned unopened, until the last one, which said only: *I will stop writing when one of you says my name.*

Nobody had said her name for forty years. So she had gone and taken two lives and nearly taken a third to make the town say it.

When she finished, the room was so quiet Sable could hear the rain starting against the windows, soft as an apology.

"That," said Margaret, sitting down, "is why I did it. You may proceed."

* * *

They proceeded. It took three days. Sable gave evidence twice, and both times felt the strange doubled sensation of telling the truth in a building where the truth had been contraband for half a century.

On the second afternoon, during the recess, she found Elodie in the corridor outside, sitting on a bench beneath a portrait of some long-dead dean, holding a paper cup of vending-machine coffee as though it had personally betrayed him.

"You know she's going down for this," Sable said, sitting beside him. "Whatever the record says. Whatever sympathy's in that room. Two people are dead, Elodie. Verity Marsh drowned in your cardigan. No document undoes that."

"I know." He turned the cup in his hands. "I'm not asking for her to walk. I keep thinking about what she said to me in the crypt. That she didn't want mercy. She wanted witnesses." He looked up. "So do I. That's the part I can't stop wanting. Everyone in that room heard it, Sable. The whole rotten ledger, read aloud, in daylight, with a magistrate taking notes. Forty years of silence ended in five minutes because a murderer refused to be quiet." His mouth twisted. "I've spent my life believing archives change things eventually. She proved they don't. Not eventually. Only loudly."

"Loudly is a method," Sable said. "I've used it."

"It shows."

He almost smiled. Then it faded, and Sable saw the other thing arrive behind his eyes, the thing that had been arriving all week.

"There's a fourth signature," he said quietly. "Reginald Blackwood. My father. And there are living beneficiaries, aren't there? People whose pensions, whose houses, whose whole lives sit on top of that trust. Father Ambrose is dead—he died before he could—" Elodie stopped, steadied himself. Ambrose's death was still a wound neither of them had dressed properly; the old canon had gone peacefully in his chair holding a ledger he'd agreed, at the end, not to burn. "But the others are alive. The churchwarden. The solicitor's estate. Half the parish council's grandfathers. If the record stands, the bequest falls. Everything it built falls."

"Yes," Sable said.

"And I'm one of the beneficiaries." He said it flatly, the way you read out a fact you've stopped fighting. "My flat above the vestry. My salary. My whole childhood, Sable. Paid for out of a stolen inheritance. I've been living in the proceeds since I was six years old."

Sable considered several comforting things to say and discarded all of them, because Elodie could smell comfort the way sharks smelled blood, and it made him worse.

"Then you know what I have to ask next," she said.

He closed his eyes. "Charges."

"Maybe. I don't decide alone. The Crown Prosecutor decides, and he decides based on what I send him." She leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Here's where I am. The original four are either dead or dying or beyond prosecution—Ambrose is gone, Reginald's gone, the solicitor's estate is a filing cabinet. Verity and Quill paid with their lives, horribly, and I won't pretend that balances anything, but the law doesn't either. What's left is the structure. The trust. The people who inherited knowingly or sleepwalked into inheriting, which is most of them, because that's how these things work—nobody robs anybody, everybody just fails to look."

"And?"

"And I can write it up as a conspiracy with surviving participants, and watch this town eat itself for a decade while lawyers bill the very trust we're trying to dissolve. Or I can recommend no charges against the beneficiaries, conditional on the record being published in full and the bequest wound down transparently, with the ward's estate—that's Margaret, legally, if anyone asks—made whole first." Sable exhaled. "Restorative justice, they call it. I call it choosing which fire to put out."

Elodie was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the corridor window. Somewhere below, the cathedral bells practised for a wedding, cheerful and oblivious, the way institutions always were.

"You're asking me," he said finally, "to help you decide whether to prosecute my own family."

"I'm asking you to tell me the truth and then decide what to do with it. Same as everyone else in this parish, for once." She stood. "You have until Friday. Then I send my report either way."

"Sable." He looked up at her, and for a moment he was six years old again, newly arrived, holding a cardboard box of belongings and a name he hadn't chosen. "Thank you for not pretending this is simple."

"It's not simple," Sable said. "It's just honest. Those aren't the same thing, but they're related. Like us."

* * *

Friday came grey and clean-washed, the bay glittering after the rain like a floor that had finally been mopped.

Sable wrote the report at her kitchen table with the window open, gulls arguing on the chimney pot, tea going cold in the way tea did when she was working. She laid it out plainly: no realistic prospect of conviction against the surviving beneficiaries for acts committed decades ago by persons deceased; strong public interest in full disclosure; recommendation that the Blackwood Bequest be dissolved by consent, its remaining funds transferred first to the estate of Margaret Thorne in restitution, remainder to the parish's general charitable fund under independent administration.

At the bottom, in the section marked *Additional Considerations*, she wrote one line:

*The undersigned notes that the archivist, E. Blackwood, holds the complete record and has indicated his intention to publish it regardless of this report's outcome. The undersigned further notes that she does not intend to stop him.*

She read that sentence four times. It was, technically, insubordinate. It was possibly career-limiting. Her old inspector in the city would have called it sentimental.

Sable thought about Verity Marsh's face in the mortuary, composed and drowned. About Thomas Quill in the font, folded like laundry. About Margaret Thorne in the dock, saying *you may proceed* as though justice were a queue she'd waited forty years in. About Elodie on the bench in the corridor, asking her not to pretend.

She left the sentence in.

* * *

The decision came back Monday morning: accepted, with a note from the CPS that could best be described as relieved. No charges against the beneficiaries. Full disclosure recommended. The matter of publication, the note said delicately, was "a question for the diocese and the archivist."

Which meant, translated from bureaucratese, that everyone had decided it was Elodie's cross to bear, and everyone was grateful it wasn't theirs.

Sable walked the report over to the cathedral herself. She liked walking in Greywater Bay in the mornings, before the tourists, when the town was mostly gulls and delivery vans and Mrs. Petchey hosing down her step with the aggression of a woman at war with moss.

Elodie was in the archive, naturally. He was at the long table with the reconstructed 1979 ledger spread out under lamps, and around it—Sable stopped in the doorway—were boxes. Dozens of boxes. Labelled in his neat hand: *1979 — Correspondence. 1979 — Trust Instruments. 1979 — Certification & Signatures. Letters of M.T., Returned, 1981–1992.*

He was packing the truth for transport.

"You've heard," she said.

"This morning." He didn't look up. "No charges. Dissolution by consent. Restitution first." He set a folder down and finally turned, and Sable saw that he looked lighter and worse simultaneously, like a man who had amputated something and was still deciding whether to miss it. "The diocese wants to 'manage the narrative.' Their word. Narrative. As if this were a novel."

"What did you tell them?"

"I told them I'm the archivist." A ghost of a smile. "It's what I'm for."

Sable came and stood at the end of the table, looking down at the ledger—the mutilated one, razored pages regrown in facsimile, the four signatures circled in his careful pencil. "You're really going to publish it. All of it. The bequest, the signatures, your father's name. Your own name, in the beneficiary lists."

"My name's already in it," Elodie said. "It's been in it since 1979. I'm just the last person to read it." He squared the boxes, aligning their edges with the table's edge, a small compulsive tenderness. "Some people won't forgive me. Mrs. Danvers has already stopped speaking to me, and she only ran the charity shop. Imagine the canon residents. Imagine the bishop."

"Imagine the alternative."

"I have." He looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "I lived in the alternative for thirty years. It had lovely panelling."

Sable laughed despite herself—one short bark that echoed off the deed chests. Then she sobered, because there was one more thing, and she'd been carrying it since Friday like a stone in her boot.

"One loose thread," she said. "Margaret. She's remanded pending trial. She's cooperating fully, pleading the truth defence, which her barrister tells me is not actually a defence. She'll likely plead guilty to manslaughter on grounds of diminished responsibility—her history, the institutions, the file I pulled. It might land. It might not." Sable hesitated. "She's asked for one thing. A visitor. Not her solicitor. Not the chaplain."

Elodie went very still. "Me."

"She said—" Sable consulted her notebook, though she didn't need to; she'd memorised it. "She said: *Tell the archivist I've left him something in the last box. He'll understand. Records are only worth what someone reads them.*"

For a moment Elodie didn't move. Then he turned, slowly, to the row of boxes behind him—to the last one on the shelf, the one labelled in his own handwriting, catalogued by his own hands years ago, before any of this began:

*Letters of M.T., Returned, Unopened.*

Nineteen letters. And, he must have known even then, tucked among them, whatever she had added since.

Elodie reached for the box lid.

Sable, who had a detective's instinct for doors about to open onto weather, stayed exactly where she was—and watched.
