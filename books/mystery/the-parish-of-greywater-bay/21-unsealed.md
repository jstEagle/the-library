## 21. Unsealed

The scanner in the archive was older than some of the documents it had eaten, and it made a sound like a contented cat when it worked and a sound like a dying gull when it didn't. At four minutes past six on a Tuesday morning, Elodie Blackwood fed it the last page of the 1979 record—the falsified death certificate, Ambrose's careful, terrible signature at the bottom—and listened to it purr.

Two hundred and eleven pages. He had counted them three times, because counting was what he did instead of weeping, and he had not trusted himself to stop at two hundred and ten.

The file was called *GREYWATER_1979_FULL_RECORD.pdf*, which struck him, even now, as absurdly plain for something that had cost so much. He had considered more dramatic names. *The Ledger of Small Mercies.* *What the Bequest Bought.* But drama was the enemy here; drama was how institutions survived scandals, by wrapping them in narrative until the truth became just another story the parish told about itself. No. He wanted it flat. He wanted it legible. He wanted it boring enough that no one could call it embellished and honest enough that no one could call it anything else.

He had built the site himself over eleven nights, teaching himself from library books because asking the diocesan IT volunteer felt like asking a burglar to help you change the locks. A simple page. The complete scanned record. A transcription, checked twice against the originals. His own statement—four thousand words, written and rewritten until every sentence could stand in a courtroom or a pulpit without flinching. And at the bottom, one line:

*Published by Elodie Blackwood, Archivist of Greywater Bay Cathedral, in accordance with the duty of the record.*

The duty of the record. Ambrose had taught him that phrase when Elodie was nineteen and terrified of making his first mistake with an irreplaceable document. "The record doesn't belong to us, lad," the old canon had said, tapping the ledger with one crooked finger. "We're only its custodians. It belongs to everyone who was ever erased from it."

Elodie had thought, at the time, that it was a lesson about dust jackets and acid-free folders.

He pressed PUBLISH at 6:14 a.m., and the cathedral bell rang the hour above him, eight strokes, indifferent as tide.

* * *

By nine, the town knew.

He learned this the way he learned most things about Greywater Bay—not by being told, but by observing the behavior of systems. Mrs. Trelawney from the gift shop did not come up at her usual ten o'clock to complain about the damp in the north transept. The verger's radio, usually audible through the vestry wall playing hymns and shipping forecasts, was silent. And at 9:40 exactly, three separate people walked past the archive door without knocking, slowly, the way people walk past a house where someone has died.

At noon, the first email arrived. It was from the Diocesan Registrar, and it was four words long: *"Please call me today."*

At 12:20, the second arrived, from a number he didn't recognize, and it was considerably longer. It began *You self-righteous little traitor* and ended with a threat of litigation that misquoted both the Data Protection Act and the Book of Common Prayer, which took a certain inventive talent.

Elodie read them standing at his window, watching the bay do its grey, patient work against the seawall. He had expected this part. He had rehearsed this part. What he had not rehearsed—he discovered this around 2 p.m., when his hands started shaking over a cup of tea he had forgotten to drink—was how much it would hurt anyway. Expectation, it turned out, was not armor. It was just pain with better paperwork.

The phone rang at 2:15. He let it go to voicemail. It rang again at 2:16, which meant whoever it was had hung up and immediately redialed, and there was only one person in Greywater Bay with that particular combination of patience and fury.

"Hello, Mrs. Penhale," he said.

"You've killed it." Her voice was steady, which was worse than shouting. Edith Penhale had run the parish office for thirty-one years and had once reduced a visiting bishop to apologizing for the length of his own sermon. "Forty years of the bequest. The roof fund. The food pantry. The winter fuel grants for the pensioners on Marsh Lane. You've put it all on the internet like—like laundry."

"Yes."

"The *pantry*, Elodie. Widow Curnow gets her Christmas hamper off that money. Are you going to tell her the truth ruined her Christmas?"

"No," Elodie said. "I'm going to tell her the truth bought it with a dead girl's inheritance, and then I'm going to help find another way to fill the hamper. A way that doesn't cost anyone their name."

There was a silence on the line long enough that he checked whether she'd hung up. She hadn't. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into something quieter and more dangerous.

"I knew Ambrose forty years. I typed his letters. I sat with him when your predecessor died and he cried into his tea, and I never once—" She stopped. Started again. "Was any of it real? The parish? Him?"

And there it was: the question underneath all the other questions, the one the whole town was asking in its various dialects. Elodie looked out at the bay.

"Some of it was real," he said. "That's what makes the rest unforgivable. If it had all been a lie, we could burn it down and be clean. It wasn't. It was a lie wearing a true coat, and the only thing to do is take the coat off, even if we freeze."

Mrs. Penhale said, very quietly, "You sound like him."

Then she hung up, and Elodie stood holding the phone for a long time, unable to decide if it had been a wound or a benediction. Possibly, with Edith Penhale, those were the same instrument.

* * *

The days that followed had a rhythm he came to think of as the tide going out—each morning revealing a little more of what the flood had covered, most of it wreckage, some of it things he'd forgotten were beautiful.

The Chapter met on Thursday and voted, seven to two, to request his resignation. The two dissenters were Canon Pryce, who was ninety and claimed to remember the war and therefore refused to be scandalized by anything, and young Deacon Ferrow, who caught Elodie in the cloister afterward and gripped his arm hard enough to bruise.

"For what it's worth," Ferrow said, "I read the whole record. Twice. My grandmother was in care in '78. Different county, but." He swallowed. "You did right, Mr. Blackwood. Everyone keeps saying you destroyed the parish. You didn't. You just stopped it from being a museum of a lie."

Elodie thanked him, and meant it, and went back to his office and sat down and shook for ten minutes, because praise lands harder than abuse when you've been braced for abuse. That was a discovery he filed away under *things nobody warns you about*.

The bequest itself dissolved faster than anyone predicted, because money, unlike grief, is efficient. The lawyers moved through the trust like water finding cracks. The restoration fund was frozen pending review. The patronage—the quiet arrangement by which the Blackwood bequest had underwritten half the cathedral's budget since 1979—was terminated by letter, on letterhead, in language so bloodless it might have been describing a cancelled magazine subscription.

On Friday, the roofers stopped work on the south aisle. Their scaffolding stood naked against the old stone, and Elodie found himself staring at it from the graveyard path, thinking: *there it is. There's the price, itemized.* Every slate they didn't lay, every pensioner's grant, every hamper—it was all priced now, all visible, all his.

He noticed, distantly, that he was standing at Verity Marsh's grave. He had no memory of walking there.

"They'd have kept paying," he told the headstone, because Verity had been the sort of woman you could talk to even posthumously; she'd have interrupted if she minded. "Another forty years. Another girl's name in another ledger. That was the alternative."

The stone said VERITY MARSH, BELOVED BENEFACTRESS, and beneath it, smaller: SHE GAVE GENEROUSLY TO THIS PARISH. Elodie looked at the word *gave* for a while. Even in death, the record lied for her. He made a mental note to speak to the stonemason, then wondered if that was madness—exhuming the dead one epitaph at a time—and decided, on balance, that it was exactly the right kind of madness, and left it on the list.

* * *

The losses came in ones and twos, the way teeth go.

Mrs. Penhale stopped speaking to him entirely, which he had expected, and then, three weeks in, left a thermos of soup outside the archive door with a note that said *Don't read anything into this. It's lentil. It needed eating,* which he had not expected, and which he read four times.

Old Mr. Trevanion, the organist, who had taught Elodie to tell a forged signature from a tired one, passed him in the nave without a nod. That one went in deep. He stood by the organ afterward, in the smell of dust and old varnish, and let himself miss the man properly, the way you're allowed to miss someone who has chosen to stop existing for you.

Canon Pryce, by contrast, invited him to sherry on Sunday and spent the entire hour complaining about the scaffolding and saying nothing whatsoever about the scandal, which Pryce clearly regarded as beneath discussion, like weather or the French.

And Sable Okafor came by on a wet Wednesday with two coffees and no warrant, which was, Elodie was learning, her version of tenderness.

"How are the walls?" she asked, looking around the archive, where he had begun, methodically, to pack.

"Standing. For now. The Chapter wants the collection moved to the diocesan repository in Truro." He said it lightly, and she saw through it instantly, because she always did.

"And you don't want it to go."

"It's not mine to want." He wrapped a folio in acid-free tissue as he talked, hands busy so his voice could stay level. "Though I'll note, for the record, that the last time these documents were sent somewhere 'for safekeeping,' four officials certified a living child dead. Archives are only as honest as the people paid to keep them."

Okafor drank her coffee and watched him work for a while. Then: "Any regrets?"

It was the question everyone wanted to ask and almost no one dared. He set down the folio and considered giving her the polished answer—the one about duty and the long arc of the record—but she had sat across from him through a murder investigation; she had earned the unpolished one.

"At three in the morning, sometimes," he said. "I wake up and I've lost Trevanion, and the roof leaks, and Widow Curnow looks through me in the lane, and I think: I had a family. Small and strange and mostly built on a crime I didn't commit—but it held weight. It bore load. And I took a crowbar to it." He turned his teacup a quarter-turn. "And then I get up, and I look at the box of returned letters—that poor girl writing year after year to people who never opened them—and the regret doesn't survive the comparison. It's not that the cost is acceptable. It's that the alternative was never actually available. Silence wasn't neutral. Silence was just the crime, continuing."

Okafor nodded slowly. "My sergeant asked me why I didn't stop you. Leaving her the ledger. Going to the crypt alone." She smiled, briefly, salt-dry. "I told him some doors you can't lock from the outside. You have to let a person walk through and close it behind themselves."

"That's either wisdom or an excuse."

"With me it's usually both." She stood, drained the coffee, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "For what it's worth—the Crown Prosecution Service announced this morning. Two of the surviving certifiers are being charged. Concealment of birth, falsification, conspiracy. It'll be months of misery for everyone involved." Her eyes met his. "They're naming you as the cooperating witness who unsealed the record. Voluntarily. Before anyone compelled you."

"I didn't do it for immunity."

"I know." A beat. "So will the jury. Eventually. Juries are slow but they're not stupid." And she was gone, out into the rain, leaving the coffee cup on his desk precisely centered on the coaster, because she was the sort of person who did that even during the apocalypse.

* * *

The last day of the month, Elodie published the second thing.

It was smaller than the record, and in some ways harder: the ward's letters, all forty-one of them, exhibited online under her true name—Margaret Renfree, called Peggy, called in childhood by the nickname that had finally hanged her enemies. He transcribed each one himself. He catalogued the envelopes, the postmarks, the slow degradation of hope from the first letter (*Dear Sirs, I should like to know if the sea is still grey at Greywater*) to the last (*I enclose my address for the final time. I will not write again. I understand now that I was never lost. I was discarded, and then declared gone, and then paid for*).

Reading them in sequence was like watching a lighthouse go out room by room.

But at the bottom of the exhibition, beneath the last letter, he added a note in his own hand, because the record deserved a custodian's honesty even now:

*These letters were held, unopened, in the archives of Greywater Bay Cathedral for thirty-nine years. They were catalogued by the present archivist in 2011, boxed, shelved, and left undisturbed. The erasure of Margaret Renfree was accomplished not only by the four men who signed her death, but by every hand that passed over her words and did not open them. Mine was among those hands. I publish these letters in her name, not the parish's, and I ask her pardon, knowing it is not mine to give.*

He posted it at dusk. Then he did something he had been putting off for weeks: he walked down to the flower shop on Fore Street, where Margaret Renfree—Peggy, the unassuming arranger who had spent a lifetime watching her murderers prosper—now worked three mornings a week, having declined, with terrifying politeness, to attend either the trial or any further meetings with him.

She was cutting stems when he came in. She didn't look up.

"The exhibition is live," he said. "Your letters. Under your name. I wrote to tell you, but I thought—I owed you the telling in person."

Snip. A rose stem fell. "Did you now."

"There's a note at the bottom. Apologizing. You can take it down if you want. It's your page. Everything on it is yours."

That made her look up. Her eyes were the flat grey of the bay in February, and they studied him the way she had studied him in the crypt, that night between the candle brackets: measuring, remembering, deciding.

"You know what's strange, Mr. Blackwood," she said. "All those years, I hated the men who signed the paper. Simple. Clean. Then you come along, opening boxes, reading my letters, publishing my whole life for the town to gawk at—and I find I hate that too. Because it's still someone else handling my story. Still someone else deciding what the record says." She set down the shears. "But." A long pause, in which the shop clock ticked like a patient heart. "You did open the box. In the end. And you did pay for it. I watched you pay. So." She picked up a white chrysanthemum, turned it in her fingers. "Leave the note. Leave the letters. And leave me be for a while longer. That's not forgiveness. It's just—enough, for now. Enough is more than I ever got from this parish. Learn to live on it."

"I will," Elodie said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Buy a chrysanthemum. I'm running a business, not a shrine."

He bought the chrysanthemum. He carried it home through the blue evening, past the cathedral with its naked scaffolding and its darkened windows, past the noticeboard where someone had already pinned a handwritten flyer: *MEETING THURSDAY — THE FUTURE OF GREYWATER PARISH — ALL WELCOME — TEA PROVIDED.* The tea provision struck him as deeply characteristic. Whatever else Greywater Bay did with its ruin, it would do it with tea.

* * *

Winter came early that year, and the parish grew small the way a fire grows small—down to the coals, down to what would actually burn.

Half the congregation drifted to St. Aldhelm's across the bay. The choir lost its bass section to a schism over whether Elodie was a saint or a vandal, a debate conducted with more heat than harmony. The Chapter appointed an interim administrator who spoke exclusively in the language of sustainability frameworks, and the food pantry was reorganized under a grant from the county council, and Widow Curnow got her Christmas hamper after all—from a different fund, with a different history, costing no one their name. Elodie helped pack it. Nobody thanked him. Nobody spat, either. He counted that as progress and entered it, privately, in whatever ledger he kept behind his ribs.

Some never spoke to him again. He had known they wouldn't. Trevanion never did, though at the carol service—a service Elodie attended in the back row like a ghost auditing its own funeral—the old man played the descant on *Once in Royal David's City* with such ferocity that Elodie understood it was a message, and that the message was *I am still alive, and so are you, and neither of us has forgiven the other, and we will both be here next year.* It was, he reflected, the most Greywater Bay reconciliation available. He accepted it.

And on a raw morning in January, with the archive half-packed and the future officially uncertain, Elodie climbed the tower stairs because the bells needed oiling and the regular ringer had resigned along with everything else, and somebody had to keep the record of small duties going or the whole edifice of the place collapsed into mere stone.

When he came back down, breathless, hands black with grease, Sable Okafor was waiting at the tower door, stamping the frost off her boots, holding a battered folder that was very obviously not an official document, because official documents did not have coffee rings on them.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you. I've been published."

"So I heard." She glanced up the stairwell, then back at him, and there was something in her face he hadn't seen before—or rather, something he had seen only once, in the crypt, at the moment the counterweight lock slammed home: the look of a person about to do something the rules technically permitted and the rules' authors would have hated. "I hear you're between positions."

"I'm between everything. It's very restful."

"Mm." She held out the folder, not quite offering it, not quite withholding it. "Unofficially, then. There's a case from 1987. Drowning. Closed as accident. Except the file's thin—too thin, the way files get when someone's paid for the thinning. And the parish that handled the inquest paperwork was—" she tapped the folder— "this one."

Elodie looked at the folder. Below, through the tower window, the bay lay grey and patient, keeping its ledger. Above, the bells hung silent, waiting to be rung.

He wiped his hands on his trousers, took the folder, and opened it.
