## 13. What Dying Men Confess

The care home sat at the end of Larkspur Lane like a house that had given up pretending to be a home. Wisteria House, the sign said, in cheerful green letters that had faded to the color of old pond water. Someone had planted daffodils along the front path years ago, and someone else had stopped weeding them, and now they grew in a wild yellow riot that was either charming or neglectful depending on how charitable you felt toward institutions.

Juniper had driven past it a hundred times on the night route without once wondering who lived inside. That was the thing about Little Hawley she was only now learning to hate: the town was full of buildings she'd never looked at twice, and every one of them held something.

She parked the cab at an angle that let her see the road—old habit, newer necessity—and sat for a moment with her hands on the wheel.

Dr. Gerald Vane. The name had come out of the dispatch log like a coin from a drain. Forty-one weekly trips to the cliffs over six years, paid in cash by Edith Marsh, and the passenger logged in Margaret Fenn's careful hand as *G.V.* Juniper had spent two days telling herself it could be anyone. Gerald Vane, retired GP, pillar of the parish, the man who had set her arm when she fell out of a tree at nine years old and told her she was braver than most boys and twice as sensible. The man who had signed her mother's death certificate.

*Time of death consistent with drowning. Circumstances accidental.*

He'd written those words with the same pen he'd used to write *Get well soon, little Juniper* on the card he sent after.

She went inside.

* * *

The woman at the reception desk had the particular cheerfulness of people who work in places where nothing cheerful happens. "Visiting hours are until four," she said. "Who are you here to see?"

"Dr. Vane."

Something flickered across the woman's face—not suspicion exactly, but the quick recalculation of someone deciding how much to say. "Are you family?"

"I'm—" Juniper paused. She had rehearsed several answers in the car. Niece. Journalist. District nurse. All of them lies, and all of them flimsy, because Juniper Crane had never learned to lie well; she'd never needed to. Being invisible meant you rarely had to explain yourself. "I knew him. A long time ago. He might not remember me."

"He doesn't remember much of anything these days." The woman's voice softened despite itself. "But he has good days. You can try. Room 9, down the corridor, through the day room. He likes the window seat."

The day room smelled of lavender polish and stewed tea. Half a dozen residents sat arranged around a television showing a program about canal boats, watched with the devotion people usually reserve for church. Nobody looked up at Juniper. For once, she was grateful for it.

Room 9 was at the end of the corridor, the window facing the back garden, which faced the sea. Of course it did. In Little Hawley everything eventually faced the sea, whether you wanted it to or not.

Gerald Vane sat in a wingback chair by the window, wrapped in a cardigan the color of oatmeal, looking smaller than any man had a right to look. He had been a big presence once—broad-shouldered, loud-laughing, the sort of doctor who filled a waiting room with his voice before he ever laid a stethoscope on you. Now his hands lay folded in his lap like two pale crabs, trembling faintly, and his eyes were fixed on the horizon with an expression Juniper recognized from the night shift: the look of someone listening very hard to something no one else could hear.

"Dr. Vane?" she said.

His head turned slowly. The eyes that found her were still sharp. That was the cruelty of whatever was eating him—the body had gone but the eyes hadn't, and behind them something was still working, still watching, still *waiting*.

"You don't know me," Juniper said, pulling the visitor's chair close. "My name is Juniper Crane."

The trembling in his hands stopped. Just stopped, all at once, like a clock being gripped.

"My mother," she said quietly, "was Marion Crane."

For a long moment there was no sound except the radiator ticking and, far off in the day room, the cheerful narrator explaining how locks worked on the Trent and Mersey Canal.

Then Gerald Vane began to cry.

It was not dignified weeping. It was the raw, gasping crying of a man whose defenses had rotted away with everything else, tears running freely down a face that seemed genuinely astonished by them, as though they belonged to someone else entirely. His mouth worked. Sounds came out—vowels mostly, shapeless, urgent—and his hands rose from his lap and grasped at the air between them as if he could pull the words out of the atmosphere by force.

"I know," Juniper said, and heard how gentle her voice was, dispatcher-gentle, the voice she used with panicked callers. "I know you're trying. Take your time. I'm not going anywhere."

*I'm not going anywhere.* How many times had she said that to strangers on the radio? *Stay on the line. I've got you. I'm not going anywhere.* She had said it to Mrs. Trelawney the night her kitchen caught fire. To the boy who called because his father wouldn't wake up. To the woman, three years ago, whose voice had gone quiet and strange mid-sentence, and whom Juniper had kept talking to for eleven minutes while the ambulance crawled out from Whitstable, and who had still died, and whose last word had been Juniper's name, spoken to a stranger she'd never seen.

Vane's hand shot out and seized her wrist. His grip was astonishingly strong. His eyes bored into hers, wet and blazing, and he made a sound that was almost a word—almost *listen*—and then the sounds collapsed again into wreckage.

"It's all right," Juniper said, though it wasn't, though nothing about this was all right, because the man gripping her wrist had written *accidental* on her mother's death certificate and was now weeping at the sight of her daughter as though she were a ghost, or a judgment, or both.

She looked around the room. On the bedside table: a plastic jug of water, a photograph of a younger Vane shaking hands with the mayor, and a small wooden box, the kind pencils come in. Beside the chair, hooked over the armrest, hung a canvas bag with *Property of G. Vane* embroidered on it in thread gone soft with age.

"Can you write?" she asked him. "If you can't speak—can you write?"

His crying slowed. He looked at her with an expression she couldn't read—something between hope and terror—and then nodded, once, hard.

She fetched the bag. Inside: a large-print crossword book, a magnifying glass, a tin of mints, and a fountain pen in a leather sleeve, kept oiled and full. A dying man who maintained his pen. She found paper in the bedside drawer—Wisteria House stationery, cream-colored, the letterhead printed in that same faded pond-water green—and put both in his lap.

Vane stared at the blank page for so long she thought he'd lost the thread. Then he bent over it, and the pen moved, and what came out was slow and shaky and beautiful, a doctor's hand trained in the era when handwriting mattered, each letter formed with painful deliberation:

**I HAVE WAITED FOR YOU**

Juniper's breath left her in a small, controlled exhale. "You wrote me a letter," she said. "Last week. Did you write it yourself?"

His face did something complicated. He shook his head—then stopped, hand hovering, and made a gesture like a man turning a key, then pointing. *Someone else mailed it. I wrote it.* Or possibly the reverse. She couldn't tell. The gestures were a language with most of its grammar missing.

"Who posted it?"

He wrote: **RUTH**

Ruth Marsh. Edith's daughter. The woman at the graveside with guilt all over her face. Juniper filed it away and leaned closer. "Dr. Vane. My mother. Tell me about my mother."

The pen hovered. Trembled. Dropped a blot of ink onto the page like a small black wound.

Then, deliberately, pressing so hard the nib nearly tore the paper:

**SHE WAS THE BEST OF US**

"And you killed her."

It came out flat, without accusation, because accusation would have been a luxury. She needed him answering, not defending. But the effect on him was electric—he flinched as if struck, and his eyes came up to hers, and what was in them was not guilt.

It was fear.

Not the fear of a confessor. The fear of a man hearing footsteps behind him.

"No," Juniper said slowly, working it through aloud, watching his face for confirmation the way she'd once watched tide tables. "You didn't kill her. You were *there*. Or you know who was. And you've been carrying it for twenty years, and Edith Marsh drove you to the cliffs every week so you could sit where it happened, and neither of you could ever say it out loud. Am I close?"

Vane's chest heaved. Tears again, silent now. He pulled the pen back to himself and wrote, in letters that shook:

**IT WAS NOT WHAT THEY SAID**

"What did they say?"

**ACCIDENT. IT WAS NOT AN ACCIDENT.**

"I know," Juniper said. "I proved the tide wrong. She couldn't have drowned where they found her."

He nodded, nodding, nodding, an old man agreeing so hard it looked like prayer. Then he gripped her wrist again, and dragged the pen, and wrote one more line, larger than the rest, the ink gouged deep:

**ASK ALDOUS**

And beneath it, underlined three times until the paper shivered:

**ALDOUS KNOWS**

"Aldous Crane," Juniper said. Her father. The harbor master who had signed the log, closed the file, shaken the constable's hand, and never once in twenty years said his dead wife's name in his daughter's hearing. "You want me to ask Aldous."

Vane collapsed back against the chair, spent, breathing hard, the pen rolling from his fingers onto the blanket. His eyes stayed fixed on hers with desperate urgency, and his lips moved, shaping silent syllables she couldn't parse—until suddenly she could, because some words are shaped simply enough that even ruined mouths make them legible.

*Before. Before. Before.*

"Before what?" she whispered. "Before what happens? Dr. Vane—who else knows? Who else is still alive who knows?"

His hand crept across the blanket toward the pen. Toward the page. He had one more thing in him; she could see it gathering like weather. She picked up the pen and pressed it into his fingers.

He wrote four letters. Slowly. With the last of his strength, it seemed, because when he finished, his hand slid off the paper and hung loose, and his eyes drifted back to the window, to the sea, and the sharpness behind them dimmed like a lamp turned down.

Juniper looked at what he'd written.

**FENN**

Margaret Fenn. Her boss. The woman who signed her rosters and counted the petty cash and had altered a dispatch record twenty years ago and carried it like a stone in her shoe ever since.

* * *

She stayed another ten minutes, but Vane was gone somewhere deep, murmuring to the window about tide times in a year long past. A nurse came in with medication and gave Juniper a look of mild curiosity and no questions, and Juniper thanked her and left.

In the corridor, her phone buzzed. Sam.

*"Where are you?"* His voice had a careful flatness she knew too well. Careful-flat was Sam's code for *bad news, delivered gently.*

"Larkspur Lane. Why?"

A pause. Long enough that she could hear him deciding.

*"There's a car outside the office, June. Been there since noon. Same one that's been following the night route—I got a proper look at the plate today, ran it through a mate at the garage."* Another pause. *"It's registered to Fenn Transport. Margaret's brother's company."*

Juniper stood very still in the corridor of Wisteria House, surrounded by lavender polish and the distant sound of canal boats, and felt the last neutral ground in Little Hawley tilt beneath her feet.

Her boss. Her father. A dying doctor. A frightened Ruth Marsh. And now a constable's car idling outside the taxi office, patient as a spider.

*"June? You still there?"*

"Yes," she said, and her dispatcher's voice came out steady even though her hands weren't. "Sam, listen carefully. Don't go near the car. Lock up early tonight, take the long way home, and call me when you're in. I mean it."

*"What did the old man say?"*

She looked back through the doorway of Room 9, at Gerald Vane murmuring to the sea, at the cream-colored stationery on his bedside table—the same cream, the same faded green letterhead, as the confession sitting in her desk drawer at home.

"He told me who to ask next," she said. "And Sam—it wasn't him. He didn't kill her. He's been trying to confess for twenty years and somebody's been making sure he couldn't."

She hung up and walked out past the daffodils, and did not allow herself to run until she reached the cab.
