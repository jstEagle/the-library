## 3. Thirty Days

The county clerk's office in Saint Alder's smelled of toner and old coffee, and it opened at eight, which meant I was standing at the door at 7:52 like a man waiting for a pharmacy that dispenses something better than antibiotics.

The clerk was a woman named Dot—her nameplate said DOROTHY PELL, but everyone who came in called her Dot, and she corrected no one, which told me she had made peace with the diminutive the way some people make peace with weather. She had reading glasses on a beaded chain and the particular posture of someone who has spent thirty years watching small towns come in to ask for things they'd rather not ask for.

"Villalobos," I said. "I need the case file on the Saint Alder's church fire. 2013. Arson investigation, if there was one."

Dot looked at me over the glasses. It was not a hostile look. It was the look of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

"Insurance?" she asked.

"Investigation."

"Same thing, half the time." She pulled a ledger toward herself, ran a finger down a column. "Fire marshal's office would have that. We'd have the property records, the permits, the—well." Her finger stopped. "You'd want the marshal's office. Out on Route 9."

"I'll go there. But while I have you—property records for the parish. Saint Alder's Catholic Church, the land parcel, and any insurance filings attached to the settlement."

"Settlement's not filed with us. That's between the diocese and the carrier." She said *carrier* the way you'd say *the dog that bit me*.

"Then the property records, and whatever the fire marshal's determination was. Public record?"

"Public record." She was already writing a slip. "You're the one from the city, aren't you. The one the millpond man was wearing."

News travels in small towns at roughly the speed of shame. I confirmed nothing. Dot slid the slip across the counter and held it down with one finger for a beat longer than necessary.

"Route 9," she said. "Ask for the fire marshal. And Mr. Villalobos—" she took her finger off the slip, "—you should know the courthouse closes at four, but the marshal's office, Ruth Calloway keeps it open later. She always has."

"Chief Calloway," I said. "The police chief."

"Same woman. Was fire marshal before they made her chief, after the fire. After everything." Dot straightened the ledger, aligned it with the edge of the counter with two small, precise taps. "She'll want to know why you're asking. Everybody will want to know why you're asking. That's not a warning. That's just the way it is here."

I thanked her and walked out into a morning that couldn't decide whether to be beautiful. Saint Alder's in October: the millpond flat as poured glass, the hills going copper at the edges, the rebuilt chapel's new steeple catching light like something that wanted to be forgiven. A town that had spent eleven years learning to look exactly like a town that had recovered.

I had the file number from the millpond incident report, and by nine-fifteen I was standing in a cinderblock office on Route 9 that smelled of woodsmoke and printer ink, reading a framed certificate on the wall that said RUTH CALLOWAY, FIRE MARSHAL, 2009–2016, and beneath it, in a cheaper frame, CHIEF OF POLICE, TOWN OF SAINT ALDER'S.

She was at her desk, and she was not what I expected, which is a sentence I should have learned to stop writing in my head years ago. I'd built her from the file: a woman who'd signed off on an arson ruling that closed a case that killed a child. I'd built her as either corrupt or broken. What sat at the desk was neither. She was maybe sixty, gray hair cut short and practical, reading glasses identical to Dot's because apparently the county issues them in bulk, and a cardigan the color of wet bark. She looked like someone who had been tired for eleven years and had made a career of not showing it.

"Mr. Villalobos." She said my name before I'd given it, and watched me register that. "The millpond man was wearing your clothes. Small town. You'll get used to it or you'll leave, and most people do one or the other fast."

"Chief Calloway. I'm here about the 2013 fire. The original investigation."

Something moved through her face—fast, controlled, like a fish under ice. "Sit down, Mr. Villalobos."

I sat. There was a window behind her desk with a view of a woodpile and, beyond it, the tree line that ran down toward the pond. She let the silence do some work before she spoke again.

"You're the investigator on the drowning."

"I'm the investigator the carrier sent on the drowning. The drowning has developed complications."

"Complications." She took her glasses off. "The dead man is wearing your clothes, carrying your hymnal—yes, we know about the hymnal—and his face belongs to a boy who's been dead for eleven years. Those complications."

"His face belongs to Daniel Okafor, according to the coroner's preliminary identification."

"According to a photograph on a missing-persons file that should never have been that easy to find," Calloway said. "Yes."

I let that sit. She was ahead of me, or she was performing being ahead of me, and with a woman like this the two were hard to distinguish. "I pulled the arson file this morning," I said. "Or I tried to. The clerk sent me here."

"The arson file is sealed."

"Sealed by whom?"

"By executive order of the county, at the request of the diocese's counsel and your carrier's counsel, jointly, in November 2013. I signed the request as fire marshal. It was the single worst signature of my career, and I've signed death notifications, so the competition is stiff." She folded her glasses and set them on the desk, and her hands were very steady, which I suspected took work. "Why are you asking, Mr. Villalobos? Not why are you here. Why are you *asking*. The drowning is a drowning. Accidental, most likely, or a suicide with a taste for theater. Either way it's a claim file, not an investigation."

"Because someone dressed a dead man in my clothes," I said. "Because they put a hymnal from Saint Alder's choir in his jacket, with an inscription I've seen before. Because the coroner identified a boy who was buried in 2013, and either the coroner is wrong, the burial was wrong, or the boy was wrong. Any one of those is a reason to ask."

Calloway looked at me for a long moment. Then she stood, went to a filing cabinet against the wall, and stood with her hand on the drawer handle without opening it, the way you stand at a door you've decided not to knock on.

"Do you know what a statute of limitations is, Mr. Villalobos?"

"I know what it is. I'm less sure why you're bringing it up."

"Arson. Second degree. Injury resulting." She turned around. "In this state, the clock runs thirty years if someone died. But that's only if it's charged as homicide-adjacent, and it never was. It was charged—well. It wasn't charged at all. It was ruled accidental, pending, and then it was ruled *closed*, and the civil settlement went through, and the criminal clock that's actually running is the fraud clock. Insurance fraud. Three years from the discovery of the scheme by the carrier, or—"

"Thirty days," I said.

I don't know how I knew. I want to tell you it was deduction—the settlement date, the discovery language, the tolling clauses—and some of it was. But some of it was the way she'd said *thirty years* and then stopped, the way her hand had gone to that drawer, the way the whole room had the held-breath quality of a place where a countdown is being lived with.

Calloway didn't look surprised. "The carrier's discovery date was October 14th, 2013. The settlement was executed on that basis. The limitations period on the underlying fraud, as your own company's counsel drafted it—cleverly, I'll grant them—expires thirty days from the anniversary of the settlement's final payment. Which is in thirty days." She sat back down. "After that, Mr. Villalobos, the parish's settlement becomes untouchable. The claim is paid, the release is executed, the diocese is held harmless, and no court in this state will hear a fraud claim on a policy that closed eleven years ago. The fire becomes what everyone has agreed it is: a tragedy. Full stop. Amen."

"And if it's reopened as a criminal matter before then—"

"Then the settlement's indemnity provisions get very interesting, and the diocese's counsel has to decide whether protecting the parish is worth what discovery would cost them." She smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a woman looking at a very old wound. "Which is why, in thirty days, it stops mattering what actually happened in that church. The truth will still be true. It just won't be *actionable*. There's a difference, and this town has been living on that difference for eleven years."

I thought about the hymnal in the dead man's jacket. *For the children of the choir, who sing us into heaven. A. Anselm, 2012.* I thought about a corpse wearing my clothes like a message addressed to me personally, and I thought about what kind of sender chooses a message that can only be read by a man with thirty days to act on it.

"Who told you the timeline?" I asked. "The thirty days. Who's counting?"

Calloway's hand went flat on the desk. "Everyone who signed anything in 2013 is counting, Mr. Villalobos. The diocese is counting. Your carrier is counting. The families who took the settlement are counting, though most of them don't know what they're counting toward. And I—" she looked at the filing cabinet, then away from it, "—I have been counting for eleven years."

"Then give me the file."

"No."

"Chief—"

"Mr. Villalobos." She said it quietly, and the quiet was worse than shouting. "I'm going to tell you something, and I want you to understand that it is not a threat, because I don't make threats, and it is not hostility, because I don't have the energy. It is fear. I am afraid, and I have been afraid since 2013, and I am telling you this because you strike me as a man who respects data, and fear is data."

She leaned forward.

"Eleven years ago, a twelve-year-old boy died in a fire that was set on purpose, in a church insured for three times its worth by an anonymous donor, and every institution that could have said so said something else instead. The diocese said it. The carrier said it. The marshal's office said it—I said it. And the town said it, because the town loved Father Anselm, and because the settlement money fed half the families on the east side for a decade, and because a town can survive a fire but it cannot survive the knowledge that its own priest lit it. So they sealed it. And the people who sealed it are still here. The monsignor is still here. The sexton is still here. The money is still here. And the last two people who tried to pull that thread—one moved away in the middle of the night and one stopped speaking to her own sister. I'm not telling you what happened to them. I'm telling you what I watched happen, and what I've spent eleven years not finding out, because not finding out is a full-time job and I have a town to run."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's a *forecast*," she said. "There's a difference. A warning implies I want you to stop. A forecast means I want you to know the weather." She opened the drawer she'd been guarding—not the sealed file, I could see that, just a drawer of current incident reports—and took out a copy of the millpond report, and slid it across the desk to me. "Here's your drowning. Accidental. Pending coroner's confirmation. That's the official paper, and it's accurate as far as it goes. What's not in it is that the pond was dragged in 2013 for a different reason and the draggers found nothing, and that the pond's depth at the point your man was found is four feet, and a grown man does not drown by accident in four feet of water unless he was put there. So. Not a warning. A forecast."

I took the report. "You're giving me this."

"I'm giving you the weather." She stood, which ended the meeting in the universal language of offices. "Thirty days, Mr. Villalobos. Whatever you're going to do, the clock is real, and it isn't yours alone. There were twelve children in that choir loft the night of the fire. The record says eleven came out. Somebody in this town has spent eleven years making sure nobody asks about the twelfth."

The floor did not move. The room did not tilt. But something behind my sternum shifted its weight, the way cargo shifts on a boat, and I stood up too carefully.

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because you asked why I'm telling you anything." She walked me to the door, opened it onto the smell of woodsmoke and October. "And because you have the look of a man who's going to keep asking whether I tell you or not. I'd rather you asked with the weather report in hand."

I stepped out onto the gravel. Behind me she said, not unkindly:

"Mr. Villalobos. Whatever you find—you should ask yourself why someone wanted *you* to find it. People who stage a body in your clothes aren't hiding from you. They're recruiting you. And I've never known a recruiter to mention the deadline."

* * *

I drove back into town with the incident report on the passenger seat and did what I always do when a case gets under my skin: I recited. It's not a party trick, whatever my mother hoped. It's maintenance. A memory like mine doesn't file things away; it keeps everything at the same temperature, all of it present tense, and the only way to keep the present tense from drowning me is to walk through the rooms of it deliberately, naming what's there.

So I named. The millpond at dawn, mist in bands, the heron that lifted off when the first responder's radio cracked. The dead man's shoes—leather, resoled, left foot worn on the outside edge, a man who walked on cambered roads. My own gray jacket on his shoulders, my own shirt, my own belt, the belt's brass buckle scratched in the lower left quadrant where I'd scratched it myself moving a filing cabinet two winters ago. The hymnal in the inside pocket, dry, which meant it had gone into the jacket after the water, which meant someone had dressed him on the bank or in a boat, which meant time, which meant calm, which meant someone who was not afraid of being seen at the pond at night.

And the inscription. *For the children of the choir, who sing us into heaven. A. Anselm, 2012.*

I had seen that handwriting before. That was the part I kept circling. Not the hymnal—the handwriting. Somewhere in eleven years of claims files and closed cases and sealed envelopes, I had stood in front of that slanted, patient script and read it, and the memory of *where* would not surface. That had happened perhaps four times in my life, and three of those times were grief, and the fourth was the reason I don't drink.

The town was waking up around me as I drove. A woman hosing down the sidewalk in front of the bakery, water smoking off the warm stone. Two men outside the hardware store who stopped talking when my car passed and resumed at a lower frequency. A school bus, and in its side window the reflected steeple of the rebuilt chapel sliding by like a needle being drawn through the town.

I parked by the green and walked, because there was a thing I needed to look at and I look at things better on foot. The parish hall notice board stood at the chapel's edge, cork and plexiglass, layered with eleven years of announcements: bake sales, blood drives, a lost cat from 2019 that someone had never taken down. And in the center, fresh, on paper still white at the edges:

*Eleventh Anniversary Memorial Mass for the Victims of the Saint Alder's Fire. All are welcome. A reception in the parish hall to follow.*

The date was thirty-two days out. Two days *past* the deadline. I stood there doing the arithmetic, feeling it click into place like a tumbler in a lock. Whatever the monsignor intended to mark with that Mass, it was not grief. It was a receipt. You hold a memorial when the account is closed. You feed the town a reception when the last paper is signed and the truth has officially expired.

Someone had planned the town's forgetting on a calendar, and had printed the invitations.

"Beautiful, isn't it."

I turned. An old man was standing by the board with a rake over his shoulder, though there were no leaves yet worth raking. He had the rolled sleeves and sun-cured forearms of a man who worked grounds, and a collar of sweat on a work shirt, and he looked at the notice the way you look at a family photo.

"The memorial," he said. "Monsignor Brandt spares no expense. The town needs it. Eleven years is long enough to grieve, they say. Time to close the book." He smiled at me, and his eyes did not join in. "You're the insurance man. The one from the pond."

"Word travels."

"Small parish." He extended a hand, dry and hard as fence rail. "Hollis. I keep the grounds. Kept them before the fire, too. Kept them the night of it, if you want the whole résumé." He said it lightly, but his hand had not let go of mine, and his grip had a message in it, the way a handshake can. "You'll be wanting to look at the church, I expect. Everyone does, first week. The old one, I mean. The ruin, down by the water. I keep that locked, but—" he released my hand, "—I could show you through, some evening, if you're the respectful type. Father Anselm always said the dead deserve visitors."

"Father Anselm," I said. "I understood he passed."

Something crossed Hollis's face then, quick as the fish under the ice in Calloway's office, and he looked at the memorial notice, and at the steeple, and at me, and he smiled his smile that wasn't one.

"Did you," he said. "Well. Towns keep their own records, Mr. Insurance Man. Ask around. See what the town remembers." He shouldered the rake. "Thirty days is a long time. And it's no time at all."

He walked off across the green, raking nothing, and I stood in front of the notice board with my heart going like a second clock inside the first one, and I understood that I had just been invited somewhere, and threatened somewhere, and that in this town those might be the same gesture.

The dead deserve visitors, he'd said. Father Anselm always said.

I looked at the memorial date again. Thirty-two days. Two days past the expiration. A service timed to bury the last question under flowers and a reception.

I took out my phone and photographed the notice, the cork board, the lost cat from 2019, everything, because I photograph everything, because forgetting is the one thing I have never been able to do and never learned to want.

Then I stood there a moment longer, in the smoke-smelling October light, and let myself think the thought I'd been carrying since the pond, the one that had no business being true and would not leave:

*Twelve children in the loft. Eleven came out.*

And somewhere under the arithmetic of it, in a place deeper than deduction, a voice that might have been memory and might have been invention whispered the beginning of a number, and I could not make out which number it was whispering, and that frightened me more than the dead man in my clothes, more than Calloway's forecast, more than Hollis's handshake.

Because I am a man who forgets nothing.

And for thirty days—maybe less—someone in this town was counting on the one thing I had never once managed:

To forget.

I got in the car. I had a choir to find, and a town full of people who all told the same story in the same words, and somewhere among them, I was certain now, was the one who'd break pattern—the one sentence that didn't match, the loose thread that thirty days would be just enough time to pull.

If it was enough. If they let it be enough.

I turned the key, and behind me the new steeple caught the sun one last time in the rearview, bright as a struck match.
