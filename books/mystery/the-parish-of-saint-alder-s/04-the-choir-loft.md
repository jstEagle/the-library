## 4. The Choir Loft

By nine the next morning I had a list of eleven names, and by ten I understood that Saint Alder's had exactly one story about the fire and everyone in town had memorized it.

I'd gotten the names from Ruth Calloway, who handed them over with the reluctance of a woman surrendering a hostage. "They were kids," she said. "Be gentle." Then she looked at me the way people look at weather reports for hurricanes — not hostile, just braced. "And Mara? Whatever you think you're going to find, remember that this town already grieved once. Don't make it do it twice."

"I'm an insurance investigator, Chief. Grief isn't my department."

"No," she said. "It's everybody's department here. That's the problem."

The first name on the list was Marcus Ellery, who now sold tractors and snowmobiles out of a lot on Route 9. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with the careful handshake of someone who'd learned to be gentle with things smaller than himself. When I told him why I was there — a routine claim review, nothing more, the drowning had raised some procedural questions — he nodded along like a man hearing a sermon he'd heard before.

"You want to know about the fire," he said.

"A little background helps."

He leaned against a John Deere the color of new money and folded his arms, and then he said: "It was the night of the Advent concert. Father Anselm had us practicing late because the bishop was coming. There was smoke in the stairwell and Mr. Hollis came up and got us out the east window. We lost everything inside. The church, the vestments, the records. But we didn't lose each other."

I want to be clear about what happened next, because it's the kind of thing that only matters to a man whose head works like mine. He said those sentences the way you say your own phone number. Not remembered. *Retrieved.* Whole, intact, in order, with the same small pause between *the vestments* and *the records* that a man uses when he's reciting something he was made to repeat until it stopped meaning anything.

"Where were you in the loft?" I asked.

"Third row, alto side."

"Who was next to you?"

Something flickered. Not guilt. More like a man checking a locked drawer he'd been told never to open. "That was a long time ago."

"It was eleven years. You'd have been fourteen. You'd remember."

"Everybody stood where they stood," Marcus Ellery said, and smiled, and the smile was the most rehearsed thing of all. "We didn't lose each other. That's what matters."

* * *

Denise Okonkwo ran the pharmacy. Priya Chandrasekaran taught fourth grade. Tomás Reyes managed the hardware store his father had opened the year the church burned. I sat across counters and kitchen tables from all of them over two days, and I brought a notebook I barely used, because I didn't need it. My memory files things whether I ask it to or not — faces, cadences, the exact grain of a lie repeated.

Here is what Denise Okonkwo said, verbatim, while she rang up a box of children's bandages behind me: *"It was the night of the Advent concert. Father Anselm had us practicing late because the bishop was coming. There was smoke in the stairwell and Mr. Hollis came up and got us out the east window. We lost everything inside. The church, the vestments, the records. But we didn't lose each other."*

Here is what Priya Chandrasekaran said, standing in her classroom after her students had gone, chalk dust still on her sleeve: *"It was the night of the Advent concert. Father Anselm had us practicing late because the bishop was coming. There was smoke in the stairwell and Mr. Hollis came up and got us out the east window. We lost everything inside. The church, the vestments, the records. But we didn't lose each other."*

Tomás Reyes got as far as *the bishop was coming* before his wife called him to the phone, and when he came back he picked up exactly where he'd left off, mid-sentence, at the same word, with the same breath before it.

Four adults. Four separate interviews. Word-for-word identical accounts, down to the rhythm, down to the pause, down to the closing benediction — *we didn't lose each other* — delivered each time with the same soft finality, like a hymn ending on its tonic chord.

Now, I've taken enough statements in my career to know that witnesses converge. Trauma flattens detail; people borrow each other's memories; a town talks and retalks its disasters until the talk wears grooves. If four people had said *there was smoke and we got out*, I'd have thought nothing of it.

But this wasn't convergence. Convergence is approximate. This was *text*. Nobody remembers a sentence that long that precisely unless the sentence was given to them. And nobody gives forty-word statements to traumatized children unless someone very much wants those children saying exactly that and nothing else.

There was another thing, smaller, the kind of thing I notice because I can't help noticing. Every one of them mentioned Mr. Hollis. The sexton. In every account, Hollis was the hand on the stairwell door, the voice in the smoke. A hero, consistently cast. And every one of them skipped over the same stretch of time — between *smoke in the stairwell* and *Hollis came up* there was nothing. No minutes. No screaming, no counting heads, no panic. Eleven children in a burning loft and not one of them remembered being afraid.

Grief has a texture. I've heard enough real grief to know it stumbles. It contradicts itself. It circles back to the wrong details — the smell of the carpet, somebody's shoes, a joke someone made too soon. Real grief is ragged.

This grief was ironed.

* * *

The fifth name on Calloway's list was Teresa Ibarra. Tess. She lived in a narrow blue house on Larkspur Lane with a garden that had gone feral in the way gardens do when their keeper loves them too much to impose order — roses rioting over a fence, tomatoes volunteering in the gravel path. She answered the door with dish soap still on her hands and looked at me for a long moment before letting me in.

"You're the insurance man," she said. "Word travels. It travels fast here because there's nowhere else for it to go."

Her house smelled of rosemary and old paper. On the wall above the sofa hung a framed photograph of a children's choir, maybe thirty kids in white robes, arranged on church steps squinting into sun. I found myself looking at it longer than politeness allowed. Something in it pulled at me the way a hook pulls — not pain, exactly. Pressure. A pressure behind my eyes like a word on the tip of the tongue, except it wasn't a word, it was a whole afternoon somewhere in my own past that wouldn't surface.

"You were in the choir," I said.

"First soprano. Eleven years old." She sat, folded her hands. "You want the statement."

"If you don't mind."

She gave it to me. *It was the night of the Advent concert. Father Anselm had us practicing late because the bishop was coming. There was smoke in the stairwell—*

"—and Mr. Hollis came up and got us out the east window," I finished, without meaning to.

She stopped. Looked at me. For the first time since I'd arrived in Saint Alder's, someone looked at me the way I imagine normal people look at things — actually seeing, actually present, no script between us.

"You know it," she said quietly. "Of course you know it. Everyone knows it. We all say it."

"Do you know why?"

"I know that Father Anselm sat us down in the parish hall two days after, with cocoa nobody drank, and told us that reporters would come, and that what we said needed to be simple, because simple things protect people and complicated things hurt them. He said the truth was too heavy for us to carry and God would carry it for us." Her mouth did something complicated. "I was eleven. I believed him. We all believed him. He was Father Anselm. He'd pulled three of us off a roof once when the gutter gave way. You don't doubt a man like that. You *can't*."

"And now?"

"Now I'm thirty-two and I water my roses and I say the sentence when people ask, because the sentence is load-bearing. You take it out of this town and I honestly don't know what falls." She was quiet a moment. Outside, a crow argued with something in the hedge. Then she said, in a different voice — lower, faster, the voice of a person stepping off a ledge:

"The thing is, there were twelve of us in the loft that night. Not eleven. Twelve."

The room went very still, or I did. "Twelve?"

"In the loft. I counted us every practice — it was a game, Father Anselm let me be the counter because I could count fastest. Twelve robes, twelve stools, twelve names on the roster taped to the wall by the organ." Her hands had come apart in her lap and were gripping each other again. "Afterward there were eleven. And nobody — nobody ever —"

She caught herself. I watched it happen, watched the ledge close back up under her feet, watched the shutters come down behind her eyes with an almost audible click. She stood, smoothed her skirt, and became a hostess again.

"More coffee?" she asked.

"Mrs. Ibarra—"

"Tess." She carried my cup to the kitchen anyway, though it was full. "You should go. Thank you for coming. I'm sorry I couldn't be more helpful. We lost everything inside, but we didn't lose each other."

The last sentence came out flat as a receipt. She knew it. I knew she knew it. Neither of us said anything else, because some sentences, once you hear them as sentences, can never be un-heard, and we had both just heard hers.

At the door she stopped me with a hand on my sleeve. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested.

"The twelfth child," she said, so quietly I had to lean in. "Everyone says there were eleven because eleven is what the list says. But lists are written by people, and people—" She stopped herself again, jaw tightening, and this time the catch looked less like fear than fury, fury at her own mouth for running ahead of her. "Go home, Mr. Villalobos. Please. Before you make them nervous."

"Make who nervous?"

She looked past me, down Larkspur Lane, toward the corner where a gray sedan sat idling at the stop sign with no particular destination visible in any direction. It had been there when I arrived. I hadn't thought anything of it. Towns have cars.

"They'll tell you the same sentence," Tess said, releasing my sleeve. "Everyone will always tell you the same sentence. That's how you'll know it's true — that's what they'd want you to think. But a sentence everybody knows by heart isn't a memory anymore. It's a wall. And walls get built for a reason."

* * *

I walked back toward the center of town the long way, past the millpond, because the long way was also the way that avoided the corner with the sedan, and I'm cautious, not brave. Cautious men live longer and write better reports.

The pond lay flat and pewter-colored under a low sky, holding the clouds upside down. Somewhere under that surface, forty-eight hours ago, a dead man had been wearing my clothes. I stood at the rail and let my mind do what it does — pull the file open unbidden, spread the photographs across the inside of my skull. The drowned man's face. The hymnal in his jacket, water-swollen, its cover stamped with a gold cross and, beneath the cross, an inscription in faded ink that the lab was still trying to lift.

*For the little singers, who keep the light. A.*

A. Anselm. Everything in this town led back to a priest who'd been dead or gone for a decade, and to a sentence spoken by five mouths in perfect unison, and to a number that wouldn't hold still.

Eleven children escaped the choir loft. That was the official arithmetic, the arithmetic of the sentence, the arithmetic the whole parish recited like grace before meals.

*THERE WERE TWELVE OF US IN THE LOFT THAT NIGHT.*

Tess had said it like a woman setting down something heavy she'd been carrying for eleven years, and then snatched it back up before anyone could see what it was. And the look on her face when she'd said it — that wasn't rehearsal breaking down. Rehearsal doesn't break down into *truth*. It breaks down into confusion, into *I don't recall*, into the flat safety of *that was a long time ago.*

What broke in Tess Ibarra's kitchen was the opposite of forgetting. It was remembering, finally, out loud, to a stranger, in daylight — and the terror on her face afterward wasn't the terror of a liar caught. It was the terror of a witness who has just remembered what happens to witnesses.

I turned once, casually, and checked the road behind me. Empty. The sedan was gone, or had moved, or had never meant anything at all.

But as I walked, I found myself doing something I almost never do, something that frightens me more than any staged drowning or rehearsed testimony ever could.

I was trying to remember being twelve years old. And reaching for it — reaching the way you reach for a stair in the dark and find one missing — I felt, for the first time in my life, the floor of my own memory give slightly, a hairline crack running through a year, through a county, through a summer I could not see at all.

Somewhere behind me, faint across the water, a church bell rang the hour. I counted the strokes without meaning to.

Twelve.
