## 10. Smoke Memory

I have a rule about churches, the same way some people have rules about dogs or elevators: I don't go in unless something is on fire. In Saint Alder's, historically, that has not been a limiting condition.

The funeral was for Edith Marchetti, eighty-nine, who had outlived two husbands, one bakery, and apparently every person in town willing to say an unkind word about her. Calloway told me about it at the diner, not looking up from her coffee. "Half the parish will be there. Including people you want to watch."

"Watching is my whole skill set," I said.

"That's what worries me." She stirred sugar into coffee she never drank. "You'll be in a room full of grieving people, Villalobos. Try to be furniture."

Furniture. Eleven years ago someone had made me furniture — moved me out of Saint Alder's like a chair nobody wanted to explain — and now the chief of police was asking me to do it voluntarily. There's a joke in there somewhere. I didn't laugh.

The rebuilt chapel of Saint Alder's stood where the old one had burned, same footprint, newer bones. Limestone that hadn't earned its stains yet, a steeple that looked like it had been assembled from a kit with one screw left over. They'd kept the name and the bell and nothing else. Grief does that. It rebuilds around the absence and calls the absence sacred.

I sat in the last pew because the last pew is the investigator's natural habitat — close to the door, close to the light, far from anyone who might want to make conversation about the weather or the dead.

* * *

The church filled the way water fills a bowl: quietly, completely, from every side. I catalogued without meaning to. It's not a choice, the memory. People think photographic memory is a party trick, a superpower, a thing you'd trade for. Mostly it's a filing system you can't shut off. Every face in that chapel got indexed: forty-one adults, nine children, Monsignor Brandt in white and gold at the altar moving through the liturgy with the smooth economy of a man who had performed grief so many times it had worn a groove in him, like a stair worn by feet.

Tess Ibarra was there. Fourth pew, left side, hands folded so tightly her knuckles had gone the color of candle wax. She didn't sing. I noted that too. Everyone else sang — badly, warmly, the way small towns sing, half a beat behind the organ — but Tess's mouth stayed closed, and her eyes stayed on the altar, and her breathing had the deliberate rhythm of a woman counting her way through something.

Brandt began the Communion hymn. The organist, an elderly man with the posture of a question mark, drew the first chords out slow, and the acolyte came down the aisle swinging the censer, and that's when the smell reached me.

Incense. Frankincense and something sweeter underneath, benzoin maybe, resin burning the way resin burns when —

When.

I want to tell you what happened next the way it actually happened, which is to say: not as a memory at all. Not as pictures. The heat came first, and it did not come from the censer.

* * *

Heat has a texture. That's the thing nobody tells you. Cold you can describe — needles, knives, a hand closing. Heat is a pressure, a weight against your whole body at once, air turned solid. It pressed on me in the fourth row of Saint Alder's while a choir of ghosts sang somewhere above, and I was twelve years old, and I was standing in the choir loft of a church that no longer existed, and the floor under my feet was warm.

Not burning. Warm. The way a stove is warm before it's hot, the way a lie is warm before it catches.

There were children around me. Robes. White collars gone gray at the edges. Someone was crying two rows back, a small wet hiccupping sound, and a boy's voice said, *not yet, don't go yet,* and the voice was calm in the specific way that only terrified people are calm, the calm of someone holding a door closed with their shoulder.

Smoke threaded along the ceiling like ink dropped in water. Below us, through the loft rail, I could see the nave — candles, pews, the gold thread of light — and the smoke was coming from beneath the stairs. Under the stairs. Which meant whoever set it knew the loft would fill before anyone downstairs even smelled it. Which meant either they were stupid or they thought the loft was empty.

A hand closed around my wrist.

Small hand. Strong. A kid's hand, gripping with everything a kid has, which is more than adults remember. And a voice — singing. Singing. Of all the things a voice could do in a burning building, this voice chose the hymn, the one we'd rehearsed for the anniversary service, *Abide With Me*, pitched low and steady, and the other children took it up because children follow sound the way iron follows a magnet, and the crying stopped, and we moved, all of us, down the stairs in a line, singing, into smoke that tasted like burnt sugar and pennies.

The hand let go of my wrist at the bottom of the stairs.

I looked back. I have looked back ten thousand times since, in the dark behind my eyes, and here is the cruelty of a perfect memory: I could see him. I could see him turning around, going back up, going back for the last ones, the hem of his robe catching the orange light, and he was still singing, and then the loft went the color of the inside of a furnace and the hymn stopped mid-phrase, mid-word, and —

"Mara."

Someone was shaking my arm. "Mara. Hey. Hey."

* * *

I came back to myself in the last pew of Saint Alder's with my heart slamming and my shirt stuck to my back and Tess Ibarra's hand on my forearm, her face very close to mine, wearing an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

"You went somewhere," she said quietly.

The recessional was playing. The congregation was filing out around us, and not one of them had noticed the man in the back pew come apart, which tells you something about funerals, or about me. My hands were shaking. I watched them shake with the detached interest of a man reading someone else's police report.

"I need to ask you something," I said. My voice came out sanded flat. "The night of the fire. The choir loft. How many kids went down the stairs?"

Her hand withdrew slowly, like she was pulling it out of a trap.

"You know how many," she said.

"Eleven escaped. That's the number in every account. Eleven children led out of the loft by Daniel Okafor." I heard myself say the next part before I decided to say it, which happens sometimes; the mouth files its report before the mind signs off. "Someone else led us down, Tess. Someone small. Someone who went back up."

The color left her face in a single tide. She looked at me the way you look at a grave you thought was empty.

"Don't," she whispered. "Don't do this here."

"Do what? Remember?"

"*Remembering is the dangerous part.*" Her voice cracked on the second word, and she caught it, pressed it flat, glanced toward the altar where Brandt was shaking hands and smiling his groove-worn smile. "Mara. Listen to me. Whatever you think you saw — whatever just happened to you — there are people in this town who have spent eleven years making sure it stays a story with a beginning, a middle, and an end. Eleven children. One hero. One accident. You start pulling threads out of that—"

"Tess." I leaned closer. "Who taught you the words?"

She stood up so fast the kneeler clattered. Around us, the last stragglers turned. She smoothed her coat with both hands, restored her face to funeral-neutral in a feat of control I genuinely admired, and said, loudly enough for the stragglers, "Take care of yourself, Mr. Villalobos. Terrible thing, losing Edith. Such a fixture."

And then, as she passed behind me into the aisle, so quietly it barely qualified as sound, so quietly that a lesser memory would have lost it:

"The stairs were on the north side. You wouldn't know that unless you'd been up there."

Then she was gone, swallowed by black coats and the bright doorway.

* * *

I sat in the emptying church for a long time after everyone left. Brandt extinguished the candles one by one, working his way toward me with the unhurried patience of a man tidying his own house, and I used the time to take inventory of what had come back, because inventory is how I keep from drowning.

The heat. The smoke under the stairs — meaning arsonist's knowledge of airflow, meaning intent. The hand on my wrist. The hymn. The robe hem going back up the stairs. The word the hymn died on: *fast.* *Change and decay in all around I see; O Thou who changest not, abide with—* He never finished it. Somewhere in the last eleven years, my mind had filed that unfinished phrase under *miscellaneous childhood noise*, the way you file a scar under *old injury, cause forgotten*. Now the file had a label, and the label was a name I didn't have yet.

Eleven years I'd carried that afternoon around like a stone sewn into my coat, and I'd never once felt its weight, because that's the trick trauma plays on a memory like mine. People assume I can't forget. Nobody warns you that forgetting isn't the only way to lose something. You can lose a thing to a locked drawer. You can lose it to a decision your eight-year-old — twelve-year-old — self made without consulting you: *this hurts too much, put it away, put it away, put it away.*

The drawer wasn't locked anymore. And everything in it smelled like smoke.

"Monsignor." I stood. My legs held, mostly. "Beautiful service."

Brandt paused, candle snuffer in hand, and gave me the smile he kept for civilians. "Mr. Villalobos. Edith would have appreciated the turnout. She baked the communion bread for thirty years, you know. We're using her recipe today."

"Is that right." I looked at the altar, the flowers, the gold. "Funny. All these years, and the church still smells the same."

Something crossed his face — fast, professional, gone — the flicker of a man checking a lock he believed was bolted. "Incense is timeless," he said pleasantly. "It's rather the point of it."

"It's not the incense." I picked up my coat. "It's what's underneath it. Resin burns sweet before it burns black. You'd know that if you'd ever been in a fire."

He didn't answer. He didn't need to. The silence between us had a temperature, and it was rising.

Outside, the evening had gone the blue-gray of a bruise healing. I walked down the hill toward town with the hymn running in my head on a loop I couldn't stop, and I made myself a promise with the particular ferocity of a man who has just learned his own mind was robbed: I was going to finish the verse. I was going to find the name that belonged to the hand on my wrist, the voice that kept singing, the boy who went back up those stairs — and I was going to carve it somewhere no one could ink it out.

My phone buzzed against my chest. Calloway.

"You need to get down to the millpond," she said, and her voice had none of the diner's dryness in it. None of anything. Just a flat, careful blankness I'd heard once before, eleven years ago, over a radio in a county I couldn't remember living in.

"Why?" I said. "What's at the millpond?"

A pause. Long enough to be its own sentence.

"It's Tess Ibarra's car," she said. "Nobody's seen Tess since the funeral."
