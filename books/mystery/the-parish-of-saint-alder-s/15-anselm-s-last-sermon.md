## 15. Anselm's Last Sermon

The care facility was called Meadowbrook Assisted Living, which was the kind of name that sounded like it had been focus-grouped to death — meadow, brook, assisted. Nothing about it suggested that a man who had once commanded a congregation of four hundred and gotten away with murder, or something adjacent to murder, was currently being helped into cardigans in room 114.

I signed the visitor log as *M. Villalobos, insurance* because lying on paper felt more honest than lying out loud. The nurse at the desk was young, kind-eyed, and professionally immune to charm.

"He has good days and bad days," she said. "Today started good. He thinks it's 2014."

"Is that better or worse than thinking it's now?"

She considered this with genuine seriousness. "For him? Better. In 2014 nobody had died yet." Then she caught herself, or caught me, and her kindness developed a hairline fracture. "You're not family."

"I'm asking about the fire at Saint Alder's. Eleven years ago."

The fracture widened. "Everyone who comes here asks about that fire," she said quietly. "The choir women bring flowers. A police officer came twice. And last month a man from the diocese came and told us Father Anselm is not to discuss parish business with visitors. Doctor's orders, he said. Except he wasn't a doctor."

"What did he look like?"

"Tall. Gray at the temples. Very polite." She looked at her hands. "He left a donation. Two thousand dollars. For his *concern*, he said."

Monsignor Brandt, paying for silence the way other men paid for parking. I thanked her, and she let me through anyway, which told me something about the relative strength of two thousand dollars versus one tired nurse's conscience.

* * *

Room 114 smelled of lavender and old paper. Father Anselm Reyes sat in a chair by the window with a breviary open upside down in his lap, reading it with total conviction, his lips moving through psalms in an order that no liturgy had ever sanctioned. He was smaller than the man in the photographs — the photographs I'd studied for three days, the broad-chested shepherd of Saint Alder's with the laugh lines and the championship softball trophies — but the eyes were the same: dark, quick, and still, even now, running calculations.

"Father Anselm?" I said.

He looked up. For a moment nothing happened behind his face, and then something did — a flicker, like a pilot light catching.

"You're one of mine," he said.

"I'm sorry?"

"One of my boys. From the loft." He patted the arm of the empty chair beside him. "Sit. You have the look. All you choir children grew up with the look — like you're waiting for the downbeat."

I sat. My heart was doing something unprofessional. "Do you know my name?"

"Names are the first thing the tide takes," he said, with a small smile that was either humility or strategy. "Faces stay longer. Give me a minute." He studied me, and I studied him back, and I realized with a cold, clinical clarity that we were both doing the same thing: searching each other's faces for information the other might not want to give.

"I'm Mara Villalobos," I said finally. "I investigate fires. Old ones."

The smile didn't move but everything behind it did. "Ah," he said. "Then you're not one of mine after all. Or you're the worst kind of one of mine." He closed the upside-down breviary. "Ask your questions, investigator. I'll answer what I can. Some days the vault opens and some days it doesn't, and neither of us chooses which."

"When did the church's insurance policy change?"

He didn't flinch. That was the first surprise. Guilty men flinch; men whose guilt has been worn smooth by eleven years of dementia don't bother.

"In September," he said. "Two thousand thirteen. Triple coverage, contents rider added, the works. I signed the application myself."

"Who arranged it?"

"A donor. Anonymous. Wonderful man." His voice took on the warm cadence of the pulpit, automatic as breathing. "He loved that church. He said it deserved to be protected."

"Protected how?"

Anselm turned to the window. Below, a groundskeeper was pushing a leaf blower across a lawn that had no leaves worth blowing, pure ritual motion. "You know what a sermon is, Mr. Villalobos?"

"It's what you're about to give me, I suspect."

"A sermon is a story you tell so that people will do what needs doing without realizing they've decided to do it." He folded his hands on the breviary. "I gave sermons for thirty-one years. I was very good. And then the roof failed, and the diocese said there was no money, and I stood in that beautiful dying building every Sunday watching plaster fall on the heads of children, and I asked God for a miracle, and God sent me a man with a checkbook instead. Which should have told me something about how my prayers were being routed."

"The anonymous donor."

"He came to me after Mass. Knew my homily. Quoted it back to me, better than I'd preached it. He said, 'Father, buildings burn. It's the oldest form of renewal the Church knows. Constantinople burned. Rome burned. Chicago rose from it.' And he said the insurance would rebuild Saint Alder's finer than before, and the premiums were already paid, and all I had to do was be elsewhere that night." Anselm's voice stayed level, gentle, terrible. "He made it sound like providence with paperwork."

"Did you know children would be in the building?"

The question landed and for a moment the lucidity wavered — his gaze slid toward the window, toward some middle distance where 2013 and whatever year his mind preferred were superimposed.

"There was never supposed to be anyone in the building," he said. "That was the whole design. Wednesday night. Empty. The sexton was to set it in the sacristy, slow and low, smoke damage mostly, enough for the adjuster. I scheduled the choir rehearsal for Thursday. I moved it myself. I announced it myself." His hands tightened on the breviary. "But someone changed the schedule back. Someone put the children in that loft on Wednesday night, and I have spent eleven years not knowing whether it was me — whether I moved it and then un-moved it and forgot, whether the sin is mine twice over — or whether someone wanted witnesses in the building, because a fire with children in it gets settled fast and quiet and nobody audits anything ever again."

The room was very quiet. Downstairs, someone's television laughed at something.

"Who set the fire, Father?"

And here Anselm Reyes, architect of fraud, author of the rehearsed grief of half a town, looked at me with an expression I recognized from the mirror — the expression of a man whose memory has a hole in it and knows exactly where the hole is shaped.

"Hollis," he said. "Walter Hollis. My sexton. Twenty-two years ringing that bell and tending those graves. He loved the building more than anyone alive. I told him it was mercy — that we were putting the old girl down gently instead of letting the diocese strip her for salvage. He cried when he agreed. He shook my hand on it." A pause. "He believed the loft was empty. I need you to write that down. Whatever else is true, he believed the loft was empty. If he hadn't believed that, he'd have gone in himself. He went in anyway, afterward. To search. They pulled him off the steps."

My pen had stopped moving. "Hollis. Is he—"

"Still employed," Anselm said, and now there was something new in his voice, something that cut through the fog like a bell through weather. Sharp. Deliberate. "That's the part I remember best, investigator, because it's the part I'm most ashamed of. When they rebuilt — when the insurance money built that bright little chapel with the padded kneelers — the diocese kept him on. Grounds and maintenance. He's been cutting the grass around the new building for eleven years, mowing over the ashes, as it were. Nobody questioned it. Nobody questions the sexton. The sexton is furniture." His dark eyes fixed on mine. "Furniture sees everything. Furniture hears every meeting. And furniture stays exactly where it's put."

* * *

He faded after that, the way a tide goes out — not all at once but in stages, each sentence shorter than the last, until he was reading the upside-down breviary again and calling me by the name of a boy named Peter who had apparently sung descant in 1998.

I sat with him another twenty minutes anyway. Partly tradecraft: lucid fragments return, sometimes, if you wait. Partly something else.

Near the end of the second hour, without looking up from the book, he said: "Twelve voices."

I went still. "What?"

"The roster said twelve. Everyone says eleven escaped. Eleven statements, eleven signatures, eleven identical little sentences." He turned a page that was upside down. "A good pastor counts his flock, Mr. Villalobos. I counted them every Sunday. Twelve." He looked at me then, and his eyes were clear as December. "I never told anyone about the twelfth. Not the diocese, not the chief, not the man with the checkbook. Because whoever inked out that name wanted him forgotten, and I thought — God forgive me — I thought maybe forgetting was a kind of protection. For the boy. Or for whoever he grew up to be."

"Father—"

"Go find my sexton," he said, and lay the breviary face-down on his lap like a man concluding a service. "And Mr. Villalobos? Be careful how you ask him. Men who love buildings will forgive you for burning them. But men who love buildings do not forgive you for making them kill children. He's carried that for eleven years, and a man carrying a thing like that is either ready to set it down—" he smiled, and it was the pulpit smile, warm and hollow and true all at once, "—or ready to swing it."

* * *

In the parking lot I called Calloway from the car and got her voicemail, which meant she was either busy or avoiding me, and given our recent history the odds were roughly even. I tried Tess Ibarra next. Voicemail. I stood in the flat white afternoon light of the Meadowbrook lot and felt the shape of the day change under me.

Because here was the arithmetic, and I am very good at arithmetic:

Brandt had visited this facility a month ago and paid two thousand dollars to wall it off. Which meant Brandt knew Anselm could talk. Which meant Brandt knew what Anselm knew. And Anselm had just handed me, free of charge, the one name that connected every thread I'd been pulling: the man who set the fire, the man who believed the loft was empty until it wasn't, the man who had spent eleven years as furniture in the diocese's front hall, hearing everything, staying exactly where he was put.

The man who had changed the schedule back, maybe. The man who had put twelve children in a loft above a fire he'd set believing it empty — or the man who'd discovered too late what his employer had done, and been bought with continued employment and shared silence.

Either way, Walter Hollis was the fulcrum. Either way, he was the only living soul besides Anselm who could swear to the scheme in words a prosecutor could use — and Anselm's testimony was dissolving daily, admissible nowhere, durable only in rooms like 114.

I checked the parish website on my phone. There he was, in the staff directory, between the music minister and the office manager: *Walter Hollis — Facilities & Grounds*. A thumbnail photo. A lean man in his sixties with a long face and careful eyes, holding pruning shears like a man who knew exactly what tools were for.

His listed hours ended at five. It was 4:40.

Saint Alder's was fifteen minutes away, and the cemetery behind the ruined church was where sextons go when the day's work thins out — I'd learned that much about parishes in the past two weeks, learning everything too late and always one step behind the people who'd had years to arrange the board.

I started the car. Somewhere behind me, in room 114, an old man was preaching to a congregation of no one, and for the first time in eleven years the truth of Saint Alder's had been spoken aloud in daylight by a voice that had been there when it was made.

By nightfall, everyone would know he'd talked. The question — the only question — was whether I reached the sexton before the sexton's employers reached him.

Or before he decided, on his own, that eleven years of carrying it was quite enough.
