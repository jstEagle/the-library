## 2. A Parish That Keeps Its Records

The bus to Marsh Hollow ran twice a day and apologized for both.

Bram Thackeray sat in the last seat with her coat folded over her knees and watched the Fens unspool beyond the fogged glass—black ditches stitched with silver, pollarded willows standing like patient witnesses, a heron motionless in the shallows with the stillness of something that had learned long ago not to be seen until it chose to be. The road was raised above the fields on an embankment of old clay, and on either side the water lay so flat and so dark that the sky seemed less reflected than admitted, grudgingly, into the land's company.

She had left the Fenland Grand in the hands of the afternoon porter, which was itself a small act of faith she did not enjoy making. But Maren Vane had paid a retainer that made faith affordable, and more than that, she had said a thing Bram kept turning over like a stone found in a pocket: *Nobody there will tell you what happened to my sister. They'll tell you what they've decided happened.*

Decided. Not remembered. Decided.

The bus sighed into Marsh Hollow at twenty past three, outside a post office that might once have been a chapel. Bram stepped down onto pavement scrubbed cleaner than any village pavement had a right to be, and her first thought—uncharitable, professional—was that the place smelled of soap. Not the honest soap of a working parish, lye and mud and wool, but something finer, lavender-adjacent, deliberate. As though someone had gone down every lane with a cloth.

She stood a moment at the crossroads and let the village look at her.

It did. That was the second thing she noticed. In most places a stranger is invisible for a full day; here, curtains shifted in a rhythm too coordinated to be coincidence, a man repainting a gate paused mid-stroke to take her measure, two women by the bakery ended their conversation—not hushed it, *ended* it—and smiled at her with the pleasant blankness of people who have agreed in advance how to receive visitors. The smiles were warm. The eyes behind them were doing arithmetic.

"Good afternoon," Bram said to no one in particular, and began to walk.

* * *

Marsh Hollow was beautiful the way a display case is beautiful.

The high street curved along the remnant of an old drainage cut, now dry and planted with box hedge, and every building along it wore its history like a pressed uniform. Black timber, white render, roses trained with geometric patience. There was not a leaning fencepost, not a bucket left out, not a single honest imperfection. Bram had worked enough grand hotels to know maintained decay when she saw it—the artful patina, the rot encouraged only where it looked picturesque. This was different. Nothing here was decaying at all. It was as if the village had been photographed in 1953 and then carefully prevented from continuing.

At the corner of the churchyard stood a garden.

A low iron gate, painted green. A plaque: **THE GARDEN OF REMEMBRANCE — ERECTED IN GRATEFUL MEMORY OF THE FLOOD OF 1947 AND THOSE WHO KEPT THE PARISH.** Inside, beds of white flowers arranged in concentric rings around a stone basin where water moved without sound, fed by some hidden pipe, circulating endlessly and going nowhere. Bram counted the rings. Seven. She would learn later that there were seven names read aloud at every anniversary dinner, one per ring, but standing there she knew only that the garden was the tidiest thing she had ever seen and that tidiness, at this concentration, stopped being virtue and became statement.

Someone had deadheaded every spent bloom. Recently. She could see the clean angle of the cuts.

"You'll want the church first."

Bram turned. An elderly man stood at the gate with a watering can he was not using, positioned as though he had been waiting for her, which perhaps he had; word of a stranger travels faster than buses in flat country.

"The church?" she said pleasantly.

"For Elspeth Vane." He nodded toward the lychgate. "Candles were lit for her. Eleven months now. Terrible business. Lovely girl." He said it in the cadence of a lesson recited. "Lovely girl. Everyone says so."

"Do you say so?"

He blinked, and for half a heartbeat something moved behind his face—surprise, or its cousin, offense—that anyone should ask him to produce the feeling rather than report it. "Everyone says so," he repeated, gently, as if correcting her pronunciation, and went back to not watering anything.

* * *

Inside St. Botolph's the air was cold wax and old stone, and there, as promised, candles burned before a side altar beneath a small framed photograph. Bram studied the photograph longer than she studied the altar.

Elspeth Vane had been thirty-one when she vanished, according to Maren. The woman in the frame looked younger, or perhaps only unfinished—a narrow face, dark hair pinned back with a severity that suggested it was always escaping, and eyes that looked slightly off-camera, as though the photographer had called her name and she had declined to answer it. Her mouth held the particular set of someone listening to a speech they intend to contradict.

Below the photograph, on a card in careful copperplate: *In our hearts and in our records.*

*Our records.* Bram wrote the phrase in her notebook and underlined it without knowing why. Underlining things without knowing why was, she had found, how her best work usually started.

"Miss Thackeray."

She turned. The man approaching down the aisle was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, dressed in a tweed jacket whose elbows had been rewoven rather than replaced—an economy practiced by a man who could afford otherwise and chose the appearance of care instead. He extended a hand dry and cool as paper.

"Aldous Crane. Parish historian. You're the sister's investigator—I'm told you'd come." He smiled. "Word travels. We are a parish that keeps its records, Miss Thackeray. Including, lately, records of you."

"I only arrived on the bus."

"And the bus arrived at twenty past three," Crane said, "and Mrs. Petchy at the bakery telephoned the vestry at twenty-four past. I don't say it as a warning." His smile widened by a precise millimeter. "I say it as a compliment to the parish. Nothing happens here unwitnessed. I have always considered that our greatest protection."

*Or yours,* Bram thought, and said, "Then you'll know why I've come better than I do."

"Elspeth Vane." He said the name the way other men said scripture, with the small reverent pause of a text long since settled. "Secretary of the Anniversary Dinner Society for six years. Organized the flower rota for the Garden of Remembrance. Reliable, punctual, devoted to this community." He gestured at the photograph. "Her loss was felt by every soul in Marsh Hollow."

"You knew her well?"

"I curated her," Crane said, and laughed at himself, a light self-deprecating sound that did not quite reach the architecture of his face. "Forgive me. Historian's joke. I knew her as we all knew her—through her work, through her minutes, through her contributions. She was exact. She never missed a meeting. She minuted everything." He paused, and something flickered there, brief as a fish under ice. "Everything."

"Did she have enemies? Disagreements? Anyone who—" Bram spread her hands. "Anyone who might have wanted her gone?"

Crane regarded her with mild, unhurried interest, the way a man regards weather arriving on schedule. "Miss Thackeray, this is a parish of three hundred and forty souls. We have disagreements the way a family does—over hedges and hymnals. But *wanted her gone*?" He shook his head slowly. "No. What happened to Elspeth was the fen. The fen takes people. It has always taken people. Ask anyone."

*I am asking,* Bram thought. *That's rather the problem.*

* * *

She spent the rest of the afternoon asking, and the answers assembled themselves into a shape she did not like.

At the bakery, a woman named Petchy praised Elspeth's handwriting. At the pub, a landlord praised her punctuality. At the shop, a boy who must have been fifteen when she vanished—he would have been nineteen now, nearly a man—praised her kindness in helping him with his reading. Each account was warm, fluent, and perfectly smooth, like stones from a riverbed, and Bram could not crack a single one open. She tried the techniques that had served her across a hundred hotel corridors: the sudden specific question (*What did she drink?*), the deliberate error (*I hear she argued with the vicar*), the long silence after an answer, waiting for the truth to rush into the gap.

*What did she drink?* — *Oh, whatever was poured, she wasn't fussy.* (From a woman who had just described, unprompted, the exact variety of rose Elspeth planted.)

*I hear she argued with the vicar* — *Never. Never once. She got on with everyone.* Said instantly, warmly, and with the faintest tightening at the corners, the look of a person repeating a line they have been given rather than a memory they own.

And the silence—she used the silence four times, and four times it was filled, smoothly, by someone changing the subject to the flood, to the garden, to the dinner. Always, eventually, to the dinner. The centenary dinner, coming in October, one hundred years since the night the sluices failed and the old world drowned. The whole parish orbited that date like moons around a planet none of them had ever seen clearly.

By five o'clock Bram stood again at the Garden of Remembrance, notebook heavy with praise, and realized she had filled eleven pages and learned nothing whatsoever about the actual woman. She knew Elspeth Vane was lovely, reliable, kind, punctual, devoted, and missed. She did not know whether she had laughed, or what at. Whether she was lonely. Whether she had ever, even once, in eleven months of being mourned by three hundred and forty souls, been *known* by one.

Every witness had handed her a portrait, and every portrait was the same portrait, and the same portrait is another way of saying no portrait at all.

The water in the stone basin went round and round, silent, going nowhere.

* * *

She found Crane again at dusk, in the vestry of the church, which was not a vestry at all but an archive.

She had not been invited; she had simply followed the light. And the light came from a room lined floor to ceiling with ledgers—green, black, oxblood—spines labeled in the same copperplate as the card beneath Elspeth's photograph. Baptisms, burials, drainage boards, school rolls, society minutes, a century and a half of Marsh Hollow bound and shelved and dusted. A lamp burned on a central table beside a pair of white cotton gloves laid out with surgical neatness, awaiting use.

Crane stood among his books with his jacket off and his sleeves rolled, and he did not seem surprised to see her, because she suspected Aldous Crane was constitutionally incapable of being surprised by anything that occurred inside his parish.

"You keep all of this yourself?" Bram asked.

"Since 1981." He touched a spine without quite touching it, the way a believer touches a reliquary rail. "Paper is patient, Miss Thackeray, but it is not forgiving. It remembers everything and forgives nothing. Most people find that frightening." He looked at her over the lamplight. "You strike me as someone who finds it comforting."

"I find it honest," Bram said. "People edit. Paper doesn't."

"No," Crane agreed softly. "People edit." He said it with such quiet satisfaction that the words seemed less like agreement than like a doctrine. "That is precisely why a parish must keep its records. Memory drifts, Miss Thackeray. Memory is sentimental, selective, self-serving. A community that trusts only memory will end up believing whatever it wishes about itself." He drew out a ledger—society minutes, 1998—and opened it to a ribboned page. "But ink. Ink is incorruptible. Whatever is written here *happened*. Whatever is omitted—" a small shrug, elegant as a card trick, "—did not need to."

Bram looked at the page. Minutes of the Anniversary Dinner Society, in a hand she recognized from the photograph card—no. Not the same hand. Older entries, a different secretary. But the format was identical: dated, signed, flawless.

"May I?" she asked, reaching for the ledger.

Crane closed it first, gently, and reshelved it. "Members' records," he said apologetically. "Society business. You understand. After poor Elspeth, we are careful about access." He smiled his millimeter smile. "Come to the dinner in October. Centenary year. Everything worth knowing about this parish will be on display that night. It always is."

* * *

The last bus back was at nine. Bram walked to the crossroads through a village going dark window by window, and at the corner by the dry drainage cut she stopped, because someone had chalked something small and fresh on the base of the old boundary stone.

Not graffiti. A single line, in a quick slanted hand:

**the keeper of names decides who was here**

She crouched. The chalk was soft-edged already, rain-threatened, written within the hour. She looked up and down the empty street. Curtains still. Hedges still. Somewhere a dog began to bark and was silenced.

Bram took out her notebook and copied the sentence exactly, preserving the slant of the letters, and as she wrote it she felt the cold particular thrill she had felt perhaps five times in her working life—the thrill of a lock turning under a key she hadn't known she was carrying. Because she had heard that phrase before, or nearly. In Maren Vane's retelling of her sister's last telephone call, cut off mid-sentence, there had been a fragment: *—he keeps the names, Mare, he keeps all the—*

Click.

She boarded the bus and took her seat at the back, and watched Marsh Hollow recede into the fen dark, tidy and lit and sealed, a display case closing. Eleven pages of praise in her notebook, and not one true thing in them. A historian who spoke of omission like a gardener speaking of pruning. A garden where even the dead flowers were tidied away before anyone could see them fade.

Everyone in that parish remembered Elspeth Vane perfectly, Bram thought, and no one remembered her at all, and both of those facts had been arranged.

She turned to a fresh page and wrote, in her own hand, the question she would carry back to the Fenland Grand like contraband:

*Who edits the editors?*

Behind her, the village lights went out one by one, in order, as though switched by a single hand.
