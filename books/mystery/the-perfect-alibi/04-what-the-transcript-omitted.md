## 4. What the Transcript Omitted

The probate office in Hollow Creek occupied the ground floor of the old Carnegie building, a structure the town had been meaning to renovate for thirty years and never had, so that the marble floors wore the color of old teeth and the radiators clanked like a ship in distress. Theo signed the visitor's log at 8:52 in the morning, under the flat gaze of a clerk named Doreen who had been in the county's employ since before Vivian Marsh disappeared and who regarded every request for old paper as a personal affront.

"Marsh, Vivian. Estate file," Theo said. "1987 through 1991, if you have it."

Doreen's pen stopped moving.

"That file's sealed," she said. "Sealed in '91 by court order. You'd need a judge's signature to see it."

"Which judge?"

The question hung in the air a moment longer than it should have. Doreen's eyes flicked up, then away, and Theo watched the answer assemble itself behind the woman's face before she spoke it.

"Judge Marsh," she said, as if the name were a stone she was setting down carefully. "He sealed it himself. Presiding."

*Of course he did,* Theo thought, and smiled her podcast smile, the one that made people underestimate her. "Then I'll need to see the order itself. The sealing order. That's public record, isn't it? The fact of the seal, if not the contents?"

Doreen looked at her for a long moment, and Theo had the distinct sense of being weighed — not against the rules, but against something else entirely. Against the town's tolerance for her, maybe. Against three years of polite phone calls and doors closed with a smile.

"Give me a minute," Doreen said, and disappeared into the stacks.

She came back with a single sheet in a manila folder, and Theo read it standing at the counter. The order was brief, boilerplate, signed in a hand she recognized now from a dozen documents: Edmund A. Marsh, presiding judge, Ninth Judicial District. *In the interest of the minor beneficiary's privacy and the orderly administration of the trust,* it read. *The estate file of Marsh, Vivian A., deceased, is hereby sealed pending the majority of the named beneficiary.*

The named beneficiary.

Not *the* beneficiary. Not *the sole* beneficiary. The phrase implied a structure — a will, a trust, a person. Theo read the order four times, photographing each line with her phone, and felt the cold thing in her chest that she'd learned to trust over three years of this work. It wasn't excitement, exactly. It was the feeling of a floorboard giving slightly under her weight and not breaking — the house settling around a truth that had been standing there all along.

"Who was the beneficiary?" she asked.

Doreen was already turning away. "Sealed," she said over her shoulder.

"Doreen." Theo kept her voice pleasant. "The will was probated in open court in 1987. Before the sealing. There would have been a reading, notices in the paper, a — "

"The *Hollow Creek Sentinel* is on microfilm at the library," Doreen said. "I'm not your research assistant, Ms. Thackeray."

"No," Theo agreed. "You're just the person who sealed the file."

She let that sit, watched the clerk's shoulders stiffen, and left before the woman could decide whether to be angry or afraid. Outside, the morning had turned gray and close, the kind of sky that made the town look like a photograph of itself. Theo sat in her car in the library lot and pulled up the Sentinel's archives on her laptop through the state library's digitization project, because she had learned long ago that the fastest way into a sealed room was usually through the newspaper that had been allowed to watch.

The probate notice ran on page four of the November 14th, 1987 edition, three weeks after Vivian Marsh's disappearance and — Theo noted — eleven days before any body was found. The estate had been declared, the will filed, and the paper printed the standard summary: *The estate of the late Vivian Marsh, daughter of the late industrialist Aldous Marsh, is to be administered in trust by her brother, Edmund A. Marsh, Esq., for the benefit of a named beneficiary whose identity is protected under the provisions of the will.*

Protected under the provisions of the will.

Theo read that sentence until the words lost their shape. She had catalogued, in forty-one episodes and eleven thousand pages of filing, every public fact of Vivian Marsh's life: the debutante photographs, the equestrian trophies, the engagement that was announced and quietly unannounced in the same year, the last confirmed sighting at the Marsh estate on the night of October 23rd, 1987. She knew the will existed. She had never been able to see it. And now she understood why — because somewhere in its language was a name that three years of asking had never once produced. Not from the family. Not from the town. Not from Roy Pell, who had confessed to the murder and couldn't describe the house.

*For the benefit of a named beneficiary.*

She drove back to her basement with the windows down and the cold air doing its work on her thoughts, sorting them the way she sorted everything, alphabetically, one file at a time. And it was there, in the archive, under **T** for *Transcripts*, that the second half of the morning found her.

* * *

The inquest transcript was the crown jewel of her collection — a photocopied, fourth-generation artifact she had obtained in her first year, from a retired bailiff who asked no questions and took no money, only a promise that she wouldn't say where it came from. The coroner's inquest into the death of Vivian Marsh, held in January 1988, after her body was pulled from the flooded quarry with lungs full of quarry water and no water in her stomach, a contradiction the coroner had noted and the town had declined to pursue. The transcript ran to ninety-one pages. Theo had read it perhaps fifty times. She knew its rhythms the way she knew her own voicemail greeting.

Page forty-seven was where Ada Lune's name appeared — *Testimony of A. Lune, Court Stenographer, sworn* — and page forty-seven was where the transcript went strange. Not redacted in the modern sense, not with black bars or the word *withheld*. Instead, the text simply stopped. A paragraph began — *The witness stated that on the evening in question she observed* — and then nothing. The next line picked up two pages later with *— no further questions for this witness,* in a different typewriter font, a different ribbon, a different day.

Two pages. Missing. Not torn out — the photocopy was continuous, the pages numbered, the sequence intact. The pages had been *replaced*. Someone had retyped the surrounding text and simply omitted the middle, so that the transcript read like a sentence with its heart cut out, the grammar closing over the wound so smoothly that only someone who had read it fifty times would ever feel the seam.

Theo had felt the seam for three years. She had assumed it was clerical. Bureaucracies lost things; that was practically their function. She had even recorded an episode about it — episode nine, *The Missing Pages* — and filed the anomaly under **A** for *Anomalies*, between *Ashford, Deputy R. (statements, conflicting)* and *Autopsy, quarry recovery*.

Now she laid the transcript on her worktable beside the printout of the probate notice, beside the sealing order, beside the letter in its evidence sleeve — *She isn't where they buried her* — and looked at all of it at once for the first time.

The will named a beneficiary whose identity was protected. The trust was administered by Edmund Marsh. The estate file was sealed by Edmund Marsh. The inquest transcript was missing exactly the testimony of the stenographer who would have been present at the probate reading — because of course she would have been, Theo realized, her hands going cold on the table's edge. Court stenographers didn't just record trials. In a county this small, in 1987, Ada Lune would have transcribed the will, the trust instrument, the probate proceedings. She would have known the beneficiary's name. She would have typed it, sworn to it, filed it.

And then she had typed the inquest testimony that said something the town could not afford to have on the record, and two pages of her testimony had been retyped out of existence, and three years later she had driven into the flooded quarry, or appeared to, and the town had held a small service and moved on.

Theo stood very still in the middle of her archive, in the amber light of the basement lamp, surrounded by the alphabetized bones of the case, and felt the shape of it close around her like water. This was the thing she had been circling for three years without naming. It wasn't a murder with a cover-up attached. It was a cover-up with a murder attached — a structure, load-bearing, built into the town's institutions the way the radiators were built into the Carnegie building, clanking away in plain sight, doing their work for decades. The sealing order wasn't a corruption of the system. It *was* the system. Edmund Marsh hadn't abused his office. His office was the abuse, wearing robes.

She thought of Roy Pell, dying in his rented room, recanting within hours because he couldn't describe a house he'd never entered. A confession forged to be *found* — by her, specifically, by the podcaster who kept the case alive — so that the record would finally close with a killer's name on it and no one would ever ask about the beneficiary again. And the second letter, the unsigned one, *she isn't where they buried her*, arriving in the same mailbox on the same morning. Someone wanted the case closed. Someone else wanted it opened. And both of them, Theo understood now, were writing to her because she had spent three years building the one thing a town like this feared: a complete record, alphabetized, cross-referenced, waiting.

She sat down and pulled a fresh sheet of paper — she still did this part on paper, the architecture of a case, because paper couldn't be subpoenaed or hacked or quietly deleted — and wrote at the top, in her small precise hand: *WHO WAS THE BENEFICIARY?*

Under it: *Who had access to the will?* Under that: *Who could seal a file, redact a transcript, and bury a stenographer — and call it all procedure?*

The answer was one name, and she had drunk tea with it, or nearly. She had stood in a courtroom gallery last spring and watched Edmund Marsh preside over a negligence case with that famous patience of his, the kindness the whole county testified to, and she had thought: *there is a man who loved his sister.* She had said as much, in episode twelve. She had built the archive's entire portrait of the Marsh family on the foundation that the brother grieved.

The floorboard gave again. This time she felt it all the way down.

Because there was one more document, one she had carried for a year without understanding it — a photocopy of a trust amendment, dated 1994, that a source had slipped her at a county fair, unsigned on the copy, referencing *the Beneficiary* in the plural in one clause and the singular in another. She had filed it under **M** for *Miscellaneous* because she couldn't make it fit anywhere else. She retrieved it now, and read it in the lamplight, and read it again, and the cold in her chest spread outward until her fingers ached.

*The Beneficiary or Beneficiaries, as the case may be,* the clause read, *shall receive distributions upon proof of continued absence from the jurisdiction of Hollow Creek.*

Continued absence. Distributions — payments — contingent on someone staying away. Someone alive. Someone the trust was paying to keep quiet, or keep hidden, or keep gone, for thirty years, in a town where everyone swore the only witness was at the bottom of a quarry.

Theo looked at the trust amendment, and then at the transcript with its hollowed-out pages, and heard, very clearly, in the voice she used for narration, the sentence that would open episode forty-two if she ever got to record it: *The Marsh fortune did not just survive Vivian Marsh's death. It required it.*

She was still holding the page when she noticed the detail that turned her blood to quarry water — the amendment's date. 1994. Seven years after Vivian died. Three years after Ada Lune drowned.

The trust hadn't been amended to settle an old estate. It had been amended the year Ada Lune died — or the year she was supposed to.

Someone had been paid to stay absent ever since. And the first payment would have gone out in 1994, in cash, by a trust administered by a judge, to a beneficiary whose name appeared in exactly one living memory: the memory of the woman who had typed the will herself.

Ada Lune hadn't witnessed the murder. Theo was suddenly certain of it, certain the way she was certain of her own filing system.

Ada Lune had witnessed the *money*.
