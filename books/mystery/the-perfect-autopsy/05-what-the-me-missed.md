## 5. What the ME Missed

The morgue smelled the way all morgues smell — like bleach trying very hard to apologize for something.

Cyrus Reyes had been in enough of them to know that the disinfectant was a lie of ambition rather than accomplishment. Underneath it was always the same honest note: cold, and finished. She signed in at the front desk of the County Medical Examiner's office at 8:40 on a Tuesday morning, badge laminated, coffee untouched because she'd learned years ago never to bring anything into a room where someone was going to ask her to look closely. Coffee made people hurry. Hurrying was how details got missed.

"Ms. Reyes." The man behind the desk stood when she gave her name, which almost nobody did anymore. He was tall in the way of someone who'd learned to fold himself politely around smaller rooms — cardigan over scrubs, reading glasses pushed up into hair going gray at the temples early, maybe forty, maybe younger with bad luck baked in. His lanyard read D. VOSS, ASSISTANT MEDICAL EXAMINER, and beneath that, in smaller type, as if the building itself were embarrassed: *provisional.*

"You're expected," he said. "Detective Quade called ahead. She said—" and here he paused, and something flickered across his face that Cyrus would spend a long time afterward trying to classify, "—she said you'd want the body before the paperwork."

"She knows me well."

"I've heard that," Daniel Voss said. "I've actually read quite a bit about you."

There it was again, that flicker. Not flirtation, not fan-boying — she'd gotten both from colleagues over the years and knew their textures. This was something more careful. The way a man looks at a locked door he has a key to but isn't ready to use.

"Most of what's written about me is wrong," Cyrus said.

"Oh, I don't think so," Voss said mildly, and led her down the corridor. "I think most of what's written about you is *incomplete.*"

* * *

The victim lay under a sheet in bay three, and Cyrus made herself do the thing she always made herself do: stand at the door for ten full seconds before approaching. Let the whole room arrive at once. The tray of instruments, arranged by size. The scale for weighing organs, gleaming and patient. The clipboard hanging by the door with its chain of signatures. The light — overhead fluorescents plus one articulated lamp angled from the left, which meant anyone photographing the body from the standard position would get a soft shadow along the right side of the face.

Details were just the world refusing to be summarized. She'd built a career on refusing to let it.

Her name was Lena Okafor. Thirty-four. Found in the drainage culvert off Halden Road two nights ago, cause of death provisionally listed as drowning, though the water in her lungs told a story with an asterisk. She wore Cyrus's charcoal wool coat, the one reported stolen from a dry cleaner on Delancey two winters back, and the cream scarf with the crooked embroidered *C* that her sister had made her the year before their mother died.

"Go ahead," Voss said quietly. "Take your time. Everyone else rushes you. I've read that too — that you stand at doors."

Cyrus glanced at him. "You've read a lot of things."

"I'm a thorough person." He said it without pride, the way another man might say *I'm allergic to shellfish* — a fact of constitution, not virtue.

She approached the body and began the walk. Crown to sole, slow, cataloguing. The coat was hers; she confirmed it against memory — the moth hole at the left cuff she'd never repaired, the way the second button had been replaced with a near-match after she lost the original in a hotel in Sacramento. All present. All correct.

Except.

"The shoes," Cyrus said.

"Laced wrong," Voss agreed, and there was something in his voice — not eagerness, exactly. Satisfaction, maybe, of the kind a teacher feels when a student finally sees it. "You noticed that at the scene, I understand. Detective Quade mentioned it. Bow tied over the left lace instead of under, heel counters crushed forward like they were put on a seated person, and—" he lifted the sheet with two fingers, delicately, a magician showing his hands were empty, "—the socks. Look at the socks."

Cyrus looked. Ribbed cotton socks, mismatched — one navy, one black. On a woman whose entire outfit otherwise matched to the stitch.

"Whoever dressed her," Cyrus said slowly, "was working from photographs. Photographs don't show sock color under boots. They don't show lace direction. You can't know those things unless you were there when she got dressed — or unless you're guessing, and guessing shows."

"Precisely." Voss pulled a stool over for her without being asked, positioned it at hip height beside the table. It was such a small courtesy, so precisely calibrated to how she worked — she liked to sit for the fine-detail pass, saved her back, kept her still — that she felt a small, unearned pang of gratitude toward him. "You wrote that once, you know. In a report. 2019, I believe. 'Guessing shows.' You used it as a section header."

Cyrus went still.

It was true. She had written that. Section four of a claim file on a warehouse death out in the valley — a file nobody outside the insurer's archive should ever have seen, because it had settled quietly and been sealed in the settlement's confidentiality clause. *Guessing shows.* Three words she'd been privately pleased with at the time, the way writers are pleased with sentences no one will ever read.

"That's not public," she said.

"No," Voss agreed pleasantly. "It isn't."

He turned to the instrument tray and began laying out what he'd need for the tissue samples he'd already offered to pull for her — a favor, he'd said, since her credentials were consultative and the lab technically owed her nothing. His movements were economical, exact, each tool placed with the handle oriented the same direction. There was a pleasure in watching competent hands work, and Cyrus felt it even through the unease now pooling coldly under her sternum.

"How does an assistant examiner come to read sealed insurance files?" she asked.

"Public records requests, mostly. Court filings. Settlements leak — they always leak. Somebody's cousin is a paralegal, somebody's angry, somebody sells a box of documents to a journalist who never writes the story." He shrugged, gentle. "Death leaves paper, Ms. Reyes. You of all people know that. You've made a career of reading it."

"And you've made a career of reading *me*?"

The question came out sharper than she intended. Voss didn't flinch. He set down the forceps, turned, and looked at her over his glasses with an expression of such open, guileless reasonableness that she almost apologized.

"I read everything about my field," he said. "You're the best in it. When your name comes up in a filing, I read the filing. Is that strange? A carpenter studying a master's joinery?" He smiled — small, rueful, self-deprecating. "My brother used to tease me about it. How I collect things. Details. He said I loved information more than people."

The past tense landed a half-second late, the way past tenses about family always do. Cyrus filed it without letting her face change. *Used to.*

"I'm sorry," she said. "Your brother."

"It was a long time ago." He turned back to the tray. "Accident," he added, and the word had a strange, lacquered quality, like furniture polished so many times the wood underneath had gone dark. "That's what they decided. That's what the file says."

Something old and cold turned over in Cyrus's chest — the instinct she'd learned to trust above supervisors, above actuaries, above her own desire to go home at a reasonable hour. But she couldn't have said why, not yet. It was just a man talking about his dead brother in a morgue, and morgues were full of those.

"Tell me about Lena Okafor," she said, and let it go.

* * *

Voss was, she had to admit, excellent at his job.

He walked her through the autopsy findings with a clarity she rarely got from physicians, who tended either to drown her in Latin or condescend into plain language so flattened it lost meaning. Voss did neither. He explained the diatoms in the lung tissue — freshwater algae consistent with the culvert, present in quantity, which argued she'd been alive when she went in. He showed her the petechial hemorrhages, careful and noncommittal about what they did and didn't prove. He flagged, unprompted, the bruising pattern on her upper arms — bilateral, faint, older than the drowning by hours — and said, "I've noted it as 'consistent with restraint, not diagnostic of it,' because that's the truth, and I know how much you hate it when reports claim certainty the tissue won't support."

"You quoted my own standards to me," Cyrus said.

"They're good standards."

They stood over the body together in the cold, and Cyrus did the thing that made her famous and made her lonely: she closed her eyes and took the photograph. Not literally — there was no camera in her head, whatever the articles said, just a habit of attention so total it functioned like one. Every detail of Lena Okafor laid down in order, cross-indexed, retrievable. The coat. The scarf. The wrong-laced shoes. The bruise map. The chipped polish on the left thumbnail, pink, reapplied over an older coat of a darker red — a woman who touched up her manicure but didn't start over. A detail no killer staging a scene would bother to fake, because it meant nothing to anyone but Lena.

Someone had loved this woman's hands, or she had. Either way, the polish was real, and the rest was theater.

"Thank you," she said, when they were done. "This was more than I needed."

"It was exactly what you needed," Voss said. "That's why I did it."

At the door, she paused. It was a habit — the pause at thresholds, the last sweep — and it saved her, though she wouldn't understand how for weeks.

"Have we met before?" she asked. "Before this. You seem—familiar."

Something happened to Daniel Voss's face then. It didn't move, exactly. It *stilled*, the way water stills over something deep, and for one unguarded instant Cyrus had the sense of looking at a man standing very quietly in a burning house, listening to whether the fire had reached him yet.

"I don't believe so," he said. "I'd remember. I remember most things."

"So do I," said Cyrus Reyes.

* * *

She didn't figure it out until she was halfway home, stopped at a red light on Cerritos Avenue with her wipers ticking against a thin rain, when the memory surfaced the way her memories always did — whole, timestamped, indifferent to whether she wanted it.

A deposition. Ten years ago, give or take. A conference room in a law firm downtown, carpet the color of oatmeal, a court reporter with a cleft chin typing in the corner. Cyrus had been thirty-one, three years into the job, testifying about a wrongful-death claim — a young man, an industrial accident, a case her employer wanted settled and sealed. And in the row of chairs against the wall, taking notes in a spiral notebook, sitting with a mother whose grief filled the room like smoke, there had been a young man. Early twenties. Thin. Watchful. He'd asked to address the mediator directly and been told the process didn't work that way, and Cyrus remembered — she would always remember, that was the curse and the whole point of her — the sound of his voice when he'd said, low and shaking:

*"Then the process doesn't work. Someone has to say what actually happened to my brother."*

Ten years. A different name on the lanyard, probably. Gray at the temples where there hadn't been any gray. But the voice — the precise, controlled, over-careful diction of a man holding himself together with grammar — she knew it. She had *filed* it. Her memory didn't lose things; it only waited.

Daniel Voss had been in that room. Daniel Voss had watched her testify, calm and procedural, in service of a settlement that buried whatever really happened to his brother. And now Daniel Voss stood over bodies in a county morgue, quoting her sealed reports back to her word for word, smiling his reasonable smile, telling her that guessing shows.

The light turned green. Cars honked behind her. Cyrus sat in the intersection of Cerritos and Ninth with her heart going like a trapped bird, assembling the thought she didn't want to assemble, the one that changed the shape of everything:

Lena Okafor's shoes had been laced wrong because the man who dressed her had learned death from documents.

And the man who knew her documents better than anyone alive had just spent an hour standing beside her, handing her instruments, being unfailingly helpful.

She drove home the long way, checking her mirrors, and called Marisol Quade from a parking lot two miles from her own house.

"Quade. It's Reyes. I need you to run a name for me — and I need you to promise me you'll do it quietly."
