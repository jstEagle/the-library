## 11. The Copyist's Signature

Cyrus Reyes had a wall in her apartment that she'd never shown to anyone. It wasn't a conspiracy board — she'd seen enough of those in her career to know they were mostly a symptom of sleep deprivation wearing a red string — but it was close. A corkboard, three photographs pinned in a row, and beneath each one a sheet of paper covered in her own handwriting so dense it looked like weather.

She stood in front of it at two in the morning, wearing her father's old cardigan and holding a mug of tea she'd forgotten existed until it went cold, and she did the thing she did better than anyone alive.

She remembered.

The first scene: the riverside, the woman in Cyrus's stolen coat, the mud like wet chocolate under everyone's shoes. The second: the parking structure on Delancey, the man folded into the driver's seat of a car he didn't own, Cyrus's watch loose around his wrist because his bones were finer than hers. The third: the apartment on Kessler Street, the woman arranged in an armchair in her mother's dove-gray blouse, hands folded, as if waiting politely for tea.

Three bodies. Three of her closed files come back from the dead, dressed in her dead things.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the board, to the tea, to nobody. "What do you keep getting wrong?"

Because that was the question. Not what he got right — he got almost everything right, which was its own kind of horror — but what he got wrong. Everyone had a signature. Forgers had theirs, and the best forgers weren't undone by their mistakes but by their consistencies. You could fake a Rembrandt once. Fake the same brushstroke three times, and you'd taught the world your hand.

She closed her eyes.

* * *

The river came back first, because it always did. The smell of it — diesel and algae and something organic underneath, the river's honest rot. Detective Quade standing at the tape line with her arms crossed like a woman guarding a door only she could see. The victim on the bank, half-turned, one arm flung forward as if reaching for the water or away from it.

Cyrus let the memory play at full resolution. This was the part people didn't understand about her gift — it wasn't a superpower, not really. It was more like being cursed with a camera that never turned off and never let her delete anything. Every scene she'd ever walked through lived behind her eyes at permanent full brightness, including the ones she would have paid good money to forget.

The coat. Her coat, the camel one with the missing button on the left cuff — she remembered losing the button, remembered meaning to sew it back on, remembered not doing it for eleven months until the coat itself disappeared from her closet along with a scarf and a pair of gloves, reported stolen two winters ago in a burglary report she'd filed with all the enthusiasm of a woman filling out a form at the DMV.

The button was still missing. Good. Accurate.

But the shoes —

Her eyes snapped open. She crossed to the desk, pulled the case file Quade had grudgingly photocopied for her — "off the record, Reyes, and if my lieutenant asks, you hypnotized me" — and flipped to the scene photographs. There. The victim's shoes, low-heeled ankle boots, laced in a neat crisscross.

Except they weren't. Look closer. The laces went over-under-over, a ladder pattern, the kind of lacing you saw in military boots and orthopedic catalogs. Nobody laced ankle boots that way. And more to the point — Cyrus was almost certain, ninety-four percent certain, which for her was a shout — the victim hadn't laced them that way either. Because the knot sat wrong. Too centered. Too tidy. Laces tied by someone working from a photograph, where you couldn't see tension, couldn't see how a foot had pushed and shifted the knot off-axis over a day of walking.

A re-enactment. By someone who had only ever seen pictures.

She wrote it down. Then she went to the second file.

* * *

Delancey Street parking structure, level three, the sodium lights turning everything the color of weak broth. The man in the driver's seat, staged to match File 5102 — the carbon monoxide claim, the one she'd closed as accidental because the garage ventilation logs supported it and fighting it would have cost the company nine months of litigation against a widow who couldn't afford a lawyer.

He wore Cyrus's watch. That detail had made the papers — THE COPYIST KILLER, someone had christened him, which Cyrus found both lazy and accurate, and she resented it for both reasons.

In the file photos, the staging was exquisite. His position in the seat matched the original claimant's. The engine-off detail was preserved. Even the coffee cup in the cupholder — cold, half-full, positioned exactly as the original scene photo showed it.

Exactly as the original scene photo showed it.

Cyrus set down her pen and stared at the wall.

Because here was the thing about scenes versus photographs of scenes: photographs lie by omission. They flatten. They crop. And whoever had staged this body had reproduced the photograph, not the event. She was sure of it now, and here was how she knew: the coffee cup. In the original scene — the real one, eight years ago, the one she'd walked herself — the cup had been tipped, a brown stain spreading across the console, because the man had been dying and his hand had knocked it. The stain was in the photograph too, dried and dark. But on this new body, the cup sat upright, half-full, pristine — and beside it, on the console, someone had *painted* a stain. Same shape, same spread, same dried-dark color.

He'd copied the stain. Reproduced the evidence of the accident without producing the accident.

"You're not recreating deaths," Cyrus said slowly, and the sound of her own voice in the empty apartment was strange, like hearing a stranger think. "You're recreating *photographs*."

She was up now, pacing, the cardigan sleeves flopping past her hands. It explained everything that had been itching at her since the river. The ladder-laced boots. The way the third victim's hands were folded — symmetrically, perfectly, knuckles aligned — when real bodies at rest were never that tidy, because real bodies slumped and settled under gravity while rigor did its uneven work. He placed hands the way they appeared in crime scene documentation: composed, legible, *readable*. Real death was illegible. His death was a well-formatted report.

And there was more. She went back to the river scene in her head, dug deeper, past the coat, past the arm flung toward the water. The victim's hair. In the original File 4471 photograph — the drowning she'd ruled accidental eight years ago, the ruling that tasted like a settlement check — the deceased's hair had been plastered across her face in a specific wet sweep, left to right. The new victim's hair was swept the same way. Left to right. Identically.

But the river flowed the other direction that night. Cyrus remembered the current, remembered the debris line on the bank. If a body had actually lain in that water, the hair would have swept right to left.

He hadn't known that. You couldn't know that from a photograph. Photographs don't record currents. Only reports do — and only if the investigator bothered to write it down.

Had she written it down? She closed her eyes, pulled the mental document. *Reyes, C., field notes, File 4471:* "...debris line indicates northward flow; decedent's hair displacement consistent with post-mortem positioning..." Yes. She'd written it. Which meant he'd read it. Which meant —

Which meant he wasn't working from crime scene photos alone. He was working from *her reports*. From her words. Her observations, transcribed into the official record, were his source material. Every error in his staging was an error in translation between what she had seen and what she had written — and every one of those errors mapped, precisely, onto the places where her reports had been incomplete, hedged, or quietly shaped to support the conclusion the company wanted.

The ladder laces: her 4471 report described footwear as "laced, intact." No pattern noted. So he'd chosen a pattern from somewhere else — a manual, a catalog, a textbook — and applied it.

The upright coffee cup with the painted stain: her 5102 report said "beverage container present at console, spillage consistent with terminal movement." He'd read *spillage*, and he'd rendered spillage as a prop. Painted it on. Because he understood that a report is a description of evidence, not evidence itself — and he had confused the map for the territory so completely that he painted maps onto territory.

"Oh," Cyrus said softly. "Oh, you beautiful, terrible pedant."

It wasn't an insult. It was the beginning of a profile, and profiles are love letters written in forensics.

* * *

By four a.m. the corkboard had grown a fourth layer: index cards, connected by red yarn that Cyrus had bought specifically for the occasion and felt slightly ridiculous about, and a single sheet of paper titled, in her neatest handwriting, WHAT THE COPYIST KNOWS.

She sat on the floor, back against the couch, and made herself say it plainly.

He learned death from documents. Not from killing — not yet, maybe never directly. Three victims, and all three had died of causes consistent with their staged scenarios: the drowning was a drowning, the CO poisoning was CO poisoning, the third — she made herself look at the card — the third had been suffocated, gently, chemically, before she was ever dressed in a dead mother's blouse. He killed them elsewhere, cleanly, and then he *composed* them. Like a photographer arranging a still life. Like a student reproducing a master's painting stroke by stroke, learning the craft.

And his medium was her archive. Her reports were his textbooks. Her phrasings were his anatomy lessons. Somewhere out there was a man who knew her professional voice better than anyone alive — better than her supervisors, better than Quade, possibly better than she did, because he'd studied it, annotated it, lived inside it.

That was the fingerprint. Not a repeated error in the sense she'd first imagined — not a slip. Something stranger and more useful: a repeated *relationship* to error. In all three stagings, wherever her report had been vague, he had filled the gap with something generic, textbook-correct, and wrong for the specific scene. Wherever her report had been precise, he was precise. He was only as good as her documentation. He was her documentation, given flesh and a needle and a van and no discernible conscience.

Which meant two things, and Cyrus wrote them both down even though one of them made her stomach fold over on itself.

One: she could predict him. Every future staging would be built from a Reyes report, and every gap in those reports was a place where he would improvise — and improvisation was where copyists failed. She knew her own reports the way he never could: she knew what she'd *left out*. Every hedge, every omission, every sentence she'd softened for the settlement department. Those soft spots were his blind spots. He thought her reports were complete. She knew they were wounded.

Two — and here the pen hesitated — he wasn't just imitating the scenes. He was imitating *her judgments*. Each staging corresponded to a file she'd closed against her instinct, and the staging always included some element that made the original ruling look foolish. The river victim positioned so the current contradicted the "accidental" finding. The CO victim with a ventilation log discrepancy sitting in plain sight. He wasn't just saying *look what you missed*. He was saying *look what you decided not to see*.

He was grading her. She'd felt it since the river without having the word for it — the sense of being marked, assessed, held to a standard. Somewhere out there was a man keeping score on Cyrus Reyes, and the score was kept in bodies.

The tea was stone cold. She drank it anyway, because her father used to say cold tea builds character, and Cyrus had long suspected her father said it because he was too lazy to reheat his.

Then the phone buzzed on the floor beside her. Quade. At 4:17 a.m., which meant either a body or a breakthrough, and Cyrus had learned that with Quade the two arrived in the same envelope.

"Tell me you're asleep," Quade said, instead of hello.

"I'm at the wall."

"The wall. The wall is a metaphor?"

"The wall is a corkboard with yarn. I've become a cliché, Marisol. Send help or coffee."

"Neither. I'm sending you a name." A pause, papers shuffling. "ME's office ran the third victim's tox panel early. Assistant examiner flagged something — says the sedative compound matches a partial trace from the second victim, the one we never got a clean read on. He wants to brief you personally. Says given your... familiarity with the files, your input might be material."

Cyrus stood very still. On the wall, three photographs looked back at her, and behind them, faint as a watermark, a fourth image assembled itself: a man in a lab coat, helpful and precise, quoting her own sentences back to her with a warmth that had seemed like respect and now felt like recitation.

"He quoted my reports," she said. "When I met him. Voss. He used my phrasing — 'displacement consistent with post-mortem positioning.' Word for word. I remember thinking he'd just read the file closely."

"And now?"

"Now I think nobody reads that closely," Cyrus said. "Nobody except the person who wrote it."

The line went quiet. Down the phone she could hear Quade breathing, thinking, deciding — the particular silence of a detective choosing which rule to bend next.

"Morgue briefing's at nine," Quade said finally. "Wear something you can run in, Reyes. And Cyrus? If this guy knows your reports better than you do—"

"He doesn't," Cyrus said, looking at the wall, at the gaps, at all the soft places where she'd signed her name to comfortable lies. "He knows my reports exactly as well as I wrote them. Which means I know every mistake he's going to make. Because I made them first."

She hung up, and she reached for her coat — not the camel one, never that one again — and began, methodically, to plan a visit to the medical examiner's office.
