## 13. Confrontation, Deferred

The archive room of the Office of the Chief Medical Examiner smelled like paper and cold metal and, faintly, like the lemon cleaner someone used on the floors at two in the morning when they thought no one would notice. Cyrus noticed. Cyrus always noticed.

She had been inside for eleven minutes.

Eleven minutes was enough to confirm what she'd hoped was paranoia: a gray four-drawer filing cabinet, unlocked, tucked behind the reference shelves where the standard textbooks went to die. Inside, manila folders arranged by date, each one bearing her name. *Reyes, C. — Final Report.* Every case she'd closed in the last decade. And not copies pulled from official channels — these were annotated. Margins dense with a small, precise hand. Red ink. Corrections.

*Shoes laced incorrectly — see p. 4 of scene photos. Subject's watch set to correct time; original file states otherwise.*

Her own handwriting stared back at her from photocopies she didn't remember authorizing, and beside it, patient as a tutor grading essays, the red pen of a man who had been studying her for years.

She had a photograph of the ledger page on her phone before she heard the door.

Not the main door — the secondary one, near the loading bay, the one that stuck and therefore never got fixed because nobody used it except somebody who wanted to come and go without being logged. The hinge gave its small complaint, and then the room changed temperature in a way that had nothing to do with climate control.

"You're supposed to badge in," Daniel Voss said mildly. "Building policy."

Cyrus did not turn around immediately. That was deliberate, though her heart was doing something unprofessional against her ribs. She finished photographing the page in front of her — the drowning file, 4471, his brother's file, though he didn't know yet that she knew that — and closed the folder with the care of someone handling evidence, which is exactly what it was.

"Security desk is empty at three a.m.," she said. "I checked."

"I know. I checked first." He came around the shelves into her line of sight, and up close, under the flat fluorescent light, he looked exactly like what he was: a tired professional in his forties with kind eyes and a cardigan that said *harmless* in three languages. "You found the cabinet faster than I expected. I'd have guessed twenty minutes. You're efficient."

"You knew I'd come."

"I hoped." He folded his hands. "Hoping and knowing are different things. You taught me that, actually. Page six of your report on the Halvorsen case. 'Hope is not an evidentiary standard.'"

God, she thought. He really had memorized them. All of them. Her sentences, living in another man's mouth.

"Daniel," she said, and watched him flinch — a small thing, a twitch at the corner of his eye, but she caught it, because catching small things was the only trick she had ever had. "Or should I say—"

"Don't." The mildness held, but barely, like ice on a pond early in the season. "That name belongs to my mother. You don't get to use it."

"Your mother," Cyrus said quietly, "spits at my name. I'm told."

Something moved through his face then — not anger. Something worse. Recognition. The look of a man hearing a sentence he'd already written.

"She told you that?"

"She didn't have to. I've read enough of your annotations to know how you talk about me. How you talk about *her*." She tapped the folder. "You grade my reports like homework, Daniel. But this one—" she lifted 4471, felt its weight, eight years old and light as a sin, "—this one has a different ink. Older. Angrier. You started annotating this one before any of the others. This was the first essay."

He looked at the folder the way other men look at photographs of the dead.

"He was nineteen," Voss said. "Did you know that? Everyone says it like it explains something. *He was nineteen, he was drinking, he was on the roof.* As if being nineteen makes water in your lungs make sense." His voice stayed level. That was the horror of it — the levelness. "The ME who signed his autopsy wrote 'undetermined.' Your company needed 'accidental.' Undetermined doesn't pay out clean. Accidental does. And there was a woman from the insurance company who came to the deposition with a legal pad full of questions she already knew the answers to, and she asked them anyway, gently, like a nurse taking a temperature, and at the end she signed her name to a report that said my brother fell."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them, a pipe ticked.

"I remember the deposition," Cyrus said.

"I know you do. That's rather the point of you." He almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth she'd seen on him, and it was worse than rage. "Photographic memory. Total recall. The investigators' investigator. Do you know what I thought, sitting in that hallway outside the conference room, nineteen myself, watching you walk out with your legal pad? I thought: *she knows.* Not suspects. Knows. Because you looked at me on your way past, and your eyes did a thing — they went soft and then hard again, like a door closing. I've spent ten years trying to find that moment in your reports. It isn't in any of them. You were very careful."

"I was paid to be careful."

"There it is." He nodded slowly, as if she'd finally turned in acceptable work. "There it is. People who sign their names to lies. Do you know what I learned in medical records administration, Ms. Reyes? A signature is the cheapest thing a human being produces. Cheaper than breath. You can put one on anything. A death certificate. A settlement. An apology that isn't one. My brother's whole life got compressed down to a signature at the bottom of a page, and the woman who made that compression possible—" he spread his hands, courteous, hospitable, "—became famous for it. Awards. Consultations. Detectives calling *her* when things don't add up."

"And so you made sure things didn't add up," Cyrus said. "Three bodies dressed in my clothes. Staged from my files. You built a museum of my mistakes and put corpses in it."

"I built *corrections*," Voss said, and for the first time his voice cracked open, just a hairline, just enough to see the furnace underneath. "Do you understand how sloppy you were? The Halvorsen staging — whoever did it put the shoes on the wrong feet, and you walked right past it. The second one, the watch — you wound it wrong. Left over right. A left-handed person wound that watch, and you never once asked yourself who in your life was left-handed. You are the best reader of death in this city and you read everything about everyone except yourself."

*Left-handed.* The word dropped through her like a stone through still water. Her mother had been left-handed. The third victim had worn her mother's clothing — clothing never photographed, never documented, clothing that existed only in a house that had been broken into twice without anything of value taken.

Except, apparently, value had been taken. Value had been carried off hanger by hanger by a man with a keycard and a grudge.

"You were in my apartment," she said. "Or you sent someone."

"I don't send anyone anywhere. I don't trust intermediaries. Intermediaries introduce errors." He glanced at the open cabinet, at the folders, almost fondly. "Everything I know, I verified myself. That's the difference between us, Ms. Reyes. You trusted the institution. I trusted the record."

"The record you altered."

"The record I *completed*." He said it without heat, which somehow made it colder. Then he stepped back — actually stepped back, giving her the aisle, giving her the door. "You should go."

Cyrus didn't move. Every instinct she owned was screaming the same contradictory instruction: *leave, stay, leave, stay.* "You're letting me walk out of here with photographs of your archive."

"I'm letting you walk out of here, period. Whether you take photographs is between you and your conscience, and we've established how reliable yours is." He adjusted his glasses. "Arresting you wouldn't be useful. Calling security would be tidy, and tidiness is your vice, not mine. Besides—" and here he smiled fully, and it was terrible, because it was genuine, "—you can't testify to any of this. Not the cabinet, not the annotations, not me. You broke in at three a.m. to photograph confidential files. Quade would have to arrest you. Your employer would have to fire you. Everything you are depends on being the person who finds the truth, and I've just spent ten minutes handing you a version of the truth that makes you the suspect. You'll notice I haven't denied anything. Denial is for people with something to lose."

"You're confessing."

"I'm *conversing*. There's a difference. Ask any court reporter." He gathered his coat off the back of the door — a plain gray coat, unremarkable, the coat of a man who had spent a decade making himself unremarkable — and paused with his hand on the frame. "My brother liked the river. Did your file mention that? Of course not. Files don't mention liking things. He used to skip stones off the pilings behind the old Meridian lab. Terrible at it. Three skips, maximum. He'd count them out loud." Voss's voice did not waver, and that was its own kind of unbearable. "When you reopen File 4471 — and you will, you're already composing the memo, I can hear it — I'd appreciate it if you included that. Three skips. Maximum. Somebody should remember something true about him, even if it has to be you."

Then he was gone, and the door clicked shut with the softness of a man who had all night and no fear.

* * *

Cyrus sat in her car in the morgue parking lot for forty minutes with the engine off and her hands at ten and two, driving nowhere.

Her memory, traitorous and perfect, replayed the conversation frame by frame. That was the curse of her gift: no blur, no mercy. She could re-watch Voss saying *you wound it wrong* forever. She could re-watch herself saying *I was paid to be careful*, and notice — now, too late, always too late — the exact moment his face changed. Not when she'd confessed. When she'd confirmed. He hadn't needed her confession. He'd needed her to say it out loud, in a room, to a witness, and the witness was him, and the recording was his memory, which was nearly as good as hers.

They matched, the two of them. That was the joke neither of them was laughing at. Two people who kept perfect records, one with a camera in her skull and one with a red pen in his hand, circling each other across a decade of paperwork.

Her phone buzzed. Quade.

*Tell me you're not doing what I think you're doing.*

Cyrus typed, deleted, typed again. *Define "what."*

*The morgue pinged an after-hours access alert. Loading bay door. Don't tell me that wasn't you.*

*It was me.*

A long pause, long enough that Cyrus watched the clock on the dashboard change minutes. Then:

*Alone?*

*Yes.*

*And?*

Cyrus looked at the dark building, at the one window on the fourth floor where a light still burned. He was up there. Logging her visit, probably. Grading it. *Subject entered at 0300 hours. Subject located archive in eleven minutes, exceeding expectations. Subject left alive.*

*I found what I was looking for,* she typed. *And then he found me.*

The reply took even longer this time.

*Cyrus. Are you okay?*

Such a simple question. Such a cop question. She thought about lying, decided lying to Marisol Quade was a debt she couldn't afford, and settled somewhere in the middle.

*He let me go. That's the part I can't stop thinking about. He had me dead to rights — breaking and entering, tampering, all of it — and he opened the door. Why would a man like that give up leverage?*

This time the answer came fast, and it landed like a diagnosis.

*Because leverage is only worth something if you want the game to end.*

Cyrus sat with that until her hands stopped shaking, which took longer than she'd admit to anyone.

Because he didn't want the game to end. He wanted it to arrive somewhere. All those corrections in red, all those graded autopsies, the museum of her failures — they weren't an ending. They were a syllabus. And a syllabus, she knew with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading documents written by people who thought they were being clever, always ended with a final exam.

She started the car. In the rearview mirror, the fourth-floor light went out — precisely, deliberately, a man turning off a lamp after finishing a chapter.

Tomorrow, she would go home and pull her brother-of-the-victim file — the real one, the one she kept locked in the closet behind the winter coats, the one she hadn't opened in ten years because opening it meant admitting what she'd signed.

Tonight, driving home through the wet streets, she let herself admit it in the privacy of her own head, where no one could subpoena it:

He was going to stage her death. It had been the plan from the beginning. Every corrected file, every stolen coat, every graded autopsy was rehearsal — and the final exam was already scheduled. He'd let her go because the proctor doesn't stop the student from studying.

He let her leave because he wanted her prepared.

The worst part — the part she'd never say aloud, not to Quade, not to anyone — was that some cold, honest chamber of her heart understood. If someone had done to her family what she'd helped do to his, she might have built a red-pen archive too.

Which meant that whatever happened next, she couldn't just catch Daniel Voss.

She'd have to beat him at the only game he'd ever cared about: getting every detail right.
