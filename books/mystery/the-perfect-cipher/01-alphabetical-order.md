## 1. Alphabetical Order

The kettle in the back room of Marginalia had a whistle like a question mark, and Tamsin Zhou had long ago stopped answering it.

They listened to it rise — that thin ascending note, climbing toward its little crisis of steam — and let it fall again on its own, because the silence afterward was part of the ritual. Rituals mattered. Rituals were the difference between a life and a pile of hours. At six-forty-five they unlocked the front door from the inside, stepped out onto the wet pavement of Corben Street, and looked both ways at an intersection that had not yet earned any traffic. Then they flipped the sign to OPEN, though no one would come before ten, and went back in to make the tea they had already made once at six.

The shop smelled the way it always smelled: old paper, binding glue, the ghost of pipe smoke from the previous owner, and beneath all of it something faintly mineral, like rain on stone, which Tamsin had never been able to source and had stopped trying to. It was the smell of a place holding still. Twelve years of holding still.

Tamsin's morning had a shape so fixed it could have been drawn by a cartographer. Tea first — oolong, steeped four minutes, never five, because five turned it bitter and bitterness was a small surrender. Then the returns cart: three books left over yesterday's closing, each to be reshelved by hand, spine aligned flush with its neighbors, the shelf's edge running true as a ruled line. Then the register count, written in a green ledger with columns so straight they looked typeset. Then the windows — glass wiped in vertical strokes only, because horizontal strokes caught the light wrong in the afternoon and made the display look smudged even when it wasn't.

Then, and only then, the alphabet.

Marginalia's fiction section ran A through Z along the north wall, and every author within each letter ran alphabetically too, and every title by a single author ran chronologically, because chronology was a kind of respect. Tamsin had bought the shop eight years ago with money that should have gone to retirement, and the first thing they'd done — before painting, before the new awning, before telling anyone in Hollowmere what they intended — was re-alphabetize everything. The previous owner had shelved by mood. By *mood*. Tamsin had spent three weeks with index cards and a stepladder imposing order on chaos, and when it was done they had stood in the middle of the floor with dust in their hair and felt, for the first time since 2013, something adjacent to safe.

Order was armor. They knew it was armor. Knowing didn't make it less necessary.

* * *

At seven-twelve, Mrs. Aldous passed the window with her terrier, as she did every morning at seven-twelve, and did not look in, as she did not look in every morning. This was not hostility. It was something more delicate — the particular courtesy of a town that had once needed Tamsin very badly and now did not know where to put them. Hollowmere had needed a detective once, the way a drowning man needs a rope, and then the rope had frayed, and then the man had drowned anyway, and nobody quite knew whether to thank the rope or blame it. So they nodded at Tamsin in the co-op and said "lovely weather" and otherwise let them be, and Tamsin had learned to receive this distance as a gift, or at least to wrap it in gift paper and keep it on a high shelf.

The terrier paused at the door, sniffed the gap beneath it, and moved on. Even the dog had decided against coming in.

Tamsin drank the second cup of tea standing behind the counter, which was also where they ate lunch, took phone calls, read manuscripts nobody had submitted, and slept sometimes, in the wingback chair, when the apartment upstairs felt too much like a held breath. The counter was oak, scarred, older than the shop itself; Tamsin had oiled it monthly for eight years until the grain came up dark and riverine. On it sat: the register, the ledger, a brass bell with a cracked handle, a jar of pens sorted by ink color, and nothing else. Nothing else was policy.

The savings lived in a spreadsheet Tamsin opened every Monday morning and closed without editing. The math was simple and getting simpler. Rent on the shop plus the flat above: eleven hundred a month. Average weekly take, this quarter: two hundred and forty pounds, and falling, because the town bought its books online now, in cardboard, sight unseen, alphabetical order be damned. Tamsin had run the projections in January with the same rigor they'd once applied to forensic timelines, and the answer had arrived with the same cold finality: fourteen months. Fifteen if they stopped heating the back room. Thirteen if the roof leaked again, and the roof would leak again; it leaked on a schedule as reliable as Mrs. Aldous and her terrier.

Fourteen months. Tamsin had closed the laptop and made tea and reshelved a book someone had left face-down and open, splayed like a shot bird, and had not thought about it again until now, standing at the counter, doing the arithmetic involuntarily, the way you can't stop counting stairs.

It didn't frighten them, exactly. What frightened them had happened twelve years ago and had a name they kept in a box. Everything since was just weather.

* * *

The first customer came at ten-oh-five, which was late by the standards of the regulars but early by the standards of hope. Dev Acharya, twenty-six, worked at the garage and read military history with an appetite that alarmed his mother. He came Tuesdays and Fridays, bought one book, stayed forty minutes, and talked about engines.

"Morning, Tam." He never managed the full name. Nobody did anymore. *Detective Inspector Zhou* had been retired along with the rest of it, and *Tamsin* sat oddly in most mouths, like furniture rearranged in a familiar room.

"Dev."

"Got anything on the Atlantic convoys? Proper stuff, not the glossy kind."

"Aisle three, bottom shelf, between Adlestrop and Ambrose." Tamsin didn't need to check. Tamsin hadn't needed to check in six years. "There's a Morison that's better than it looks. Ugly jacket. Good bones."

"You should put the good ones face-out," Dev said, the way he said it every time. "You'd sell more."

"I know," Tamsin said, the way they answered every time, and did not explain that face-out books were a lie told to browsers, that spines told the truth, that a wall of spines was honest the way a witness list was honest — names, order, nothing dressed up. Dev grinned and went off down aisle three, and Tamsin heard him find the Morison by its ugly jacket, because of course he did; the system worked. That was the whole consolation of systems. They worked whether or not anyone thanked them.

Through the front window, Hollowmere assembled itself in the grey light. The bakery van. The school crossing guard, Mrs. Okafor, raising her palm at a road empty of children because term hadn't started. The war memorial with its wet bronze names. A town of nine hundred souls that had once, briefly, been the center of a national story, and had hated it, and had spent twelve years sanding the memory smooth, and mostly succeeded.

Mostly.

Tamsin watched the street the way they watched everything now — with attention but without appetite, a sentry at a post whose war was over. Old habit. In the job they'd called it *reading the room*, and Priya Raghavan, back when she was a constable with ink-stained fingers and a hero-worship she tried to hide under professionalism, used to say, *You don't read rooms, guv, you interrogate them.* She'd meant it as a compliment. It had been accurate, too. Tamsin had never met a room they trusted.

Priya was Detective Inspector now herself, up in Ashford. They exchanged a card at Christmas. Two lines, printed, no warmth wasted. It was the correct amount of contact between people who shared a case the way some people share a grave.

* * *

The afternoon brought rain and no customers, which was normal, and then at half past three it brought the postman, which was also normal, and among the circulars and a utility bill there was a parcel — brown paper, string, addressed in a hand Tamsin did not know, slanted and careful, the letters formed rather than written.

Inside: a book. Secondhand. *The Collected Poems of W. S. Merwin,* a good edition, foxed at the edges. No note. No card. Just the book, and tucked into the gutter of page fifty-something, where a bookmark might live, a pressed flower — a violet, flattened to a stain of purple, older than it looked.

Tamsin stood very still in the back room with the rain ticking on the skylight.

They did not touch the flower. That was instinct older than retirement: don't disturb the scene. They looked instead, cataloguing the way they'd been trained to catalogue — the parcel paper, ordinary, hardware-store grade; the string, tied with a grocer's knot, right over left, left over right; the handwriting, ungendered, unhurried, the pen lifted cleanly between letters. Someone who cared how things looked. Someone who had time.

And the violet. Pressed violets were not a thing people did anymore. Pressed violets were a thing that had been done to Tamsin once, a lifetime ago, in a different life, by a different person, in a summer that existed now only in a sealed file and a locked storage unit across town.

No. That was sentiment talking, and sentiment was a bad investigator. Violets grew everywhere. Half the county pressed flowers into books; it was practically a regional industry. There was no reason —

There was no reason to feel the floor tilt.

Tamsin set the book down on the worktable, squared its edges with the table's edge, and made themselves go through the motions: checked the parcel for a return address (none), for postage stamps (none — hand-delivered, then, or franked somewhere local and carried the last mile by hand), for anything else hidden in the wrapping (nothing). Then they washed their hands, which was not procedure, just nerves wearing procedure's coat, and made tea they did not drink, and finally, at a quarter to five, with the light going blue outside, picked up the Merwin and turned to the marked page.

Page fifty-four. The poem was short. Tamsin read it twice, and the second time their mouth shaped the words without permission, because they had read this poem aloud once, years ago, to someone sitting on a sea wall with their shoes off, someone young and frightened and luminous with survival, someone Tamsin had promised — promised, with the whole weight of a detective's word, which is supposed to be the heaviest word there is — that they would keep safe.

*Your absence has gone through me / like thread through a needle. / Everything I do is stitched with its color.*

The shop was quiet. The rain had stopped. Somewhere below the floorboards, in the cellar where the overstock lived in ranked, labeled boxes, Tamsin could have sworn — though they would not have sworn to it, not professionally, not ever — that they heard a sound. A soft sound. Paper settling, perhaps. Or a box lid being set back down with care, the way you close a book you've finished reading.

Or the way you close one you've only just begun.

Tamsin stood in the doorway of the back room for a long moment, listening, the teacup cooling untouched in their hand. Then they did what they always did with things that frightened them. They put them in order. Book upright, parcel folded flat, flower transferred with tweezers into an envelope, envelope labeled in pencil: *RECEIVED [date]. UNSOLICITED. PROVENANCE UNKNOWN.*

They filed the envelope in the drawer beneath the register, under V — not for violet, but for the only other word that fit, the one they refused to write on the label.

Then they went upstairs, and checked the lock on the apartment door twice, and lay awake in the dark doing what they had done for twelve years, which was not remembering.

Below them, in the dark shop, the shelves held their breath in perfect alphabetical order, and waited.
