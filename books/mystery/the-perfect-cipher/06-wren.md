## 6. Wren

The drive to Ashcombe took forty minutes and cost me most of a tank of gas I couldn't afford to fill again until Friday. I told myself it was routine. A name on a list. The 2013 Marlow case had three surviving witnesses, and Wren Calloway was simply the first one I could find without asking anyone for help — no phone calls to old colleagues, no favors from records clerks who remembered my face from the wrong kind of newspaper.

That was a lie, of course. I was good at those. Twelve years of practice.

I'd found Wren the way I found everything now: through the internet, which had been young and stupid when we last spoke and had since grown into an archive more thorough than any I'd kept. *Wren Calloway, LCSW. Grief & Trauma Counseling, Ashcombe Center for Whole Recovery.* There was a photograph on the practice's website — Wren at thirty-four, silver-streaked hair cut close, standing in front of a wall of plants with the particular half-smile of someone who has learned exactly how much of themselves to show a camera. The eyes were the same. That was the thing about eyes; you could change everything else, but the eyes stayed like handwriting.

I sat in the parking lot of the Ashcombe Center for eleven minutes, engine ticking, watching clients come and go. A woman with a tissue already in her hand. A teenage boy in a letterman jacket walking like his shoulders were carrying furniture. Ordinary grief, ordinary traffic. And somewhere inside that beige building with its inspirational vinyl lettering on the glass — *HEALING IS NOT LINEAR* — was the person whose testimony I had once carried out of a courtroom in my own two hands, careful as a bird with a broken wing.

The person I'd promised, on a night neither of us should have been awake, that nothing would ever touch them again.

Something had touched them. Something wearing my scarf.

---

The receptionist smelled of peppermint oil and competence. "Do you have an appointment?"

"I'm an old friend," I said, which was also a lie, or had been once, before it became something else. "Tamsin Zhou. If you tell them I'm here, they'll see me."

I hadn't known if that was true until I said it. But the receptionist picked up her phone, murmured, listened, and her expression did something small and complicated — surprise folding into sympathy — before she hung up. "They'll be with you in a moment. Can I get you water?"

"No, thank you."

I stood in the waiting room among the plants and the soft lighting and the basket of smooth stones labeled *TAKE WHAT YOU NEED*, and I understood that I had walked into a machine built for exactly what I was about to try to do, and that Wren operated it.

The door opened. "Tamsin."

Not *Detective*. Not *Ms. Zhou*. Just my name, said the way you'd say the name of a book you'd read so many times the spine had gone soft.

Wren Calloway had gotten taller, or I had gotten shorter — time does one or the other. They wore a charcoal cardigan over a gray shirt, sleeves pushed up, forearms bare, a thin scar on the left wrist I didn't remember and made myself not look at twice. Their hair was iron and silver. Their hands were still beautiful, long-fingered, restless only when they thought no one was watching.

"Your receptionist looks worried," I said.

"She should. You have the face of someone about to ask me something terrible." Wren glanced back toward the hallway, then tilted their head toward a side door. "Come into my office. It's soundproofed. People say things in there they don't mean to say anywhere else."

"That doesn't worry you? Saying things?"

"It's the not-saying that worries me." They held the door. "It always has. With you especially."

---

The office was warm without being soft. Two chairs angled toward each other, a third by the window nobody would sit in. Certificates on the wall, real ones, earned. A box of tissues positioned with geometric precision between the chairs — close enough to reach, far enough away that reaching for it was a decision.

Wren sat, crossed their legs, and waited. That was the first unnerving thing: they let the silence run. Most people can't stand past four seconds. Wren gave it ten, watching me with the patient attention of someone being paid by the hour, and when I finally shifted, they almost smiled.

"You've gotten better at waiting," they said. "You used to fill silence like a man bailing water."

"You've gotten good at making it."

"Occupational hazard." They folded their hands. "You're here about the body in the ravine."

Not a question. I felt the floor tilt a degree, the way a ship tilts when you realize the horizon has moved. "How do you know about that?"

"Tamsin." Gently, the way you'd correct a child reaching for a stove. "It was on the news. A woman found dead in Miller's Ravine, dressed in clothes that belonged to a retired detective. Do you know how many retired detectives live within a hundred miles of this county?" They let that sit. "One. And I read everything that happens in this county. Old habit. When you grow up the way I grew up, you learn to watch the weather in other people's lives."

"The victim hasn't been publicly identified."

"No. But she will be. Dana Okafor, forty-one, home health aide. She used to clean offices at the county courthouse — including, I'd imagine, the records annex." Wren said it plainly, without flourish, the way you'd recite a grocery list, and I felt every hair on my arms rise. "You came all this way to ask me whether I knew her. So ask."

"Did you know her?"

"No." A pause, precise as a scalpel placement. "But I know why you're really asking, and it isn't about her. It's about the clothes. It's about the scarf with the *TZ* stitched in the collar, which your mother gave you the winter after your father died, and which you wore the entire year I knew you, and which you were wearing—" their voice caught, barely, a hairline fracture in poured concrete, "—the night you promised me nothing would ever touch me again."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere down the hall a door closed softly, the building breathing around us.

"You remember that night," I said.

"I remember every night of 2013. That's not a boast, Tamsin. It's a diagnosis." Wren leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the counselor fell away and something older looked out — nineteen years old, sitting on the curb outside the Marlow house with a blanket around their shoulders, refusing the hospital, refusing everyone but me. "PTSD doesn't erase. It files. Everything, cross-indexed, forever. Smells. Sounds. The exact weight of a blanket. The exact pitch of a detective's voice when he lies to make you feel safe." Their eyes held mine. "You lied beautifully. I want you to know that. I believed you for almost six months."

"Wren—"

"I'm not finished." No anger in it. Worse than anger: procedure. "Then I started reading about trauma responses, because I needed to understand what was happening inside my own skull, and I learned that protectors lie, and that some of them lie to the person they're protecting, and some of them lie to themselves, and the second kind is much more common and much more dangerous." They sat back. "So. Ask me what you came to ask."

I should have asked about Dana Okafor. About alibis, whereabouts, the last seventy-two hours. I had rehearsed the questions in the car, neat as index cards. Instead what came out was:

"Why grief counseling?"

Wren blinked — genuine surprise, the first unguarded thing they'd shown me. Then they laughed, short and low, and pressed the heel of a hand briefly against one eye. "Of all the questions. Twelve years, and that's the one you drive forty miles to ask."

"It matters."

"Does it? Or do you just need me to still be the person you saved?" They studied me, and I watched them decide to answer anyway, because that was the cruelty of Wren Calloway — they always answered, eventually, and the answers were always worse than the question deserved. "Because I spent nine years being the thing that happened to me. And then I spent three years learning that other people carry the same weight, and that watching them set it down, even a little, even badly — it was the first thing that ever made the carrying feel like it had a purpose." A shrug. "Also, grief is honest. Nobody stages a grief. What you see in that chair is what's actually there. After 2013, I wanted to spend my life in rooms where nothing was staged."

The word landed between us and neither of us picked it up.

*Staged.*

Priya's voice in my kitchen, three nights ago: *Whoever did this had access to sealed evidence photos. This wasn't improvised. It was composed.*

And here was Wren, fluent in the vocabulary of scenes, of staging, of what people show versus what's there — telling me, in effect, *I have built my entire life around the difference.*

Or telling me nothing at all. That was the trap of knowing someone. Every fact fit too well.

"Tell me about the case," I said quietly. "2013. Tell me what you remember."

And Wren's face changed — not closed, exactly. Organized. Like watching a librarian shelve a room in three seconds flat.

"What do you want first?" they said. "The house? The timeline? The part where Thomas Marlow's sister-in-law signed the guest ledger at 9:40 p.m. under a name that wasn't hers?" They ticked each item off on those beautiful fingers, unhurried, accurate, and my blood went cold and stayed cold. "Or the part your report left out?"

The radiator clicked. Down the hall, muffled, someone was crying, and someone else was saying *take your time, take your time.*

"You've read the file," I managed.

"I've *lived* the file." Wren stood, walked to the window, hands in their pockets, silhouetted against the parking lot where my car sat looking small and incriminating. "You think I spent twelve years not thinking about it? I got a license to sit with the worst days of strangers' lives, Tamsin. Do you know what I'm actually doing in that chair, hour after hour? I'm doing what you taught me on the curb. Watching. Filing. Waiting for the truth to walk in and sit down." They turned. In the window light their eyes were pale and bottomless. "Sometimes it takes twelve years. Sometimes it walks in wearing your mother's scarf on a dead woman's throat."

My mouth went dry. "Is that a confession?"

"It's a *session*," Wren said. "Fifty minutes. We're at forty-one." They checked their watch — an old habit, mine once, borrowed forever — and then did something I wasn't prepared for. They came around the desk and stopped close, closer than procedure, closer than safety, and their voice dropped to the register I had heard exactly once, on the curb, in the dark: *"Go home, Tamsin. Check your shelves. Count what's missing. And when you figure out what comes next — chapter two, chapter three — ask yourself whether you want to stop it, or whether you just want to be there when it ends."*

Then they stepped back, composed, kind, the respected counselor of Ashcombe County, and opened the office door.

"Thank you for coming in," Wren said warmly, loudly, for the benefit of the hallway. "Same time next week?"

---

I don't remember the drive home. That's not true — I remember it perfectly, which is the problem. Every mile filed and cross-indexed, the way Wren taught me without meaning to: the rain starting north of the county line, the wipers keeping imperfect time, my own hands at ten and two like a student driver, like a man holding a wheel over a drop.

At the shop I did what they told me to do, because apparently some part of me still took direction from Wren Calloway the way a compass takes direction from north.

I counted my shelves.

Poetry: complete. History: complete. The mystery section, alphabetized to the spine labels, immaculate — except that the gap where my own monogrammed scarf had lived in the closet upstairs now had a twin downstairs, a gap in the coat rack by the door shaped like a wool overcoat I hadn't worn since spring.

And lying flat on the counter, squared to the edge with a precision I recognized the way you recognize a signature on a check —

a returned book. *The Remains of the Day.* Inside, a bookmark of heavy cream paper, no writing except four words in a hand I'd know blindfolded:

**Chapter Two. Read carefully.**

I stood in my dark shop holding the note while the streetlight came on outside, and somewhere across town, or across the county line, a phone was ringing in a police station, and Detective Priya Raghavan was about to learn that the file had turned its page.
