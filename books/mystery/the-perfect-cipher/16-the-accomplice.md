## 16. The Accomplice

People imagine that killing requires rage. They picture clenched fists, hot blood, a scream tearing loose. They have watched too many films. What I have learned — what twelve years of listening to other people's grief taught me, hour by hour in a carpeted office smelling of chamomile and hand sanitizer — is that killing is mostly bookkeeping.

You keep accounts. You note what was taken. You determine the interest owed. And then, with great patience, you collect.

I was good at my work. That is not vanity; it is diagnosis. Grief counselors who are bad at it flinch from the details. The widow says *he died on a Tuesday* and the bad counselor hears only *he died*. The good counselor hears Tuesday — hears the way she holds the day like a stone she turns over and over, because the day itself is all she has left of him. I never flinched. I collected Tuesdays the way other people collect stamps. I have a whole album of them, filed behind my eyes: every client, every loss, every small terrible particularity that no one else would think to preserve.

Tamsin Zhou taught me that. Though Tamsin would not remember teaching it, because Tamsin taught it in a police interview room in 2013, when I was nineteen and shaking so hard the chair legs chattered against the linoleum, and a detective with careful hands and careful voice said: *Start at the beginning. Details matter. The details are how we know you're telling the truth.*

Details matter. Yes. I have built a cathedral out of details since then, and every stone of it came from that room.

* * *

Marcus arrives at six, as he always does, letting himself into the house on Alder Street with the key I gave him three months ago. He carries groceries. He always carries groceries. Tonight it is the makings of a simple dinner — chicken, lemons, something green — because Marcus believes that feeding someone is a form of prayer, and he prays for me nightly whether I ask him to or not.

"You look tired," he says, hanging his coat on the hook I installed at exactly the height his coat requires. I measured. Of course I measured. Marcus is six foot one and stoops slightly, an apology built into his posture, and his coat hangs better at sixty-eight inches than at the standard seventy.

"I've been working," I say.

"The case?" He says it carefully, the way people say *cancer* around the sick. "The one in the news?"

"The one in the news," I agree, and smile at him, and he smiles back with that open, unguarded face that made me choose him the way a carpenter chooses wood — by grain, by absence of knots.

Here is what Marcus knows: that I survived something terrible in 2013. That the detective who protected me later abandoned me, left town, let the case rot and my name curdle in it. That I have spent years unable to sleep, unable to finish sentences about that autumn, until recently — until the work began — and now I sleep like a child. Here is what Marcus believes: that the work is therapy. That I am writing a book. That the book is called *The Perfect Cipher*, a title I fed him gently, chapter by chapter, watching him warm to it the way you warm your hands at a fire you have told him is decorative.

Marcus thinks he is helping a survivor heal.

Marcus is helping me finish a testimony.

I chop lemons while he sears the chicken, and our shoulders touch at the counter, companionable, domestic, and I feel the ordinary human warmth of it — I want to be honest about that, even here, even now. I am not a monster in the storybook sense. Monsters don't notice the smell of thyme. I notice everything. That is the entire problem.

"Chapter Sixteen," Marcus says, dishing up. "Is that where we are?"

"We're ahead of schedule." I twirl pasta onto my fork. "The detective is starting to remember."

"Tamsin." He says the name softly, reverently, the way I trained him to without ever once instructing him. For months I spoke of Tamsin Zhou only in the tone reserved for wounds too deep to probe, and Marcus, being Marcus, learned to hold the name like something broken. "Do you think they'll understand? When it's done? What you were trying to say?"

I set down my fork.

This is the question, isn't it. This is the hinge on which everything swings. And Marcus asks it with such naked hope — hope *for* me, hope *in* me — that I almost tell him the truth right there over the lemon chicken. Almost.

"They kept a file on me," I say instead. "Did I tell you that? A physical file. Every detail of what happened, catalogued, cross-referenced. My clothes. My handwriting samples. The way I said certain words. They alphabetized my life, Marcus. And then they buried half of it."

"That's monstrous."

"It's worse than monstrous. It's *careful*." I laugh, and hear how the laugh sounds — bright, unhinged at one seam, quickly mended. "Monsters are careless. Tamsin Zhou was never careless. Which means everything in that file was a choice. Every omission was a choice. And if the file was a kind of testimony — their testimony — then someone has to complete it. Someone has to write the chapters they were too much of a coward to write themselves."

Marcus reaches across the table and takes my hand. His palm is warm and slightly floury. "And that's you."

"That's me," I say, and squeeze his fingers, and mean it, and do not mean it, and both of these things are true in the way that two things are true inside a cipher: separately legible, jointly unreadable.

* * *

Later, after he has washed the dishes and gone home to his small apartment above the hardware store, I sit at my desk with the file.

Not the original — the original lives in evidence storage under a seal Tamsin's own signature once broke and re-broke, a fact Priya Raghavan will discover soon enough, poor ambitious Priya, whose career is currently being built on my architecture like a house built on a grave. No. What I have is a reconstruction, assembled over four years from memory, from court records, from the sealed photographs Priya signed out in 2019 and never properly accounted for. People think secrets are hard to find. Secrets are the easiest things in the world. People carry them like organs, and organs show through skin if you know how to read the body.

I read bodies professionally.

Page forty-one. Photograph seven. The pose I reproduced in the ravine last month, down to the angle of the head, the fall of the hair — except I used Tamsin's own scarf, monogrammed, stolen from a closet I had walked through a hundred times in the years when Tamsin still let me close enough to see the hinges of their life. The scarf was the message. Not *look what I did*. Look what *you* did. Look what your file made of me.

Because that is the thing no one understands, the thing the newspapers will never print: I am not avenging myself. Revenge is small. Revenge is a closed loop, a fist closing on its own heat. What I am doing is *editing*. Tamsin wrote a document about 2013 that lied by subtraction, and a lie by subtraction is still a lie, and lies have consequences, and the consequence of this one is that I have spent twelve years being the wrong person in the wrong story — the traumatized witness, the poor broken bird, the girl-shaped hole in a case file — when the truth is that I was there. I saw all of it. I carried all of it. And the man whose death started everything, the man in photograph one, deserved a testimony that didn't stop halfway to protect the feelings of the person writing it.

Tamsin protected me. Everyone says it like a kindness. *They buried the evidence to protect you.* As if protection and erasure were the same act. As if being saved and being silenced were synonyms.

They alphabetized me. I am going to make them read me aloud.

* * *

On Thursday I go into town, because the rhythm matters. Killers in stories hide; killers in life shop, and wave, and ask about each other's mothers. I buy coffee at the café where the barista knows my order, and I walk past the bookshop — Zhou's Books, the paint peeling, the window display arranged with that heartbreaking precision Tamsin can't help, order as armor, shelves as ramparts — and I look in the glass at the figure moving between the aisles.

Older. Grayer at the temples. Still moving through the world like someone defusing a bomb that went off years ago.

I loved them. I need to write that down somewhere, even if only here, even if only in the ledger I keep behind my eyes: I loved Tamsin Zhou, at nineteen, in the worst year of my life, with the whole stupid desperate love of a drowning person for the rope. And the rope held. And then the rope climbed up out of the water and went home and filed a report, and I learned that being rescued is just another way of being handled.

The bell above the shop door chimes when I enter. Tamsin looks up from the counter, and I watch the recognition move through their face like weather — surprise, pleasure, fear, guilt, arriving in that exact order, each one legible, each one catalogued. They think they're reading me. They have always thought they could read me. It is their great gift and their fatal flaw: they read everyone, and they believe that reading is the same as knowing.

"Wren." Their voice does the careful thing, the interview-room thing. "It's been a long time."

"Too long." I trail a finger along the poetry shelf. Neruda. Rilke. The collected Dickinson, spine cracked from the year I lived above this shop and read it aloud in the evenings while Tamsin pretended to do inventory downstairs. "You still shelve by genre first, author second?"

"Alphabetical within category. Always."

"Always," I echo, and smile, and let the smile hold one beat longer than comfortable. "Some things survive anything, I suppose."

Their eyes flicker — toward the register, toward the phone, toward the door. They are wondering if I know about the murders. They are wondering if I know about the scarf. They are wondering, beneath all of it, in the place where they keep the things they buried, whether I have come to collect.

I buy the Dickinson. I pay cash. At the door I pause, hand on the frame, and say, without turning around:

"You should really recheck your files, Tamsin. I think you'll find you're missing a chapter."

Then the bell chimes behind me, and the street takes me back, and I am once again Wren Calloway, grief counselor, gentle voice, steady hands, the survivor everyone in two counties would swear on a Bible couldn't hurt a moth.

* * *

Marcus calls that night, excited, breathless. He's been to the bookshop too — he goes most Saturdays, has for years, that's how I found him, the quiet regular in the corner armchair reading mysteries he never buys. He tells me Tamsin seemed shaken today. He tells me the window got broken last week, did I hear, and all the books about secrets were turned face-out on the shelves, which he found — he searches for the word — *meaningful*.

"It's the book," he says. "Isn't it? Your book. It's working on them already. The truth is getting in."

"Yes," I say, lying in the dark with the phone against my ear, looking at the ceiling where the storm shadows move. "The truth is getting in."

"And when it's finished — when the last chapter's done — you'll be free. We'll both be free. I keep thinking about that. About after."

After. Poor Marcus, planning an after. I close my eyes and see page fifty-three of the reconstruction, the photograph of the courthouse steps, and the note I have already written, already sealed, already scheduled for delivery tomorrow morning: *The final scene will be performed at the old courthouse. Attendance is mandatory. The confession you never gave — I will give it for you.*

One chapter remains. In it, Tamsin will finally say aloud what the file only whispered. And I will stand in the crowd, or perhaps I won't — perhaps I will watch from the gallery above, from the place where witnesses sit — and when it is done, when the testimony is complete, the account will balance, and I will finally, finally be able to put down the weight I have carried since I was nineteen years old.

Unless Tamsin does what Tamsin always does. Unless they edit again.

I sit up in the dark, suddenly cold, struck by a thought I should have had weeks ago: Tamsin Zhou reads everyone. It is their gift. And I have been performing for them for months — the calm counselor, the wounded survivor, the returning ghost — performing flawlessly, flawlessly, flawlessly.

But flawless performance leaves no fingerprints. And Tamsin trusts nothing that leaves no fingerprints.

Which means that somewhere in that cluttered, meticulous, alphabetical mind, a question has already begun to form. And questions, in Tamsin Zhou's hands, become traps.

I look at the reconstruction on my desk, at the final chapter still blank, waiting for the courthouse — and for the first time since this began, I wonder whether I am finishing their draft, or walking into one.
