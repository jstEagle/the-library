## 6. Everyone Swears She's Dead

The coroner's office had moved twice since 1994 and shrunk each time, the way public institutions do when a town decides it would rather forget them. Dr. Halloran—*Mr.* Halloran, Mara corrected himself; coroners in these parts were solicitors first and doctors only by courtesy of an old statute nobody had repealed—worked out of two rooms above the chandlery on Quay Lane, behind a door whose frosted glass had been painted over so many times it was more white than glass.

Mara climbed the stairs at nine on Wednesday morning, carrying a folder of photocopied case notes under his arm like a hymnal. The smell of the stairwell was tar and old rope and, beneath it, something sweeter—the chandlery's stock of candle wax melting somewhere below.

Halloran was expecting him. That was the first thing Mara noticed, and the first thing that set his teeth on edge. When he'd telephoned the day before, the old man had said *come whenever suits you*, no questions, no wariness, as though a retired bookseller asking about a thirty-year-old drowning were the most natural errand in the world. As though he'd been waiting for someone to ask.

"Constable Iversen." Halloran rose from behind a desk buried under tide charts and unopened post. He was seventy-something now, a long grey face like driftwood, but his handshake was still dry and firm. "You'll be wanting to know about Brigid Calloway."

Mara hadn't mentioned her name on the phone. He kept his own face still, the way he'd learned to keep it still across three decades of customers haggling over paperbacks. "Among other things."

"You'll be wanting to know about her because you've had letters." Halloran sat back down and laced his fingers over his stomach. His eyes, pale as skimmed milk, didn't leave Mara's face. "I heard from Ewan Trelawny's hospice nurse last week. She told me a man had been asking after him. A man who used to be police. I put the rest together myself. I'm old, Mr. Iversen, not stupid."

"And what did you conclude?"

"That the past has come up on the tide again, same as it always does." He gestured at the chair opposite. "Sit down. Ask me what you came to ask."

Mara sat. The chair creaked under him like a boat taking water. "Brigid Calloway," he said. "She was the last person known to have seen Ailsa Renwick alive. She gave evidence at the inquest. And a week later she was dead herself. Drowned. Is that right?"

"That is what the record says." Halloran reached into a drawer without looking, the way a man reaches for a pipe or a pair of spectacles, and produced a thin buff folder. He laid it on the desk between them but did not open it. "Drowned off the Gull Rocks, seven days after the inquest closed. Body identified by clothing and by a ring. Cremated in Penzance. Ashes scattered, according to the family—though she hadn't any family left in the county by then, so I've never been entirely sure who did the scattering."

"The record says," Mara repeated. "You're choosing your words carefully, Mr. Halloran."

"I'm an old man who'd like to die in this town and be buried in its churchyard, and there are people here who'd deny me both if I spoke carelessly." Halloran opened the folder at last, turned it around, and pushed it across. "Read it. Then ask me again."

The report was short. Shorter than any death report Mara had ever handled, even in those years when he'd been too green to know how much paper a proper investigation generated. Date of discovery: 14 October 1994. Location: rocks below the coast path east of Gull Point. Circumstances: apparent suicide following bereavement-related distress. Identifying features: clothing, a silver ring engraved *B.C.*, and—"and what?" Mara looked up.

"Go on," Halloran said quietly. "It's the last line. Read the last line."

*Body viewed and identified at scene by Coroner's Officer. Remains released for cremation without further examination.*

"There's no medical examiner's signature," Mara said slowly. "There's no description of the body beyond the clothing and the ring. There's no photograph. There's nothing here that proves anyone saw a face."

"No," Halloran agreed. "There isn't."

"Whose handwriting is this?"

"Mine. Dictated to me over the telephone by the officer who attended the scene, and countersigned by my predecessor before I'd finished writing it." Halloran's voice was flat as slate. "I was three months into the job. I asked why there was no doctor present. I was told the body had slipped into deep water before it could be secured and that identification had been made at the scene by persons qualified to make it. I asked who those persons were. I was told that question had already been answered to the satisfaction of everyone who needed satisfying, and that if I valued my position I'd initial the page and move on to the next one."

"So you initialed the page."

"I initialed the page." For the first time the old man's composure cracked—not into guilt exactly, but into something wearier and older than guilt, the expression of a man who had carried a small stone in his shoe for thirty years and had finally stopped noticing it except on cold mornings. "I was twenty-nine years old and I had a wife and a baby and a mortgage, and the man on the telephone was a superintendent, and I did what I was told. You of all people will understand that, Constable Iversen. They tell me you understood it very well once."

The words landed where they were meant to land. Mara let them sit there. Outside the window a fishing boat was coming in on the morning tide, its wake folding the harbour water into long grey pleats.

"Who identified the body?" he asked at last.

"I don't know. I never knew. But I'll tell you what I do know, since you've come all this way and since I'm past caring what it costs me." Halloran leaned forward. "A week after Ailsa Renwick drowned, half this town believed Brigid Calloway had killed her. Jealousy, they said. Over a man—Trelawny, some said; over money, others said. Her evidence at the inquest had been shaky. She'd seen a boat, she said, but couldn't say whose. She'd heard voices, she said, but couldn't swear to them. The town decided she was lying, and then the town decided she was guilty, and then she died, and the town decided God had settled it. It was very tidy. It was the tidiest thing I ever saw in forty years of coroners' work, and tidiness in a drowning is the one thing that should make you look harder, not close the file."

"And you never looked harder?"

"I looked harder exactly once." Halloran smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of self-knowledge. "I wrote to the registrar in Penzance asking for the cremation certificate. Do you know what came back? Nothing. No certificate. No record of a cremation at any Penzance chapel or crematorium on or near that date. I wrote twice more. After the second letter I received a visit from a solicitor acting for the harbour trust, informing me that further correspondence on the matter would constitute harassment and that the trust took a dim view of harassment of its officers. I was thirty years old, Mr. Iversen. I stopped writing letters."

Mara closed the folder. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, something was moving with slow, cold purpose, like water finding cracks in a wall. "The harbour trust," he said. "That's the second time today I've heard that name."

"It won't be the last." Halloran stood, signalling an end, and walked him to the door. On the landing, with the harbour spread out below them—bright, innocent, busy with gulls—he gripped Mara's arm with surprising strength. "One more thing, and then we're done. The night Brigid Calloway supposedly died, I was called out to a different matter entirely—a heart attack at the Trelawny house, Ewan's mother, God rest her. I drove the coast road both ways, going and coming, past the turnoff to Gull Point. Both times there was a car parked at the layby. Both times, the same car. Dark blue Rover, county plates. I mentioned it to the sergeant—you'd have known him—and he told me I'd been mistaken, and that was the end of it. But I wasn't mistaken. I'd sold that model of car myself, before I studied law. I know what a dark blue Rover looks like at dusk."

"Whose car was it?"

"If I knew that, I'd have been a braver man than I was." Halloran released his arm. "But I'll tell you this for free, Constable. In this town, when everyone swears to the same story, word for word, year after year—that's not memory. That's maintenance. Somebody's been keeping that story painted."

* * *

The retired harbourmaster lived in a low stone cottage at the top of Fore Street, the last house before the road gave up on the town and surrendered to moorland. His name was Jago Pentreath, and Mara remembered him from 1994: a barrel of a man with forearms like mooring posts and a voice that could carry across a gale without a megaphone. Thirty years had bent him into a question mark, but the voice, when he opened the door, was unchanged.

"Mara Iversen." Pentreath looked him up and down with the frank appraisal of a man used to judging vessels. "They said you'd gone soft. Books and biscuits. Come in, then, if you've come about the Calloway woman, and mind the cat."

The cottage interior was a museum of a working life: brass instruments, framed photographs of trawlers, a ship's bell polished bright enough to see your face in. The cat, an enormous grey tom, occupied the best chair by the fire and permitted them to sit elsewhere.

Pentreath poured tea without being asked, strong enough to stand a spoon in, and listened while Mara laid out his questions with the same careful phrasing he'd used on Halloran. Brigid Calloway. The drowning. The record.

"Aye, she drowned herself," Pentreath said, when Mara finished. "Poor lass. Guilt does that. She knew more about Ailsa Renwick's death than she told at the inquest, and it ate her alive. We all said so at the time."

"We?"

"The harbourmen. The lifeboat crew. Everyone who worked the water." He shrugged, a mountain range shifting. "Water gives up its dead, Mr. Iversen. Always has, always will, sooner or later. Except hers never came up. Which proved how far out she must have gone, see. Past the rocks, past the soundings. Determined to drown, she was."

Mara set down his cup. "Mr. Pentreath. May I see your logbooks?"

The old man went very still. The fire ticked. The cat opened one eye and considered them both, then closed it again.

"Now why," Pentreath said slowly, "would a bookseller want to see my logbooks?"

"Because I was a policeman for eleven years, and I know that a harbourmaster's logbook records every body recovered from these waters for the last fifty years. Because Dr. Halloran just told me there's no cremation certificate for Brigid Calloway anywhere in Cornwall. And because I think you've been waiting thirty years for someone to ask."

For a long moment Pentreath said nothing. Then he laughed—a short, harsh bark with no humour in it, the laugh of a man kicking an engine that won't start. "Waiting. Aye. That's one word for it." He hauled himself out of his chair, crossed to a dresser against the far wall, and knelt with a groan that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than his knees. From beneath the bottom drawer he drew a canvas-wrapped bundle, heavy and square, and laid it on the table between the teacups.

"My logs. Forty-one years of them. Every launch, every recovery, every wreck, every body the sea gave back." His hands, thick-knuckled and spotted, unwrapped the canvas with unexpected tenderness. "When I retired in '98, I was supposed to hand them all over to the trust. All but one. This one." He tapped the volume for 1994. "This one I kept. And every year since, on the fourteenth of October, I take it out and I read one page, and I put it away again, and I tell myself next year I'll do something about it. And next year I don't. Because I'm a coward, Mr. Iversen, and cowards live long lives. Look at me."

He opened the book to a ribbon marker and turned it toward Mara.

The entry was dated 14 October 1994, in Pentreath's blocky, uncompromising hand:

*0740. Report of body sighted below Gull Pt. Launch dispatched w/ Coxswain T. and Dep. Hmstr. Searched 2 hrs. NO BODY RECOVERED. Clothing fragments recovered from rocks (1 shoe, ladies, size 5). Sea state rough. Search suspended 1030 on instruction of Harbour Trust. Further search prohibited.*

Mara read it twice. Three times. The words did not rearrange themselves into anything kinder.

"On whose instruction?" he asked. His voice sounded distant to himself, as if it were coming through the hull of a boat.

"Deputy harbourmaster took the call. Man named Corran Vase, if you can believe it—trust man, liaison, always hanging round the office those weeks. He said the family wished it. Said dragging the water would distress them further." Pentreath's jaw worked. "Distress *them*. Aye. And then a week later, before I could raise it at the board meeting, the death certificate comes through signed and sealed, and there's nothing left to raise. Case closed. Body cremated. Poor lass. Move along."

"But there was no body."

"There was no body." Pentreath said it simply, the way a man states the weather. "No body, no inquest, no certificate ought to exist. But certificates are paper, and paper is written by men, and men in this town write what the trust tells them to write. I learned that young. I just never learned to say it out loud until tonight."

Mara stared at the page. The firelight made the ink gleam faintly, as though the words were still wet. *NO BODY RECOVERED.* Four words, capitalised in the old convention for emphasis, sitting in a ledger like a stone in a shoe. Somewhere beneath the dread, a colder thought was forming, precise and terrible: *if Brigid Calloway never went into the water, then someone wanted the town to believe she had. And the only reason to fake a drowning is that the person you're drowning is still alive—or that killing her outright would raise questions a quiet disappearance wouldn't.*

"She ran," he said aloud. "Or someone helped her run. And the trust let the town believe she was dead."

"Or the trust needed her dead on paper," Pentreath said, "for reasons that had nothing to do with grief at all." He closed the book and wrapped it again, slowly, like a man dressing a wound. "There's one thing more. The shoe we recovered—ladies' shoe, brown leather, size five. I logged it into evidence. Two days later it was gone from the store. Signed out by the same man. Vase. I asked him what the family wanted with a dead woman's shoe, and he looked at me the way you'd look at a dog that's barked at the wrong stranger, and he said, *Jago, some things belong in the sea.*"

The fire had burned low. Outside, the light was failing over the moor, and the first lamp in Fore Street came on below the window, a small yellow defiance against the gathering dark.

Mara stood. His legs felt odd, as though the floor of the cottage were pitching gently, the way decks do. "Why give me this now? Why keep it thirty years and hand it to the first man who asks?"

Pentreath looked at him for a long time, and in the old man's pale eyes Mara saw something he recognised from his own mirror: the particular shame of a man who obeyed when obedience was unforgivable.

"Because you're the one they sent the letters to," Pentreath said. "And because whoever wrote that second letter—the one telling you to ask about the witness—they wanted you digging. So somebody, somewhere, thinks the truth can still hurt the right people. And I'm too old to dig, Mr. Iversen. But I'm not too old to hand a spade to a younger man." He pushed the wrapped logbook across the table. "Take it. Keep it somewhere they can't burn it. And when you find out who parked that dark blue Rover at Gull Point—because you will find out—say her name out loud. Say *Brigid Calloway* where the whole town can hear it. That's all I ever wanted. Somebody to say the name."

Mara took the book. It weighed almost nothing and felt like it weighed everything.

He walked down Fore Street in the dark with the canvas bundle under his coat, and the town closed around him—lit windows, supper smells, a dog barking somewhere above the quay—and for the first time in thirty years Mara Iversen understood that the silence he'd taken for peace had never been peace at all. It had been held. Maintained. Painted fresh, year after year, by hands he was only beginning to name.

And somewhere in that silence, if a shoe and a ring and a forged certificate were anything to go by, a woman everyone swore was dead might be walking around alive—hidden in plain sight, kept quiet by money, waiting for whatever the letters were pulling toward.

He reached the shop, unlocked the door, and stood a moment in the dark among his books, listening to the tide turn in the harbour below.

Then he went inside, bolted the door behind him, and did not sleep.
