## 8. Vivienne's Ledger

The rain came in off the sea the way it always did in Greywater Bay—not falling so much as arriving sideways, like something with an errand. Mara Iversen stood across the road from Renwick House for the better part of an hour, beneath the dripping eave of a shuttered chandlery, telling himself he was only looking.

He had been telling himself that since noon.

The house sat above its own terraced gardens like a ship run aground and refitted as a monument: granite walls the colour of wet slate, windows tall and dark, a weathervane in the shape of a brigantine turning slowly against the grey sky. Thirty years ago he'd been inside it once, as a constable taking statements after Ailsa Renwick's body came out of the water. He remembered the smell of beeswax and cold tea, the portrait of Aldous Renwick on the stair, and the way the whole household had moved around him like water around a stone—polite, seamless, impenetrable. He had been twenty-four years old and invisible then. He was fifty-eight now, and invisibility, he was learning, could be worn like armour if you stopped resenting it.

Vivienne's car was gone. It had gone at ten past four, taillights flaring briefly in the rain as she turned toward the coast road—dinner in Penzance, her housekeeper had mentioned to the fishmonger, who had mentioned it to Ruth Calloway, who had telephoned the bookshop with the particular satisfaction of a woman handing over ammunition. Mara had let the phone ring three times before answering. Some part of him still wanted to be the sort of man who didn't take calls like that.

But the second letter had said *ask about the witness*, and the harbourmaster's logbook said no body was ever recovered from Brigid Mennear's drowning, and the coroner's file said *suicide, presumed* with a presumption so thin you could read the truth through it like newsprint through cheap paper. And somewhere behind all of it, someone had written to him twice—once to confess, once to warn—and both letters knew things about 1994 that only two kinds of people could know: the guilty, and the dead.

He crossed the road.

* * *

The wall around the Renwick grounds was older than the house, built of the same granite, topped with shards of slate set in mortar like a jaw of broken teeth. In his day there had been a gap where the wall met the boathouse, a place where boys slipped through to steal apples or row out to the wreck they weren't supposed to know about. The gap was still there, wider now, framed by a rusted hinge and a door that hadn't closed properly since the last century. Mara supposed that told him something too: that the family's sense of being untouchable had outlasted even their interest in locking anything.

The garden was a wilderness of wet roses and statuary gone green with lichen. He kept to the gravel path because gravel announces strangers and flagstones forgive them, and he came around the east wing to the estate office—a low annex built onto the house's flank sometime in the fifties, with its own door and its own small windows, the kind of room where men like Aldous Renwick did the arithmetic of other people's lives.

The door was locked. Of course it was. But the window beside it was the original casement, wood swollen and paint cracked, and when Mara worked his penknife into the frame the way he'd once learned to do in a police training room in Truro, thirty years of rot gave up without a fight. The latch clicked open with a sound no louder than a knuckle cracking.

He stood in the rain for a moment afterward, listening. Wind in the pines above the terrace. The sea working at the rocks below, patient as a clerk filing papers. Nothing else.

*I'm not a policeman anymore,* he thought, easing the window up. *I'm a bookseller committing burglary.* The thought should have frightened him. What it did instead was make him feel, for the first time in weeks, entirely awake.

Inside, the office smelled of dust and old paper and, faintly, of pipe tobacco long smoked but never quite gone—the ghost of Aldous Renwick hanging in the room like a tenant who refused to be evicted by death. A desk under the window, oak, scarred. Filing cabinets along one wall, brass handles dulled to brown. Shelves of ledgers and bound volumes, shipping registers, tide almanacs going back decades. A lamp with a green glass shade. Everything ordered, everything labelled, everything exactly where a careful man would have left it—because careful men believed order was a form of innocence.

Mara pulled on the cotton gloves he'd brought in his coat pocket—the same gloves he used for handling first editions—and began with the cabinets.

The first held correspondence, filed by year, and he made himself go slowly through 1994 despite the cold crawling up his arms. Letters of condolence. Letters from solicitors regarding probate. A single letter from the Harbour Trust, typed on thick cream stock, thanking Aldous for his "discretion in a difficult period" and confirming that "the matter of the seabed lease will be regularised at the next quarter-day." No signature he recognised. No explanation of what discretion meant. But the date was eleven days after Ailsa drowned, and six days before the inquest returned its verdict of accidental death, and Mara stood holding the page under the weak light from the window and felt the old shame move in his chest like water finding a crack.

He photographed it with his phone, hands steadier than he expected them to be, and moved on.

* * *

It took him forty minutes to find the ledger, and when he found it, it wasn't hidden at all.

That was the thing that unsettled him most, later—that it sat on the shelf with all the others, spine unmarked except for the year ranges stencilled in fading gilt: *1979–2004*. Aldous Renwick had not concealed it because Aldous Renwick had never imagined anyone would come looking. Secrecy, for men like him, was not a lock on a door. It was simply confidence that no one would ever think to turn the handle.

Mara carried it to the desk, laid it flat, and opened it under the green-shaded lamp he now allowed himself to switch on, low.

The early pages were what he expected: estate accounts, wages for gardeners and grooms, payments to the Harbour Trust, charitable subscriptions to the church roof fund, sums entered in a small, precise, upright hand. Aldous's hand. Mara had seen it before, on the statement Aldous had given police in '94—*I cannot imagine why anyone would harm my cousin; she was a dear girl, though headstrong about boats*—and the memory of that bland, unhurried sentence rose in him now with a bitterness that surprised him by its freshness.

Then, in November 1994, a new entry.

*B. M. — £500.*

No explanation. No category. Just the initials and the sum, entered between a payment to the farrier and the quarterly coal bill, as ordinary as weather.

Mara turned the page. December: nothing. Then, the following November, again: *B. M. — £500.* And the year after. And the year after that. The same month each year—the third week of November, which Mara counted back from and found landed within days of the anniversary of Ailsa Renwick's death. Five hundred pounds, rising over the years as the pound itself shrank—£650, then £800, then, by the nineties' end, £1,000—paid every year, every year, every year.

Brigid Mennear. B. M. The witness who had seen something on the shore that night, whose statement had been taken and then somehow dissolved, who had drowned herself in the estuary a week after the inquest according to every soul in Greywater Bay—or according, at least, to every soul who had ever been asked, which amounted to the coroner, the harbourmaster, and the collective, comfortable silence of a town that preferred its tragedies tidy.

Except no body had ever been recovered. And here was Aldous Renwick's own ledger, paying her, annually, for thirty years.

Mara sat down in Aldous's chair without meaning to. The leather creaked under him, and for a moment he had the vertiginous sense of occupying the dead man's shape, seeing the room as Aldous must have seen it—this desk, this lamp, this book open under his hands—and understanding, with a clarity that felt almost physical, what kind of man writes a bribe into his household accounts between the coal and the farrier. Not a man tormented. Not a man afraid. A man doing maintenance. A man servicing a debt the way one services a roof: regularly, without sentiment, because neglect would cost more.

Ailsa Renwick had been nineteen years old. She had been struck hard enough to bruise bone, and she had gone into water cold enough to stop a heart in minutes, and the man responsible had paid five hundred pounds a year to keep the only witness quiet, and had recorded it in the same book as the church roof fund.

Mara realised his jaw ached and made himself unclench it.

He turned forward through the decades, faster now, following the entry like a thread through dark cloth. 1998. 1999. 2003, the last full year of Aldous's life. Then, in November 2004—the year Aldous died—the entry appeared in a different hand. Still neat, but rounder, less upright, the ink blue-black instead of black.

Vivienne's hand. Mara had seen it often enough in the past fortnight: on the condolence card she'd sent to Ewan Trelawny's hospice, on the note she'd left at the bookshop after their interview, all charm and italic flourishes. *B. M. — £1,200,* written in Vivienne Renwick's elegant, confident script, the year her father died.

She had found the ledger. She had understood it. And she had kept paying.

Mara turned the pages forward with a care that had nothing to do with the gloves. 2005. 2010. 2015. Every November, the same entry, the same hand, the sums creeping upward with the cost of everything else. 2020. 2022. Last year. *B. M. — £2,400.*

Thirty years. A living ghost, salaried by the dead.

* * *

He might have sat there until dawn if the sound hadn't reached him.

At first he took it for the sea—it had that same rhythm, that patient push and pull—but the sea was below the terrace and this was above it, on gravel, and it resolved itself, as he went cold and still, into footsteps. Two sets. Unhurried. Voices, low, indistinct, the words eaten by the rain but the cadence unmistakable: people talking comfortably, people with every right to be where they were.

The lights of a car swept across the curtains of the main house, throwing the shapes of the window frames huge and crooked across the office ceiling, and Mara did what thirty years of instinct told him to do, which was nothing. He switched off the lamp. He closed the ledger. And then, instead of fleeing through the window he'd come in by—which would have put him in open view of the drive—he stepped backward into the space between the filing cabinets and the shelving, a slot of darkness perhaps eighteen inches wide, and stood there while the office door's lock rattled and the door opened and light spilled across the floorboards a yard from his shoes.

Two people came in, shaking off rain. A woman's voice, low and amused: "—told you, the heating's on a timer, it's like a mausoleum until six." A man answered, something about the cellar and a case of the '09 claret, and Mara placed them without seeing them: Vivienne, home early, and a guest, some friend from the coast road dinner, the evening rearranged around weather. Their footsteps crossed toward the cellar door in the corner. A bolt drew back. Bottles clinked, muffled, distant.

Mara breathed the way he'd once been taught to breathe in stakeout cars—slow, through the nose, counting—and looked at the ledger lying shut on the desk in plain view, and understood that he had perhaps ninety seconds to decide what kind of thief he intended to be.

Taking it was impossible; its absence would be noticed by morning, and whatever else he was now, he couldn't afford to announce himself. Leaving it intact bought him nothing either, not really—except that photographs, he reminded himself, were evidence enough. He had the entries. He had the dates. He had the change of hand in 2004 that tied Vivienne to thirty years of hush money she had claimed, in his own shop, over tea she'd barely touched, to know nothing about.

In the cellar below, the woman laughed at something.

Mara lifted the ledger, opened it to the November pages, and photographed them methodically—every entry, every year, the transition from Aldous's hand to Vivienne's, the final payment from last autumn. His phone's shutter click seemed enormous in the silent room. He muted it with a thumb and started over, and finished, and closed the book, and slid it back onto the shelf in the exact position his memory insisted it had occupied, spine flush with its neighbours, dust line undisturbed.

The cellar bolt shot home. Footsteps returned, paused—he felt rather than heard them pause—near the desk. Near him. Eighteen inches of cabinet and shadow and his own held breath.

"Did you leave the lamp on?" the woman said.

"I switched it off," the man said. "Green one? Yes. Off."

A silence, three seconds long, in which Mara's heart performed the only honest work in the room.

"Old houses," Vivienne Renwick said lightly, and the door closed behind them, and the lock turned, and their voices receded up the gravel toward the lit windows of the main house, arguing pleasantly about wine.

Mara stayed in the dark another five minutes anyway. Then he let himself out through the swollen window, pulled it down behind him, and walked back through the wet roses with the rain on his face and the photographs burning in his pocket like coals.

* * *

He did not go straight home. He walked down through the town instead, past the darkened shops, past the harbour where the boats knocked gently at their moorings, and stood a while at the rail above the slipway where the water lay black and moving. This was the shore. Not the exact spot—nothing was ever the exact spot—but near enough. Somewhere out there, under the grey swell, lay the wreck the trust owned the seabed over, and somewhere on this stone, thirty years ago, a girl had died for knowing about it, and a boy in a constable's uniform had written down what he was told to write.

He had come to the ledger hoping for a name. He had found something worse than a name: a system. Payments like tides, regular and impersonal, sustained across three decades and one death and one inheritance—as though the silence itself were an asset of the estate, managed, maintained, passed down with the silver and the shipping shares. And Vivienne had signed her name to it, year after year, in that beautiful hand. Out of fear, maybe. Perhaps even out of something she mistook for kindness. It didn't matter. Fear explained; it did not excuse. He was learning the difference late, and at cost.

The triumph he'd felt at the desk, that hot clean surge of *found it, found it, found it*, had curdled somewhere on the walk down into something colder and more familiar. Because a payment requires a payee. For thirty years, someone had collected. Someone had cashed those cheques or received those transfers, someone with a bank account and an address and a reason to keep taking money from the family of the woman she'd watched die—or the woman she'd helped bury, if the letters were to be believed, if Ewan Trelawny's confession was the confession of a boatman and not merely the confession of a dying man's guilt.

And whoever collected that money knew, tonight, that Mara Iversen was asking questions. The second letter had told him so plainly enough: *she isn't the last*. The threat had already arrived at his shop once, in ink. It needed only a reason to arrive in person.

He turned up the lane toward the bookshop, and saw, before he saw anything else, that the streetlight outside it was out.

He stopped. The lamp had been working that morning—he remembered its orange cone on the wet pavement, the moth that battered itself against it every night in season. Now the window of the shop lay in darkness, and the darkness had a wrongness to it, a texture, the particular quality of a room recently disturbed. Rain ticked on the awning. Down the street, a dog began to bark, not frantically but steadily, the way dogs bark at something that isn't leaving.

Mara stood very still at the mouth of the lane, an old man in the rain with thirty years of other people's secrets in his pocket, and understood with perfect clarity that the ledger had not been the discovery tonight.

It had been the invitation.
