## 17. Rising Tide

The ledger had lived for three days in the space behind the till, wedged between the *Oxford Dictionary of Quotations* and a water-damaged box of Agatha Christies that no one had ever bought. Mara had told himself this was caution. He knew better. It was the same instinct that had kept his old case notes in a biscuit tin under the floorboards for thirty years—the instinct of a man who could not bring himself to destroy a thing and could not bring himself to be caught holding it.

Brigid's testimony was not written down. She would not permit it. "Paper burns," she'd said in the hospice room, her voice dry as driftwood, "and paper can be found." So her account existed only in his memory and hers, two old people holding the same fire between them, and he had begun to understand that this was the point. She had spent thirty years being the only witness. She intended, in the end, to be the only witness again—spoken, not filed, given mouth to mouth in front of the town rather than surrendered to an evidence bag and a courtroom that could be managed.

He had argued with her about it. Gently, at first, and then not gently at all.

"You want justice," she'd said, "or you want a file? I've seen what this town does with files, Mr. Iversen. I was one."

Now, closing the shop on Thursday evening, he paused with his hand on the light switch and looked at the ledger's hiding place and thought, not for the first time, *I should move it.* And thought, as he always thought, *Tomorrow.* The shop had made him soft. Thirty years of shelving other people's secrets had taught him that most secrets keep, that the world's appetite for digging is shallower than its appetite for forgetting. He had built his whole life on that arithmetic.

He turned off the light and went home to his flat above the chandler's, and he slept, for the last time in that life, like a man with nothing between himself and the truth.

* * *

He woke to the smell.

Smoke has a grammar. Woodsmoke speaks of hearths and comfort; coal smoke of industry; but the smoke that came through the gap beneath his door at twenty past two in the morning had a different syntax entirely—petrochemical, greedy, wrong. He was upright before his mind had finished the sentence, and by the time he reached the window he could see it: an orange bloom pulsing behind the shop's rear wall, the stock room window a rectangle of furious light.

He did not remember the stairs. He remembered the cold of the cobbles through his socks, the harbour water black and flat as poured tar, the heat arriving at his face in waves that came faster the closer he ran. The back door was already too hot to touch. Through the stock room window he could see the shelves of the overflow stock curling, the spines of a hundred unsold paperbacks lifting and blackening like leaves in a bonfire, and above it all the smoke rolling along the ceiling in a slow, deliberate tide.

*The ledger.*

He knew, even as his hand closed on the iron latch of the side gate, that the ledger was ash. He knew it the way you know a thing in dreams. And he went in anyway.

The shop's interior was a cathedral of smoke. He dropped low, where the air still held, and crawled the length of the biography section on his forearms, the heat pressing down on his back like a hand. The till. The shelf. His fingers found the gap behind the *Dictionary of Quotations* and closed on—nothing. On the crumbled spine of a Christie and the cold brick behind it. Gone. Taken, or burned where it lay; it didn't matter which. The ledger had been the whole point of the fire, and the fire was the message, and both had been delivered.

He should have crawled out then. Any sensible man would have. Instead his hand, moving through the smoke on its own errand, found the shelf beneath the till where he kept the things that were not for sale: the biscuit tin with his old case notes. The two letters. The third letter, the threat, which he'd kept because a threat is also a document. And the tide table—Ailsa's tide table, with her small left-hooked handwriting climbing the margins like ivy, the boat that shouldn't have been out, the times she'd noted in a schoolgirl's careful pencil.

The tin was warm. The table was not yet burning. He folded it into his shirt against his chest, gathered the letters blind, and crawled back out through a world that had narrowed to heat and coughing and the sound of his own name being shouted—Ruth's voice, he would learn later, Ruth who had seen the glow from her father's old house on the hill and run down in her dressing gown to find the bookshop wearing a crown of fire.

He came out through the side gate as the first engine arrived, and sat down hard on the cobbles with the tide table crushed against his sternum, and coughed until he tasted blood and salt, and could not have said which of them was his.

* * *

They let him back past the tape at dawn, when the fire had gone down to a red ruin and the harbour smelled of char and brine. The stock room was gone. The rear third of the shop was a black cavity open to the sky, the joists like ribs, and the rain—because of course it had begun to rain, the grey sea-light coming down soft and indifferent—hissed and steamed in the wreckage.

Ruth found him standing in the doorway of his own ruined refuge.

"You went *in*," she said. Not a question. Her hands were shaking; she'd wrapped them in a blanket she'd brought from somewhere and now she wrapped the rest of it around his shoulders, because he was soaked and shivering and had not noticed.

"The ledger's gone."

"I know. I'm sorry." A pause. "Mara. You went in for paper."

He looked at her. In the grey light, with the smoke still rising off the ruin, he found he could say it plainly, which surprised him. "It was the only thing left of her handwriting. Of what she knew. Everything else about Ailsa Renwick is other people's version—her family's version, the trust's version, the inquest's version. That table was her own voice. I wasn't going to stand in the street and listen to it burn."

Ruth nodded slowly, and he saw her thinking of her father, of the order he'd taken and the thirty years he'd carried it, and she said, "Then it's a good thing Vase is a worse arsonist than he is an enforcer," and told him what the fire officer had told her: the stock room burned hot and fast, but the fire had not spread to the front of the shop. Someone had wanted a message, not a massacre. Someone had wanted the shop closed, not the town alarmed.

"Or," Mara said, "he wanted me to know he could have done worse."

"Same message, either way." Ruth looked out at the water. "What do you have left?"

He unwrapped the bundle from his chest, coughing, and laid it on the counter that had survived: the biscuit tin, warped at one corner; the three letters; the tide table, its edges scorched brown, Ailsa's pencil lines intact beneath a skin of soot that he wiped away with his sleeve, gently, the way you'd wipe a child's face.

"This," he said. "And Brigid."

* * *

The hospice again, that afternoon, with the storm the forecasters had been promising finally shouldering in from the southwest. The harbour was white-capped and the gulls flew backwards and the windows of Ewan Trelawny's old room rattled in their frames, though Ewan was three days gone and the room had been given to no one yet. Brigid sat in the chair by the window with her hands folded, and she had heard the news before he said a word—the town's telegraph being what it was—and she did not look surprised.

"He came to the hospice gate on Tuesday," she said. "Stood there a while. I watched him from this window. He didn't know me. Thirty years, and he looked straight through me, because I'm an old woman with a caretaker's apron, and that's the disguise they gave me and the disguise I kept." Her voice was flat as slate. "He was checking that I still worked here. That I was still where they'd put me. Then your ledger surfaced, and he understood that the place they'd hidden me had become the place I was striking from."

"He burned my shop, Brigid."

"I know what he burned." She turned from the window at last, and her eyes were the eyes from the photograph he'd found in her room—young, fierce, drowned in that other life and risen again with something hard in place of what the drowning took. "I'm sorry for it. Truly. That shop was the first place in this town that ever felt like it was keeping you instead of hiding you, and I know the difference, better than anyone living." She rose, and stood close, and the wind slammed a gust of rain against the glass like a handful of gravel. "But hear me, Mara Iversen. He didn't burn it to frighten you. He burned it because he's been told to. The trust's directors are old men with one instinct left, and it's the same instinct Aldous had on the shore in 1994: make the problem small, make it ash, make it a story about an accident. You are the problem now. You and me."

"And the tide table."

"And the tide table." Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. "He missed it because it was under your shirt, and because he's a man who burns libraries without reading them."

Mara sat down heavily on the edge of the narrow bed. His lungs ached. His hands, he noticed, were still trembling, and he let them. "The ledger's gone. Vivienne's copy—"

"Is safe. She's not a fool, whatever else she is. She moved it to her solicitor's strong room yesterday, and she's given instructions that it opens if she dies. She told me herself, on the phone, in a voice I'd have paid money to hear thirty years ago." Brigid looked back out at the storm. The sea beyond the harbour wall was coming over the top in long white smothering sheets. "Spring tide's Friday," she said. "Two days. Lowest water in nineteen years. The wreck will stand clear of the sand for a few hours, the way it did that night, the way it did when Ailsa found it."

"You said paper burns. You said you'd only testify spoken, in front of the town."

"I said a file can be managed. I still say it." She turned, and now it was a smile, or the ghost of one, weathered down to the grain. "But I've been thinking, in this room, with the wind coming. I've been thinking that I've spent thirty years being careful, and careful is just another word for buried. Vase burned your shop because he believes we can be made small. And there's only one answer to that, and it isn't a ledger, and it isn't a letter, and it isn't a file in a strong room."

Mara felt it coming, the way you feel a swell before it breaks.

"Friday," Brigid said. "Low water. You, me, and Ruth, and whatever the sea's kept for us. And then—" she straightened, and the caretaker fell away from her like a coat, and what stood there was the girl who had run along the shore in 1994 and seen everything, "—then we read it out loud. All of it. On the harbour steps, in daylight, with the whole town standing in the rain. And you'll stand beside me, Mara. You'll tell them what your sergeant ordered, and what you logged, and what your silence bought. That's the price. That's always been the price."

He looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the storm threw itself against the hospice walls, and somewhere below a shutter came loose and banged and banged against its frame like a heartbeat.

"My shop's ash," he said finally. "My ledger's ash. I've got a scorched tide table, three letters, and a cough that tastes of copper." He rose, and held out his hand, and she took it, her grip dry and strong as kelp rope. "It's the first time in thirty years I've had nothing left to hide behind. Friday, then."

Brigid nodded once, and turned back to the window, and watched the sea rise.

* * *

He walked home along the harbour road in the teeth of it, the rain coming sideways off the water, and stopped where the tape still crossed the ruin of his shop. The front windows had held. Behind the glass, in the light of the workmen's lamps, he could see the biography section dripping, the counter where he'd laid Ailsa's table that morning, the small brass plaque—*Iversen's, est. 1998*—blackened but still screwed to the doorframe.

He had built the shop as a hiding place and had told himself for thirty years it was a life. Now it was a shell with his name on it, and the name had never been more visible, and he found—coughing, soaked, forty years of caution burned down to the joists—that he was not afraid.

He took the letters and the tide table from inside his coat, and instead of hiding them, he carried them home in the open, through the storm, past the harbourmaster's office and the dark windows of the trust, past the harbour steps where on Friday morning he would stand and give the town back its truth.

The tide was coming in. It always came in. But tomorrow, for a few hours, it would go out—further out than it had gone in nineteen years—and the sea would give up what it took.

He meant to be standing on it when it did.
