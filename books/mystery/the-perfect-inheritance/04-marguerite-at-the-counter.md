## 4. Marguerite at the Counter

The rain came in off the water around four o'clock, the way it always did in Greywater, arriving sideways and without apology. Zephyrine Voss watched it streak the front window of his bookshop and thought about how the town had spent a hundred years building itself against the sea and losing the argument daily. Salt crusted the glass in pale ferns. The gutters ran brown with pine needles. Somewhere down the street, the church bell tolled five—early, or broken, or simply tired of keeping accurate time for a town that had stopped believing in it.

He had not opened the shop that day. He rarely did anymore, though he kept the sign in the window because removing it would have been an admission of something, and Zephyrine had built his entire second life on never admitting anything.

At half past six, Marguerite Hale knocked on the glass with two knuckles, the way she always did, as though the door were a thing to be greeted rather than opened.

"You're closed," she said when he let her in, shaking rain from her coat onto his mat like she owned the place. "Good. I brought wine and I didn't want witnesses."

She held up a bottle wrapped in a dish towel. Her hair was dark at the roots going silver at the ends, an unfinished argument between decades, and her eyes had the particular alertness of someone who had spent thirty years reading other people's handwriting and trusting none of it.

"I'm cataloguing," she said, by way of explanation, though no explanation was needed. She came every few evenings now, spreading Merrick's papers across his back counter under the lamp while he pretended to shelve books and listened to her talk. "The estate office is a tomb. Bram Quill keeps everything in boxes labeled *Miscellaneous*, which in lawyer language means *I was paid by the box*. I need a table. I need light. I need," she set the bottle on the counter with a decisive click, "to not be alone with these papers tonight."

Zephyrine took the bottle and turned it over. A red from somewhere inland, where they grew grapes in soil that had never tasted salt. "You could have gone to the library."

"The library closes at eight."

"You could have gone home."

"Yes," she said, and looked at him with that directness that always arrived a half-second before he was ready for it. "I could have."

* * *

They worked by lamplight while the rain worked on the roof. Marguerite had a system—she always had a system—that involved acid-free folders, a numbering scheme penciled lightly enough to be erased, and a running commentary delivered in a low voice, half to him and half to the dead man whose correspondence she was sorting.

"Aldous Merrick wrote letters the way other men breathe," she said, holding a page up to the light. "Constantly, and without much thought for who'd have to clean up after. Look at this—one line to his banker, one line to his mistress, same afternoon, same pen pressure. No guilt anywhere in the hand." She turned the page toward Zephyrine. "Do you see how the *t's* go flat when he's lying? It's very consistent. He lied often enough to have a signature for it."

"Most liars do," Zephyrine said. "It's the honest ones you have to watch. They haven't practiced."

Marguerite smiled at him over the paper. "And which are you?"

"Honest," he said. "Obviously."

"Obviously," she agreed, and let it lie there between them, unexamined, the way she let most things lie. It was one of the many mercies she performed without appearing to perform anything at all.

He made coffee instead of opening the wine—not yet—and moved through the stacks restocking shelves that didn't need restocking. This was the shape their evenings had taken over the past weeks: her at the counter with the dead man's papers, him orbiting her at a careful distance, both of them pretending the arrangement was incidental. He knew what it was. He had known since the third evening, when she'd fallen asleep in the chair by the stove and he had stood watching her for eleven minutes—counted them, counted them the way he counted everything—before waking her gently and hating himself for having counted.

Zephyrine Voss had spent twenty years being no one. It had been a discipline, like fasting. You learned to move through a town at the edge of people's attention, to be the bookseller nobody remembered meeting, to answer questions with questions until the questioner lost interest. Invisibility had saved his life once. He had built it brick by brick, deliberately, and he had told himself for two decades that it was a fortress.

Standing in his own shop, watching Marguerite Hale smooth out a letter written eighty years ago, he understood suddenly and uncomfortably that it had also been a cell.

"Voss." She said his name the way she said everything—plainly, without ornament. "Come look at this."

* * *

The document she'd found was a single sheet, filed between an inventory of the estate's shipping interests and a receipt for coffin hardware dated three days before Aldous Merrick's death. Zephyrine noted the coffin receipt and said nothing about it. Some observations were better kept in the pocket.

"It's a reference," Marguerite said. "Look—the estate inventory lists *one codicil to the last will and testament of Aldous Merrick, executed the twelfth of March, ninety-eight years prior.* One codicil. Referenced, numbered, entered into the record." She tapped the line with her finger. "But there's no codicil in the file, Voss. I've been through every folder Quill's office holds. There's the will, there's the inventory, there's the probate correspondence. No codicil. It was executed—it has a date, a number, presumably witnesses—and then it vanished from the record before probate ever closed."

"Could it have been revoked?"

"If it were revoked, there'd be a revocation instrument. Lawyers don't just lose documents; they bill for losing them." She sat back and rubbed her eyes with the heels of her hands. "No. Either it was removed deliberately, or it was never deposited here in the first place. Which means either someone stole it, or Merrick kept it somewhere else. Somewhere private."

Zephyrine stood very still beside the counter. Outside, the rain had thickened into something with intent, hammering the awning, running loud in the street gutter. The lamp threw their shadows long across the floorboards, hers bent over the papers, his standing apart, and he noticed—he always noticed—that even their shadows declined to touch.

"What would a codicil contain?" he asked. "In your experience."

"Anything. An addition. A correction. A new beneficiary." She said it lightly, archivally, a professional musing. But then she went quiet, and he watched her arrive somewhere she hadn't planned to go. "Or a removal. Sometimes a man gets to the end and wants to take something back. Disinherit a nephew. Cut out a charity that's grown fat on him. Name someone the family never—" She stopped herself. Started again, more carefully. "Merrick changed his will twice in the last decade. Both times in favor of the trust. Corwin, Odette Ferris's charity, the usual circle. If there was a third change, a final one, made days before he died..."

"Then everyone currently living comfortably off that trust," Zephyrine said, "has been spending money that may never have been theirs."

Marguerite looked up at him. The lamplight caught the silver in her hair and held it there. "You say that like a man reading a menu."

"I say it like a bookseller with an interest in local history."

"No," she said, not unkindly. "You don't. You say it like a man who used to do something else." She held his gaze for a moment longer than comfort allowed, then returned to the papers, letting him off the hook with the ease of long practice—or perhaps not practice, perhaps instinct, perhaps the simple animal wisdom of knowing which doors not to knock on yet. "Everyone in this town used to do something else. That's why they live here."

* * *

Later, past ten, she finally opened the wine, and they drank it from mismatched cups because he owned no glasses worth offering. She sat on the counter itself, boots swinging slightly, a habit that would have scandalized the archive society, and told him about her mother.

Not much. Just pieces, offered the way you toss bread to water—to see if anything rises. That her mother had come to Greywater young and alone. That she had worked, briefly, in a house on the cliff. That she had left before Marguerite was old enough to ask questions, and died before Marguerite was old enough to stop asking them.

"She never talked about those years," Marguerite said, turning her cup slowly. "I used to think it was sadness. Now I think it might have been something closer to a contract."

"People keep secrets for all kinds of reasons," Zephyrine said. "Payment. Shame. Protection. Sometimes all three at once, wearing each other's coats."

"And you? What coat are yours wearing?"

He should have deflected. Twenty years of discipline said deflect; make a joke, turn the question, become the bookseller again, smooth and forgettable as endpaper. Instead he heard himself say: "Mine are wearing duty. Which looks like shame from the outside."

Marguerite studied him. She had a way of looking at a person the way she looked at a forged signature—not with suspicion exactly, but with total attention, reading the pressure of the strokes. Whatever she read in him tonight, she folded away without comment.

"Well," she said, sliding off the counter and gathering her coat. "The codicil exists. Someone wanted it not to exist. And I intend to find it, because it's the only interesting thing this town has produced in a hundred years, and I refuse to let Bram Quill's filing system win." She paused at the door, rain drumming above her, and added, quieter: "Thank you for the table. And the light. And the—" a small gesture that encompassed the shop, the cups, the evening, him, "—all of it."

"Any time," Zephyrine said, and discovered he meant it, and discovered further that meaning it frightened him more than any case ever had.

She went out into the rain. He watched her cross the street under her umbrella, a dark figure moving through the lamplight and the wet, and he stood at the window long after she'd turned the corner, holding his cup of her leftover wine, aware that he was smiling and unable to make himself stop.

Then he turned back to the counter, to the papers she'd left squared and waiting for morning, and his eye caught the page she'd marked with a slip of ribbon: the coffin receipt. Three days before death. Paid cash. And beneath the itemized cost of oak and brass and lining, in the undertaker's cramped hand, a final notation he was certain she had not yet seen:

*Extra charge — double lining, per Mr. M.'s instruction. Reason given: "for the crossing."*

Zephyrine stood in the lamplight, the smile fading from his face, and understood that Aldous Merrick had known, three days before he died, exactly what kind of journey his body was expected to make—and had paid, in advance, to make certain no one would ever find it wanting.
