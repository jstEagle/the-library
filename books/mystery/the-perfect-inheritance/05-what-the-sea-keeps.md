## 5. What the Sea Keeps

The sea caves beneath Merrick Point had been named by a man who never learned to swim.

Ilsa Brandt stood at the lip of the lowest cave with her boots in six inches of cold water and thought about that. Aldous Merrick had built his estate on the cliff above, had drawn the first survey maps of Greywater's coastline, had labeled these caverns on his charts in his own careful hand—*the Mermaid's Parlor*, *the Long Throat*, *the Drowned Chapel*—and yet the man had been terrified of deep water his whole life. She'd read it in Marguerite Hale's archival notes, one line among thousands: *A.M. refused all invitations to sail. Kept to the cliff paths.*

A man afraid of the sea who chose to be buried in sight of it. A town that buried its founder and then spent a hundred years not asking why the coffin came up empty.

"Constable!" The shout came from inside the Long Throat, echoing strangely, the way sound did in these places—bounced twice, arriving late, as if the cave were deciding whether to repeat it. "You'll want to see this."

She waded forward. Her torch beam skated across the water, which was the color of old bottle glass, green-black and moving with a slow breathing swell. Two divers from the Port Ellery unit were already surfacing in the inner chamber, their regulator bubbles breaking the surface in silver strings. One of them held something dark against his chest.

The cave swallowed her light. That was the thing no photograph prepared you for—the way the darkness down here wasn't an absence but a substance, thick and patient, older than the town above it. Greywater had existed for a hundred years. These caves had been swallowing light since before there were names for what they were.

"Careful on the shelf," the diver called. He was a broad man with a beard crusted in salt, and he hauled himself onto the rock ledge with the object cradled like a child. "It's coming apart. Whatever it is."

It was a strongbox. Or it had been. Iron gone to lace, rusted through in great weeping blooms, the lid collapsed inward like a ribcage after the breath has left it. Seaweed hung from the handles in black tassels. When he set it down on the ledge, a fine silt of rust flakes spread across the stone like dried blood.

"Where?" Ilsa asked.

"Inner chamber. The Drowned Chapel." He nodded back toward the throat of the cave, where the passage narrowed and then opened again into a space she'd only seen on the survey maps. "Wedged under a shelf, maybe four meters down. Deliberately wedged—I'd stake my ticket on it. There were stones stacked around it. Not rubble. *Stacked.* Somebody put it there and meant it to stay."

"Somebody," she said, "a hundred years ago?"

He looked at her the way divers looked at land people—with a kind of amused pity—and shrugged. "Could be last week, the way the iron's gone. Salt doesn't keep calendars. But the stacking, the placement—" He wiped his face with a forearm, leaving a streak of rust across his cheekbone. "Whoever sank it wanted it found eventually. Or wanted it *not* found until they said so. Those are different intentions, Constable, and from down there, I can't tell you which."

Ilsa crouched beside the box. Her torch threw hard shadows into its ruined interior. Empty. Of course empty. A century of tide would empty anything. But the interior lining had been lead—she could see the dull grey metal clinging to the corners like skin—and lead was not what you lined a strongbox with if you were keeping coins or papers you didn't care about. Lead was what you used when you cared very much. Lead kept out water. Lead kept out time, for a while.

And there, pressed into the rotted wood of the lid's underside, half-swallowed by corrosion but unmistakable—a seal impression. Wax long dissolved, but the matrix had bitten deep enough to leave its ghost: a circle, and within it a ship at anchor, and around the rim, letters she could read even now, even here, even with her heart beginning to move too fast.

*A MERRICK · GREYWATER · ANNO DOMINI*

The founder's seal. The same impression that appeared on the original deed of incorporation, on the bronze plaque outside the church, on the cover of every schoolchild's history workbook in Greywater. And the same seal, according to Marguerite Hale's meticulous cataloguing, that had closed the codicil referenced in Aldous Merrick's estate papers—referenced, indexed, and never filed. A document that existed everywhere except in the world.

"Bag it," Ilsa said. "All of it. The stones from around it too. I want every one of them photographed in place before anyone breathes on them."

The diver raised an eyebrow but said nothing. He'd worked with police before. He knew the tone.

She stood there a moment longer after the others moved off, letting her torch play over the walls of the Mermaid's Parlor, over the pale ribs of rock and the dark water sliding in and out with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. Somewhere behind her, deeper in, the Drowned Chapel waited with whatever else it kept.

The sea keeps things, her grandmother used to say. It just doesn't keep them *for* you.

* * *

By afternoon the strongbox sat on a steel table in the evidence room of the Greywater constabulary, looking smaller and more pathetic than it had in the cave—rust and ruin, a thing that had once been important enough to hide and was now important enough to argue over. Ilsa had called the Port Ellery forensic unit, then called them again when they hadn't answered fast enough. She had logged the seal impression three times in three separate registers because some part of her, superstitious in a way she despised, believed that things written down only once had a way of disappearing.

She spread the tide tables across her desk and tried to make them confess.

That was the phrase Zephyrine Voss had used, two nights ago, sitting in his bookshop among the smell of paper and dust and lamp oil, turning his teacup without drinking from it. *Tide tables will tell you more than witnesses do, Constable. Witnesses lie to protect themselves. Water doesn't know how.* She had thought it was one of his performances—he performed constantly, she was learning, the way shy men perform shyness itself—but standing over the tables now, she understood he'd simply been telling her how he saw the world. Everything was a document. Everything testified.

The tables told her this: the spring tides were building. The lunar cycle was swinging toward its perigee, and in nineteen days Greywater would see the lowest tide of the year—low enough, the harbourmaster's notes suggested, to expose the floor of the Drowned Chapel to air for nearly ninety minutes. Ninety minutes. In a hundred years of tides, how many times had that chamber stood open to the sky? A handful. And in nineteen days it would stand open again.

Which meant the box had not been hidden so much as *scheduled*. Sunk where it could be reached only rarely, by someone who knew the tables, who knew the caves, who knew that a secret placed below the low-water line was safer than any vault. You couldn't pick a lock underwater. You couldn't burn what the sea covered.

Unless you were racing the water yourself.

Ilsa sat back and felt the dread arrive—not suddenly, but the way fog arrives, unnoticed until it has soaked everything. Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: if the box had rested in the Drowned Chapel for a hundred years, why had it surfaced now? Tides didn't read newspapers. Centennial exhumations didn't move water. Something—or someone—had disturbed it. And whoever had taken the contents had done so recently enough that the stacked stones were still stacked, that the placement still bore intention rather than accident.

Someone had gotten there first. Someone who knew exactly what the box was, exactly where it lay, and exactly when the water would give it up.

Or someone who hadn't needed the water's permission at all.

She pulled the case file toward her—the official file, the one she'd been living inside for two weeks now—and reread the inventory of what should have been in the coffin. Then she turned to the page she hated: the coroner's historical addendum, the transcription of the 1924 drowning verdict, the one containing the detail about the time of death that had never been made public. The detail that had appeared, word for word, in a note left on her windshield. The file felt different in her hands lately. Heavier. As though it had been handled by more hands than hers.

*Authored,* Zephyrine had said. *This case feels authored, Constable. Read it aloud sometime. Listen to the sentences. Somebody wrote this town's history the way a novelist writes a plot—leaving out everything that doesn't serve the ending.*

She'd laughed at the time. She wasn't laughing now.

* * *

At dusk she walked out to Merrick Point alone.

She told herself it was procedure—scene assessment, tidal observation, familiarization with approach routes—but the truth was simpler and less professional: she wanted to stand where the killer might have stood. She wanted to look at the water the way they must have looked at it, and see whether it looked different from up here. Whether intent changed the view.

The cliff path was overgrown but passable, winding down through gorse and wind-bent pines to the estate's ruined boathouse. The house itself stood above, shuttered and grey, owned by the trust, maintained by nobody, waiting the way old houses wait—with the specific patience of furniture. Below the boathouse, the rocks fell away in black shelves to the water, and the mouths of the caves opened and closed with each swell, exhaling a low moan that she felt in her sternum more than heard.

From here, she could see how it would be done. A boat at night, engine cut a hundred meters out, oars the rest of the way. A diver—or a strong swimmer with a weighted line—slipping into the Long Throat on a rising tide, when the current ran inward and helped rather than hindered. The box already waiting, or the box brought along. Stones stacked. Work completed in the dark, in the cold, in a place where a scream would go unheard and a body would go unfound until the sea grew tired of it.

It would take someone who knew these caves intimately. Who had swum them as a child, perhaps. Who had grown up on stories of the Drowned Chapel and the founder who feared the water and hid his secrets in it anyway.

Her radio crackled. "Constable Brandt, copy?"

"Go ahead."

"We finished the grid search of the inner chamber before the tide turned. You'll want the photographs tomorrow, but there's one thing." A pause; she could hear wind on the other end, and the wet slap of gear. "There's a niche in the far wall, natural formation, about chest height. Something had been resting in it—there's a clean silhouette in the growth, like a shadow burned into the stone. Rectangular. Book-sized, maybe. Whatever sat there was removed recently. The algae hasn't grown back over the outline. That takes months, not decades."

Book-sized. She looked out at the darkening water and thought of a bookshop on Harbour Street with its lights burning late, and a retired detective who read everything, and an archivist who catalogued everything, and a missing codicil that existed everywhere except in the world.

"Anything else in the niche?"

"Sand. And this." A pause. "We bagged it. Looks like a button. Bone or horn, old-fashioned, the kind off a heavy coat. Could've been there a hundred years. Could've dropped off somebody's sleeve last winter. Down here, Constable, everything looks like it's been waiting forever."

Everything looks like it's been waiting forever. She wrote it down anyway, because that was the job: writing down the things that couldn't be known yet, in the faith that someday they'd become the things that always had been.

* * *

She found Zephyrine Voss where she knew she'd find him—in the bookshop, behind the counter, with a lamp turned low and a tide table of his own already open on the desk, because of course he'd run the same numbers. He looked up when the bell over the door announced her, and for a moment neither of them spoke. The shop breathed around them: paper, dust, the faint sweetness of old glue. Outside, the harbour slapped its slow rhythm against the pilings.

"You've been to the caves," he said. Not a question. "You have cave silt on your left boot cuff and salt drying in a line on your coat hem. And you walk like someone who's spent the day being argued with by water."

"The box was there," she said. "Empty. Seal impression matches the codicil. Wedged under a shelf with stacked stones, in the Drowned Chapel, four meters down." She pulled off her coat and sat in the chair across from him, the chair customers weren't offered, the chair she suspected he'd bought for her without ever saying so. "And there's a second site. A niche in the wall with a book-shaped absence in it. Recently vacated."

Zephyrine was quiet for a long moment. His hands, folded on the desk, went very still—the stillness she'd learned to read as his version of shouting.

"Nineteen days," he said finally. "To the lowest tide of the year. When the chapel floor stands open and anyone can walk in dry-shod and see exactly what's there—or exactly what isn't."

"Yes."

"So our author has a deadline." He turned the tide table toward her, and she saw he'd marked the dates in pencil, the same dates she'd circled in ink, and felt the strange cold comfort of converging minds. "Whatever they took from that box, they're holding it until the water gives them their alibi. On the lowest tide, the chamber is accessible to anyone—to a recovery team, to a curious swimmer, to *us*. Anything found exposed on that day looks like discovery, not retrieval. They've arranged for the sea to take the blame." His mouth curved, but there was no warmth in it. "It's elegant. It's the kind of solution I would have designed twenty years ago, and I hate that I recognize the handwriting."

"Then whose handwriting is it?"

"That," Zephyrine said softly, "is the question I've been sitting with since your windshield note. Someone who knows the caves. Someone who knows the tides. Someone who knows what was in that box well enough to want it—and knows this file well enough to quote it." He reached for his teacup, remembered it was empty, and set it down again. "Someone patient, Constable. The sea is patient. Whoever learned their methods from it learned the right lessons."

Ilsa looked past him, through the dark window, toward the invisible line of the cliffs and the water working away beneath them. Nineteen days. Somewhere out there, in the green-black breathing dark, the Drowned Chapel waited with its emptied box and its book-shaped shadow, keeping what it kept, giving up what it gave up, on a schedule someone else had written.

"When the tide goes out," she said, "we need to be standing on that floor before anyone else reaches it."

Zephyrine nodded slowly. Then he glanced toward the stairs that led up to his rooms, and his voice, when it came, was quieter than she'd ever heard it.

"There's something else you should know first. About what's under this shop."
