## 12. A Question of Alibis

The file sat on Ilsa Brandt's desk like something drowned and washed back up.

She had been staring at it for twenty minutes without opening it, the way one stares at a letter that can only contain bad news, hoping the paper might soften, might rewrite itself. Outside her office window the harbour lay flat and pewter-coloured under a sky the colour of wet slate, and the gulls were working the tideline with their usual indifference to human grief. Greywater in October was a town holding its breath. She could feel it in the way people crossed the street when they saw her coming, in the way conversation died in the Anchor & Chain whenever she pushed through its door.

They had loved the exhumation as a spectacle. They did not love it as a murder investigation.

Ilsa opened the file.

Marguerite Hale. Age thirty-four. Archivist, contracted through the county historical society to catalogue the Merrick estate papers. Residence: the flat above the chandlery on Quay Lane. No prior record, not so much as a parking fine. Next of kin: none living — mother deceased eleven years, father unknown or unrecorded. And beneath all of that, in Ilsa's own careful hand, the sentence she had written three days ago and had been trying to unwrite ever since:

*Alibi for night of 14–15 September: states she was at home alone. No corroboration.*

It was thin. It was thinner than thin. The coffin had been found empty at seven-forty on the morning of the fifteenth, which meant the grave had been opened sometime between dusk and dawn, and Marguerite Hale could produce no living soul who had seen her between nine o'clock that evening and the following noon. She said she had been reading. She said she had fallen asleep over Merrick's correspondence and woken with her cheek creased by a paperclip. It was the kind of alibi an innocent person gave, because innocent people did not imagine they would ever need one.

It was also exactly the kind of alibi a clever person would give, if they understood that innocence sounded like this.

Ilsa closed the file again and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the dark bloomed orange.

Her superintendent had been very gentle about it, which was how she knew the pressure came from higher up. *Follow the evidence where it leads, Constable,* he'd said, in the tone men used when they meant *don't*. *But follow it.* Behind him, in the corridor, the mayor's assistant had been waiting with his coat already on, and everyone in the building knew what that meant. Greywater wanted this case to be a stranger's crime, a drifter's crime, a crime committed by nobody at all. What Greywater could not survive — what the centennial committee, the heritage board, the tourist brochures could not survive — was a killer with roots.

And Marguerite Hale had roots now. Shallow ones, recent ones, but roots: a job touching the dead man's papers, a friendship — more than a friendship, if the town's gossip was to be believed, and the town's gossip was usually six weeks ahead of the truth — with Zephyrine Voss.

There it was. The other name. The one Ilsa had been circling for days the way her tongue circled a cracked tooth.

* * *

She found him in the bookshop, as she always found him, shelving.

He heard the bell over the door and didn't turn around. That was Zephyrine all over; he acknowledged your arrival the way the tide acknowledged the moon, invisibly, totally. She watched him slide a battered copy of some maritime history into its place with the tenderness other men reserved for sleeping children, and only then did he look at her, and she saw immediately that he knew why she'd come. He always knew. Twenty years of unofficial consultations had taught her to read him, and what she read now was a man braced behind a door he had already decided not to open.

"Constable Brandt," he said. "You look like a woman carrying a warrant."

"I'm carrying questions."

"Those are heavier." He came around the end of the shelf, wiping his hands on a cloth that had once been white. "Tea?"

"No." Then, because it felt like surrender to refuse twice: "Yes. All right."

He made it without asking how she took it, because he knew, because he remembered everything, and that was precisely the problem. She sat at the little table by the window among the stacks while he moved through the shop lighting nothing, though the afternoon was dim enough to want it. The bookshop smelled of dust and cedar and old glue, and underneath those smells, faintly, of the sea — it got into everything in Greywater, into the books, the bedsheets, the bones.

"Marguerite Hale," Ilsa said.

Zephyrine set the teapot down with exaggerated care, as though the saucer were an instrument that might register tremors. "What about her?"

"You know what about her."

"I know what the town says about her. The town also says Aldous Merrick ascended bodily into heaven, and that the fog is caused by the sighs of drowned sailors, so forgive me if I don't build my case on local testimony."

"Her alibi is nothing. You know that too."

"She was home reading." He sat down across from her, and his eyes — grey, patient, maddeningly calm — held hers. "Most murders are committed by people with better things to do than read."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest answer I have."

Ilsa wrapped her hands around the cup. The heat was almost painful and she was grateful for it; it gave her something honest to feel. "Then here's what I have, and you can tell me how wrong I am. She catalogued the Merrick papers. She had access to every document in the estate, including whatever references exist to the missing codicil. She had motive sitting in her lap before anyone else even knew it existed — if there's a second will out there, and if it names someone unexpected, the archivist is the first person positioned to know, and possibly the last person who'd want it found. She has no family, no ties, nothing anchoring her to Greywater except—" she stopped.

"Except me," Zephyrine said quietly.

"Except you. And you've spent three weeks consulting on this case while refusing to tell me what you've found in your own cellar—"

Something moved behind his face. It was small, quickly mastered, but Ilsa had spent her career learning to catch small things, and she caught it. "My cellar," he repeated.

"The storm damage. The repairs. You've had workmen in and out for two weeks and you've told me nothing about what they found, and don't insult me by saying they found damp." She leaned forward. "Zeph. I have stood beside you on cases for twenty years. Unofficially. Off the record. I have let you see files I could have lost my pension over, and you have never once withheld from me. So tell me why you're withholding now, and tell me honestly whether it's because the truth points at her."

The silence stretched. Rain had begun against the window, fine and needling, blurring the harbour lights into smears. Somewhere upstairs in the flat above the shop, or perhaps only in Ilsa's imagination, a floorboard settled.

"What I know," Zephyrine said at last, "is not yet knowledge. It's suspicion wearing knowledge's clothes. If I hand you suspicion, you'll have to act on it. That's your curse — you're an honest officer in a dishonest town. And if I'm wrong, I'll have burned the one person in this town who—" He stopped himself, but the sentence had already shown its shape.

"Who what?" Ilsa asked softly. "Who matters to you?"

He looked away, toward the rain-blurred window, and did not answer, and the not-answer was louder than anything he'd said all afternoon.

"You love her," Ilsa said. It wasn't a question and she didn't frame it as one. "And I understand. God knows I do. But you have to hear how this looks from where I sit. The man advising me on the case is protecting the prime suspect, and the prime suspect is the woman he loves, and neither of them will account for the night a hundred-year-old corpse walked out of its grave. Duty versus loyalty, Zeph. I've got the same war going on inside me. The difference is I can't afford to win it the wrong way."

"And if you're wrong about her?" His voice had gone low, roughened. "If you drag her through an interrogation, and the town — this town, which ate her mother alive thirty years ago over less — decides she's the heir who dug up the founder? You won't get that back for her. Greywater doesn't return what it takes."

"Greywater doesn't return what it takes from anyone," Ilsa said. "That's why somebody has to be willing to take things from it." She stood, drained the tea in one swallow, and put coins on the table although he waved them off. "Formal interview. Thursday, ten o'clock, at the station. She can bring a solicitor. Tell her that yourself — I'd rather she hear it from a friend."

At the door she paused, hand on the bell-frame, and made herself say the thing she had come half to say and half to unsay:

"For what it's worth — I hope I'm wrong. I've never wanted to be wrong about anything more." A breath. "But Zeph — whoever emptied that grave knew the tide tables, knew the cemetery groundskeeper's rounds, knew the exact hour the constabulary van would be at the far end of the peninsula. That's not a stranger. That's someone who has studied this town the way you study it. Maybe the way *only* you study it. And until I can rule her out, she's the person closest to both of us."

The bell chimed her out into the rain.

* * *

Thursday came grey and windless, the kind of day when the sea looked less like water than like poured metal, and the interview room smelled of floor polish and old fear.

Marguerite Hale sat on the far side of the table with her hands folded, and Ilsa thought: *she looks like someone cataloguing the room.* Because she did — her gaze moved along the walls, the barred window, the recorder, with a professional's unhurried attention, as though she were assigning each object a number and a place in some vast index. It was either the composure of complete innocence or the discipline of long preparation, and Ilsa hated, professionally and personally, that she could not tell which.

The solicitor was a bored young man from the county list who kept checking his watch, which suited Ilsa; she wanted no theatre here, only the record.

"Ms. Hale. Walk me through the fourteenth of September. Evening onward."

"I closed up the archive room at the historical society around six. I bought bread and a tin of sardines from the shop on Fore Street — Mrs. Tregowan will remember, she always remembers. I went home. I worked on the Merrick correspondence until late. I fell asleep at the table. I woke past noon the next day and heard the bells, and then the shouting, and then I knew something had happened at the cemetery before anyone told me what."

"You were alone all night."

"Entirely."

"No telephone calls? No visitors?"

"One call, earlier — from Zephyrine Voss, about a mislabelled box of letters. Around eight, I think. Ten minutes."

Ilsa noted it. Even that small kindness of his — checking on her, or checking on the letters, or checking on something else entirely — was now a thread in the weave. "And during the night. Did you hear anything? Anything at all — footsteps, a vehicle, the church bells marking hours?"

Marguerite considered the question with the seriousness of a scholar weighing a variant manuscript. "Rain, starting near midnight. A dog somewhere down the lane, the one that barks at gulls. And at some hour I can't fix — two, maybe three — a car engine, idling, then gone. But Quay Lane hears engines all night. The chandlery gets deliveries."

"Did you leave the flat at any point?"

"No."

"Can anyone confirm that?"

"No." For the first time something flickered across her face — not guilt, Ilsa decided, but the particular exhaustion of a person watching a trap assemble itself out of their own ordinary life. "Constable, I'm aware of how this sounds. I've catalogued enough confessions to know what an absence of witnesses looks like on paper. But I would ask you to consider one thing. If I had opened that grave — if I had the strength, the tools, the knowledge, the *reason* — would I have left the coffin empty? Would I not rather have removed the body entirely, or at least had the wit to invent a friend who'd swear I was at her kitchen table playing cards?"

It was, infuriatingly, a good point. It was the point Zephyrine would have made, in exactly those words, and Ilsa wondered briefly — uselessly — whether Marguerite had learned to think like him simply from loving him, or whether the resemblance ran the other direction, back through time, to some older source.

"Tell me about the codicil," Ilsa said.

"There isn't one. Not in the records. There's a reference — a marginal note in Bram Quill's own hand, dated two years before Merrick's death: *'see codicil, filed separately.'* Nothing filed separately exists. I've searched every box twice."

"But you believe it exists."

"I believe Aldous Merrick was a man who filed his secrets the way other men filed receipts." Marguerite's voice stayed level, but her hands had tightened on each other, knuckles paling. "And I believe whoever took him out of that grave wants what the codicil says to stay buried. Which makes me, as the person cataloguing his papers, either their greatest obstacle or their most convenient suspect. Possibly both."

* * *

Afterwards, Ilsa stood in the station corridor with the interview transcript still warm from the photocopier, and let herself feel the full weight of what she'd done.

Nothing in the room had convicted Marguerite Hale. Nothing had cleared her, either. She was precise where a liar might be vague and vague where a liar might be precise; she offered the codicil freely, which a guilty person might do as inoculation; she named Zephyrine's phone call without being prompted, which was either transparency or pre-emption. Every answer cut both ways. Ilsa had interviewed killers who sounded like saints and innocents who sounded like suspects, and Marguerite Hale sounded like a woman telling the truth in a language Ilsa had not yet learned to trust.

Her superintendent appeared at the end of the corridor, drawn by some institutional instinct for discomfort. "Well?"

"Thin. Consistent. Inconclusive."

"Inconclusive doesn't close files." He lowered his voice. "The heritage board called again this morning. They want this resolved before the centennial gala. Quietly, if possible."

"Quietly," Ilsa repeated. "Three people are dead, sir. Two of them before I was born, one of them last week in a boat he'd sailed for forty years. Quiet went out with the tide."

He retreated the way superintendents do, leaving her alone with the transcript and the rain starting up again against the high windows.

That evening she walked out to the end of the breakwater, because she did her clearest thinking where the wind could get at her, and stood watching the light on the point swing its slow white arm across the darkening water. Three accidents. One empty grave. A missing document worth killing for, and a killer who moved through Greywater like weather — everywhere, invisible, patient. And the two people best positioned to help her find that killer were standing in a bookshop right now, keeping something from her, bound together by a loyalty she couldn't subpoena.

Duty versus loyalty. She had framed it that way to Zephyrine, and it had sounded clean when she said it. It did not sound clean now. Now it sounded like two hands pulling the same rope in opposite directions, and somewhere below the rope, in the dark water, the truth going on drowning.

She turned back toward the town, toward the scatter of lamplit windows climbing the hill, and found herself counting them out of habit — the way she'd counted them as a child, safe in the knowledge that every lit window meant someone home, someone accounted for. Tonight one of those windows was the bookshop's, and behind it, no doubt, Zephyrine and Marguerite sat with their heads bent over the same secret, and Ilsa understood with sudden cold clarity that whatever they were hiding, they believed they were hiding it to protect someone.

So did every killer she had ever met.

She was halfway back along the breakwater when her radio crackled — the duty desk, apologetic, urgent. A report from the historical society's night steward. Someone had been in the archive rooms after hours. Nothing taken, as far as they could tell.

Except that every box of Aldous Merrick's correspondence — every single one — had been re-shelved in the wrong order.

Someone had read them all. Recently. Carefully. And put them back almost perfectly, the way a person puts things back when they have memorized not just the contents, but the catalogue itself.

Almost perfectly — but not quite. And Ilsa, standing in the wind with the spray coming up cold off the stones, thought of one person alive who handled those papers daily, who knew their order the way a pianist knows a keyboard, and who had sworn she'd searched every box only twice.

Two times, Marguerite had said.

The boxes said otherwise.
