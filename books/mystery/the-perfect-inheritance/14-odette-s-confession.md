## 14. Odette's Confession

The Ferris house sat at the crooked end of Harbour Lane, where the street gave up on straightness and let the buildings lean toward the water like old men eavesdropping on the tide. Marguerite had passed it a hundred times without seeing it—grey clapboard gone silver with salt, a garden of sea thrift and broken shells, a blue door that might once have been bright. Now she stood before it in the flat grey light of an afternoon that couldn't decide whether to rain, her knuckles raised to knock, and understood why she had never seen it: Odette Ferris had spent seventy years perfecting the art of being overlooked.

Marguerite knew the technique intimately. She'd been practicing it herself all week.

She knocked.

The door opened before the second rap landed, as though Odette had been standing behind it, hand on the latch, counting Marguerite's footsteps up the lane. She was smaller than Marguerite remembered from the charity galas—a wren of a woman in a cardigan the colour of weak tea, her white hair pinned with the severity of someone who had decided long ago which battles to stop fighting. Her eyes were not severe at all. They were tired in the particular way of someone who has been waiting a long time for something bad to finish arriving.

"You're the Hale girl," she said. Not a question. "I wondered when you'd come."

"Mrs. Ferris, I—"

"Come in before the gulls see you. They report everything in this town." She stepped back, and the smell of the house folded around Marguerite: beeswax, dried lavender, and beneath it something older, the mineral breath of a house that had stood through too many storms. "Tea's already made. I made it when I saw you turn off the esplanade. You walk like your mother."

Marguerite stopped on the threshold, one foot on the worn stone, and the sentence went through her like cold water through cloth.

"You knew my mother?"

Odette's face did something complicated—a flinch folded inside a smile folded inside grief. "Come in," she said again, gentler now. "That's rather the point of you being here, isn't it? Though I suspect you don't know it yet."

* * *

The parlour was a museum of small survivals. A clock that ticked too loudly for the room. Photographs in oval frames, faces faded to sepia ghosts. On the mantel, a single object held pride of place: a small brass bell, green with age, its handle worn smooth.

"Charity work," Odette said, following Marguerite's gaze as she poured tea into cups so thin the light came through them. "Forty years of charity work, and the town remembers me by the galas and the donation plaques. That bell rang at the opening of the Merrick Children's Home in 1954. Aldous Merrick handed it to me himself and told me to ring it every time someone tried to buy my silence." Her mouth twisted. "I never rang it. I kept it instead. As a reminder of what I wasn't brave enough to do."

Marguerite sat because her legs told her to, not because she'd decided to. The tea trembled faintly in her cup. "You knew about my mother. About Merrick being her father."

"I knew about you before you knew about yourself, child." Odette settled into the chair opposite, folding her hands as if preparing for a long confession—which, Marguerite thought wildly, was exactly what this was. "Your mother came to Greywater in 1968. Nineteen years old, pregnant, furious. She'd found letters—Aldous's letters—in her adoptive mother's things after the funeral. He'd paid for her upbringing the same way he paid for everything he was ashamed of: quietly, generously, and at arm's length. She came here to confront him."

"And did she?"

"He wouldn't see her." Odette's voice flattened. "He sent me instead. I was his... intermediary. His conscience-keeper. He'd installed me at the head of his charitable trust years earlier precisely so there would be a respectable channel for money that could never be traced to sentiment. Your mother stood in my parlour—younger than you are now—and asked me why a man who built a children's home wouldn't look at his own child." She looked down at her tea. "I gave her the envelope he'd prepared and told her it would be better for everyone if she didn't come back. She threw the tea in my face. Right there, where you're sitting. Then she took the envelope anyway, because she had you to think of, and she left, and I have thought about her every day for fifty-four years."

The clock ticked. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed its one note.

"The payments continued," Marguerite said. Her own voice sounded far away, borrowed. "To my mother. Until she died."

"Until she died," Odette confirmed softly. "And then to you—or rather, into an account in your name that no one ever told you about. Aldous's instruction. 'When the girl is ready,' he said. He was always saying things like that. When the girl is ready. When the time is right. When the tide turns." She smiled without humour. "He loved a tide metaphor, our founder. Built a whole town on one."

"Then the codicil—"

"Is real." Odette said it plainly, and the plainness was worse than any drama. "I witnessed it. March 1971, in this room. Bram Quill drafted it; I signed as second witness. It named your mother, and after her, you. He sealed a copy in lead and dropped it in the caves himself—he liked ceremony, our Aldous, even private ceremony—with instructions that it should surface only when the water gave it back. Poetry, he called it. I called it madness. But I signed."

"Why are you telling me this now?" Marguerite set down her cup because her hands had begun to shake in earnest. "Why not weeks ago, when the grave was opened? Why not to Constable Brandt?"

And here Odette Ferris finally looked afraid.

It moved across her face like weather—the composure of seventy years thinning all at once, showing the frightened girl underneath who had taken an envelope of hush-money and thrown away nothing but tea. She rose, crossed to the writing desk beneath the window, and returned with a sheet of paper held between finger and thumb, as though it might bite.

"Because three days ago, this came through my letterbox. And because yesterday, someone followed me home from the market. And because I am seventy-nine years old, Miss Hale, and I have been quiet since 1954, and I find that I would rather be dead than quiet a moment longer."

* * *

The note was written in block capitals, black ink, on paper that had been torn—not cut—from a larger sheet. Marguerite read it twice, and on the second reading the words began to arrange themselves into a shape she recognized, the way a face emerges from a crowd, and the recognition arrived not as thought but as a physical event, a dropping sensation behind her ribs.

**THE FOUNDER ASCENDED. WHAT THE SEA GAVE BACK BELONGS TO THE SEA. YOU KEPT YOUR SILENCE FOR FIFTY YEARS. KEEP IT NOW, AND BE REMEMBERED KINDLY. SPEAK, AND BE REMEMBERED AT ALL.**

"What is it?" Odette asked, watching her face. "You've gone white as the crockery."

"It's the phrasing." Marguerite heard her own voice as if from another room. "'What the sea keeps.' 'Remembered kindly.' Mrs. Ferris—I need to show you something. Do you have the other notes? You said notes, plural, when we spoke on the telephone—"

"Three. Four with this one." Odette fetched them from the desk: two more block-capital messages, months apart by their postmarks, each more ornate in its menace. *THE TIDE TAKES WHAT IT IS OWED.* *SILENCE IS THE FIRST DUTY OF THE DEVOTED.*

Marguerite laid them out on the low table between the teacups, side by side, and stared at them until they blurred.

Because she knew these sentences. Not their content—she'd never seen their content before tonight—but their music. Their cadence. The peculiar, formal, almost liturgical rhythm of them. She had spent last spring cataloguing the archive in Zephyrine Voss's cellar, box after box of his private notebooks, forty years of a detective's mind poured out in ink, and she had read enough of them—skimmed, truly, only skimmed, checking dates against the collection inventory—to know the way that mind moved on the page. The aphorisms. The inversions. *What the sea keeps, the sea catalogues.* She could hear him writing it. She could see the n, the careful slant of it, in the dark behind her eyelids.

Someone had read those notebooks. Someone had read them closely, lovingly, the way scholars read scripture—and had learned to speak in Zephyrine's voice.

Or.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, terrible:

Or Zephyrine had written them.

No. She rejected it the way you reject a stumble on a staircase—one instant of free fall, then the body's firm refusal. She had watched him open his notebooks for Ilsa Brandt with the reluctance of a man handing over organs. She had watched him confront his own archive looking for the leak, hunting himself as rigorously as he hunted anyone. Whatever else Zephyrine Voss was, he was not a man who performed innocence; he was a man who barely believed he possessed any.

But someone had stood where she had stood, among those boxes, and read, and studied, and waited. For years. The postmarks went back eighteen months. Eighteen months of someone rehearsing Zephyrine's sentences in the dark, sending them to an old woman, emptying a grave, drowning a clerk, cutting brake lines—all of it conducted in the borrowed grammar of the man Marguerite loved.

"Miss Hale." Odette's hand was on hers, cool and dry and steady. "You've thought of something."

"I've thought of something awful." Marguerite gathered the notes together with hands that would not quite obey her. "Mrs. Ferris, I have to take these to Constable Brandt. And then—" She stopped. Swallowed. "There's a man I need to talk to. Tonight."

"The bookseller." Something knowing and sad crossed Odette's face. "Voss. Yes. He came asking questions too, last week. Sat exactly where you're sitting and asked me about the trust's disbursements with those grey eyes of his that make you feel like a ledger being audited." She paused. "I lied to him, you know. Told him I'd lost touch with the estate papers decades ago. Habit, I suppose. Fifty years of habit." She looked at the notes in Marguerite's hands. "Don't make my mistake, girl. Whatever it costs. Silence is how this thing in Greywater feeds. It has eaten from my silence for half a century, and look what it grew into."

At the door, with the evening coming down over the harbour and the first spit of rain arriving sideways, Odette caught Marguerite's sleeve.

"One more thing. The night your mother came here, throwing tea, demanding answers—there was someone else in the house. In the kitchen, listening. Aldous's secretary. A young woman then, devoted as a dog. She watched your mother leave with such hatred, Miss Hale. Such pure, patient hatred. I told myself it was loyalty." Odette's grip tightened. "I've been telling myself that for fifty years. But hatred doesn't usually wait that patiently unless it's learning something. Unless it's taking notes."

The rain thickened. Down the lane, the lights of the town were coming on one by one, and somewhere among them a bookshop window glowed, and inside it a man was probably bent over his notebooks, writing sentences that someone else had learned to steal.

Marguerite walked fast, the notes pressed against her chest like contraband, and the vertigo did not lift with distance or motion. Because the question had rooted itself now, and it would grow whether she watered it or not:

Who had been inside Zephyrine's life for years—and how long had they been standing just behind him, reading over his shoulder, learning to be him?

She reached the esplanade and stopped, breathless, and looked back once at Harbour Lane, at the crooked houses leaning toward the darkening sea.

In the upstairs window of the Ferris house, a light went out.
