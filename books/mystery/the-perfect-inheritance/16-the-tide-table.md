## 16. The Tide Table

The case file had been open on Ilsa Brandt's desk for three days, and it had begun to feel less like a document and more like a tide pool—something alive beneath the surface, waiting for her to lean close enough to be pulled in.

She had pinned everything to the corkboard behind her desk at the constabulary, a habit she'd learned from Zephyrine Voss years ago, back when she was a constable with more questions than authority and he was a bookseller who happened to notice things. Photographs, timelines, the rotted strongbox pulled from the sea caves, the wax seal impression that matched the missing codicil. Red thread connected the three old deaths to the new ones. The board looked, from a certain angle, like a nervous system. Like something thinking.

Tonight, past eleven, with the station empty and the radiator ticking like a patient clock, she stood before it with a mug of cold tea and let the pattern speak.

The exhumation had been scheduled for the first of the month. The coffin found empty on the first of the month. The strongbox discovered by divers on the fourteenth—no, she corrected herself, *found* on the fourteenth, but it had been down there for years, the rot in the leather said decades. The brake failure on the cliff road had come nine days after the exhumation. The clerk's drowning, six days after that. And the taunting note in Odette Ferris's possession, undated but recent, the paper still crisp.

She had been reading the case forward, as investigators do—from crime to crime, from grave to grave. Tonight, for the first time, she read it backward.

She took down the tide table from the hook by the door. Greywater's harbormaster printed them every year, a slim grey booklet with a sailboat on the cover, and most years it hung untouched until some fisherman needed to argue about the morning catch. She flipped to the current month and ran her finger down the columns, and the cold that moved through her had nothing to do with the draft under the door.

The exhumation date had not been chosen by the town council. She had assumed it had—some anniversary, some civic convenience, a Tuesday that suited the digger's schedule. But the first of the month was not an anniversary of anything. It was, according to the tide table, the date of an unusually low tide. Not the lowest of the year—that was still to come—but low enough. Low enough that the sea caves beneath the Merrick estate, drowned most hours of most days, would have stood open and breathing for nearly three hours.

She turned the pages. The fourteenth, the day the divers went in: another trough in the long rhythm of the water. The night of the brake failure: the tide had been at its lowest ebb in six weeks, the cliff road slick with sea fog that rolled in only when the water pulled far back from the rocks. The clerk's drowning—the boating accident, the *second* boating accident, the echo of the first—the man had gone into the water at slack tide, at the exact hour when the current through the cave mouth ran gentlest, when a body could be carried in and settled on the floor of the deep channel without fighting the sea.

Every date on her board sat on a trough in the tide table like a stone set in a wall.

Ilsa set the booklet down. Her hands were steady, but only because she made them be.

*He doesn't work around the tides,* she thought. *He works with them.*

The killer had not merely chosen convenient moments. The whole architecture of it—the exhumation, the sabotage, the drowning, the disposal—had been laid over the year's tidal rhythm the way a composer lays a melody over a bass line. Which meant the plan had been made long ago. Which meant it was not a plan at all. It was a score, and he had been playing it for months, perhaps years, waiting for the water to give him his cues.

And if the water had given him his cues, it would give her his next one.

She turned to the final pages of the booklet, to the table for the weeks ahead, and there it was, printed in the harbormaster's plain type: four days from tonight, the new moon, the lowest tide of the year. Minus two point three meters. The sea would pull back from the cliffs like a held breath released, and the floor of the caves—the deep channel, the places no diver could safely reach in the dark—would stand exposed to the open air for the better part of an afternoon.

The strongbox had been found in the outer cave, where the divers could go. But the wax seal impression had been clean, deliberate, *presented*. A thing left to be found. And a man who left one thing to be found was a man who had put something else where it could not be found—until now.

The codicil. The original. Marguerite Hale's inheritance, the document that named an heir no one had expected, the paper Bram Quill swore had never been filed, that the estate searches had never turned up. If it still existed—and everything in this case insisted that it did, that the killer was a man who destroyed nothing he could instead hide—then it had gone into the water. Weighted. Dropped deep. Sunk in the channel where the tide would keep it for him, safe in the dark, until the one afternoon of the year when the sea itself would hand it back.

*Four days,* Ilsa thought. *He's been waiting four years for four days.*

She sat down at her desk and pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward her, and began to write it out, because writing made thought honest.

The codicil was signed—she was certain of this now, certain the way one becomes certain of a thing only after doubting it past all reason—shortly before Merrick's death. The letters Marguerite had found said as much: the founder planning to disinherit them all, then changing his mind after meeting someone unnamed. A document like that, naming an unacknowledged daughter, would have been worth killing for a hundred years ago and was worth killing for now. The killer had taken it—had been there, at the death or the burial or the reading of the will, close enough to lift it—and he had not destroyed it.

That was the part that had troubled her from the beginning, and it troubled her still: he did not destroy. He emptied the coffin but left it in the ground. He emptied the strongbox but left the seal. He drowned the clerk but staged it as an accident Greywater would accept, because Greywater preferred its accidents, because an accepted accident was a kind of preservation. He was not erasing evidence. He was *curating* it. He was keeping the record intact and only rearranging what the record said.

So the codicil would not have been burned. It would have been *filed*—in the only archive he trusted, the one that opened twice a day and kept its own schedule and answered to no constable. Weighted with lead, she guessed, the way the strongbox had been weighted. Dropped into the deep channel. And there it would lie, paper in lead, lead in cold black water, until the new moon.

She looked at the board again, and the nervous system of it seemed to shift, the red thread rearranging itself in her mind into something else: a countdown. Every crime a note struck on the way to this one date. The exhumation to unseal the grave and prove, to whoever watched, that the story could be opened. The brake failure to silence Corwin, the last living soul who might contest the legend. The clerk to sever the paper trail. All of it, all of it, a procession moving toward the low water—and toward whatever the low water would expose.

*So will whatever else was sunk with it.*

The thought arrived without permission, the way the worst ones did. She set down her pen.

The codicil might not have gone into the channel alone. A man who curated, who staged, who left seals and empty coffins and notes quoting a dead detective's first case—a man like that did not sink a document. He sank a *tableau*. Whatever lay at the bottom of that channel had been arranged, and arranged for an audience, and she did not yet know who the audience was meant to be. But the note in the strongbox had been addressed to Zephyrine Voss by name. The brake failure had been a reproduction of a death Zephyrine had solved in secret. The killer was not hiding from the old detective. He was performing for him.

Which meant the lowest tide of the year was not the killer's deadline.

It was his *invitation*.

Ilsa stood, crossed to the window, and looked out at the harbor. The water lay black and smooth under the streetlamps, patient as a held secret. Somewhere out past the breakwater, past the cliff line where the Merrick estate stood dark against the stars, the caves were flooding with the rising tide, filling up again with all the things they kept.

She thought about calling Zephyrine. It was past eleven; he would be awake—men like him were always awake, she suspected, lying in the dark above his books, listening to the town breathe. She thought about telling him everything: the tide table, the countdown, her certainty about the channel. And she thought about what he had withheld from her, years ago, the evidence he had sat on while three people were buried under the word *accident*. The betrayal of that still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Wary alliance, she had called it, and wary it remained. If she told him about the tide, he would come to the caves. He would insist. And if the killer was performing for him, then bringing Zephyrine to the lowest tide of the year was bringing the killer his audience, his closing night, exactly as invited.

But not telling him was its own kind of danger. He was the only other person alive who could read this case the way she was beginning to read it, and if she went to the caves without him and something waited there—something with a knife, or a plan, or twenty years of devotion behind its hands—she would be alone with it in a hole in the world where the sea had withdrawn but would, in a few hours' time, come flooding back.

She picked up the telephone, set it down, picked it up again.

*Four days.* The words had begun to tick in her like the radiator, like the clock in the tower, like everything in Greywater that counted time without caring what it counted toward.

She dialed.

It rang twice. "Voss." His voice was exactly as she'd known it would be: awake, dry, already braced.

"I've been reading the tide table," Ilsa said. "The killer has too. Every date on my board sits on a low tide—the exhumation, the brake failure, the clerk. He built the whole thing on the water's schedule. And in four days there's a new moon. Minus two-three. The lowest tide of the year." She heard him breathe, and in the breathing, the sound of a man arriving at the same conclusion from the other side. "The codicil is in the deep channel, Zephyrine. Weighted and sunk. And in four days the sea is going to give it back—to us, or to him, or to whoever he wants standing on that cave floor when the water pulls away."

A long silence. Down the line, faintly, she heard a clock, and beneath the clock something else—paper moving, as if he had his own tide table open already, as if he had known, or half-known, and had been waiting for her to say it aloud.

"You said *whatever else was sunk with it*," he said at last. "I heard it in your voice. You think it's not just the codicil down there."

"I think a man who empties coffins and leaves them in the ground doesn't sink a document. He sinks a scene. And I think he's arranged it to be found at the lowest tide of the year, by whoever he's been performing for since the beginning." She pressed her palm flat against the cold window glass. "Which means in four days, we don't find his ending. We walk into it."

"Then we have four days," Zephyrine said, "to decide who walks in first."

The line went quiet. Out on the black water, the tide had already begun its long turn, filling the caves inch by inch, keeping everything exactly where he had left it—patient, faithful, counting down to the new moon the way it had counted down for a hundred years.

* * *

She did not sleep. She told herself she would, after one more pass through the file, but by two in the morning she had moved from the desk to the floor of the station house, the tide table and the case photographs spread around her in a half-moon of lamplight, and the pattern had stopped being a pattern and become a sentence, and she was reading it over and over trying to change the grammar.

Because there was one entry that did not fit, and it had been bothering her for hours the way a splinter bothers the hand that can't reach it.

The clerk's drowning. She had placed it on the tide table—slack water, gentle current, the body carried into the deep channel. But when she went back to the harbormaster's figures for that night, the numbers refused her. Slack tide had come at 11:40. The clerk had last been seen alive at the Anchor at half past nine, by three witnesses, and the coroner's estimate—based on water temperature, on lividity, on the stomach contents that had come back that afternoon—put him in the water between ten and eleven. An hour before slack. An hour when the current through the cave mouth ran hard, when anything dropped into the channel would have been swept *out* to sea, not settled into it.

Either the coroner was wrong. Or the killer had not needed the tide that night—had known the channel well enough, in the dark, against the current, to place a body exactly where he wanted it. Which meant he had been into the caves before. Many times. Recently. Which meant the channel floor was not a grave he had dug once and left.

It was a room he had been furnishing.

Ilsa sat back on her heels. The lamp hissed. Somewhere down the street a dog began to bark at nothing, or at something, and stopped.

She thought about the divers' report, and pulled it toward her. The outer cave, where the strongbox had been found, had been surveyed thoroughly—photographs, measurements, the silt line mapped. But the deep channel, the inner passage, had been written off in a single line: *unsafe at current conditions, floor not visible, recommend survey at extreme low water.* The divers had noted, almost as an afterthought, that the channel floor showed what they took to be natural rock formations. Concretions, they'd called them. Oddly regular concretions.

Oddly regular.

She was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, shrugging into her coat, and she stood a moment in the empty station with her hand on the light switch, arguing with herself the way she had been arguing with herself all night. It was three in the morning. The caves were flooding. There was nothing to see but black water and a long wait, and every instinct of the job said go home, sleep, come back with daylight and a warrant and backup.

But the job had also said, three years ago, that a bookseller's silence was none of her business, and she had believed it, and three people had gone into the ground under the word *accident* while she filed her reports and went home to sleep.

She turned off the light. She did not go home.

She drove out along the cliff road instead, slow, headlights sweeping the fog, and parked where the track dropped toward the Merrick estate, and got out, and stood at the rail above the water. The tide was high now, the sea pressed close against the cliffs, and beneath it, invisible, patient, the caves were full. Somewhere down there in the dark, in the channel the divers had called unsafe, lay a floor of oddly regular concretions, and a lead-weighted codicil, and whatever else a devoted hand had spent years arranging for the lowest tide of the year.

Four days. The moon was already dying overhead, a thin paring of light behind the fog.

She took out her notebook and wrote the date of the new moon at the top of a fresh page, and under it she wrote the hour of the low water, and under that, in letters she pressed hard enough to read by touch alone, she wrote: *He wants an audience. We choose the audience.*

Then she closed the book, and stood a while longer at the rail, listening to the sea work at the cliffs below, patient as its keeper, keeping his secrets until the water could keep them no longer.
