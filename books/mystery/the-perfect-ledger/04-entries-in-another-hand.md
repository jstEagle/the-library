## 4. Entries in Another Hand

The watcher's hands were steady. That was the first discipline: steadiness. A trembling hand wrote a trembling sentence, and a trembling sentence was a confession waiting to be read.

From the third-floor window of the old Hollis Building — vacant since the pharmacy moved out, its glass still bearing the ghost-letters of a optometrist long dead — she watched Sebastian Dravenmoor cross Wexford Street at 8:14 in the morning, exactly as he crossed it every morning, his coat collar turned up against the river damp, his stride that of a man who had never once in his life been late and never once in his life been early. Punctuality was its own kind of ledger, she thought. Every man kept one, whether he knew it or not. Dravenmoor simply kept his in his head.

She opened the diary on her knees.

*October 3rd.* She wrote it in Clara Voss's hand, which was to say, in her own hand now; the two had merged years ago, the way a river merges with the bed it has carved. *He walked past the bakery today and did not stop. He always stops on Thursdays. Today is Thursday. Something has changed in him.*

It was true. She had seen it in the set of his shoulders as he passed the window of Marlowe's Bakery — the small hitch, the half-turn of the head, the way his eyes went not to the pastry case but to the reflection in the glass, checking the street behind him. Three days ago he had reopened her file. Three days ago the hunter had picked up the scent again, and now the hunted was watching him hunt.

There was a symmetry to it that pleased her. Dravenmoor believed himself the keeper of Hollowmere's secrets because he remembered everything. But memory was only half of accounting. The other half was recording. And she had been recording for seven years — every entry, every date, every sum, every sin — while his perfect recollection sat locked inside his skull like gold in a vault no heir would ever find.

She dipped the pen. Clara's blue-black ink. Always the same brand; she bought it from the same stationer in Corvane, two towns over, paying cash, wearing the gray coat and the plain expression of a woman who was nobody at all.

*He will go to the Hall of Records this morning,* she wrote. *He will pull the deed transfers for the Aldrich parcel, 2019, because yesterday Mrs. Fenwick told him about the apple orchard that vanished. He thinks he is following threads. He does not know the threads were laid for him, one at a time, like bread crumbs by a careful child — except my crumbs lead backward, to the house where the witch lives, and the witch is me.*

She paused. Below the window, Dravenmoor had stopped at the corner. He was looking up — not at her window, not quite, but at the building itself, at the row of dark glass eyes above the shuttered pharmacy. For three full seconds he stood there, and she did not move, did not breathe, did not exist.

Then he walked on toward the records hall, and she let her breath out slowly, and added a single line:

*He looked up. Not yet at me. But soon.*

* * *

Sebastian felt the eyes before he could name them.

It was an old instinct, one he distrusted precisely because it had no evidentiary basis — a feeling, nothing more, and feelings were the currency of people who couldn't remember. He had spent his whole life trusting the archive in his head over the weather in his gut. But standing before the microfilm reader in the Hall of Records, the Aldrich parcel deed glowing green on the screen, he became aware of a sensation he had no clean word for: the sense of being *described*. As though somewhere, someone were writing him down.

He shook it off and read.

The deed transfer was unremarkable on its face — forty acres sold by Edith Aldrich to the Founders' Land Trust for $1,200, a sum so insultingly small it functioned as a signature. He remembered Edith Aldrich. He remembered the week the orchard came down, the smell of the burning prunings drifting across town, the way old Mrs. Fenwick had stood at her fence for three days watching. What he had never done — what no one had ever asked him to do — was hold that memory up against the paper record and see how neatly they matched. Two witnesses agreeing perfectly after seven years. One of them made of flesh. One of ink.

His filing system, when he got home that evening, was the same as it had been for twenty years: case folders alphabetized, cross-referenced, each document sleeved and dated in his own precise hand. He pulled the Voss folder from the drawer marked **V** and laid the diary beside it, and the sight of them together produced in him a feeling he recognized from only one other occasion in his life — the Merrow case, eleven years ago, the drowned boy whose death he had helped file as accident because the alternative was a war with the Founders' Circle that he'd lacked the ammunition to fight. He had buried that guilt so deep that even his memory seemed to have agreed to keep it in a sealed room. But the diary had found the door.

Because there it was, in the entry dated three days ago:

*The investigator keeps his files in alphabetical order. He believes order is innocence. He should ask himself why the Merrow file sits between Marsh and Mott, complete, closed, and wrong.*

Sebastian sat down slowly in his kitchen chair.

No one knew about the Merrow file. No living person. Chief Renner had been there, yes, but Renner would sooner confess to murder than to having signed off on a drowning called an accident. The file existed in exactly two places: Sebastian's drawer, and Sebastian's memory. And yet here was Clara Voss — or whoever wore Clara Voss's hand — writing about it as casually as a neighbor commenting on his hedge.

He turned pages backward, hunting for the moment the diary had begun to know too much. There — the entry from his second day on the case:

*He works nights. Tea, not coffee. He reads standing up, pacing, because sitting still makes him feel like a suspect.*

And earlier still, the very first entry after the file was reopened:

*They gave him my name again. Good. I have waited seven years for someone with a memory like his. Let him come. I have left the trail lit like a runway.*

Sebastian read that line four times. *Left the trail lit.* It changed the shape of everything. He had imagined himself a bloodhound stumbling onto a cold scent. But the entries weren't slips. They were invitations. Someone was feeding him the case the way you feed a fire — one log at a time, timed precisely, each piece placed where his curiosity would find it. Mrs. Fenwick and her orchard. The deed. The dates in the margins that matched the donor plaque outside the Founders' Hall.

He was not investigating the diary.

The diary was investigating *him*.

* * *

That night the watcher sat in the dark of the Hollis Building with a thermos of tea and the diary open on her knees, and she wrote the day's account while it was still warm.

*He found the Merrow reference within hours of bringing the diary home. Faster than I estimated. He sat very still for a long time — I could see the lamp through his kitchen window, and the lamp did not move, and a man who does not move for forty minutes is either asleep or rearranging his entire understanding of the world.*

She smiled, and the smile felt strange on a face that hadn't been seen in seven years. Faces atrophied, like anything unused.

*Here is what he does not yet understand:* she wrote, the pen moving in the sure, small script that had fooled a police evidence clerk and would fool God Himself if it came to that. *I chose him. Of everyone in Hollowmere, I chose the man who cannot forget, because I am building something that must not be forgotten. A ledger needs two things to be true: a record, and a witness. I am the record. He will be the witness. And when the Founders sit down to their anniversary dinner and raise their glasses to seven generations of theft dressed up as legacy, the witness will stand among them holding the whole account in his head, unable to plead ignorance, unable to forget, unable to look away.*

*That is not cruelty. That is bookkeeping.*

She stopped. Below her, far down Wexford Street, a light had come on in the records office — no, not the records office. The police annex. Chief Renner's window, burning at nearly midnight. She watched it, and her pen hovered, and then she wrote the last lines of the entry carefully, because they were the most important ones she had written in seven years:

*Renner is awake tonight. He has heard that Dravenmoor pulled the Aldrich deed. He will call Harrow in the morning, and Harrow will do what Harrow always does, which is the thing he did to me. Seven years ago they decided I was a problem to be filed away. They are about to learn what happens when the file writes back.*

*One more thing. The investigator's guilt is load-bearing. If he opens the Merrow file — truly opens it, not just looks at the cover — he will stop being their instrument and start being mine. I have left the boy's real autopsy discrepancy where only a man with a perfect memory can find it. Page nine. The water in the lungs.*

*Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow we see what kind of man remembers.*

* * *

Sebastian didn't sleep. At two in the morning he stood in his kitchen in the yellow light, the diary open on the counter, and made tea he forgot to drink, and did the thing he had avoided for eleven years: he drove to the annex, woke the night clerk with his badge and his reputation, and pulled the Merrow file.

Page nine. The water in the lungs.

He remembered the autopsy report perfectly — of course he did, he remembered everything — but he had never once *looked* at it, not really, because looking would mean comparing, and comparing would mean knowing, and knowing would make him complicit in a way that filing had somehow never managed. Freshwater drowning. Danny Merrow, found in the reservoir. Except the lab notes said saline. Salt water in a boy's lungs who drowned in fresh water. Somebody had moved the body. Somebody had drowned him somewhere else — a bathtub, maybe, or a boat on the coast — and dumped him in the reservoir to make it look local, and the report had been quietly amended, and Sebastian had signed the closure memo with a steady hand and a sealed room in his head.

Salt water.

He stood in the annex corridor under the buzzing fluorescent tube and felt the sealed room open all at once, all its air rushing out, and understood with total clarity that whoever was writing Clara Voss's diary had known this would happen. Had counted on it. Had left page nine lit like a runway.

He was not the hunter. He had never been the hunter. He was the witness being built, entry by entry, into someone else's perfect ledger — and the worst of it, the part that made his hands go cold around the file, was that he wasn't sure he objected.

He carried the Merrow folder home through the empty streets, and behind him, high in the dark of the Hollis Building, a pen scratched steadily against paper, recording every step.
