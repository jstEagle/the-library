## 3. The Ledger in Plain Sight

The state auditor's letter arrived on a Tuesday, which is the day I reserve for hating paperwork that arrives on Tuesdays. It was one page, typed in a font that suggested the state had purchased its word processors in bulk sometime during the Reagan administration, and it said, in essence, that the Office of the Coroner of Marrow Creek would be subject to a records compliance review in six weeks, and that all files — active, cold, and archival — should be catalogued to current standards.

Current standards. As if the standards had been standing still all this time, waiting for me to catch up.

I read the letter twice, then a third time aloud to Mr. Pemberton, my cat, who was asleep on top of the 1997 homicide files, which is where he sleeps, because cats understand instinctively that the best place to sit is wherever you need to be next.

"Six weeks," I told him. "Six weeks to make thirty years of other people's deaths presentable."

He yawned. Cats are not impressed by deadlines. Cats have never once been impressed by anything, which is either wisdom or a failure of imagination, and I've never decided which.

Here is the thing about being a coroner in a town the size of Marrow Creek: you are also, by necessity, the archivist, the records clerk, the IT department (a desktop computer and a fax machine that wheezes), and occasionally the person who unjams the filing cabinet drawer that sticks every June when the humidity comes up off the creek. I have been all of these things for twelve years, since Ada Quill retired and handed me the keys along with a tin of shortbread and the sentence, "The dead are patient, Juniper. It's the living you have to watch."

I'd always assumed that was a joke. Ada had a way of saying things that were either profound or nothing at all, delivered in the same dry voice, so you never knew which ones to write down. I'd written that one down anyway, on the inside cover of my first case log, back when I still wrote things down.

The filing system in the back room of the coroner's office was Ada's system, and I had never once questioned it, because questioning Ada's system would have been like questioning the water table or the route of the creek. It simply was. Cold cases went in the gray boxes, alphabetized by surname. Archival materials went in the green boxes, by year. And the old books — the ledgers, the registers, the funeral home records that predated my employment by half a century — went on the oak shelving along the east wall, catalogued in Ada's small, slanted, immaculate hand.

It was that hand I was thinking about, six days into the re-cataloguing project, when I found the ledger.

* * *

The ledger itself was unremarkable at first glance. Marrow Creek Funeral Register, 1984–1996. Bound in green cloth that had faded to the color of old bottle glass, corners worn soft, spine cracked in the way of books that have been opened ten thousand times. The Marrow Creek Funeral Home had gone out of business in 2003, swallowed by a conglomerate out of Coeur d'Alene, and their records had been boxed and shipped to my office because, I suppose, no one else wanted them and I had never learned to say no to a box.

Ada had catalogued it in 2004, according to the label on the spine. *MC-FH-1984/96. Quill, A. Cross-referenced.*

Cross-referenced. That word sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone, though I couldn't have told you why. Not yet.

I had been at this re-filing business for six days, and I will confess that I had developed a system of my own, which was to do the least pleasant boxes first, on the theory that misery compounds and should therefore be spent early. The funeral registers were not unpleasant, exactly. They were just old, and old paper has a smell — dust and glue and something faintly sweet, like the ghost of flowers — that gets into your sinuses and stays there.

I opened the ledger on the long steel table where I do my examinations, and I flattened it with the glass weights, and I began, as is my habit, at the beginning.

The first thing you learn reading funeral registers is that they are not about death. Not really. They are about paperwork. Name of deceased. Date of death. Date of interment. Cause of death, as certified. Plot number. Next of kin. Officiant. It is a book of transactions, and the people in it are line items, and there is something in that which should offend me more than it does. Perhaps I have been in this business long enough that I have made my peace with being a line item myself someday. *Villalobos, J. Cause of death: stubbornness. Plot: TBD.*

The entries from 1984 and 1985 were ordinary. Heart failure, pneumonia, a farm accident involving a hay baler that I remembered from my mother's stories — the Kowalski boy, though by 1985 he'd have been sixty. The handwriting changed every few years as the funeral directors came and went, and I found myself reading the way I read everything: not for the information, but for the texture. For what the paper remembered that the people didn't.

It was on the fourth day, somewhere in the middle of 1989, that the texture changed.

* * *

I want to be precise about what I noticed, because precision is the only prayer I have ever known how to say.

The dates were out of sequence.

Not by much. Not in any way that would jump out at a casual reader, or even at a state auditor with a checklist and a plane to catch. But I have spent twelve years reading dates the way other people read faces, and a date out of order hits me the way a wrong note hits a musician. Entry forty-one, March 1989. Entry forty-two, March 1989. Entry forty-three, February 1989. A month earlier, filed as if no one had noticed, or as if someone had wanted it tucked between its neighbors like a card forced into a deck.

One entry could be a clerical slip. Clerical slips happen; I have made them myself, though never, I'd have sworn on my mother's grave, in a book of the dead. But I turned the page, and there was another. And another, three weeks later. By the time I reached 1991 I had counted six dates that sat wrong in the sequence, the way a bone sits wrong in a badly healed fracture. Functional, if you don't look. Grotesque, if you do.

I did what I always do when something bothers me, which is to make tea and pretend I'm not bothered, and then to bother anyway.

I sat back down with the ledger and my reading glasses and a yellow legal pad, and I started over from the beginning, this time reading like a coroner instead of a reader. Slowly. Twice. With a pencil in my teeth.

The initials were the second thing.

Every register has its repeat customers, so to speak — the same families burying their elders across decades, the same officiants, the same plot sections filling up like a parking lot. But this was different. Certain sets of initials kept surfacing in the cause-of-death column, in the certification lines, in the margins where the funeral director noted special instructions. *H.D.* *V.H.* *R.M.* They appeared in combinations, always the same combinations, always in the years I'd flagged for the date anomalies, and always — and this is the part that made the tea go cold in my cup — always adjacent to deaths that had been ruled accidental.

Drownings. Falls. A house fire. A single-car collision on a road so straight that the county had never once bothered to paint a center line, because there was nowhere to drift to.

Seven of them, between 1985 and 1994. Seven accidental deaths in a town that averages, in a good year, one.

I know what you're thinking, because I thought it too, standing at the steel table with my glasses pushed up into my hair: small towns are dangerous places, Juniper. Grain augers and icy bridges and old men with bad hearts and rifles. Accidents cluster. It means nothing.

And then I found the codes.

* * *

Every cause of death in the register was written out in full, in the handwriting of whichever funeral director held the pen that year — *myocardial infarction, carcinoma, cerebral hemorrhage* — except for seven. The seven. For those, someone had used a code. Two letters and a number, small and neat, written in a hand I did not recognize: not the florid script of the eighties, not the tired ballpoint scrawl of the nineties. Small. Controlled. The handwriting of a person who was being very, very careful.

*Q-7. Q-7. M-3. Q-7.*

I have a reference shelf. It is not a large shelf, because coroners in small towns do not require large shelves, but it contains every coding manual and classification standard issued in my professional lifetime, and three that predate it. I pulled them all down and I looked up Q-7 and M-3 and the two others, and I will save you the suspense: they do not exist. Not in the ICD revisions, not in the state's vital statistics codes, not in the funeral industry's internal classifications, not anywhere. They are codes from a system that was never published, which means they were never meant to be read, which means they were never meant for the auditor, the state, or God.

They were meant for whoever held the key.

I sat down on my stool and I looked at that ledger for a long time. The afternoon light had moved across the steel table and was now lying on the floor like something that had given up. Somewhere down the hall the fax machine wheezed at nothing, the way it does, and outside a truck went by on the county road, and Marrow Creek went on being Marrow Creek, and I sat in the middle of it all with a book of the dead that had been lying to me.

That was when I looked at the catalogue label again.

*MC-FH-1984/96. Quill, A. Cross-referenced.*

Cross-referenced by Ada Quill, retired coroner, my mentor, the woman who taught me how to suture a mouth closed and how to tell a widow the truth without breaking her, the woman who gave me the tin of shortbread and the sentence about the dead being patient. Ada, whose filing system I had trusted for twelve years the way you trust the water table.

Ada had catalogued this ledger. Ada had held it in her careful, capable hands — the same hands, incidentally, that had taught me to write a case report, small and controlled and precise — and she had labelled it, shelved it, and left it on the east wall where it sat in plain sight for twenty years, and no one, in all that time, had ever opened it.

Plain sight. I have buried enough people to know that plain sight is the best place to hide anything. The cemetery is in plain sight. Nobody looks at it. Everybody drives past it at forty miles an hour, and the dead keep their own counsel under the grass.

But here is what I could not get past, sitting there in the failing light with the ledger open in front of me: the anomalies were not hidden. That's what I kept coming back to, turning it over like a stone in my pocket. The dates were wrong in a way you'd only catch if you read the whole sequence. The initials were there for anyone with patience. The codes were right there in the cause-of-death column, small but legible, unencrypted, unexplained. Whoever had written them had not concealed them. They had *filed* them.

And Ada — Ada, who cross-referenced everything, who once made me redo an entire year of intake logs because I'd abbreviated *September* — Ada had catalogued this book and shelved it, and if she had read it the way she read everything, then she had seen the wrong dates and the strange initials and the codes that don't exist, and she had written *cross-referenced* on the spine in her immaculate hand, and she had put it back on the shelf.

Which meant one of two things. Either Ada Quill had gotten sloppy in 2004, in the one instance in her professional life where sloppiness mattered most — and I will tell you now, before I know anything else, that I do not believe that, I do not believe it the way I do not believe the sun rises in the west — or Ada Quill had seen all of it, understood all of it, and filed it away to be found.

By whom? By me? That's vanity. By anyone? By no one? By the dead themselves, who are, as she once told me, patient?

I closed the ledger and I did not put it back on the shelf.

* * *

That night I took it home, which I have never done with any record in twelve years, and which I told myself was for security purposes, and which was a lie, because the lock on my office door is better than the lock on my house. I took it home because I did not want to be in the office with it after dark, and because I did not want to be without it after dark either, and both of those impulses are, I recognize, the impulses of a person who has started to be afraid, and I am not a person who scares easily. I handle the dead for a living. The dead have never once threatened me. It's the living who make my hands unsteady, and something in that ledger was alive — some intent, some old and patient purpose — and it had been sitting on my shelf, breathing quietly, for twenty years.

I made tea I didn't drink. Mr. Pemberton sat on the ledger, which I chose to interpret as protective rather than obstructive. And I went through it one more time, page by page, and this time I wrote down everything: the seven dates, the seven names — I'll spare you the recitation, but I will tell you that two of the surnames were ones every person in Marrow Creek can say in their sleep, because they're on the bank and the feed store and the plaque outside the county courthouse — and the seven codes, and the initials in the margins, and one more thing I hadn't seen before.

In the margin of the last coded entry, dated October 1994, beside the name of a man whose death was ruled a fall from the water tower, there was a mark. Not a code. Not an initial. A small, slanted dash of ink, four strokes, like a tally mark or a signature reduced to a gesture.

I had seen that mark before. Not in a ledger. Not in a file.

I had seen it in the margins of my own training notes, twenty years ago, in the hand of the woman who was teaching me how to write things down — a little shorthand flourish, a habit, the kind of mark a person makes without thinking, the kind of mark that gives you away the way a limp gives you away in the dark.

I set down the pencil. My hands were not unsteady, I'm pleased to report. My hands were perfectly steady. It was everything else that had come loose.

The ledger had been catalogued by Ada Quill. And somewhere in its margins, in a code only she could read, Ada Quill had signed her name.
