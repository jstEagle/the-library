## 4. What Elias Knows

I rehearsed the speech for thirty years. In motel bathrooms, in rented cars, in the parking lot of a courthouse in Reno where a clerk told me records from 1989 had been "misfiled" and then looked at me like I'd asked her to explain gravity. I had versions. Short versions for bartenders. Long versions for lawyers. A version with diagrams for a private investigator in Sacramento who took my money and then, apparently, took a vacation from returning calls.

None of the versions survived contact with Juniper Villalobos.

She didn't interrupt me. That was the problem. I'd built my whole delivery around the assumption that people interrupt — that they need managing, redirecting, that grief makes them fidget and disbelief makes them talk. Juniper sat behind her desk with her hands folded and her eyes on me like I was a document she'd been asked to authenticate, and every rehearsed sentence I'd carried into that office dissolved somewhere between my chest and my mouth.

So I started over. From the beginning. The true beginning, not the tidy one.

"My mother's name was Lena Wren," I said. "Before she married my father, it was Lena Ochoa. She came to Marrow Creek in the spring of 1989 to work as a bookkeeper for the Hollis land office. She drowned in Miller's Bend on the fourth of August. I was nine."

The office was quiet in the way I'd already learned offices in small towns are quiet — not empty quiet, but attentive quiet, like the room itself was taking notes. A ceiling fan turned overhead with the lazy skepticism of a thing that had seen every kind of story walk under it.

"I was in Reno with my father that summer," I went on. "They were separated. Not divorced — separated, which in 1989 meant she was trying to decide something and he was trying not to find out what. She wrote me letters. Actual letters, on paper, because I was nine and phones cost money and she was careful with money." I stopped. Swallowed. "The last one arrived the week after she died. Postmarked the day before."

Juniper's eyes didn't soften. I want to be clear about that, because I'd expected softness and braced against it, and what I got instead was worse and better: attention. Complete, unblinking, professional attention, as though my mother were a body on her table and every detail I gave her was evidence.

"The coroner's report said accidental drowning," I said. "Alcohol involved. Point-two-one."

"High," Juniper said. Her voice was low and even. "For a woman who swam, that would be very high."

"She swam every morning of her life. Lake Mead, the Yuba, whatever water was nearby. My father called her part otter." I laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, and it came out wrong. "She wouldn't have been drunk at Miller's Bend at eleven at night. She was afraid of that bend. She wrote about it. The current, she said. The current doesn't care how well you swim."

"And the alcohol?"

"Never tested, as far as I can tell. The point-two-one came from a witness statement. A man named Corliss who said he'd seen her drinking at the Drayton taproom earlier that evening. Corliss worked for the Draytons. He worked for the Draytons for another twenty years, right up until he retired and moved to Phoenix, where I found him in 2019 sitting on a porch that cost more than my mother's whole life."

Juniper leaned forward a fraction of an inch. "What did Corliss tell you?"

"Nothing, at first. Then he told me everything, and then he told me he'd never said any of it, and then his daughter came to the door, and I left." I rubbed my face. "But before the daughter — he said he'd been paid to say he saw her drinking. Fifty dollars. He said everyone got paid for something back then. He said it like that made it better. Like it was a town-wide discount program on conscience."

* * *

I should explain the thirty years, because thirty years is a strange thing to spend on anything, and people always want to know what kept me going. The honest answer is that nothing kept me going. I simply never stopped. There's a difference.

I grew up on the story of my mother's death the way other kids grow up on bedtime stories — except mine had a hole in the middle where the plot should be. My father drank himself into the grave by 1997, and his last coherent sentence to me was, "She was going to tell them something." Not who. Not what. Just *them*, with a weight on the word that I've been carrying ever since, like a stone in a coat pocket I'd forgotten I was wearing.

I became the kind of man who reads county records for fun. I got good at it. Good at the microfiche machines that eat quarters and the clerks who eat time and the particular smell of a records basement, which is the smell of paper slowly winning its argument against memory. And over the years, the hole in my mother's story started to look less like a hole and more like a window — because on the other side of it, I kept seeing the same faces.

"The Hollis land office," I told Juniper, "was the front for something called the Hollis-Drayton consortium. Formally it didn't exist. Informally, it bought up Marrow Creek the way water buys up a basement. And here's the pattern." I pulled out the folder — my folder, the one I'd carried across four states and into every bad motel between here and the coast. "Between 1985 and 1994, nine people died in this county in ways that were ruled accidental. I've got the names. A foreman who fell off a bridge he'd walked across every day for a decade. A surveyor whose truck went off Miller's Bend Road — same bend, same current, different year. A woman named Ruth Calloway who died in a house fire two weeks after refusing to sell the parcel her grandmother left her. And my mother, who was the bookkeeper, and who — according to her own letters — had found something she called 'the book of names.'"

I laid the pages out on Juniper's desk. She looked at them the way I imagine she looks at everything: without hurry, without mercy.

"Every one of those deaths," I said, "cleared land. Every one. A parcel freed up, a holdout removed, a title uncontested. And every one of those parcels ended up consolidated under the consortium by 1995, which is when they sold the whole assembly to the state for the reservoir expansion and the highway reroute. Do you know what that land was worth?"

"I can guess."

"Forty million dollars in 1995 money. For nine deaths and a stack of accident reports."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment. The fan turned. Somewhere down the hall a phone rang twice and gave up.

"You said nine," she said finally. "The ledger —" She stopped herself. Something moved behind her eyes, some internal filing system shuffling drawers. "I should show you something," she said, and then, as if correcting a clerical error in her own speech: "I mean, I may have something you should see. If you're right about the dates."

"You have it already," I said. "Don't you."

It wasn't a question, and she didn't treat it like one. She stood, and for the first time since I'd walked into her office, she looked uncertain — this woman who handled the dead for a living, who'd taken my retainer without flinching at the amount, who'd watched me lay out a thirty-year conspiracy on her desk with the composure of a chess player. Uncertainty looked strange on her. It looked, if I'm honest, human. It looked like the first real thing I'd seen in Marrow Creek.

"Follow me," she said.

* * *

The ledger sat in her hands like it weighed more than paper should. She'd carried it out from the back room wrapped in a clean white cloth, the way you'd carry a thing that was either evidence or a relic, and in Marrow Creek, I was learning, the distinction had never mattered much.

"1985 through 1994," she said. "It's the funeral ledger from Quill & Sons — Ada Quill's old firm. She was the county coroner for thirty years. She trained me." Her thumb moved along the edge of the page without turning it. "I catalogued this myself, four years ago. I filed it under routine. I have been re-filing the cold cases this week, and I found — anomalies."

"Show me."

She opened it, and I will tell you what I felt, because it wasn't triumph. I'd imagined this moment so many times — the proof in my hands, the vindication, the scene where I finally got to be right after thirty years of being the strange man who wouldn't let his mother rest. What I felt instead was cold. The specific cold of a thing becoming real. For thirty years my mother's death had been a wound, which at least meant it was mine. Now it was becoming a fact, and facts belong to everyone, and everyone in this town was going to have to look at it.

"Here." Juniper turned the ledger toward me. "August 4th, 1989. Lena Ochoa Wren."

My mother's name, in a stranger's careful hand. Date of death. Disposition. And in the cause column, a notation I'd seen a hundred times in a hundred reports: *accidental drowning.*

But Juniper's finger moved to the margin, and there — small, penciled, almost invisible — was a code. Letters and numbers in a sequence that meant nothing to me and, by the tightness around her mouth, something to her.

"That's not standard," she said. "None of these are. There are seven entries in this ledger with the same marginal code. Seven. And the dates —" She exhaled. "The dates don't sit right. Two of the burials are logged out of sequence, like they were entered late. Entered *after* something. And the cause-of-death notations use a format that wasn't adopted in this county until 1991, showing up in entries from 1986."

"Meaning?"

"Meaning somebody wrote this ledger at least partly in retrospect." She looked up at me. "Meaning it wasn't a record of what happened. It was a record of what was *decided*."

The fan turned. I heard my own breathing.

"Ada Quill catalogued this," Juniper said, and her voice had gone carefully flat, the way voices go when they're holding something down. "Ada Quill was the coroner for every one of those deaths. She signed the reports. She taught me how to do this job. She taught me that the dead can't lie, so we don't get to lie for them."

"Juniper." I said her name for the first time. It felt like stepping off a curb. "Whoever wrote that code knew what really happened. That's not a conspirator's ledger. That's a *witness's*. Somebody in the middle of it was keeping score."

She closed the ledger slowly, both hands, like shutting a casket.

"I need to think," she said. "I need to — there's an order to this. There's a right way to open a case. You don't just —" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again, the flatness had cracked, and underneath it was something rawer. "She was my mentor. She was the closest thing I've had to family in twelve years."

"I know what it is to find out someone you loved was standing in the water," I said. "Metaphorically. Or not."

She looked at me then — really looked, the professional attention dropping away for one unguarded second — and what was in her face wasn't pity or disbelief. It was recognition. The look of a woman who had also spent years talking to people who couldn't answer, and had just realized the living could be worse.

"Mr. Wren," she said. "Elias. I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to think before you answer, because I've watched this town bury things for twelve years and it's very good at it." She set the ledger on the desk between us, her hand flat on the cover. "How far are you willing to go?"

I thought of my mother's last letter, postmarked the day before she died, sitting in a shoebox in a motel room eleven miles from here. *I found something, sweetheart. I found the book of names. When I'm done with it, we'll never have to worry about anything again.* She'd underlined *never* twice. She was careful with money and careless with underlines, and I have spent thirty years wondering what she thought she was finishing.

"As far as it takes," I said. "I've got nowhere else to be. That's not self-pity. It's just inventory."

Something almost like a smile crossed Juniper Villalobos's face — brief, wry, gone. "Inventory," she repeated. "That's the first honest thing anyone's said in this office in years."

She picked up the ledger and held it against her chest, and I watched her make a decision — watched it happen behind her eyes the way weather happens behind a window, gradual and then all at once.

"There's someone I need to talk to," she said. "Someone who might be able to tell us what this code means. She's retired. She lives out past the water tower." A pause, and then, quieter, like a confession: "She also signed my mother's — your mother's — death certificate."

The floor did not move. The fan kept turning. But I felt it anyway, the thirty years tilting under me, the whole long investigation pivoting on its axis toward a house past the water tower where the woman who had taught Juniper Villalobos everything was presumably having her morning coffee, thirty years and three days' drive from the last time anyone had asked her the right question.

"Take me with you," I said.

"No." Juniper was already reaching for her coat. "You wait here. If I'm wrong about her, I don't want a witness. And if I'm right —" She paused at the door, and the morning light caught the ledger in her arms. "If I'm right, Elias, then the person who taught me to love the dead helped bury your mother. Give me an hour to find out which woman I've been living next to."

The door closed behind her. I stood alone in the office of the Marrow Creek coroner, surrounded by filing cabinets full of the town's finished stories, and I took out my phone and photographed every page of the ledger I could remember, because I had learned the hard way that in Marrow Creek, paper was the only witness that never got paid to lie.

Then I sat down to wait, and for the first time in thirty years, waiting felt like the beginning of something instead of the whole of it.
