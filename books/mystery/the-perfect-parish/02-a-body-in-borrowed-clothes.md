## 2. A Body in Borrowed Clothes

The phone rang at 5:47 in the morning, and Ines Eriksen knew before she answered it.

She had learned to read the shape of bad news the way sailors read weather — not from any single sign but from the pressure of the air itself, the way a room holds its breath. Her phone lay face-down on the nightstand, glowing through its case like something buried alive, and the ringtone cut through the pre-dawn silence of her apartment with a persistence that felt personal. Nobody called at 5:47 with good news. Nobody called at 5:47 unless the world had come apart somewhere and they needed her to look at the pieces.

"Eriksen." Her voice came out gravelled, unused.

"Ms. Eriksen, this is Officer Brandt, Ashcombe PD. We need you down at St. Aldwyn's. Now, please."

"Is this about the podcast? Because if it's about the—"

"There's been a death." A pause, long enough for her to hear him breathing, or maybe breathing for her. "Ma'am — it's — I think you should just come. Detective Quist is asking for you specifically."

The line went dead, or he hung up, which amounted to the same thing. Ines sat on the edge of her bed in the blue-grey light leaking through the blinds and felt the particular cold that comes not from fear but from recognition. Three seasons of her podcast had taught her the grammar of these calls. *Asking for you specifically* was never good. *Asking for you specifically* meant her name was already tangled in it, threaded through like a stitch pulled tight.

Outside, Ashcombe was still asleep. The streets were wet from overnight rain, shining under the streetlamps like poured pewter, and her breath fogged as she crossed the parking lot to her car. She drove too fast through empty intersections, past the shuttered bakery, past the war memorial with its bronze names going green, toward the parish that had become, over three years and twenty-two episodes, less a place than an obsession with a spire.

St. Aldwyn's rose out of the mist at the end of Church Lane, its flint tower dark against a sky just beginning to bruise pink at the edges. There were police vehicles parked at angles, their lights turning slowly, painting the old stone blue and red and blue again, and there was yellow tape, and there were people — uniforms, a photographer crouched low, and a shape beneath a tarp on the broad stone steps leading up to the west door.

A shape. Not yet a person. Ines had learned that distinction too, and she hated herself a little for how fluently she spoke it.

Quist stood at the bottom of the steps with her hands in the pockets of a coat that had seen better decades, watching Ines get out of the car with an expression that gave away nothing except exhaustion. Detective Mara Quist was a woman built out of angles and patience, grey-streaked hair cropped short, eyes the flat grey of the sea before weather. They had a history, the two of them — three deaths' worth of history, each one a small war fought over jurisdiction and truth, each one ending in the same uneasy armistice: Quist got the credit, Ines got the story, and between them they got the answer.

"You said it was urgent," Ines said. "You never say urgent."

"No," Quist agreed. "I don't." She didn't move toward the tarp. She didn't move at all. "I need you to stay calm, and I need you to listen to me before you look at anything."

"Quist."

"I mean it, Eriksen. Whatever you're about to feel — hold onto it until I've said my piece, because if you run up those steps the way your face says you're going to, you'll contaminate a scene I can't afford to have contaminated."

Ines's heart was doing something strange and arrhythmic, a stuttering she hadn't felt since her mother's case file first landed in her hands eleven years ago. "Just tell me."

Quist looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind the grey eyes — pity, maybe, or its professional cousin — and then she jerked her chin toward the steps.

"The deceased is male, mid-fifties, no identification on the body. Found by the verger at four-twenty this morning when he came to unlock for early communion. No visible trauma. Doc's initial read is exposure or cardiac event, but—" Quist stopped. Started again, and Ines understood that even Quist, who interviewed killers without blinking, had rehearsed this sentence on the drive over. "But he's dressed in your clothes, Ms. Eriksen. Or close enough that I need you to tell me what's yours."

The words went into Ines like cold water into a wound. "That doesn't make sense."

"No," Quist said. "It doesn't. Come and see."

* * *

Detective Mara Quist had worked homicide long enough to know that the dead told two stories: the one their bodies told, and the one somebody else had dressed them in. Most of the time those stories matched. This morning, standing over the body on the steps of St. Aldwyn's while the sky went from bruise-pink to pale gold, she was looking at a man wearing a third story entirely — one that belonged to a living woman standing six feet behind her, breathing too carefully.

The victim was black, lean, somewhere north of fifty, his face composed in sleep or something near it. His shoes were wrong — cheap canvas slip-ons, worn through at the heel — but above them he wore a wool coat in deep forest green, tailored, expensive, with horn buttons that Quist had already photographed and bagged duplicates of. The scarf at his throat was charcoal cashmere, frayed at one end in a way that suggested years of wear rather than purchase. And around his neck, hanging against the scarf, was a lanyard. Navy blue. Printed text.

**THE COLD CASE FILES — INES ERIKSEN — PRESS/CREW ACCESS.**

It was Ines's lanyard. Quist had checked the episode credits; the same lanyard appeared in a behind-the-scenes photo from season two, slung over a chair in Eriksen's studio. It was Ines's scarf — she'd worn it in her publicity headshot, and Quist had spent enough time around the woman to recognize the frayed end without consulting a photograph. The buttons on the coat matched buttons missing from a green coat Quist had seen Eriksen wear to two separate press conferences, though whether this was *that* coat or its twin was a question for forensics and for the woman herself.

Somebody had studied Ines Eriksen. Not casually, not from across a room. Somebody had gotten close enough to take things off her body or out of her closet, close enough to know exactly which details made her *her*, and then had dressed a dead man in the assembled costume and laid him on the steps of the parish at the center of her life's work.

Quist had seen staging before. Killers staged scenes to misdirect, to buy time, to turn suspicion sideways. But this wasn't misdirection. Misdirection pointed away. This pointed *at* — at Eriksen, at her podcast, at the three deaths she'd dragged back into the light. It was a message written in clothing, and the message read: *this is what happens to people who dig here.*

Or it read something else. That was the part itching at the back of Quist's skull as she watched Eriksen approach the body with the careful, terrible composure of someone holding a door shut against a flood.

"Tell me what you see," Quist said. "Out loud. Take your time."

Eriksen crouched — the photographer had finished, the body could be approached now — and studied the dead man's face with an intensity that made Quist think, not for the first time, that there was something slightly frightening about the woman. Not the true-crime-groupie hunger she'd learned to distrust in others of the type. Something more disciplined than that. More wounded.

"I don't know him," Eriksen said quietly. Then she stopped. Quist watched the change move through her like a current through water — shoulders stiffening, breath catching, the color draining from her face in real time. "Wait. Wait, I do. I've never met him, but I know his name. Daniel Okafor. He was the sexton here — he left, must be two years ago now. He emailed me." Her voice had gone thin. "He emailed me after my second episode aired. He said he'd seen something. The night Thomas Vance died. He said he'd tried to tell people and nobody would listen, and he thought maybe I would."

Quist kept her voice level. "Did you answer him?"

The silence lasted three seconds, four, five. Long enough. Quist had interrogated enough people to measure guilt in seconds, and Eriksen's silence weighed a full tonne.

"No," Eriksen said. "I meant to. I had forty-one unread emails that week and his got buried, and then I found it again and I flagged it, and then—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "Then I got busy with season three and I told myself I'd call him when the episodes wrapped. He was a witness, Mara. He was a witness who came to *me*, and I left him sitting in my inbox for fourteen months."

Quist let that sit. There was nothing useful to say to it, and she'd learned long ago that comfort offered at crime scenes curdles into something worse than silence.

Instead she crouched beside her and lowered her voice. "Here's what I need from you, and I need it straight. Is any of this yours?" She gestured at the coat, the scarf, the lanyard. "Physically yours. Missing from your possession."

Eriksen looked at the lanyard for a long moment. "The lanyard, yes. I lost it months ago — I assumed it fell behind a shelf in the studio. The scarf..." She touched the frayed end without touching the man. "I have a scarf like this. I've had it for years. I can't swear this is mine and I can't swear it isn't. The coat — I have a coat that color. My dry cleaner is on Marlow Street. Hartwell's. They've had pieces of my wardrobe for years; half this town uses them."

"Hartwell's," Quist repeated, and wrote it down even though she wouldn't forget it. "We'll be paying them a visit."

"Mara." Eriksen's voice dropped further, and now Quist heard what she'd been waiting to hear all morning: the fear underneath the discipline, raw and unglamorous. "Whoever did this didn't just kill a man. They built him. They took the specific details of *me* and assembled them onto a stranger and put him on the steps of the parish I've spent three years pulling apart. That's not a killer covering tracks. That's a statement. That's someone telling me they know exactly who I am — down to the lanyard, down to the fraying on my scarf — and that they can make me into a corpse whenever they decide to."

"Yes," Quist said.

"And they chose him." Eriksen's jaw tightened. "Of every man in Ashcombe, they chose the one witness I failed. Do you understand what that means? They're not just threatening me. They're punishing me. They're showing me the cost of every email I didn't answer, every call I didn't make. This is a lesson with a body attached."

Quist stood, knees cracking, and looked down the length of Church Lane where the town was beginning to wake — lights coming on in kitchen windows, a paper boy's bicycle rattling past the corner, ordinary life resuming its orbit around a set of stone steps that had suddenly become the most important thirty feet in the county.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "You go home. You do not record anything, you do not post anything, you do not so much as hum a melody about this. Officially, you are not involved. Unofficially—" She hesitated. This was the part that violated about six sections of procedure, and she heard her own career whispering warnings in her ear, and she said it anyway. "Unofficially, I'll call you when the pathologist reports. Because whoever did this wanted a fourth accident, Eriksen, and they wanted it to close your case forever. And I have spent enough of my life watching cases close to know I hate the taste of it."

Eriksen looked up at her, and for once there was no argument in her face, no journalist's reflexive resistance. Just the cold.

"They studied me," she said again, softly, like a prayer said backwards. "For months, probably. Years. Every detail. And I never saw a single one of them looking."

"That," said Quist, "is what frightens me too."

* * *

Ines drove home with the windows down despite the cold, needing the air, needing anything to dilute the smell of the scene — rain-wet stone, coffee from a thermos somewhere, and beneath it all the faint sweetish note she knew from autopsies attended and would never manage to forget.

Her apartment, when she let herself into it, looked different. That was the thing nobody warned you about. She stood in her own doorway and looked at her own coat hooks, her own closet, her own shelf of archived drives, and everything looked like evidence now. Everything looked like a stage set someone else had walked through, touching things, memorizing things, choosing.

She went to the closet and counted her coats twice. The green one hung where it always hung. She checked the pockets: tissues, a receipt, a hair tie. Nothing missing that she could identify, which was somehow worse. You couldn't guard against a thief who left everything in place.

Then she sat down at her desk, opened her laptop with hands that only shook a little, and searched fourteen months of archives until she found it.

**From: d.okafor@stalwynsparish.org**
**To: ines@thecoldcasefiles.com**
**Subject: The night Mr. Thomas died**

*I don't use computers much so forgive me. I was sexton at St Aldwyns 19 years. The night Mr. Thomas died I was locking the vestry and I saw a car I knew in the lane and a person getting out of it who should not have been there. I told Father Ambrose and he told me some things are God's business and some things are the family's and I should pray on which was which. I prayed. I am still praying. But I think you are a person who writes things down and I think maybe somebody should write this down.*

*There was a girl once also. Before your time. Ask the plaques why there is no Vance among them from that year.*

He had signed it simply, *Daniel*, as if that were introduction enough — and it should have been. It should have been.

Ines sat very still in the grey morning light, the email glowing on the screen, and felt the last of her triumph from the night before drain out through the floor. Twenty-two episodes. Three deaths solved. A career built on being the person who answers, who listens, who refuses to let the forgotten stay forgotten.

And Daniel Okafor had asked her to write it down, and she had buried him in an inbox, and now he was lying under a tarp on the parish steps wearing her name around his neck.

*Ask the plaques why there is no Vance among them from that year.*

She reached for her notebook, and her pen, and began.
