## 6. The Sexton's Key

The rooming house on Corvane Street smelled of boiled cabbage and old radiators, and the landlady's grief had the particular texture of guilt — the kind that comes from having liked someone without ever once asking about his life.

"He was quiet," Mrs. Halloran said for the third time, wringing a dish towel that was already dry. "Paid on time. Helped me carry in the groceries when my hip was bad. I keep thinking — I keep thinking he knew something was going to happen to him, and he never said a word."

"Why do you think that?" Ines asked.

Mrs. Halloran hesitated at the foot of the stairs, one hand on the banister worn smooth as soap. "He gave me an envelope last week. Said if anyone came asking after him — police, reporters, anyone official — I should give it to them. Not to just anyone." Her eyes flicked up to Ines, and there it was: the recognition she'd been holding back since Ines introduced herself on the doorstep. "You're her. The podcast woman. He listened to you, you know. Every week. Had it on in the kitchen while I did the ironing. That voice of yours coming through the wall."

Ines felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs, where guilt kept its permanent residence these days.

"Did you listen?" she asked quietly.

"To yours? A bit." The dish towel twisted again. "I didn't care for it much. All that digging up of dead things." She turned and climbed the stairs. "Room's at the end. Police have been through it, but they left it mostly as they found it. They were looking for a reason he died. I don't think they were looking for why he lived."

* * *

The room was small enough that Ines could touch both walls with her outstretched arms, and she did, briefly, the way you might take the measure of a coffin. A single window looked out on an airshaft where pigeons muttered and shifted like old men at a bus stop. A bed made with military corners. A hot plate. A kettle, still sitting where he'd last set it down, its lid slightly askew, waiting for water that would never boil.

Daniel Okafor had lived here for eleven months, according to the rent book Mrs. Halloran showed her, and in eleven months he had made almost no mark on the place. No photographs on the walls. No books stacked on the sill. A Bible on the nightstand, its spine cracked at Psalms. A coat hook behind the door holding a single gray cardigan that still seemed to hold the shape of his shoulders, and Ines had to turn away from it for a moment because grief does that — it ambushes you through the smallest domestic details, a cardigan, a kettle, the ghost of a man in the bend of fabric.

She had never answered his email.

That was the sentence her mind kept returning to, turning it over like a stone in a pocket, feeling all its edges. *Dear Ms. Eriksen, I saw something the night Mr. Aldous Vance died. I have not told the police because they will not believe a sexton. But you seem to be someone who believes people like me.* Sent fourteen months ago. Read — she remembered reading it, remembered the flicker of interest, the mental note to follow up — and then buried under research for episode six, under the logistics of a live show, under the ordinary sediment of a busy life. She had meant to answer. She had *meant* to.

And now Daniel Okafor lay in the county morgue wearing her scarf, and the only conversation they would ever have was the one she'd declined to have.

"You're sorry," Mrs. Halloran said from the doorway. It wasn't a question. She'd been watching Ines's face. "I can see it. Whatever it is you're sorry for."

"Yes," Ines said. "I am."

"Well." The landlady sniffed, but it wasn't unkind. "Sorry doesn't bring anyone back. But it's more than most people manage. Get on with your looking, then. I'll be downstairs."

* * *

The police had gone through the obvious places: drawers, mattress, the space beneath the floorboards that every rooming house tenant knows about and every investigator checks first. They'd taken his phone, his papers, his passport. What they'd left was everything that didn't look like evidence — which, in a room this bare, was nearly everything, and therefore nothing, and therefore exactly where a careful man would hide what mattered.

Ines sat down on the edge of his bed and let herself see the room the way he must have seen it. Not as a sexton's lodging. As a listening post. He had spent thirty years working at St. Aldwyn's — she'd confirmed that much with the diocesan records office that morning — sweeping its floors, digging its graves, winding its clocks. He had heard things. Seen things. And then, late in life, he had heard a podcast by a woman who dug into exactly the kind of things he'd spent decades pretending not to notice, and something in him had decided to reach out.

And when she didn't answer, he hadn't stopped. He'd started gathering.

She began with the Bible. People hide things in Bibles the way they hide things under mattresses — it's the first place everyone looks, which makes it either the worst hiding place or the best, depending on whether you trust the seeker's patience. She fanned the pages. Nothing fell. But the cracked spine opened naturally to Psalm 94, and a pencil line, faint as a breath, underscored a single verse:

*For the Lord will not forsake his people; he will not abandon his heritage.*

His heritage. Or theirs. The Vances' — three hundred years of it, celebrated next month with candles and canapés while the man who'd swept their church floors lay tagged and refrigerated.

Ines closed the Bible and pressed her palm flat against its cover for a moment, the way you might press a hand to a shoulder. Then she got to work.

* * *

It took her two hours.

She went through the room the way she'd learned to go through archives — not searching for objects but for absences, for the negative space a removed thing leaves behind. A rectangle of less-faded wallpaper behind the chest of drawers: something had hung there, framed, until recently. A drawer that stuck on its runners, its bottom lined with newspaper dated four months ago, though the items above it were older — meaning something had been added, or taken, and the arrangement disturbed. A coat pocket containing a ticket stub from the county records office, dated the same week as his email to her. He'd been pulling documents even then.

Under the loose bottom of the wardrobe — not hidden, exactly, just *placed*, the way a man hides a thing when he wants to be able to find it himself in the dark — she found a shoebox.

Inside the shoebox: a key, and half a photograph.

The key was iron, old, heavy enough to sit in her palm like a small animal. It was the kind of key that belonged to a door built before electricity, before plumbing, before anyone imagined the parish would need locks at all. Tied to its bow with fishing line was a paper tag, yellowed and soft with handling, and on the tag, in Okafor's small, precise handwriting:

**Vestry undercroft. 14 March.**

Fourteen March. The date hit her like cold water. Fourteen March — the night of the first death. The night Aldous Vance, the family patriarch's brother, tumbled down the north stair of the bell tower in what the coroner called a tragic misstep, what the parish called God's will, and what Ines's first season had called, in a title that made Quist grind her teeth, *A Very Convenient Fall.*

Okafor had been the sexton on duty that night. Okafor had seen something. And whatever door this key opened, he had believed it held the proof.

The photograph was torn — not damaged, *torn*, deliberately, a clean vertical rip removing nearly half the image. What remained showed a young woman standing on the church steps, squinting against sun, caught mid-laugh by whoever held the camera. Dark hair cut short in a style that belonged to another decade entirely. A cotton dress printed with small blue flowers. Her arm was flung around someone outside the frame — the missing half — and the intimacy of the gesture said *sister*, said *best friend*, said *the person I love most in this picture has been cut away*.

On the back, in pencil, in a different hand than Okafor's — rounder, quicker, a girl's hand: *M.V., Easter '69.*

Ines sat very still on the dead man's bed with the photograph in her hands and felt the shape of the thing begin to assemble itself, the way a dark room assembles itself when your eyes finally adjust.

She knew the Vance family album. She had studied it — really studied it, frame by frame, generation by generation, for the podcast. Three hundred years of Vances documented with the obsessive thoroughness of people who understood that a family's power lived in its archive. Christenings. Weddings. Confirmations. Hunting parties. The founder's statue. Eleanor's wedding, Julian's christening, Rosalind's graduation, each event preserved, captioned, *curated*.

There was no M.V. from Easter 1969. There was no dark-haired girl laughing on the church steps. There was no gap in the sequence where she should have been — and that was the thing that raised the hair on Ines's arms. Families lose people all the time; albums record the losses, the funeral cards tucked between pages, the black-edged portraits. The Vance albums recorded nothing. Not a death. Not a departure. Not even an absence. This girl hadn't been mourned out of the family's memory.

She had been *edited* out. Preemptively. Thoroughly. The way you'd remove a name from a ledger so no one would ever think to check the account.

"M.V.," Ines whispered to the empty room, and the pigeons in the airshaft answered with their dry, indifferent murmuring.

Margaret Vance? The name surfaced from her research notes — a daughter listed in a 1950s parish register, born to the patriarch's generation, and then... nothing. She'd noted it months ago as a curiosity, a thread too thin to pull with a season deadline looming. A girl who appeared in a baptismal record and vanished from every document afterward. She'd assumed emigration. Marriage. The ordinary dissolutions of women's names across the twentieth century.

But ordinary dissolutions didn't come with a sexton hiding keys and photographs in a rooming house in Corvane Street. Ordinary dissolutions didn't get men poisoned and dressed in a podcaster's clothes.

* * *

Outside, the light had gone the color of tarnished brass, and the radiator in the corner knocked and sighed like something trying to speak. Ines stood at Okafor's window, looking down into the airshaft, and let the grief finish its work on her. Because that's what grief was for, she'd learned long ago, when her mother disappeared and left nothing behind but a coat in a station locker and a hole shaped like a question: grief was the raw material. Resolve was what you built from it.

Her mother had been erased too. Not by a family — by circumstance, by indifference, by a police force that closed the file after eleven weeks and a town that stopped asking after a year. Ines had built her whole life, her whole strange career, on the refusal to let erasure stand. *Memory kept is memory defended.* She'd said it in episode one, and she'd meant it.

Daniel Okafor had believed the same thing. He'd believed it alone, in this narrow room, with a kettle going cold and a psalm underlined in pencil, and when the institution failed him — when the podcast woman didn't write back — he'd kept faith with the truth anyway. He'd gathered his evidence. He'd tagged his key. He'd waited for someone to come asking.

Someone had come asking. Just not her.

Ines photographed the tag, the key, the photograph, front and back, the pencil inscription, the tear line. She photographed the underlined psalm. She photographed the rectangle of unfaded wallpaper and the sticking drawer and the ticket stub, building a record of the room itself, because rooms testified too, if you knew how to listen.

Then she wrapped the key and the photograph in the gray cardigan from the hook — it seemed right, somehow, that they should travel together, the man's warmth around the woman's face — and put them in her bag.

At the door she paused. The kettle sat on the hot plate, lid askew, patient as a gravestone.

"I'm sorry," she said aloud, to the room, to the man who had lived in it, to the version of herself fourteen months younger who had read an email and filed it away. "I'm here now. I'll finish it."

The radiator knocked twice, and she chose to hear it as an answer.

* * *

Downstairs, Mrs. Halloran was waiting with the envelope.

"I forgot, with all the upset," she said, pressing it into Ines's hands. "He said give it to whoever came asking who wasn't police. You're not police, are you?"

"No," Ines said. "I'm worse."

The envelope was unsealed. Inside was a single sheet, folded once, in Okafor's precise hand. Not a letter — a list. Dates. Seven of them, spread across fifty years, each annotated in a few clipped words. *Bell tower. Boathouse. Car on Mill Road. The doctor's surgery.* Each date, Ines realized with a chill that started at her scalp and worked downward, corresponded to a death the parish had absorbed and forgotten — some famous, some obscure, two she'd never heard of at all. And beside the final date, the one that hadn't happened yet, beside the anniversary dinner circled on every calendar in town, Okafor had written only three words:

*They'll need one more.*

Ines stood in Mrs. Halloran's cabbage-scented hallway, the cardigan-wrapped key heavy against her hip, and understood that Daniel Okafor had known exactly how he would die — and had mailed his testimony forward anyway, past his own murder, into the hands of the one person stubborn enough to read it.

The erased girl in the photograph smiled up at her from inside the bag, mid-laugh, forever 1969.

"Hold on," Ines told her softly. "I'm coming to find out who tore you out."
