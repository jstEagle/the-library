## 11. The Ledger Room

The key was heavier than it looked.

I'd carried it for three days in the inner pocket of my coat, wrapped in a handkerchief like a relic, and every time my fingers found its shape through the wool I felt the same small electric jolt — the sense of holding something that had been waiting for me. An old iron key, black with age, tagged with a paper label gone amber at the edges. On the label, in a careful clerk's hand: *Vestry — Lower. 14 March.*

The fourteenth of March. The night Thomas Vance's car went off Harrow Bend into the flooded quarry, the first of the three accidents I'd spent a season unmaking. Daniel Okafor had kept a key to the room beneath the vestry, and he had dated it to the night the killing started. He hadn't known what he was holding. Or maybe he had known exactly, and that was why he was dead.

The church at midnight was a different animal than the church by day. By day St. Aldwyn's was all honeyed stone and candle-glow, a building that performed its own history for tourists and wedding parties. At midnight it breathed. The radiators ticked and knocked in the walls like something pacing. Rain had come in off the moor around ten, and now it worked at the clerestory windows with soft persistent fingers, and the stained glass — Aldwyn himself, mitred and serene, holding a model of this very church like a man presenting his alibi — shifted through colors that had no business existing in the dark: a bruised green, a drowned violet.

I let myself in through the sacristy door. Father Ambrose had given me the code weeks ago, back when he still thought I was writing a fond history of the parish tercentenary, and either his memory had failed him or his conscience had, because the code still worked. That should have made me feel welcome. Instead it felt like walking into a trap someone had forgotten to spring shut.

*You've broken into churches before,* I told myself, which was true, though never one where I was reasonably sure a murderer still said Mass.

* * *

The vestry smelled of cedar and old incense and, underneath both, the mineral smell of stone that has been cold for three hundred years. I left my torch off until the door was closed behind me, then clicked it on low, cupping my hand around the beam out of some instinct I couldn't name — as if light itself could be witnessed against me.

The room was narrow, hung with vestments in glass-fronted cabinets, gold thread dulled to the color of old teeth. Chasubles for every liturgical season. A white one for weddings and funerals, which in a parish like this were often the same families wearing different expressions. Along the east wall ran a bank of memorial plaques I'd photographed a dozen times already, their brass donation figures gleaming dully under my beam — the numbers that repeated, that cycled, that meant something I hadn't yet cracked. I touched the nearest one. Cold as a headstone. They were all, in a sense, headstones.

And behind them — behind the third plaque from the end, the one commemorating *A Faithful Benefactor, 1968* — a seam in the paneling so fine I'd have missed it if Okafor's key hadn't told me to look.

I pressed. Nothing. I pressed again, lower, and felt a click deep in the wood, a sound like a knuckle cracking, and a section of paneling swung inward on hinges someone had oiled recently enough that they didn't even whisper.

Cold air came out of the dark beyond, air with a taste to it — chalk and iron and time. Steps descended ahead of me, stone worn into shallow bowls by centuries of feet. I stood at the top for a long moment, listening to the rain, to the radiators ticking their slow animal walk through the walls above, to my own pulse doing its idiot drumroll in my ears.

Then I went down.

* * *

The ledger room was smaller than I'd imagined and larger than it had any right to be — a vaulted chamber perhaps twenty feet square, dug into the bedrock the church stood on, older than the church itself if the masonry was any guide. The stones were cyclopean, unmortared in places, sweating moisture that caught my torchlight like a scatter of coins. Someone had installed electric lighting once; a bulb hung dead from a flex in the center of the ceiling, and a switch by the stairs did nothing when I tried it. Of course not. Whatever happened down here wasn't meant to happen with the lights on.

Shelves lined three walls, floor to vault, and on the shelves sat the parish's memory in leather and board: ledgers. Hundreds of them. Baptisms, marriages, burials going back to the founding — those would be the public registers, duplicated upstairs. But these were different. These were bound in uniform black calf, stamped in blind tooling with no title at all, and arranged in date order with a precision that spoke of institutional care, the kind of care people only take with things they're ashamed of.

I pulled the earliest volume and opened it on the trestle table that stood under the dead bulb, and there it was, plain as a confession: the accounts of the Vance Trust, kept in a copperplate hand, every disbursement entered, initialed, and ruled off. Payments to solicitors. Payments to masons. Payments, year after patient year, to *Harrow Quarry Co.* — maintenance of an access road nobody used. Payments to a Dr. Fenwick, recurring across decades, the amounts rising gently like a tide, annotated only ever with a single letter: *M.*

Margaret.

My breath fogged in the cold. I turned pages faster, the paper soft and foxed as moth wing, and the pattern assembled itself the way patterns do — slowly, then all at once. Every death I'd traced was here, not named but legible to anyone who knew what to look for. A payment to the coroner's clerk the week after Thomas Vance went into the quarry. A *discretionary gift* to the widow of the groundskeeper who fell from the bell tower two years later. A round sum to an insurance investigator who had, per the local papers, retired suddenly to Spain. Each entry initialed. Each initial the same looping, confident *E.V.*

Eleanor. Signing off on the machinery of her family's survival the way other women signed Christmas cards.

But it was the marginalia that stopped my heart.

In the volumes covering the late sixties and early seventies, a second hand appeared in the margins — pencil, faint, hurried, growing fainter and more hurried as the years went on. Small, slanted letters, pressed hard enough in places to emboss the page. Not the accountant's copperplate. This was a younger hand, an angrier one, and it tracked the money like a prisoner marking days on a wall.

*£40 sent to Miss M. V., per agreement. Received £12.*

*Quarterly allowance due Lady Day. Nothing come. Asked again.*

*Dr. F. paid £200. For what? For WHAT?*

And then, in the volume for 1971, in letters gouged so deep the pencil had torn the paper:

*I am not dead. Whoever reads this — I am NOT DEAD.*

I sat down on the trestle bench because my legs had decided the matter without consulting me. The torch beam trembled on the page. Somewhere overhead, water moved through old pipes, a long throaty gurgle, and I flinched hard enough to knock my elbow against the shelf.

She had been down here. That was the thing that undid me — not the forgery, not the payoffs, I'd been braced for those — but the simple physical fact that Margaret Vance had stood in this room, in this cold, and written her pleas into the margins of the book that documented her erasure. She'd had access once, before they re-keyed the door, before they sealed it behind a memorial plaque with a pious inscription. A young woman sneaking down beneath the vestry to keep her own accounts, because the family's accounts were lies, because the will was forged, because she had been declared dead in a country she'd never seen while she was alive and pregnant and asking, in pencil, in the dark, *for what?*

They hadn't killed her. Killing her would have required admitting she existed. Instead they'd done something worse and cheaper: they'd made her a rounding error. And she had fought it the only way left to her — in graphite, in margins, in a room no one was supposed to enter.

*I am not dead.*

"No," I whispered to the page. "You're not."

* * *

I photographed everything. Torch wedged in the shelf gap for a steady light, phone camera silent, flash off — page after page, volume after volume, the marginalia especially, each penciled grievance captured twice in case the first image blurred. My hands had steadied. That was the strange mercy of work: fear could be set aside like a coat while the hands did what the hands knew how to do. I'd felt it before, in the archive in Cork, in the coroner's office in Harrow Bend, that clean cold focus that arrived precisely when things got dangerous, as if some part of me had been built for exactly this and had simply been waiting for the rest of me to catch up.

I was photographing the 1974 volume — Margaret's hand nearly gone now, the entries between her notes stretching from months to years, a voice being lowered by degrees into silence — when I heard the footsteps.

Above me. Through the floor.

Not the radiators. Not the pipes. Footsteps — unhurried, deliberate, crossing the vestry from the direction of the sacristy door. A floorboard complained, then another. Someone who knew the building well enough not to rush and well enough to know which boards sang.

I killed the torch.

Darkness came down like a lid. In the sudden black the cold got colder, or I noticed it more, and my own breathing sounded enormous, a bellows in a tomb. I stood absolutely still, phone pressed flat against my stomach, screen down, and did the arithmetic I should have done on the stairs: Ambrose's code still working. The panel hinge freshly oiled. A key tagged and saved for fifty years by a sexton who died asking questions.

Someone maintained this room. Someone came down here.

The footsteps crossed the vestry and stopped. Directly overhead — or directly over the plaque. I heard a faint metallic sound, brief, familiar, and my stomach dropped through the floor: a key, being fitted to a lock. No — to the panel. The same click I'd felt, transmitted down through the wood and stone, and then the seam of pale light widening at the top of the stairs as the hidden door swung open.

A silhouette filled it. Backlit by the vestry's dim emergency lamp, I could make out only shape: tall, still, head tilted as if listening. Listening for me. The figure stood there for one second, five, ten — long enough for my heartbeat to become the loudest thing in the county — and then it began to descend.

One step. Two. The stone gave each footfall a hollow voice, and the stairwell acted as a funnel, carrying the sound straight down to me, and I understood with terrible clarity that whoever this was could hear everything too — including, if I moved, if I breathed wrong, if my phone buzzed in my hand, the fact that the room was occupied.

There was nowhere to go. One stairway, one door, and a body between me and both. The shelves offered shadows, not exits. I eased backward along the wall, one silent inch at a time, until my shoulder blades found the gap between two shelving units, a recess barely deeper than my spine, and I folded myself into it and became a piece of the architecture.

Three steps from the bottom, the footsteps stopped again.

The silence stretched. Rain on the church above, faint as memory. My lungs burning with held air. And in the dark, close enough now that I could hear fabric settle, the person at the foot of the stairs exhaled — a long, slow breath, almost a sigh — and spoke into the darkness, quietly, conversationally, in a voice I knew.

"I can hear you breathing, Ms. Eriksen."
