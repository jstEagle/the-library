## 21. Margaret's Testimony

The interview room at the county courthouse had once been a jury deliberation chamber, and it still carried the smell of old varnish and older decisions. Morning light came through the high windows in pale, dusty columns, falling across the table like something laid out for inspection. Detective Mara Quist had chosen the room deliberately — no recording equipment bolted to the walls, no two-way glass, nothing that would make an eighty-one-year-old woman feel like evidence. Just a tape recorder the size of a cigarette pack, a pitcher of water, two glasses, and a box of tissues she'd placed within reach without comment.

Margaret Vance sat with her hands folded on the table, and Ines, watching from the chair against the wall where Quist had permitted her to sit, thought: *she folds her hands like someone who has spent a lifetime being told not to touch things.*

She was smaller than her photograph. The girl in the torn picture Okafor had kept all those years had been all dark hair and defiance, chin lifted as if the camera owed her money. The woman at the table was bird-boned, translucent at the temples, dressed in a cardigan the color of weak tea that Rosalind must have bought her. But the eyes were the same — gray-green, unblinking, with a kind of patient ferocity banked behind them like coals that had waited fifty years for air.

"Whenever you're ready," Quist said gently. "Take your time. We have all day."

Margaret looked at the recorder. Then at Ines. Something passed across her face — not recognition exactly; they had never met. Recognition of *purpose*, maybe. Of another woman who had come looking.

"You're the one who reads plaques," Margaret said. Her voice was dry, unhurried, the voice of someone who had done all her talking to herself for decades and saw no reason to rush now.

"I read everything," Ines said. "It's a failing."

"It's a discipline." A ghost of a smile. "My mother taught me cipher games at that dinner table. She thought I didn't know what the numbers meant. Nobody in that house ever thinks you know."

Quist pressed record. Stated the date, the time, the names present. And then she said, "Mrs. Vance — or should I say Miss? You were declared dead in 1971."

"I was declared *inconvenient*," Margaret said. "The death certificate was just tidier."

* * *

She began, as Ines had known she would, with the will.

"My grandfather Aldwyn Vance drew his final will in March of 1968. I sat in the study while Mr. Pell — the solicitor, a soft man with ink-stained cuffs — read the provisions aloud. One-third each to my father, my aunt Camille, and to be held in trust for me until my twenty-fifth birthday. My share was not small. It was the Harrowgate properties and the river leases. It was, my father liked to joke when he'd had enough sherry, *the part of the family that actually works*."

Her hands stayed folded. Only her voice moved.

"He died that October. And by Christmas, the will everyone remembered was a different will. Trust-managed entirely, disbursements at the discretion of the surviving executors — which is to say, at the discretion of whoever held the pen. I found the original draft in the ledger room when I was twenty-two. I used to go down there because it was the only room in that house where nobody watched me. I found my grandfather's hand on every page except the signature page, and the signature page was newer paper. Do you know how a forged document smells? Like ambition."

Ines felt the words land somewhere beneath her ribs. Fifty years, and the woman still knew the smell.

"I confronted my father. That was my error. Not the confrontation — the *location* of it. I did it at home, over supper, where the housekeeper could hear, where the walls had ears the way parish walls always do." Margaret paused, poured water with a steadiness that seemed rehearsed by deprivation, drank. "Two weeks later, Dr. Fenwick pronounced me pregnant and unstable. Both true, only one relevant. There was a young man — a seminarian, actually, though that detail never made it into any family discussion. He died in a car accident outside Ashford before our child was born. I have always understood that the accident and my father's solicitor shared a calendar."

The room was very quiet. Even the light seemed to lean in.

"They told me I was going to a clinic in Switzerland. Rest, they called it. I was put in a car at four in the morning by two men whose names I never learned, and I signed papers I was told were admission forms. They may have been. Or they may have been the declaration of my death — I've never been permitted to see them. What I know is this: my daughter was born in a facility outside Bern in June of 1972, and she was taken from me when she was nine days old. I was told she'd gone to a good family. I asked which family. They increased my medication."

Quist's pen had stopped moving. "Your daughter," she said carefully. "Do you know—"

"I know she was registered under a name I wasn't given. I know that every year on the fourteenth of June, someone paid for a candle at St. Aldwyn's — I learned this much later, from a kind nurse who smuggled me a parish bulletin the way other women smuggle gin. Someone paid for a candle for a child who was never dead either." Margaret's eyes came up, and for the first time there was something wet and furious in them. "I did the arithmetic, Detective. I'm old, not stupid. Somebody in that family has known where I was for fifty years. Somebody has been paying to keep me *comfortable* and *quiet*, and comfort and quiet are the same word in that house."

* * *

From her chair by the wall, Ines watched Quist work, and understood why the detective had let her stay. This was not an interrogation. It was an exhumation, and Quist knew you did not rush an exhumation; you let the ground give up its dead at its own pace.

"The threats," Quist prompted, hours later, after water and sandwiches and a walk to the window. "You mentioned your father's solicitor. After 1972."

"After everything." Margaret's mouth thinned. "Every few years, a letter. No return address. Always the same sentence in different clothes: *Be content where you are.* Once, after I wrote to a cousin, they moved me. Three hundred miles overnight, no explanation, new name on the door. I learned not to write to cousins." She turned her hands over, palms up, as if showing they were empty. "Do you know what captivity looks like when it's funded properly? It looks like kindness. Fresh flowers. A piano tuner twice a year for a piano no one plays. Doctors who listen so attentively and hear nothing. For thirty years I was a pension line item in a parish ledger, Detective. Entry forty-four. I read my own maintenance costs in a dead man's margins, written by my own hand when I was young enough to think writing things down would save me."

"It did save you," Ines said quietly.

Margaret looked at her then, fully, and Ines felt the weight of half a century settle onto her shoulders like a coat.

"Yes," Margaret said. "That is the cruelest thing about it. It saved me and it buried me in the same stroke, and I have never decided which I ought to be grateful for." She straightened slightly, and something in her spine reorganized itself — the posture of a woman who had once sat at the Vance dinner table expecting to inherit a third of an empire. "Now. You'll want the names. Eleanor signed the first transfer order in 1974 — I recognized the handwriting on the carbon copy they foolishly left in my file for six weeks. Julian knows nothing; he was a child, and later he was a coward, which is its own kind of childhood. And Rosalind—" Here Margaret stopped. Her voice did something complicated. "Rosalind visited me. Twice a year, for years, under a false name. She'd sit and hold my hand and tell me about gardens. I thought she was hired company. It took me eleven visits to realize she had my mother's way of folding a napkin." A long breath. "She was keeping me alive and keeping herself safe in the same motion. I know that arithmetic too. I invented it."

* * *

The statement ran past noon, past two o'clock, past the point where Quist had to send out for more tapes. Ines sat through all of it, taking no notes, wanting nothing between herself and the words. When it was done — when Margaret had initialed each page with a fountain pen Rosalind had brought her, the nib scratching like a small animal — Quist gathered the pages, and the machinery of the state began, at last, to grind.

It ground quickly, once it started. That was the strange part. Ines had spent a year documenting how fast the machinery could be *stopped* — inquests closed, files sealed, witnesses scattered. She had never seen it run at full speed. Within seventy-two hours of Margaret's statement, a High Court judge froze the Vance estate in its entirety. The trust — three hundred years of accumulated, laundered, inherited weight — entered dissolution proceedings. Forensic accountants arrived in Ashcombe like weather, occupying the vestry annex with portable scanners and boxes of archival gloves.

And on a bright, cold Thursday morning, Ines stood on the churchyard path and watched the plaques come down.

They worked in pairs, conservators in white cotton gloves, prying each brass rectangle from the nave walls with heat guns and thin blades, photographing everything, bagging everything. Father Ambrose stood in the doorway of the sacristy with his hands clasped behind his back, watching his church get taken apart rivet by rivet, and his face was the face of a man attending his own funeral while still ambulatory. Every few minutes his lips moved — Ines couldn't tell if he was praying or reciting donation amounts from memory.

"There she is," Quist said, coming up beside her on the path, coffee in each hand. She handed one over. "Entry forty-four."

On a trestle table by the south porch, the removed plaques lay in rows, brass gleaming dully under polythene. Ines walked down the line of them. *In memoriam. In memoriam. In loving memory.* Generations of Vances, their gifts, their years, their pieties — and threaded through them, invisible for fifty years unless you knew the hymn numbers, the cipher, the ledger of a murdered inheritance hiding in plain sight among the mourned.

She stopped at the oldest plaque, the founder's, Aldwyn Vance himself, 1723–1798, *whose bounty built this church*. Someone had already tagged it with an evidence label. Ines looked at the brass face that had watched ten generations bury their secrets beneath its gaze.

"You know what gets me," she said. "It all worked. Right up until it didn't. Fifty years. Two inquests. Four deaths. A whole family raised inside the lie like pearls around grit. And it all came apart because a sexton kept a photograph and a woman kept writing in margins."

"Because people keep things," Quist said. "That's the whole job, Eriksen. Everybody thinks it's about catching liars. It's about finding the keepers." She nodded toward the church doors, where Margaret Vance stood on the step in a wool coat, blinking in the daylight, Rosalind's hand light under her elbow. "She asked if you'd walk with her. Through the churchyard. She wants to see where they put the memorial stone."

"What memorial stone?"

"The one with her name on it. 1971. They gave her a plaque too, apparently. Small one. North wall." Quist's mouth twisted. "Cheapest brass in the building."

* * *

The north wall memorial was the size of a hymnal. *MARGARET ALDWYN VANCE, 1949–1971. Requiescat in pace.* Below it, in smaller letters: *Given by her devoted family.*

Margaret stood before it for a long time. The wind moved the yew branches overhead, and the light came down through them in shifting coins.

"Fifty-three years," she said at last. "I've been dead longer than I was alive." She reached up and touched the letters of her own name, her gloved fingers tracing the M, the V. "It's not even good brass. Feel it. Thin. They economized on my death the way they economized on everything."

Ines said nothing. There was nothing to say that the woman didn't already know more deeply than anyone living.

"They'll take this one down too, I suppose. Bag it. Label it." Margaret almost smiled. "Good. Let them weigh it. Let some technician write *evidence of forgery, adjacent* on the tag. It's the only honest work this piece of metal has ever done." She turned from the wall, slowly, and looked out over the churchyard — the tilted stones, the Vance plot with its granite monoliths, the parish beyond with its tidy roofs and its terrible memory. "You know what I want, Ms. Eriksen? Not the money. The trust can dissolve into seawater for all I care. I want my name back in the register. Births, marriages, deaths — I want to be *in* the book again, in the right column, with the right dates. I was erased from the ledger. Put me back in it."

"We're restoring your name," Ines said. "Episode by episode. The whole season. Everything the archive holds."

Margaret studied her for a moment. "A podcast," she said, tasting the word like an unfamiliar sweet. "Fifty years ago that would have been a scandal sheet and a man with a camera outside the gates." Another pause. "Do it anyway. Tell it loudly. My father hated loud."

Then her gaze drifted, and caught, and held — on the far corner of the churchyard, where a small newer stone stood apart from the Vance plot, modest granite, fresh flowers despite the season. Ines followed her look and felt her breath stop.

Margaret was already walking. Slowly, one hand on Rosalind's arm, past the leaning slates of the old dead, to the corner where the ground was newer. She stopped before the stone and read it twice, her lips moving.

*DANIEL OKAFOR, sexton of this parish, who kept what was entrusted to him.*

Below it, the dates. Below that: *He saw.*

Nobody had claimed the body. So the parish had buried him — Ambrose's doing, Ines guessed, the one courageous act of a coward's autumn — and someone had chosen the words. She looked at Rosalind. Rosalind looked at the ground.

"He kept what was entrusted to him," Margaret said softly. "Yes. That's right. That's exactly right." She bent, with effort, and laid her gloved hand flat on the cold granite, the way you'd steady yourself against a door. "Whoever wrote this knew him better than the inquest did."

"He emailed me," Ines heard herself say. The words came out low, rough. "Eight months ago. Said he'd seen something the night of the first death. I had three solved cases and a season finale to cut and I thought — I thought he was another crank. I never answered." Her throat closed. "He died wearing my clothes, Mrs. Vance. He died because he kept reaching for the same thread I was pulling, and someone decided to close both accounts at once."

Margaret straightened. She looked at Ines for a long moment, and then she did something Ines did not expect: she took Ines's hand in both of hers, her grip surprisingly strong, her skin papery and warm.

"Child," she said, "I spent fifty years learning the difference between guilt and grief. Guilt says *you should have answered*. Grief says *he deserved an answer*. Only one of those is yours to carry, and it isn't the first one." She released her hand. "Answer the next one. That's the whole liturgy. Answer the next one."

* * *

Evening came down over Ashcombe like a lid settling. Ines stood at her kitchen window with tea going cold in her hand, watching the last light leave the parish tower, and let the day move through her the way tide moves through a channel — the frozen estate, the dissolving trust, the plaques in their evidence bags, Margaret's hand on Daniel Okafor's stone, *answer the next one*.

Restitution, she thought. Everyone said the word like it was a gift arriving. But it came the way all the Vance money had always come — late, weighted, and at somebody's expense. Margaret got her name back at seventy-four instead of twenty-five. Julian got his suspicions confirmed instead of his father's love. Rosalind got survival instead of absolution. And Daniel Okafor got a stone with three lines on it and a stranger's podcast promising him the fourth.

Her phone buzzed on the counter. She almost didn't look. Then she did.

An email. New address, no signature block, subject line blank. The message was four words long:

**"There were two daughters."**

Ines set down the cup. Outside, the tower went dark against the last bruised band of sky, and she stood very still in her kitchen, reading the sentence again, and again, feeling the ground shift beneath a case she had thought — tonight of all nights — was finally, finally closed.

Somewhere out there was a grave with the wrong name on it. Or a birth certificate no one had ever audited. Or a ninth-day-old baby registered under a name Margaret Vance had never been given.

She opened a reply window. Her cursor blinked in the empty field, patient as a pulse.

*Answer the next one,* Margaret had said.

Ines began to type.
