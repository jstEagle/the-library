## 1. The Retainer

The woman who walked into my office on the last Tuesday of October was rehearsing something.

I noticed it the way I notice everything — without wanting to, without being able to stop. Her left hand kept curling and uncurling in her lap as she crossed the room, fingers folding over the clasp of her handbag like a metronome set to a tempo only she could hear. She'd chosen the chair across from my desk before I offered it, which meant she'd been in this building before, or a building like it, or had studied photographs of both. Her coat was cashmere the color of wet slate, expensive but not new; the cuffs had been turned once and re-stitched by hand. A widow's economy, I thought later. At the time I thought nothing at all except: *she is practicing.*

"Mr. Crane?" Her voice matched the coat. Controlled, gray, with one thread of color running through it that she hadn't quite managed to dye out.

"That's me." I didn't stand. People expect investigators to stand, to shake hands, to perform the rituals of trust. I've found it better to let them do all the work. "You found me through—"

"The obituary of Daniel Voss. Two years ago. The coroner called it a fall from his own roof. You called it something else, and you were right." She sat down and arranged herself with the precision of someone arranging flowers for a funeral. "I read every word of the coverage."

Daniel Voss. The name landed in my chest like a coin dropped down a well — I heard it falling for a long time before it hit bottom. Voss had been my third. Three deaths in nine years that the city had filed under *accident* and that I had refiled under *murder*, each time using nothing but what was between my ears. I don't carry a notebook. I've never needed one.

"You're well informed, Mrs.—"

"Ashford. Vivian Ashford."

The name meant something, though it took me a moment to find the drawer it lived in. Ashford. Ashford Fine Art, on Meridian Street, the gallery with the brass door handles polished by forty years of gloved hands. Edmund Ashford. I'd seen his photograph in the society pages the way you see weather maps — regularly, without interest. A silver-haired man with a collector's smile, standing beside paintings he owned and women who wore jewelry worth more than the paintings.

"Mrs. Ashford," I said. "What can I do for you?"

She opened her handbag. Her hands were steady now — steadier than they'd been crossing the room, which told me the trembling had been for the walk, not the work. From the bag she drew an envelope, cream-colored, heavy stock, and laid it on my desk with both hands, the way people lay down cards they're afraid of losing.

"I want to hire you," she said, "to prove that my husband's death will be natural."

I looked at the envelope. Then at her. Then back at the envelope, because looking at her required a different kind of effort, and I wasn't ready to spend it yet.

"Will be," I repeated.

"Yes."

"Not *was*. Not *may have been*." I tapped the envelope once with my index finger. "*Will be.*"

"That's correct."

There are sentences that arrive in an office like mine wearing disguises. Clients say things that sound strange and turn out to mean something ordinary — a wife who wants proof her husband's death *would have been* natural so she can fight the insurance company, a son who suspects the hospice nurse, a business partner who smells arsenic in the estate's future. I've learned to translate. But this sentence arrived naked. It made no attempt to disguise itself, and that was the first wrong thing about it. Wrongness has a texture. Most people walk past it their whole lives. For me it registers the way a misfiled document registers in a clean archive — a single card out of order in ten thousand, and suddenly I cannot see anything else in the room.

"Mrs. Ashford," I said carefully, "your husband is alive."

"He is."

"And healthy?"

"He had a physical in September. Excellent results. His doctor used the word 'boring.'" Something moved behind her eyes when she said it — not amusement. Closer to grief, which was impossible, because there was nothing yet to grieve. Unless grief, for some people, is less an event than a skill. Something you keep sharp.

"Then I'm afraid I don't understand the engagement. If you want life insurance investigated after a death, you come to me after the death."

"No." She leaned forward, and the light from my one window caught the left side of her face, and I saw — involuntarily, completely — the fine topography of her: the powder settled into a crease beside her mouth, the pulse ticking in her throat at maybe ninety beats a minute, twice what her voice advertised. A small scar above her right eyebrow, old, stitched by someone careful. The faint smell of lilies, the funeral flower, worn as perfume or absorbed from a florist's shop within the last day. "You misunderstand me, Mr. Crane, and I understand why. So I'll say it plainly, and then I'll never say it again. My husband is going to die six days from now. On the fourth of November. And when he dies, everyone — the police, the coroner, the insurance company, our friends — will call it natural causes, because that is what it will look like. What I am paying you for is to make certain that it isn't merely what it *looks* like. I want the truth established in advance. Before there's a body. Before anyone has a reason to lie about it."

The radiator in the corner ticked. Somewhere below, the streetcar sang its wire-song up Meridian. I sat very still, because stillness is how I think — motion scatters detail, and detail is the only currency I trade in.

Six days. The fourth of November.

And here is the thing about my mind, the thing that has made me useful to lawyers and unbearable to dinner companions: I do not remember the way other people remember. Other people keep a shoebox of impressions. I keep the archive entire. When Vivian Ashford said *the fourth of November*, my mind did not hear a date; it retrieved one. It went into the file marked *Ashford* — thin, mostly society-page clippings, a gallery anniversary announcement from three years ago — and it laid the date against everything else in the folder, the way a bank teller lays a bill against the light.

The fourth of November was the anniversary of the gallery's founding. Forty-one years, this year. There would be an exhibition. There would be two hundred guests and champagne and Edmund Ashford standing under the lights saying goodbye to each of them like a man settling accounts.

That was the second wrong thing. A man doesn't schedule his death for his own party unless the party is the alibi.

"Who told you he's going to die?" I asked.

"No one told me." Her hands were folded now, perfectly still, the metronome stopped. "That's rather the point. No one will ever tell you anything either, Mr. Crane. That's what makes you valuable. Things simply happen around you, and afterward you see them."

"Things haven't happened yet. That's the difficulty."

"No," she agreed softly. "That's the difficulty."

I opened the envelope. Inside was a cashier's check for twenty thousand dollars — five times my usual retainer, drawn on a bank whose name I recognized from the trust departments of old money — and beneath it, a single sheet of paper, typed, no letterhead. My eyes took it in the way they take in everything: wholly, permanently, in perhaps four seconds. I could recite it back to you now, years later, punctuation included. I could tell you the typewriter was a Royal portable with a chipped lowercase *e* and a ribbon due for changing, that the paper had been stored somewhere with cedar, that the typist had hesitated before one word long enough to leave a faint double-strike ghost.

The letter said:

*Mister Crane — Retainer for services rendered the week of November 4th. Scope: establish cause of death of Edmund Ashford as natural. Payment doubled upon conclusion. Do not contact the client's husband. Do not contact the client's attorney. All inquiries route through the undersigned.*

It was dated November 10th.

Six days *after* the death she predicted. Four days from today.

I set the letter down and looked at her for a long time. She bore it well. Most people fidget under my attention; they feel it the way skin feels a draft. She simply returned it, gray-eyed, patient, a woman waiting for a train she was certain would arrive.

"This letter is postdated," I said.

"It's dated correctly. You'll finish the work by the tenth."

"If your husband dies on the fourth, and the work concludes by the tenth, why does the retainer predate the death by—" I stopped. Recalculated. Laid the dates side by side in my head where they refused to sit quietly. "Why does the check clear today?"

"Because I'm paying you in advance for work you'll do later. Surely that's how retainers function."

"It's how retainers function," I said, "when the work already exists. When the work hasn't happened yet, it's not a retainer. It's a prophecy with a price tag."

For the first time, something cracked in her composure — not much, a hairline fracture, the kind you only see if you've spent your life reading surfaces. Her thumb pressed hard against the clasp of her handbag, and I watched the knuckle blanch.

"Take the case or don't, Mr. Crane."

"Tell me why."

"Because you're the best there is at seeing what everyone else misses, and I need someone to see this." She stood, smoothing her coat with both hands, and for a moment she looked older than her face, older than the room, like a figure in a painting that has been overcleaned until the true age shows through the varnish. "My husband is a good man who has made enemies. Quiet ones. Patient ones. If something happens to him next week, I will be alone in a house full of people telling me it was his heart, and I will have no one — no one — willing to look past the surface. You look past surfaces. It's the only thing you do."

"And if I look, Mrs. Ashford, and find nothing? If his heart simply stops at his own anniversary party?"

"Then you'll have proven it was natural," she said. "Which is what I'm paying you for."

She said it without blinking. I have interviewed killers who blinked more.

* * *

After she left, I sat in the gray light of my office for the better part of an hour and did what I always do with a problem: I laid it out flat and examined its seams.

The facts, in order of arrival. One: a wealthy woman hires me to investigate a death that has not occurred, on a specific date, six days out. Two: the payment is excessive. Three: the paperwork is postdated to follow the event, meaning whoever prepared it was certain of the outcome — certain enough to commit it to typing, certain enough to date it. Four: she knew about Daniel Voss, whose case was never in the papers beyond a paragraph. Five: she smelled of lilies. Six: she lied to me at least once, though I couldn't yet locate the lie, only feel its weight, the way you feel a false bottom in a drawer.

People lie with their mouths and tell the truth with their hands, and hers had told me several truths she'd have paid money to withhold. The rehearsal tremor. The funeral-flower scent. The scar. The way she'd said *our friends* with a small hitch, as though the word *friends* belonged to a language she was translating from.

But it was the dates that kept itching. I spread them across the inside of my skull like photographs on a darkroom table. Today, the twenty-ninth of October. Death, the fourth. Conclusion of services, the tenth. Retainer tendered, today. Every document consistent with a timeline that had already been written — beginning, middle, end — and then run backward and dressed up as a request.

Nobody hires a man to prove a death natural. Grief doesn't work that way. Hope doesn't work that way. Only fear works that way, or guilt, and guilt wears fear's coat so well that even I sometimes have to hold it up to the light.

I should have refused. Twenty thousand dollars buys a great many refusals, and I've earned most of mine. A case with no body, no crime, no client story that survived thirty seconds of pressure — any sensible man walks away. I have a rule about it, in fact, unwritten like everything else about me: *take the cases the world has already finished writing.* Autopsies, not prophecies. The dead don't change their stories.

But the wrongness of it. God help me, the wrongness of it. It sat in my chest like a card filed backward in a perfect cabinet, and I have never in my life been able to leave a cabinet imperfect. My mind does not permit it. Some people are haunted by ghosts. I am haunted by disorder.

So I took the check to the bank, and I cashed it, and I came back to my office and pulled the Ashford folder and read it front to back until the pages were photographed onto whatever film my memory uses, and then I sat by the window as the streetlights came on and watched the city perform its evening errands, and I thought about the fourth of November.

An anniversary exhibition. Champagne. Two hundred witnesses. A man saying goodbye.

And a widow who had practiced her grief in advance, down to the tremor in her hands, asking me — no, *paying* me — to certify the ending before the story had a chance to surprise anyone.

There was one more thing, and I didn't understand it until I was turning off the desk lamp, and then it arrived all at once, complete, the way the important things always do. In the folder, beneath the society clippings, was a program from the gallery's fortieth-anniversary exhibition three years ago. I had glanced at it a hundred times without interest. Now I looked at it properly, and my memory served it up in full resolution: the featured work, the centerpiece, the painting Edmund Ashford had built forty years of respectability around.

A Vermeer. *Woman Reading a Letter at an Open Window.*

And beneath the title, in the curator's small print, a line I had read and forgotten in the same instant, the way I forget nothing:

*On loan from the private collection of E. Ashford. Acquired 1994.*

1994. Thirty years ago. The year, I thought, reaching for a fact I shouldn't have had any reason to reach for — the year of a fire I had been trying not to think about for three decades.

I put on my coat. Whatever Edmund Ashford was going to die of, it wouldn't be boredom, and neither, for the next six days, would I.
