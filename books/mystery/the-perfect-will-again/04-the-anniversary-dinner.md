## 4. The Anniversary Dinner

The invitations went out on a Tuesday, cream cardstock with gold-embossed lettering, and by Wednesday the whole of Ash Hollow was talking about nothing else. One hundred and fifty years since the town's founding. One hundred and fifty years of births and burials, harvests and hard winters, all of it to be celebrated under one roof at the Hollow Creek Grange Hall, where the long tables would be dressed in white linen and the mayor would raise a glass to the town's endurance.

Rafael Halloran read the invitation twice, then set it face-down on the kitchen table as though hiding its face might make the event itself disappear.

It wouldn't. It never did.

The sesquicentennial dinner came every twenty-five years — the centennial, the hundred-and-twenty-fifth, the hundred-and-fiftieth — and each time Rafael had attended out of obligation, standing near the punch bowl with a cup they never drank from, watching the town assemble itself like a family portrait no one wanted to sit for. But this year was different. This year there was a body in the morgue wearing Rafael's clothes, and every person who would be seated at those long tables was, in some way or another, connected to the cases Rafael had spent years quietly correcting.

Dale Petrie, whose brother's fall from the quarry overlook had been ruled an accident until Rafael proved the railing bolts had been cut. The Wexler cousins, whose aunt drowned in Miller's Pond with water in her lungs that didn't match the water in the pond. Old Mrs. Calloway, who still lit a candle for her son even though the fire marshal's report on his death had more holes than the smoke-damaged drywall. They would all be there. They would all raise their glasses. And somewhere among them, if Rafael's instincts were right — and their instincts were almost always right, that was the curse of them — someone would be watching to see how Rafael behaved under the lights.

Rafael spent Thursday doing what Rafael always did when dread arrived: working. The morgue was quiet, the paperwork caught up, so they cleaned instruments that were already clean and reorganized drawers that were already organized, and when that failed to settle the nerves, they walked the cemetery.

Autumn had turned the maples along the back fence the color of embers. Rafael moved down the rows without needing to read the stones, the way other people navigate their own homes in the dark. Third row from the oak: Dale Petrie's brother, the stone reading *beloved brother, taken too soon*, which was true in a way the carver never knew. Fifth row: the Wexler aunt, her dates bracketed by lilies someone still planted every spring. Near the fence line, where the ground got soggy in March: Mrs. Calloway's son, his marker leaning two degrees off plumb because the frost heave had been working at it for years and no one corrected it.

Three graves. Three accidents that weren't. Three truths Rafael carried alone because carrying them publicly would have meant reopening wounds the town had spent years salving over, and because — Rafael admitted this only to themselves, here, among the stones — because correcting the record in private felt safer than being the person who told Ash Hollow it had buried its own mistakes.

*You keep graves the way other people keep secrets,* Imogen Voss had said once, half-mocking, during one of their uneasy coffees after the mill road body. She hadn't been wrong. That was the worst part.

Rafael stood at the fence line a long moment, looking at the leaning stone, then walked home as the light went amber and thin.

* * *

The note came Friday morning.

Rafael found it tucked inside the morning paper on the porch, folded in thirds, which was how Rafael folded everything — case notes, letters, the linen napkins they pressed for occasions exactly like the one now looming. The recognition landed before the words did. That particular crease pattern, crisp and symmetrical, was a habit so ingrained Rafael did it without thinking, the way some people drum their fingers or bite their thumbnails.

The handwriting was worse. It was Rafael's handwriting.

Not an imitation — not the wobbly forgery a stranger might attempt, all careful loops and hesitations. This was fluent. The slightly leftward slant. The way the *f* dropped below the baseline. The small habit of crossing *t*'s with a single swift stroke that sometimes clipped the neighboring letter. Rafael held the note up to the window light and felt the floor tilt gently beneath them, because there was no question, none, that this was their own hand — and no question either that they had never written it.

Four lines, in ink:

*You correct the record in the dark.*
*I correct it in the light.*
*Come to the dinner. Sit where everyone can see you.*
*We'll finish what you started.*

Rafael read it four times. On the fourth reading, their hands were steady enough to notice what the panic had obscured: the paper was old stock, the kind sold at the stationery shop on Main Street that closed six years ago. The ink had dried into the fibers with age. Whoever wrote this hadn't dashed it off last night. They had practiced. For weeks, maybe months — writing lines in Rafael's hand the way a forger drills a signature, filling pages and burning them, until the slant and the stroke and the clipped *t*'s lived in their muscles the way they lived in Rafael's.

And they'd needed source material. A letter, a ledger, a signed form — something Rafael had written and handled, something with enough sample text to learn from. Rafael thought of the coroner's office, the sign-in logs, the condolence cards they'd written to the Petries and the Wexlers and Mrs. Calloway, each one filed or kept or treasured by its recipient. Any one of those hands could have passed a page of Rafael's writing to someone else. Or kept it. Or studied it.

Rafel set the note down and washed their hands, though they weren't dirty. Then they called Imogen Voss.

She arrived in nineteen minutes, which meant she'd probably been sitting in her cruiser somewhere nearby, doing the same thing she'd been doing for two weeks: circling Rafael's life like a hawk that couldn't decide whether the field held prey or poison. She took the note with gloved fingers, read it twice, and Rafael watched her face do the thing it always did — the flicker of suspicion first, quick and involuntary, before instinct dragged it back toward belief.

"You're sure it's not yours."

"I'm sure I didn't write it."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's my handwriting," Rafael said. "Every letter of it. Which means whoever wrote it has spent more time studying me than I have ever spent studying anyone, including the dead." They paused. "And I study the dead very closely."

Imogen turned the note over. Blank. She held it by the corner, tilting it against the gray morning light. "Old paper. Old ink. This wasn't rushed." She looked up. "Who handles your writing?"

"Anyone I've ever sent a card. Signed a form. Left a note for the deputy covering nights." Rafael heard their own voice go flat and clinical, the tone they used for autopsy findings, and was grateful for it. "The list is longer than either of us wants it to be."

"The dinner," Imogen said. "It says come to the dinner."

"I noticed."

"And you're going?"

Rafael looked at the note again. *We'll finish what you started.* The words sat in their chest like a swallowed stone. What Rafael had started — the quiet corrections, the secret exhumations of truth, the three files rewritten in private — had been done in darkness precisely so that no one would ever finish anything, so that the knowledge could rest unburdened and unacted-upon, a mercy and a cowardice at once. Someone had found that darkness. Someone had read every page.

"I'm going," Rafael said. "They want an audience. I want to know why. Those two things can stand in the same room."

Imogen was quiet for a moment. Outside, a truck rattled past, dragging its shadow across the kitchen wall. When she spoke, her voice had lost its procedural edge. "Rafael. If this person is willing to kill a stranger and dress him in your clothes, a note is not a threat. It's a program. They've already told you how the evening goes."

"Then tell me not to go," Rafael said. "Tell me it's unsafe, that you'll post officers at the doors, that we cancel the dinner and search the grange hall top to bottom. Give me one official reason to stay home."

Imogen opened her mouth. Closed it. They both knew the arithmetic. Canceling the sesquicentennial dinner would take an act of the town council, and the town council answered to the mayor, and the mayor had already told the newspaper the dinner would proceed *come hell or high water, as is tradition*. And even if it could be canceled — even if Imogen burned every favor she had — the killer would simply choose another stage. Another gathering. Another night.

"They'll be there," Rafael said quietly. "Everyone connected to those three cases. Everyone who might have kept a piece of my handwriting. Everyone who had reason to hate the record as it stands." They met Imogen's eyes. "One hundred and fifty years of Ash Hollow under one roof, Detective. And our killer just sent their RSVP."

* * *

That evening Rafael laid the note flat on the kitchen table beside the cream-colored invitation, the forged creases aligned with the embossed border, and studied the pairing like a pathologist studying two slides. The town's celebration and the killer's summons, side by side, written in the same small-town grammar of formality and threat.

Rafael thought about the three graves again. About the leaning stone by the fence line, two degrees off plumb, waiting years for someone to correct it.

Then they thought of something else — something that surfaced slowly, the way bodies surface in cold water. Every file Rafael had ever quietly corrected lived in the records office. Every annotation, every cross-reference, every private addendum slipped into the public archive where Rafael believed no one but themselves would ever think to look.

Someone had looked. Someone had read. And someone had learned to write like Rafael well enough to announce, in Rafael's own hand, that the work was not finished.

Rafael picked up the phone to call Imogen, then set it down. Some things needed saying in person.

Tomorrow they would compare files. Tomorrow they would walk through the fall, the drowning, and the fire, and find out exactly how much of Rafael's secret life a stranger had memorized.

Tonight, Rafael sat alone with the note until the lamp burned low, listening to the wind strip the last leaves off the maple outside, and understood — with the calm certainty they usually reserved for the dead — that the anniversary dinner would not end the way the mayor planned.
