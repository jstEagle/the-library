## 8. Baiting the Hook

Rafael hadn't slept. The note sat on the kitchen table where it had sat all night, propped against the salt cellar like a place card at a dinner no one wanted to attend. *You'll be there Saturday. So will I.* Written in Rafael's own hand — the slight rightward lean of it, the way the crossbar of the *t* drifted a hair too far — so exact that Rafael had spent an hour writing the alphabet over and over just to prove to themselves that their own pen could not have made it.

The kettle clicked off. Rafael didn't pour.

Instead they spread three folders across the table: the fall from the quarry overlook, the drowning in Miller's Pond, the fire on Corliss Street. Three accidents that were not accidents. Three corrections that lived only in Rafael's head and in these pages, pages that had been read by someone else's eyes, annotated in pencil, returned to the drawer as if nothing had happened. And beneath them, the fourth file — the one that had started everything without meaning to. *Eleanor Marsh, age nine. Accidental drowning.* Ruled accidental by a coroner who hadn't looked hard enough, or hadn't wanted to. Rafael had looked. Twenty years too late, but Rafael had looked, and then had done nothing with what they found except carry it like a stone in the chest.

That was the flaw in the design of Rafael's whole quiet life. They had believed that correcting the record privately was enough. That truth, once known by one person, counted for something. Wendell Marsh had spent twenty years proving otherwise.

Rafael picked up a pen — a different pen, cheap ballpoint, nothing like their own — and began to write.

*New evidence has come to light regarding the death of Eleanor Marsh (1999). The original findings have been reexamined. A full disclosure will be presented at the Ash Hollow Sesquicentennial Dinner, Saturday evening, records room, 8 p.m.*

They read it back twice. It was a lie built out of a truth: there was no new evidence, only old evidence that had never been given its day. But Marsh wouldn't know that. Marsh would know only that the record he had guarded, grieved over, and finally avenged was about to be opened again — publicly, formally, in front of the whole town — and that whatever he had done to keep his daughter's name buried alongside her body would come apart under fluorescent light while he stood in a room full of neighbors holding a glass of cider.

He would come to the records room. He would come to stop it, or to destroy it, or to add another name to his ledger. Any of those brought him through a door with Rafael sitting behind it and Imogen Voss standing beside it.

It was a trap. It was also, Rafael understood, an invitation to be killed. Marsh had already dressed one corpse in Rafael's clothes. He knew how Rafael moved, wrote, thought. If the plan failed, the next body wearing that cardigan might not be a stranger's.

Rafael folded the page into an evidence sleeve, then took up the phone.

"Voss."

"It's me. I've written something you need to see, and I need you to hate it first, so you can tell me honestly whether it works."

A long pause on the line. "The diner," she said. "Twenty minutes. Not the station."

"Not the station," Rafael agreed. "Never the station again."

* * *

Imogen arrived early and took the corner booth at the Bluebird Diner, back to the wall, sightline to both doors. Old habit. She'd been doing it since the body on the mill road, since the moment she'd realized that somewhere in Ash Hollow was a person who studied people the way she studied crime scenes — patiently, completely, with love, almost.

Rafael came in at 7:42 exactly, coat damp, face gray with a sleeplessness Imogen recognized because she was wearing it herself. They ordered coffee neither of them drank. Rafael slid the evidence sleeve across the formica.

She read it once. Then again, slower, the way she read anything that might end up in front of a review board.

"No," she said.

"That's the hating part. Now tell me whether it works."

Imogen set the page down and pressed her palm flat on it, as if it might get up and walk. "You're proposing we fabricate evidence. Not withhold it, not interpret it loosely — fabricate it. Forge a case document, plant it in a municipal building, and use it to lure a suspect into a location where, if this goes wrong, there is no warrant, no backup plan on paper, and no record that either of us authorized anything." She looked up. "Do you understand what I'm describing? Because I just described my entire career going into a shredder."

"I understand exactly what you're describing." Rafael's voice was low and even, the voice Imogen had heard them use with grieving families — the voice of someone who had learned to deliver terrible things gently. "I also understand that Wendell Marsh killed four people, dressed one of them in my clothes, and announced in my handwriting that he'll be at the dinner on Saturday. The lawful version of this is that I tell you everything, you write a report, the report goes to the prosecutor, the prosecutor says we don't have probable cause to hold a retired archivist who's never raised his voice in sixty years, and on Saturday night Wendell Marsh walks out of that dinner with whoever he chooses and disappears into twenty years of careful, patient silence. You know it. I know it. He knows it better than either of us."

Imogen turned her coffee cup a quarter turn. Outside, a delivery truck rattled past, and the window shivered.

"The problem," she said finally, "isn't that your plan is wrong. The problem is that it's right, and I can't put it in a file. There's no clean way to do this. Either I do it dirty or I watch it not happen."

"So don't put it in a file." Rafael leaned forward. "I'm not asking you to forge anything. I wrote that page. My hand, my idea, my risk. All I'm asking is that on Saturday night, when a man comes through the records room door expecting me alone, you happen to be standing in the hallway. Off duty. Out of uniform. Visiting a friend."

"A friend." Imogen almost laughed. "I arrest people for less than what you're asking me to not-do."

"Yes," Rafael said. "And you became a detective because somewhere in you there's a ledger too. Names the system got wrong. I've seen how you look at the cold cases. Don't pretend the procedure is the only thing keeping you honest — it's also the thing that's kept Eleanor Marsh dead and unnamed for twenty years while her father burned down half a town's worth of lives trying to say her name out loud."

The diner noise went on around them — the grill hissing, a waitress calling *order up*, rain starting against the glass — but the booth felt sealed off, underwater. Imogen thought about the mill road body. About the replicated autopsy tag, perfect down to the font. About the fact that Wendell Marsh had learned to imitate Rafael Halloran more faithfully than Rafael Halloran's own friends could describe them. He had studied every file. Which meant he had studied hers, too. Which meant he knew about the corner booth, the back-to-the-wall habit, maybe even the quarter-turn of the coffee cup. She was being read right now, possibly; the thought crawled up her spine and settled at the base of her skull.

"If we do this," she said, "we do it so it can't hang either of us if it breaks wrong. No texts. No notes. Nothing written after tonight. This conversation ends when we leave the booth, and afterward we were two people who had pie."

"We didn't order pie."

"Afterward we had pie, Halloran. Learn to lie better, it's the job."

Something moved across Rafael's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the first Imogen had seen there in days. "And if it goes wrong? If he doesn't come alone, or doesn't come at all, or comes with something worse than a knife?"

"Then it goes wrong," Imogen said, "and I stand in front of you, because that's the part of the job I'm still allowed to choose." She slid the evidence sleeve back toward them. "One condition. The planted file — the fake disclosure — it has to contain something real. One true fact about Eleanor's case that he'll recognize, something only the killer and the person who solved it would know. If it's all forgery, he'll smell it. He's spent his life among documents. Forgeries are all he reads."

Rafael was quiet for a moment. "Her shoes," they said at last. "The original report says she was found barefoot. She wasn't. One shoe was recovered downstream, wedged in the culvert grate, and it was logged and then left out of the file. I found the log entry. The shoe was red canvas, left foot, size two. Nobody ever told the family it existed."

Imogen stared at them. "You've known that for years."

"I've known that for years."

"And you never told anyone."

"There was no one to tell," Rafael said simply, "until there was you."

Imogen looked at the evidence sleeve, at the forged promise inside it, and felt the strange vertigo of the last weeks resolve into something solid. Loyalty to procedure had gotten her a commendation and a filing cabinet full of unsolved cases. Loyalty to the dead had gotten Rafael a closet with missing clothes and a target on their back. Somewhere between those two failures was the only thing that had ever actually worked: two people deciding, off the books, that a nine-year-old girl in a red canvas shoe deserved to have her name said out loud.

"Saturday," she said. "8 p.m. I'll be in the hallway. You'll be at the desk. And Halloran—" she held their eyes, "—you wear something he won't recognize. Let him meet you for the first time."

Rafael nodded, pocketed the sleeve, and stood to go. At the edge of the booth they paused.

"Pie," they said. "For the record."

"For the record," Imogen agreed, and watched them walk out into the rain.

She stayed another ten minutes, finishing the coffee gone cold, running the plan forward and backward like a film she could already see cutting together: the toast, the applause, the tall old man slipping away down the corridor with his careful archivist's stride. It would work. Or it would end them both.

When she finally left, she paid cash for two slices of pie she never ordered — and noticed, as the waitress rang it up, that the specials board by the register had been wiped clean and rewritten in a neat, upright, meticulous hand.

Wendell Marsh still did the chalkboard at the Bluebird on Fridays.

He had written today's date, and underneath it, in letters small enough that only someone looking would see:

*See you Saturday.*
