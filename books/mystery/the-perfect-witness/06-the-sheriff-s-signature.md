## 6. The Sheriff's Signature

Dale Pruitt kept the Ashford file in the bottom drawer of his desk, under the hanging folder marked MISC. 2019, because a man who hid a thing too well might as well nail it to the courthouse door.

He hadn't opened it in five years. He didn't need to. He could recite it the way other men recited scripture or batting averages: Daniel Ashford, twenty-six, found in the shallows off Pier Three at the Vane Point Marina on the morning of October 9th, blood alcohol point-one-one, no signs of struggle, ruled accidental drowning by coroner's report signed off by Dr. Feld and countersigned — Dale's own signature, blue ink, steady as a lie — by Sheriff Dale R. Pruitt. Case closed October 11th. Forty-eight hours, start to finish. Fastest drowning investigation in Marlow Bend history, if anyone had bothered to keep score.

Nobody bothered. That was the whole point of Marlow Bend.

He was doing the thing he did most mornings now, standing at the window of his office with coffee he'd stopped tasting years ago, watching the square wake up — Dot's Diner flipping its sign, the flag snapping on its pole, the courthouse clock stuck at 11:40 since the county stopped paying for repairs — when he saw Theo Sterling cross the street toward the sheriff's office.

Pruitt's stomach did what it always did when he saw Sterling, which was nothing dramatic, just a slow tightening, like a ratchet clicking one notch. The lawyer moved the way he always moved, unhurried, hands in his coat pockets, a man so unremarkable you could watch him walk the length of Main Street and afterward swear nobody had passed by at all. Five years ago that quality had been Pruitt's favorite thing about him. These days it felt like watching a knife walk around in a sheath.

The bell over the door jingled. Deputy Kayla Moss looked up from the front desk, said something, and Sterling said something back — polite, low — and then he was standing in Pruitt's doorway with a manila envelope under one arm.

"Sheriff."

"Sterling." Pruitt gestured at the chair across from his desk without sitting down himself. He never sat across from lawyers. It put you at the height disadvantage twice over. "What brings you out? Somebody's estate need probating?"

"Something like that." Sterling took the chair anyway, settled into it like a man planning to stay. He set the envelope on his knee but didn't open it. "I've been doing some work for a client. Estate planning, mostly. But it touches on an old matter, and I wanted to look at a file before I gave advice I'd regret."

"Which matter?"

"Daniel Ashford."

The name landed in the office the way a stone lands in a well. Pruitt heard it all the way down. He kept his face where he kept it, somewhere between bored and mildly annoyed, the expression he'd practiced in the mirror during his first year wearing the badge, back when the badge still meant something to him.

"That's a closed case," Pruitt said. "Closed clean. Accidental drowning. You want to read the report, it's public record, Kayla can pull it for you."

"I've read the report." Sterling's voice stayed level, conversational, a man discussing weather. "That's sort of the problem. It's four pages long. There are no autopsy photographs attached. No toxicology beyond the initial screen. And there's a witness statement from a Ruth Calloway dated October 10th, 2019 — except Ms. Calloway left Marlow Bend in March of 2018. She signed a statement eight months after she moved away."

Pruitt drank his coffee. It tasted like the inside of the mug. "Clerical error," he said. "Dates get fat-fingered. You know how it is."

"I do," Sterling said. "I also know that a clerical error doesn't usually come with a missing autopsy photo log. And it doesn't usually come with a case closed in forty-eight hours on a drowning victim whose father was a state senator's brother-in-law." He paused, and for the first time something moved behind those gray eyes — not accusation, worse than accusation. Interest. "I'm not here to make trouble, Sheriff. My client has a legitimate concern, and I can't advise him responsibly until I understand what actually happened at that marina. I'm asking to see the full file. Everything. Including whatever's not in the copy at the clerk's office."

"And why would there be more here than at the clerk's?"

"Because there always is," Sterling said quietly. "You taught me that, Dale. Back when we were both younger and the town still trusted both of us."

Pruitt set the mug down. His hand was steady. He'd have bet money his hand was steady. "I'll tell you what I told the last reporter who came sniffing. Boy got drunk, boy went in the water, boy drowned. It happens on every lake in America three times a summer. There's no story there."

"I didn't say there was a story," Sterling said. He stood, tucked the envelope back under his arm, and buttoned his coat — a small, precise motion, a man closing a ledger. "I said I needed to see the file. Think about it. I'll be at my office."

He left. The bell jingled. Through the window, Pruitt watched him cross the square, unhurried as ever, and turn up toward the law offices, and disappear into the ordinary morning like a coin dropped into deep water.

Kayla appeared in the doorway. "He ask for the Ashford file?"

"He did."

"You going to give it to him?"

Pruitt looked at her. Kayla was twenty-nine, good at her job, honest in the way people were honest until they learned better. He remembered being twenty-nine. He remembered thinking the file would be closed for good, sealed by time the way the lake sealed over Daniel Ashford every winter.

"It's in my desk," Pruitt said. "It stays in my desk."

* * *

The call came at 4:17 that afternoon, and Pruitt knew who it was before he answered, because in Marlow Bend there were only maybe six people who called the sheriff directly instead of going through dispatch, and only one of them made the phone feel heavier before you picked it up.

"Sheriff Pruitt." Judge Raymond Hobb's voice was dry and pleasant, the voice of a man reading a menu. "Do you have a minute?"

"For the judge, always."

"Mm." Papers rustled on Hobb's end, though Pruitt would have bet the farm there were no papers at all. "I hear Elias Crane retained Theo Sterling this week."

News traveled fast in Marlow Bend, faster than water downhill. "I heard that too."

"And I hear Mr. Sterling paid a visit to your office today regarding a certain closed matter."

Pruitt walked to his door, checked the empty hallway, and shut it. "He asked to see the file."

"What did you tell him?"

"Told him the case was closed and the report's public record."

A pause. On the line, Pruitt could hear the particular silence of a man choosing words the way a surgeon chooses instruments. "Dale. Let me save us both some time. Marcus Vane is a friend of this county. He has built half of what stands upright in Marlow Bend, and the other half he's financed. When a man like that loses a business partner's son to a tragic accident, the community grieves, and then the community moves on. What the community does not do is reopen old wounds because a dying accountant with a grudge hired the town's most discreet lawyer to go digging."

So Crane's name was already in Hobb's mouth. Pruitt felt the floor tilt a degree, the way it had tilted five years ago, the night Raymond Hobb had called him at home — ten o'clock, brandy in the judge's voice — and explained, gently, reasonably, that the Ashford boy's death was a tragedy that needn't become a catastrophe, that Dr. Feld had already reviewed the findings, that the family wanted peace above all, and that Dale's career, his pension, his name on that new annex plaque, would be best served by a prompt and tidy resolution.

He'd told himself it was true. He'd told himself it for five years, until the telling wore smooth as river stone.

"He's asking questions," Pruitt said. "I can't stop a lawyer from asking questions. Man's got a right—"

"A right to what's in the file," Hobb agreed pleasantly. "Which is why I'm calling. There's been some talk at the county level about records retention. Old files, thin files, files that generate questions. The cleanest thing — for everyone, including you, Dale — would be for that file to come down to my chambers for review. Official review. I'll sign the transfer order myself. Chain of custody, all perfectly proper."

Pruitt's hand had gone cold around the receiver. "You want me to hand you the file."

"I want the file somewhere it can't be misconstrued," Hobb said. "Sterling is thorough. You remember what happened with the Kessler audit — the man finds seams nobody knew were seams. If he walks into your office with a subpoena next week, and the file's gone, that looks like concealment. If the file's in judicial custody, pending review, why, that looks like diligence. There's a difference, Dale, between hiding a thing and keeping a thing safe. We're keeping it safe."

"We're burying it."

The silence on the line stretched long enough that Pruitt heard the clock on his wall, the one that worked, ticking through four full seconds.

"Careful," Hobb said at last, and the pleasantness was gone now, folded away like a napkin after dinner. "Five years ago you signed that report. Your signature, your decision, your case. Nobody ordered you to close anything — I seem to recall a phone call in which you yourself concluded the evidence pointed to accident. That's what your notes say. That's what the record says. If a man wants to revisit the record, he revisits yours first. Not mine. Not anyone else's. Yours." A breath. "I'm offering you shelter, son. Take it or don't. But make your choice tonight, because Mr. Sterling strikes me as a man who doesn't knock twice."

The line went dead.

Pruitt stood in his office while the light went from gold to gray to blue. Down on the square, Dot's Diner lit up, and kids cut across the grass past the war memorial, and everything looked exactly the way it had looked five years ago, the night he'd driven home from the marina with Daniel Ashford's wet shoes in an evidence bag on his passenger seat and a phone call ringing in his ears.

He opened the bottom drawer. MISC. 2019. The folder underneath, thin as a pamphlet, four pages plus a witness statement plus a photograph of a young man grinning on a boat deck, sun-flared, forever twenty-six.

His signature sat at the bottom of the final page. Blue ink. Steady as a lie.

He thought about Theo Sterling's face when he'd said *you taught me that, Dale*. He thought about Kayla's question — *you going to give it to him?* — and how he hadn't answered, and how the not-answering had felt, even then, like signing something else.

He thought about the fact that Hobb had known about the visit within hours. Had known Crane's name. Which meant somebody was feeding the judge, which meant somebody besides Pruitt was afraid, and afraid men made mistakes, and mistakes were how cases like this came apart — not with confessions, but with a thread pulled loose by a quiet man in a gray coat who didn't knock twice.

Pruitt took the folder out. He held it over the transfer envelope Hobb's clerk had messengered over that morning — the judge had sent the paperwork before he'd even called, that was the kind of careful Raymond Hobb was — and he stood there a long moment, the folder in one hand, the envelope gaping open on the desk like a mouth.

Then he put the folder back in the drawer, closed it, and locked it, and sat down at his desk, and poured the cold coffee out into the sink in the corner, and told himself — smooth as river stone — that he simply needed more time to think.

At 6:02 the next morning, before the flag went up and before Dot flipped her sign, a car Pruitt didn't recognize parked across from his house and didn't leave. Two men sat in it. They didn't get out. They didn't need to. When Pruitt stepped onto his porch with his lunch pail, the driver lifted a phone, took one photograph of him standing there, and put the phone away.

By seven, Pruitt was in his cruiser outside the courthouse, and by seven-fifteen he had dialed a number he'd sworn he'd never dial, and when the voice on the other end said, "Sheriff's office," Dale Pruitt heard himself say, in a voice he barely recognized as his own:

"The file's still in my desk. And I think I need to talk to whoever's asking about it — before somebody makes sure neither of us gets the chance."

On the other end of the line, Theo Sterling was quiet for a moment. Then: "Come see me, Sheriff. Bring the file. And Dale — don't tell anyone you're coming."
