## 9. Statute of Limitations

The law library at the county annex smelled like dust and old decisions, and I loved it the way some men love a bar. Everything in it was settled. Every argument had been made, every verdict rendered, every grief reduced to a citation number. You could stand in the stacks and feel the whole machinery of justice humming around you, indifferent and complete.

I was there on a Tuesday morning to pull the criminal code volume for an unrelated probate question — a dispute over a hunting cabin, two brothers who hadn't spoken since Reagan — when my eye caught the heading on the facing page.

*Limitations of Prosecution.*

I don't believe in omens. I believe in filing systems. But I pulled that volume too, and carried both to a carrel by the window, and started reading the way you start reading anything when your mind is already circling a problem: without deciding to.

Murder, in this state, has no statute of limitations. That's the rule everyone knows from television. Kill someone, and the state can come for you thirty years later, on your deathbed if necessary. The law's memory for murder is infinite.

But manslaughter is not murder. And negligent homicide is not manslaughter. And involuntary manslaughter — the charge a clever prosecutor reaches for when the evidence won't support intent — carries a limitations period.

Five years.

I sat very still in the carrel and let the arithmetic assemble itself, because I could feel it assembling, the way you feel a will's clauses locking together before you've finished reading them.

Daniel Ashford died on October 15th, five years ago. The sheriff's report said accidental drowning, but Elias Crane's letters said otherwise, and Crane — whatever he was, prophet or fraud or something worse — had put a date on Vivian Ashford's death with the confidence of a man reading from a schedule.

October 14th. Eleven days away when he hired me. Ten now.

And if Daniel Ashford had been killed — not murdered in the first degree, not with premeditation and malice, but drowned in a moment of panic or rage or cold calculation that a prosecutor would have to prove intent to sustain a murder charge — then the clock on his death had been running ever since. Five years. Expiring October 15th.

Crane's prediction wasn't a prophecy.

It was a deadline.

* * *

I walked back to my office instead of driving, though it was a mile and a half and starting to rain, because I needed the motion to think. Marlow Bend in the rain is a different town — softer, blurrier, the brick storefronts bleeding color onto the sidewalks. Nobody looked at me. That was the arrangement I'd had with this town for five years: mutual invisibility. They didn't see me; I didn't see them. It had worked beautifully right up until a stranger walked into my office with a checkbook and a prophecy and made seeing things my job again.

By the time I reached my door I had the shape of it, and the shape was ugly and elegant at once, the way the best traps are.

Someone killed Daniel Ashford five years ago. Not cleanly enough to survive a real investigation, but cleanly enough to survive the one they got — a thin file, a bought witness, a sheriff's signature, a case closed before the body was cold in the morgue drawer. They'd gotten away with it because the file said *accident*, and files are how towns remember.

But files can be reopened. Witnesses can recant. A dying accountant with a conscience and a ledger can start writing letters. So the killer had spent five years waiting out the only thing more powerful than any of that: time itself. On October 15th, the limitations period on the lesser charges would lapse forever. No prosecution for the drowning. No reopening that could stick. Whatever Crane knew, whatever Ruth Calloway — if that was even her name yet, whoever she turned out to be — might someday say, it would be history, not evidence. Grief, not crime.

Unless.

Unless the killer did something new. Something with its own fresh clock.

If Vivian Ashford died on October 14th — one day before the old statute expired — her death would be a brand-new crime. A new investigation, a new file, a new set of charges with decades of limitations runway. And here was the part that made me stop under the awning of the hardware store and stand in the rain like a man who'd forgotten where he was going:

A second death would bury the first one permanently. Because the town would spend its outrage on the new body. Who reopens a five-year-old drowning when there's a fresh corpse in the same family? The old case would be folded into the new one as background, motive, color — *the poor woman lost her son, and now this* — and never examined on its own terms again.

Two deaths, one stone. The second killing would be the perfect alibi for the first.

I went inside, hung up my wet coat, and stood in front of my filing cabinets for a long moment. Row after row of green folders, alphabetized, cross-referenced, dead. Five years ago I'd learned what happens to a man who opens files he shouldn't. Now I was about to do it on purpose.

I pulled the Ashford folder — the thin one, the estate-planning pretext folder, all two pages of legitimate work — and behind it, in a section of the drawer I'd labeled *Personal / Pending*, I started a new file. At the top I wrote the date, and beneath it:

*October 14th is not when she dies. October 14th is when he needs her to die.*

There's a difference, and the difference was the trap.

* * *

Crane had given me a phone number written on the back of a dry cleaner's ticket. No address. When I called it, a woman answered — hired, I guessed, from the flat professional way she said the number back — and put me through to a room where the line picked up on the fourth ring and Crane's voice came through thinned and papery, like paper itself.

"You've found it," he said. Not hello. Not Theo. Just that, delivered with the mild satisfaction of a teacher watching a student arrive at the answer unassisted.

"Found what."

"The clock." A pause, wet and labored. He'd told me he was sick; I'd assumed it was a prop, part of the performance. Listening to him breathe, I revised that. "You went to the library. I knew you would. You're a filings man, Mr. Sterling. You think in deadlines and jurisdictions. It's why I chose you."

"Chose me for what, exactly? Because I've been trying to write down your job description and it keeps coming out nonsense. You hire an estate lawyer to prevent a murder you claim to foresee. You pay triple. You give me a date, a method, a victim. And now I find out the date isn't a prediction at all — it's a countdown. So which is it, Crane? Are you warning her, or timing her?"

The silence on the line lasted long enough that I checked whether the call had dropped.

"Both," he said finally. "Neither. You're asking the wrong question."

"Then correct me."

"Why does a man count down to a statute expiring?"

I leaned against the window, watching rain crawl down the glass. "Because he's afraid," I said. "Because every day past the expiration is a day he's safe, and every day before it is a day he isn't. Because somewhere out there is a witness, or a document, or a ledger—"

I stopped. Crane heard me stop.

"Yes," he said softly. "Go on."

"—and the countdown isn't just about safety. It's about urgency. If he wanted simply to wait it out, he'd wait. But he can't afford to wait, because something is coming loose. Something that expires *with* the statute, or before it. So he has to act inside the window, and he wants—" I closed my eyes. "He wants the town to know it's coming. He wants it on the record. Why would a killer want the date known?"

Another silence. Then, so quietly I almost missed it: "Because he isn't the one who wants her dead."

The line went dead in my hand. Not hung up — dropped, the connection folding into static and then nothing, the way calls die in this valley when the rain comes down off the ridge. I stood holding the phone, replaying it.

*Because he isn't the one who wants her dead.*

Which meant either that Crane was lying — layering misdirection the way he layered everything — or that the architecture of this thing was different than I'd drawn it. Not killer and target. Something else. A man counting down who wasn't the man pulling the trigger. A letter-writer who claimed foreknowledge because he had knowledge, and claimed prophecy because the truth was unsayable.

I thought about the specificity of the letters. Drowned in her own bath. October 14th. Not threats, exactly — I'd read them a dozen times now, and threats have a texture, a hunger. These had none. They read like stage directions. Like a man describing something he'd already watched happen once, and couldn't watch twice.

I opened the Ashford file and laid Crane's letters side by side on the desk, and for the first time I read them not as predictions but as testimony. What did this man know, and how did he know it?

Third letter, second paragraph: *"She will be alone in the house. The staff leaves at eight. The water runs hot in that bathroom — the pipes were replaced in March, she complained about it at the garden club, ask anyone."*

Ask anyone. A stranger wouldn't know about the pipes. A stranger wouldn't care. But someone who'd been inside that house — recently, casually, welcome — would know exactly how the details assembled into a death.

And the fourth letter, the one that had struck me as cruelty dressed as precision: *"Tell her the water doesn't have to be deep. Tell her that."*

I'd taken that for menace. Sitting there in my office with the rain ticking against the glass, I heard it differently. That wasn't a threat. That was a man telling a mother how her son died, in the only language he could afford — wrapped in the grammar of a threat so that no one would look too closely at the grief underneath.

*The water doesn't have to be deep.* Daniel Ashford had drowned in the marina, in six feet of water, a strong swimmer, according to a file with no autopsy photos. And this man knew — knew the way a witness knows — that depth had nothing to do with it.

I sat back. My hands weren't entirely steady, and I noted that with the detached interest of a lawyer observing a fact adverse to his client.

* * *

At four o'clock I drove out to the courthouse annex where the clerk of court keeps the civil judgment rolls, and I spent ninety minutes doing what I do best: reading other people's money. If Crane had been an accountant — and everything about his precision suggested he had been — then somewhere in the public record there might be a trail. Corporations leave shadows. Money leaves footprints even when the people spending it wear gloves.

I found the shadow at 5:20.

Bendwater Holdings, LLC. Registered five years and two months ago — seven weeks after Daniel Ashford drowned. Single-member structure, registered agent a firm in the state capital that exists, as far as I could tell, to be a registered agent. Its articles of organization listed a purpose so broad it was meaningless: *"any lawful business activity."*

But Bendwater Holdings owned property. One parcel. Recorded in the deed index the following spring: a quarter-acre lot on the east shore of the reservoir, purchased from the Ashford estate's land portfolio — sold, I confirmed with a second trip to the records room, in the liquidation of assets following Daniel's death, when Vivian, shattered and half-distracted, had signed off on a batch of sales prepared by counsel she no longer remembers hiring.

The purchase price was forty percent over assessed value. Buyers don't overpay by forty percent unless they're buying something other than land. Silence, maybe. Loyalty. Or a debt disguised as a transaction — payment routed through a shell so clean that no ledger line anywhere says *hush money*.

And Bendwater Holdings' organizer — the name on the initial filing, buried in the capital firm's boilerplate — was a company called Vane Valley Development.

I sat in the records room until the clerk started stacking chairs, looking at that name. Vane. I didn't know a Vane. Marlow Bend's power ran through names I did know — Hobb at the county bench, Pruitt behind the sheriff's desk, the Whitfield banking family, the church league, the school board. Half-owner of the town, Crane had almost said, before saying nothing at all. Whoever Vane was, he'd kept his name off the marquee and attached it only to a shell company buying a meaningless quarter-acre at a crooked price, seven weeks after a boy drowned.

Seven weeks. Long enough for the funeral flowers to dry. Short enough that the payment and the death belonged to the same conversation.

I copied the deed reference, the filing dates, the parcel number. My handwriting came out neat, because handwriting is the last thing to panic.

* * *

That night I didn't sleep much. I lay in the dark running the timeline forward and backward like a proof, looking for the flaw.

Five years ago: Daniel Ashford drowns. File closes in nine days. Witness statement signed by a woman who'd already left town. Sheriff signs. Case dead.

Five years minus seven weeks ago — no. Seven weeks *after*: Bendwater Holdings forms, buys its crooked parcel. Payment made, or begun, or promised.

Since then: nothing. Five years of nothing, which is the most expensive thing anyone in this town had ever bought.

Then, three weeks ago: Elias Crane walks into my office with a prophecy and a checkbook. Letters begin arriving at Ashford House. And somewhere in the dark, a man — the man who paid, or the man who killed, or God help us both — starts counting down twelve days to legal immunity, needing one more death to seal the first one shut.

The flaw, when I found it, wasn't in the logic. It was in the assumption underneath it. I'd been assuming the countdown belonged to the killer — that October 14th was *his* date, chosen by him, controlled by him. But Crane's voice on the phone had said otherwise. *Because he isn't the one who wants her dead.*

What if the countdown belonged to two men? One who needed Vivian Ashford dead before the statute expired — and one who needed everyone to *see it coming*, because a crime everyone was warned about couldn't stay buried afterward. A man forcing the town to watch, so that when it happened, the old case would crack open along with the new one. Penance wearing the mask of menace.

Or — and this was the version I liked least — one man playing both parts. Engineering the terror, engineering the date, engineering the death, and engineering his own absolution in advance. Some men don't want to escape justice. They want to arrange it.

Either way, the shape of the thing was clear now, and the shape was a trap — I just couldn't yet tell who was standing inside it.

I got up at 2 a.m., sat at the kitchen table, and wrote one sentence on a legal pad:

*Find out who Marcus Vane is before October 14th — because on October 15th, it won't matter anymore.*

Then I packed a bag. Because in the morning I was driving to the capital, to the offices of the firm that filed Bendwater Holdings' papers, and I was going to do the one thing I'd sworn off doing five years ago.

I was going to make myself visible.
