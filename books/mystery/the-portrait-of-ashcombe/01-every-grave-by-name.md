## 1. Every Grave by Name

The fog in Ashcombe did not lift so much as it grew tired of holding on.

Wren Voss had learned this over eleven years of walking the cemetery at dawn — that the mist came down off the ridge around four in the morning, settled into the hollows between the headstones like a guest who intended to stay for breakfast, and then, somewhere near six, simply lost interest and thinned out without ever formally announcing its departure. She had timed it once, out of a coroner's instinct for measurement, and found the fog's retreat varied by as much as forty minutes depending on whether the tide was coming in at Miller's Bend. The sea breathed, and Ashcombe's weather was the exhale.

She walked the rows now with her collar up and her breath making small ghosts of its own, and she said the names.

Not all of them. Not this morning. There were one thousand two hundred and six graves in the Ashcombe municipal cemetery, counting the old section behind the iron fence where the ground had sunk into soft green swales, and she could not do all of them before her nine o'clock appointment with a body that had been fished out of the harbor netting. But she did what she always did: she started at the gate and worked inward, and she named every soul she herself had certified.

"Edmund Garvey," she said, passing the granite stone with the little carved anchor his daughters had insisted upon, though Edmund had never been on a boat in his life and had drowned, technically, in three inches of water in his own cellar. "Heart attack. You fell like a sack of meal, Ed, but you were gone before you hit the floor, and I made sure everyone knew it."

That mattered to her. It was the part of the work nobody thought about — not the cutting, not the weighing of organs, not the careful cataloguing of what alcohol had done to a liver over sixty years — but the sentence afterward. The line in the report. *Cause of death.* Two or three words that would follow a person past their own memory, past their children's memory, into whatever archive the world kept when everyone who remembered your face was gone too. Wren took those words seriously the way some people took scripture seriously. You did not guess. You did not round. You did not write something kind when the truth was merely true, and you did not write something true when kindness was owed.

"Margaret Osei." The stone was newer, the letters still sharp-edged. "Pneumonia, aged ninety-one, and I want you to know I told your grandson you went in your sleep, which was a lie, because you fought it for four days like a woman clearing a room of intruders. But he needed the sleep version. I stand by it."

She permitted herself exactly one lie per decade, roughly speaking. She kept a private accounting of them, the way an accountant keeps a ledger of rounding errors — small, deliberate, defensible. Margaret Osei's grandson had been seventeen and already carrying more than a boy should carry. The sleep version had been a mercy, and Wren believed mercies were like sutures: applied correctly, in the right place, they held everything together.

The fog was thinning. Through it, the cemetery revealed itself in stages — first the pale suggestion of stones, then the stones themselves, then the names on the stones, arriving one row at a time like actors taking their marks. Wren liked this hour better than any other in the day. At dawn the dead were not yet a civic problem. Nobody wanted anything from them. Nobody was disputing a will or demanding a second autopsy or standing in her office doorway with the particular expression of a person about to ask her to change a word.

"Thomas Aldous," she said, and slowed.

The stone was modest, gray granite going soft at the corners with lichen, the way stones did here after twenty years of salt air. Thomas Aldous, 1931–1974. She had not certified him — he'd died long before her time, and the file, when she'd once pulled it out of idle curiosity, had been thin as a communion wafer. Heart failure, the old coroner had written, in handwriting so confident it dared you to question it. Wren had questioned it anyway, privately, over coffee, the way she questioned most things in Ashcombe. A forty-three-year-old man with no cardiac history, found at the bottom of his own stairs, and nobody had asked why there was a bruise on his shoulder shaped like nothing in particular.

She had let it go. That was the arrangement in a town like this: you let things go, and in exchange the town let you be the sort of person who noticed things without making a career of it.

"Rest easy, Tom," she murmured, and moved on, because there was no profit in standing still.

* * *

By the time she reached the middle rows, the fog had given up entirely and the light came in low and gold across the water side of the cemetery, catching the tops of the cypress trees that lined the seawall. Wren unbuttoned her coat, which was her way of acknowledging that the day had officially begun.

"Henry Pruitt," she said. "Cirrhosis, and I wrote it plain even though your brother offered me two hundred dollars to write 'natural causes,' which, Henry, I want you to appreciate the irony of, since cirrhosis is natural in the sense that gravity is natural."

Henry Pruitt had been a bootlegger's grandson and a generous tipper and a liar in all directions except the bar, where he was scrupulously honest about proof. She'd liked him. She'd written the truth anyway, and his brother had sulked at her for a year, and then the brother had died of the same thing and she'd written the same words, and somehow that had settled the account between them.

This was the part of her life she would have trouble explaining to anyone who didn't live in a town of four thousand souls at the edge of a cold sea. People assumed the loneliness of her work came from the bodies. It didn't. The bodies were fine. The bodies were the easiest company she kept — they wanted nothing, performed nothing, lied about nothing anymore. The loneliness came from the living, from being the person who stood at the exact point where a family's story about itself collided with the physical evidence, and from having to say, gently, over and over, across years: *I'm sorry, but that isn't what happened.*

She was good at it. That was the prideful thought she allowed herself each morning, right here, somewhere around row fourteen, walking among the people whose endings she had gotten right. She was good at it the way a lighthouse keeper is good at it — not glamorous, not thanked, but precise, and necessary, and very much alone on the rock.

"Dolores Finch," she said, tipping an imaginary hat toward the stone with the ceramic fawn beside it. "You told me yourself, the week before, that you weren't going to make it to spring, and I told you not to be morbid, and you were right and I was wrong, and I have never forgiven myself for underestimating you."

Dolores had laughed at that. From her deathbed. A dry little laugh, like paper turning.

* * *

At the far corner, past the yew tree that the town council had been threatening to remove since 1987 and never would, because half the town had been kissed under it at one point or another, Wren stopped at the newest stone. Three weeks old. The grass over it hadn't knit yet; you could see the seam in the earth like a scar that hadn't decided what it was becoming.

"Samuel Ireton," she read aloud, though she didn't need to. "Sixty-eight. Pancreatic cancer, diagnosed late, endured badly, ended well. Your daughter held your hand. I checked her pulse afterward, Sam, because she gripped hard enough that I worried, and she looked at me and said, 'He waited for me.' And I wrote in my report that he died at 4:12 in the morning, but what I actually believe is that he died at 4:11, and spent one minute being dead purely out of politeness."

She stood there a moment longer than the other graves warranted. Samuel had brought her tea during her own bad winter, two years back, when pneumonia had flattened her and there had been nobody else — no husband, no sister, no children, just a woman alone in a drafty house above the funeral office with a thermometer in her mouth and a cat who expressed concern mainly by sitting on her chest. Samuel had come by every day for nine days with tea and gossip and absolutely no pity, which was the only currency Wren accepted.

And then he'd died, and she'd signed his certificate, and that was the arithmetic of a town this size. Everyone you loved eventually crossed your desk. She'd known it when she took the job. Knowing it and living it were different animals.

"You were a decent man," she told the stone. "In a town full of decent men who are also hiding something, which is, I suppose, the human condition."

She turned back toward the gate.

* * *

The walk home took her along Harbor Street, past the shuttered cannery, past the bakery where Mrs. Calloway was already pulling the first loaves — the smell reached her a full block early, yeast and warmth, the smell of a town that believed tomorrow would mostly resemble today. Wren waved through the glass. Mrs. Calloway waved back with a flour-white hand and mouthed something that was either *good morning* or *your cat's in my window again,* and Wren decided it was safer not to investigate.

Her office sat above the old chandlery on Quay Lane, two rooms and a cold-storage annex that the town had grudgingly upgraded in 2009 after a state inspector used the phrase *frankly unacceptable* in writing. Wren climbed the outside stairs, fed the cat — a gray, judgmental creature named Verdict, whom she had acquired by accident when he wandered into the morgue one Tuesday and refused to leave, on the grounds, apparently, that a building where nobody argued was his natural habitat — and put the kettle on.

It was while the kettle was heating that she saw the letter.

It had been slid under the door sometime in the night, which was itself unusual. Ashcombe did not have a night mail culture. Ashcombe's mail arrived at ten-fifteen with Mr. Petch, who sorted it on his porch and delivered it in order of how much he liked you, a system everyone complained about and nobody changed. A letter under the door at five in the morning meant someone had walked up those stairs in the dark, in the fog, deliberately, and had not wanted to be seen doing it.

The envelope was heavy stock, cream-colored, unmarked. Her name was typed. Not handwritten — typed, on what she guessed was an actual typewriter, given the slight unevenness of the strike. Inside was a single sheet, also typed:

*Mrs. Voss. Or Dr. Voss. Forgive me — I don't know which you prefer.*

*Coroner,* she thought. *Neither. Coroner.*

*I need you to prove something. Not to find something — prove it. One fact, verified to your professional satisfaction. On March 14th of this year, according to the burial ledger of St. Brigid's Church, a death occurred. I am prepared to pay you triple your standard fee to establish that it has not happened yet.*

*Come to the Anchor Inn this evening, if you're willing. Ask for Mr. Ellery.*

*Burn this.*

Wren read it twice. Then a third time, standing in her kitchen with the kettle beginning its low climb toward whistling, while Verdict watched her from the counter he was not allowed on, with the expression of a creature who had seen many strange things enter this office and expected this one to end badly.

March 14th. Nine days away.

A death recorded in advance. Which was impossible, obviously. Ledgers recorded what had happened; that was their entire nature, their whole solemn contract with the past. Unless someone had made an error. Unless someone was playing a joke. Unless —

Unless someone knew something.

Wren set the letter on the table, weighted it with her teacup, and looked out the window at the harbor, where the fishing boats were going out in ones and twos, their lights still burning against the last of the dawn. Somewhere out there was Miller's Bend, and the tide, and the body waiting for her at nine o'clock, a man the net had caught, ordinary and explainable, a death with a cause and a date behind it, safely in the past tense where deaths belonged.

She thought about the ledger at St. Brigid's. She'd seen it once, years ago, helping Father Anselm find a record for an insurance claim. A great fat volume bound in green, a century of burials in a beautiful, patient hand. She remembered thinking, at the time, that whoever had kept it had loved the work — that the book had the composure of something tended rather than merely maintained.

She poured her tea. She drank it standing up, looking at the letter, doing the thing she did best, which was noticing.

Triple fee. A stranger who knew her name and her hours and her stairs. A date nine days out, written in ink in a church basement, claiming a death that hadn't happened.

Most people in Ashcombe would laugh and use the letter to light the stove. Wren Voss finished her tea, tucked the letter into the inside pocket of her coat — the pocket where she kept things she wasn't ready to think about — and picked up her bag for the walk to the morgue.

She would go to the Anchor Inn tonight. Of course she would. Curiosity had killed considerably more than cats in her experience, and she'd never once managed to treat it as a warning rather than an invitation.

But first, she had a drowned man to meet, and a sentence to write for him — two or three words, plain and true, that would follow him wherever he was going.

She locked the door behind her. Below, the fog was burning off the water, and the town of Ashcombe lay open in the morning light, decent and hidden and smiling, every grave in it accounted for.

Almost every grave.
