## 2. The Retainer

The bell above the door of Voss & Daughters — Coronial Services had a certain irony to it that Wren had never bothered to explain to anyone, mostly because there were no daughters and never had been. The sign was her father's joke, inherited along with the building, the embalming table she used as a desk, and the habit of talking to the dead as though they might answer.

"Closed," she called from the back room, where she was elbow-deep in paperwork for a man named Otis Prine who had died of natural causes at seventy-nine and was, even now, making her life difficult by having outlived everyone who could sign his forms.

"I'll wait," said a voice at the front. "I'm told you're very thorough."

Wren wiped her hands on a towel that had seen better decades and went out to see who was standing in her lobby at twenty past five on a Tuesday, when the fog came off the harbor thick enough to spread on toast.

He was pale. That was the first thing. Not sickly-pale, not frightened-pale, but the pale of paper kept too long in a drawer — the pale of something meant to be read but not seen. He stood with his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral he wasn't sure he'd been invited to, and he looked around her office the way people always did: at the framed certificates, the anatomical chart curling at one corner, the jar of pens that said FUNERAL HOME OF ASHCOMBE across them because her father had ordered two hundred by mistake in 1988 and she'd be buried with the last of them if she lived long enough.

"Mr.—?" she prompted.

"Ellery." He said it like it might not hold his weight. "Just Ellery is fine."

"Just Ellery," Wren repeated. "Well. Just Ellery, we're closed, but I'm a public servant in the way that a lighthouse is a public servant, which means I'm technically always on and personally always tired. What can I do for you?"

He sat down when she gestured, perching on the edge of the chair like it might be electrified. Up close, she revised her assessment. He wasn't old — thirty-five, maybe forty — but he had the particular exhaustion of someone who hadn't slept properly in weeks, the kind that sits behind the eyes rather than under them.

"You're the coroner," he said.

"For eleven years. Before that I was the coroner's daughter for twenty-six, which is worse, because at least the county pays me now."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one. "You're not what I expected."

"People say that." She leaned against her desk. "Usually they mean they expected a man. Sometimes they mean they expected someone nicer. Which was it?"

"The first." He set his hat on his knee and smoothed it once, twice, three times, a small compulsive circle. "Miss Voss, I need to hire you. Not — I want to be clear about this — not for anything you'd consider normal work."

"Mr. Ellery, I once certified a death caused by a man being struck by a frozen tuna. My definition of normal has a great deal of latitude."

"A fish?"

"Two hundred pounds, fell off a processing crane at the cannery. The report reads *blunt force trauma, aquatic in origin*, and I have never once regretted writing it." She folded her arms. "Try me."

He looked at his hat. He looked at the window, where the fog was pressing its face against the glass like a dog wanting in. Then he reached into his coat and produced an envelope — a long one, the kind invitations come in — and laid it on the desk between them with both hands, carefully, the way you'd set down something that might go off.

"That's nine hundred dollars," he said.

Wren did not touch it. Nine hundred dollars was triple her standard fee, and both of them knew it, and the fact that he knew it meant this was not going to be a simple matter of paperwork.

"Retainer," he said. "For one job. One question, really. You don't have to answer it today. You don't have to answer it ever, if you look into it and find there's nothing there. But I need someone official to look. Someone whose word carries weight in this town. Someone who—" he hesitated, choosing, "—who doesn't owe anybody anything."

"In Ashcombe?" Wren raised an eyebrow. "That's a tall order. Half the town owes the other half their Christmas turkeys."

"You don't," he said simply. "I checked."

*He checked.* Wren let that sit for a moment. It should have been flattering. It was, instead, the sort of thing that made the hair on her forearms take notice — the quiet, methodical attention of a man who had researched her the way she researched bodies. She found she didn't entirely mind. There was a professional courtesy in it.

"All right," she said. "Ask your question."

He took a breath, and she watched him gather himself the way she'd watched witnesses gather themselves on stand a hundred times — the moment before the truth comes out, or the lie dressed up as one.

"St. Brigid's Church keeps a burial ledger," he said. "It goes back over a hundred years. Every grave in Ashcombe's churchyard, every name, every date. Father Ambrose showed it to me last week — proudly, the way he shows everything, like the church invented paper." His hands had gone still on his knee. "There's an entry dated March fourteenth. This year. Next week."

"Burials get entered ahead sometimes," Wren said. "Pre-need arrangements. Not unusual."

"This isn't a pre-need arrangement. It's not a plot reservation or a service booking. It's a completed entry, Miss Voss. Name, date, cause. Written in ink that's faded like it's been sitting there fifty years." His voice dropped, and the fog outside seemed to lean closer to hear. "And the name in that entry — I need you to prove that the person it names is still alive. That no body matching it exists, that no death certificate has been filed, that nothing has happened. Yet."

"*Yet,*" Wren repeated.

"Yet," he agreed, and had the grace to look embarrassed about it.

She studied him. Pale, sleepless, careful hands, nine hundred dollars in an envelope, and a story about a church ledger written in impossible ink. In eleven years of coronial work she had heard stranger things — the frozen tuna, for one, and the year the Hollis twins both insisted they'd seen their late mother buying oranges at the market — but she had learned early that the strange stories divided cleanly into two kinds: the ones told by fools, and the ones told by frightened people. Fools embellished. Frightened people got precise.

Ellery was being very precise.

"What name?" she asked.

His jaw tightened. "I'd rather not say. Not yet. If I tell you the name, you'll go looking for the person, and if the person finds out they've been looked for—" He stopped. Started again. "Let's just say the name is why I'm paying triple. Discretion costs extra everywhere else in the world. Why not here?"

"Because here discretion usually means somebody's husband is in town who shouldn't be." She tapped the envelope without picking it up. "Here's my problem, Mr. Ellery. You're asking me to verify a non-event. That's not how death works. I certify what has happened; I don't certify what hasn't. There's no form for it. Believe me, I've wished there were."

"There's a form for everything in this town," he said. "That's rather the problem."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the foghorn at Miller's Bend sounded its low, patient note, the one she'd slept beside her whole life and only noticed when it stopped. Something about this man — the precision, the pallor, the way he'd said *yet* like a man setting down a stone he intended to build with later — snagged in her the way a hook snags in a sweater. You could ignore it. You'd only make it worse.

Professional curiosity, she told herself. That was all this was. The same itch that had made her open Otis Prine's file four times tonight looking for a signature that wasn't coming.

"One condition," she said. "I'll take the job. But I work in the open. I check records, I ask questions, I do it the boring way. If your mystery requires sneaking around St. Brigid's after dark, you've hired the wrong woman. I sneak badly. I knock into things."

For the first time, Ellery smiled — a real one, brief and crooked, gone almost before it arrived. "Boring is exactly what I'm hoping for. Boring means nothing's happened yet."

"There's that word again."

"It's my favorite word this month." He stood, and shook her hand, and his grip was cold and dry and brief. "March fourteenth. Nine days. If you find nothing — if you find the entry is a clerical error, a prank, water damage, anything — then keep the money and forget my face. I mean that sincerely. Forgetting my face would be a kindness to us both."

"And if I find something?"

The smile was gone. He put on his hat, and in the brim's shadow his face went back to being unreadable paper.

"Then you'll understand why I couldn't say the name," he said, "and you'll wish I'd paid you quadruple."

He let himself out. The bell rang, the fog swallowed him somewhere between her door and the streetlamp, and Wren Voss stood alone in her office holding an envelope full of money she already suspected she'd earned the hard way.

* * *

She didn't open the envelope right away. That was a discipline her father had taught her — *count it tomorrow, Wrennie, so the number has time to become real* — though she suspected what he'd really meant was *sleep on it before you spend it*. Instead she made tea, sat down across from Otis Prine's unfinished file, and found herself unable to read a single line of it.

Nine hundred dollars. Triple fee. A stranger who'd *checked* on her — who knew she owed nobody anything, which meant he knew about the debts other people had tried to give her. Sheriff Colm's favor calls. The mill owner's Christmas hams, delivered every December with a smile like a closed door. She'd refused the hams for six years running and he'd sent them anyway, and she'd donated them to the food pantry with a note that said *from your anonymous friend at the morgue*, which she considered the pettiest act of civic virtue in county history.

A man who knew all that, and wanted her specifically. Wanted her *clean*.

She got up, paced once around the embalming table — her thinking circuit, twelve steps, worn into her since she was nineteen — and then, because she was who she was, she went and got her coat.

St. Brigid's was a ten-minute walk, and the fog made it feel like walking through cold milk. The town moved around her in pieces: the Lamplighter's window glowing amber, Mrs. Tull's cat sitting on the war memorial with the smug authority of a creature that had outlived several committees trying to remove it. Wren nodded to the cat. The cat declined to acknowledge her, which she respected.

Father Ambrose was locking up when she arrived, fumbling his keys the way he always did, a big soft bear of a man who had buried half her clients and cried at most of them.

"Miss Voss!" He beamed at her through the fog. "Come to plan your funeral? You're young yet, but I admire forward thinking."

"Father. Do you know a Mr. Ellery? Pale fellow, looks like he sleeps in his hat."

Ambrose's keys stopped jingling. It was a small thing, a half-second pause, but Wren had spent eleven years watching faces for the half-second before the story caught up with itself, and she saw it plain.

"Ellery," he said slowly. "Yes. Yes, he was in — last week, maybe. Looking at the old records. Researchers come through sometimes. Genealogy folk." He smiled, and the smile was fine, and the half-second before it had not been fine at all.

"Genealogy," Wren said. "Of course. Well, I won't keep you. Actually — one thing. The burial ledger. The old one. Is it kept here, or in the archive?"

"Archive room, mostly. Locked, but Father—" he caught himself, laughed. "But *I* have the key, obviously. Why do you ask?"

"No reason. A question came up about a record. Professional thing." She patted his arm, which was like patting a friendly horse. "Goodnight, Father. Mind the fog."

"You too, Miss Voss. And Wren—" he called after her, and his voice had gone gentle, the voice he used at gravesides. "Whatever the question is. Some questions are better left as questions. The answers are heavy, and I've nowhere to store them."

She walked home through the milk-white streets and thought about that, and decided it was either profound wisdom or the vaguest warning she'd ever received, and that with Ambrose it was usually both.

* * *

At home she finally opened the envelope. Nine hundred dollars, in twenties, counted twice. Beneath the bills, folded small, was a slip of paper she hadn't noticed — a single line in the same careful hand that had addressed the envelope:

*The violet is the mark. Watch for the violet.*

Wren sat with that for a while. Then she got up, fetched the good bottle she kept for occasions she couldn't predict, poured two fingers, and drank to Mr. Ellery, wherever he was hiding.

"Watch for the violet," she said aloud to her empty kitchen. "Mysterious strangers who leave notes in envelopes. Frozen tuna. A church ledger with impossible ink." She turned her glass in the lamplight. "Eleven years, and the town finally gets interesting the week I'm caught up on paperwork."

Somewhere out in the fog, the horn at Miller's Bend sounded again — low, patient, counting the hours like it knew something she didn't.

Nine days until March fourteenth.

Wren finished her drink, set the glass in the sink, and went to bed knowing perfectly well she wouldn't sleep — and that first thing in the morning, boring or not, she was going to pay a visit to the archive room at St. Brigid's, whether Father Ambrose had anywhere to store the answers or not.
