## 4. Nine Days

Wren Voss did not sleep so much as negotiate with sleep, and by four in the morning the negotiations had collapsed entirely.

She sat at her kitchen table with the ledger open under a lamp she'd bought secondhand from the estate sale of a man who had died mid-crossword — the puzzle still half-finished, which Wren had always found either tragic or honest, depending on the day. Today it felt like an omen with a sense of humor. She had her own half-finished puzzle in front of her, and unlike the crossword man, she intended to finish hers or die trying.

The thought arrived before she could stop it. *Die trying.* She laughed once, a short ugly sound in the empty kitchen, and poured herself coffee that had been sitting since midnight and tasted like punishment.

Here was what she knew, laid out in her careful coroner's hand on three sheets of legal pad:

One. The burial ledger of St. Brigid's recorded deaths by date, name or initials, cause, and officiant. Standard. Boring. The kind of book that made even other clerics yawn.

Two. It was too clean. A century of burials, and not one ink blot, not one smudged thumbprint, not one entry where a tired hand had slipped. Real records were messy the way bodies were messy. This one was embalmed.

Three. Certain entries — she'd counted eleven so far — were written in ink that was faded *wrong*. Faded like paper that had aged fifty years waiting for its date to arrive. Entries dated years after the ledger's binding. Deaths scheduled like dental appointments.

Four. Beside each of those eleven entries, someone had drawn a small flower. A violet, five petals, done in a single confident stroke by a hand that had drawn it many times before.

And five — this was the one she kept circling back to, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth — the names weren't names. They were initials. Two letters, sometimes three, followed by a dash and a number she hadn't yet cracked.

She'd spent yesterday telling herself there was a boring explanation. There usually was. In twenty-two years as Ashcombe's coroner she had learned that ninety percent of mysteries dissolved under the harsh light of paperwork. The remaining ten percent dissolved under more paperwork. Nobody wanted to hear this at dinner parties, which was fine, because Wren didn't get invited to dinner parties, and had come to regard this as one of the genuine perks of her profession.

But this morning, with the fog pressing against the windows like something that wanted in, she stopped making excuses for the ledger and started treating it like what it appeared to be: a document written by someone who expected to be read by no one, and therefore wrote in code.

*Initials,* she thought. *Why would a burial record use initials?*

Because they weren't burial records. Because whoever wrote them wasn't recording the dead. They were *selecting* them.

Her pen hovered over the pad. She wrote the first coded entry out longhand:

**14 March 1903 — E.M. — 7 — violet**

Seven. She'd assumed it was an age, because the first time she'd seen it, she'd wanted it to be an age, because if it was an age then it belonged to some poor child buried a century ago and grief was at least a familiar country. But there was no E.M., age seven, in St. Brigid's ground. She'd walked the rows twice with a clipboard, checking stone against stone. No E.M. anywhere.

So what was seven?

She tried dates. Days of the week. Psalm numbers. She tried reading it upside down, which produced nothing except a headache and a brief, humiliating moment where she thought she'd discovered the word "L" repeated.

Then she tried it as a count.

Eleven coded entries. She listed them all, dashes and all:

E.M.—7. T.O.—12. R.K.—4. H.B.—9. S.D.—15. M.L.—6. J.P.—11. C.W.—8. A.F.—13. D.N.—5. W.V.—9.

She stared at the column of numbers. 7, 12, 4, 9, 15, 6, 11, 8, 13, 5, 9.

Nothing. Sums to nothing. Unless—

Unless the number wasn't part of the code. Unless the number was a *count*. A tally. An ordinal. Entry seven. Entry twelve. Entry four.

She pulled the full ledger across the table — the whole century of it, every legitimate burial, the ones with full names and causes and grieving families — and counted. The seventh burial recorded in the ledger's first year: **Edmund Marsh**, drowned, body never recovered from the quarry. The twelfth: **Thomas Okafor**, a laborer who had, according to the county gazette from 1911, simply walked off the mill property one evening and never been seen again. The fourth entry of 1919: **Ruth Kessler**, missing, presumed fled to the city, though she'd left behind her coat, her savings, and a sister who placed a notice in the paper every year for thirty years asking her to come home.

Wren set down her pen very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands are not steady enough to trust.

They weren't codes. They were *cross-references*. The coded entries pointed backward into the ledger itself, into the real burials, into the real missing. Every initial matched a person who had vanished without a grave. And the ledger had given them graves anyway. Neat, orderly, fictional graves, slotted into the record between honest mourners, invisible unless you went looking — and who went looking? Who cross-checked a church burial register against a hundred years of missing-persons notices?

A coroner, that's who. A coroner with insomnia and a bad feeling and nothing better to do at four in the morning than count graves.

"Hello," Wren said quietly to the kitchen. "You clever, horrible book."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the fog had thickened to the texture of milk, and somewhere down the street old Mrs. Prewitt's dog started up its nightly argument with absolutely nothing, which Wren normally found comforting. Tonight it sounded like a warning being wasted on the wrong audience.

She worked until dawn. It was slow going — the county archive only kept gazettes back to 1920 in any organized fashion, and before that she was relying on the historical society's scrapbooks, which were less an archive than a mood — but entry by entry, the pattern held. Every coded line resolved to a real disappearance. A fisherman in '31. A schoolteacher in '48 whose fiancé swore she'd left him at the altar, though she'd taken neither ring nor suitcase. A drifter in '66 whom nobody missed officially but whom the barkeep at the Harrow Inn had asked after for weeks, according to a letter he'd written his brother that the brother had thoughtfully preserved and the historical society had thoughtfully filed under "Miscellaneous, Emotional."

Eleven people. Eleven vanishings spread across a hundred and twenty years, each one explained away in its own era — ran off, drowned, moved on, must have — and each one given a quiet fictional burial in the same immaculate hand, marked with the same small violet.

Not predicted. *Recorded.* These had already happened. Whoever kept the ledger wasn't forecasting death.

They were keeping score.

* * *

By nine she had showered, dressed, and stood in front of her own bathroom mirror practicing a neutral expression, because there was one more thing she had to do and she needed her face to cooperate.

The eleventh entry. The last one. The one in ink that wasn't faded at all yet — fresh, recent, written within the last few months by the look of it.

**14 March — W.V. — 9 — violet**

She had checked the ledger's real burials against it the night before, expecting another match, another ghost from the files. Entry nine of the current year. There was no ninth burial yet; the year was young, the ground soft, only eight plots filled since January.

So the cross-reference didn't point backward. For the first time in a hundred and twenty years, the ledger pointed forward.

Nine days.

And the initials were hers.

She had known this since the church archive. She had carried it home like a coal in her chest. But knowing a thing and *reading* a thing were different animals, and now, with the pattern proven, with eleven confirmations stacked behind it like dominoes, she had to stand in her own bathroom and look at her own face and say it out loud, because Wren Voss believed that a fact unspoken was a fact only half examined, and half-examined facts killed people.

"W.V.," she told the mirror. "Wren Vivian Voss." Her middle name had been her grandmother's; her grandmother had been a hard woman from a hard family and Wren had spent forty-one years mostly ignoring it. Now it felt like the ledger knew her better than she'd let anyone else try.

The mirror said nothing, which was typical of mirrors.

"Nine days," she added, for thoroughness. "March fourteenth."

There was a version of this where she laughed. She could feel it hovering nearby, the hysterical option, the one where a stranger pays triple rate to prove a death hasn't happened, and the death turns out to be yours, and the whole thing is such a perfect cosmic joke that the only dignified response is laughter. She gave it a fair hearing. She'd always prided herself on being open-minded.

Then she brushed her teeth, because dread was no excuse for neglect, and went to work.

* * *

The morgue smelled of formalin and floor wax and, faintly, of the lemon drops Wren kept in the third drawer for witnesses who needed their hands busy. She liked the morgue. It was the only room in Ashcombe where everyone told the truth eventually.

She pinned the eleven names above her desk — Edmund Marsh through D.N., the drifter, Daniel Noyes, 1966 — and stepped back and looked at them the way she'd look at an autopsy board. Cause of death: unknown, unknown, unknown, unknown, unknown, unknown, unknown, unknown, unknown, unknown, pending.

Eleven unsolved disappearances. In a town the size of Ashcombe, eleven was not a statistic. Eleven was a *tradition*.

And here was the thing that had her pacing now, boots clicking on tile, the lemon drops rattling in the drawer when her hip bumped it: the explanations had all been *local*. Ran off. Moved on. The city swallowed them. Every single vanishing had been narrated away by neighbors, by clergy, by law enforcement, by the gazette — smoothly, quickly, and without any apparent appetite for digging. She'd read the old sheriff's reports. Half of them were closed within a week. One — Ruth Kessler's — contained the sentence "no evidence of foul play," followed by a case note in different ink: *"Family satisfied."*

Except Kessler's sister had placed newspaper notices for thirty years. That is not what satisfied looks like.

"Somebody smoothed all of this over," Wren said to the eleven names. "Somebody with a stamp and a shrug."

Which raised the question she'd been circling since the archive: who, in Ashcombe, held the stamp?

She thought about Sheriff Dane Colm, who had held his office for nineteen years the way an oak holds a hillside — seemingly permanent, mildly reassuring, impossible to imagine removed. Colm smiled easily. Colm remembered everyone's kids' names. Colm had once helped Wren carry a gurney up the Harrow Inn stairs during an ice storm and had joked the whole way, terrible jokes, the kind that made the work bearable, and she had liked him for it, genuinely, the uncomplicated liking of long acquaintance.

Which meant, if the last two days had taught her anything, he was exactly the right person to distrust, and exactly the wrong person to trust, and she had no way to know which without looking him in the face and lying to him a little.

She hated this part. She was bad at lying to people she liked. She was, if she was honest, only middling at lying to people she didn't.

She picked up the phone, put it down, picked it up again, and finally dialed the sheriff's office while staring fixedly at Edmund Marsh's name, as if a drowned man from 1903 could lend her strength, which, frankly, she'd take from wherever she could get it.

"Sheriff's office, Colm speaking."

"Dane. It's Wren Voss."

"Well, hey. Twice in one month. People will talk." Warm. Unhurried. The voice of a man with nothing on his desk but sunshine.

"I've got a records question. Church-related, actually. You'd know — does the diocese ever send people around to audit the old parish registers?"

A pause. Small. A quarter-second, maybe less. The kind of pause most people wouldn't catch, but Wren timed pauses for a living; she'd watched a thousand faces decide whether to tell the truth, and she knew the sound of a man choosing his footing.

"Audit? Can't say I've ever heard of one. Why, somebody sniffing around St. Brigid's?"

"Just a stranger in town. Hired me for some genealogy work." The lie came out smoother than she deserved. "Odd fellow. Paid too much. You know how it is."

"I surely do." And there it was — the easy laugh, the rehearsed-feeling deflection, a door closing so politely she almost thanked it. "Well, if he gives you trouble, you holler. Genealogists." Another little laugh. "Say, how's the knee? You favoring it with this weather?"

And just like that he was asking about her knee, and she was answering about her knee, and the conversation rolled on for another four minutes about weather and Mrs. Prewitt's dog and the price of roof repair, and it was pleasant, it was genuinely pleasant, Dane Colm was a pleasant man, and Wren hung up the phone and stood in the cold of the morgue with her heart going like a trapped bird.

Because she had never mentioned the stranger to Dane Colm. Not his name, not the retainer, not the errand. And a man with nothing to hide doesn't ask, *somebody sniffing around St. Brigid's?* — doesn't reach for the subject like a hand reaching for a dropped knife.

Above her desk, eleven names watched her with the patience of the long dead.

"Okay," Wren said to them. She took down a fresh sheet of legal pad paper and wrote at the top, in her best evidence-room capitals: **NINE DAYS.** Underneath it: **1. Find who keeps the ledger. 2. Find why Ellery cares. 3. Do not die stupidly.**

She looked at item three for a moment.

"I'm adding these as I think of them," she explained to Marsh, to Kessler, to Okafor and Noyes and the schoolteacher who never got a name in the file, only a footnote. "It's not a plan yet. It's a plan-shaped object. But I've worked worse cases from worse starting points, and I have one advantage nobody in a hundred and twenty years has had."

She tapped the ledger's ghost-list, all eleven confirmed matches.

"Nobody ever checked your math before. Somebody finally is."

Outside, the fog was lifting off the parking lot in slow gray rags, and down at the end of the street, past the diner and the feed store, the white steeple of St. Brigid's stood against the brightening sky like a finger pressed to lips.

Nine days. And the first person she needed to out-stare was the sheriff who smiled.
