## 6. Ellery Vanishes

The Saltmarsh Motel sat at the edge of Ashcombe like a man who'd wandered into the wrong funeral and decided to stay anyway — twelve rooms of peeling mint-green paint, a neon VACANCY sign missing its C so that it cheerfully announced *V A ANCY* to the highway all night long. Wren had certified two deaths there over the years, both natural, both guests who'd checked in for the crab festival and never checked out. She thought of the motel the way she thought of most things in Ashcombe: with fond exasperation.

She parked her coroner's wagon — a retired ambulance that still smelled faintly of antiseptic and someone else's bad decisions — in the lot and looked up at room nine.

Ellery's window was dark. The curtains were drawn tight.

That was the first thing. Mr. Ellery, pale as a candle stub, nervous as a cat on a griddle, had kept his curtains open day and night during their three meetings. He'd said it was because he liked to see who was coming. Wren had privately suspected it was because he liked to see himself reflected in the glass while he talked, the way some men check their hair. Either way: closed curtains were not his habit, and habits, in her experience, were the fingerprints people left on their own lives.

"Morning, Doc!" called Denny Pruitt from behind the office counter, where he was doing something violent to a coffee machine with a screwdriver. Denny ran the Saltmarsh the way his father had before him, which was to say he collected money, dispensed keys, and asked no questions whatsoever, a policy he considered the bedrock of hospitality.

"Morning, Denny."

"You here about the fella in nine? He checked out early. Real early. Like four-in-the-morning early." Denny gave the machine a final twist and it shuddered into a gurgle that sounded like drowning. "Didn't even want his deposit back. Who does that?"

"Someone in a hurry," Wren said.

"Or someone polite," Denny offered. "You want coffee? It's only mostly broken."

"I'll pass, thank you."

"Suit yourself. It's got character."

Wren crossed the gravel to room nine. The door was unlocked. That was the second thing.

She stood on the mat a moment, listening. The sea was doing its usual work against the breakwater, patient and repetitive, like a lawyer who knows he'll win eventually. No sound from inside. She pushed the door open with one gloved finger.

The room was empty.

Not *vacated* empty — she'd seen vacated rooms, rooms with the sad archaeology of departure: a forgotten phone charger, a ring in the bathtub, a Bible moved to the nightstand by someone who'd read Gideon's advertising. This room was *erased*. The bed was made with hospital corners so crisp they could have cut bread. The carpet had been vacuumed in perfect parallel lines. The bathroom gleamed. Even the little paper band around the toilet seat was fresh, which meant either Ellery had brought his own or —

No. Nobody traveled with toilet seat bands. Somebody had *replaced* it. Somebody had come in after him and restored this room to a state of such aggressive neutrality that it stopped being a room where a man had lived for eleven days and became a room where no man had ever been.

Wren had spent twenty years reading rooms. Dead bodies told you everything if you knew how to listen — the bruise that didn't match the story, the pill bottle refilled three days too soon, the dust shadow where a photograph used to hang. Rooms were just bodies that hadn't learned to lie yet.

This room was lying. Fluently. With confidence.

She worked it methodically, the way she'd work any scene, narrating aloud to herself because she'd long ago stopped being embarrassed about it. "Bed, made, recently — pillowcases still smell of starch. Dresser, wiped. Not dusty-wiped, *damp*-wiped. Someone came through with a cloth within the last—" she touched the dresser top, held her fingers to her nose "—six hours. Ammonia. Cheap cleaner. Denny stocks the same stuff under the sink in the office; I've seen the bottle."

The closet: empty hangers, evenly spaced, five of them, like soldiers at attention. The wastebasket: new liner, bone dry. Under the bed: nothing but a coin, a nickel, which she left exactly where it was because moving evidence before photographing it was a sin she'd stopped committing around the time she turned thirty.

The bathroom mirror had been cleaned so thoroughly it seemed apologetic. The shower curtain hung with mathematical precision. She opened the vanity cabinet and found — nothing. Not even the complimentary soap. Denny's soap was terrible, waxy, shaped like a scallop shell, and universally abandoned by guests. Every vacated room in this motel had at least one scallop soap in it, like a signature. Room nine had none.

Somebody had taken the soap. Somebody had taken the *soap*, and the matchbook, and — she checked — the local phone directory from the nightstand drawer, and the pad of Saltmarsh stationery, and every single pen.

"You," Wren informed the empty room, "are the tidiest crime I have ever met."

And then she saw the pillow.

She'd registered the made bed without really looking at it — hospital corners, duvet smoothed, pillows plumped. But the pillow nearest the door, the one a guest would sleep on if he slept facing the room, the way nervous men sleep, had something resting on it. Small. Flat. Purple-gray.

A violet.

Pressed flat between what felt like two layers of wax paper, delicate as a moth's wing, the petals gone translucent with age or care. It had been placed dead center, aligned with the pillow's seam, with the same maddening precision as everything else in the room.

Wren did not touch it. Her hand hovered an inch above it and stayed there, the way your hand hovers over a stove element you *know* is off but don't quite trust.

Because she knew this flower. She'd spent two nights with the St. Brigid's ledger open on her kitchen table, and beside certain entries — not all, only the ones whose dates hadn't arrived yet, only the ones still waiting — there was a small inked drawing in the margin. A five-petaled flower. She'd assumed it was a printer's ornament, some Victorian flourish the ledger's compilers had copied down the decades. She'd even found it charming, in the way you find a skull-and-crossbones charming when it's on a pirate flag in a children's book.

It was not charming. It was a signature. And now it was sitting on a motel pillow where a frightened man had paid her triple rate to prove a murder hadn't happened yet.

"Okay," Wren said to the room, and her voice came out steadier than she felt, which was the entire trick of her profession and, increasingly, of her life. "Let's think."

She thought. She took off her glasses, polished them on her sleeve, put them back on, and thought some more, because that was a ritual her mother had taught her and rituals kept the panic in its cage.

Fact one: Ellery had left at four in the morning, voluntarily, declining his deposit. Frightened men who flee at four in the morning do not stop to vacuum. Fact two: whoever cleaned this room had done it with professional thoroughness and local supplies. Fact three: they had missed nothing except — deliberately, pointedly, *theatrically* — the violet. You don't strip a room to the mattress seams and then leave a flower unless the flower is the message. Fact four: the flower matched the ledger marks.

Conclusion, unwelcome: Mr. Ellery hadn't simply run. He'd been *collected*. And whoever collected him wanted Wren to know it. Wanted her to walk into this scrubbed, silent, scallop-soap-less room and find the one object that would connect his disappearance to the book of scheduled deaths sitting in her kitchen drawer under the tea towels.

They weren't hiding from her. They were *corresponding* with her.

"That's either very good news," she told the pillow, "or very, very bad news, and my professional opinion is leaning hard toward the second one."

* * *

She drove back into town with the violet sealed in an evidence bag riding shotgun, belted in, because if she was going to be paranoid she might as well be thorough about it. The morning had thickened into proper Ashcombe weather, fog rolling in off the water in great gray blankets, swallowing the cannery roof, the church spire, the hills, until the town seemed to exist only wherever Wren happened to be looking, like a stage set assembled just ahead of her and struck just behind.

Her office above the pharmacy smelled of coffee and formalin, which she considered the two great pillars of civilization. She hung up her coat, fed the radiator, which clanked its gratitude, and sat down at her desk with the evidence bag in front of her.

The violet stared back. Or rather, it lay there being pressed and dead and small, and Wren stared at it, which amounted to the same conversation.

"Right," she said. "Options."

Option one: Ellery had panicked, packed, fled, and some hyper-tidy stranger had coincidentally deep-cleaned room nine and left a flower. Probability: roughly equivalent to the tide running backward.

Option two: Ellery had been taken by whoever maintained the ledger, and the flower was a warning to her — *stop asking questions, or your entry stops being a forecast.* Chilling, coherent, and it fit the facts like a glove fits a hand.

Option three — and this was the one she kept turning over like a stone to see what lived underneath — Ellery had been taken by whoever maintained the ledger, and the flower was not a warning at all. It was a *receipt*. Proof of service rendered. As if the disappearance itself were an entry in the book, completed on schedule, initialed with a bloom.

She got out her notes. Three meetings with Ellery, and she'd written everything down afterward, because a coroner who doesn't write things down is just a person with opinions. First meeting, Tuesday, the retainer: cash, triple fee, in an envelope that smelled faintly of machine oil. Second meeting, Thursday, the church steps: he'd asked her whether the ledger could be forged, whether ink could be aged artificially, whether paper could be made to look a century old. Good questions. Too good. Those were the questions of a man who already suspected forgery, which meant he already suspected the ledger wasn't prophecy at all but *planning*. Third meeting, Saturday, the diner booth by the window: he'd barely spoken. He'd watched the door. He'd watched the windows. When the waitress refilled his coffee he'd flinched hard enough to slosh it, and then he'd laughed at himself, high and thin, and said, "I'm not usually like this. I'm actually a very calm person. Ask anyone. Well. Don't ask anyone."

And then he'd slid a napkin across the table with a name written on it — Tomas Reeve, Harrow Inn — and said, "If anything happens to me, don't go to the sheriff. Go to the inn. Ask for the man who counts."

Wren had thought, at the time, that the poor man was simply unraveling. Fear did that; she'd seen it in witnesses, in widows, once in a judge. But now, sitting in her office with a pressed violet for company, the pieces rearranged themselves into a shape she didn't like. Ellery hadn't been unraveling. He'd been *leaving her a trail*. The questions about forgery. The watching of doors. The napkin. A man who suspects he's being hunted doesn't waste his last calm hours on small talk; he plants flags.

*Ask for the man who counts.*

She hadn't gone. She'd meant to, she'd told herself she would, but the weekend had intervened — a drowning at Coyle's Point, paperwork, Sheriff Colm stopping by with doughnuts and that easy smile of his, talking about the weather for forty minutes while somehow learning everything about her week and revealing nothing about his own. By Monday, Ellery's trail had gone cold in the ordinary way trails do, buried under the living.

Now it wasn't cold. Now it was a flower on a pillow, and flowers, unlike men, did not check out at four in the morning on their own.

Wren picked up the phone, dialed the county records office, and asked a bored clerk named Franny to pull every Ellery she could find — first name unknown, age fiftyish, soft hands, eastern accent, possibly false name entirely. Franny sighed the sigh of a woman whose coffee was getting cold and promised results by Thursday.

Then Wren pulled out the county map and drew a pencil line from the Saltmarsh Motel to the Harrow Inn. Eleven miles of coast road. Twenty minutes' drive. She looked at the line for a long time.

Every instinct she owned — and twenty years of death had given her excellent instincts, honed like scalpels, occasionally inconvenient at dinner parties — told her that the Harrow Inn was the center of whatever web had caught Mr. Ellery. Every other instinct, the older ones, the ones from before she was a coroner, from when she was just a girl in this town who'd learned that the world could take a person and give you back nothing but questions — those instincts told her to burn the map, move to a city, and take up stamp collecting.

She put the map away. She made a fresh pot of coffee. She wrote in her notebook, in her careful coroner's hand:

*Subject: ELLERY, first name unknown. Status: missing, presumed collected. Evidence: one (1) violet, pressed, wax paper. One (1) napkin, name of T. Reeve, Harrow Inn. Note: room sanitized by unknown party with local materials. Note: flower matches ledger marginalia. Conclusion pending.*

Then, after a moment, she added:

*Note to self: you are not afraid. You are furious. These feel similar. They are not.*

That was the truth of it, she realized, setting down the pen. Somewhere between the church archive and this morning, the fear had undergone a chemical change, the way grief does, the way standing water does. She was still afraid — her hands hadn't entirely stopped their fine tremor since she'd read her own initials in a century-old ledger — but underneath the fear, something had hardened into resolve, dense and unglamorous and permanent. Mr. Ellery had come to her because she was the one person in Ashcombe whose job was to stand over the dead and say *this is what actually happened.* He'd paid her triple. He'd trusted her with the worst thing he knew.

And she had let him check out alone.

"Not again," she said quietly, to the violet, to the fog pressing at the window, to nobody. "Whatever you people are doing in this town, whoever you are, however long you've been doing it — you picked the wrong coroner to send flowers to."

The radiator clanked. Downstairs, the pharmacist's radio played something tinny and cheerful. The fog swallowed the street whole.

Wren capped her pen, pocketed the evidence bag, and reached for her coat.

Eleven miles of coast road. Twenty minutes. And somewhere at the end of it, a man who counts.

She was halfway down the stairs when the phone rang — the office line, the one that only rang for official business. She picked it up on the fourth ring, breath half-held.

"Wren?" Sheriff Colm's voice, easy as Sunday, but with something threaded through it she couldn't name. "You'll want to come down to Miller's Bend. Tide brought something in."

She closed her eyes. Outside, the fog waited, patient as ever.

"On my way," she said.
