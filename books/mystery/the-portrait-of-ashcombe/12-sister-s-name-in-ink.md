## 12. Sister's Name in Ink

Wren Voss had a rule about coffee: it was not to be trusted before the second cup. The first cup was a formality, a handshake between her and the morning. The second was where actual thought began.

She was halfway through the second cup, sitting at her kitchen table with the photocopied pages of St. Brigid's ledger spread out like a losing hand of cards, when she realized she'd been avoiding something.

She'd been avoiding it for two days, actually, which was impressive, because Wren prided herself on being the kind of woman who ran toward unpleasant things. She ran toward bodies. She ran toward grieving families who wanted to yell at someone and had settled on her. She had once run toward a wasp nest because a neighbor's kid was crying about it, and she still bore the scar on her left forearm as proof that courage and intelligence were not the same organ.

But this morning, with the fog pressing against the kitchen window like a cat wanting in, she admitted it plainly: she had not yet looked up the initials of the man who killed her sister.

Not because she didn't know them. God help her, she knew them by heart. D.E.M. — Dale Everett Marsh. She could have carved them into stone. For eleven years those three letters had lived in her head the way a splinter lives under skin: small, ignorable on good days, maddening on bad ones, always there.

No, she hadn't looked him up in the ledger because some part of her — the part that had spent two decades learning to be careful with hope, the way you learn to be careful with eggs — knew what she would find if she looked.

And she wasn't ready to know whether she'd been right.

* * *

Her sister's name had been Juniper. June, to everyone who loved her, which was most of Ashcombe, because June Voss was the kind of person who learned your dog's name before yours and greeted both of you accordingly. She'd been twenty-six. She'd worked at the flower shop on Corben Street and kept a jar of wild violets on the counter every spring, "because they're free and they don't apologize for being small," which was June all over.

The crash happened on the Harrow Road, on a night in October so dark the streetlights seemed to have given up as a matter of policy. A car came around the bend at — the reconstruction said — somewhere north of eighty miles an hour, took June's little blue hatchback broadside, and did not stop.

They found the car two days later, abandoned in the long-term lot at the ferry terminal, burned nearly past recognition. But not quite. Wren, who had just finished her certification and was working her first year as deputy coroner, identified the VIN herself, kneeling on wet asphalt with a flashlight in her teeth while a state trooper hovered behind her saying *ma'am, you really shouldn't touch that.*

Dale Everett Marsh. Thirty-one years old. Two prior DUIs, both pled down to reckless driving, both thanks to a father who owned half the timber rights in the county and a lawyer who billed by the syllable. The case against him was circumstantial — no witnesses willing to swear they'd seen him driving, no blood in the burned wreck, a rental agreement that conveniently named his cousin. The DA, facing an election, faced it right into the ground. No charges. Not even an inquest worth the name.

Wren had sat in the courthouse parking lot afterward for forty minutes, hands on the wheel, doing absolutely nothing, which was the hardest thing she had ever done. Then she drove home, hung up her coat, and decided that if the law would not look closely at the dead, she would make looking closely at the dead her entire life.

People assumed it was grief that made her a coroner. It wasn't, exactly. It was arithmetic. June deserved one honest set of numbers in this world, and nobody else was going to do the math.

* * *

So now, at her kitchen table, with the fog thick enough to slice, Wren laid the ledger photocopies flat and smoothed them with both hands, the way she smoothed a sheet over a body — a gesture so habitual she caught herself doing it and almost laughed, and then almost cried, and settled for drinking her coffee.

The ledger's cipher had come apart for her the way old locks sometimes do: not with force but with patience. Dates were real. Names were initials. And beside each entry, in ink so faded it might have been written by a ghost with a fountain pen, a small pressed violet was drawn — or rather, the same violet, drawn again and again across a century, with the same slight asymmetry in the left petal, the same artist's tic. Whoever drew those flowers had drawn them all. One hand. One hundred years.

That thought alone should have kept her up nights. It had, in fact. But there was a second column she hadn't fully decoded until last night, sitting up with a magnifying glass and a pot of tea gone bitter from neglect. Tiny annotations, tucked into the margins like marginalia in a monk's psalter. Most entries carried a single word in that same antique hand:

*Corrected.*

Some carried nothing. Some carried a question mark, faint as breath on glass. And a handful — maybe one in ten — carried a date. A date after the death date. A date when, presumably, whatever the ledger scheduled had actually been done.

Wren had built a chart on butcher paper taped to her pantry door. Disappearances going back sixty years, matched against ledger initials, matched against correction dates. The fit was too good. Eleven unsolved vanishings in Ashcombe's recent memory, and eleven entries marked *corrected*, each within a year of its scheduled date. Whatever this book was, it wasn't prophecy. Prophecy missed. This thing hit.

Which meant the ledger was not predicting deaths.

It was keeping appointments.

She had sat with that realization for a long time last night, feeling the floor of her understanding tilt beneath her like a boat in weather. And underneath the vertigo, quieter and more dangerous, another thought had surfaced, the one she'd been dodging for two days:

*If the ledger keeps its appointments — then check the year after October.*

*Check 1999.*

* * *

She found it at 7:14 in the morning, though she would later remember it as taking hours, because time does that when the world rearranges itself.

There it was, on a page dated October 1988 — wait. She frowned. Went back. Checked her own chart. The crash had been 1998; she'd misread the faded numeral, a common fault of the photocopy. October 1998. Entry: *J.V.* — and Wren's whole body went cold, because for one insane heartbeat she thought the ledger had scheduled her sister, had marked June the way it had marked Wren herself, and the grief and the fury rose in her throat like floodwater—

But no. Below J.V., in smaller letters, a second line. *D.E.M.*

Scheduled: March 1999.

And in the margin, in that patient antique hand, the word she now knew better than her own name:

*Corrected.*

With a date. April 12th, 1999.

Six months after the crash. Six months after the DA declined to press charges. Six months after Wren had sat in that parking lot doing nothing.

Someone had done something.

* * *

Wren stood up. Sat down. Stood up again. Paced the length of her narrow kitchen four times, which in her house was less pacing than slow rotation, and finally put her palms flat on the table and made herself breathe the way she told hysterical widows to breathe. In for four. Out for six. The dead deserve a steady hand, her first mentor used to say, and so do the living, and right now you are neither, so breathe anyway.

Dale Everett Marsh.

She remembered the last time she'd seen him. Not in court — he'd barely shown in court, arriving once in a gray suit that fit him like an apology he refused to finish. The last time was eight months after the crash, at the gas station on Delver Street. He'd come out of the store with a paper bag and found her filling her tank, and for a moment they'd simply looked at each other across two pumps, and he had said — she would never forget it, the sheer unbelievable gall of it — "I'm sorry about your sister."

Not *I didn't do it.* Not *you've got the wrong man.* Just: sorry. The way you'd say it about weather.

And she had said nothing, because anything she said could be used, and she was a coroner, not a fool, and she had driven home shaking and cleaned her entire kitchen at midnight like a woman possessed.

Now she pulled the county records she'd requested weeks ago — routine follow-ups, she'd told the clerk, estate matters — and found what she'd half known she'd find. Dale Everett Marsh, born 1967. And then nothing. No death certificate. No property transfer. No tax record after spring 1999. In a county that documented everything down to who'd paid their dog license late, Dale Marsh had simply stopped existing.

An unsolved disappearance. One more name on the long list Wren had pinned to her pantry door. One more entry marked *corrected.*

Except this one wasn't a stranger. This one was hers.

* * *

The strange thing — the thing she kept turning over like a stone with something alive under it — was that she didn't feel glad.

She had imagined this moment, in the early years. She'd be lying if she denied it. There were nights, especially the first few Aprils, when she'd lain awake and constructed elaborate fantasies in which justice arrived for Dale Marsh wearing whatever face the universe chose, and she had never once felt ashamed of those fantasies, only tired afterward, the way you're tired after carrying something heavy up stairs.

But now that it had apparently happened — now that some hand, some violet-drawing, ledger-keeping hand, had done the thing the law would not — she felt as if the ground had been pulled out from under twenty years of her own architecture.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't make balance: if someone had corrected Dale Marsh, then June's death had mattered to someone besides Wren. Someone in Ashcombe had loved June enough — or hated Dale enough, or believed in something enough — to act. And that meant that for eleven years, while Wren had walked her cemetery reciting names, proud of her clean reports and her steady hands and her lawfulness, someone else had been walking a different path entirely, doing the other work, the work she'd told herself was impossible.

And that meant every other entry in this ledger — every disappearance she'd charted, every *corrected* — belonged to somebody's June. Somebody's sister. Somebody's unpunished crime, answered in the dark by strangers who never signed their work.

It was either the most monstrous thing she'd ever uncovered or the most merciful, and the fact that she genuinely could not tell which frightened her more than the entry bearing her own name ever had.

"Okay," she said aloud, to the kitchen, to the fog, to nobody. Her voice sounded strange in the empty house, rougher than she intended. "Okay. Think."

Think. All right. Facts first. That was the discipline; that was the trade.

Fact: Dale Marsh vanished in April 1999, consistent with the ledger's correction date.

Fact: whoever maintained the ledger in 1999 was maintaining it still — the March 14th entry with her own initials was in the same hand, or near enough. Which meant either remarkable institutional continuity or something worse.

Fact: the ledger had scheduled her death nine days out, and then Mr. Ellery — pale, nervous, triple-fee Ellery — had hired her to prove it hadn't happened yet, and then vanished from his motel room leaving nothing but a pressed violet on the pillow. The same violet. The same left-petal asymmetry.

She'd told herself Ellery had fled. Paid his fee, spooked himself, run. It was the comfortable reading.

But if the Circle — if that's what this was, a circle, a society, a *something* with a century of practice — if they had corrected Dale Marsh for June, then they were capable of correcting anyone. Including a pale stranger who asked inconvenient questions about March 14th.

Wren's stomach dropped slowly, like an elevator whose cable had begun to sing.

Ellery hadn't run. Or if he had run, someone had helped him pack. Either way, the violet on that pillow hadn't been a goodbye. It had been a receipt.

* * *

She needed air. She needed to move. Twenty years of habit said: when the mind snarls, walk the cemetery, say the names, let the stones do their quiet work. So she buttoned her coat, pocketed the photocopies without quite deciding to, and stepped out into the fog.

Ashcombe at dawn was a town underwater. Gulls argued somewhere above the harbor, invisible. The bakery on Corben Street was already breathing warm yeast into the grey, and old Mrs. Tull was sweeping her step with the aggressive rhythm of a woman who considered fog a personal insult. Wren nodded to her. Mrs. Tull nodded back with the exact degree of warmth required by neighborliness and not one degree more, and Wren thought, absurdly, *do you know? Do any of you know? Have you all been living inside this book your whole lives?*

The cemetery gate was cold under her hands. She walked the rows without needing to read the stones, her boots finding the paths her feet had memorized, and stopped where she always stopped.

June's stone was modest granite, the way June had been modest everything. *JUNIPER VOSS, 1972–1998. SHE KEPT THE FLOWERS.* The town had insisted on the epitaph; Wren had only checked the spelling.

"I found him," Wren said quietly. It felt ridiculous, talking to granite, and she did it anyway, because the ridiculous things are usually the true ones. "In a manner of speaking. I found what happened to him. I think." She crouched, brushed a curl of moss off the base of the stone with her thumb. "Somebody got there first, June. Nineteen ninety-nine. Before I even knew to ask."

The fog drifted. Somewhere a crow made its opinion known.

"And here's the part I can't figure," she went on, because honesty was a discipline too, "is how I'm supposed to feel about it. Twenty years I wanted this. And now it turns out wanting it makes me one vote in favor of the thing that's scheduled to kill me in nine days. That's a hell of a ballot box, isn't it?"

The stone said nothing, being stone. But standing there, Wren felt the shape of the next question arrive, the way answers do — sideways, unwelcome, inevitable.

Whoever corrected Dale Marsh in April 1999 had been a member of this circle. Had held a chair, drawn a violet, kept the book. And that person — or that person's successors, because the hand in the ledger never aged — had watched Wren for twenty years. Watched her become a coroner. Watched her bury herself in the dead. And had never once told her. Had let her sit in that courthouse parking lot doing nothing, had let her scrub her kitchen at midnight, had let her build her whole life on the belief that no one was coming.

Why keep her ignorant for two decades and then schedule her death?

Unless the ignorance had been the point. Unless watching her was the point. Unless—

Unless they hadn't been waiting for her to stop asking questions.

They'd been waiting for her to start.

* * *

Wren straightened, knees cracking in protest — fifty-one was a rumor she intended to deny publicly — and looked out over the cemetery, the rows of stones dissolving into white, every grave by name, every name accounted for except the ones that weren't anywhere at all.

Eleven disappearances. Eleven corrections. And somewhere in this town, or beneath it, or behind one of its pleasant painted doors, the people who kept that book were going about their mornings. Buying bread. Sweeping steps. Nodding the exact degree required.

One hand, unaging, across a hundred years. She'd written that in her notes last night and moved past it, because there had been larger horrors. But now, standing in the fog with her sister's name at her feet, the smaller horror caught up with her.

Ledgers get copied. Ledgers get rebound. But handwriting — handwriting is a person. The violet with the crooked left petal was a person. And people do not draw the same flower for a century unless they are very disciplined, very well-trained, or very much not people at all.

Every record has a source. Every copy has an original. And if the original hand belonged to someone who had been writing since 1902 —

Then the book wasn't the mystery. The book was just the pen.

Wren turned for home, and her eye snagged, as it always did, on the far edge of the cemetery, where the oldest stones leaned together like conspirators — and beyond them, half-lost in fog, the dark gabled silhouette of the Harrow Inn, its windows unlit, its sign creaking on its bracket with a sound like a slow heartbeat.

She had driven past it ten thousand times. Everyone had. It was furniture; it was background; it was the inn where salesmen slept and nobody local ever stayed.

She thought of the guestbook she'd once glimpsed behind the desk, years ago, leather-bound and enormous, and of the keeper — Tomas, the town called him, Tomas Reeve — who had looked exactly the same at her sister's funeral as he looked today.

Exactly the same.

Wren stood in the fog a long moment, her breath and the mist becoming indistinguishable, and felt the last certainty of the morning go out of her like a tide.

"Well," she said to June's stone, softly. "I think I know where the hand lives."

She started walking. Not home.
