## 15. The Counting Man

Wren Voss had performed four hundred and eleven autopsies in her career, and she could count on one hand — a hand missing two fingers, if she was honest about it — the times she had slept well afterward. The night at Harrow Inn had not joined that short list.

She had not slept so much as hovered above sleep, the way fog hovers above the harbor: present, gray, waiting for something to burn through it. At 3 a.m., footsteps had passed her door. They had paused. She had counted the silence — eleven seconds, twelve — before they moved on. And inside the wardrobe, scratched into the old cedar panel where no honest guest would ever look, she had found tally marks. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Grouped in fives like a prisoner's calendar or a schoolteacher's arithmetic, marching down the wood in neat columns, some faded to ghosts, some still sharp enough to catch a fingernail.

She had not added them up. Some part of her understood, with the animal clarity she usually reserved for corpses, that adding them up would be a kind of answer, and answers in Ashcombe had a way of arriving with their shoes off, quiet, already standing behind you.

So instead she made tea.

The Harrow Inn's kitchen at dawn was the sort of room that seemed designed by someone who believed comfort was a moral failing. Stone floor, iron stove, a table scrubbed so many times its grain had gone shy. Wren sat wrapped in her coat with a chipped mug of tea that tasted faintly of iron — everything in this town tasted faintly of iron, which she supposed was fitting — and watched the light come up over the mill pond through a window so old its glass rippled like water.

That was when she saw him.

He came down the back stairs with a lamp still lit in his hand, though the sun was well up, and he moved the way old men move who have decided long ago that hurrying is a form of vanity. He was small. That surprised her. She had expected, from the footsteps, from the tallies, from the whole accumulating weight of the last nine days, someone large. This man was narrow and dry as a winter reed, with white hair combed with great care and hands that looked like folded paper.

He set the lamp on the sideboard, noticed her, and did not startle.

"Miss Voss," he said. "You're up early."

"You know my name."

"I know everyone's name." He said it the way another man might say *I know where the spoons are kept* — a plain statement of household fact. "It's my business, knowing names. Tea's bitter this morning. The water's been tasting of the pipes again. Let me make you a fresh pot."

And here was the thing Wren would turn over later, turning it like a stone in her pocket looking for the soft underside: he sounded *kind*. Not menacing, not sly, not performing innocence the way Sheriff Colm performed his easy smile. Kind the way a doctor is kind when he's about to tell you the scan came back wrong. Kind because there is nothing left to be gained by cruelty.

"I didn't hear you come down," she said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "I expect you didn't."

He filled the kettle from the pump, set it on the stove, and took down two cups — not one, two — as though he'd known she'd be sitting in his kitchen at dawn, which, she thought with a fresh crawl of cold along her spine, perhaps he had.

"You're Mr. Reeve," she said. "Tomas Reeve. You've run this inn forty years."

"Forty-one in the spring." He arranged the cups with a small fussiness that was almost endearing. "My father ran it thirty years before me, and his mother before him, though she called it a boarding house and charged extra for baths. People say I haven't changed the place. I tell them changing things is how you lose them."

"There are photographs," Wren said. "In the historical society. Photographs of the Harrow from 1902. There's a man standing on the porch."

The kettle began its low gathering rumble toward a boil. Reeve did not look at her.

"A big fellow," Wren went on, watching him. "White-haired. Same face. Same careful part in the hair. I checked the date twice, because I assumed I'd misread it. I don't misread things, Mr. Reeve. It's the one professional skill I have never once faked."

Now he looked at her, and his eyes were pale gray and very clear, and what lived in them was not guilt. It was tiredness. It was the tiredness of a man who has carried a heavy thing up and down the same stairs for so long that his shoulders have grown around it, and who has stopped expecting anyone to offer to help.

"You counted the photographs," he said. "Did you also count the marks in your wardrobe?"

The question landed in the kitchen like a dropped glass. Wren kept her voice level, mostly. "You know about those."

"I know about everything in this house. It's my house." He poured the tea when the kettle sang, and pushed the good cup toward her — the one without the chip. "Four hundred and six," he said quietly. "If you were wondering. Four hundred and six groups of five, and three besides. I started the first column myself, in 1961, when I took over from my mother. Before that it was other hands. Before that, other hands again. We are not an imaginative family, Miss Voss. We are a faithful one. There is a difference, and the difference has cost this town more than anyone will ever say out loud."

"Counting what?"

He sat down across from her, folding himself onto the hard chair with a wince, and for a moment he looked exactly like what he appeared to be: an old innkeeper having a morning chat, the kind of man who would tell you the radiator in room six knocked and to thump it twice. The ordinariness of him was worse than menace. Menace she knew how to fight. Ordinary kindness, sitting across a kitchen table offering her tea, was a different problem entirely.

"Counting nights," he said. "Nights when someone in this town needed watching. Nights when a name in a certain book grew close to its date, and we wanted to be sure the person slept soundly right up until they didn't." He sipped his tea. "I passed your door last night. I stood there a while. I'm sorry if it frightened you. I was listening to see whether you were sleeping, and you weren't, and I thought — well. I thought, *she counts too.* Coroners count. It's why you were always going to find us, eventually. We only ever hid from people who didn't pay attention."

Wren set down her cup so her hand wouldn't betray her. "Then let's not pretend. You know why I'm here. A man named Ellery hired me—"

"Yes."

"—to prove that a death written in St. Brigid's ledger hasn't happened yet. My death. March 14th. Nine days from now."

"Eight now," said Reeve gently. "Days go faster than you'd think. Especially these ones."

"And you're telling me you stand outside doors counting nights, and you keep tally marks in wardrobes like some Victorian ghost, and you want me to believe—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and she pressed on anyway. "What do you want me to believe, Mr. Reeve? That it's all bookkeeping? That my name in that ledger is just... arithmetic?"

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a gull argued with the chimney, and somewhere down the lane the ferryman's engine coughed awake, and the ordinary sounds of Ashcombe going about its morning made the kitchen feel, absurdly, safe.

"What I want," he said finally, "is for you to stop."

"Stop?"

"Stop digging. Go home. Lock your door — not that it matters, but it will make you feel better, and feeling better matters, at our age. Or yours, anyway." He smiled, and the smile was so genuinely warm that it frightened her more than anything Colm had ever done with his teeth. "Miss Voss, I have read every entry in that ledger since 1961. I have written some of them. I have stood where you're standing — figuratively, my knees couldn't manage literally — and I am telling you, as one counter to another: whatever you think you'll find at the bottom of this, it isn't what you hope. There's no villain down there. Villains are simple. What's down there is older than villains, and sadder, and it does not stop because a clever woman asks it nicely."

"Then it should stop because I ask it *unkindly*."

"Oh, I don't doubt you'd try." He nodded at her with something that looked horribly like approval. "You've got your sister's stubbornness."

The words went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. Wren stood so fast her chair skidded. "You leave her out of—"

"Her name is in the ledger too," Reeve said softly. "Not as a judgment. As a grief. There's a page near the front — you haven't found it yet, you're too busy being brave in basements — where we write the ones we failed. The ones the law failed first and we failed second. Your sister is on that page, Miss Voss. In my handwriting. I wrote her in the year after the crash, and I have thought of her every March since." His eyes glistened, and Wren realized with a lurch that the tears were real. "Do you imagine I liked writing your name on the other page? I have known you since you were a girl coming in here with your father for Sunday dinner, stealing sugar cubes when you thought nobody saw. I saw. I always see. That's the whole curse of me."

Wren stood there shaking, half fury, half something dangerously close to belief, and the tea steamed between them like a small truce neither had signed.

"Why tell me any of this?" she managed. "If you wanted me to stop, terrifying me would work better than kindness. Kindness makes people curious."

"It does," he admitted. "It's a flaw in my character. My mother warned me." He rose, slowly, joints announcing themselves, and crossed to the window where the light was strengthening over the water. "I'm telling you because in eight days, either your name gets a line drawn under it, or it doesn't, and I have discovered in my long and tedious life that people deserve to know which mornings might be their last. It's a courtesy. The Circle has forgotten most of its courtesies, but I keep mine. Someone has to remember why we started."

"The Circle," Wren repeated. "The Violet Circle."

Reeve went very still — not the stillness of guilt but of a man hearing a word spoken aloud that had lived only in whispers for a century, the way a name sounds strange in your own mouth.

"You've been in the archives," he said.

"I've been everywhere. I'm a coroner. Dead ends don't scare me; I live with one professionally."

Despite everything — despite the wardrobe, the footsteps, the photograph from 1902 — the old man laughed. It was a real laugh, rusty and brief, and it humanized him so completely that Wren felt the ground shift under her certainties. Monsters did not laugh at coroners' jokes. Monsters did not steal sugar cubes for little girls and remember it sixty years later.

"Go home, Miss Voss," he said, when he'd recovered. "Please. Take the ferry tomorrow and don't come back, and in eight days you'll read about a tragic accident that befell some other poor soul, and you'll drink your tea, and you'll grow old, and you'll never have to know whether we were right about anything." He held her gaze, and his pale eyes were wet and steady. "Because I will tell you the worst of it, freely, as a gift: sometimes we *are* right. That's the trap of it. If we were always wrong, someone would have burned that ledger a hundred years ago. It's the times we're right that keep the chairs filled."

"And if I don't go?"

He looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something she hadn't expected. He reached into his waistcoat pocket and laid something on the table between them: a pressed violet, flat and papery, purple gone soft with age, its stem taped to a slip of paper bearing a date decades old.

"Then you'll need to understand what you're walking into," he said. "And I'd rather you learned it from me than from Chair Four."

"Chair Four?"

But Reeve was already moving toward the stairs, lamp in hand, unhurried, an old man climbing toward an old room, pausing at the bottom step to say, without turning around:

"Sheriff Colm keeps files, Miss Voss. Everyone assumes he keeps them in his office, because everyone assumes sheriffs are honest about where they hide things. Count on nothing in this town except that someone is always counting." He climbed two steps, stopped again. "And Miss Voss — the tea. Drink it while it's hot. Bitterness is bearable warm. Cold, it's just truth."

His footsteps went up the stairs — she counted them, fourteen, fifteen — and paused overhead, directly above her, for exactly eleven seconds. Twelve.

Then they moved on, and Wren Voss sat alone in the kitchen of the Harrow Inn with two cups of tea, one pressed violet, and the growing, dreadful understanding that the kindest man in Ashcombe had just told her, in the gentlest possible language, that her sheriff was a murderer — and that the only person in town she might have trusted was the one who'd promised to kill her in eight days.

She drank the tea. It was bitter.

She was already planning how to break into the sheriff's office.
