## 23. Chair Four

The trouble with joining a secret society of vigilantes, Wren discovered in her first week as Chair Twelve, was the paperwork.

There wasn't any. That was the trouble. No minutes, no bylaws beyond the three rules Reeve had recited to her in the crypt — unanimous vote, violet proof, no children — and no record of anything except the ledger itself, which lived in a locked drawer beneath St. Brigid's and which Wren was now, absurdly, trusted not to steal.

She had stolen it anyway. Or rather, she had borrowed it, carefully, on a Tuesday morning while the rest of Ashcombe slept off its opinions about her funeral. She sat at her own kitchen table with the ledger open under a lamp, a pot of coffee going cold beside her, and did what she had done for twenty years with other people's documents: she read it like a coroner.

"You look terrible," said the cat, or would have, if cats could talk. His name was Biscuit, he had adopted her two winters ago on the grounds that she was clearly incapable of feeding herself without supervision, and he was currently sitting on the margin of 1931, judging her.

"One of us has to," Wren told him. "I'm dead. Dead women get a pass."

Biscuit blinked slowly, unconvinced, and put one paw directly on the entry for March 14th. Her entry. *W.V.* The ink had gone brown with age, though the paper around it was white as milk. Eleven years before she'd ever heard of the Violet Circle, someone had written her name in this book and waited.

She moved his paw. He forgave her, mostly.

Here is what Wren knew now, laid out in her head like organs on a steel tray:

The Circle had twelve chairs. She had seen them — masked, robed, their voices flattened by the crypt's stone into something sexless and ancient. Chair One presided; that was Reeve, though he called himself only the Keeper when he wore the mask. Chairs Two through Twelve voted. She was Twelve. The newest chair always was; Mrs. Aldous had let that slip before clamping her mouth shut like a purse snapping closed.

And somewhere among those eleven masked figures who were not Reeve and not her sat Chair Four — the member who, according to the rules as Reeve had explained them, should not exist.

"Every judgment requires unanimity," Reeve had told her, standing in the crypt with his lantern held low, so the light climbed his face instead of falling from it, which made him look like every portrait of every founder at once. "No name enters the ledger without all twelve voices. No correction proceeds without all twelve. It has been the rule since 1898."

"And yet," Wren had said.

"And yet." He had looked tired then, in the way of a man who has been carrying something heavy for forty years and has recently noticed the weight. "There are entries in that book I did not write. Judgments I do not remember voting on. Someone adds names between gatherings, and the ink is old when we find them, and I cannot prove—" He had stopped himself, the way careful men stop themselves. "You have an eye for documents, Chair Twelve. It is why you were chosen."

*It is why you were chosen.* She thought about that sentence a great deal, usually at three in the morning, staring at her ceiling. Chosen for her skill, or chosen because they believed she was already compromised — a coroner who falsified reports for a mill owner was exactly the kind of person the Circle collected, the way a tide collects things that float.

Either way, she had a job to do, and the job was Chair Four.

* * *

She began where she always began: with the dead.

If Chair Four was forging entries — adding names after printing, as she'd found in Colm's copy back when she still thought Colm was merely a warden and not, possibly, something worse — then the forged names would have a texture different from the old ones. The genuine entries had a pattern. She'd decoded enough of it by now to feel it in her hands, the way you can feel a fracture through gloves. Real judgments followed crimes. Real judgments followed *proof* — the violet proof, some token or testimony the Circle gathered before it acted. And real judgments, however monstrous the method, aimed at monsters. Men who beat wives and walked away smiling. A doctor in 1953 whose anesthesia errors killed four children and cost him nothing but a relocation. The ferryman — no. Not the ferryman. The ferryman was Colm's addition too, she was nearly sure of it, judged through his daughter in defiance of the third rule, and the thought of it made her hands want to close into fists and stay there.

So she made a list. On the left side, every entry she could date to the last thirty years — the period when, if Reeve was honest, the rot had set in. On the right side, everything she knew about the town's unpunished crimes from the same period. Twenty years of autopsies, inquests, and quiet conversations with grieving people gave her a long right-hand column.

The left column was longer.

That was the finding, there in the kitchen with the coffee cold and Biscuit asleep on the 1940s: there were more names in the ledger than there were monsters in Ashcombe. Unless the town had been quietly producing villains at a rate of nearly two per year — unless Ashcombe, population four thousand, was somehow a hothouse of the unpunished wicked — then somebody was padding the book.

Wren took a fresh page and started matching names to grudges.

It was ugly work, guessing at other people's hatreds. But a town is a small organism, and Wren had spent two decades listening to it breathe. She knew who feuded with whom over property lines and fishing rights and a church pew dispute that had outlived both original disputants. She knew which families spoke to each other at funerals only. And she knew — because everyone in Ashcombe knew, the way everyone knows the weather — Sheriff Dane Colm's short list.

Colm was a smiler. That had been her first note about him, back in Chapter One of this whole catastrophe, when she'd tested him with a fabricated question about the church records and watched him deflect with the smoothness of a man rehearsing for a play nobody had told him was on. He smiled at traffic stops. He smiled at domestic calls. He smiled at Wren across her autopsy table while she stitched up the drowned man from Miller's Bend, and she had smiled back, both of them performing a friendship that might be real, might be theater, and was certainly now a duel.

But even smilers keep lists. And Colm's list was public knowledge in the way of small towns: everyone knew the sheriff had enemies, and everyone pretended not to know.

There was Dale Pruitt, who had beaten Colm's brother at cards in 1998 and then, worse, told the story at the Legion hall with gestures. There was the schoolteacher, Miss Ferro, who had reported Colm's nephew for selling pills behind the gym and refused to retract it. There was old Sam Okafor, who had filed the complaint about evidence handling in 2009 that got Colm passed over for the county job. Wren wrote the names down and felt slightly sick writing them, because these weren't monsters. These were men and women who had embarrassed Dane Colm.

Then she checked the ledger.

Dale Pruitt, entered 2001, marked corrected. Miss Ferro, entered 2011 — *corrected*, though Wren had attended Miss Ferro's funeral herself and certified the heart attack, and had never once wondered, until this moment, whether the heart attack had been wondering about her. Sam Okafor, entered 2009, the same year as the complaint. Corrected.

Three for three. Wren sat back and let the lamp hum.

"No," she said aloud, because saying it aloud made it real, and she needed it real. "No, that's not justice. That's just a man with a grudge and a key to the drawer."

Biscuit opened one eye, decided the crisis didn't concern him, and went back to sleep.

* * *

The problem with suspicion, Wren had learned, was that it wanted to be certainty, and certainty was how towns like Ashcombe ended up with ledgers in the first place. So she did what she always did when her heart ran ahead of her evidence: she slowed down and got more evidence.

The forged entries had a tell. She'd found it comparing Colm's copy against the church original — entries added after printing, in ink matched to age by someone patient and skilled. But patience leaves fingerprints of its own. The forger's hand, however well disguised, had habits. Wren had spent years reading death certificates, and every hand has a signature rhythm: the way it crosses t's, the pressure at the end of a stroke, the little hesitations where a writer thinks faster than the pen moves.

She photographed every suspect entry and pinned them above her desk, and late at night she compared them against handwriting samples she gathered with the shameless thoroughness of a woman who had once identified a murderer by his apostrophes. Colm signed citations. Colm signed the incident reports that crossed her desk after every unattended death. Colm had, eleven years ago, co-signed the paperwork on a drowning at Miller's Bend, and his signature sat there in blue ink, confident and looping.

The forged entries were disguised — a spikier hand, a different slant, the kind of disguise a clever man adopts when he knows documents might someday be compared. But disguise is a costume, and costumes fit the body underneath. The *e* in *corrected* pinched shut at the top in every forged entry. In Colm's incident reports, when he wrote fast — and he wrote fast on reports involving people he disliked — the *e* pinched shut at the top.

Wren stood in front of her wall of paper for a long time that night, coffee untouched, and felt the righteous anger come up in her like heat off summer asphalt.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing she couldn't put down. Wren Voss had spent nine days — no, more than that now, weeks — learning to understand the Circle, and understanding was a door that swung both ways. She understood the mill girls of 1898, abandoned by every court in the county, taking justice into their own ink-stained hands. She understood the generations who kept the rule alive because sometimes, God help them, the rule worked — because her sister's killer had been stopped by it, stopped by strangers with a ledger and a code, and part of Wren would be grateful for that until she died.

But Dale Pruitt had won at cards. Miss Ferro had done her job. Sam Okafor had filed a complaint. Those weren't corrections. Those were a sheriff settling scores inside a machine built by better people than him, wearing their righteousness like a stolen coat.

And the worst of it — the part that made her jaw ache at night — was that she had liked him. Somewhere under the suspicion, in the weeks of shared glances and mutual performance, some traitorous part of Wren had enjoyed sparring with Dane Colm. He was quick. He was funny, in a dry way that never quite committed to a joke. He was the closest thing to a colleague her strange double life had produced.

Which meant, she supposed, that she understood the mill girls completely, because betrayal makes vigilantes of us all.

* * *

She needed one more thing before she took it to Reeve. Proof of access. Anyone could forge ink; only certain people could reach the drawer beneath St. Brigid's.

The drawer, she learned by asking casual questions of Father Ambrose — a gentle, half-deaf priest who assumed her interest in church archives was grief-related, which in a sense it was — required two keys. The church kept one. The other had been cut in 1971, per the parish maintenance log, for "the constable's office," and the log noted, in a later hand, that the key passed from Sheriff Hobb to Sheriff Colm in 1989 along with the badge and the filing cabinets.

One key. One office. One man.

Wren closed the maintenance log and thanked Father Ambrose, who pressed a lemon drop on her and asked after her health with such sincere warmth that she nearly confessed everything on the spot, out of sheer hunger for one uncomplicated kindness.

Instead she walked home through the fog, past the cemetery where her own headstone was already going green at the base — *WREN VOSS, BELOVED CORONER OF THIS TOWN* — and let the anger settle into something colder and more useful.

At her kitchen table she laid it all out one final time: the pinch-shut e's, the grudge list, the key, the dates. Pruitt, Ferro, Okafor, the ferryman, and — she checked the ledger again, because she had been avoiding this line all week — the entry dated for next spring, initials *T.F.*, Thomas Ferro, Miss Ferro's son, who had recently testified in a deposition about the pill case. The grudge, inherited. The sin, visited forward. Colm wasn't just breaking the rules. He was breeding new ones.

Wren put her palm flat on the page, over the child-adjacent name, and made herself very quiet.

Reeve deserved to know. The Circle deserved to know. But Reeve was the Keeper, and Keepers protect systems, and systems protect themselves, and there was every chance that bringing this to the crypt would end with the machine closing ranks around its corruptor — or worse, with Wren exposed as the coroner who faked her death and infiltrated them, which she was, and which would go badly for her in approximately eleven different ways.

No. If she brought this to the Circle, she needed to bring it armed. She needed something Colm couldn't smile away.

And there was one piece of leverage she hadn't touched yet, because touching it meant opening a door she'd nailed shut years ago: the mill owner. Aldous Wrenner — no, Harlan Grieve, the mill owner, the man whose crimes she had buried in falsified reports, the man whose sins had gotten her judged in a crypt by candlelight. If anyone in Ashcombe knew what Dane Colm did in the dark — if anyone had watched the sheriff for years the way powerful men watch the law — it was Harlan Grieve.

He owed her. He knew he owed her. And Wren had learned, in the crypt, exactly how much it costs to carry a debt like that.

She took out a clean sheet of paper, wrote *Harlan Grieve* at the top, and underlined it twice.

Outside, the fog came up off the harbor and swallowed the streetlights one by one, and somewhere in it — she was sure of it now, sure the way coroners are sure — Dane Colm was awake too, adding a name to a book, smiling at whatever he was smiling at.

Wren turned off the lamp.

"Time to visit the mill," she told the dark, and Biscuit, loyal to the end, did not get up.
