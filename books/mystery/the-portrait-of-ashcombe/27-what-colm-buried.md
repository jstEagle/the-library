## 27. What Colm Buried

There is a particular freedom in being dead, and it is not the freedom people imagine.

It is not the freedom of consequencelessness — I still felt consequences, keenly, the way a tooth feels cold water. It was smaller and stranger than that: it was the freedom of being unwatched. For thirty-six years I had been Wren Voss, coroner, spinster, the woman who signed things. Every eye in Ashcombe had a file on me, even if the file lived only behind the eyes themselves. Now the town had filed me away under *deceased*, and deceased women can walk through a sheriff's office at two in the morning without anyone's stomach dropping.

Well. Almost no one's.

I had waited three days after my funeral before making the attempt. Three days of living in the back room above Harrow Inn, eating Tomas Reeve's terrible stew — he cooked like a man who believed seasoning was a form of vanity — and listening to him hum hymns off-key while he pretended not to study me across the table. Three days of being nobody, which turned out to be itchy work. Grief makes you restless, and I had a fresh grief to be restless about.

Ellery.

The name sat in me like a splinter too deep for tweezers. The pale stranger who had walked into my office with his hat held to his chest like a man entering church, who had paid me triple my fee with bills so crisp they could have cut bread, who had asked me to prove that a death hadn't happened yet — he was dead. Had been dead, I now suspected, since long before I ever understood what he was asking. And Sheriff Dane Colm, who smiled like sunshine through a clean window, had been the one to bury the fact.

I intended to find out how deep the grave went.

* * *

Colm's house sat on Cordwainer Lane, a tidy white clapboard affair with blue shutters and a garden that said *a good man lives here* in the loud, rehearsed voice of a stage set. I had been inside it once, years ago, when he'd hosted the department's Christmas gathering and I'd stood near the punch bowl for an hour counting the exits out of habit. Old habits, as it turns out, do not die even when their owners do.

The back door lock was a joke — a pin tumbler older than my mother would have been, if my mother had lived past the year the Circle corrected her neighbor. It gave up in forty seconds. I let myself into the dark kitchen, where a dish rack held two plates drying side by side, and paused.

Two plates. Colm lived alone. Unless the second plate belonged to a woman whose existence I'd never heard mentioned, or unless the sheriff of Ashcombe was the sort of man who washed dishes in pairs out of optimism, something in that kitchen was wrong in a way I couldn't yet name. I filed it under *later* and moved toward the stairs.

His office was on the second floor, facing the street, because Colm was a man who liked to be seen. The desk was neat. Too neat — the same immaculate order as St. Brigid's burial ledger, and I have learned in this life that tidiness is often just guilt wearing its Sunday clothes. I started with the desk drawers and found nothing but tax receipts, fishing licenses, and a photograph of Colm at twenty-two, squinting beside a bass the size of a small dog.

The real archive was under the floorboard beneath the rocking chair.

I know how that sounds. I know that in any other town, searching under floorboards marks you as either a novelist or a lunatic. But Ashcombe had spent a century teaching me that its secrets were kept the way its fog kept the harbor — thoroughly, patiently, and directly beneath the surface everyone walked on. The board rocked when I stepped near the chair, and once I noticed it, I couldn't stop noticing it, the way you can't stop hearing a clock once it's ticked wrong in your presence.

Underneath: a metal document box, unlocked, because men who hide things under floorboards are protecting them from wives and cleaners, not from coroners who refuse to stay dead.

Inside the box, the smell hit me first — old paper, pipe tobacco, and underneath both, faintly, violets.

* * *

He kept everything. That was the first thing I understood, kneeling on Colm's floor in the dark with a penlight between my teeth. He kept everything the way a magpie keeps everything, except a magpie hoards shiny things and Colm hoarded proof. Newspaper clippings going back decades. Photographs of men leaving the mill owner's office looking gray as dishwater. A 1987 court transcript with passages underlined in pencil so hard the paper had torn. And there, in a folder labeled in his square, confident hand — *V*, for violet, or perhaps for victory — the thing I had come for.

A ledger. His own copy. I already knew about that one; I'd seen it before my death, before Chair Four and the votes and the crypt beneath St. Brigid's. What I hadn't seen was what lay loose inside the back cover, folded twice, soft at the creases from handling.

Photographs.

The first showed the Blue Heron Motel, room six, taken from across the parking lot at night. The window glowed yellow. Through the gap in the curtains — a gap that had been there when I searched that room myself, though I'd assumed it was carelessness — a figure sat on the bed, hunched over papers. Even blurred, even from behind, I knew the slope of those shoulders. Ellery, alive, working. The date stamp read March 2nd. Nine days before my scheduled death. Twelve days before I'd found his room stripped bare with a single pressed violet on the pillow.

The second photograph was the same room, same angle, later — the timestamp read 11:47 p.m., March 3rd. The window was dark. And standing by the door of room six, caught mid-stride, his face turned just enough toward the camera to catch the light —

Dane Colm.

Smiling. Of course he was smiling. The man smiled at funerals, at autopsies, at the photographs of his own crimes. Somewhere in Ashcombe there was probably a mirror he smiled at, and I suspected the mirror smiled back out of professional courtesy.

My hands were steady. I want to record that honestly, because I've spent my life around hands that shake — the bereaved, the guilty, the witnesses — and mine did not shake, and some cold, clinical corner of me noted that fact and wondered what it meant about the woman holding the penlight. Then I turned to the third photograph and steadiness stopped mattering.

It was Ellery. Close up. Taken after.

I won't describe it. Not because I lack the words — God knows I've written worse descriptions in autopsy reports, in language so clinical it could sterilize instruments — but because Ellery deserved better than to become a paragraph in someone else's accounting. He deserved the truth instead, and the truth was this: the man who hired me had not been a crank, not a blackmailer, not a relative of some old victim come to settle scores. He had been exactly what he appeared to be, which in Ashcombe made him the rarest creature of all — an honest stranger.

* * *

I sat on Colm's floor until my legs went numb, reading.

Ellery's real name was Elias Wrenner. I laughed when I read it — a short, ugly laugh in that dark office, the kind of laugh that comes out of you sideways when grief finds the trapdoor in your chest. *Wrenner.* He had chosen the alias "Ellery" from his own middle name, and "Wren" — he had chosen my name. Or half of it. When he walked into my office and asked for Wren Voss by name, he had already known who I was, and he had picked his false name the way you pick a gift for someone: carefully, personally, hoping they'll understand someday.

The documents told the rest in his own cramped, careful handwriting. Elias Wrenner, archivist, formerly of the county historical society. In 1994 he had catalogued a donation of papers from a deceased Ashcombe family and found references to the Violet Circle — references that should not have existed outside the crypt. He had spent four years quietly pulling threads. He had learned what I had learned, and more: he had learned that the Circle's founding rule, *no punishment without unanimity*, was being broken. He had learned about Chair Four. He had come to Ashcombe to expose the corruption from within, and he had needed a coroner — specifically me, specifically the one person in town whose signature carried legal weight and whose sister's name sat corrected in the ledger — to help him prove it.

And Dane Colm had killed him for it, eleven days before Ellery ever reached my office.

No. That wasn't right either. I checked the dates again, and the correction mattered, the way corrections always matter to a coroner. Colm had killed him *after* Ellery reached my office. After the retainer. After our first meeting, our second, after the afternoon Ellery stood in my doorway twisting his hat and said, *You'll want to look at the ink, Miss Voss. Look at how patient the ink is.* Colm had watched us meet. Colm had let me take the case, let me walk straight into the machinery, because a coroner chasing the ledger was useful cover — and because, I understood with a nausea that rose slow and tidal, Colm had wanted me frightened. Frightened people run toward the Circle's protection or flee the town entirely. Either way, the herd thins.

Ellery had been the variable Colm couldn't predict. An outsider with no history in Ashcombe, no grave in the ledger, no chair at the table. So Colm had erased him. Stripped the motel room, pressed a violet on the pillow — the Circle's calling card, deployed as a warning to anyone else poking at the edges — and buried the man himself somewhere I would never think to look, because I would never think to look at all. Dead men don't hire coroners. That was the whole trick. That had always been the whole trick.

Except Ellery had mailed a package to himself at the Ashcombe post office, general delivery, the day before he died. I found the receipt tucked in with the photographs, and taped to the receipt, a key. Small, brass, stamped with a number: 214.

Post office box 214. Still rented. Still paid, I would learn, a decade in advance — Ellery had been an archivist, and archivists believe in the future even when they're standing in the last five minutes of their own.

* * *

I put everything back exactly as I'd found it. Floorboard, chair, lamp angled just so. If Colm came home and sensed disturbance, I wanted him to blame the house settling, or his own conscience, or the fog. I let myself out the back door and stood a moment in his garden, among the tidy rows of marigolds, breathing air that smelled of soil and someone else's respectable life.

The second plate. I thought about it again, standing there. Two plates in the dish rack. And then I thought about the folder marked *V*, and the way certain entries in Colm's copy of the ledger had been added after printing, and a picture assembled itself with the awful click of a bone knitting back into joint: Colm hadn't been hiding the Circle from a wife. There was no wife. The second plate was habit, or loneliness, or — more likely, knowing what I knew now — the plate of a man who set a place at his own table for the version of himself he pretended to be. The good sheriff. The smiling public man. They dined together every night, and only one of them washed the dishes.

I walked home through the fog — *home* being the back room at Harrow Inn, a phrase that still fit my mouth like a borrowed coat — and Reeve was waiting up, as he always was, a candle burning low and the stew pot mercifully empty for once.

"You've been to his house," Reeve said. Not a question. The keeper of Harrow Inn had spent a century learning to read people by their footfalls, and mine had apparently come up the stairs sounding like a verdict.

"Yes."

"And?"

I set the brass key on the table between us. It caught the candlelight and threw a little crooked shadow against the teapot, and for a long moment neither of us touched it.

"Elias Wrenner," I said. "That was his name. He was an archivist. He found the Circle's tracks in some donated papers four years ago and followed them here." My voice did something I didn't authorize — cracked, just slightly, on *here*. "Colm killed him the week after he hired me. Before I ever decoded the first entry. Everything I've done since, I've done walking over his grave."

Reeve closed his eyes. When he opened them, the pity in them was worse than judgment would have been. "I wondered," he said quietly, "when you'd learn it. I hoped you wouldn't. There's a difference, Miss Voss, between a wound that heals and a wound you keep picking open to prove it's yours."

"He tried to save a child," I said. "He tried to stop what's coming for the ferryman's daughter, and he died in a motel room with a flower on his pillow, and the town wrote him down as *no next of kin*. I signed paperwork like that. I've signed hundreds." I picked up the key. It was warm from the table, or from my hand, or from four years of waiting in a dead man's pocket. "Box 214. General delivery. He mailed himself his own evidence, Reeve. Which means somewhere in that post office, sitting behind a little brass door, is everything we need to bury Dane Colm so deep even Ashcombe will lose the address."

Reeve looked at the key a long time. Then he looked at me, and something in his face shifted — the innkeeper receding, the keeper of the third generation surfacing, the man who had warned me gently to stop finally deciding, perhaps, to help me go on.

"The post office opens at seven," he said. "You'll want to be first in line. And Wren—" He stopped. In forty years of running an inn he had presumably mastered the art of saying things smoothly, and here he fumbled like a boy. "Elias chose you. Out of everyone. You might carry that heavier than the rest of it, or you might let it be the thing that carries *you*. I've seen both ways. One of them ends better."

Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like a congregation leaning in, and somewhere below the town, beneath St. Brigid's, twelve chairs waited in the dark for the vote that would decide a child's future — and mine.

Seven o'clock was four hours away. I sat with the key in my fist and did not sleep, and did not need to, and when the sky finally went the color of dishwater I was already standing at the post office door, first in line, watching the clerk amble up the street with his thermos, taking his sweet time, while behind a wall of little brass doors a dead man waited to speak.
