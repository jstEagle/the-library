## 31. Tunnels of the Named

The dark behind me was full of shouting.

I ran the way I had never run in my life — not in gym class, not from dogs as a girl, not even that time in county when a body I'd signed off on turned out to be breathing and everyone agreed it was better if we didn't discuss it. My shoulder hit stone, my palm found wet rock, and I followed the only instinct left to me: down, away, anywhere that wasn't the crypt where twelve chairs were scraping against flagstone behind me.

"Left," I told myself out loud, because hearing my own voice kept me from screaming. "Go left."

It was the wrong left. It was, in fact, a dead end shaped like an apology — a low arch bricked shut sometime before electricity, its mortar crumbling like old cake. I stood there panting, listening to the pursuit fade or deepen, unable to tell which, and that was when the lantern light caught something at my feet.

A bone. A femur, yellowed and clean, laid across the floor not scattered but *placed*, the way you'd set a spoon beside a plate.

And tied around it, faded almost to nothing, a strip of violet ribbon with a paper tag.

I crouched. My knees cracked like the mortar. The tag was stiff with age, the ink brown, the handwriting — God help me — familiar. That same patient, unaging hand that had filled a century of burial records at St. Brigid's.

*E. MARSH, 1911.*

I knew that name. Of course I knew that name. Every coroner knows the names that come before them; we inherit them like debts. Edwin Marsh, drowned, ruled accidental, 1911. Except he hadn't been in the cemetery. I'd checked once, years ago, on a slow Tuesday, because his file had bothered me the way a crooked picture frame bothers you — no grave, no marker, no plot purchased. A man of Ashcombe who simply stopped existing.

He hadn't stopped. He'd been *filed*.

"Oh," I said softly, to the dark, to Edwin Marsh. "Oh, you poor man. They put you in the basement."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being hunted: after the first ten minutes of terror, your legs get bored. Fear is a fuel that burns hot and fast, and underneath it, stubborn as sediment, is the thing that made me a coroner in the first place — the inability to walk past a question.

So while half of me listened for footsteps, the other half knelt in the dirt and counted bones.

Not one skeleton. Not two. The alcove beyond the bricked arch opened into a gallery — a long, low chamber where the tunnel widened, and along both walls, on shelves cut into the living rock, the dead of Ashcombe lay in rows. Not coffins. Bones, arranged with terrible tenderness: skulls facing forward, long bones bundled, hands folded where hands could be found. Each one tagged. Each tag violet.

I lifted the lantern higher and read.

*M. QUINN, 1923.*
*A. FOLEY, 1938.*
*R. OKONKWO, 1967.*
*S. DELGADO, 1989.*

Names I knew from files. Names I'd typed myself onto certificates marked ACCIDENT, UNDETERMINED, CLOSED. Ruth Okonkwo — the schoolteacher who vanished the same winter her complaint against the mill foreman went missing from the sheriff's log. I remembered her photograph from the case box: a woman squinting into sun, holding a chalkboard eraser like she was about to throw it at somebody who deserved it.

They'd had her down here for fifty-seven years.

"You're not evidence," I told her, and my voice did something embarrassing halfway through. "You're people. You're — you're a whole congregation, and they shelved you."

My breath fogged in the cold. Somewhere water dripped with the patience of a metronome. And I understood, standing in that gallery, why the Circle had lasted a hundred years without anyone finding it. They hadn't hidden their work. They'd *archived* it. Every correction, every judgment, every person the town's law had failed and the town's vigilantes had erased — catalogued, ordered, dusted, presumably by Tomas Reeve with a feather duster and a clear conscience, generation after generation.

It was the most organized horror I had ever seen, and I say that as a woman whose career has included a fishing boat, a freezer, and a bet between two brothers that went very wrong.

I walked the rows. I couldn't help it. Some part of me — the part that recites names over graves at dawn, the part that believes the dead are owed the small dignity of being counted — moved shelf to shelf with the lantern, reading each tag aloud in a whisper, because a name spoken is still a name witnessed.

"Margaret Quinn." Pause. "Ruth Okonkwo." Pause. "Samuel Delgado."

Delgado. 1989. I'd been nineteen when he disappeared. I remembered the flyers stapled to the mill fence, curling in the rain, until one day they weren't there anymore and nobody asked why. His daughter used to sell honey at the farmers' market. She sold it for forty years, always with a little sign that said ASK ME ABOUT SAM'S BEES, and nobody ever asked, and eventually the sign came down too.

I pressed my hand flat against the cool stone above his skull. "Somebody asked," I said. "Late, but somebody asked."

That was when I heard the footsteps again — distant, unhurried, more than one pair — and realized with a lurch that I had stopped caring whether they found me. Which was dangerous. Which was exactly how Colm would want me: soft, distracted, kneeling among the dead instead of running.

I took the lantern and went deeper.

* * *

The tunnels under Ashcombe are older than the town's manners. The old-timers called them the smugglers' runs, though what was smuggled changed with the century — brandy, then wool, then men avoiding a war, then, apparently, justice. The passages branched like root systems, and I navigated by the oldest trick there is: follow the water downhill and trust that whoever dug these things needed drainage too.

Twice I passed side chambers. Twice I lit them and wished I hadn't, and then, being me, lit them anyway.

More shelves. More tags. In one chamber, the arrangement changed — newer, the bones wrapped in linen instead of laid bare, the tags typed instead of handwritten. The Circle had modernized. Somewhere in the nineties someone had gotten a label maker, and I felt a laugh climb up my throat that wasn't really a laugh at all, because what else do you do when you find bureaucracy in a bone cellar?

In another chamber there was a table. A plain wooden table, a candle stub, and a ledger — not the great ledger itself but a working copy, a slim notebook titled in that same hand: *PENDING.* I didn't touch it. I looked at it the way you look at a snake sunning itself on a path, and then I kept walking, because whatever was written in it, I already knew the shape of the next name, and it belonged to a nine-year-old girl who liked to draw boats.

That thought got me moving faster than any footsteps could.

But the gallery kept pulling me back. That's the truth of it, and I won't dress it up. Every branching, I chose the way that passed more shelves. Because here was the thing I was coming to understand, the thing that sat in my chest heavier than fear:

Nobody had ever mourned these people.

The town had held funerals for empty caskets sometimes, when it suited the story. Had said *moved away*, had said *ran off with a traveling man*, had said *these things happen*. Mothers had died not knowing. Daughters had grown old not knowing. And all the while, thirty feet beneath the church where those same families prayed, their people lay in the dark with violet ribbons, tended but unnamed aloud, kept like records instead of loved like losses.

I am not a sentimental woman. I have handled four hundred bodies and I have never once cried at work, a record I intend to defend. But I stood in that tunnel with a lantern shaking in my hand, and I said every name I could read, and when I ran out of light to read by I said the ones I remembered from files, and when I ran out of those I just said, "You're known. You're known. You're known," like a prayer with no theology in it, just bookkeeping for the soul.

Because that's the job, isn't it? That's what I'd told Ellery, back when he was alive and pale and paying me triple to prove a death hadn't happened yet. Somebody has to witness. Somebody has to stand at the edge of the pit and say *this one mattered*. I'd thought that meant signing papers upstairs. It turned out it meant this: a damp tunnel, a dying lantern, and a woman talking to bones because no one else ever had.

* * *

I don't know how long I walked. Long enough that my legs went from burning to numb to some third state I can only describe as *administrative*. The water sound grew, then changed pitch, and I came out into a wider vault where a stream ran along a carved channel — deliberate, engineered, Victorian-proud — and the ceiling rose high enough that my lantern barely reached it.

And there, on the far wall, the naming stopped.

A section of shelving stood empty. Not abandoned-empty. *Waiting*-empty. Fresh-cut wood, no dust, maybe five years old. Beside it, a stack of linen wrappings, folded neat as church linens. And on the shelf's end panel, carved small and precise, ready to receive its first resident:

*W.V.*

They'd built mine in advance. Measured me for a shelf the way a tailor measures a sleeve.

I sat down on the cold floor across from it, mostly because my legs quit, partly because I wanted to look at it properly. You spend enough years around death, you develop a professional relationship with it, and I'll tell you honestly: my own shelf didn't frighten me as much as it should have. What frightened me was the craftsmanship. Someone had sanded this. Someone had planed the edges smooth so no bone would snag. Even now, even rotten, even with Colm forging entries and chairs turning against me, there was care in it. That was the trap of the Violet Circle, the thing I finally saw plain as the lantern flame: they had never been monsters who didn't love. They were people who loved *wrong*, loving order more than mercy, tidiness more than truth, and the love was what made it rot so slowly and so completely.

"They were going to shelve me next to Ruth Okonkwo," I said to the empty space. "She'd have hated that. She'd have thrown an eraser at somebody."

The stream chuckled over its stones. I chose to take it as agreement.

I got up. I had a child to think about, a sheriff to ruin, three chairs in that crypt who might still be turned my way if I lived long enough to argue. Grief was a luxury I could carry but not stop inside. So I did what I always do with grief: I packed it flat, labeled it, and put it in the pocket nearest my heart, where it rides.

I was turning back toward the branch that sloped upward toward the ossuary mouth when the lantern guttered — and in its last stutter of light, I saw it.

One more shelf. Set apart, near the stream, smaller than the others. And on it, a single occupant, wrapped in linen gone gray with age, the tag so faded I had to hold it an inch from my nose:

No name. Just a date. *1898.*

The first year. The founding. Whoever lay there had been corrected before the Circle even had a proper name for itself — back when it was, if Reeve's story held true, a handful of mill workers protecting girls nobody else would protect. This one had been first. Or last. Or both.

I unwrapped the linen with the care I'd want shown to my own sister. Beneath it: small bones. A young frame. A woman, I judged, seventeen, eighteen at most. One wrist healed badly, an old break set by someone who didn't know how or didn't care. A mill girl. One of the ones they'd started all this to protect, and failed, and buried their failure down here where the water could keep the secret.

"I've got you," I whispered. "Whoever you are. I've got you now."

And that was when the light behind me changed — not my dying lantern, but a new one, steady and white, swinging around the bend of the passage with footsteps I knew.

Heavy boots. Unhurried. The tread of a man who has never once hurried toward anything because everything eventually comes to him.

Sheriff Dane Colm stepped into the vault, lantern high, smile easy, gun loose in his other hand the way a man carries a hymnal he's sure you'll sing from.

"Well now," he said warmly, glancing past me at the small bones in my arms, at the empty shelf carved W.V., at the century of the named stacked silent along the walls. "Look at you. Found the family album."

Behind him, deeper in the dark, more lights were coming.
