## 36. The Grave That Wasn't Hers

The paperwork took three weeks. It should have taken three days, but Ashcombe had never been a town that hurried toward its own uncomfortable truths, and exhuming a casket that had already been blessed, sung over, and wept upon by half the congregation of St. Brigid's was, according to Father Lund, "a matter requiring pastoral sensitivity."

"It's a matter requiring a shovel," Wren told him.

"Wren." He said her name the way he said it now — carefully, as if she were a restored painting and he was afraid of touching the wrong part. "You were buried there. There are people in this town who prayed at your grave."

"I know. I read the cards." She had. Forty-one sympathy cards, most of them sincere, two of them from Circle members whose handwriting she recognized from the ledger, one of them — this had made her laugh in a way that alarmed the postmistress — from Sheriff Dane Colm, currently residing in county jail, which meant someone at the jail had helped him mail it, which meant even his guards liked him, which was exactly the kind of thing that had let him run a secret court for years. *Rest well, Wren,* the card said. She'd pinned it above her desk as a reminder that evil could have lovely penmanship.

Father Lund sighed the sigh of a man who had lost every theological argument with her since 2011. "Three weeks," he said. "For the diocese."

"For the diocese," she agreed, and went home to finish the harder paperwork: finding out who she was burying instead of herself.

* * *

The body in the casket had come to her the way bodies came to everyone in Ashcombe eventually — through Tomas Reeve.

He'd arrived at her door on a Tuesday evening in April, holding a hat he clearly didn't know what to do with, turning it in his hands like a man trying to fold a map back into its original creases. Forty years running Harrow Inn, a face unchanged since photographs taken in 1902, and still — this was the thing Wren had learned about him, the thing that kept her from hating him outright — still capable of looking like a tired old man with something heavy to carry.

"You found him," she said, before he could speak. She'd known it was coming. After the ledger burned, after the tunnels were mapped and photographed and the bones tagged and catalogued, there remained the question of names. Thirty-one sets of remains beneath St. Brigid's. Twenty-nine identified through parish records, census pages, dental charts, and the kind of patient archival work that Wren had discovered she loved more than she'd ever loved autopsies. Two remained nameless.

"One of them," Reeve said. "Come in?"

She stepped aside. He sat in her kitchen like a man sitting in a confessional that had been redecorated against his will, and he laid a photograph on the table between them. A young man, maybe twenty-five, dark-haired, with the particular squint of someone who spent his life near water.

"Ferryman's assistant," Reeve said. "Nineteen sixty-one. His name was Daniel Okafor."

Wren looked at the photograph for a long moment. "Okafor," she repeated. "There are no Okafors left in Ashcombe."

"No. There weren't many then, either. He came up from the coast to work the river crossing when old Miller's knees gave out. Good worker. Quiet. Sang while he rowed, my mother used to say — you could hear him across the water on foggy mornings." Reeve's hands were flat on the table now, steadying himself or the table, hard to say. "In 'sixty-one, a woman named Edith Marsh accused him of theft. Money from her till at the ferry house. The sheriff at the time — Colm's grandfather, God help us — didn't investigate. Didn't need to. The Circle convened, and they voted, and they were wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"The money turned up six months later. Edith had miscounted. Miscounted again, actually — she'd miscounted once before, in 'fifty-eight, and a drifter paid for that one too." Reeve's voice did not waver, but it went very quiet, the way water goes quiet right before it goes over the edge. "By the time the error was discovered, Daniel Okafor was in the tunnel under a tag that said only *corrected, spring '61.* And nobody — not one of them, not the men who voted, not the women who sewed the violet into his coat pocket — nobody went back and fixed it. Because fixing it would mean admitting the whole machine could be wrong. And if the machine could be wrong once, it could be wrong always. So they buried the mistake along with him and called it regret."

Wren thought of the tally marks inside the wardrobe at Harrow Inn. She thought of Ellery, pressed violet on a stripped pillow, trying to stop all of this from inside and dying for it. She thought of her own falsified autopsy reports, and the shame that lived in her chest now like a tenant who paid no rent.

"And you knew," she said. "You've known since—"

"Since I carried the tea down to the gathering in 'seventy-three and heard old Aldous mention it the way you'd mention a fence that needed mending." Reeve met her eyes. "Yes. I knew. I was Chair Seven by then and I knew, and I told myself the system was bigger than one mistake, and that lie is the reason I'm an old man who keeps an inn and can't sleep past three in the morning. You asked me once why I warned you to stop. It wasn't to protect the Circle, Wren. It was because I hoped you'd stop before you became me."

It was the most honest sentence anyone in Ashcombe had spoken to her since her funeral, and she let it sit between them for a while, the way you let bread rise.

"All right," she said finally. "Daniel Okafor gets his name back. What do you need from me?"

"Nothing. That's rather the point." He stood, put on the hat, took off the hat, held it again. "I'll pay for the stone. I'd like — if it's permitted — I'd like to carry him."

* * *

The exhumation happened on a bright cold morning in May, with a backhoe rented from a man named Petch who asked no questions because Wren paid him double and because in Ashcombe, asking questions had recently become unfashionable among people who valued their evenings.

She insisted on doing the final work herself. The casket came up intact, heavier than it should have been, and when she opened it in the small brick receiving vault behind the cemetery chapel, she found what she had placed there in March: a body purchased from a medical school two states away, dressed in her spare clothes, arranged with her own hands in the dark while her heart hammered and her mind recited the steps like a recipe. Her blood on the collar. Her hair — she'd cut a lock, God forgive her vanity — tucked at the temple. A closed casket, because Ashcombe grieves beautifully but verifies poorly.

"Poor devil," murmured Petch, doffing his cap at the stranger wearing her face's clothes. "Whoever he was."

"He was nobody," Wren said. "That was the problem."

She worked through the morning transferring the remains — the real ones, Daniel Okafor's, brought up from the tunnel in a padded case she'd borrowed from the state archive and never returned — into a proper casket. Reeve helped, silent, competent, handling the bones with a gentleness that made her throat tight. When they finished, the man in the casket wore a good suit donated by the undertaker, and in his breast pocket, where the Circle would have sewn a violet, Wren placed instead a small card with his name and dates and the words *Sang while he rowed.*

"That's not standard epitaph practice," Reeve observed.

"Neither was any of this. We're improvising."

He almost smiled. On his unaging face, an almost-smile looked like weather changing.

* * *

The funeral was held on a Saturday, and Wren had braced herself for six attendees and a stiff wind. Instead, the little cemetery filled.

Word had traveled — word always traveled in Ashcombe, faster than the fog — and the town had spent two months digesting the story Wren and Reeve and the surviving reasonable chairs had fed it: that a historical society had uncovered evidence of vigilante killings spanning a century; that Sheriff Colm had abused the system for personal ends; that coroner Wren Voss had infiltrated the ring, faked her death to do it, and dismantled it from within. The story was ninety percent true, which in Wren's professional experience was the maximum truthfulness any town could absorb without indigestion. The remaining ten percent — the twelve chairs, the votes still standing, the fact that half the respectable names in the church directory had once raised their hands to condemn a neighbor — had been sealed away with Reeve's confession, locked in a box that both of them hoped would stay shut and neither of them believed would.

So they came. The mill workers came, because the founding story of 1898 — protection for the mill girls — had leaked just enough to make them proud rather than ashamed. Mrs. Aldous came in a wheelchair pushed by her son, who stood slightly apart and would not meet anyone's eyes, and Wren decided to let him carry that a while longer. Father Lund came, having negotiated his conscience down to officiating "a service of restitution, which is not the same as absolution, and I want that noted in the record."

"Noted," Wren said. "I'll write it myself."

Even the ferryman came — the current one, the one whose daughter's name had nearly followed Wren's into the ledger, the man Wren had saved by casting the sole dissent. He stood at the back with his cap in his hands, and when the service ended he approached her awkwardly, a man assembling gratitude out of parts he wasn't familiar with.

"They say you stopped it," he said. "The vote. On my girl."

"On your daughter," Wren corrected gently. "And I didn't stop it alone. Three others voted with me, in the end. Remember that part. It matters more than me."

He nodded, unconvinced, and pressed something into her hand: a river stone, smooth and gray, worn round by decades of water. "Daniel used to skip stones like this across the bend," he said. "My father talked about him. Said you could set your watch by his singing." He cleared his throat. "Seemed like he should have one."

Wren closed her hand around the stone. It was warm from his pocket, and it weighed almost nothing, and it was the heaviest thing she'd held since the ledger.

* * *

Reeve spoke the eulogy. He'd offered, and Wren had considered refusing — he had, after all, been Chair Seven when the mistake was buried — but restitution, she was learning, was not a clean transaction. It was muddy and shared and required the guilty to stand in the open air and say names aloud.

"His name was Daniel Okafor," Reeve began, and his voice cracked on the second word, and he started again, and the second time it held. "He was twenty-five years old. He came here to work, and he sang on the water, and he was killed by men who called it justice because calling it murder would have required them to be brave." A murmur went through the gathered town, not of protest but of recognition — the sound of people hearing a true thing said plainly, perhaps for the first time in decades. "We cannot give him back his life. We cannot give back the sixty-four years his mother waited for a letter that never came — and yes, I wrote to her, once, a lie about a drowning, and it was the second-worst thing I have ever done. What we can do is say his name. In public. On stone. Where the rain can wear it and the children can read it and no one can ever again pretend he was a correction."

He stepped back. Father Lund prayed briefly and without ornament. Then, because Wren had insisted, the congregation sang — badly, in the key of whatever each person happened to have brought that morning — and the song was an old river hymn, and halfway through the second verse Wren realized several of the older attendees knew a descant line to it, a local variant, and she understood with a jolt that they were singing it the way it had been sung on the water, the way Daniel Okafor might have sung it, and that some part of him had been preserved all these years in the one place the Circle couldn't reach: other people's memories.

She did not cry at her own funeral. She cried at his.

* * *

Afterward, while the undertaker's men lowered the casket and the first shovelfuls rang on the lid, Wren stood a little apart with the river stone in her pocket and watched the headstone go in. Granite, simple, funded by a former innkeeper:

**DANIEL OKAFOR**
1959 – 1961
*Sang while he rowed*

The dates were wrong, of course — born 1936, dead 1961, but the parish records had lost his birth year and Wren refused to guess, so the stone gave him only the year the town had failed him, which felt honest in a way full dates wouldn't have. Underneath, smaller, at her insistence:

*Wrongly judged. Rightly remembered.*

"You know," said a voice beside her, and it was Mrs. Aldous, wheeled close by nobody — her son had drifted off to smoke, and apparently indignation powered the chair. The old woman looked up at the stone with milky, unfooled eyes. "Edith Marsh was my aunt. The till woman. She knew, you know. Found the money under the floorboard that autumn and never told a soul. Carried it to her grave. Used to get a look on her face whenever anyone mentioned the ferryman's boy, like she'd swallowed a bee."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're collecting truths now, dear. Everyone in this town can see it. You walk around with that notebook picking up the things we dropped." Mrs. Aldous patted Wren's hand with fingers like dry leaves. "And because there's something else. Something Edith said at the end, when her mind was going soft. She said the boy wasn't the only one. She said—" the old woman's voice dropped, though there was hardly anyone left to hear, just wind in the yew trees and the soft thud of earth on wood, "—she said the till money in 'fifty-eight, the drifter, that wasn't a miscount either. She said someone *moved* it. Someone in the family. Someone who wanted the Circle busy on a Saturday night."

Wren went very still. "Busy on a Saturday night doing what?"

Mrs. Aldous smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile, and for a moment Wren saw the widow's husband in it, the man who had been *corrected* in 1974. "That, dear, is the question you're going to ask next, isn't it? I can see the notebook coming out already." She gripped Wren's wrist, surprisingly strong. "Ask it quietly. Not everyone who buried mistakes wanted them staying buried. Some of us have been waiting sixty years for someone with a shovel."

Then her son returned, and the moment folded itself away like a letter, and Mrs. Aldous was merely a tired old woman being wheeled home.

Wren stood alone by the fresh grave as the crowd dispersed, the river stone in one pocket, her notebook suddenly heavy in the other. She opened it to a blank page and wrote at the top, in careful letters:

*Edith Marsh. Till money, 1958. Who moved it?*

Below that, after a moment's thought, she added a second line — because restitution, she was learning, was never finished, it only changed addresses:

*Ask Colm. He'll know. He always knew everything.*

She closed the notebook, touched the granite once — cold, solid, permanent, a name that would outlast everyone who had tried to erase it — and started down the hill toward the county road, and the jail, and the man with the lovely penmanship.
