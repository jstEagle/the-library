## 37. Colm's Cell

The county jail sat forty minutes east of Ashcombe, past the mill and the marsh and the long gray stretch of road where my sister had died and where, eleven years later, someone else had died for it — a man whose name I'd found in a ledger written in ink that should have faded decades before he bled.

I drove out on a Tuesday in late April because Tuesdays were visiting days, and because I had spent three weeks finding reasons not to go, and the reasons were running out.

Reeve had told me not to. "Nothing in that cell belongs to you anymore," he said, standing in the doorway of Harrow Inn with his century-old face doing its best impression of concern. "You burned the ledger. You named the bones. Let the courts have him."

"The courts have him," I said. "That's rather the point."

He didn't laugh. Reeve rarely laughed, which was one of the many things about him I was still deciding how to feel about.

Sheriff Dane Colm — former Sheriff Dane Colm, though the paperwork hadn't caught up to his shame yet — was awaiting trial on four counts of murder, seven counts of falsifying records, and one count of something the prosecutor kept calling "abuse of public trust," as if trust were a municipal water supply he'd been tapping illegally. Which, in a way, he had.

I signed the visitor log. The deputy behind the glass looked at my name, then at me, then back at my name, doing the arithmetic everyone in Ashcombe had been doing since the funeral that wasn't mine.

"You're supposed to be dead," he said finally. Not hostile. Almost apologetic, the way people are when reality has inconvenienced them personally.

"I got better."

He stamped the form.

* * *

They brought me to a room with a Formica table bolted to the floor and two chairs bolted to opposite sides of it, so that even reconciliation would have to occur at a regulated distance. A window of reinforced glass let in light the color of dishwater. Somewhere down the corridor a door clanged, and I thought: every town has a sound like this underneath it. Ashcombe's was foghorns and church bells. Underneath, all along, it had been this.

Colm came in without cuffs, which surprised me until I remembered that he'd spent twenty years shaking hands with half the deputies in this building. He'd lost weight in custody. His smile — the easy, sunlit smile that had made half the town trust him and the other half vote for him — was still there, but it hung on his face now the way a picture hangs on a wall in an empty house. Technically present. Nobody home to look at it.

"Wren Voss." He sat down across from me. "You know, you're the only ghost I know who visits."

"I'm the only ghost you know who won." I stayed standing. It felt important to stay standing, though I couldn't have told you why until later, when I understood it was because sitting down across from him felt too much like taking a chair.

"Sit," he said. "Please. You came forty minutes to stand?"

"I came forty minutes to look at you." I sat anyway. Pride is a luxury; curiosity is cheaper.

For a while we just looked at each other. He had been the warden of the Circle — Chair Four, keeper of the ledger's second copy, the smiling face that met me at every turn of the investigation with rehearsed deflections. He had also, in the end, tried to kill me in a tunnel full of named bones, and failed, and been undone by the very system he'd corrupted, judged by the rule he'd broken: no punishment without unanimity. Three chairs had turned against him. Reeve had cast the deciding voice. And Dane Colm, who had spent his career deciding who deserved correction, had been corrected himself, by the only law the Circle had ever truly honored.

"You look well," he said. "Dead suits you."

"Flattery. In here, that must cost extra."

"They gave me a radio," he said, ignoring this. "I listen to the Ashcombe station. They did a piece on the tunnel bones last week. All those names read aloud." His voice did something complicated. "Forty-one names. I knew maybe thirty of them. Did you know that? Thirty of them, I could have told you their middle names."

"That's not the comfort you think it is."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

* * *

Here is what I had prepared on the drive: a speech. I am a woman who prepares speeches the way other people prepare casseroles — in advance, in quantity, against emergencies. The speech went roughly: *You do not get to make us the same. You broke the rules we all agreed to break, and the difference between us is not that I'm better than you, it's that I stopped.*

It was a good speech. I'd workshopped it against the windshield for most of Route 9.

But Colm started talking first, and Colm, it turned out, had also prepared a speech, and his was better rehearsed, because he'd had weeks in a cell with nothing to do but rehearse.

"We're the same," he said. "That's what you can't admit. That's why you came all the way out here — not to gloat. To check. To see if there's a difference, and if there is, to measure it." He leaned forward, and the empty-house smile flickered into something older and more tired. "You falsified autopsies, Wren. For years. You buried a mill owner's crimes under your own signature. You sat in our circle and heard your own case read aloud, and you didn't walk out — you took the chair. You voted. You learned the rules. You liked the rules. Don't tell me you didn't. You liked being the one who knew."

The room was very quiet. Down the hall, that door clanged again, patient as a tide.

"Go on," I said, because it was true, all of it, and I had promised myself on the night I burned the ledger that I would never again flinch from a true thing just because it was aimed at me.

"I added names," Colm said. "You know that. Four names. Men who hurt people I—" He stopped. Started again. "My daughter was fourteen. One of them was her teacher. The courts gave him six months and a register listing nobody checks. So I wrote his initials in the ledger, and I pressed a violet, and I waited for March." He spread his hands. "And you want to know the difference between us? Here it is, Wren. There isn't one. You'd have done the same. Maybe you *did* do the same — you just used tape recorders instead of violets."

There it was. The speech I hadn't planned for. And the terrible thing — the thing I want to be honest about, because honesty is the only inheritance I've got left — is that some part of me, some cold administrative part that had spent twenty years weighing bodies and causes of death, wanted to agree with him. Wanted the symmetry. Symmetry is comforting. Symmetry means nobody has to be the monster alone.

But I thought of Ellery. Pale, nervous Mr. Ellery, who had paid me triple my fee to prove a death hadn't happened yet, who had left a pressed violet on a stripped motel pillow like a signature or an apology, who had been trying to stop the Circle from within and had paid for it with his life — a life I wouldn't be able to properly mourn for another few chapters of my own story, though I didn't know that yet. I only knew the shape of the hole.

"No," I said.

Colm raised an eyebrow. Twenty years of small-town authority lived in that eyebrow.

"No, we're not the same," I said. "Listen carefully, because I've only got the one visit in me. You're right about the facts. Every fact. I falsified reports. I took the chair. I voted. I liked knowing things." I held up a hand before he could enjoy that. "But here's what you keep missing, Dane, and it's the whole ballgame. When I found out the ledger had my sister's killer in it — marked corrected, avenged by strangers before I ever knew to ask — do you know what I felt?"

"Gratitude," he said softly.

*Rage,* actually. Grief. Vertigo. The floor of my whole life tilting. But I said, "I felt robbed. Because it wasn't mine. Because some committee of masked strangers decided my grief for me, scheduled it in ink, filed it under corrections, and moved on to the next name. And when I found the next name after mine was a child — a little girl being punished for what her father did — I didn't reach for the ledger, Dane. I reached for the fire."

His jaw worked. "You broke unanimity."

"I *broke the ledger.* Unanimity was your god, and look where it got you — devoured by your own congregation. That's the difference. Not purity. I don't get purity; I buried that with my fake corpse. The difference is direction. You corrected downward — toward the weak, toward whoever your anger landed on. I'm trying, God help me, to correct upward. Toward the systems. Toward the daylight. Toward naming things instead of numbering them."

"You sound like a eulogy."

"I've had practice. I attended my own."

And there — briefly, treacherously — the real smile. Not the sheriff's smile. Something younger, from before the Circle, before the violets, maybe before Ashcombe itself had gotten its hooks into him. "God," he said. "You always were funny. Nobody ever mentions that. Everyone talks about the coroner with the steady hands and the sad eyes, and nobody says she's funny."

"My sister thought so."

The name landed between us on the bolted table like a coin dropped in a quiet church. He knew whose name I meant. He'd read the ledger entry himself — initials, date, correction confirmed — the year after the crash on Route 9.

"I didn't do that one," he said quietly. "For what it's worth. That was before my time on the chair."

"It isn't worth anything," I said. "That's the thing I came to tell you. It isn't worth anything, and neither is your remorse, and neither is my guilt. Worth is what you do forward. You did four murders forward. The court will do the arithmetic."

* * *

We sat a while longer. I asked him questions — not for the trial; the prosecutors had everything they needed from Reeve's sealed confession and my testimony. I asked because a coroner's habit dies hard: you document, you witness, you write the name down. Where the second ledger's pages went. Who else had known about Ellery. Whether he had hesitated, that night in the bone gallery, when I refused to kill him and made the observers watch mercy happen in real time.

He answered most of it. On the question of hesitation, he was silent for a long moment, and then he said, "You stood there with a femur in your hand and offered me a way out that didn't require me to win. Nobody had ever done that. Everything in that crypt was win or be corrected. And you just... stood there. Being different." He rubbed his eyes with both hands, and for a second he looked like a tired man instead of a fallen idol, which is the more damning costume. "Yeah. I hesitated. It didn't take. But it happened."

"Noted," I said, and meant it in the professional sense: entered into the record, dated, witnessed. Closure doesn't require absolution. It only requires the record to be true.

When the deputy knocked to say time was up, I stood, and Colm stood too, and for a moment we were just two people who had known each other badly for years, separated by a table bolted to the floor so that nothing could be thrown across it.

"There's one more thing," I said. I took it from my coat pocket: the pressed violet. Ellery's violet, from the stripped motel room, the pillow, the message nobody had decoded until it was too late. It had ridden in my coat through the funeral, the tunnels, the burning, the exhumation, the long spring. Its petals had gone the color of old paper. It smelled faintly of nothing at all, the way pressed things do — memory pretending to be scent.

"What is it?" Colm asked, and then he saw it, and his face did something I'll be describing to my notebook for a long time. Recognition. Fear. And underneath both, horribly, longing — the longing of a man looking at the emblem of the only church he'd ever believed in, the one that had just eaten him alive.

"Ellery's," I said. "The man you killed. He hired me to prove a death hadn't happened yet. He was trying to save the next name on the list. He was trying to save *me*, and I didn't know it, and you made sure I never would." I slid the violet across the table, into his side of the border, where he'd have to look at it. "I'm leaving it with you. Not as forgiveness. As evidence. Of what the thing you loved actually costs. You'll have years in here to study it. Study it."

"You can't just—"

"I'm a coroner," I said. "I decide what gets left with the deceased. Consider yourself deceased."

The deputy opened the door. Colm picked up the violet with both hands, careful as a man handling ashes, and I walked out without looking back, because looking back is for ghosts, and I was busy being born.

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had turned gold the way April afternoons do when they're showing off. I sat in my car in the visitor lot and did not start the engine for a good ten minutes, because my hands wanted to shake and I preferred they did it privately.

Closure without absolution. That was the phrase I'd carried in like a talisman, and it turned out to be exactly right and exactly insufficient, the way phrases are. What I actually felt was lighter and stranger: the sensation of setting down a bag I'd forgotten I was carrying. Not peace. Relief's rough draft.

I drove back toward Ashcombe with the windows down, past the marsh, past the mill — closed now, under investigation, its owner's taped confession doing its slow legal work — past Miller's Bend, where the tide had once delivered a body right on schedule, eleven years late.

And it was somewhere around the second mile marker that the thought arrived, the one that would rearrange my next several weeks, the way thoughts do when you've finally cleared enough room for them.

Ellery. Mr. Ellery, pale and nervous, triple fee, pressed violet, dead in a ditch under a false name because false names were how the Circle worked and how he'd hidden inside it. I had visited Colm's cell and left my grief there like a coat check ticket.

But Ellery had no cell. Ellery had a plot in the county potter's field with a number where a name should be, and I was, as of this month, the only person in the world who knew he deserved better — and the only person stubborn enough, unemployed enough, and recently-dead enough to find out what that name was.

I reached over to the passenger seat, where my new notebook lay — blank pages, no dates ahead, no judgments pending — and at a red light I wrote a single line:

*Find out who he was.*

The light turned green. I drove home to Ashcombe, and the fog came off the water to meet me like it always did, and for the first time in my life it felt less like a shroud than like a curtain going up.
