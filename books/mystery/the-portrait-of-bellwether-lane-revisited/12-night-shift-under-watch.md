## 12. Night Shift Under Watch

The car appeared on a Tuesday, though Petra wouldn't understand the significance of that until later, when she'd mapped the whole surveillance window against the depot's delivery schedule and found nothing — no pattern, no reason, no logic except the logic of someone who wanted her to feel watched without ever quite catching the watcher at it.

It was a gray sedan, the color of dishwater, parked across Bellwether Lane from the Calloway Cabs depot with its nose angled toward the garage doors. Not directly opposite. Not so close as to be obvious. A careful forty feet up, in the shadow of a sycamore whose roots had buckled the sidewalk into a low ridge, so that the car sat slightly nose-down, like an animal lowering its head to drink.

Petra noticed it at 2:47 a.m., during the dead hour between the last airport run and the first hospital pickup, when the city exhaled and the dispatch radio went quiet enough to hear the refrigerator in the break room cycling on and off. She was standing at the big window with her third coffee of the shift, watching the rain come down in that fine, indecisive drizzle that never quite committed to being weather, and she saw the sedan's exhaust plume — a soft gray ghost rising from the tailpipe, curling, dissolving.

An idling engine at three in the morning, in the rain, outside a taxi depot.

She had been a night dispatcher for eleven years. She knew every legitimate reason a car might sit outside the depot at that hour: a driver waiting out a fare, a delivery truck early, a couple arguing in private, an insomniac who liked the hum of the garage. She catalogued them all automatically, the way she catalogued everything, and the sedan fit none of them. Its wipers weren't on. Its interior light never came on. Nobody got out, nobody got in. It just breathed there in the dark, exhaust rising like a signal fire in slow motion.

Petra did not go outside. She did not turn on the floodlights. She did nothing at all except lift her mug in a small, private toast toward the windshield, a gesture she was almost certain couldn't be seen through the rain and the reflection of the depot's fluorescent glow, and then she went back to her desk and wrote in her personal log — the one that lived in a composition notebook, not in any company system — the time, the plate's partial number as best she could read it, and the words *first night*.

Because she already knew, with the cold certainty of a woman who had spent her career learning that coincidences were just patterns that hadn't finished introducing themselves, that there would be a second night.

There was.

* * *

The second night, Wednesday, the sedan came at 1:15 and stayed until 4:40. Same spot under the sycamore. Same dishwater gray, though now Petra had time to study it properly through the depot's long lens — a pair of binoculars she'd bought two years ago for no reason she could name and had never once used until now. She read what she could read: a rental bar code sticker on the rear bumper's lower left corner, half peeled. A parking stub under the wiper, the kind issued at the municipal garage on Delmore Street, eleven blocks away. And a faint, rain-blurred shape on the rear windshield — the ghost-outline of a sticker that had been removed, scraped off with a razor but not washed, the adhesive still holding its silhouette like a shadow of a shadow.

Someone who didn't want to be identified. Someone careful enough to rent a car and strip its markers, but not careful enough to account for adhesive residue and a parking stub they'd forgotten was under the wiper. Careful, but tired. Or careful, but rattled.

Petra logged it all. Then she did the thing that eleven years of night shifts had trained into her the way water trains a stone: she thought three moves ahead.

If someone was watching the depot, they were watching for one of two things. Either they were watching *her* — which meant the third envelope, the one that had appeared in her dispatch tray with its torn ledger page and its message — *you're close enough to be dangerous* — had been a warning issued by someone with eyes on the building. Or they were watching for *Iris* — which meant they believed, as Petra had come to believe, that the dead woman was not dead, and that whatever trail she'd laid through the city's fare records and rain-slicked back streets would eventually lead home to Bellwether Lane.

Either way, the watcher was waiting for movement. For Petra to go somewhere. For someone to arrive. For the trap Iris had built to spring.

Fine, Petra thought, setting down the binoculars. Let's give them a trap of their own.

* * *

The depot's dispatch system was old — older than Petra, in its architecture, though the hardware had been swapped out twice — and one of its oldest features was also its most useful: the ability to log a call without ever transmitting it. A phantom booking. A fare that existed only in the system's memory, timestamped and routable and completely fictional. It was a feature designed for training new dispatchers, so they could practice assigning cars to calls without confusing the actual drivers, and in eleven years Petra had used it perhaps a dozen times.

She spent Thursday afternoon, her off hours, building a fiction.

The decoy calls had to be perfect. Too obvious and the watcher would smell the bait; too subtle and they'd miss it entirely. Petra thought about what she herself would do, if she were the one in the sedan, and worked backward from there. A watcher couldn't hear the radio traffic inside the depot — the building was cinder block and the windows were double-paned — so they'd be watching for *physical* signals. Cars leaving. Lights. Petra herself, moving.

So she built the fiction in layers.

Layer one: at 11:50 p.m. Thursday, she logged a phantom pickup for 12:30 a.m. at the Delmore Street municipal garage — the same garage whose parking stub sat under the sedan's wiper. A call from a payphone, the kind of call that couldn't be traced to a phone, only to a place. The destination: the riverfront industrial lots, a dead end of shuttered warehouses where nothing happened at any hour and a car could sit for an hour without anyone noticing.

Layer two: she asked Dov to stage it. The old mechanic owed her nothing and knew too much already, but when she'd laid it out for him over bad coffee in the break room — *I need a car to roll out at 12:25, lights on, idle at the curb for ninety seconds, then drive off like it's got somewhere to be* — he'd looked at her for a long moment with those eyes that had been waiting twelve years for someone to ask the right questions, and he'd said only, "Which car?"

"Unit nine. The one with the transmission whine. It'll sound wrong leaving the lot, and wrong is memorable."

Dov almost smiled. "You want them to follow it."

"I want them to *choose* to follow it. There's a difference."

Layer three was Petra herself. At 12:40, ten minutes after unit nine rolled out on its ghost fare, she would leave the depot on foot — umbrella up, coat collar turned, walking the six blocks to the all-night diner on Corliss where she ate breakfast on every shift. A predictable movement. A *baited* movement. If the watcher followed her, they'd leave the sedan empty under the sycamore, and Petra would know they cared more about her than about the depot. If the watcher stayed, they cared about the building. And if the watcher split — one to follow the taxi, one to follow the woman — then there were two of them, and Petra needed to know that most of all.

She wrote the whole plan in the composition notebook, tore the page out, and burned it over the break room sink, watching the ink curl and blacken and go to ash, and she thought, not for the first time that week, of Marcus Hale — of whatever he had burned in his own sink the night she'd sat across from him asking careful questions — and she wondered if burning things was simply what people did when the truth got too heavy to carry in the open.

Then she went to work.

* * *

The rain came back at midnight, harder this time, a real rain with intent behind it, hammering the depot's flat roof in a static hiss that swallowed the sound of the garage doors. Petra sat at her console with the lights dimmed to their night setting, the radio murmuring its low traffic of insomniacs and night workers, and across the street the sedan slid into the sycamore's shadow at 12:05 — fifteen minutes earlier than the night before. Eager. Or nervous.

*Good*, she thought. *Nervous people make mistakes.*

At 12:25, unit nine rolled out of the garage with its transmission whining like a cat in a bag, dome light on, wipers slapping, and turned left toward Delmore at exactly the speed of a driver with a fare waiting. Petra watched the sedan through the binoculars. For eight full seconds, nothing. The exhaust plumed. The wipers stayed off.

Then the brake lights flared — once, briefly, the driver's foot hovering — and the sedan pulled out from under the sycamore and followed unit nine into the rain.

*One*, Petra thought, and her heart was going hard now, not from fear but from the fierce, clean joy of a pattern resolving, the same joy she felt when a cipher's columns suddenly aligned. *They took the bait. They care about the cars.*

She gave it four minutes. Then she logged a second phantom call — 1:15 a.m., pickup at the depot itself, destination the Corliss diner — and walked out into the rain with her umbrella and her coat and her own heartbeat, and she did not look at the empty space under the sycamore because looking was a tell, and she had stopped being prey somewhere around the second night, and prey looked at the thing that hunted it.

The diner was warm and yellow and smelled of burnt coffee and bacon grease, and Petra took her usual booth by the window because the plan required her to be visible, and she ordered her usual eggs because the plan required her to be *predictable*, and all the while she watched the reflection in the diner's glass — the street, the rain, the gray sedan that came sliding up Corliss at 12:51 and parked two doors down with its lights dying and its engine ticking.

They'd left the taxi. They'd left the depot. They'd followed *her*.

She ate her eggs slowly. She read the paper she'd brought for the purpose. She paid in cash, tipped in cash, and left at 1:30 exactly, and the sedan's engine turned over before she'd reached the door, and she felt its headlights come up behind her like a hand on the back of her neck — not close, never close, forty feet back, the same careful distance — all the six wet blocks to the depot, where she went inside and locked the door behind her and stood in the dark of the dispatch room with the rain hammering the roof, and let herself feel it.

Fear, yes. But underneath the fear, something harder. Something that had been forming all week like a stone forming under pressure.

*They're not watching the depot. They're watching me. Which means the letters weren't a warning to back off. They were a warning to stay on the path — or to get off it. And whoever is in that car doesn't know which I've chosen.*

* * *

The third night, Petra changed the game.

She'd spent her Friday afternoon at the county records office and the DMV counter, and by evening she had two facts and a suspicion. Fact one: the sedan's partial plate — she'd finally gotten a clean read of four characters through the binoculars on the second night — matched a midsize gray sedan rented Thursday morning from a chain lot at the airport, paid for with a corporate card belonging to a security consulting firm whose name meant nothing to Petra but whose address meant everything: it was a mail drop two blocks from Bellwether Lane. Fact two: the firm had been incorporated eleven years and four months ago.

Twelve years since Iris Vane disappeared. Eleven years and four months since someone had built a shell to watch the streets around her empty house. The timeline didn't point at Hale — Hale's obsession, whatever shape it took, had the desperate heat of the present tense, not the patient architecture of a decade. This was something older. Something that had been waiting in the dark since before Petra ever opened those first two envelopes.

The suspicion was simpler: whoever rented the car had done it *Thursday morning*. The day after Petra's second night of watching. The watcher wasn't a single continuous surveillance — it was being renewed, re-tasked, re-hired, in response to *her*. Someone was feeding instructions to someone else, and the instructions were changing as fast as Petra moved.

Which meant the sedan was the hand, and the hand had an owner, and the owner had made one mistake Petra intended to exploit.

At 11:30 p.m. Saturday, she logged a phantom call — pickup 12:15 a.m., Bellwether Lane itself, the corner in front of the boarded Vane house, number nine. Destination: the storage facility on the east side, the one whose unit number sat at the end of Iris's decoded route like a signature at the bottom of a letter. Then she called Dov.

"Unit nine again?"

"Unit nine again. But this time, when you get to Bellwether, don't stop. Roll past number nine slow — slow enough to be reading the numbers — and keep going. I want whoever's watching that house to see a Calloway cab slow down in front of it at midnight. I want them to *wonder*."

"And what will you be doing?"

Petra looked out the big window at the rain, at the empty space under the sycamore where the sedan would not be tonight, because tonight the watcher had a new post. "I'll be doing what dispatchers do," she said. "Listening."

* * *

It worked almost too well.

At 12:19, eight minutes before the phantom pickup, a second car — a dark hatchback, plates mud-smeared, the kind of vehicle that lived its whole life in the periphery of attention — turned onto Bellwether Lane from the north end and slowed as it passed number nine. Petra wasn't there. She was at her console, eleven blocks away, watching it all through the depot's street camera — a grainy wide-angle feed she'd never had reason to review before this week and now checked like a pulse. The hatchback rolled past the Vane house. Rolled past the sycamore. Pulled into the shadow of the shuttered laundromat on the corner and went dark.

Waiting for the cab.

At 12:15, unit nine made its slow, whining pass, dome light on, wipers going, and Petra watched the hatchback's silhouette lean forward — someone inside craning to see the car's number, the driver's face — and then the hatchback's headlights came on and it followed, exactly as designed, exactly as hoped, trailing unit nine east toward a storage facility where there was no fare and no passenger and no one at all except an empty taxi rolling through the rain on a ghost call.

Two cars. Two nights. Two sets of eyes, and Petra was certain now they didn't belong to the same body. The sedan's watcher was professional — careful distances, rental cars, stripped stickers. The hatchback was amateur — mud on the plates, impatience in the lane changes, and a silhouette in the driver's seat that Petra had studied frame by frame until her eyes ached, because it looked wrong for a hired hand and right for something else. Young. Broad-shouldered. A way of hunching over the wheel that she'd seen once before, in a photograph on the mantel of Marcus Hale's living room: Daniel, seventeen years old, in a Calloway Cabs training jacket, hunched over the wheel of a car he was learning to drive.

She couldn't prove it. Not yet. The camera was too grainy, the rain too heavy, the silhouette too much a matter of posture and instinct. But instinct had closed every case she'd ever worked, and instinct was telling her that the son of the man who wrote the false confession was out tonight following a taxi to a storage unit, and that the father who'd written it didn't know — or worse, *did*.

Petra sat back in her chair in the dark dispatch room, the radio murmuring, the rain coming down, and she opened the composition notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in her small, square, unbreakable hand:

*Two watchers. One paid, one family. They know about the storage unit. Which means Iris's route isn't secret anymore — it's a race.*

She looked at the words for a long time. Outside, the sycamore dripped into the empty parking space where the gray sedan used to breathe its gray ghost into the night, and eleven blocks away, in a storage unit at the end of a dead woman's trail, something waited — and Petra understood, with the cold clean clarity of three o'clock in the morning, that she was no longer deciding whether to go there.

She was deciding whether to get there first.

Her phone buzzed against the console. An unknown number, local prefix. She stared at it through two full rings, three, and on the fourth she picked up, and a voice she had heard only once before — low, level, a woman's voice with rain in it, the voice from the second letter, the voice that had promised *the ledger will finish what the confession started* — said four words:

"They're ahead of you."
