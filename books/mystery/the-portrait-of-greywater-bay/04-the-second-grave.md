## 4. The Second Grave

The flagstone had been bothering Ines for three days.

It sat in the northeast corner of the crypt, beneath the oldest arch, where the founding families' markers thinned out and the stone gave way to rougher work—slabs laid by masons who hadn't bothered to sign their craft. Every other tile in that corner bore a name, a date, a small carved flourish. This one was blank. Worse than blank: it was *new*. The mortar around its edges was a paler grey, the kind of pale that only came from a mix poured within the last hundred years, in a crypt whose oldest stones predated the town itself.

Ines had catalogued eleven thousand artifacts in his nineteen years as archivist. He knew the cathedral's bones the way other men knew their own hands. And this stone did not belong.

He knelt beside it now, past midnight, with a lantern turned low and Odette crouched across from him, her grandfather's testimony folded in her coat pocket like a talisman.

"You're sure about this," she whispered. Not a question. A dare.

"The mortar is wrong," Ines said. "Everything else down here is right. That's how I know."

Odette worked the pry bar along the eastern edge while he held the light. The slab resisted, then sighed—a wet, grinding sound, like something being asked to speak after a long silence—and lifted. Beneath it, not the packed earth and old rubble he'd expected, but a seam of darker soil. Turned soil. Soil that had been opened and closed again by human hands.

Ines's mouth went dry. "We'll need brushes. Trowels. I have conservation tools upstairs—"

"Ines." Odette's voice had gone flat and careful. She was pointing at the edge of the excavation, where the lantern light caught something pale curved against the dark earth. Bone. A finger's length of it, lying exactly where a hand might rest if a hand had been folded there by someone in a hurry.

Neither of them spoke for a moment. Above them, through feet of stone, the cathedral held its breath.

"We document first," Ines said finally, because procedure was the only language he had for terror. "Photographs. Measurements. We don't touch anything until—"

"Until what?" Odette said. "Until the people who put him here come back with better shovels?"

She was right, and they both knew it. They worked through the small hours with the desperate tenderness of people unwrapping something fragile and furious. The remains lay shallow—no more than four feet down, curled on one side, wrapped in what had once been canvas and was now a lacework of rot. No coffin. No marker. No name.

But the skull—

Ines lifted it last, cradling it in both palms the way he'd handle a folio worth more than the building around them, and turned it toward the light. There, above the left ear, a depression the size of a silver dollar. Clean-edged. Deliberate. The kind of fracture that came not from a fall but from an instrument swung with intent.

"He didn't drown," Odette said quietly. Her voice cracked on the second word. "My grandfather wrote that he saw Vane alive at the docks that night. Alive, and arguing. And they called him a drunk and buried the paper." She looked up at Ines, and her eyes were bright and hard. "They hit him. They carried him down here. They laid him under their own church."

Under their own monument. Under the marble names of the men who would be remembered as founders, benefactors, builders of Greywater Bay.

Ines set the skull down with exaggerated care, because his hands were shaking and he could not afford to drop it. "The will," he said. "Vane left everything to the town. Not to his partners. If he'd died naturally, they'd have inherited nothing but his goodwill. But if he disappeared—if he *drowned*—before the ink dried on any challenge—"

"Then the town keeps his money," Odette finished, "and they keep their secrets, and everyone lives happily ever after except the man under the floor."

Triumph should have tasted sweeter. Instead it tasted of dust and old violence. Ines photographed everything twice, sketched the position of the remains in his notebook, bagged a sample of the canvas wrapping, and recorded the exact coordinates of the flagstone in the archive's master plan. Evidence. Chain of custody. The words felt absurd down here in the dark, but they were all he had.

They re-laid the slab precisely as they'd found it, swept the mortar dust from their knees, and climbed the stairs into a dawn so grey it looked like the inside of a closed book.

They never got the chance to come back.

* * *

Odette heard the news at the harbormaster's office, where she'd gone to copy her grandfather's original testimony from the municipal files before someone thought to lose those too.

The clerk behind the desk wouldn't meet her eye. "You should hear it from your friend," he said. "Or from a lawyer. Whichever comes first."

By noon the whole town knew: the cathedral crypt had been broken into overnight. Desecration of a grave site. The police had sealed the crypt with chains and posted a constable at the door. And the man they wanted to question—the man whose keys, whose access, whose *nineteen years of unsupervised nights* made him the obvious suspect—was Ines Pemberton.

She found him on the steps of the archive annex, sitting very still, his hands flat on his knees, watching two officers carry out boxes of his own records as evidence. His face had the terrible calm of a man reading his own obituary and finding it well-written.

"They're saying I moved him," Ines said without looking up. "That I dug him up to fake the discovery. That the skull fracture proves nothing because *I'm* the one who reported it." He laughed once, a dry sound with no humor in it. "Nineteen years cataloguing other people's lives, Odette. Do you know what my file says? It says *no known associates*. It says *quiet, reliable, unremarkable*. Apparently unremarkable men crack skulls and stage miracles."

"Then we prove otherwise."

"With what?" Now he looked at her, and she saw the fear underneath the calm, and worse—the beginning of doubt. "Every record that could corroborate us is in that building. My notes. My photographs. The master plan with the flagstone marked. They've seized all of it. And the families—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Marisol tried to reach me this morning. She said the mayor's office has already released a statement. *An isolated act of vandalism by a disturbed employee.* Disturbed. Nineteen years, and that's my epitaph."

Odette sat down beside him on the cold stone step. Below them, Greywater Bay glittered, indifferent and beautiful, keeping its secrets the way it always had.

"My grandfather spent forty years being called a liar," she said. "He died with that testimony in a drawer because no one would listen. I am not letting them do it twice. Not to him, and not to you." She stood, brushing off her coat with the same brisk motion she'd used on the crypt floor. "They took your evidence. Fine. We'll get more. Four families, Ines. Four houses full of descendants who've been sitting on this for a hundred years, and at least one of them is tired of carrying it."

Ines looked up at her—at this woman he'd known for barely a week, who believed him when the whole machinery of the town had decided not to—and felt something shift in his chest, unfamiliar and frightening. Not hope exactly. Something more stubborn than hope.

"The Harrowgates still hold the old dock ledgers," he said slowly, standing, his mind already moving, cataloguing, planning. "And Eleanor Vane's line didn't die out entirely. There's a granddaughter. Elderly now. Lives outside town." He paused at the bottom of the steps, looking back once at the cathedral rising grey against the grey sky, at the constable at its door, at the chains on the crypt where a murdered man lay waiting for justice.

"They think sealing the door ends this," Ines said. "They forget I've spent my whole life learning what's written down. And somewhere in this town, in some drawer or attic or dying memory, the rest of it is written too."

He turned away from the cathedral and did not look back again.
