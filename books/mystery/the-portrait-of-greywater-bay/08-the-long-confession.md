## 8. The Long Confession

They brought him to the archive at dawn, because the police station had burned with the east wing and there was nowhere else that felt neutral anymore.

His name was Tobias Merrick. He was sixty-one years old, a retired harbormaster, a man whose family name was carved into the cathedral's benefactor wall in letters taller than Ines's hand. He sat in the reading room with his wrists bandaged from the fire, hands folded on the table like a schoolboy waiting for a scolding, and he looked at the scorched gap in the shelves where the parish registers used to live, and he wept.

Not quietly. Not the dignified weeping of a founding-family heir. He sobbed, wet and ugly, into his ruined hands, while Odette stood by the door with her arms crossed and Marisol hovered near the window, and Ines sat across the table and waited, because waiting was the one skill his whole life had trained him for.

"I didn't mean to burn it," Merrick said finally. "I was meant to take the ledgers. Take them and burn them somewhere else. But the smoke alarm—the heat—I panicked." His voice cracked. "A hundred years my family kept this quiet. A hundred years. And I'm the one who ends it because I couldn't carry a box down a staircase."

"Tell me what you were keeping quiet," Ines said.

Merrick looked up. His eyes were red-rimmed and, beneath the fear, there was something else—something Ines recognized from his own mirror. Relief. The terrible relief of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"You've seen the grave," Merrick said. "Under the flagstones. You've seen what we did to him."

"We?"

"My grandfather. And Vane's partners. Halloran and Ashcombe and my grandfather Merrick." He said the names like he was spitting out stones. "The four of them built this town together, and then Vane decided it should belong to the town. Everything—the docks, the mills, the land—to the people who worked it. Can you imagine? 1924, and he wanted to give it away."

"So they killed him."

Merrick flinched as if struck. Then, slowly, he nodded. "He found the smuggling ledgers. The dockworker wages—their real wages, what they actually got after the 'fees' the company deducted. He'd written it all down. He told them he was going to the newspapers on the first of October." Merrick's voice dropped to almost nothing. "On the twenty-ninth of September, they took him out on Halloran's boat. My grandfather held him down. Halloran—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Halloran did the rest. They weighted him, but the river gave him back three days later, downstream, and there were too many eyes on the body, so they couldn't just make it disappear. So they made the drowning official instead. Paid the coroner. Wrote the register entry. Buried an empty coffin with full honors while the whole town wept for their beloved founder."

"And the real body," Ines said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "The crypt."

"The crypt was half-built then. The oldest section, under the founders' chapel—they poured the floor themselves, the four of them, before the masons came in. There's a space under the northeast corner. They laid him there at night, faceup, arms crossed, like a king." Merrick laughed, and it was the worst sound Ines had ever heard. "Like a king. Under their own monument. They put him in the one place no one would ever dig—in the house of God, under the men who killed him. Grandfather said it was penance. I think it was a joke. I think Halloran thought it was funny."

* * *

The confession took two hours. Odette wrote it down longhand because the recorder felt like a violation, and Merrick, oddly, seemed to prefer it that way—a man's words going onto paper in ink, witnessed, the way things had been done when the crime was committed. He signed each page. His hand shook, but the signature was steady; a lifetime of signing harbor manifests had left its mark.

When it was done, Marisol drove to the county seat to fetch the district attorney herself, because the Greywater police could not be trusted—the chief was an Ashcombe by marriage—and she returned at dusk with a tired woman named Prosecutor Vance, who read the confession twice, asked Merrick three questions, and then closed the folder and said the sentence Ines would hear in his sleep for weeks afterward.

"It's true," Vance said. "And none of it matters."

The reading room went very still.

"Statute of limitations," she said. "Murder has none, but everyone who committed it is dead. Conspiracy has lapsed. Evidence tampering, falsification of records—all expired decades ago. The arson last week, yes, that we can charge. Merrick will answer for the fire. But Aldous Vane's murder?" She spread her hands. "There is no court in this country that can try a corpse's killers a hundred years on. I'm sorry. You have a confession and bones and letters, and legally, it amounts to a historical curiosity."

"A historical curiosity," Ines repeated.

"Ines." She softened. "I believe you. Every word. But the law is not a moral instrument. It's a procedural one, and its procedures ran out on this a long time ago."

She left. Merrick was taken into custody, and as the officers walked him past the reading room he turned his head and looked at Ines, and mouthed something. *Tell them anyway.*

* * *

That night Ines sat alone in the archive among the smell of smoke and the gaps where history had been torn out, and he let the despair come, because he had learned that if you didn't let it come, it stayed longer.

It wasn't fair. That was the whole of it, the child's cry at the center of everything. It wasn't fair. A man had been murdered by his friends, erased from his own town, buried beneath his murderers' names, and the law—the great machine Ines had been taught to trust, the system of records and registers and sworn testimony that was his entire religion—simply shrugged. The statute had run. The ink had dried. Case closed.

He thought about Eleanor's letters in their box. *They think the river will keep their secret,* she had written. *But rivers always give back what they are given.* Eleanor had waited her whole life for justice and died without it. Her granddaughter had handed Ines the truth like an inheritance, and now he was supposed to file it away under HISTORICAL CURIOSITY and go back to cataloging?

No.

The despair didn't leave. It changed shape. He felt it happen, sitting there in the dark among the surviving shelves—felt it turn over like a stone in a current, like the river giving back what it was given. If the courts wouldn't hear it, then the courts were not where truth lived. Truth lived here. In records. In testimony. In the open, where people could see it.

The law had failed Vane. The town had not yet had the chance to.

Ines pulled a fresh sheet of paper toward him and began, in his careful archivist's hand, to plan a ceremony.

* * *

Marisol found him there at midnight, still writing, surrounded by lists.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I feel wonderful," Ines said, and found, to his surprise, that it was nearly true. "Marisol. Centenary night is Saturday. The whole town will be here for the reburial of the empty coffin—the mayor's ceremony, the speeches, the cameras. Every descendant of every founding family, standing in front of the crypt they sealed."

"Yes."

"They're expecting a funeral." He looked up at her. "Let's give them an exhumation."

Marisol studied him for a long moment. Then she smiled—not her gentle verger's smile but something sharper, older. "You'll need witnesses," she said. "People who won't look away."

"Odette first. Then the dockworkers' descendants. Everyone Eleanor's letters named. Everyone the wage ledgers cheated." He stood, and his knees cracked, and he didn't care. "The law says it's over. Fine. Then we take it out of the law's hands. We put the bones on the altar and the letters in the air and we let Greywater Bay be the jury."

Outside, the fog was rolling in off the water, the way it did every night, patient and gray and full of what it carried. Somewhere out there, the river moved in the dark, giving back what it was given.

Three days, Ines thought. Three days until centenary night.

He picked up his pen and began to write the invitations.
