## 3. Little Hawley

The town of Hawley's Cross had exactly two topics of conversation: the weather and the tragedy. The weather was discussed to avoid the tragedy, and the tragedy was discussed only in the lowered voices people reserved for churchyards and cancer diagnoses. So when Zephyrine Okafor walked into Marlowe's Bakery on the morning after the body was found, she knew within four seconds—by the way conversation dropped like a dropped hymnal—that the crypt had made it out of the cathedral and into the wild.

"Morning," she said, because invisibility was a discipline, and disciplines required practice even on bad days.

"Morning, Miss Okafor." Ted Marlowe rang up her usual—a cheese roll and a lemon tart she would pretend was for a colleague—and his eyes did the thing everyone's eyes were doing that morning, which was flick toward her face and away again, as though she might be wearing the dead man's coat.

She wasn't. She was wearing her second-best cardigan, oatmeal-colored, deliberately forgettable. She had spent ten years perfecting the art of being overlooked, and if there was one thing she knew how to do, it was dress like furniture.

"Heard you had some excitement down at the old place," Ted ventured.

"We had a break-in. The police are handling it." *A break-in.* As though someone had stolen the candle fund.

"That's not what I heard."

"I imagine not," said Zephyrine, and took her roll, and left before he could ask what she'd heard, which was nothing, which was everything, which was a drowned man in a locked room wearing her mother's brooch.

* * *

Back in the archive, she tried to work. This was her remedy for most afflictions: cataloguing. There was always something to catalogue. The cathedral's records went back to 1740, and the paper was patient in a way people were not. She set herself the small, achievable task of rehousing the vestry minutes from 1988–1991, and got through eleven pages before she realized she'd read the same sentence about roof slates six times.

The man in the crypt had been wearing her grey coat.

Not *a* grey coat. Not a similar coat. Her coat—the one hanging on the peg by her desk at this very moment, because she'd worn the black one yesterday, and the grey had been here all along, safe, untouched. Which meant somewhere in this town there existed a copy of her coat, cut from the same cloth, fitted to the same shoulders, and pinned at the throat with her mother's brooch, the enamel wren with the missing left eye, the one thing of her mother's she had never once let out of her sight until—

Until she had. Three weeks ago. To be cleaned. Mrs. Petchey's alteration shop, the only place in Hawley's Cross that did such work, run by a woman who had held the brooch in her palm and said, "Lovely old piece, this. You don't see enameling like it anymore."

Zephyrine pulled the accession ledger toward her, not because it would help, but because her hands wanted something official to do. Then she stopped.

The fingerprints. Detective Inspector Ferro had said it almost casually yesterday, at the bottom of the crypt stairs, watching her more than the body: "We'll have identification soon enough. Prints are in the system." And now the whole town was whispering, and whispers in Hawley's Cross traveled faster than fire.

She should wait for the police to tell her. That was the correct procedure. That was what an invisible person would do—wait, keep her head down, dust something.

Zephyrine Okafor had been invisible for ten years, and it had kept her alive and small and safe, and this morning she found she simply couldn't bear it.

She took off her reading glasses, put them back on, and walked upstairs to find Father Anselm, who was ninety-one years old and technically retired and technically not supposed to know anything, and who therefore knew everything.

* * *

Brother Anselm was in the warming kitchen doing a crossword, which he did every morning between Lauds and whatever it was he did next, and he looked up at her over his spectacles with the mild expression of a man who had been waiting for her since dawn.

"You'll want the register," he said.

"The burial register?"

"For 1994." He turned back to his crossword. "Seven across is 'penitent,' by the way. If you were going to offer help, which you weren't."

"How did you know what I—"

"Child." He filled in the word without looking up. "I sealed that crypt door myself, thirty years ago, on the bishop's orders. Do you think I haven't been counting the days? Do you think anyone in this building has thought about anything else since yesterday?" He set down his pen. His hands, she noticed, were not quite steady. "The register's in the strong room. Second shelf. And Zephyrine—" he used her name rarely enough that it landed like a stone in still water, "—whatever you find, remember that paper tells the truth even when people don't want it to. That's why we keep it. That's why they fear us."

"They?"

"Archivists." He smiled faintly. "The only priesthood with subpoena power."

* * *

The strong room smelled of cold stone and vellum, and Zephyrine loved it the way other people loved their mothers' kitchens—which was, she reflected, pulling down the 1994 register, perhaps not entirely a coincidence.

The book was fat and buckram-bound, its spine cracked white with use. She carried it to the reading table under the window and opened it to October.

And there it was. Halfway down the page, in the careful copperplate of the parish secretary of the day:

*Hawley, child, ("Little Hawley"), aged 7. Buried 14 October 1994. Crypt, north wall, family vault. Service conducted by Rev. A. Mowbray. Shroud and pall furnished by E. Okafor, seamstress.*

Her mother's name. Her mother's actual name, in ink, in the record of the boy in the crypt—the boy whose fingerprints now belonged to a drowned man thirty years older than the child who'd gone into the ground.

Zephyrine sat very still. Outside the window, a jackdaw argued with the guttering. Somewhere below her feet, forty feet down, the crypt waited with its broken seal and its terrible guest.

Her mother had sewn the shroud. She remembered—she had been nine, and memory from nine arrives in fragments, lit like slides in a darkened room: her mother at the kitchen table late at night, pins in her mouth, white silk pooling like spilled milk; her mother crying without noise, which Zephyrine had assumed at the time was about the sad work of sewing for a dead child; her mother saying, *hold this edge flat, Zephie, straight as truth,* and little Zephyrine holding it, proud to be useful, while the radio murmured about a christening party at Ashcombe Hall where something had happened that the radio was not permitted to say.

Three weeks later her mother was dead. Heart failure, said the doctor. She was forty-four.

Zephyrine had spent thirty years believing that. She had built her entire quiet, careful, hidden life on top of that belief the way you build a house on a foundation you're not allowed to inspect.

Now the foundation was making noises.

*He lived,* she thought, and the thought arrived with the calm of things that are true whether or not you can survive them. *Little Hawley didn't die in 1994. He survived. He grew up. He came back. He came back wearing my coat.*

But then—who was in the coffin? Who had her mother shrouded, whose funeral had the whole town attended, whose grief had made five families untouchable for three decades?

The register offered no answer. Registers never did; they recorded what was done, never what was meant. But there was one more line, added later in different ink, brownish and official:

*Vault sealed per order of the Consistory Court, 20 November 1994. No further interments. Key retained by Chapter.*

No further interments. For thirty years, no one had gone in and no one had come out, and the town had agreed, collectively, gratefully, to stop looking. Hawley's Cross was very good at not looking. It was practically the local industry.

Zephyrine closed the register and pressed her palm flat against the cover, as if taking its pulse.

"Paper tells the truth," she murmured, "even when people don't want it to."

Then she did something she had never done in ten years of meticulous, deliberate unremarkableness.

She took out her phone and photographed the page.

* * *

Detective Inspector Ferro had been a policeman long enough to know that towns were organisms, and Hawley's Cross was an organism with an immune system. Most places, when a body turned up, the reaction was fear, then gossip, then boredom. Here, the reaction had been—there was no better word for it—*fortification*. Within twelve hours of the discovery, he had received three complaints about police "heavy-handedness" at the crypt door, one letter from a solicitor representing "the interested families" reminding him of data protection obligations regarding the deceased's identity, and a phone call from the mayor's office asking, in a voice like syrup over gravel, whether it wouldn't be best for everyone if the press were discouraged.

Discouraged from what, the mayor hadn't specified. That was the trick of it. Nobody ever specified. They just stood near the well and looked meaningful.

Ferro had grown up in a town like this—not this one, but its cousin, two counties over—and he recognized the architecture of a closed case. Not closed by police. Closed by consensus.

Which was why the fingerprint result, sitting on his desk in its neat Home Office envelope, felt less like an answer and more like a detonator.

He read it again anyway. He was a thorough man; thoroughness was his armor.

*Subject: unidentified male, recovered 12 March, St. Brannoc's Cathedral crypt. Prints matched to records held under: HAWLEY, [name redacted pending next-of-kin verification], DOB 8 June 1987. Record source: juvenile print set taken 1993, Hawley's Cross Primary School, routine collection.*

Born 1987. Dead, officially, in 1994, aged seven. Recovered yesterday, aged thirty-six, drowned in a locked vault, dressed in a woman's coat with a woman's brooch at his throat.

Ferro had worked murders, frauds, one memorable case involving a ferret and an inheritance, and he had learned that the cases that kept him up at night were never the bloody ones. They were the ones where reality had a seam in it—where two true facts refused to lie flat against each other. A boy dies in 1994. A man with the boy's fingerprints drowns in 2024. Both facts were documented. Both facts were true. And between them lay a gap exactly wide enough to hide a murder—or two, or five, depending on how many people had needed hiding.

His sergeant, Wren, leaned in the doorway with two teas and the expression of a woman delivering bad news she'd rehearsed.

"Guv. The school records. 1993 print collection—it was voluntary, parental consent. Guess whose parents consented."

"Hawley's."

"Hawley's. And guess who else was in the class list that year." Wren set a tea on his desk. "Okafor. First name illegible in the scan, but the surname's right. Same school, same year group, give or take."

Ferro looked at the tea. He thought about the archivist—Okafor, the younger one, the one who'd stood at the bottom of the crypt steps yesterday with her hand pressed flat against the wall like she was listening for a heartbeat in the stone. He thought about the way she'd looked at the dead man's coat. Not puzzled. *Recognizing.* There was a difference, and he'd built a career on the difference.

"And the family?" he asked. "Our John Doe. Next of kin?"

"That's the interesting bit, guv. Or the depressing bit. Officially, Little Hawley's next of kin are—" she checked her notebook— "the Ashcombe family. Guardianship granted 1994, after the parents' deaths. Car accident, that autumn. Before the funeral."

"Before the funeral," Ferro repeated.

"Mm. Parents die in September. Boy dies in October. Vault seals in November. Very busy year for the founding families." Wren drank her tea. "Except the boy didn't die. So either he came back from the grave to blackmail somebody, or somebody spent thirty years making sure a living child stayed legally dead. And guv—one more thing. The land registry. The Hawley holdings, the house, the trust—all of it passed on the death certificate in '94. To the guardians. To the Ashcombes. Everything a seven-year-old owned became theirs the day that coffin went in the ground."

Ferro turned the fingerprint report face-down on his desk, which was a habit he had when a document bothered him, as though it might behave better if it couldn't see him.

A dead boy's estate, inherited by the very families who buried him. A living man, returned after thirty years, drowned in the vault where his own legend was entombed. And somewhere in the middle of it, a seamstress named Okafor who had sewn the shroud and died three weeks later, leaving behind a daughter who dressed like furniture and looked at coats like confessions.

"Wren," he said. "Find out everything about the 1994 funeral. Attendees, orders of service, who paid for the flowers. And get me the coroner's file from that year."

"It'll be sealed. Consistory Court order."

"Then get me the *index* of the coroner's file. Even sealed files have indexes, and indexes have page numbers, and page numbers tell you how big the story was." He picked up his tea. "And ask the archivist to come in. Gently. She's the sort who spooks."

"She's the sort who hides evidence, more likely," said Wren. "You saw her face when we mentioned the prints."

"I saw her face," Ferro agreed, "when she realized we'd mention them eventually. Different thing entirely." He sipped. "People who hide evidence are hiding something. People who already know what's coming are hiding from something. I want to know which one she is."

* * *

Zephyrine locked the strong room, climbed the ninety-three steps to the nave, and stood for a moment in the great hush of the cathedral, where the afternoon light came down through the east window in colors the glassmaker had chosen four hundred years ago and God had apparently approved.

Below her feet, behind a door sealed by order and silence, lay a man wearing her mother's brooch.

Above her head, in the clerestory, the jackdaws quarreled.

And in her mind, clear as the day it happened, rose a fragment she had never known what to do with: her mother, the night before the funeral, sitting in the dark kitchen with the finished shroud folded over her arm like a sleeping child, saying—to Zephyrine, or to God, or to no one—*"It isn't him, Zephie. Whatever they tell you. It was never him."*

She had been nine. She had thought her mother meant the sewing wasn't good enough.

Zephyrine Okafor walked back down to the archive, took off her glasses, and allowed herself, for exactly one minute, to be afraid.

Then she opened the accession ledger, turned to a fresh page, and began—in her small, precise, unremarkable hand—to write down everything she knew, dated and signed, because Brother Anselm was right: paper told the truth even when people didn't want it to.

She was on the third line when she remembered the dead man's rented room. The constable had mentioned it yesterday, offhand, to Ferro: a room above the ironmonger's on Cordwainer Lane, taken a month ago, paid cash, searched that morning and found nearly empty.

Nearly empty.

Zephyrine looked at the clock. Ferro's team had finished with the room at noon. The police tape was still on the door, but the constable on it was young, and it was teatime, and she was, after all, the cathedral archivist—with legitimate business in any building that touched the parish rolls.

Cordwainer Lane was a seven-minute walk.

She was out the door before she finished deciding.
