## 15. One of Five Falls

The note arrived the way all of Aldous Pike's communications arrived: folded into thirds, sealed with wax he clearly melted himself over a kitchen stove because no respectable seal ever looked quite that lopsided, and smelling faintly of pipe tobacco and engine oil.

Zephyrine found it waiting in her archive drawer on Thursday morning, which was alarming in itself, because she had taken to locking that drawer after the last note. She stood very still for a moment, listening to the cathedral breathe above her—the creak of old timber, the distant cough of a verger, the pigeons conducting their eternal committee meeting in the belfry—and decided, as she had decided several times lately, that being frightened was becoming a full-time occupation and she would like a holiday from it.

She opened the note.

*Boathouse. Saturday, six o'clock. Come alone. I have something you'll want to see before I decide whether to give it to you or to someone else. — A.P.*

Below it, in different ink, added later and pressed hard enough to tear the paper slightly: *Bring nothing. Tell no one.*

"Charming," Zephyrine said to the empty archive. "Absolutely charming. You'd think a man who owned half the riverfront could afford a secretary to be menacing on his behalf."

But her hands were not steady as she refolded it. Aldous Pike had been the second initial on the ledger—*A.P.*, written in a hand that grew shakier as the payments continued through the years, until the final entry, dated only fourteen months ago, looked like it had been signed during an earthquake. Whatever Pike knew, whatever guilt he carried, it had been eating him alive. And men who were being eaten alive did one of two things: they confessed, or they made arrangements.

She spent the day doing what she always did when she couldn't bear to think about something, which was to catalogue something else with ferocious precision. By four o'clock she had reorganized the entire collection of parish newsletters from 1971 to 1989 by month rather than year, which no one had asked for and no one would ever notice, and which gave her exactly forty minutes of genuine peace.

It was Ferro who broke it, appearing at the archive door with his coat still wet from the rain outside and his expression doing something complicated.

"Miss Okafor."

"Detective Inspector." She set down her pencil with the deliberate calm of a woman who had practiced this exact moment in her head. "You've come about the hymnal."

He blinked. "What hymnal?"

And there it was—that small, cold drop of water down the spine. Because if Ferro hadn't come about the hymnal, then he didn't know about the copy of the ledger she'd hidden inside *Hymns Ancient and Modern*, number 214, wedged between the pages where the spine had cracked. Which meant he'd come about something else. Something newer.

"What hymnal?" he repeated, watching her face with the patience of a man cataloguing her the way she catalogued newsletters.

"Nothing," she said. "A joke. Archivists are famous for them."

"We're not known for that," Ferro said. "I've read the literature." He came into the room without being invited, which she noticed, and closed the door behind him, which she noticed more. "Aldous Pike is dead."

The pencil rolled off the desk. Neither of them moved to pick it up.

"Drowned?" she heard herself ask, because apparently her mind had already gone there, already assembled the pattern, already drawn the line between a drowned stranger in a locked crypt and whatever came next.

"In his boathouse. Slipped on the wet boards, hit his head, went into the water. That's what it will say, anyway, once we write it." Ferro pulled out the chair across from her desk and sat down heavily, and for the first time since she'd met him he looked tired—not the performative tiredness of policemen in novels, but the real kind, the kind that comes from having been awake since three in the morning looking at things you can't unsee. "Except his boots were dry, Miss Okafor. Both of them. And there's a bruise on his temple consistent with being struck by something with a rounded edge—a winch handle, perhaps—and the water in his lungs is river water, not boathouse bilge. He went into the river after he was already unconscious. Or he was put there."

* * *

They talked for two hours, and by the end of it Zephyrine had told him nearly everything, which surprised her more than it seemed to surprise him. The ledger. The five initials. Verity Ashcombe's tea and her soft, terrible offer. The altar cloth and the letters worked into its hem. She kept back exactly two things: the hymnal, and the note from Pike—which felt less like deception now than like holding one card close to the chest in a game where everyone else was cheating.

"You were going to meet him," Ferro said, when she finally showed him the note after extracting a promise—verbal, unsatisfying, but a promise—that she wouldn't charge her with obstruction for having kept it. "Saturday. Six o'clock. Alone."

"He wanted to talk. He sounded frightened."

"People who sound frightened are either about to confess or about to run. He wasn't running. He'd just had the boathouse repainted." Ferro rubbed his eyes. "Which means he was getting ready to stay. Which means somebody decided he shouldn't."

"You think it's the same person who killed Hawley."

"I think Hawley was blackmailing five families. One of them is dead of natural causes—Verity Ashcombe is dying whether anyone helps her along or not. Another is dead in a boathouse with dry boots. That leaves three people who might reasonably worry they're next, and one person who has every reason to make sure the list gets shorter before it gets read aloud." He stood, buttoning his coat. "Don't go anywhere alone. Don't answer your door after dark. And Miss Okafor—if anyone else sends you a note inviting you to a secluded location at dusk, do me the professional courtesy of telling me first."

"I'll consider it."

"That's not a yes."

"It's archivist for 'I'll weigh the archival evidence and render a decision.'"

For a moment—one brief, unguarded moment—Ferro almost smiled. Then he left, and the cathedral settled back around her, and Zephyrine realized she was sitting alone in a stone room beneath a building full of hiding places, and that somewhere out in the rain was a person who had killed twice and knew her name.

* * *

Friday passed in a haze of dread disguised as diligence. She answered the archive door only after asking who it was, twice. She ate lunch at her desk rather than walking to the bakery on Cornmarket, and missed the almond croissants so acutely that it nearly qualified as grief. Mrs. Petchy, the sacristan, brought her tea at three and stayed to gossip about the flower rota, entirely unaware that she was performing the single most valuable service of the day, which was simply being another human body in the room.

"You look peaky, dear," Mrs. Petchy said, patting her hand. "Are you sleeping?"

"Not especially."

"Well. You want camomile. My mother swore by it. Of course my mother also swore by putting a key down her back for sciatica, so I'd take her medical advice with a grain of salt, but the camomile can't hurt." She gathered her cup. "Oh—I nearly forgot. There was a gentleman asking after you yesterday. In the vestry."

Zephyrine's tea went cold in her hands. "Asking after me."

"Handsome older fellow. Well-dressed. Said he was researching his family and needed the burial registers." Mrs. Petchy frowned, remembering. "Only, when I said you kept those and you'd be along directly, he said never mind, he'd found what he wanted, and off he went. Quick as anything. Left a smell of cigars behind him."

"Did he give a name?"

"He did, but I've lost it—it was one of those family names, you know, the ones on all the plaques. Ashcombe? No—" She snapped her fingers. "Pike. That was it. Mr. Pike."

Aldous Pike, dead since Thursday morning, had been in the vestry on Wednesday asking for burial registers. Or someone had been wearing his name.

"Mrs. Petchy," Zephyrine said carefully, "did he seem ill to you? Unsteady? As though he'd been drinking?"

"Oh, no, love. Steady as a table. Lovely manners. Bit sad around the eyes, the way rich men get." She paused at the door. "Why? Is something wrong?"

"No," said Zephyrine, who was beginning to suspect that everything was wrong, and that it had been wrong for thirty years. "Everything's fine."

* * *

Saturday arrived grey and dripping. At five o'clock, against every instruction Ferro had given her and several she'd given herself, Zephyrine put on her grey coat, pinned her mother's second-best brooch at her throat—for luck, though luck seemed a thin thing to pin one's hopes to—and walked down through the town toward the river.

She did not go to the boathouse. She wanted that understood, even if only by herself and God and whichever pigeon happened to be following her. She went instead to the public footpath along the far bank, where she could see Pike's boathouse from a distance of two hundred yards, lit by its own small window, sitting on its stilts above the black water like a heron pretending to be asleep.

If Pike was there, she would watch. If he wasn't, she would go home and tell Ferro everything, including the hymnal, including the note, including the fact that a dead man had walked into her vestry. She had rehearsed the speech on the walk down. It had sounded very brave in rehearsal.

At ten past six, the light in the boathouse window went out.

Zephyrine stood very still on the muddy path. Boats did not turn their own lights off. Men going about evening business did not, generally, sit in darkness. Her sensible self—the self that had spent a decade perfecting invisibility, that knew every sightline of this town the way other people knew their own kitchens—told her to walk away now, quickly, without running, the way prey animals did it.

Her other self, the new one, the one that had been growing all week like a plant in a dark cupboard, took out her phone and called Ferro.

"It's me," she said, when he answered. "I'm on the towpath opposite Pike's boathouse. Don't lecture me. The light just went out and nobody turned it off."

There was a silence on the line long enough that she checked whether the call had dropped.

"Stay exactly where you are," Ferro said. "Do not cross the footbridge. I'm fifteen minutes away."

"Fifteen minutes is a long time."

"It's the time there is." A pause. "Miss Okafor. Is there any chance he's expecting you tonight?"

"He was expecting me Saturday at six. It is Saturday at six-ten."

Another silence, longer, and in it she heard him stand up, heard a car door, heard the world on his end start moving fast.

"Get off the path," he said. "Now. Behind you, up the bank, into the trees. Go."

She went. She climbed the slick grass with her heart hammering and crouched among the alders, mud soaking through her coat, and watched the boathouse across the water. For a while, nothing. Rain on the river. A moorhen complaining somewhere. The ordinary machinery of an English evening, ticking along.

Then a shape came out of the boathouse door—low, quick, sure-footed on the wet boards in a way that spoke of long familiarity. It paused at the top of the steps. It looked across the river, directly at the trees where she was hiding, for a count of five that lasted approximately a geological era.

Then it went down the steps, untied a small dinghy, and rowed away downstream, unhurried, oars dipping without a sound, until the dark took it.

Zephyrine stayed in the trees until Ferro's torchlight found her, and even then she couldn't speak at first. He hauled her up the bank, wrapped his own coat around her shoulders without comment, and walked her to the car, and it was only when the doors were shut and the engine was warming that she managed it:

"The man in the vestry. Mrs. Petchy saw him Wednesday. He gave the name Pike. But Pike was already dead by Thursday morning, and the man I just watched row away—he knew these banks. He knew that boat. Ferro—" Her teeth were chattering; she hated it and kept talking anyway. "Whoever he is, he's not a stranger passing through. He's someone who belongs here. Someone who's been here the whole time."

Ferro stared through the windscreen at the black river, at the empty boathouse, at the town beyond it with its lit windows and its plaques and its founding families, and when he finally spoke, his voice was flat and cold and certain.

"Yes," he said. "And I think I know whose face was under that hat. Because I've seen it hanging in your archive, Miss Okafor. Third portrait from the window. The one they dedicated at the anniversary dinner twenty years ago."

He started the engine.

"There's a photograph of him shaking hands with the bishop."
