## 3. The Widow Who Remembers Nothing

**INTERVIEW NOTES — V. ALDER, 10:14 A.M.**
*Subject claims significant memory impairment. Claims she cannot recall the night of March 3, 1995. Claims, in fact, to recall very little of anything after the Nixon administration. Note: subject's tea service is arranged with the handle turned precisely forty-five degrees. Her pill organizer is sorted by day and color. Senility does not alphabetize.*

I wrote that last line in my head, not on the page. Some observations you keep in reserve.

The drawing room at Saint Alder's had been restored within an inch of its life—the fire's scorch marks sanded out of the floorboards, the smoke damage repainted a color the catalog would call *oyster*. But the window over the eastern wing still held glass from before the fire, wavy and green-edged, and through it the light came in bent, the way light comes into water.

Vivienne Alder sat by that window in a chair the color of clotted cream, wearing cashmere the color of her chair, looking for all the world like an ornament someone had forgotten to dust.

"Mrs. Alder," I said. "Oscar Dravenmoor. Meridian Mutual. We spoke on the phone."

"Did we?" Her voice was dry as old lace. "I don't recall."

"You'll forgive me if I take notes anyway."

"Forgive you." She smiled without warmth or malice, the smile of a woman who has outlived everyone entitled to judge her. "You were always so careful, Miss Dravenmoor. So *careful*."

I hadn't told her my name was Dravenmoor. I had. On the phone. Three days ago. I checked—I always check—and yes, I had said it twice.

So why did it land like a blade laid flat against the table?

* * *

**INTERVIEW NOTES — cont.**
*Subject asked about weather. Subject asked about my journey. Subject offered tea, which I declined, which she ignored, which means there is now tea in front of me going cold at a rate I can calculate to the minute. Stalling tactics noted. My stalling or hers—unclear.*

Here is what I remember about Vivienne Alder from thirty years ago, unbidden, unwanted, arriving the way all my memories arrive—complete, timestamped, indifferent to whether I've invited them:

March 4, 1995. The morning after the fire. She stood on the gravel drive in a nightgown under a borrowed coat, ash in her silver hair like premature snow, and when the investigators arrived she said, in a voice of terrible calm, *"He was reading in the study. He was always reading in the study."*

She never varied from that sentence. Not once, across four interviews. He was reading in the study. He was always reading in the study. I remember thinking, at twenty-two, how composed she was. How brave.

I remember thinking nothing else about it at all. That's the part that keeps me up now.

* * *

"I understand this is difficult," I said. "The anniversary coming up."

"The anniversary." She turned her teacup a quarter-inch on its saucer. "Thirty years. They want me to sit at the head of a table and be sad on schedule. Julian's idea. Julian has ideas the way other men have debts."

"Can you tell me about your husband?"

"Edmund." She said the name the way you'd read it off a headstone. "Edmund read in the study."

The sentence again. Word for word. Thirty years, and the sentence hadn't weathered a single syllable. Most people's memories erode; hers had been lacquered.

"Mrs. Alder," I said carefully, "when we spoke on the phone, you said you might not remember our conversation. You said your doctors call it—"

"*Dementia of the Alzheimer's type.* Yes. My doctors say a great many things. They say them loudly, because they assume the ears go before the mind." She looked at me then—fully looked, and her eyes were pale gray and quick as a bird's. "They are wrong about which goes first."

* * *

**INTERVIEW NOTES — cont.**
*Subject lucid beyond stated condition. Recommend reassessment of witness reliability. Recommend—*

I stopped writing. Because she said:

"You were the girl in the doorway."

The pen made a small involuntary stroke across the page, a comet tail of ink.

"What doorway?" I asked. My voice did something I didn't authorize. It went young.

"The north door. Of the study." Vivienne set down her cup with a click precise enough to be a metronome. "The night of the fire. There was a girl standing in the doorway. Young. Dark coat. She had a notebook, and she wasn't writing in it. Just holding it. Watching the room like—" she searched, found, delivered— "like she was photographing it with her eyes."

My memory produced the scene before I could stop it: the study, gutted, black water pooled in the grooves of the charred floorboards, investigators moving through the wreckage with their slow professional gravity. And in the doorway—me. Twenty-two years old, new badge, new coat, notebook open and blank in my hand. I remembered being there. Of course I remembered being there. I remember everything.

What I had never remembered—not until this moment, not until she handed it back to me like a returned letter—was that anyone had *seen* me.

"You were there," I said. "That night. At the scene."

"I lived here, Miss Dravenmoor. Where else would I be? My husband had just burned to death in his own house." She smoothed her sleeve. "Everyone was very kind about getting me out of the way. Everyone was very kind about everything. Kindness is what people do instead of telling you things."

"And you saw me."

"I saw a girl who was watching Inspector Corliss the way the rest of us watched the fire." Her gray eyes settled on mine and stayed. "As if he were the thing that couldn't be explained."

* * *

The tea cooled. I let it. Outside, a gardener's leaf blower droned across the lawns like a distant propeller, some machine of the present insisting on itself while the past filled the room.

I should have ended the interview. Protocol said so. The subject was hostile, or confused, or performing senility, or performing lucidity—any of those meant terminate and regroup. Instead I heard myself ask:

"Why do you remember me? After thirty years. With your condition."

"My condition," Vivienne said, "is extremely convenient. Do you know what people tell a woman who can't remember? Everything. All of it. Their whole lives. I have heard more confessions than any priest in the county, and none of them ever worried, because none of it would stick." She leaned forward slightly, and the light through the old warped glass broke across her face into pieces. "But here is what my doctors don't know. What fell out of my mind was arithmetic. Dates. Where I put my glasses. What stayed—" she tapped her temple once, lightly, "—was the important inventory. And one item on that inventory is a girl in a doorway whose face I have seen exactly twice. Once in 1995. Once three days ago, on my telephone screen, when a woman named Dravenmoor called about a dead man wearing stolen clothes."

"You recognized my voice."

"I recognized *you*. Voices don't change as much as people hope." She folded her hands. "Now. Ask me what you came to ask me, and I will answer honestly, which is more than anyone in this house has managed in thirty years."

* * *

**INTERVIEW NOTES — cont.**
*Subject states willingness to give full account. Caution: subject's account may be performance. Caution: subject's account may be true. These are not mutually exclusive.*

"What happened the night of the fire?" I asked. "Your account. Not the file's."

Vivienne was quiet long enough that the leaf blower outside died and restarted, died and restarted.

"Edmund was not reading in the study," she said.

The sentence I had carried for thirty years—he was reading in the study, he was always reading in the study—cracked straight down its middle.

"He told me that afternoon he'd be working late. He said he was expecting someone. He was frightened—not of the person, mind. Frightened of what the person knew. There's a difference, and I could hear it." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, folded in her lap, had gone white-knuckled. "I went to bed. At some hour I woke, and the house was wrong. You know how a house sounds when it's wrong. I came downstairs and the east wing was lit orange, and there were men on the lawn, and Inspector Corliss took my arm and walked me to the drive and said, *'Mrs. Alder, whatever happens, you were asleep. Edmund was reading in the study. Say only that.'*"

The wind moved the curtains. Neither of us moved at all.

"He gave you the sentence," I said.

"He gave everyone sentences. That was his gift. He could hand a person their memory like a coat—here, wear this, it fits, don't look at the label." For the first time, her composure flickered, and underneath it was something older and rawer. "And I wore mine for thirty years, Miss Dravenmoor, because I was eighty-percent certain no one would ever check the seams. And then a dead man turned up in clothes from *your* closet, and I thought: ah. Someone is checking the seams at last."

"Who was Edmund expecting that night?"

"I don't know. I have spent thirty years not knowing, and I am telling you it is the only thing left that frightens me." She rose, slowly, joints announcing themselves, and crossed to the window. "But I know what Edmund kept in the boathouse. And I know that in the weeks before the fire, he burned papers in that fireplace every evening—except he burned them *wrong*, too fast, like a man destroying evidence rather than filing correspondence. And I know that when the girl in the doorway appeared at the scene, Inspector Corliss looked at her—" she turned, and her eyes were terrible and clear, "—not with surprise. With *recognition*. As if he'd been expecting her too."

* * *

**INTERVIEW NOTES — final**
*Interview terminated by subject at 11:02 a.m. Subject's parting statement recorded verbatim below.*

She walked me to the drawing-room door herself, refusing the cane propped beside her chair, and at the threshold she stopped and looked up at me with those quick gray eyes.

"One more thing," she said. "The girl in the doorway. In 1995. She was holding her notebook upside down. Did you know that? Gripped it so hard the cover bent, and it was upside down, and she never noticed, because she wasn't seeing the room at all. She was seeing something she'd already decided not to see."

"That's not in my notes," I said. "From that night. None of that is in my notes."

"No," Vivienne agreed softly. "It wouldn't be."

She closed the door with a click as precise as her teacup, and I stood in the corridor of the restored house with my perfect memory replaying the scene on command—the doorway, the notebook, the bent cover, upside down, upside down—and for the first time in thirty years, I could not trust the playback. Because either Vivienne Alder had just described a detail no living person could know.

Or she had just told me what I myself had seen, and buried, and never once written down.

In my car, engine idling, I opened my notebook to a fresh page and wrote the date, and beneath it, hands not entirely steady:

*Tomas Reyes. Train station alibi. Find him before someone else does.*
