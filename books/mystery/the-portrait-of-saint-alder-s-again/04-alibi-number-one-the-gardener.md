## 4. Alibi Number One: The Gardener

Tomas Reyes lived forty minutes south of Saint Alder's, in a ground-floor apartment in a town called Marrow Creek where the main street had three bars, two churches, and one hardware store that doubled as the post office. I knew the address because I had written it down in 1995, in blue ballpoint, on the back of an interview card that still smelled faintly of the cigarette smoke in Corliss's office. I had not needed to write it down. I never need to write anything down. But Corliss liked his investigators to have paper trails, and so for thirty years I have kept notebooks full of things my mind already held, like a woman with perfect vision buying glasses she doesn't wear.

The apartment building was stucco the color of weak tea. A plastic flamingo stood in the gravel yard, faded to pink chalk. On the door of unit 3B, someone had taped a hand-lettered card: *Knock hard. He doesn't hear well.*

I knocked hard.

It took four minutes. I counted them, because counting is what I do when the alternative is feeling. In those four minutes I catalogued: the smell of jasmine from a neighbor's trellis, the buzz of a failing fluorescent tube behind me, the sound of a television inside playing something with applause, and beneath it all, a wet, laborious breathing that I recognized before I could name it. My father died of emphysema when I was nineteen. I know what lungs sound like when they are negotiating their final contract.

The door opened on a chain, then fully, and there he was—Tomas Reyes, seventy-one years old if the records were right, looking ninety. He was thin in the way that illness thins people, not age: the flesh drawn back from the structure of him, cheekbones and collarbones announcing themselves like scaffolding under a tarp. His hands were enormous. Gardener's hands, knuckles swollen, nails permanently crescented with soil even now, even after thirty years away from any garden worth the name.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he said, in a voice like dry leaves:

"You're the girl."

Not *the investigator*. Not *the insurance woman*. Not *Miss Dravenmoor*.

*The girl.*

"I'm Oscar Dravenmoor," I said. "I worked the Alder case in 1995. I'd like to ask you some questions about your statement."

He didn't move. Behind him, the television applauded something no one had done.

"They told me you were dead," he said.

"Who told you?"

"The inspector." He pronounced it carefully, the way you handle a word that once burned you. "Corliss. Years ago. He came here, maybe fifteen years back, sat where you're standing, and told me the case was closed and everyone who touched it should stay closed too." His eyes, watery and dark, moved over my face. "You look alive to me."

"I'm alive," I said. "May I come in?"

*

The apartment smelled of menthol and old coffee. A recliner faced the television; a hospital-grade oxygen concentrator hummed in the corner like a patient insect, its tubing snaking across the carpet to a chair by the window where Reyes clearly spent his days watching the gravel yard and the plastic flamingo and whatever else Marrow Creek offered, which was not much. Photographs covered one wall. Children, grandchildren, a younger Reyes standing in front of hedges trimmed so precisely they looked drawn rather than grown. The Alder hedges. I recognized them instantly—the yew walk along the east terrace, the topiary peacocks flanking the conservatory steps. In the photograph he wore a green canvas apron and held shears the size of my forearm, and he was laughing at whoever held the camera.

"You kept the picture," I said.

"It was a good job," he said. "Thirty-one years. Edmund Alder paid fair and asked twice a year how my mother's hip was doing. You don't forget a man like that." He lowered himself into the recliner by degrees, an operation that seemed to cost him, and gestured at the kitchen chair. "Sit. Ask. I'm dying anyway; nothing costs me anything now."

I sat. I took out my notebook, though I did not open it. It is a prop. It has always been a prop. People tell you more when they think you are recording than when they know you are memorizing.

"Tomas," I said, "on the night of October ninth, 1995, where were you between ten o'clock and midnight?"

He almost smiled. "You've asked me that before. Word for word. Same pause after 'midnight,' same little tilt of the head. You used to tilt your head when you waited. Do you know you do that?"

I hadn't known. It unsettled me more than it should have—that this dying man held a version of me from thirty years ago, some small physical signature I hadn't catalogued in myself. I am the only archive I trust. Apparently I don't even read myself completely.

"Indulge me," I said.

"I was at the train station," he said. "Marrow Creek station. The eleven-forty freight comes through but doesn't stop; the last passenger train north was nine-fifty. I went down to see off a friend, Esteban Luna, who was moving to Sacramento. We drank coffee from the machine. He got on the nine-fifty. I stayed and watched it go, because that's what you do for a friend, and then I drove home and went to bed." He recited it flatly, without inflection, a catechism worn smooth by thirty years of repetition. "And the next morning the house was burning and Mr. Alder was dead inside it, and the police came, and I told them the same thing, and they gave me back my ticket stub and said thank you very much."

"And the stub," I said. "The eleven-forty platform ticket. Time-stamped 9:47 p.m., validating your presence at the station until nearly ten."

"Yes."

"That stub anchored your alibi. And your alibi anchored the timeline. If you were at the station, then the fire's origin window narrowed, and every other account had to fit around yours." I heard my own voice go clinical, the way it does when I'm frightened. "You were the fixed point, Tomas. Everything else turned around you."

The oxygen concentrator hummed. Somewhere outside, a dog began barking at nothing and lost interest.

"Mr. Dravenmoor—" he started.

"Dravenmoor is my name. Edmund was Mr. Alder."

Something moved through his face then, quick as a bird's shadow. Pity, maybe. Or fear wearing pity's coat.

"Mr. Alder," he corrected softly. "Yes."

I leaned forward. "Here's what I've come to ask you, and I want you to understand why I'm asking. Three weeks ago a man was found dead at the Alder estate wearing clothes stolen from my apartment. Someone is sending me messages, Tomas. Someone wants me to reopen what I closed. And I have begun to suspect that what I closed was never actually solved—it was *finished*, which is a different thing entirely. So I'm going to ask you a question I should have asked thirty years ago, and I want you to look at me while you answer it."

His hands lay open on his knees, palms up, the way a man's hands do when they have nothing left to hold.

"Was the ticket stub yours?"

*

I want to be precise about what happened in the silence that followed, because precision is the only religion I have ever practiced, and what happened tested it.

Reyes looked at me. The television murmured. The concentrator hummed. And in the space of perhaps six seconds I watched a man run a calculation he had been running for three decades—weighing the dead against the dying, loyalty against truth, the cost of a sentence against the cost of carrying it.

Then he said: "No."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"No," he said again, steadier. "It wasn't mine. I never bought a platform ticket that night. Why would I? I drove. I parked in the lot. Nobody buys a ticket to stand on a platform and watch a friend leave." He drew a breath, and it whistled going in, a thin high note like wind under a door. "The morning after the fire, Inspector Corliss came to the estate—I was still there, we all were, nobody slept—and he took me into the potting shed alone. He had the stub in a little evidence envelope. He said, 'This is yours, Tomas. You bought it at 9:47. You were at the station.' And I said, no, sir, I was at the station but I didn't buy—"

He stopped. Coughed, deep and wet, into a handkerchief that came away spotted. When he could speak again, his voice had dropped to almost nothing.

"And he looked at me. That's all. He just looked at me, the way a man looks at a horse he's deciding whether to keep or put down. And he said, 'Tomas. Your sister Rosa. She's been in this country eleven months on a visitor's visa that expired in August. One phone call from me and she's on a bus to the border by Friday.'" Another breath, another whistle. "I had twelve nieces and nephews depending on what Rosa sent home. So I said yes, sir. The stub is mine. I was at the station. Thank you, Inspector."

I sat very still. This is a discipline: when the ground moves, hold still and let it finish.

In 1995, I had held that stub in my own gloved hand. I remembered it exactly—of course I remembered it exactly—Marrow Creek Transit Authority, serial MT-4471902, time-stamp punch slightly rotated, the paper soft at one corner where a thumb had worried it. I had noted the rotation. I had noted the softened corner. I had concluded, in a memo I still can recite verbatim, that the wear pattern was *consistent with ordinary handling prior to collection*, and I had built a cathedral on top of it. Four alibis. One closed case. A commendation letter framed above my desk for six years until I took it down because guests kept asking about it.

The stub hadn't been evidence. It had been a script. Corliss hadn't found Tomas's alibi—he had *issued* it, complete with physical corroboration, and let my memory do the rest. My memory, the thing I trusted over every living soul. He had handed me a forged anchor and watched me swear the ship was seaworthy.

"Why?" I asked. My voice came out level, which surprised me. "Why you? Why make you the fixed point?"

"Because I was nobody," Reyes said simply. "The family had lawyers. The widow had doctors. The son had a country club full of friends who'd swear to anything. Me, I had a sister with expired papers and no one who'd miss an hour of my evening." He managed the ghost of the smile from the photograph. "And because I was the one who watered the east terrace that day. I saw things. Maybe he wanted my evening accounted for most of all."

My head came up. "Saw what?"

He looked toward the window, toward the gravel yard, toward thirty years of distance.

"That afternoon," he said, "around five, maybe half past, I was deadheading the roses along the boathouse path. And a car came down the service road that I didn't know. Dark sedan, county plates. It parked behind the boathouse, out of sight of the house—which is a thing you notice, in a gardener's trade, when a man parks where he can't be seen from windows. And a gentleman got out. Gray suit. Silver hair. Carried a leather case, the kind lawyers carry." Reyes's eyes came back to mine. "He met Mr. Alder at the boathouse dock. They talked maybe twenty minutes. Mr. Alder did most of the talking, and he was angry—not shouting-angry. Cold-angry. The kind where the voice goes quiet. And the gray-suit man kept touching his wrist, checking his watch, like he had somewhere better to be."

"Cold-angry," I repeated. "About what?"

"I couldn't hear. Wind was off the water, wrong direction." He shrugged, and the shrug cost him. "But I'll tell you what I remember, girl, since you're the remembering kind. After the gray-suit man left, Mr. Alder stood on the dock a long time. Just standing. Looking at the water. And then he took something out of his jacket—a flat thing, an envelope or a folder—and he didn't throw it in the water. He walked it up to the boathouse and went inside with it. Locked the door behind him, which he never did. Mr. Alder believed doors were for cities."

An envelope or a folder. A locked boathouse. A man who would be dead within six hours.

Somewhere in my skull, in the immaculate, temperature-controlled vault where I keep everything, a shelf collapsed. Not a memory falling—a *structure*. Thirty years of confident architecture, load-bearing walls I had signed my name to, and I could feel them shifting, settling, groaning under the weight of one dying gardener's afternoon.

"Did you tell Corliss this?" I asked. "The car. The meeting. Any of it?"

"He never asked what I saw," Reyes said. "That's the thing I want you to carry out of here, since you came all this way. In thirty years, he never once asked me what I *saw*. Only where I *was*. There's a difference, girl. Where-you-was is a story you can give a man. What-you-saw is a story he has to take from you." He folded his huge ruined hands together. "You were different. You were polite. You wrote everything down in that little book and thanked me for my time. I thought: there's a girl who will get it right someday."

"I got it wrong," I said.

"You got what you were given," he said. "There's a difference there too."

*

I stayed another hour. It felt obscene to leave quickly, as though speed would confirm that I had come only to extract and not to witness. He told me about Rosa, who had gotten her papers eventually, in '98, through a church program Corliss had mysteriously never mentioned again. She lived in Fresno now, four grandchildren. He showed me photographs. I memorized every face, the way I memorize everything, and for the first time in my life the habit felt less like armor and more like devotion—someone ought to hold these faces somewhere permanent, and today, in this room, that someone was me.

At the door, he caught my sleeve with fingers surprisingly strong.

"The gray-suit man," he said. "You'll want to know if I could identify him again. I could. Silver hair combed straight back, like a movie actor from before the war. Tall. And one more thing I didn't say inside, because inside there's a neighbor who hears everything." He glanced past me at the empty yard, then back. "When he got back in his car, he checked his watch again. Big watch, heavy band. And he stood there a moment in the open door, and the sun caught it, and I remember thinking: that's a strange watch. The hands weren't right. Stopped, maybe. A rich man's watch that doesn't run."

A stopped watch.

I drove north with the windows down and my heart going like a trapped bird, and I did not let myself finish the thought, because finishing it had a shape I already recognized, a shape I had seen three weeks ago on a dead stranger's wrist in a stranger's borrowed coat—

No. Not a stranger's coat. *My* coat. Everything in this case was mine now, everything circled back to me, and the thought arrived unbidden and total, the way the truest ones do:

Corliss wears a watch stopped at 11:47. I have seen it a thousand times and never once asked him why.

I pulled onto the shoulder of Route 9 and sat with the engine ticking and the jasmine air coming through the window, and I understood that the vertigo in my chest was not fear of what Reyes had told me.

It was fear of what I had *already known*, for thirty years, filed neatly in a mind that never forgets—and never, until tonight, had been allowed to *look*.

I turned the car around. There was a photograph I needed to find—the one of a twenty-two-year-old girl standing outside the Alder boathouse, an image that exists nowhere in my perfect memory.

And suddenly I needed to know whose camera had taken it.
