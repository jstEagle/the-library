## 6. The Anniversary Dinner Invitation

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which mattered because Tuesdays were the day I did nothing but file.

I have a system. Thirty years of systems, actually, each one built on the last like sedimentary rock: claims cross-referenced by date, by claimant, by adjuster, by weather on the day of loss. My apartment in the Meridian Building has one wall of shelves and no photographs. Photographs are redundant when you're me. I remember what they'd show.

The envelope was heavy stock, cream, with a deckled edge and my name—*Ms. Oscar Dravenmoor*—engraved in ink so black it looked wet. The return address was Aldermoor House, Saint Alder's Point. There was no stamp. It had been hand-delivered, which meant someone had stood in the hallway outside my door at some point in the last eighteen hours, close enough to hear my television through the wall.

I put on gloves before I touched it. Not because I expected prints—though I found them—but because handling evidence is a discipline, and discipline is the only religion I've ever kept.

The card inside read:

*The family of Edmund Alder requests the honor of your presence at a dinner commemorating the thirtieth anniversary of his passing, to be held at Aldermoor House on the evening of November 9th.*

Below that, in a different hand—a fountain pen, right-slanted, pressure heavy on the downstrokes—someone had added a single line:

*You were there. Come back.*

I sat very still for four minutes and eleven seconds. I know because I watched the clock do it.

Here is what you need to understand about me: I don't forget things, and I don't misplace them, and I don't confuse them. Every moment of my life is filed and cross-indexed, available on demand, in color, with sound. Most people find this impressive for about ten minutes and then find it exhausting forever. I learned early to stop mentioning it. A woman who remembers everything is either a miracle or a threat, depending on who's doing the remembering about whom.

And here is what I remembered just then, unbidden, the way the worst things always arrive: a doorway. Cold air. The smell of smoke that hadn't started yet, or had already ended—I've never been able to fix which—and a man's voice behind me saying, *You saw nothing tonight, Oscar. That's an order.*

Vance Corliss's voice. Vance Corliss's order. And I, twenty-two years old and desperate to be worthy of him, had obeyed it so completely that for three decades I believed I had seen nothing at all.

The dead man in my coat had been found eleven days ago in the east wing of Aldermoor House, wearing my charcoal blazer, my gray trousers, my oxblood scarf—the good cashmere, the one I'd bought myself the year I closed the Alder fire case. His fingerprints were on this envelope now, lifted and matched against the coroner's card in under an hour because I keep a lab contact who owes me favors and doesn't ask why.

Thomas Wren. That was his name, once we finally got it. Retired archivist. No next of kin listed. A man whose entire profession was keeping other people's records straight, found dead in a stranger's clothes, holding a stranger's life up to the light like a message in a bottle.

The prints on the envelope were smudged. Postmortem handling. Someone had pressed his fingers to the paper after he was already dead—or nearly. The lab couldn't tell me which, and I hated them a little for the honesty.

So: a dead man's hands had delivered my invitation. Or a living man had used a dead man's fingers as a signature, which is somehow worse, because it means whoever sent this wanted me to know exactly how personal it was. Not a threat. An RSVP.

I set the card down and did what I always do when the world tilts: I catalogued.

Envelope: cream laid paper, 110-pound weight, engraved not printed—you can feel the die impression with a fingertip, and I could. Card: matching stock. The family crest embossed at the top, an alder tree over water. The typesetting was professional, done within the last two weeks; the ink smell was still faintly present, and engraving ink has a shelf life I could have testified to in court.

The handwritten line was the anomaly. Fountain pen, iron gall or a modern equivalent, written by someone who presses hard. Right-slanted. The *Y* in *You* had a tail that hooked left, a habit I'd seen exactly once before in thirty years of reading documents.

Julian Alder signs his correspondence that way. I'd seen it on the settlement paperwork from 1995, on the thank-you note he'd sent me after the case closed—*Thank you for your discretion*, which at twenty-two I'd taken for courtesy and now understood as tuition paid.

But Julian hadn't written this line himself. I was almost sure. The slant was his; the pressure wasn't. Julian wrote like a man signing checks. This line was written like a man carving something into a door.

Someone imitating Julian. Or Julian writing carefully, deliberately, the way you write when you want your own hand to be recognizable but your emotion to be legible too.

Or a third option I didn't let myself finish thinking, because the third option wore a stopped watch and called me *kiddo* and had taught me everything I knew.

* * *

I spent Wednesday verifying what could be verified, which is my version of prayer.

The dinner was real. The Alder estate office had filed an event permit with the county for November 9th, catering contract with Marchetti's, forty-two place settings. Julian had given an interview to the *Coastal Register* about the "recovered assets" funding a new memorial wing—recovered assets being a phrase that should have made any investigator's teeth itch, since recovered from whom, and by whom, and where had they been hiding for thirty years.

Cordelia Alder was still abroad, according to her assistant in Lisbon, though the assistant hesitated half a second too long before saying it, and I have built a career on half-seconds.

Vivienne Alder had been seen twice that week in the village, buying black gloves and a bottle of sherry, sharp-eyed and upright, telling the pharmacist she was expecting company. The pharmacist told the butcher. The butcher told my lab contact. In a town like Saint Alder's Point, privacy is a rumor everyone agrees to repeat.

And the dead man—Wren—had checked into the Harborside Inn nine days before he died, paying cash, registering under the name E. Alder. The innkeeper remembered him as quiet, "a paper sort of fellow," who asked one question at the desk: whether anyone from the family still visited the boathouse.

The boathouse. Where I'd stood at twenty-two, in a photograph I had never taken, never seen, never known existed until I found it folded in Wren's effects—an image of me that my own memory insisted was impossible, standing outside a building I had no reason to visit, on a night I supposedly remembered perfectly.

My memory does not make mistakes. That has been the load-bearing wall of my entire self. And yet there I was in silver gelatin, younger, thinner, lit by a porch lamp, looking directly into the camera with an expression I recognized as mine but could not place the moment for. Someone had photographed me and then someone had made sure I never saw it, and both of those someones had known me well enough to know that the photograph would eventually undo me more thoroughly than any bullet.

You can't threaten a woman who remembers everything. But you can show her a picture her memory doesn't contain, and watch the whole architecture sway.

* * *

Thursday morning I laid it all out on my kitchen table the way I used to in the field: envelope, card, print card from the lab, photograph of the photograph, timeline notes in my own hand. Evidence doesn't scare me. Evidence is honest even when people aren't.

What scared me was the geometry.

Wren dies wearing my clothes. Wren's dead fingers sign my invitation. The invitation summons me to a room containing every surviving member of the family whose patriarch burned to death while I stood in a doorway and agreed to see nothing. Refuse, and I lose the only access I will ever get to the ground truth—Vivienne's eyes, Julian's slips, whatever Cordelia knows and won't say over a phone line. Accept, and I sit down to dinner with a killer who has already demonstrated fluency in the language of staging: costumes, props, postmarks, corpses arranged like punctuation.

Every instinct I'd honed in thirty years said *stay away*. Every instinct I'd honed in thirty years had also, once upon a time, accepted four immaculate alibis without asking who wrote them.

I made coffee I didn't drink and thought about the handwriting again. The heavy downstrokes. The hooked Y. The sentence itself: *You were there. Come back.* Six words, and every one of them chosen. Not *we request your attendance*. Not the family's voice at all. A personal voice, wearing Julian's slant like a borrowed coat—

like my blazer on a dead man.

That was the thought that finally landed, cold and clean as a scalpel. Whoever sent this had a sense of humor. A terrible, precise, archival sense of humor. They dressed a corpse in my clothes; they dressed a threat in Julian's handwriting. Everything in this case came to me secondhand, worn by someone else, and the question was never *what is this object* but *whose body was it taken from*.

I looked at the invitation for a long time. Then I did something I have done perhaps five times in my adult life.

I picked up the telephone and dialed a number I still know by heart, though I haven't used it in six years.

"Corliss."

"Vance. It's Oscar."

A pause with warmth poured into it, like cream into black coffee. "Kiddo. It's been too long." And then, before I could speak: "Let me guess. Cream envelope. Deckled edge. Somebody's idea of hospitality."

The kitchen went very quiet. Outside, a gull screamed once over the harbor and gave up.

"You've received one," I said.

"I received mine Monday." He said it lightly, but I heard the thing underneath it, the thing he'd taught me to listen for: the beat before a lie settles into its final shape. "Family wants a full house for the anniversary. Sentimental lot, the Alders." Another beat. "You're going, of course."

It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either. It was a door held open, and I have walked through enough of Vance Corliss's doors to know that some of them lock behind you.

"Why do you say that?" I asked.

"Because you're you," he said, and laughed, and the laugh was exactly as I remembered it, warm and gravelled, unchanged by thirty years, and that was its own kind of evidence. "You never could leave a room without knowing what was in it. Go. Wear something nice. And Oscar—" the warmth thinned, just at the edges, like ice over moving water, "—do me one favor. Whatever you see at that dinner, however strange it gets—call me first. Before anyone else. Promise me that."

Thirty years ago, a younger woman with perfect recall and imperfect judgment had promised this man things in a doorway, and it had cost a patriarch his life's truth and cost me—what? I was only beginning to count.

"I'll call you," I said, which was not a promise, and hung up before he could hear the difference.

Then I took out a pen and wrote my reply to the Alder family in my own plain, upright, unremarkable hand, the hand of a woman who has spent her whole life making sure no one remembers her face:

*Ms. Dravenmoor accepts, with pleasure.*

I sealed it, addressed it, and set it by the door for the morning mail. Then I went to my closet and stood in front of it for a long moment, taking inventory of what was missing—the blazer, the trousers, the scarf, all still logged as evidence in a murder no one seemed to be investigating—and chose instead a black dress I'd bought for funerals and never had occasion to wear.

November 9th. Forty-two place settings. One of them mine.

Somewhere across town, someone who knew my closet and my mentor's phone habits and the exact weight of my curiosity was setting another place, and waiting to see if I'd come.

I had six days to learn everything the dead archivist knew.

I turned off the lights and got to work.
