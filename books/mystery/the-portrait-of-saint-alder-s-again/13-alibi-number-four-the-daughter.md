## 13. Alibi Number Four: The Daughter

The call came at 6:14 in the morning, and I remember the exact time because I was awake anyway, because I had been awake since three, because sleep had become a country I could visit but no longer live in.

"Ms. Dravenmoor. This is Cordelia Alder."

I knew the voice before she finished the sentence—not from any recording, not from any file, though I'd read her statement a hundred times over thirty years, four sentences long, the shortest of the four alibis. I knew it because it sounded exactly like what it was: a woman who had rehearsed this call on an airplane for eleven hours and was now discovering that rehearsal is useless against the thing you've been avoiding for half your life.

"You're supposed to land Thursday," I said.

"I landed an hour ago." A pause. The sound of a car—engine hum, tires on wet pavement. "I need to see you. Not at the house. Not anywhere my mother's people can see us. There's a chapel on the coast road, past the old cannery. Saint Brigid's. It's deconsecrated. Nobody goes there."

"I'll be there in forty minutes."

"No," she said, and her voice did something I hadn't expected—it cracked, just once, like ice under a boot heel. "Come now. Before I change my mind. I've changed my mind about this every day for thirty years and I don't trust myself to survive another one."

She hung up before I could answer.

* * *

Saint Brigid's sat on a bluff above the gray water, its steeple leaning like a man listening hard. The doors were open. Inside, the pews had been sold off years ago; only six remained, arranged in two crooked rows, and Cordelia Alder sat in the last one with her coat still buttoned and a travel bag at her feet, as if she hadn't decided whether she was arriving or fleeing.

She was sixty-one and looked fifty, the way money and distance can preserve a person like wax. But her hands gave her away. They were folded in her lap and they were shaking, and when she saw me come down the aisle she didn't stand, didn't offer one of the family's famous composed greetings. She just watched me walk toward her the way you watch something you're afraid will turn out to be a mirage.

"You're smaller than I remembered," she said.

"You were seventeen," I said. "Everyone was bigger then."

That almost made her smile. It died halfway up her face.

"Sit," she said. "Please. If I look at you standing there with that notebook I'm going to lose my nerve, and I flew eleven thousand miles to lose it here instead of in Lisbon."

I sat. Two feet between us, close enough to see that her eyes were Edmund Alder's eyes—the same pale gray, the same unsettling directness—and that she had been crying recently, or not sleeping, or both.

"I read your file," she said. "My brother keeps copies of everything. Julian showed it to me last night, after the rehearsal dinner, thinking I'd want to be prepared. Your report on the fire. Four alibis, all verified. All clean." She turned to face me fully. "You wrote that my statement was 'consistent, cooperative, and unremarkable.'"

"It was."

"It was none of those things," Cordelia said. "It was dictated. Word for word. And you wrote it down and called it the truth, and I have spent thirty years wondering if you were a fool or a accomplice, and I flew here to find out which."

The word *accomplice* landed in the cold air between us and stayed there.

"Tell me what happened," I said.

"That's not how this works." Her chin came up, and for a moment I saw the girl from 1995, the one who'd stood in the drawing room in a borrowed cardigan while men in suits asked her questions. "You tell me something first. One true thing. Then I'll decide whether you get mine."

I thought about what to give her. I had so little left that I trusted. My memory—the archive I'd built my whole self around—had seams in it now, places where Vance Corliss's careful hands had smoothed the record until it read like fiction. But there was one thing I knew was mine, one thing no one had fed me, because no one else had ever known it existed.

"The night of the fire," I said, "I wasn't supposed to be on the grounds. Corliss brought me along as a training exercise—he liked to show me off, make me catalog details he'd quiz me on later. He parked the car at the service gate and went inside to speak with someone. I waited by the boathouse. And when the alarms started, I ran toward the main house, and I stopped in the east doorway, and I saw—" I stopped.

"Saw what?"

"A silhouette on the upstairs landing. Someone standing very still, watching the smoke, not running. Everyone else was screaming and running and that person just stood there. And in my memory, for thirty years, that person has been Vivienne Alder. That's what Corliss told me afterward. He said, 'You saw the widow watching her husband die.' He said it so many times that it became what I remembered." I heard my own voice go flat, the way it does when I'm frightened. "But I've spent the last two weeks taking that night apart, and I can't find a single reason why Vivienne would stand still. And I can't find the original image underneath what he told me. He edited me, Mrs. Alder. He reached into my head and rearranged the furniture, and I let him, because he was the best investigator I ever met and I trusted him more than I trusted my own eyes."

Cordelia was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind came off the water and pushed against the old windows, and somewhere in the walls a bird shifted in its nest.

"He was there," she said. "That night. On the grounds."

The words came out level, but her hands had stopped shaking, which told me everything—that the saying of it was the hard part, and the having-said-it was already changing her.

"Say that again," I said.

"Cordelia," she corrected softly. "If we're going to do this, we use names. Cordelia. And yes. I saw him. Vance Corliss. In the garden, below my father's study window, at quarter past eleven. I know the time because I was sneaking back into the house—I'd climbed out to meet a boy my father hated, which is why my own statement was so short. I lied about where I'd been, and Corliss helped me lie, and in exchange I kept quiet about seeing him." She laughed, a terrible small sound. "Do you understand? My alibi was clean because it was purchased. He took my little sin—a seventeen-year-old sneaking out to kiss a boy—and he built a wall around it, and behind that wall he hid whatever he was doing in my father's garden at quarter past eleven on the night my father burned."

"What was he doing?"

"I don't know. I never knew. I only know what I saw: he was crouched by the study window, and he had something in his hands—papers, maybe, or a case—and when the first alarm went off he didn't run toward the fire like everyone else. He walked. Calmly. Toward the service gate. Where your car was parked." Her gray eyes fixed on mine. "Where *you* were waiting."

The floor of the chapel seemed to tilt. Because I had that piece too—I'd had it all along, filed under things too small to matter: Corliss returning to the car at 11:31, breathing hard, telling me the exercise was over, driving me home in silence, stopping twice to check the rearview mirror. I'd logged his heartbeat as *elevated, consistent with exertion.* Exertion. He'd been walking calmly through a garden, and his heart had been pounding like a man who'd just done the worst thing of his life.

"Why are you telling me now?" I asked. "Thirty years, Cordelia. What changed?"

She reached into her coat and drew out an envelope, old and soft at the corners, and held it without opening it.

"My father wrote me a letter the week before he died. I found it again last month, going through my mother's things after her stroke. He said he'd found something at the office—irregularities, he called them, in accounts he trusted. He said if anything happened to him, I should ask one question of one person." She looked up. "He didn't name the person. He said I'd know them when they came asking the right question. And then two weeks ago, a stranger dies on my family's property wearing your clothes, and my mother starts talking about a girl in a doorway, and my brother announces a memorial wing funded by money nobody can account for, and I realized—" her voice finally broke, properly this time, and she let it— "I realized the question has been waiting thirty years for someone brave enough to ask it, and I am so tired of being afraid."

"What's the question?" I asked, though some part of me already knew, some part that had been assembling it for weeks out of ticket stubs and coached statements and a photograph that shouldn't exist.

Cordelia opened the envelope with fingers that trembled and read aloud, her father's handwriting marching across the page in ink gone brown:

"*If I am taken, find the one who counts other people's memories. Ask them what they counted on the night of the fire. Whatever they say they saw—ask them who taught them to see it.*"

The silence after was absolute. The wind. The bird. My own pulse, loud as surf.

"He meant you," Cordelia said. "He meant Corliss. And he meant you, Oscar—the witness he knew would be standing in that doorway, holding the truth in her head like a loaded gun nobody thought to aim." She folded the letter closed. "So here is my question, and I need you to answer it honestly, because everything depends on it. Are you still his? Is there any part of you that still belongs to the man who built our lies?"

I thought of Marisol Finch packing a suitcase with shaking hands. Of Tomas Reyes dying by inches with a ticket stub that was never his. Of a dead stranger in my own coat, sent like a letter addressed to me alone. Of Vance Corliss's watch, stopped at 11:47, worn like a trophy or a warning.

I thought of the diary in my apartment—eleven years old, childish handwriting, the one record Corliss had never touched because he never knew it existed.

"No," I said. And then, because it deserved more than one word: "I'm mine. For the first time in thirty years, I'm entirely mine. And I intend to spend that ownership putting him in a room he can't talk his way out of."

Cordelia studied me for a long moment, the way her father must have studied ledgers, looking for the irregularity, the number that didn't behave. Then she extended her hand across the two feet of cold air between us, and I took it, and her grip was stronger than her shaking had promised.

"Then we stop being witnesses," she said. "Both of us. Tonight. You and I are going to reconstruct that garden, minute by minute, and we're going to start with the one thing he can't edit—"

She turned the letter over. On the back, in the same browned ink, Edmund Alder had written a single line, a postscript I hadn't seen:

"*P.S. — The ward knows where the second copy is. Tell her the boathouse was always hers.*"

Cordelia looked up at me, and her face was doing something complicated—confusion giving way, slowly, terribly, to recognition.

"Oscar," she said quietly. "Why would my father write that to *you?*"
