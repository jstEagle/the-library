## 1. The Coat You Lent the Dead

The rain came in off the Fens like it had a grudge, and by two in the morning the radio was nothing but static and water.

Oscar Kavanagh worked the night desk at Fenline Cabs the way other people prayed — hands moving, mind elsewhere, half-listening for the voice that mattered. The dispatch room smelled of burnt coffee and wet wool, one lamp, three monitors, and a window that showed nothing but its own reflection and, beyond it, the black sheet of the drainage channels where the town ended and the drowned land began.

The call came in at 2:14. A woman's voice, or something shaped like one, shredded by wind and signal.

"I need a cab. Marsh Road. Near the old sluice."

"Marsh Road doesn't get pickups after midnight," Oscar said, already typing anyway. "Where are you coming from?"

A pause long enough to count raindrops in.

"She's coming from the water," the voice said, and the line went dead.

Oscar sat with the handset against their ear for ten full seconds, listening to the dial tone breathe. Then they did what night dispatchers do: logged it as a prank, flagged the number, and sent it down the queue to Ilori at the station because the number had called twice before that week and Ilori had asked to see everything from it.

The cab went out anyway. Unit 12, Dev Achebe driving, because Dev never said no to a fare and never asked why the address was a sluice gate in a storm. He radioed back at 2:41, his voice wrong in a way Oscar had heard only once before, years ago, on another bad night.

"Osc. There's someone in the water. At the outfall. I can't— I think she's dead, man. I think she's been dead a while."

* * *

By four a.m. the Fens had given her up.

They laid her on the towpath under a tarp that did nothing against the rain, and Oscar stood where the police tape allowed them to stand, which was too close, close enough to see the water beading on the plastic like the ground itself was sweating. Detective Ilori found them there. She always found people; it was her particular genius, making you feel located.

"You dispatched a car here tonight."

"I dispatched a car everywhere tonight. It's what I do."

"To this grid reference. From a caller who quoted your private cell number in the log from Tuesday." Ilori held up her notebook but didn't open it. That was worse than opening it. "Walk me through your evening."

Oscar walked her through it. The prank call. The dead air. Dev's voice cracking over channel three. They kept their hands in their coat pockets because they'd noticed, an hour ago, that they couldn't stop touching the seam of the lining, and Ilori struck them as a woman who catalogued fidgets.

"The caller knew the sluice," Ilori said. "Knew the outfall. Knew that stretch of bank floods above ankle height so no tire tracks survive. That's not a prankster. That's a local with a memory."

"Half this town has a memory. The other half is related to it."

Ilori almost smiled. Then she nodded toward the tarp, where a forensic tech was photographing something laid beside the body on a steel tray: a coat, dark wool, heavy, waterlogged into a shape like a sleeping animal. A scarf, coiled next to it, oxblood red gone nearly black.

"We'll need the victim's name before we release anything," Oscar said. "For the record. For my log."

"We don't have a name yet." Ilori watched them the way you watch a door you suspect of being locked for a reason. "But we have clothing. Distinctive clothing. Hand-mended cuff, left wrist. Charcoal wool, double-breasted, older cut. You own a coat like that, Mr. Kavanagh?"

The rain filled the silence for both of them.

Because Oscar did own a coat like that. Had owned one. It had hung on the back of the apartment door for eleven years, mended at the left cuff by a woman named Petra who'd run the all-night diner and sewed like she argued — quickly, roughly, permanently. It had hung there until six weeks ago, when Oscar came home from a double shift and the hook was empty and the scarf drawer was short one scarf, and they'd told themselves what everyone tells themselves about small losses in cheap apartments: *misplaced, lent out, imagined.*

"I used to," Oscar said.

"Used to."

"It was stolen. Weeks ago. I didn't report it because—" Because reporting felt like inviting the world in. Because the coat was the last thing Petra ever gave them. "Because it was just a coat."

Ilori wrote that down. Oscar watched her write it and understood, with a lurch like a floor giving way, that in her notebook those words were not grief. They were motive.

* * *

They let Oscar go at dawn, which felt less like release and more like being returned to circulation, a coin passed hand to hand until someone checks its face. The rain had thinned to a grey drizzle. The Fens lay flat and shining to the horizon, drained fields below sea level, held dry by pumps and concrete and a hundred years of stubbornness — land that only existed because people agreed very hard that it should.

Oscar drove home past the drainage station, the big brick pump house on the canal, its windows boarded since the county sold it off. Someone had chained the gate properly this time. Someone always did, eventually.

At the apartment, they stood in front of the open closet for a long time. Empty hanger. Empty hook. The shape of absence has a grammar, and Oscar read it now the way you reread a letter after bad news and find sentences you swear weren't there before.

The coat hadn't been stolen. Stolen things disappear. This had been *taken* — carefully, deliberately, weeks in advance, the way you gather props.

Someone had dressed a dead woman in Oscar Kavanagh's coat and sent her into the Fens like a message addressed to them personally.

And then the other thing arrived, the thing that made Oscar sit down on the edge of the bed with their head between their knees. Not the frame. The *familiarity.* The sluice gate. The outfall. The flooded bank. Twenty-two years ago — Oscar had been nineteen, working summers at the diner, learning to drive a cab from Dev's father — a woman had come out of that same water. Everyone remembered it the way towns remember weather: vaguely, wrongly, as atmosphere. She'd been found near the station. There had been talk of a coat, too. Talk that stopped abruptly, the way talk does when someone important makes a phone call.

Her name had been Alma. Her daughter had grown up two streets over, a quiet girl who waited outside the police station every year on the anniversary until they stopped letting her wait inside the doors.

Maren. Maren Voss.

Oscar picked up the phone before deciding to, and dialed the number they'd known by heart since they were nineteen and had never once dialed.

It rang four times.

"I know why you're calling," a woman's voice said. Flat, tired, awake — the voice of someone who had also not slept. "You've seen the news. And you're wondering the same thing I've been wondering since three a.m., which is how a stranger ends up in the water wearing a dead man's coat, twenty-two years after my mother drowned wearing one too."

The line hummed. Somewhere behind her, a radio murmured the same bulletin Oscar had been ignoring all morning.

"They never released details about my mother's coat," Maren Voss said. "Not to the paper. Not to me. So whoever put that woman in your clothes tonight knows things that were never public. Things that live in exactly one place."

"A file," Oscar said.

"A sealed file," she said, "that somebody opened."
