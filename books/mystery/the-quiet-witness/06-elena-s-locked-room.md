## 6. Elena's Locked Room

There are two kinds of people who hire you to investigate a murder that hasn't happened yet. The first kind are frightened, and frightened people are easy — they answer questions, they flinch at loud noises, they tell you the truth in the general direction of it. The second kind are hiding something, and the tell is always the same: they give you too much. Too much money, too much detail, too much grief, pre-packaged and ready to deploy.

Elena Marsh had handed me an envelope of cash thick enough to stop a bullet and a story polished to a mirror shine. And I'd spent two weeks polishing it further, checking her background the way you check a mirror before you trust what it shows you.

What I found was nothing. Not the nothing of an empty room — the nothing of a room that's been staged. No parking tickets. No late library books, and yes, I checked, because in my experience nobody's life is that clean unless someone's been scrubbing it. No social media before the age of thirty-two. No debts, no lawsuits, no exes with restraining orders. A woman who had apparently been decanted into existence at twenty-five with a job, an apartment, and a sister.

People say they want a clean record. What they mean is they want a record. A record is a history. Elena had a résumé.

So on a Tuesday in October, with the rain coming down like the sky was trying to confess to something, I picked the lock on her apartment door in the Fenwick Street brownstone and let myself into the one room in this case nobody had staged.

I want to be clear about my ethics here, because my lawyer — the one I can't afford — would want me to be. I had a key. Well. I had the *capacity* for a key, which in the state of Massachusetts is apparently a distinction that matters to nobody. And I had a client paying me to protect her sister from a murder, which meant I was already inside the moral gray area; I was just doing a little interior decorating.

The apartment smelled like lavender and old paper. That second smell should have been my first clue. Lavender is what people buy when they want an apartment to smell like something. Old paper is what an apartment smells like when it's been living a secret life.

* * *

The front room was exactly what you'd expect. Small, tidy, a couch with one throw pillow placed at a mathematically correct angle. A kitchen that had been used recently and cleaned thoroughly. A bookshelf of paperbacks arranged by color, which is the interior design equivalent of a personality transplant.

It was the bedroom closet that broke the case open, which is fitting, because closets are where we put the things we intend to deal with later and then don't.

Behind a hanging row of winter coats — in October, in a closet she clearly didn't expect anyone but herself to open — there was a cardboard file box. Not a fancy one. The kind you get free when a liquor store is feeling generous. The flaps were soft with handling.

I sat down on the floor of a stranger's closet, rain hammering the window behind me, and opened it.

Newspaper clippings. Dozens of them. The *Herald*, the *Gazette*, the *Fenwick Ledger* — the small paper that covered the county courts back when the county still had a paper. All of them about the Holloway killing.

I knew the clippings before I read them, is the thing. I had the same articles in my own files, in my studio, in the box I'd carried through three apartments like a reliquary. *MARSH CONVICTED IN HOLLOWAY SLAYING.* *JURY DELIBERATES ELEVEN HOURS.* *APPEAL DENIED; MARSH TO SERVE LIFE.* Thirty years of headlines about a woman named Catherine Holloway, found dead in her marsh-front house on the Point, and the man convicted of putting her there: Thomas Okafor— no. Not Okafor. That name comes later in this story and it belongs to someone else entirely.

Thomas Reyes. That was the name on the clippings. Thomas Reyes, forty-one at the time of the trial, a groundskeeper at the Holloway estate, the last person to see Catherine Holloway alive and the first person the police saw afterward. Convicted on circumstantial evidence and a footprint. Sentenced to life. Appeal after appeal after appeal, each one thinner than the last, each one covered in a smaller and smaller font until the *Ledger* finally stopped writing about him at all.

I sat in the lavender-smelling dark with thirty years of Thomas Reyes in my lap and felt the floor tilt under me, because I knew that face.

Not from the clippings, though the clippings had it — a younger man, hollow-eyed, in a suit that didn't fit, flanked by a public defender who looked like he'd rather be anywhere else on earth.

I knew it from the photograph that lay at the bottom of the box, in an envelope labeled, in Elena Marsh's careful handwriting, *Dad.*

* * *

Here's what I'd been telling myself for two weeks: Elena Marsh was a frightened woman with a rich sister and a vivid imagination, or a grifter running a long con on a podcaster's vanity, or — my favorite theory, the one I'd nursed like a houseplant — a true-crime obsessive who'd gotten too deep into my episodes and started living inside one.

Here's what was actually true: Elena Marsh was the daughter of the man I'd spent four years of my life telling strangers was innocent.

The photograph was recent. Prison visiting room, cinder-block wall, a vending machine blurred in the background. Thomas Reyes at seventy-something looked like a coat someone had emptied out — big frame, nothing left in it. But the eyes were the same eyes from the trial photos, and one of them was looking slightly off-camera, at whoever held the camera, with an expression I recognized because I'd spent years imagining it: the look of a man who has told the truth so long that the truth has stopped mattering, even to him.

On the back, in the same careful hand: *Dad, March. He laughed twice. He's getting thinner.*

I sat there a long time. Long enough that the rain stopped and started again. Long enough that the lavender stopped smelling like lavender and started smelling like what it was — a chemical standing in for a feeling, the way the tidy front room stood in for a life.

Because here's the harder thing, the thing that came in under the betrayal like water under a door: she'd hired *me*. She'd walked into my studio, sat across from me, watched me ask her questions about her sister's safety, and she'd known — she'd known the whole time — that I had a four-year-old podcast with a hundred thousand downloads an episode dedicated entirely to the proposition that her father was innocent. Episode one of *The Quiet Witness* was called "The Footprint." I'd spent eleven episodes on Thomas Reyes. She'd listened. I know she'd listened, because in our first meeting she'd quoted my own framing back at me — "the case that closes itself" — and I'd been flattered.

Flattered. God.

She had hired the one investigator in New England who was already on her side, and she'd lied to him anyway.

* * *

I did what any professional does with a discovery like this. I sat in a closet and got angry, then I got methodical.

The clippings weren't just collected. They were *worked*. Margins dense with Elena's handwriting — dates cross-referenced, contradictions flagged in red, a timeline assembled along the inside of the box lid in colored thread and pins like the world's most restrained conspiracy board. She'd done what I'd done. She'd done it longer. She'd done it with skin in the game, which is the difference between a hobby and a wound.

One clipping was newer than the rest, and it stopped my hands.

It wasn't about the Holloway case. It was a society-page item from the *Herald*, four years old, with a photograph: *MARSH AND HOLLOWAY FAMILIES REUNITE AT CHARITY GALA.* And there, mid-laugh, glass of wine raised — Daniel Marsh. Vivian's husband. The charming surgeon with the airtight alibi. Standing beside him, hand on his shoulder, an older woman the caption identified as *Mrs. Eleanor Holloway, mother of the late Catherine.*

The Marsh family and the Holloway family. Before Elena ever walked into my studio. Before the letter. Before any of it, the families had been *reuniting at galas*, and nobody — not me, not the original detectives, not the appellate attorneys who'd been grinding at this case for decades — had thought to connect the name on the convicted man's daughter with the name on the convicted man's victim's estate.

Wait. Let me get this right, because I had to get it right twice, sitting there on the closet floor, before the shape of it stopped sliding around.

Elena Marsh. Thomas Reyes's daughter, carrying her mother's name — I found the marriage certificate later, folded into a manila folder like an apology: *Reyes–Marsh, 1989.* She'd taken her mother's name and built a clean record around it, a record so clean it was a locked room, and the thing locked inside it was a photograph of her dying father and thirty years of newspaper.

And her sister — the sister whose murder had been predicted in an anonymous letter, the sister I was being paid a small fortune to save — was married to a man who'd been attending Holloway family galas.

I put the clipping down very carefully, the way you put down something that might go off.

* * *

The rest of the apartment took me an hour, and I'll be honest, I tore it apart. Not violently — I photographed everything first, a habit from the podcast, from the years when I learned that the difference between evidence and a souvenir is whether you can put it back. But I went through the place like a man looking for a pulse.

I found the drafts.

In a fireproof box under the bed — a fireproof box, under a *bed*, in the apartment of a woman with no criminal record and no parking tickets — there were letters. Six of them. Drafts of the letter. The letter. The one sitting in my studio safe in an evidence sleeve, the one that had started all of this, the one predicting Vivian Marsh's murder on the fifteenth of November, by a specific method, in a specific place.

The drafts were in Elena's handwriting.

I want to tell you I felt triumphant. Every instinct a true-crime podcaster has spent years cultivating — the instinct that smells a lie at forty paces, the instinct that keeps you going back to the file at two in the morning — every one of those instincts stood up in that lavender-smelling bedroom and said *told you so.* The client wrote the threat. The murder prediction was fiction. Case closed, episode over, refund the money and go home.

But I've been doing this long enough to know that the moment an answer arrives feeling like a *told you so*, you should check it for a pulse. And this answer had one. Because the drafts weren't a forgery exercise. They were a *research* exercise.

Elena hadn't invented the details of the predicted murder. She'd assembled them. I could see the assembly — a legal pad tucked behind the drafts, her handwriting again, listing the elements of the Holloway killing: the marsh house on the Point. The anniversary date. The method, which the papers never printed and the trial transcript sealed. And next to each element, a source notation.

Most of the sources I recognized. Public record, trial coverage, my own episodes — she'd cited me, which stung in a way I wasn't ready to examine.

But two of the notations weren't public. They weren't in the trial transcript. They weren't in my episodes, and I'd have staked the podcast on that, because those two details were the reason I'd spent four years insisting Thomas Reyes was innocent — details that had never been confirmed anywhere, that existed in exactly one place: the memory of a court stenographer named Alma Petrie, who had told me, off the record, eighteen months ago, in a diner booth in Fenwick, that the record had been struck and she'd kept her copy anyway.

Elena's notation next to those two details said: *A.P. — through D.*

Through D.

I sat on the edge of a stranger's bed, holding a legal pad in a dead woman's— no. Not a dead woman. In a lying woman's fireproof box, and I read *through D* about forty times, and every time I read it, the D got heavier.

Daniel. It had to be Daniel. The charming surgeon with the alibi booked months in advance, the husband who'd laughed at a charity gala with the mother of a murdered woman, the brother-in-law who'd somehow handed his wife's sister information that had been sealed by a court thirty years ago.

Or — and here the room got very cold — D was someone else, someone I hadn't met yet, and I was doing what I always did, which was building a suspect out of the first charming man who stood still long enough.

* * *

I put the apartment back together. Every pillow, every clipping, every draft, back in its fireproof box, back under the bed, back behind the winter coats. I wiped what needed wiping, though honestly, if Elena noticed I'd been there, we were past the point where fingerprints mattered.

Before I left, I stood in her doorway and looked back at the tidy front room, the color-sorted books, the throw pillow at its correct angle. And I understood the apartment for the first time. It wasn't a home. It was a cover page. The whole place was a title page for a book whose actual contents were in a liquor-store box in a closet — thirty years of a man dying in a cell for a murder he didn't commit, and a daughter who'd spent her entire adult life learning how to make people believe a lie, because nobody would listen to the truth.

She'd hired me because I believed her father was innocent. She'd lied to me because — why?

That was the question I carried down the brownstone stairs and out into the wet street, and it's the question that kept me walking past my car, past my studio, all the way to the Fenwick diner where I knew she took her coffee at nine.

Because here's the thing about lies. Most of them are walls. You find them, you knock them down, you move on. But some lies are *doors* — badly built, badly hidden, built by someone who half-wants them found. Elena Marsh had a fireproof box under her bed and a notation that said *through D* and a sister married to a man who'd been to Holloway galas, and every single one of those things was findable by anyone who cared to look.

She'd hired the one person in New England who would look.

She'd lied to me the way you lie to a bloodhound — by handing it the scent.

The diner's bell rang when I pushed the door open, and there she was, third booth from the window, stirring a coffee she wasn't drinking, and when she saw my face she didn't look surprised.

She looked like a woman who'd been waiting for me to find the closet.

"Elena," I said, sliding into the booth across from her, the photograph of her dying father burning a hole in my jacket pocket. "We need to talk about your dad."

Her spoon stopped moving.
