## 7. Marcus Hale, Perfect Witness

Marcus Hale had given a hundred interviews in his life, and he had learned long ago that the secret was not to remember the truth. The secret was to remember a better one.

He stood in front of the hallway mirror in his house on Crowsneck Point, adjusting the collar of a soft blue oxford shirt — nothing formal enough to look rehearsed, nothing rumpled enough to suggest carelessness. Sixty-one years old now, and the mirror was still kind to him. Silver at the temples, a swimmer's shoulders that had softened but not surrendered, and eyes that people always described as warm, though Marcus knew them for what they were: well-lit rooms with the furniture arranged just so. Nothing incriminating on display. Nothing worth finding.

Yara Thornbury would be here in twenty minutes.

He had listened to all nine episodes of her podcast twice. Once as an audience member, admiring the craft — she had a way of letting silence do the heavy lifting, of asking a question and then simply waiting until the other person's discomfort filled the room like water filling a hull. And once as a professional courtesy to himself, cataloguing every technique, every angle of approach, every place where her curiosity sharpened into something hungrier.

She had solved three deaths the county had spent decades misfiling. She was careful, patient, and — this was the part Marcus respected — she did not need to be liked. Most investigators, amateur or otherwise, wanted their subjects to open up to them. They performed empathy like a handshake. Yara Thornbury asked her questions the way a surgeon asks permission to cut: politely, and already moving.

So Marcus had prepared. Not lies, exactly. He had learned decades ago that lies were amateur work — brittle things that cracked under repetition, that required a second lie to hold up the first, a third to hold up the second, until a man spent his whole life doing structural maintenance on a building that should have been condemned. No. What Marcus had built instead was a memory. A complete, furnished, load-bearing memory of the night of August 9th, 1997, so thoroughly inhabited that it had become, in every way that mattered, true.

He had been practicing it for twenty-six years. That was longer than most marriages lasted. Longer than most careers. He had told it to state troopers and insurance adjusters, to Edmund's lawyers and Vivian's therapists, to his own wife before the divorce, to his daughter when she'd asked, at fourteen, why Grandpa Edmund never came to Christmas anymore. The memory had been polished by so many tellings that it no longer felt like a story. It felt like the night itself.

The doorbell rang at 2:04 p.m. Four minutes early. He noted it with approval. Punctuality disguised as eagerness — she was testing whether he'd make her wait, or whether he'd come to the door too fast. He waited ninety seconds, then opened it with a smile calibrated to surprise rather than expectation.

"Ms. Thornbury. You found us without getting lost — the GPS always wants to send people through the marina road."

"I took the marina road," Yara said. "I wanted to see the water."

Of course she had. Marcus stepped back and let her in, watching her take the foyer the way he'd watched buyers take houses he was selling: noting what they noticed, and what they were pretending not to notice. She was smaller than she sounded on the microphone. Sharper through the jaw. She carried a canvas bag with a recorder visible at the top, deliberately visible, because hiding it would have been a confession of intent and showing it was a confession of confidence.

"Can I get you something? Iced tea, coffee? I have a bottle of wine open from lunch, if you're the type who records over a glass."

"The type," Yara said, "who keeps both hands free."

He laughed — genuinely, he noted with some interest; the laugh had escaped before he could select it — and led her through to the sunroom overlooking the point. He'd chosen the sunroom for the interview the way a chess player chooses an opening. The light was flattering but not staged. The view included the water, which would put her in mind of the case without him having to mention it. And the room contained exactly two photographs of the Hollis family, both carefully selected: Edmund at his seventieth birthday, laughing, hand on Marcus's shoulder; and the 1997 regatta photo, taken three weeks before the drowning, four figures on the dock in matching club jackets.

Four figures. Let her count them herself. People trusted what they discovered more than what they were told.

* * *

For the first forty minutes, Marcus gave her nothing but texture.

He talked about the town as it had been — the cannery still running then, the summer people and the year-round people and the invisible border between them that everyone policed and no one acknowledged. He talked about Edmund Hollis, whom he'd known since they were boys diving off the same piling, and about Edmund's grief, which had "changed shape but never shrunk." He talked about the lake the way a man talks about a dog that bit someone he loved: with affection, and a wariness he let her hear.

"You've interviewed the family," Marcus said, refilling her tea unasked, an old host's trick that made people feel indebted without knowing why. "But I wonder if you understand what that summer did to them. Edmund didn't lose a son that night. He lost the version of himself that believed the water was on his side. The Hollises were a boating family going back four generations. After '97, Edmund sold the big boat within a year. Never went out past the buoy line again. A man doesn't do that out of guilt, Ms. Thornbury. He does it out of heartbreak."

"Heartbreak," Yara repeated. She turned the word over like a coin she suspected was counterfeit. "And you? You were out there that night too, weren't you? On the water somewhere."

There it was. Early — earlier than he'd expected, and he made a mental note to raise his estimate of her again. She wasn't going to spend an hour on pleasantries. She'd spent forty minutes building a runway, and now she was landing the plane.

"I was," Marcus said, and let the memory come forward, because it always came when called, obedient as a dog. "I was nineteen that summer. Working the fuel dock at the marina, living above my parents' garage. Daniel Hollis was my best friend — we'd grown up together, raced dinghies against each other when we were ten. That night he called me around seven, said he and a couple of friends were taking the *Cormorant* out to watch the meteor shower from the middle of the lake. Asked if I wanted in." He paused, and shook his head, and let the pause do its work. "I said no. I had an early shift. That's the whole story of my life, Ms. Thornbury — the story is the shift I worked instead of the shower I missed."

"And the friends?"

"Daniel, obviously. And a girl — Catherine Lyle, she was visiting her aunt for the summer. And—" he frowned, and here was the piece he had practiced hardest, the small stumble that made everything around it solid, "—there was a fourth. A local girl. Nineteen or twenty. She wasn't really part of our crowd; she'd come along at the last minute, I think Daniel knew her from the diner. I never caught her name. Honestly, by the time the reports started flying, I'd half convinced myself I'd imagined her. Grief does that. It edits."

*Edits.* He watched the word land. He had chosen it the way a man chooses a knife — not to wound, but to show he understood wounds. Everyone edits. Even you, Ms. Thornbury. Especially you.

Yara wrote nothing down. He noticed that too. She kept her hands wrapped around the cold teacup and her eyes on him, and he understood that the notebook was a prop she deployed for people she wanted comfortable, and that with him she preferred the naked instrument of attention itself.

"You say you never caught her name," Yara said. "But you remembered she existed. Twenty-six years, and you remember a girl whose name you never learned."

"I remember the life jackets," Marcus said quietly. This was the masterstroke, the detail he had seeded years ago in a deposition, knowing it would surface eventually, surfacing now like a body rising when it's good and ready. "There were five vests in the *Cormorant*'s locker. I know because I checked them out to Daniel that afternoon — it was procedure, we logged every loaner. Five vests, four people going out. I used to lie awake wondering about that fifth vest. Whether somebody grabbed it. Whether it made any difference at all." He looked out at the water, and let his voice roughen by a degree — one degree, no more. "You want to know what I remember about that night? I remember standing on the fuel dock at six-forty in the morning, watching the rescue boats come back in empty, and thinking: I signed out five vests, and only three people are coming home."

It was beautiful, and he knew it was beautiful, and the shame of that knowledge passed through him like weather — noticed, named, released. Because here was the thing Marcus had learned about himself across twenty-six years of curation: he no longer knew where the performance ended. There had been a night, long ago, when he had chosen what to remember, and the choosing had hurt, and then it hadn't, and then the chosen version had grown roots and bark and rings, and now when he walked through the memory of August 9th, 1997, he tripped on its furniture like anyone else. He grieved Daniel in that memory. He felt the cold of that morning. If you cut him, in some deep chamber of him, he bled 1997.

That was what made him safe. Liars got caught because they lived apart from their lies. Marcus lived inside his.

* * *

"So help me understand the timeline," Yara said, an hour in, her voice still pleasant, still unhurried. "The *Cormorant* goes out around eight with four people. Storm comes through around eleven. Mayday call at 11:42. Wreck found the next morning off Gull Rock. And you're at the marina the whole time?"

"Asleep, mostly. Then on the dock from dawn."

"No second boat out that night? Sometimes on meteor nights the harbor empties — everyone wants a piece of the sky."

"Small harbor, quiet lake, storm forecast," Marcus said mildly. "The *Cormorant* was the only boat foolish enough to go out. That's what made it so terrible. Nobody else was on the water to reach them." He sipped his tea. "Why do you ask?"

"Just counting," Yara said. "I count things. Boats, mostly, lately."

Something moved behind her eyes — not suspicion exactly, but the place suspicion lives before it moves in. Marcus smiled at her, easy and unhurried, and offered her the gift he had prepared.

"You know, since you're counting," he said, "there's someone you should talk to. Ray Coombs. He ran the marina office back then — booking log, fuel ledger, the works. Retired up to Harlow now. Ray was the one who noticed the vest count was off, actually, back when it mattered, and the state police treated him like he was senile for bringing it up. Ruined him a little, honestly. A man like that carries a grievance for twenty-six years. Grievances make good soil for memory." He spread his hands. "I'm not saying Ray knows anything. I'm saying if I were telling your story, I'd want his ledger in chapter one."

It was flawless, and he delivered it flawlessly: the generous redirect, the self-deprecating humility, the buried implication that the official record had failed a decent man. And it was even true, mostly — Ray Coombs had noticed the vests, had been brushed off, had nursed the insult ever since. What Ray did not know, what Ray could not know, was that the marina's carbon-copy logbook pages for August 1997 had gone into a burn barrel behind the office in October of that year, during a late-season cleanup Ray himself had supervised while fighting the flu, and that the replacement ledger — the clean one, the one that would survive — had been begun in Marcus's own handwriting, in his position as acting dockmaster, in the week after the funeral.

Ray would send her chasing a ghost with a grievance. Ray would cost her a month, maybe six weeks. By then the anniversary dinner would have come and gone, the toast would have been raised, the ritual completed, another year sealed.

Yara stood to leave. At the door, she paused — and Marcus felt the moment tilt, the way water tilts before it finds the crack.

"One more thing, Mr. Hale. The girl. The fourth passenger nobody named. When you half-convinced yourself you'd imagined her — what did you imagine she looked like?"

And there it was, the question he had not prepared, because he had prepared against every question about facts and none about faces. For a fraction of a second — less than a heartbeat, but he would replay it later, hating himself for it — Marcus Hale's mind went to a real face. Dark hair plastered wet across a forehead. A voice on the dock, raised, frightened, saying words he had spent a lifetime agreeing not to hear.

"Amy something," he heard himself say smoothly. "Amy. Local family, moved away after. Why do you ask?"

"No reason," Yara Thornbury said, and smiled at him with teeth she'd borrowed from someone kinder. "Thanks for the tea, Mr. Hale. And for the lead. I'll be sure to give Ray Coombs your regards."

She walked down his driveway toward her car, and Marcus stood in the doorway watching her go, performing — for whom, he couldn't have said — the posture of a man waving goodbye to a guest.

Then he closed the door, leaned his forehead briefly against the cool wood, and went to find his phone.

Because Amy was wrong. He knew it was wrong the moment it left his mouth — the name belonged to a girl from the class of '96, a girl who had moved to Ohio in 1994, a girl who had nothing to do with anything. He had reached for a placeholder and pulled out a decoy, and Yara Thornbury had watched him do it with those patient, counting eyes.

She hadn't corrected him. That was the worst part. She'd written it down — he'd seen her finally take out her notebook, right at the end, writing *Amy* in letters he could almost read from across the room.

In the kitchen, Marcus dialed a number he knew by heart and listened to it ring, and while it rang he looked out the window at the lake, flat and silver and holding everything it had ever been given.

"It's me," he said, when the click came. "We have a problem. She asked about the fourth passenger."

A pause. Then a voice he had known for fifty years, thin and old and suddenly very afraid:

"Marcus. What did you tell her?"
