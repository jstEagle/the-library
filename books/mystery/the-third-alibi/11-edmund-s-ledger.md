## 11. Edmund's Ledger

The ledger lived where it had always lived: in the bottom drawer of the writing desk in Edmund Hollis's study, beneath a false bottom his carpenter had built in 1998 and sworn never to speak of, and which the carpenter had kept that oath until the day he died, which was the last time anyone but Edmund had seen the inside of the drawer.

He opened it now with hands that no longer obeyed him cleanly. The tremor had come on slowly, the way rot comes into a pier — invisible at first, then suddenly you could see straight through the boards. He steadied the green ledger book against the desk's leather blotter and turned to the page marked by a ribbon he'd tied there himself, years ago, when the entries were still annual and still simple.

*1999. V.H. $40,000. Final payment. Matter closed.*

That was what he'd written. *Matter closed.* He remembered the satisfaction of the pen strokes, the way the ink had dried crisp and certain in the lamplight. Twenty-six years ago, and he could still feel that night's relief like warmth from a fire that had long since gone out.

He poured two fingers of the Macallan — the doctor said one, the doctor could go to hell — and carried glass and ledger to the chair by the window. Outside, the lawns ran down toward the water, silvered now under an October moon, the same water, always the same water, patient as a creditor.

He began, as he did every year on this date, to count.

It was a ritual older than the toast. The family raised their glasses to *those the water took*, but Edmund counted alone, in this room, with this book, because someone had to keep the arithmetic honest even if no one else would look at it. He had learned long ago that grief is not what kills families. Arithmetic is. Grief you can pour a drink over. But a number that doesn't reconcile — that sits there, wrong, year after year, staring up at you from a page — that will hollow a house out from the inside until nothing is left but the walls and the lies holding them up.

He turned back to the beginning.

*1997.* The first entry wasn't money at all. It was a list, written in his own younger hand, steady then, a hand that had signed contracts worth millions without hesitation: *What we know. What they can prove. What it costs.*

Three columns. He'd been proud of those columns. A man who builds a company out of a single fishing charter and forty years of nerve learns early that every disaster is a negotiation, and every negotiation has a price schedule.

The list of names. His daughter-in-law's brother, Daniel, nineteen, dead in the water before anyone understood what was happening — that column read *what they can prove: nothing*. The marina attendant who'd logged one boat going out and never asked why two came back — *$6,000, and a transfer to the Inverness branch, far from this county*. The coroner's clerk who'd misfiled the toxicology report — *$4,000, cash, once*. Vivian, who had stood on the dock and watched and afterward couldn't stop shaking — *V.H., ongoing*.

And at the bottom, in smaller letters, almost apologetic: *M.Z. — paid to forget. See separate record.*

Marguerite Zhou. Nineteen years old. The fourth passenger whom no report ever named, because by the time the reports were written she had already agreed she was never there. Edmund had sat across from her in this very study, this very chair, three weeks after Daniel drowned, and slid an envelope across the desk, and watched a frightened girl become a fiction.

"You were never on the boat," he'd told her. "You have never been on any boat. If anyone asks, you spent that night studying for exams."

She'd looked at the envelope, then at him, and something in her young face had frightened him more than the coroner or the sheriff ever had. Not greed. Not fear. *Understanding.* She'd understood exactly what she was looking at, and exactly what it was worth, and exactly how long she could make it last if she was careful.

"Mr. Hollis," she'd said quietly, "I'm going to be careful."

And she had been. That was the thing he'd never told anyone, the thing that had curdled his sleep for twenty-six years: Marguerite Zhou had taken less than he'd offered. She'd set a figure herself — modest, almost insultingly modest for what she knew — and she'd asked for one thing besides the money. She'd asked him to write down, in his own hand, that four people went out that night and only the truth about the fourth was negotiable.

He'd refused, of course. You don't put blackmail material in your own handwriting.

But she'd smiled — a small, tired smile, old beyond its nineteen years — and said, "Then I'll just have to remember well enough for both of us."

* * *

Edmund drank and turned pages.

The payments to M.Z. had run eleven years. Annual, punctual, always in cash, always collected by her personally at a diner off the interstate where she'd order coffee she never drank and slide the envelope into her purse without breaking eye contact. Eleven years, and then 2008, and the entry read:

*M.Z. — final payment per agreement. She signs release. Matter closed.*

The second time he'd written *matter closed*, and this time he'd believed it, because she'd signed the release in front of him, a notarized document her lawyer had drafted, releasing all claims, acknowledging full settlement, agreeing to silence in perpetuity. He'd kept the release in the safe behind the portrait of his late wife. He had gone home that night and slept eight hours for the first time since 1997.

Marguerite Zhou had died fourteen months later. Heart failure, the obituary said, at thirty-three. Edmund had sent flowers and felt — he admitted this to no one, least of all himself — felt *lighter*. The last living witness outside the family, gone. The story sealed in stone: three survivors, one accident, no fourth passenger.

So why, he thought now, setting down the glass, was there an entry dated 2010?

His hand found the page before his mind agreed to look at it. The handwriting was his own. The date was his own. But the entry —

*M.Z. estate — $15,000 — "continuation costs" — delivered via courier, per instruction.*

Continuation costs. He had no memory of writing it. He had no memory of authorizing it. And yet there it was, and below it another, 2011, and another, 2012, the amounts climbing — fifteen, eighteen, twenty-two thousand — and the notation changing over the years from *continuation costs* to *maintenance* to, in the most recent entries, simply a letter: *M.*

Every year. Every single year for fourteen years after her death, someone had been collecting Marguerite Zhou's hush money, and Edmund Hollis had been signing the disbursements.

He told himself the obvious lie first: he was old, he was forgetful, some lawyer in the family office handled it, some standing instruction he'd issued and forgotten. He got up, went to the safe, spun the dial with fingers that shook worse now, and pulled the file box marked with a year he barely recognized.

The standing instruction was there. Dated 2009 — a year *after* the final payment, a year *before* the release, which meant the dates themselves were impossible — typed on the office letterhead, signed E.H. in ink that matched his pen. *Continue annual disbursement to beneficiary of record until further notice.*

Beneficiary of record. He called his attorney at nine o'clock at night because terror does not keep business hours, and Harold Whitcomb, bless his cautious soul, answered on the second ring.

"Edmund. It's late. Everything all right?"

"Harold. The Zhou disbursements. Who's the beneficiary of record?"

A pause. Papers shuffling somewhere in a lit study in town. "Zhou... I don't have anything under Zhou, Edmund. Are you sure that's—"

"The payments. Fourteen years. Someone's been collecting them. There's a standing instruction with my signature."

Another pause, longer. When Harold spoke again his voice had changed, gone careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice. "Edmund, I reviewed your discretionary accounts in the spring, during the estate planning. There are no Zhou payments. There haven't been any payments matching that description through the firm in over a decade." A breath. "Whoever's been collecting, they're not collecting through me."

Edmund thanked him and hung up and sat in the dark with the phone warm in his hand.

Not through the attorneys. Not through the family office. Which meant the standing instruction was forged, the signature forged, the whole apparatus of payment constructed outside every legitimate channel — which meant whoever built it had access to his accounts, his letterhead, his hand. Had studied his hand. Had practiced it, probably, the way you practice anything, patiently, over years.

Or had simply never needed to practice, because they'd grown up watching him sign things.

* * *

He made a third drink and did not taste it.

Think, he told himself. Think the way you thought in 1997, when the sheriff's men were dragging the lake and you sat at this desk and built the architecture of a lie so sound that two inquests upheld it. Who touches the money?

Vivian. His sister, seventy-eight now, sharp as broken glass and twice as fragile. Her payments had ended in 1999 — *matter closed* — except. Except he pulled the later pages and found, threaded among the M. entries, occasional notations in his own forged hand: *V.H. — supplemental — medical.* Small amounts. Kindnesses, they looked like. He'd assumed he'd authorized them in some soft-hearted moment; Vivian's health had failed in the teens and he'd have given her anything. But he hadn't. He was almost sure he hadn't. And Vivian, when he'd mentioned it once at Christmas, had looked at him with an expression he'd filed away at the time as senility and now understood, with a cold turning in his chest, as fear.

Marcus. Family friend, honorary son, the boy who'd been at the dock that night, who'd rowed out with the search party at dawn, who'd given the statement so polished the sheriff had called it the cleanest witness account he'd read in twenty years. Marcus managed the family's charitable trust. Marcus had power of attorney during Edmund's hospitalization in 2016. Marcus had, Edmund realized with the slow horror of a man watching floodwater rise in a cellar, access to everything. Letterhead. Accounts. His signature, hundreds of times, on hundreds of documents, all of it innocent, all of it witnessed, all of it building a perfect reference library of Edmund Hollis's hand.

But why would Marcus collect Marguerite Zhou's money? She was dead. Her claim had died with her. Unless—

Unless the money was never about her claim.

Unless the money was about keeping someone quiet about something else entirely, and Marguerite Zhou's name was simply the channel, the dead woman's name a pipe through which payments could flow forever without anyone asking questions, because everyone who might ask had already agreed, in 1997, never to look at that part of the ledger again.

Edmund set down the glass. His reflection looked back at him from the black window — an old man in a dark room, holding a book that had finally begun telling the truth.

There had been two boats that night. He had paid to bury that fact along with everything else, and he had believed, for twenty-six years, that he was the architect of the burial. Sitting here now, counting, he understood that he had merely been the treasurer. Someone else had drawn the blueprint. Someone else had decided what the family would remember, and had been maintaining the structure ever since — patching it, funding it, forging his hand whenever the mortar cracked — and had killed, if the podcast woman was right, and she was right, he'd listened to all three episodes twice, had killed at least twice to keep the structure standing.

The podcast woman. Yara Thornbury. He'd had Marcus look into her, of course, standard precaution, and Marcus had reported back with that easy smile: *she's chasing ghosts, Edmund, she'll move on.* And Edmund had wanted to believe it, and so he had.

But now, sitting with the ledger open to fourteen years of payments to a dead woman, he understood something else, something that made the whiskey finally turn sour in his throat.

The letters. Marcus had shown him the first one — the confession letter the podcaster had received, the one describing details sealed from public record. *Crank mail,* Marcus had said. *Every famous case attracts them.* And Edmund had nodded, because the handwriting analysis Marcus showed him said elderly writer, formal schooling, tremor in the letters — and Edmund had thought, absurdly, *they think it's me,* and had laughed about it over brandy.

Tremor. Formal schooling. An old hand.

His hand. Or a hand taught to imitate his — and who had spent decades handling his documents, his signature, his correspondence?

He looked down at the ledger, at the latest entry, dated this past spring, in his own perfect forged hand:

*M. — $25,000 — annual — "until further notice."*

Until further notice. Until the structure needed reinforcing. Until someone wavered. Until a podcaster started counting boats.

Edmund closed the ledger and pressed both trembling palms flat against its cover, as if he could hold the truth shut inside it, and spoke aloud to the empty room, to the water beyond the window, to the boy who had rowed the second boat home heavy:

"It's you. It's been you the whole time."

Outside, down on the dark lawn, a shape moved between the hedges — unhurried, familiar, checking the windows the way it did every year on this date, the way it had checked them for twenty-six years.

Edmund reached for the phone to call Vivian, and stopped, because he suddenly could not remember whether Vivian was someone he needed to warn —

or someone who needed warning about him.
