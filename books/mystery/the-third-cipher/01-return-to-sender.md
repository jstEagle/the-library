## 1. Return to Sender

The trouble with running a bookshop in a town the size of Ashcombe was that everyone knew exactly which books you were ashamed of.

Edmund Eriksen had learned this over twenty-two years of trade, and he was reminded of it every Tuesday morning when he sorted the returned mail at the counter before opening. There it sat, in its rubber-banded bundle from the post office: three copies of *Maritime Law for the Layman* (ordered by mistake, returned by three separate customers who had each, independently, decided to be polite about it), a paperback romance with a cover so lurid that Mrs. Pettibone had asked him to keep it behind the register "where the children can't see it," and — as always — a slim volume of local history that nobody in Ashcombe wanted, because everybody in Ashcombe had lived it.

He untied the band, set the romance aside for Mrs. Pettibone's discreet collection, and began the small ritual of re-shelving failure. It was good work for a Tuesday. Honest work. The kind of work that asked nothing of a man except that he show up and know where things went.

That had been the whole appeal of bookselling, if he was honest about it. Twenty-two years ago he had traded a career in which knowing where things went could ruin lives for one in which it merely kept the poetry section tidy. He had never once regretted it out loud.

The bell above the door jingled, and he said, without looking up, "We open at ten, but I'll make an exception for anyone carrying biscuits."

"No biscuits." The voice belonged to young Peter Halloran from the post office, holding his canvas sack like a man delivering a summons rather than a parcel. "Sorry, Mr. Eriksen. This one came back marked undeliverable, but the address is right here in town, so I thought — well. Thought you'd want to look at it yourself."

"Undeliverable where?"

"That's the odd bit." Peter set a single envelope on the counter, face up, and stepped back from it slightly, the way people step back from things they don't want responsibility for. "It went out to the old Kessler place, up past the reservoir. House has been empty since March. Nobody there to sign."

Edmund looked at the envelope. It was addressed to him — Edmund Eriksen, The Third Cipher Books, Fore Street — in a hand that made something cold settle behind his breastbone before his mind had even finished reading the name. He knew that handwriting. He had known it for forty years, across birthday cards and chess score sheets and one memorable apology written after Frank Delaney had reversed his car into Edmund's garden wall and blamed, in flowing cursive, "the treachery of the gearbox."

But this wasn't Frank's writing. It was close to it — that same old-fashioned schoolroom formality, loops polished by a fountain pen — but narrower. More careful. A hand that had practiced being legible.

And the postmark read Ashcombe, dated the day after Dr. Aldous Marsh's funeral.

"Peter," Edmund said, keeping his voice at the pleasant register he used for recommending mysteries to tourists, "who sent this?"

"No sender. That's why it bounced, near enough — no return address, and nobody home to receive it. Regulations say it comes back to us." Peter shrugged. "Could be a prank. Kids do it sometimes."

"Kids," Edmund agreed, "do it sometimes."

He waited until the bell jingled again and Peter's footsteps faded down Fore Street before he picked up the envelope. It was heavier than a letter ought to be. Several pages, then. He turned it over. Sealed, unopened, addressed in that familiar-unfamiliar hand.

For a long moment he simply stood there in his own shop, surrounded by the comfortable smell of paper and dust and the lemon polish he used on the counter every morning whether it needed it or not, and let himself not open it. There was a discipline to not opening things. He had spent fifteen years practicing it.

Then he slit it open with the bone-handled letter knife — a gift from Frank, naturally, engraved *To Edmund, who always cuts to the chase* — and began to read.

* * *

The letter began without greeting.

*I have watched you for some time, Mr. Eriksen, which I expect will not surprise you. You are a man who notices being noticed.*

Edmund read standing up. Then he sat down on the stool behind the register, because his knees suggested it. Then he read it again.

*Fifteen years ago, on the last night of August, Clara Voss drowned in Harrow Lake. The finding was accidental death. You signed the report closing the case. So did Dr. Aldous Marsh, who examined her body, and Chief Inspector Hale, who ordered it closed, and Frank Delaney, who was there on the dock and has never once said so aloud.*

*I know because I was there too.*

*Three men saw something that night and buried it. One of them is dead now, and God rest him or don't, he does not get to take the truth into the ground with him. Marsh is gone. You and Delaney remain. Before this town sits down to its anniversary dinner and raises a glass to the sainted doctor, I intend for the truth to be sitting at the table with you.*

*I am not threatening you. Threats are for people who want something. I want nothing except what should have been given freely fifteen years ago: the truth, spoken where Maren Voss can hear it. You may do it yourself, quietly, before the dinner. Or you may wait, and watch it done for you, loudly, in front of everyone you have spent two decades being respectable in front of.*

*You were kind to me once, Mr. Eriksen. I have not forgotten that either. Consider this your courtesy copy.*

*There is no signature. You will know soon enough who I am. Everyone in this town knows more than they say. That has always been our chief industry.*

Edmund read it twice, as instructed, though the second reading was less reading than interrogation — going back over each sentence, pressing on the phrasing, hunting for the tell. *Courtesy copy.* Which implied other copies. He thought of Frank, forty years of friendship folded into the phrase *you will know soon enough*, and felt the particular nausea that comes from realizing a trap has been built around someone you love using materials you supplied yourself.

Because that was the thing. The handwriting. He turned the pages toward the window light and looked at them properly, the way he'd once looked at forged receipts and altered ledgers in another life. The loops, the pressure, the way the writer crossed their t's with a single decisive stroke — it was a hand he had seen before, many times, in a context he couldn't yet place. Familiar the way a voice heard through a wall is familiar. He knew it. He simply couldn't yet say from where.

Outside, Ashcombe went about its morning. Mrs. Aldous — no relation to the doctor, a distinction she maintained fiercely — swept her step across the street. Two boys argued cheerfully about football outside the bakery. On the lamppost by the green, the notice for the Founders' Anniversary Dinner flapped gently in the breeze: *Honoring the Memory of Dr. Aldous Marsh, Beloved Physician and Friend to Ashcombe for Forty Years. All Welcome.*

Three weeks since the funeral. The whole town had turned out. Edmund had gone, mostly because not going would have required an explanation, and explanations were the one currency he no longer dealt in. He had stood at the back of St. Brannoc's while the vicar spoke of a life of service, and he had sung the hymn, and he had shaken hands at the door, and all the while a small dry voice in him — the old detective's voice, the one he kept fed but muzzled — had remarked that it was remarkable how thoroughly a town could bury a man, given enough flowers.

Now somebody was digging.

* * *

He opened the shop at ten because routine is a load-bearing wall. The first customer was a tourist couple wanting recommendations for "something atmospheric," and Edmund sold them Daphne du Maurier with genuine pleasure, because whatever else was true, books remained blameless. The second was Mrs. Pettibone, collecting her contraband romance with a wink. The third was nobody; the third slot he filled with standing very still behind the register and looking at the safe.

The safe had come with the shop, bolted beneath the floorboards under the register by some previous proprietor with secrets of his own. Edmund had inherited it along with the ledgers and the smell. In it lived the shop's cash float, his passport, his mother's wedding ring, and — in a folder marked with a plain typewritten *V* — everything he had ever collected on the drowning of Clara Voss.

He had not opened that folder in fifteen years. He told himself this was discipline. Some mornings, brushing his teeth, he admitted it might also be cowardice wearing discipline's coat. Either way, the folder stayed shut, and the safe stayed locked, and the town stayed quiet, and that arrangement had held longer than most marriages.

He knelt, spun the dial — 4-19-27, the date of a day he did not think about — and set the letter inside on top of the folder, where it lay like a stone placed on a grave.

Then he closed the safe, straightened up, and made himself a cup of tea, because dread is best managed warm.

* * *

The afternoon brought Old Cobbins in to complain that the shop's window display was "morbid," by which he meant the stack of gothic novels faced outward, and Edmund talked him down to a mild grievance and sold him a western to seal the peace. By four o'clock the light had gone honey-colored through the front glass, and Edmund found himself doing what he always did at four o'clock: cataloguing.

It was the habit of a lifetime. Every person who came into the shop got filed, automatically, the way a lighthouse files ships. Mrs. Pettibone: widowed, sharp, reads romances and obituaries with equal attention. Cobbins: lonely, performs grumpiness, tips generously at Christmas. Peter Halloran: honest, nervous, exactly the sort of postman who follows regulations even when regulations are inconvenient — which meant the letter truly had been sent without a return address, by someone who either didn't care about getting it back or hadn't expected it to fail.

Which raised the question: who sends a confession to an empty house?

Nobody. Nobody sends a confession to an empty house. Unless they don't know it's empty — unless they're working from old information, from memory, from a map of the town drawn years ago. Or unless the wrong address doesn't matter, because the letter was never really meant to arrive by post at all. Because the point was never delivery. The point was the postmark. The point was a date, stamped in official ink: *the day after the burial.* Proof, if proof were ever needed, that whoever wrote it had been alive and present and watching while Ashcombe laid its beloved doctor in the ground.

Edmund set down his pen. Somewhere in the stacks, the radiator ticked as it cooled, a sound like a slow clock.

He thought about the phrase *courtesy copy*. Courtesy implies a relationship. Courtesy implies the writer believed Edmund owed them something, or they owed Edmund something, or — most likely — that there existed between them some shared history that made a threat feel almost like a kindness. *You were kind to me once.* When? He had been kind to half the town in the diffuse, noncommittal way of a man atoning for something. Kindness as camouflage. He had never suspected it would come back addressed to him.

And underneath all of it, patient as sediment, the other thought: *Frank.*

Frank Delaney, who owned the vineyard on the south slope and hosted half the town's celebrations and had pulled Edmund bodily out of the River Tarn when they were nineteen and stupid, hauling him gasping onto the bank while Edmund coughed up half the river and Frank laughed that terrified laugh people laugh when they've just saved a life and haven't decided yet whether to be proud or sick. Frank, whose handwriting the letter-writer imitated so closely it had stopped Edmund's heart for a full second. Frank, named in ink, on the dock, the night a girl drowned.

Edmund had spent fifteen years not thinking about that night. He was, he reflected, extremely good at it. He had built a whole life on the skill — the shop, the routines, the alphabetized private file of every secret in Ashcombe that he consulted only when a secret threatened to leak, plugging holes like a Dutch boy with a filing system. Not-thinking was his profession now. He had retired into it.

But the letter sat in the safe beneath his feet, and it did not permit not-thinking. It had been engineered not to. Whoever wrote it understood him — understood that a detective, even a rusted one, even one buried under twenty-two years of polite commerce, cannot leave a document like that alone. It was bait shaped exactly like his conscience.

He locked the till, turned the sign to CLOSED, and stood a moment in the dimming shop with his hand flat on the counter, feeling the grain of the wood, the solidity of the life he had built on top of the hole.

Tomorrow, he thought, he would begin. Quietly. The way he used to. He would pull the folder. He would talk to Ida Brand, if her health allowed and her discretion didn't. He would find out who wrote in a hand like Frank's, and why they chose now, and what exactly three men had seen on a dock at the end of August fifteen years ago that was worth killing a girl for and worth unburying a doctor for.

Tonight, though, tonight he would do what he always did. He would walk home past the green, nod at the anniversary notice, cook a simple supper, and sleep — or lie awake performing sleep — in the narrow bed above the shop.

At the door he paused. Through the glass, across the street, the lamppost notice flapped once more in the evening wind, and for a moment the shadow it cast on the pavement looked less like a celebration and more like a summons.

*Before the dinner,* the letter had said. Six weeks away. The whole town would be at Frank's table.

Edmund turned off the lights and stepped into the dusk, carrying the weight of a letter he already knew he could never put down — and wondering, with the first real chill of the season settling into his bones, how many other copies were sitting on other doorsteps in Ashcombe tonight, waiting to be opened.
