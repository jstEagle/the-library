## 3. Three Names

The letter stayed in the safe for two days before Edmund could bring himself to take it out again.

He told himself this was method. A detective—retired, but the habits didn't retire with you—did not rush back to evidence. Evidence kept. Evidence was patient. Evidence sat in the dark of a floor safe beneath a cash register in a failing bookshop and waited for a man to be ready, and if a man was never ready, well, then the evidence had lost nothing by waiting.

The truth was less dignified. The truth was that Edmund Eriksen, sixty-eight years old, keeper of approximately four thousand books and one alphabetized archive of other people's sins, was afraid of a piece of paper.

He opened the shop at nine as always, though the bell over the door had rung for exactly three customers since Monday, and one of those had been Mrs. Petch looking for a gift for a grandchild she did not, as far as Edmund knew, have. He made tea he didn't drink. He straightened the mystery section, which was ironic and which he appreciated on the days he could appreciate anything. And at half past ten, with the morning light coming through the front window at the angle that made everything look kinder than it was, he knelt down, spun the dial, and took out the letter.

*Mr. Eriksen,* it began. No *Dear*. No *Detective*. Just his name, set down by a hand that pressed too hard on the downstrokes, the way people do when they are writing something they have rehearsed for years.

*You were there.*

*On the fourteenth of June, fifteen years ago, three men stood on or near the dock at Voss Point and saw what happened to Clara Voss. Dr. Aldous Marsh saw it. You saw it. Frank Delaney saw it. All three of you buried what you saw, and all three of you have lived above that grave ever since.*

*I am not going to tell you what to do. I am only going to make sure you cannot pretend anymore. The town will gather soon for its anniversary dinner, and it will raise a glass to the good doctor, and I want every one of you to hear that toast and know what is under it.*

*You have until then.*

*There is no signature because you already know who I am. That is rather the point.*

Edmund read it twice. He had read it twice the first night, standing in the dark of the shop with his coat still on, and twice more in bed with the lamp on until one in the morning, and now twice again in daylight, where it should have been smaller. It wasn't. In daylight it was worse, because in daylight he could see how calm it was. This was not a madman's letter. Madmen shout. This whispered.

*Three men stood on or near the dock.*

He had spent two days telling himself the letter was a crank's work. Ashcombe produced a crank roughly once a decade, like a comet; the last one had spent a summer mailing the parish council detailed diagrams of the church spire's secret purposes. But cranks got details wrong. Cranks wrote *I know what really happened* and then delivered nothing but fog. This letter delivered names. Three of them. Precise as a receipt.

And one of them was Frank Delaney.

Edmund set the letter on the counter, flat, and looked at it the way you look at a dog that has bitten someone. Then he went to the back room, where the archive lived—not the safe, the archive: eleven boxes of manila folders, each labeled in his own careful hand, each containing a secret that had passed through Ashcombe and been buried there. Fifteen years of small-town sin, filed alphabetically. Adulteries under A through D mostly, thefts under T, one genuinely puzzling incident involving the vicar and a goat under M for Miscellaneous. He had built it in the first year after Clara Voss died, though he had never admitted to himself why. A detective who stops detecting doesn't stop needing to. He just points the need somewhere quieter.

Folder V he had never reread. Not once in fifteen years. He knew exactly where it was—third box, between U and W—and he knew exactly what was in it, and the knowing had been enough. Knowing from a distance. Knowing the way you know a scar is there without looking at it.

He did not open it now either. Instead he put on his coat, locked the letter back in the safe, turned the sign to CLOSED FOR LUNCH—a lie so habitual it barely qualified as one—and walked out into a morning that smelled of cut grass and the lake, because everything in Ashcombe eventually smelled of the lake.

* * *

Frank Delaney's vineyard sat on the south slope above the water, and it was, everyone agreed, having a good year. The grapes hung fat and honest in their rows, the tasting room had been repainted a cheerful cream, and Frank himself was out among the vines when Edmund arrived, secateurs in hand, silver-haired and sun-browned and looking precisely like what the town believed him to be: the most decent man in Ashcombe.

"Edmund." Frank's face did the thing it always did—opened like a gate. "Tell me you've come about wine and not books. I can help you with one."

"Wine," Edmund said. "A case of the red. The anniversary dinner committee wants something local for the toast."

"Then they've got taste." Frank wiped his hands on his apron and led him toward the barn, talking the whole way—about the weather, about the phylloxera scare two valleys over that hadn't touched them, thank God, about Ruth's roses winning something or other. Frank talked the way rivers ran, easily and over everything, and Edmund had loved him for it for forty years, and had learned, somewhere along the way, to listen for the stones underneath.

Because here was the thing about Frank Delaney that almost no one in Ashcombe knew, and that Edmund would carry to his own grave if the letter allowed it: thirty-one years ago, on a February afternoon when the river below Miller's Bend had come up brown and fast, Frank Delaney had gone into that water after Edmund Eriksen without a second's hesitation. Edmund had slipped from the bank—foolish, showing off for a girl, reaching for a branch—and the current had taken him under twice before Frank's arm closed around his chest. Frank had been twenty-two and strong as an ox and scared out of his mind, and afterward, sitting on the bank coughing the river out of his lungs, Edmund had said, "I owe you my life," and Frank had laughed and said, "You owe me a pint, is what you owe me."

They had never spoken of it again. That was the arrangement of their friendship: Frank saved your life and then pretended he hadn't, so that you could go on being a man instead of a debt. Forty years of pints and arguments and Christmases and, for the last fifteen years, a certain carefulness around the subject of the lake, which Edmund had told himself was grief, shared grief, the ordinary weight of a town that had lost a child.

He watched Frank pull bottles from the rack, count them, wrap them, and thought: *he was there.* The letter said it. The letter named him with the same flat certainty it used for Marsh and for me, and either the writer is lying, or—

Or Frank had stood on that dock fifteen years ago and seen whatever Edmund had spent fifteen years not seeing, and had said nothing, and had gone on pulling Edmund's friend-weight beside him through a thousand Sundays as if the silence cost nothing.

"You're quiet," Frank said, handing over the case. "That's a detective word for trouble, your quiet."

"I'm retired."

"You're retired the way the lake is dry." Frank smiled, and the smile was the old smile, warm and crooked, and Edmund felt the betrayal move in his chest like a swallowed stone. Because that was the cruelty of it—he couldn't even hate the man cleanly. Hating Frank would be like hating his own heartbeat.

"Frank," Edmund said. "Do you remember the summer Clara Voss died?"

The secateurs stopped moving. Only for a moment—a hitch, a skipped stitch—and then they resumed, snipping a stray runner with great attention. "Everyone remembers it. Terrible thing. Accident, they said."

"They said."

Frank looked up then, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind his eyes—something old and tired and frightened—and Edmund recognized it, because he shaved above the same thing every morning. Then Frank clapped him on the shoulder, the grip firm and familiar, and said, "Give my best to the dinner committee. And Edmund—whatever's got you walking around with that face, it'll keep. Most things do."

Most things do. Edmund carried the wine to the car and sat in it for a full minute without starting the engine, listening to the wind move through the vines, and understood that he had just heard a guilty man talk about keeping things.

* * *

He did not burn the letter. That seemed important, somehow, to notice. A man with something to hide burns things; the whole history of concealment was a history of ash. Edmund fed his stove that evening with yesterday's newspaper and nothing else, and set the letter on the kitchen table under the lamp, and made himself read it a fifth time, slowly, like a man defusing something.

*All three of you have lived above that grave ever since.*

Above. Not beside. Above—the way you live above a cellar. The image snagged in him and wouldn't let go, because it was true, it was exactly true, he had built his whole quiet life on the floor above that June night the way a house is built above its foundations, and he had called the foundations *an accident* and furnished the rooms and gotten on with it.

What had he actually seen, that night? He had asked himself this in the dark for two days and received the same answer the dark always gave: a blur of lantern light, voices raised near the dock, Marsh's white coat moving away up the path, and then, later, the terrible stillness of the water at dawn with the search boats on it. He had been forty minutes away in his head, he remembered—called out for some domestic at the Hollis farm, arrived late, caught only the edges. Edges. He had closed the case on edges. Hale had wanted it closed—Hale had *asked*, in that voice that was not quite a request—and Marsh had certified drowning, and Edmund, tired and ambitious and eager to believe the town's beloved doctor, had signed the report that said *accidental* and gone home and slept, God help him, he had slept.

And Frank? What edges had Frank caught? Frank, who was there. Frank, whose name sat on the page in the writer's careful hand with no explanation, no accusation beyond the naming itself—as if the naming were enough, as if the writer knew that Frank Delaney's name on a list of witnesses was a complete sentence.

Edmund poured a whisky he didn't taste and thought about the postmark. The day after Marsh's funeral. Whoever wrote this had waited for the doctor to die—had counted the days, perhaps—and that meant the writer feared Marsh alive. Feared him, or needed him gone. Either way, the letter was not grief and not madness. It was strategy. Someone had declared a campaign, and the anniversary dinner was its deadline, and Edmund and Frank were its targets—or its instruments. He turned the page over. Blank. He held it to the lamp. Nothing. The paper was ordinary stationery, bought anywhere, sold at the village shop in packs of fifty.

Which meant anyone in Ashcombe could have written it.

Which meant, Edmund thought, setting down the glass, that everyone in Ashcombe was now, briefly, a suspect—and that he, the retired detective with the alphabetized secrets and the signature on the old report, was the most compromised investigator this case could possibly have. If he pursued it, he pursued his own indictment. If he buried it, he buried it a second time.

He went to bed at midnight and slept badly, exactly as predicted, badly in the specific way he remembered from thirty years of casework: the mind running silt-tests all night, hauling up images—lantern light, white coat, Frank's arm around his chest in cold brown water, Frank's hand on his shoulder today among the vines, the same grip, the same warmth, and underneath both, the question he could not put down:

*What did you see, Frank? What have you been carrying all these years while you carried me?*

He woke at four with the answer to a different question entirely, the kind of gift sleep sometimes leaves on the pillow. The letter said *you already know who I am.* Fine. But the writer also knew things—knew all three names, knew the date, knew the dock. That knowledge hadn't come from nowhere. There had been a fourth presence on the shore that night, someone unlisted, unnamed, watching. Hale's reports—what he could recall of them—mentioned no witness. But Hale's reports had mentioned very little, now that Edmund let himself actually think about them, which was itself a finding.

Someone had seen everything. Someone had waited fifteen years. And someone had chosen this moment—Marsh dead, the town gathering, the clock running—to begin feeding the past back into the light, one name at a time.

Edmund got up in the dark, dressed, and drove into the sleeping village just as the sky went grey. The bookshop was cold. He knelt at the safe, spun the dial, and did not take out the letter. Instead he reached past it, deep into the shelf behind the till, and drew out the third box of the archive, and set folder V on the counter in the lamplight, its tab soft with fifteen years of not being touched.

His hand rested on it for a long moment.

Then, from the street, came the sound of the first delivery van, and somewhere beyond it the lake, invisible and patient, holding whatever it held.
