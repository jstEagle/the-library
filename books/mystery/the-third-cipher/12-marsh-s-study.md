## 12. Marsh's Study

The Marsh house had been sealed for three weeks, which in Maren Voss's experience was exactly long enough for a town to stop looking at it and start talking about it again.

She knew this because she'd spent those three weeks doing what she did best: listening. Fifteen years of running the anniversary dinner committee had taught her that grief and gossip traveled on the same roads through Ashcombe, and if you stood quietly at the crossroads with a clipboard, people told you everything. Mrs. Petch at the bakery thought the house should be turned into a memorial garden. Mr. Okafor at the hardware store thought it should be sold to a developer from the city who'd already sniffed around twice. And young Danny at the post office—Danny who could not keep a secret any more than he could keep his shirt tucked in—thought the whole thing should be left alone because "dead doctors deserve peace, Miss Voss, same as anyone."

Dead doctors, Maren thought now, standing in the shadow of the Marsh house's side gate as the last light went amber over the lake, do not get to decide what they deserve.

Her sister hadn't gotten to decide either.

She checked her watch. The estate lawyer, a damp little man named Pruitt who smelled permanently of photocopier toner, had announced at the reading of the will that the house would remain sealed "pending inventory," which everyone understood to mean pending the family in Manchester deciding whether they wanted the good silver or just the money. Three weeks of inventory pending. In Maren's experience—and she had a great deal of experience with committees, which were simply bureaucracies with casseroles—"pending" meant "nobody has gotten around to caring yet."

Which meant the back door lock was probably still the same lock it had always been.

Which meant—

"You're going to catch your death standing out there," said a voice behind her, and Maren nearly ascended on the spot.

It was Ida Brand. Retired constable Ida Brand, seventy-one years old, wrapped in a coat the color of wet slate, holding a thermos like a sidearm.

"Ida." Maren pressed a hand to her chest. "Good Lord. You can't sneak up on a person like that."

"I've been standing here four minutes," Ida said. "You never once looked behind you. If I were a burglar you'd be halfway to the county line by now." She unscrewed the thermos cap, poured something steaming into it, and held it out. "Cocoa. It's got a splash of something in it that makes the cocoa worth drinking."

Maren took it. Her hands needed something to do besides shake. "I wasn't going to break in."

"No," Ida agreed pleasantly. "You were going to stand in the dark beside a locked door of a dead man's house, holding a hairpin, for entirely innocent reasons."

Maren looked down. She was, in fact, holding a hairpin. She hadn't even registered taking it from the little dish by her front door; her hands had apparently made their own committee decision.

"It's my sister's anniversary this year," Maren said. "Fifteen years. The dinner is in nine days. And everyone in this town is going to raise a glass to Aldous Marsh, saint of Ashcombe, and I am going to have to smile while they do it, unless I find out—" She stopped. The cocoa was very warm. The something-in-it was doing its work. "Unless I know."

"Know what?"

"That's the trouble, Ida. I don't know what. That's why I'm here with a hairpin."

Ida looked at the house for a long moment. The upstairs windows were dark; the study light on the ground floor had gone out weeks ago, when the sealing tape went up. Somewhere down the lane a dog was making its opinions about the moon known.

"I worked the scene that night," Ida said, so quietly Maren almost missed it under the dog. "Fifteen years ago. Before I was walked off it."

Maren went very still. "You never said."

"You never asked the right question until about two minutes ago." Ida screwed the cap back onto the thermos. "I'm not here, Maren. Officially, I was never on this lane, and neither were you. But if a person were to go through that side gate, she'd want to know that the study window latch has been broken since 2011, because Dr. Marsh was too cheap to fix it and too proud to admit it. He complained about it every year at the summer fete. Treated it like a charming anecdote." A pause. "'Anyone could walk right in,' he used to say, laughing. 'Good thing nobody wants anything of mine.'"

The dog barked twice more and gave up. The streetlight at the corner of the lane buzzed on, indifferent.

"Why are you telling me this?" Maren asked.

Ida started walking away, slow, unhurried, a woman merely out for evening air. Over her shoulder she said: "Because fifteen years ago somebody told me to stop asking questions, and I obeyed, and I've hated myself in alphabetical order ever since. Go on, then. Hairpin's no use on a sash window. Use your fingers."

* * *

The window latch was exactly where broken latches go to die: stubborn, rusted, and secretly delighted to be forced. Maren got it open on the third try, hauled herself over the sill with all the grace of a woman who had not climbed through a window since 1989, and landed in the dark of Aldous Marsh's study among a smell of cold pipe smoke and old paper.

She crouched there for a moment, heart hammering, listening.

Nothing. The house ticked and settled around her like an old ship at anchor. Down the hall, the grandfather clock—the one Marsh had wound every Sunday, the one the obituary had made such a fuss about—had stopped at some hour or other, its pendulum hanging slack, and somehow that was the saddest thing Maren had seen in weeks.

She stood. Let her eyes adjust. The study resolved itself out of the gloom: leather chairs, bookshelves, the big desk facing the window so that whoever sat there could look out at the lake. Of course it faced the lake. Everything in this town faced the lake, and pretended it wasn't looking.

There was a sheet over the desk chair—estate sealers' tarpaulin, gray and institutional—but the desk itself was bare. Drawers locked, presumably. Papers inventoried or removed. Maren tried the drawers anyway, gently, feeling like a burglar in a play where everyone knows their lines except her. Locked, locked, locked, and then the bottom-left drawer slid open with a small obscene squeak, revealing nothing but a tin of pencils and a rubber band ball.

"Well," she murmured to the empty room, "you were a genius with a stethoscope and a fool with a filing system."

It was Edmund Eriksen who'd taught her that trick, actually—not the drawer trick, the talking-to-rooms trick. He claimed he thought better aloud, and that a man who ran a failing bookshop was entitled to converse with whatever was available. She'd been in his shop twice now, ostensibly about an out-of-print book on lake currents, and both times she'd come away with the distinct sensation of having played chess against someone who kept assuring her he didn't know how the pieces moved.

She trusted him the way you trust a locked door: provisionally, and with a backup plan.

The study offered her the obvious first: the bookshelves. Medical texts, mostly, spines cracked with use. A surprising run of poetry—Marsh the beloved, Marsh the cultured. And there, on the bottom shelf, wedged between a history of the county and a water-stained atlas, a hollow: books pushed aside to make a gap roughly the size of a ledger.

Maren knelt. Ran her fingers along the shelf bottom. Nothing taped there. Checked behind the atlas. Dust, and a dead spider with better real estate sense than most of Ashcombe.

Then she saw it—or rather, she saw the absence of it. The gap in the books was clean. Recently cleaned. Whatever had lived there had been taken out recently enough that the dust outline was still sharp, a pale silhouette of a rectangle on the wood, like a chalk mark around a missing body.

Someone had beaten her here.

She sat back on her heels and let the disappointment move through her, all the way through, the way she'd learned to let things move after Clara died—because if you stopped them halfway they set up housekeeping in your chest and never left. Someone had come for Marsh's private papers. Someone else in this town full of people raising glasses to a saint had crawled through a window in the dark.

Or—

No. Not necessarily. Pruitt's inventory team might have taken it. The family in Manchester. Any number of innocent explanations, each one flimsier than the last, and Maren had spent fifteen years learning to distrust flimsy.

She was about to give the room one final, resentful survey before climbing back out the way she'd come when she noticed the fireplace.

Not the fireplace itself—a handsome stone affair, swept clean by whoever prepared houses for sealing. What she noticed was the hearthstone at the back, second from the left, which sat a half-inch proud of its neighbors. Not loose, exactly. Just proud. The way a man stands slightly forward of a group photograph when he believes himself important.

Maren worked it with her fingernails, then with the hairpin, which finally found employment after all, and the stone came free with a grinding whisper and a small avalanche of soot.

Behind it: a oilcloth packet, tied with butcher's twine.

"Oh," she said softly, to the room, to Clara, to nobody. "Oh, you clever, paranoid old man."

* * *

She took the packet to the window seat, where the last gray light from the lake gave her just enough to read by, and untied the twine with fingers that would not entirely behave.

Inside: a ledger. Black cloth boards, unmarked. And beneath it, folded into quarters, a single sheet of paper covered in Marsh's neat doctor's hand—the handwriting of a man who wrote prescriptions and expected them to be obeyed.

Maren opened the ledger first.

It was exactly what it appeared to be: accounts. Columns of dates, amounts, and payees, in that same tidy script. The early entries were mundane—grocers, the coal merchant, a donation to the church roof fund that made her snort despite everything. Saint of Ashcombe, indeed; he'd given twelve pounds ten toward the roof and taken credit for the whole cathedral.

Then, near the back, a separate section. Separate columns. Separate rules.

Annual entries. Same date every year—the twenty-third of June. She felt the date land in her stomach before her mind caught up. The twenty-third of June. Two days after Clara had drowned. Two days after the funeral that Maren had stood at with her mother's hand crushing hers, watching the whole town weep for a girl they'd all decided to stop thinking about by autumn.

Same amount every year: two hundred pounds, rising over time to five hundred as the years went on, inflation being apparently no respecter of secrets. Same payee, every single entry, fifteen entries in all:

**R. Halloway — retainer, as agreed.**

Retainer. As agreed. As though silence were a service rendered quarterly, invoiced, perhaps eligible for a discount on prompt payment.

Maren read the name four times. R. Halloway. R. Halloway. The letters refused to resolve into anyone. She turned the name over in her mind the way she'd turn over a strange coin found on the pavement: Frank Delaney? No. Edmund Eriksen? No—she'd have bet the shop lease on it, and she'd seen enough of Edmund these past weeks to know that whatever he was guilty of, it wore the shape of guilt about paperwork, not payments. Chief Inspector Hale? Hale was an H, but Hale was Hale, and besides, what would a chief inspector need with a false name?

R. Halloway was nobody. R. Halloway was a ghost with a bank account.

Except ghosts, Maren thought, staring at the neat columns, don't need retainers. Ghosts don't cost two hundred pounds a year for fifteen years. Somebody living had been paid to stay quiet, and it wasn't the men whose names had surfaced so far, and the realization arrived not as triumph but as a kind of vertigo—she had spent fifteen years building a picture of that night, frame by frame, and someone had just handed her a piece that belonged to a different puzzle entirely.

She unfolded the single sheet of paper.

It was a letter, unsent, undated—or dated, rather, only by its position in time: written in a shakier hand than the ledger, late in his life. Marsh's voice, unmistakable even in draft, that measured bedside reasonableness that had charmed three generations of Ashcombe:

*"If you are reading this, then I have failed to destroy it, which will not surprise Ruth—"*

No. Not Ruth. Maren blinked and read again. *"—which will not surprise anyone who knows me. I have kept accounts all my life. It is the one honesty I never managed to abandon. The annual sum continues to be necessary. The recipient's circumstances have changed but the obligation has not. When I am gone, the arrangement ends, and I imagine that is precisely when the trouble begins. I have bought fifteen years. I did not buy forever. No one can afford forever."*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. The whole letter was a signature.

Maren sat in the darkening study with the ledger in her lap and felt the discovery curdle, slowly, the way milk curdles—nothing dramatic, just a slow turning of something nourishing into something you couldn't swallow. She had wanted a villain. She had wanted, if she was honest, Frank Delaney, or even Edmund Eriksen, because a villain with a face is a problem you can solve. What she had instead was a dead man's arithmetic and a name that matched no one, and the growing, sickening suspicion that the story of her sister's death had more doors in it than she'd ever known existed, and she'd only just found the hallway.

Somewhere below the window, gravel crunched.

Maren froze. The sound came again—unhurried footsteps on the drive, and then the particular metallic complaint of the front gate, the one every child in Ashcombe had learned to swing on and every adult had learned to ignore.

A flashlight beam swept across the study ceiling, above her head, through the gap under the study door.

And then a voice—calm, familiar, and carrying the mild, reasonable tone of a person who has done this sort of thing before—said clearly through the window she'd left open:

"Maren. You can bring the ledger out, or I can come in and we can discuss why you didn't tell me about the hairpin. Your choice, but I'd hurry. Pruitt's night man does his rounds at half past."

Edmund Eriksen stepped into the moonlight below the sill, hands in his coat pockets, looking up at her with the expression of a man who had alphabetized every secret in Ashcombe and had just watched a new one file itself under M.
