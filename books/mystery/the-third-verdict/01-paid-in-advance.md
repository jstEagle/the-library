## 1. Paid in Advance

The rain came in sideways that afternoon, the way it always did in October, as though the weather had a grudge against the town of Marrow's Bend specifically and was content to take it out on my awning. I was in the back of the shop, doing what I did most evenings — which is to say, pretending that rearranging the poetry section constituted work, and not a slow-motion surrender to the fact that nobody in this town had bought a book of poetry since the Clinton administration.

The bell over the door rang. I didn't look up. In six years of owning Fairweather's Books — a name I chose the way a criminal chooses a getaway car, something forgettable with good mileage — I'd learned that the bell meant one of three things: a lost tourist, a neighbor escaping weather, or a person who wanted to use the bathroom and felt guilty enough to buy a bookmark. None of these required my full attention.

"I'm looking for the owner," said a voice that hadn't learned yet how to lie about being calm.

I looked up. The man in my doorway was maybe thirty, rain-soaked in the committed way of someone who'd walked a long way rather than park where people could note his license plate. He had the posture of a filing clerk and the eyes of a man who'd seen something in a filing cabinet that filing cabinets should not contain. His coat was the color of wet cardboard, which, to be fair, is also the color of most of my inventory.

"That's me," I said. "Mostly. Legally, the shop owns itself at this point. I'm just the staff."

He didn't laugh. People who don't laugh at that joke are either humorless or terrified, and I've built a small career — a very small, very unprofitable career — on telling the difference. His eyes went to the window first, then the door, then the window again. Terrified, then. But disciplined about it. That combination always interests me, in the way that a strange noise under your car interests you on a road with no shoulders.

"Freya Fairweather," he said, reading it off the hand-lettered sign I'd never gotten around to updating.

"Close enough. Can I help you find something? We have mysteries in the back, though I should warn you the endings are all spoiled by mildew."

He crossed to the counter with the careful steps of a man walking on ice that hadn't frozen yet. Up close he smelled of rain and cheap coffee and something underneath both — the faint chemical ghost of a library archive, that vanilla-and-dust scent of old paper being preserved against its will. An archivist, then. Or a thief with good taste.

"My name is Corin Vale," he said. "I need to hire you."

"You've got the wrong shop. This is a bookstore. The detective agency is two towns over and also fictional."

"I know who you are."

And there it was — the sentence I'd spent six years making sure no one would ever say to me, delivered in my shop between the cookbooks and the remaindered atlases, on a Tuesday, in the rain. I want to tell you I felt fear. What I actually felt was something worse and more useful: a kind of professional curiosity, the old machinery waking up in my chest like a furnace in an abandoned house, groaning, coughing, remembering what it was for.

"I think you have me confused with someone else," I said, and my voice did what I'd trained it to do, which is nothing at all.

"Maybe." He set a leather envelope on the counter. Old leather, cracked at the corners, the kind of thing that outlives its owners. "Maybe not. Either way, I need someone who can read a document the way — " he paused, choosing, " — the way a particular kind of person reads a document. Someone who used to do it for a living."

"I sell books."

"You sell books at a loss. I checked. Everyone checks." He said it without apology, which I resented and admired in equal measure. "You're not doing this for money. Which makes you the only person in this town who can't be bought off it."

"That's a very generous interpretation of bad business."

He almost smiled. It didn't survive the trip to his eyes. "I need you to prove a murder hasn't happened yet."

The rain filled the pause. It's good at that, rain. It's been covering awkward silences since before we invented them.

"That's an unusual phrasing," I said. "Most people want you to prove a murder *has* happened. It's the easier direction. Bodies are very persuasive."

"The murder is dated three weeks from today. November ninth." He said the date flat, the way you'd read an account balance you didn't like. "It's in a ledger. An old one. Centennial ledger for the town, 1912 to 2012, the kind they print to sell at the historical society and nobody reads. Except someone wrote an entry in it for a date that hasn't occurred. A murder. Victim unnamed. Location — " he swallowed, " — location listed as this address."

I looked at the shop. At the sagging shelves, the radiator that clanked like a prisoner rattling a cup along the bars, the reading nook by the window where Marisol Vane came every Thursday to drink tea she brought herself because I'd stopped stocking anything but coffee. At the life I had built the way you build a sandcastle below the tide line — knowing it wouldn't hold, hoping the sea would be busy elsewhere.

"And the town historical society sent you," I said, "because they're concerned about the accuracy of their records."

"The historical society doesn't know it's gone. I borrowed it. I'm an archivist. I catalogued an estate out past Hollis Creek, and this was in a locked case with things that — " He stopped himself. I watched him stop himself, and I made a note of what he'd chosen not to say, because what a frightened man won't tell you is usually the whole story wearing a coat.

"Things that what, Mr. Vale?"

"Things that shouldn't have been in a private collection." He pushed the envelope an inch toward me. "Triple your best rate, paid in advance. I don't need you to solve anything. I need you to tell me it's a forgery. A joke. A hoax by some bored teenager with a fountain pen and a grudge against the town. I need you to prove it's *nothing*, so I can sleep again."

"And if I can't prove it's nothing?"

His jaw did something complicated. "Then I suppose I'll have found out what you actually are."

There was a time — a whole other life, a whole other name, a whole other me who is legally, officially, and quite thoroughly dead — when I would have had a dozen questions ready and the leverage to ask them. That me is buried under a headstone outside town with a good lie on it. The me behind this counter sells books at a loss and knows the exact day the pension check clears.

"I don't take cases," I said. "I don't investigate anything. I don't even investigate why my landlord's rent increases never arrive on the same date twice. I sell books. Badly. That's the whole enterprise."

"Then I'll leave this anyway." He turned the envelope around and slid it across the counter until it bumped my hand. "Payment for your time. Even if your time is only long enough to tell me to go to hell."

"Mr. Vale —"

"Corin." He was already moving toward the door, and there was something in his hurry that I recognized with a sick, familiar lurch: the walk of a man who has looked at a document he was paid to file and understood that the document was looking back. "The shop closes at six. I'll come back tomorrow. Think about the November ninth entry. That's all I'm asking you to do. Think about it."

The bell rang. The rain took him.

* * *

I should have put the envelope in the drawer. I want that on the record, wherever the record of my life is kept these days, which is a cemetery plot and a set of books that don't balance. A smart woman puts the envelope in the drawer and spends the evening alphabetizing biographies and thinking about anything else.

I opened it on the counter, under the lamp, with the rain hammering the windows like a crowd that wanted in.

Cash. A great deal of it, in twenties, banded and old and soft as cloth. Three times my best rate, and my best rate was a fiction I'd invented to make people go away. Underneath the cash, a single sheet of paper, photocopied, slightly skewed, the kind of copy made in a hurry on a machine that needed toner. A ledger entry. I read it twice.

The first time, my mind did what minds do — it looked for the trick, the typo, the tell. The entry was typeset like the others around it would have been, in that municipal serif that makes every official record look like a summons. *November 9. Homicide. Victim: pending. Location: the bookshop, Marrow's Bend. Verdict: third.*

The second time, I stopped reading the words and started reading the document, because that's the other thing the old machinery does when you feed it. The paper was a photocopy, but the entry it copied had been typeset — typeset, not typed, not handwritten — in a font that did not exist in 1912 and did not exist in 2012 either, unless someone had access to a very specific press and a great deal of patience. Someone who wanted the forgery to be found. Someone who wanted it to look official and wrong at the same time, like a passport that's perfect except for one letter in your name.

And the phrase. *Verdict: third.* A ledger doesn't have verdicts. Ledgers have entries. Verdicts come from courtrooms, from juries, from judges — from people paid to decide what happened. A murder record that lists a verdict before the murder, a verdict numbered as though it were one in a series —

I put the page down. My hands were steady. I noted that they were steady, the way you note a strange calm in a dog before an earthquake.

Then I lifted out the last item in the envelope, the one that had been sitting beneath the cash with its weight distributed all wrong, too flat, too familiar, and I knew what it was before I turned it over. Some part of me had known since the bell rang. Some part of me had known for six years, every time a car slowed outside the shop, every time the phone rang and the caller breathed before speaking.

A detective's badge. Brass, worn soft at the edges, the number stamped deep. Mine. The one that had gone into the river with the rest of that life, or was supposed to. The one that should have been rusting under six feet of cold water and a better class of lie.

The rain kept on. The radiator clanked. Somewhere down the street, Marisol's porch light came on, warm and yellow and oblivious, and I stood in my failing bookshop holding the one object in the world that could get me killed, feeling the old furnace roar back to life behind my ribs.

Three weeks. November ninth. A murder dated in advance, at my address, with a verdict already rendered — and my dead badge paying for the privilege of knowing.

I should have put it in the drawer.

Instead, I turned off the lamp, and in the dark, with the rain covering everything the way it always does, I got down the centennial ledger that Corin Vale had left tucked inside the umbrella stand like a man leaving a bomb — and I started to read.
