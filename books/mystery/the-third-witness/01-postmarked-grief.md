## 1. Postmarked Grief

The envelope arrived on a Tuesday, which felt insulting. Tuesdays are for dentist reminders and coupons for gutter cleaning, not for the kind of mail that rearranges your life. It sat in the stack of fan correspondence my producer had forwarded — three months of it, unopened, because I'd sworn off the Cove case the moment I uploaded the final episode of *Dead Water* and watched it become the most downloaded thing I'd ever made.

Success, it turns out, smells like salt water you can't wash out of your hair.

I almost didn't open it. The handwriting stopped me first. Not the fan-scripted *LOVE YOUR VOICE SEBASTIAN* scrawled in gel pen, but something older, careful, the letters formed the way people used to be taught — with a fountain pen's ghost of pressure, each stroke deliberate as a signature on a will.

*Ms. S. Brightwater.*

Not Sebastian. Ms. Brightwater. My family name, delivered back to me like a returned package.

I live alone with a cat who tolerates me and a wall of index cards mapping forty years of a drowning case that officially never interested anyone. Thomas Merrick, nineteen years old, went into Brightwater Cove on the night of August 14th and did not come out. The coroner said accident. The town said nothing at all, which is its own kind of testimony. My podcast spent ten episodes pulling that silence apart thread by thread, and the threads kept leading home — to the harbor master who wouldn't meet my eyes, to the aunt who hung up on me twice, to an inheritance that arrived in my childhood like manna and never once explained itself.

But here's what nobody listening knew: I wasn't chasing Thomas Merrick. I was chasing the shape of a door in my own house that everyone walked around and no one opened.

So when I slit the envelope with a butter knife because I couldn't find scissors, my hands were already shaking, and I told myself it was caffeine.

* * *

The letter was two pages, written in ink gone slightly brown at the edges, as if it had been waiting somewhere dark and damp for a long time. Or as if it had been written long ago and only recently decided to exist.

*I am writing this because Margaret is dead now,* it began, *and she was the last one keeping the promise.*

Margaret Voss. The final witness. The woman whose funeral I'd covered in episode ten from a respectful distance, narrating over archival audio while she was lowered into ground she'd spent four decades standing on the shore above. She'd given one interview in her life, to a newspaper in 1987, and retracted it within the week. I'd built half a season on her retraction.

*You will want to know why I waited. The answer is cowardice, and then habit, and then the discovery that they are the same thing worn long enough.*

I read the next line four times.

*Thomas Merrick did not drown by accident. He drowned because three people stood on the breakwater and let him, and one of us could have stopped it and did not. I was there. Cole Fenwick was there. And the third —*

The pen had pressed so hard here it nearly tore the paper.

*— the third was your grandfather, Aldous Brightwater. You were seven when he died. You think he left you that money out of love. Ask yourself instead what love costs, and what silence pays.*

I set the letter down on my kitchen table, next to the cat's dish, and discovered I was standing in my living room with no memory of crossing it. That's the thing about dread. It doesn't announce itself. It just relocates you, quietly, the way the tide moves a body.

* * *

Here is what I know about Edith Brightwater, and I know it the way you know a scar — intimately, without wanting to.

She raised me after my parents' car went off Route 1. She was fifty-one then, brisk and unreadable, a woman who expressed affection through casseroles and criticism in equal measure. She paid for my college out of the inheritance — *Aldous's gift*, she always called it, capital-G Gift, as though it were a religious event rather than a trust fund. When I turned twenty-six and started asking questions about where the money came from, she stopped calling. Not angrily. Just completely, the way a lighthouse goes dark: no drama, only absence.

Forty years ago, Edith was twenty-three. Old enough to know better, young enough to be ordered around. She'd worked summers at the harbormaster's office under Cole Fenwick, who was then a young deputy with a jaw like a cliff face and ambitions he never quite confessed to.

And she signed her letter *E.B.* As if I might not know. As if there were another E.B. in the world whose handwriting I'd recognize across a crowded postmark.

I called her. Of course I called her. The phone rang six times, and I counted every one like a pulse.

"You got my letter." Her voice was flat as slate. Not a question. She'd known the moment I dialed; nobody else calls from that number.

"Aunt Edith."

"Don't." A pause long enough to hear the sea in it, or maybe just her breathing. "Don't do the podcast voice with me, girl. I've heard your show. Everyone in this town has heard your show. We listen to you take our dead apart every Thursday like it's church."

"Then you know I have to ask."

"You don't have to do anything. That's the lie we tell children." Something clinked on her end — a cup against a saucer, the small domestic percussion of a woman pretending her hands were steady. "Margaret's funeral was Saturday. Monday the letter went in the post. Do the arithmetic if you must, but do it alone."

"Why now? Why not ten episodes ago, when I was begging you—"

"Because Margaret was alive," she said, "and Margaret asked. And because—" The sentence broke off like a wave against rock. When she came back, her voice had dropped to something I'd never heard from her: small. "Because I'm seventy-one years old, Sebastian, and I am tired of carrying a man's secret in my chest alongside his photograph."

"What photograph?"

Silence. Then, very quietly: "Come home. If you're going to dig, dig where I can watch you. There are things in that town that would rather you dug blind."

She hung up before I could ask what things. She's good at that. Forty years of practice, apparently.

* * *

That night I laid everything out on the floor of my apartment like a crime scene of my own life. Ten seasons of research, four hundred index cards, the coroner's report I'd paid a favor to copy, tide charts for August 14th, and now this letter, weighted down at the corners with the cat's toys because my hands weren't steady enough to trust it to gravity alone.

Two other witnesses, she'd named. Cole Fenwick — expected, confirmed, the man who'd looked through me during every interview like I was weather. And Aldous Brightwater. My grandfather. The man whose portrait hung in Edith's hallway, whose money bought my education, whose name I wear like a coat I can't take off.

I thought about the way the town closed ranks in every interview. Not hostile — worse, courteous. Doors opened. Coffee poured. Stories gently steered away from the cove toward safer waters, the cannery fire, the '78 flood. I'd called it loyalty in the show. I'd called it grief.

It was choreography. It had always been choreography, and I'd been dancing in it my whole life without hearing the music.

The cat climbed into the middle of the evidence and sat down, which struck me as either commentary or comfort. I chose comfort. I'm allowed one delusion per crisis.

At midnight I did the thing I always do when I can't sleep: I read the letter again. And this time I noticed what grief had made me miss — a second sheet, folded inside the first, thinner, older. A list, in the same careful hand:

*What I saw:*
*The boat returning without him.*
*Aldous on the breakwater, and he was not surprised.*
*Cole holding the lantern so the light fell wrong.*
*Margaret crying, and being told to stop.*
*The locket.*

The locket. No explanation. Just those two words sitting at the bottom of the list like a stone at the bottom of a well, and beneath them, added later, in fresher ink:

*The tide gives back what it takes, eventually. It has begun.*

I sat on my floor surrounded by forty years of someone else's silence and understood, finally, why the case had chosen me before I ever chose it. This wasn't a story I was reporting. It was a debt I'd inherited, accruing interest since before I could walk, and the letter on my floor wasn't a confession.

It was a summons.

I booked a flight to Maine before the sun came up. In the seat-back pocket literature, wedged between the safety card and someone's abandoned gum wrapper, I found myself writing one note on a napkin, the first entry in whatever this season would become:

*Three witnesses. One locket. Come home and count who's still breathing.*

Somewhere behind me, three thousand miles east, a town was already watching the road for my rental car.
