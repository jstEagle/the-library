## 1. The Retainer

The rain had been falling on Marlowe Street since Tuesday, and by Thursday afternoon it had worked its way into everything — the gutters, the grout between the flagstones, the seams of my coat, and finally, inevitably, my mood. I sat in my office above the shuttered milliner's shop with my shoes off and my feet on the radiator, watching water trace silver veins down the window glass, and I was thinking about closing early when the man came up my stairs.

I heard him before I saw him. That is not a boast about my hearing; it is an observation about his walking. Most people climb unfamiliar stairs with a certain apology in their step, half-hesitant, ready to retreat. This man climbed as though the stairs had been built for him and he was mildly disappointed in their craftsmanship. Each footfall was deliberate, evenly spaced, unhurried. A man who had never once been told no, or who had stopped hearing it so long ago that the word had lost its meaning.

He filled my doorway at four minutes past three. Tall — taller than me by a head and then some — with the kind of good looks that seem manufactured rather than grown: dark hair swept back from a high forehead, a jaw you could have used to true a wheel, eyes the grey-green of harbor water over sand. His coat was camel hair, cut close, dry despite the weather, which meant either a car waiting at the curb or an umbrella held by someone else. His gloves were kid leather, dove-grey, and he held them folded in one hand like a small dead animal.

"Miss Brightwater." He said it as though confirming a reservation. "Corwin Vale."

"I don't take walk-ins on Thursdays," I said, which was a lie. I took walk-ins whenever they could pay, which is the privilege and the shame of the freelance investigator: we are open all hours to anyone with money and a problem, and closed to everyone else.

"Then consider this Friday," he said, and smiled, and crossed the room without being asked, and sat down in the client chair across from my desk as though he had sat in it many times before. "I'm told you have a memory like a photograph."

"I'm told a lot of things."

"What I've read suggests it's more than talk. The Halloway matter. The drowned archivist. You testified once in front of Judge Merrick and recited, from memory, eleven months later, the exact wording of a lease clause you'd seen for ninety seconds." He leaned back, crossing one ankle over his knee. "Merrick called you 'a human affidavit.' I enjoyed that."

"You've done your reading."

"I do everything thoroughly, Miss Brightwater. It's why I'm here."

I put my feet down and reached for the notebook I kept in the desk drawer — cheap, spiral-bound, mostly for show, since anything worth recording lived behind my eyes anyway. But clients like to see a notebook. It makes them feel their money is going somewhere visible.

"So," I said. "Tell me what happened."

"Nothing has happened," Corwin Vale said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only religion I keep, and because what he said next deserves to be set down exactly as it was said, in the order it was said, with the pauses where he chose to place them.

He unfolded his hands. "Three weeks from tonight — the night of the twenty-sixth — my uncle, Elias Vale, will be found dead in his study at Vale House. Locked room. Heart failure, if we're lucky; something less natural if we're not. And I would like to hire you to establish, beyond any doubt, that this murder has not yet occurred."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere below, the milliner's sign swung on its bracket and knocked against the brick — knock, knock, knock — a sound I had heard ten thousand times and never noticed until that moment.

"That's not a sentence," I said finally. "It's several sentences wearing a trench coat."

He laughed. It was a good laugh, warm, unforced, and I noted — professionally, coldly, the way I note everything — that he deployed it precisely where a lesser man would have gotten defensive.

"My uncle is a shipping magnate," he went on. "Vale & Sons, though there are no sons anymore, only nephews and a niece and a great deal of tonnage. He is seventy-one years old, he has the heart of a man who has spent five decades shouting at harbormasters, and he has recently executed a new will that — how shall I put this — rearranges the family furniture considerably. My sister Imogen is disinherited under it. Entirely. Cut off with a single line and a Latin phrase my solicitor had to translate twice."

"And you?"

"I do well enough. Better than well. Which is why the will interests me less than the timing." He produced, from an inner pocket of his coat, a document in a cream-colored folder, and laid it on my desk with both hands, flat, squaring its corners to the edge of the blotter as though aligning it were a small sacrament. "This is a copy of the new will. Look at the date of execution."

I looked. I looked longer than I needed to, because I wanted him to see me looking, and because some part of my mind — the part that never sleeps, the part that files everything whether I ask it to or not — had already begun to scream.

The will of Elias Ambrose Vale was dated the twenty-sixth. Not signed on the twenty-sixth. *Dated* the twenty-sixth — the date Corwin Vale had just named, three weeks hence, as the date of his uncle's death. The ink of the date sat slightly proud of the paper, fresher than the rest, or perhaps I imagined that; I couldn't yet know, and knowing the difference between what I observed and what I inferred is the discipline of my whole life.

"This is dated for a day that hasn't happened," I said.

"Yes."

"Wills are dated when they're executed. Signed, witnessed, the whole liturgy."

"Yes."

"So either your uncle's solicitor made an extraordinary clerical error—"

"He didn't."

"—or someone backdated a document to match a death that hasn't occurred, which means someone knows the death is coming, which means—" I stopped. The sentence had a cliff at the end of it and I could see the drop.

"Which means," Corwin Vale said gently, "that either my uncle intends to die on the twenty-sixth, or someone intends it for him. And I am paying you triple your rate — I know your rate; I asked — to find out which, and to prove, if you can, that no murder has taken place. Yet." He smiled again, and this time the smile did not reach the harbor-water eyes. "Preventive work, Miss Brightwater. Think of it as insurance. You investigate for insurers, I'm told. Consider me insuring against a crime."

* * *

There are moments when a case takes hold of you, and every investigator I've ever respected describes it the same way: not as a decision but as a symptom. A fever. You tell yourself you can still decline, still push the folder back across the desk, still go home and eat whatever's in the icebox and sleep a dreamless sleep. But your pulse has already changed key, and you know, in the wordless way you know a storm is coming off the water before the first drop lands, that you are already inside the thing.

I should have declined. Let me say that plainly, since this account will be read — if it is read at all — after everything, by people who will want to know whether Ansel Brightwater saw the trap. I saw *a* trap. I did not see *the* trap. There is a difference, and the difference cost a woman her freedom and nearly cost her her name, and I will carry it the way I carry everything: permanently, in perfect focus, unable to blur.

But that Thursday afternoon, all I knew was this: a beautiful man sat in my shabby office with rain on the windows and a will dated for a future death, offering me triple rate to prevent a murder that hadn't happened yet.

Anomalies are my weakness. I'll confess it the way other people confess drink. Most cases are mud — flat, brown, featureless, a husband's infidelity or a warehouse pilferage, matters that yield to patience and nothing else. But once in a great while a case arrives with a wrongness in it, a single fact that does not fit any honest world, and my mind seizes on that fact the way a terrier seizes a rat, and shakes it, and will not put it down. A will dated three weeks ahead of itself. A death scheduled like a steamer's departure. A client who wanted the murder proven *not yet committed* — not prevented, mind you, not even investigated, merely *verified as pending*. The phrasing snagged in me like a fishhook.

"Why not go to the police?" I asked.

"Because the police require a corpse, Miss Brightwater, or at minimum a crime. I have neither. I have a date and a feeling. They'd laugh me out of the precinct house, and my uncle — who is very much alive and very much aware of his own mortality — would be humiliated, and I would have spent my one opportunity to act quietly." He spread his gloved hands. "You, on the other hand, deal in anomalies professionally. And you are discreet. Your references confirm it."

"Who gave you my name?"

"A mutual acquaintance. You wouldn't thank me for naming them." He rose, smoothed his coat, and placed a second envelope beside the will — heavy, sealed, and when I weighed it in my hand afterward I understood that it contained three weeks of my fee, paid in advance, in cash. Triple rate. He hadn't exaggerated. "You'll want to see Vale House. My uncle keeps townhouse hours; he's at home most evenings after seven. Tell the housekeeper, Mrs. Tarn, that you're expected. She won't question it — she questions nothing, which is either a virtue or a tragedy, I've never decided which."

"And if I find evidence that a murder is being planned? Actual evidence?"

"Then you bring it to me first," he said, from the doorway, "and we decide together what to do with it. After all—" and here he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at me with an expression I would spend six weeks taking apart, memory by memory, trying to find the warning hidden in it, "—you're the only person in this city who can testify to what the world looked like *before*. Everyone else only sees what's left afterward. Good evening, Miss Brightwater. I'll be in touch."

His footsteps descended my stairs with the same metronome certainty they'd ascended. The street door opened and shut. When I got to the window, wet glass fogging where I breathed, I saw a black car pull away from the curb through the rain, wipers sweeping, taillights dissolving into the grey.

* * *

I sat up most of that night with the will, though I hardly needed to sit with it at all. By midnight I could reproduce it entire — every clause, every codicil, every Latin phrase — projected on the inside of my skull in its own handwriting, sharp as type. That is the gift and the affliction of what I am: I do not remember documents, I *contain* them. Page nine, clause four, the disinheritance of Imogen Vale, "for causes sufficient unto myself, *satis causa*," witnessed by R. Pell and M. Oduya, executed — dated — the twenty-sixth of the month, a day that had not yet dawned.

At two in the morning I got out a fresh sheet of paper and wrote down everything I could recall of Corwin Vale himself, because faces fade for normal people and mine do not, and I wanted the record while it was clean. The camel-hair coat, dry. The gloves, dove-grey, folded. The way he squared the will's corners to my blotter — a man who straightens objects without noticing he does it, which usually means a man who needs the world arranged, who cannot abide a loose thread or an unsquared corner. The pause before "*yet*." The smile that arrived half a beat after his eyes finished moving, like thunder after lightning, as though the smile were a translation of something happening elsewhere in him.

And the date. Always the date. The twenty-sixth, sitting on the page in ink that might have been fresher than its neighbors, might have been pressed there by a different hand, might have been — I made myself write the word, though writing it made it real — *forged*.

By four I had filled six pages. By five I had memorized those too, which is the particular madness of my condition: I now possessed a perfect memory of my own speculations, indistinguishable in texture from the facts themselves unless I disciplined the filing. Facts: the will, the date, the money, the man. Inference: everything else. I drew a line down the page and sorted them, and the inference column ran three times longer than the fact column, and I sat looking at it while the rain thinned towards dawn and the milliner's sign knocked its slow knuckles against the brick.

Triple rate. I turned the phrase over and over. People pay triple rate for two reasons: desperation, or misdirection. Desperation I understood — a nephew frightened for an uncle would empty his accounts gladly. But misdirection paid *in advance*, misdirection that bought not my success but my *presence* — that purchased, above all, a witness, a woman whose testimony about dates and documents would someday be worth more than gold — that kind of payment had a shape to it, and the shape was wrong. You do not hire a human affidavit to prevent a crime. You hire her to certify one.

I burned the thought in the sink of my mind and fished it back out twice before sunrise.

At seven, the light came up the color of dishwater. I dressed, loaded my camera, pocketed the retainer envelope — I am honest, but I am not a fool, and anomaly or no, the money was real — and stood a moment at the window looking east toward the river district, where the cranes of Vale & Sons stood against the brightening sky like the masts of drowned ships.

Three weeks until the twenty-sixth. A will dated for a death. A client who wanted the future verified rather than changed.

I locked my office door behind me and went down into the rain to meet it.
