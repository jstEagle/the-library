## 1. The Will That Wasn't

The courier arrived at 4:47 on a Thursday afternoon, which was, Orsen Brightwater would later reflect, exactly the kind of hour bad news preferred. Too late to start anything new. Too early to blame the day's fatigue. The light through her office windows had gone the color of weak tea, and she was three lines into a closing letter for the Halloway trust—clean work, finished work, the kind that ended in signatures and silence—when the buzzer sounded.

She pressed the intercom without looking up. "Office closes at five."

"Delivery for Brightwater." The voice was young, bored, already halfway back down the stairs in spirit.

"Leave it with the building."

"It's signature-required. Sealed instrument." A pause, and then, with the faint relish of someone who did not know what he was carrying: "Probate."

Orsen set down her pen. She had learned, over eleven years of practice in the town of Grayhollow, that the word *probate* arrived attached to two kinds of envelopes: the kind that contained money, and the kind that contained trouble wearing money's clothes. She buzzed him up because refusing a sealed instrument at the door was the sort of small discourtesy that came back to you later, in front of a judge.

He was a narrow young man in a gray windbreaker, holding a padded envelope against his chest like a shield. He watched her sign the little screen with the wariness of someone who had been yelled at by lawyers before, and left before the elevator doors had fully opened.

The envelope was heavy for its size. Orsen turned it over twice under the desk lamp. No return address. The sender line read only: *Estate of M. Voss — per instruction.* The paper was good stock, cream-colored, the kind that cost more per sheet than most people's stationery budgets allowed. Someone had paid attention to the details.

That should have been her first warning. Details were her territory.

She slit it open with the bone-handled knife she kept for the purpose—a gift from her brother Pell, back when they still exchanged gifts—and drew out a document bound with a single black ribbon, tied in a bow so precise it looked machine-made.

*Last Will and Testament of Maren Ilsa Voss.*

Orsen knew the name the way everyone in Grayhollow knew the name. Seven years ago, Maren Voss had walked out of her house on Corliss Street on an ordinary Tuesday in October and simply ceased to exist. No body. No note. No withdrawals from her accounts, no sightings, no credit card activity, nothing. Her coat had turned up folded neatly on the riverbank below the old tannery walk, dry as a sermon, though the police report would eventually say otherwise—the report said *waterlogged*, said *consistent with drowning*, said all the things reports say when they have already decided their ending. The search had lasted nine days. The case had closed itself out of embarrassment. Missing persons files in towns like Grayhollow didn't stay open; they went into drawers, and drawers went into basements, and basements flooded, and that was that.

Maren Voss had been twenty-six years old. She was now thirty-three, if she was anywhere at all.

Orsen read the will standing up, which was not her habit. Her habit was to sit, to read twice, to annotate in pencil. But something about the document made her want it at arm's length, the way you hold a letter that might be from someone dead.

It was short. Short wills were either very honest or very strange. This one managed both. It named no executor among the living. Instead, in language so formal it felt almost archaic, it appointed *the attorney who opens this instrument* to probate the estate, and it directed that the estate be administered "in full daylight, before witnesses, with nothing sealed and nothing deferred."

Nothing sealed. Orsen looked at the ribbon, the wax, the courier's signature requirement. Whoever wrote this had wanted it opened loudly.

Then she reached the notarization block, and stopped.

The jurat was dated the fourteenth of March—four years ago. Which meant the will had been executed, signed, sworn, and stamped a full three years after Maren Voss vanished from the face of the earth. Three years after the coat on the riverbank. Three years after the drawer in the sheriff's basement.

A will notarized by a dead woman.

Orsen sat down slowly. This was the moment, she understood even then, where a careful lawyer set the document down, called the bar's ethics line, and declined the engagement in writing. Dead clients could not retain counsel. Estates of the missing required court appointment, sureties, declarations of death after seven years—all of it procedural, all of it slow, none of it hers unless a judge handed it to her. There was a version of Orsen Brightwater who did exactly that, billed nothing, slept well, and never thought about it again.

She was proud of being that version. She had built a reputation on it. *Closable*, Ada Quill used to call her, half-teasing, when Ada was still a detective and Orsen still brought her coffee at the station. *You don't leave threads hanging, girl. You tie them off or you cut them clean.* It was the highest compliment Orsen knew how to want. In a town that ran on old families and older secrets, discretion wasn't just professional courtesy—it was currency, and Orsen had spent eleven years becoming solvent.

So she did the responsible thing. She picked up the phone to call the ethics line.

And as she lifted it, her eye caught the notary stamp.

It was a good stamp, crisp, deeply impressed into the paper: an embossed seal with a set of scales, flanked by the words *Corven & Ashe, Notaries Public, Grayhollow*. Underneath, in faded blue ink, a signature she didn't recognize—*R. Corven*—and a commission number.

Orsen put the phone down without dialing.

She knew Corven & Ashe. Everyone in Grayhollow real estate knew Corven & Ashe; they had stamped half the deeds in the county for forty years. And she knew, with the particular clarity of a memory she hadn't asked for, exactly when they had stopped existing.

The fire had been six years ago—three years ago this coming autumn. She had driven past it, actually, the night it burned: the old brick office on Weaver Street lit orange from inside, firemen hauling filing cabinets into the rain, a century of the town's paper history going up in smoke while the owner, old Corven himself, stood on the curb in his slippers looking not devastated but *relieved*, which Orsen had noticed at the time and filed away and never once needed until this exact moment.

The firm had dissolved within the year. Corven had moved to his daughter's place upstate. The commission number on this stamp belonged to a man whose authority had expired with the fire.

Which meant the seal on this document was either forged—or it had been pressed into warm wax by a hand that owned the original die, sometime before the fire, and dated forward.

Either way, someone had gone to considerable trouble to make a dead woman's will look legitimate. And had done it *well*. The paper was right. The ink was right. The ribbon was right. Only the date was impossible, and the date was the whole point. Whoever prepared this instrument had wanted the impossibility found—not hidden, *found*—by precisely one person: the attorney who opened it.

Orsen stood at her window for a long time. Below, Weaver Street was putting on its evening lights. Somewhere a bus sighed at the corner. The town went about its business, seven years comfortable in the story it had told itself about Maren Voss: sad girl, sad coat, sad river, closed file.

Her pride, that clean closable pride, sat in her chest like a stone going cold.

Because here was the thing she could not stop noticing, the thing that curdled the professionalism in her mouth: the will didn't ask her to find Maren Voss. It didn't mention her disappearance at all. It treated her as what the law insisted she still was—alive, absent, her estate in suspension. It was, technically, the most legally correct document Orsen had ever held. As if whoever drafted it knew probate law better than she did. As if they knew *her*.

*Per instruction,* the envelope said. Whose instruction?

Orsen looked at the clock. 5:12. The ethics line had closed at five. She told herself that was the only reason she hadn't called.

She locked the will in the bottom drawer of her desk—the one that stuck, the one no one else knew stuck—and stood there a moment with her hand flat on the wood, feeling absurdly like a woman guarding a door.

On her way out, she paused at the coat rack. Through the frosted glass of the outer office, the last of the daylight lay across the empty reception chairs in long amber bars. Everything in order. Everything closable. Eleven years of tidy endings stacked behind her like a wall.

"One anomaly," she said aloud to the empty room, testing how it sounded. It sounded like the first thread of something that did not intend to be tied off.

She took the stairs down two at a time, and instead of turning toward home, she turned toward Corliss Street—to the sealed house of a woman three years dead on paper and nowhere at all in fact—telling herself she was only going to look at the outside. Just the outside. Just to see.

The porch key was in the county lockbox, and she was the kind of lawyer who knew which clerk kept the copy, and by full dark she was standing in Maren Voss's front hall with a flashlight, telling herself this was still diligence, still procedure, still the responsible beginning of a proper refusal.

The dust on the floor lay thick and silvered, seven years deep, unbroken except for one set of marks leading from the front door to the study desk—fresh, shallow, days old at most.

And the desk drawer stood open an inch, as if someone had recently been reading.

* * *

Orsen stood in the doorway of the study for a long count of ten, listening to the house breathe around her. Old houses breathed. This one breathed through gaps in the window casements and the loose chimney flashing, a low tidal hush, and beneath it she could hear the particular silence of a room that had been waiting.

The flashlight beam crossed the desk. Oak, heavy, positioned under the window where the streetlight reached. On its surface, dust lay undisturbed in a smooth gray skin—except for the rectangle where a book had recently rested, a clean pale shadow in the grime, and beside it a faint arc where something round, a cup perhaps, had been set down and taken away.

Someone had sat at this desk. Recently. Within the week, if the dust told true, and dust in a sealed house was honest in a way witnesses rarely were.

The open drawer was the second drawer down on the left. Inside it, Orsen's beam found a single object: a diary bound in dark green cloth, its spine cracked white from use, a rubber band holding it shut that had dried to brittleness and snapped at one end.

Every instinct she had trained for eleven years said *don't touch it*. Chain of custody. Contamination. Call the sheriff, call Ada, call anyone, make it someone else's problem in a properly documented way.

But the sheriff's department had declared this house searched seven years ago. The sheriff's department had written *no signs of struggle, no indication of planning* in a file that also claimed a dry coat was waterlogged. If she called them tonight, by morning the diary would be in an evidence bag in a basement drawer, and drawers in Grayhollow had a way of forgetting what they held.

Orsen pulled on the thin cotton gloves she kept in her coat pocket for handling old deeds, and lifted the diary out as gently as lifting a sleeping bird.

It was heavier than it looked. She carried it to the window, where the streetlight gave a cleaner light than her flash, and opened it to the first page.

*Maren I. Voss. If found, return to me—or to whoever is finally asking the right questions.*

Orsen laughed once, a short involuntary sound with no humor in it, and the house seemed to lean in.

She turned pages. The early entries were ordinary to the point of heartbreak—grocery lists bleeding into reflections, complaints about the shipping office where she worked, a wry running grievance about the price of coffee and the manners of her guardian's associates. A life in ink. A real one. Orsen read fast, skimming, hunting dates, and the dates marched steadily forward through months and years—

And did not stop.

Not in October, seven years ago. Not when the coat was folded on the riverbank. Not when the file was closed. The handwriting continued, thinner now, more careful, letters formed with deliberate control as though the writer had learned to write quietly. The entries grew shorter, coded, watchful. Numbers appeared in the margins. Phrases repeated like a refrain:

*He counts everything. He thinks I don't see him counting.*

Orsen checked the final dated entry, and her thumb stayed pressed on the page longer than she intended.

Three years after the vanishing. The same season as the impossible notarization. Maren Voss had been writing in this house—or somewhere—invisible, alive, watching someone, for three full years after her town had buried her in paperwork.

The last entry was one line long, and Orsen read it four times.

*If you are reading this, the will has found you. Good. Now count with me.*

Outside, far down Corliss Street, a car slowed. Orsen killed the flashlight without deciding to—her body decided, some older instinct than training—and stood motionless in the dark study of a woman the whole town agreed was dead, listening to the engine idle, then pull away.

Her heart was going hard. Her professional pride, that beautiful closable reputation, lay somewhere back at the office in the bottom drawer with the sealed will, and she understood that she was not going to call the ethics line tomorrow. She was going to open a file. Her own file, unofficial, unwitnessed, unclosable.

Orsen slid the diary inside her coat, against her ribs, where she could feel it like a second heartbeat, and let herself out of the house, locking the door behind her with hands that were not quite steady.

Behind her, in the study, the dust was already settling back over the pale rectangle on the desk—as if the book had never been there at all.

As if someone had left it out for exactly one night, and exactly one reader.
