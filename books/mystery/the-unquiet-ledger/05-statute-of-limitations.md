## 5. Statute of Limitations

Ada Quill's house sat at the end of Corliss Street like a period at the end of a long sentence—small, square, and final. Orsen had been coming here since she was twenty-three and green as pond water, back when Ada was still wearing a badge and teaching her that a case file was a living thing: it breathed, it grew, it could be starved.

Tonight the porch light was on, which meant Ada expected her. The porch light was always on when Ada expected her. It was one of perhaps four uncomplicated kindnesses in Orsen's life, and she had never once thanked her for it out loud, because Ada would have made it strange.

She knocked twice, then let herself in, because knocking twice was also part of the ritual.

The house smelled of coffee kept too long on the burner and the ghost of cigarette smoke from a habit Ada had quit eleven years ago but still furnished rooms around. She was in the kitchen, reading glasses pushed up into gray hair cropped short as a hedge, a police scanner murmuring to itself on the counter like an old man talking in his sleep.

"You found something," Ada said, without looking up.

"Hello to you too."

"You called at nine p.m. on a Tuesday and asked if I was busy Thursday. You're never polite unless you're scared." She finally looked up over the rims of her glasses. Her eyes were pale blue and unhurried, the eyes of a woman who had spent thirty years watching people lie and had gotten very good at waiting them out. "Sit down. Tell me about the dead girl."

Orsen sat. The kitchen table had a wobble to it that Ada had refused to fix for two decades—she said a detective should learn to work on uneven surfaces—and Orsen set her bag down with the diary inside it and felt the weight of it like a stone in a satchel.

"Maren Voss," Orsen said. "Vanished seven years ago. Presumed drowned—the coat came out of the river, no body ever surfaced. Declared dead three years ago so the estate could sit in trust. Yesterday a courier delivered a will for probate."

"That's not unusual."

"The will is dated four years *after* she disappeared."

Ada took off her glasses entirely. That was the thing about Ada Quill; she didn't react to information, she reacted to what the information *cost* the person delivering it, and right now she was measuring Orsen like a tailor.

"Notarized by whom?"

"A firm that burned down two years before the signature date."

Ada was quiet for a moment. Then she got up, poured two cups of the scorched coffee without asking, and sat back down. In Ada's language, that was a full sentence.

"Tell me the rest," she said. "All of it. Don't organize it first. I want it raw."

So Orsen gave it to her raw. The sealed house with its undisturbed dust and the one drawer recently opened. The diary with entries continuing past the vanishing date. The man who counts. The coded numbers she hadn't cracked yet. The retainer paid through a shell trust she hadn't traced yet. She talked for twenty minutes while the scanner muttered about a fender-bender on Route 9 and the coffee went cold in her hands, and Ada listened the way she'd always listened—not nodding, not prompting, just receiving, like a lock accepting each tumbler of a key until something inside her clicked into position.

When Orsen finished, Ada turned off the scanner. The silence that followed had texture to it, dense as felt.

"Do you know why I retired?" Ada asked.

"You got shot."

"I got shot *because* I wouldn't stop pulling a thread." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "Harlan Dree case, 2011. You were still in law school. Everyone told me the file was closed, the evidence was clean, the widow did it. And the file *was* clean. That's what bothered me. Files aren't clean. Files are messy, they're contradictory, they've got coffee stains and half-legible statements and one deputy who spells 'perpetrator' wrong every single time. A clean file means somebody cleaned it."

Orsen felt the floor tilt slightly under her certainty. "You think Maren Voss's file is clean."

"I think you should find out." Ada leaned forward. "Here's your problem, and you're not going to like hearing it, so I'm going to say it once and then we're going to eat the stew I made yesterday because you look like you've been living on takeout and adrenaline. The murder statute in this state is twenty years, but the *fraud* statutes—the estate fraud, the forgery, the false certification—those run seven. Seven years from the act. Maren Voss vanished seven years ago this spring."

The number landed in Orsen's chest like a dropped weight.

"Ninety days," Ada said. "Give or take. After that, even if you prove someone forged that will, even if you prove the whole drowning was staged—it becomes a civil matter. Money changes hands, nobody goes to prison. Whoever built this knew exactly how long they needed to stay quiet. They're not hiding forever, Orsen. They're hiding until the clock runs out."

* * *

They ate the stew. Ada insisted. It was venison, from a hunter Ada tolerated, and potatoes, and it was better than anything Orsen had cooked in a year, which she chose not to mention because Ada would have taken it as an opening for a lecture about self-care that would last until midnight.

It was over the second bowl that Ada said, "Get me the original investigation file."

"I can request it through—"

"No. Not through channels." Ada set down her spoon. "Channels are how it gets laundered. You request a file, it passes through three desks before it reaches you, and every desk is a chance for a page to go missing or a memo to appear. If someone shaped that investigation—and everything you've told me says someone did—then the official copy is the weapon, not the record." She looked at Orsen over the steam. "I know a clerk at Records who owes me. Tally Ferrow. Granddaughter of a fisherman, sharp as broken glass, bored out of her mind. She'll pull the original intake copies—the ones that never left the building."

"And if those are shaped too?"

"Then we'll know *how* they're shaped, and that tells us who held the pen." Ada's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Every editor leaves fingerprints, Brightwater. That's the one lesson thirty years buys you."

Orsen turned her cup slowly on the uneven table. "There's something else. The retainer that hired me—I traced it halfway. Shell trust. I don't have the name yet, but whoever set it up wanted me on this case specifically. My reputation for discretion. Someone wants a careful lawyer quietly walking toward the truth."

"Or walking toward the version of the truth they've laid out for you." Ada's voice was flat and even, the voice she'd used in interview rooms for decades. "Think about it. Seven years this person sits quiet. Then, ninety-some days before the statute dies, they hire the best closer in the county to probate an impossible will. Why now? Why you?"

"To close the estate cleanly. Legitimize the paperwork before anyone looks."

"Maybe." Ada let the word hang. "Or maybe they need the file reopened just enough to be officially examined and officially found empty. A lawyer of your standing certifies the record, the clock expires, and it's over *with your name on the certificate.*" She tapped the table once, softly. "They're not using you despite your reputation, Orsen. They're using it. Discretion cuts both ways. The same quality that makes you safe to hire makes you safe to frame."

The kitchen felt colder than the stew could fix. Orsen thought of the courier's neutral face, the sealed envelope, the elegant weight of the paper. She had been flattered by the anomaly. She had felt chosen. Now the word had a different taste.

"So what do I do?" she asked. "Refuse the case?"

"Lord, no." Ada almost smiled. "You do the job better than they planned. You get there first. Every manipulator builds a road and prays you walk it straight—they never budget for the client who stops mid-bridge and starts looking over the rail." She stood, collected the bowls, and paused at the sink with her back to Orsen. "Ninety days. Work it backwards. Whatever has to happen for charges to stick—a confession, physical evidence, a witness—it has to happen inside that window, and it has to happen in a way they haven't rehearsed. Which means you can't tell me everything, and I can't tell you everything, and we both have to be comfortable being lied to a little by everyone including each other."

"That sounds miserable."

"It sounds like my marriage." Ada rinsed a bowl. "One more thing. The diary. The later entries—you said they're coded?"

"Number sequences. I haven't broken them yet."

"Don't break them alone, and don't break them where you found them." Ada dried her hands and turned around, and for a moment Orsen saw not the retired mentor but the detective who had walked into Harlan Dree's barn in 2011 and come out with a bullet in her shoulder and a conviction that cost her the career she loved. "If someone opened that drawer recently, they know the diary exists. If they know it exists, they either couldn't retrieve it or they *left* it. Both possibilities mean they're watching what happens next. So whatever you do with that book, do it fast, do it somewhere they can't see, and assume every hour you hold it, someone is deciding whether you're a threat or a tool."

* * *

Orsen drove home with the windows cracked despite the cold, letting the night air scour the smell of woodsmoke and venison out of the car. The town slid past—dark storefronts, the shuttered marina, the war memorial with its brass names going green. Somewhere in all that ordinariness lived a man who counts, a man who had waited seven years for a clock to run down, and who had picked up a phone and hired her with ninety-one days left on the meter.

At a red light she pulled the diary from her bag and set it on the passenger seat, just to feel it there. Just to know it was real.

Her phone buzzed. An email from the records office confirming her formal request for the Voss investigation file—standard processing, ten business days.

Ten business days. Through three desks. Through whatever hands had shaped it the first time.

Orsen watched the light turn green and didn't move. Then she typed a text to a number Ada had scribbled on the back of a stew-stained recipe card:

*Tally Ferrow. Tomorrow. Originals only.*

The reply came in under a minute, from a number she didn't recognize:

*I know. I've been waiting for someone to ask.*

Below it, a photograph loaded slowly over the weak signal: a scan of an intake report, the first page of the Voss file, dated the week Maren disappeared. Orsen read it twice, her breath fogging the windshield.

Everything matched the public record—except the responding officer's name, which appeared nowhere in any summary she'd ever seen, struck through in the original and initialed by someone whose initials she didn't yet recognize.

Someone had edited the file on day one.

And day one had a name.
