## 8. Found Family, Borrowed Office

The office of Brightwater Probate & Estate occupied the second floor of a converted bank on Corliss Street, all original oak paneling and a vault door that no longer locked anything but Orsen's filing cabinets. She had inherited the lease from the man whose practice she'd bought out eleven years ago, and in that time she had never once worked past seven o'clock. Closable cases closed during business hours. That was the whole point of them.

At 9:40 on a Thursday night, the vault door stood open and four people were eating lo mein off case files.

It had not been planned. It had happened the way weather happens—gradually, then all at once.

* * *

It started with Pell.

Orsen hadn't spoken to her brother in two years, not since their mother's estate—a bitter, protracted probate that had ended with Pell accusing her, over email, of "treating family like a docket." He was a forensic accountant in Harrowgate, forty miles east, the kind of man who found fraud the way truffle pigs found fungus: by smell, and with an unsettling amount of digging. When she'd finally worked up the nerve to call him about Maren Voss's ledger numbers—the sequences from the diary that refused to resolve into anything sane—he'd listened for ninety seconds and said, "Send me everything," which was either forgiveness or professional hunger, and with Pell it had always been impossible to tell the difference.

He arrived Friday morning with a garment bag, a laptop covered in stickers from conferences he'd attended out of obligation, and the expression of a man who intended to be useful and then leave.

"Two weeks," he said, setting the bag down inside her door like a border marker. "I'll look at your numbers, I'll tell you what they mean, and then I'm going home."

"Fine," Orsen said.

They both knew it was a lie. They'd been telling each other polite lies since they were children sharing a bedroom, and both of them had gotten very good at hearing through them.

Pell spread the diary's number sequences across his laptop screen and went quiet in the way that made Orsen's chest ache with old memory—the silence of a boy doing homework at the kitchen table while she watched cartoons, both of them alone together. By Saturday afternoon he looked up, gray around the mouth.

"These aren't random. They're page references. Ledger pages. And the figures attached to them—" He turned the screen toward her. "Someone was running a double set of books, Ors. A real one and a pretty one. Your missing woman was reading the real one."

Orsen felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it did when a case stopped being paper and became a person. "The Voss shipping business," she said. "Finch administers it now."

"Then your guardian angel is holding the pretty books," Pell said, "and somebody buried the honest ones."

* * *

Tally Marsh came next, though "came" undersold it. She arrived.

The fisherman's granddaughter was twenty-six, a records clerk at the county annex, and built like someone who had grown up hauling crab pots—compact, quick, permanently faintly sunburned. Orsen had interviewed her grandfather about the coat in the river, and Tally had sat in on the interview uninvited, arms crossed, correcting her grandfather's timeline twice with the gentle ruthlessness of a person who handled official records for a living.

"You said Tuesday," she'd told him. "It was Wednesday. You always say Tuesday because that's when you drink with Denny."

Now she appeared at Orsen's office door on Monday morning holding a box of photocopies like a casserole.

"I pulled every document filed under the Voss name since 1990," she said. "Also every document filed by Halvard Finch, because I got curious, and curiosity is free at the annex." She set the box down. It landed with a thud of real weight. "There's a lot."

"This is confidential material," Orsen began.

"It's public record," Tally said. "I checked. Also, my granddad hasn't slept right since you talked to him. He keeps saying the coat was dry. Nobody believed him thirty years ago when he told the truth about things, and nobody believes him now, and he's eighty-one, and I would like this to be the one time being right does something." Her chin came up. "So. Put me to work or I'll just work."

Orsen looked at her—at the stubbornness, the grief worn as fuel—and thought: Ada is going to love her.

Ada Quill did love her, almost immediately, though she expressed it by assigning Tally the most tedious task imaginable: cross-referencing every date in Maren Voss's diary against every document in Tally's box. Ada herself had claimed the corner of the office nearest the window, where she'd installed a folding chair and an ashtray she wasn't allowed to use, and spent her days reading the police file from the original investigation with the expression of a woman rereading a letter from a lying friend.

"They were steered," she said on Wednesday, tapping the incident report. "Look at this. First responder notes a witness statement about a car near the ferry terminal. The car detail vanishes from every subsequent version. Somebody went back through and sanded it off." She glanced up. "You don't sand a file unless you're hiding grain."

* * *

Thursday night was when the takeout happened.

It was Pell who ordered it—four orders of lo mein and a mountain of dumplings from the place on Delancey—announcing that "nobody finds embezzlement hungry." Ada contributed a bottle of rye from her late husband's cabinet, which she carried in a paper bag like contraband and poured into coffee mugs. Tally brought nothing but ate like a stevedore and asked questions.

It should have been a working session. It started as one. Pell had the ledger reconstruction projected on the wall, columns of green light crawling across the oak paneling, and for two hours they bent over it together—Ada spotting patterns in the dates, Tally matching diary entries to filings, Pell narrating the money's journey in the flat, awed voice he used only for beautiful terrible arithmetic.

"Here," he said, circling something. "Year three after she disappears. The trust pays out a 'conservation easement' to a company called Meridian Holdings. Meridian has no employees, no website, and an address that turns out to be a mail drop two blocks from Finch's office. Two hundred thousand dollars a year, every year, for six years." He leaned back. "She saw this. Before she vanished, she saw the early version of this. The diary numbers are her audit trail. She was building a case against her own guardian, and then she—" He stopped. Looked at the wall. "And then she stopped existing."

The room went quiet. Outside, rain had started, soft against the tall windows, turning the streetlights below into smears of gold.

"She didn't stop," Tally said quietly. Everyone looked at her. She was turning her coffee mug—Ada's rye untouched in front of her, because Tally was twenty-six and careful. "The entries keep going. Past the vanishing. We matched three of them to dated documents in my box. She was alive and writing after the whole town decided she was dead." She looked up, and there was something fierce and wet in her eyes. "Which means somewhere out there is a woman who's been alone for seven years watching everybody believe she drowned."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The rain filled the silence.

"That's why we're doing this," Orsen heard herself say. She hadn't meant to speak; the words came up from somewhere older than the case. "Not the statute clock. Not Finch. Her. Whoever she is now, wherever she is—she left a trail because she wanted someone to find it. Seven years is a long time to keep talking if you think no one's listening."

"We're listening," Tally said simply.

Ada raised her mug. "To the listening," she said.

Pell groaned—"You've known these people for four days, Ada, you can't start toasting"—but he raised his coffee mug too, and Orsen caught him doing it, caught the small traitorous smile, and felt something in her chest unlock that had been rusted shut for two years.

They ate. The lo mein went around twice. Ada told a story about a stakeout in 1987 involving a suspect, a ferret, and a fire alarm that Orsen had heard before and laughed at anyway. Tally explained, with diagrams drawn on a napkin, how the county's filing system could be gamed by anyone patient enough, and Ada took notes. Pell argued with Orsen about whether their mother would have liked any of this people—"She'd have hated all of you," Orsen said fondly, "she hated everyone who wasn't afraid of her"—and the argument was warm in the way only arguments between siblings can be, the kind where the fighting itself is the affection.

At some point Orsen looked around her office—the vault door open, the case files serving as dinner trays, the whiteboard already crowded with arrows and question marks, four coffee mugs sweating rings onto a table that had held nothing but depositions for a decade—and understood that something had shifted that she couldn't put in a file.

Her whole career, she had prized closable cases precisely because they required nothing from her but competence. Open a door, walk through, close it behind you. No one waited on the other side. No one needed feeding at nine p.m. No one noticed if you didn't come back tomorrow.

This case was unclosable in every way that mattered, and the room full of people eating noodles off her evidence proved it. She had opened a door and a family had walked through it sideways, carrying takeout.

"You're smiling," Pell said suspiciously.

"I'm billing you for the lo mein."

"There it is."

* * *

Later—past midnight, when Ada had gone home in a cab she insisted on paying for and Tally had fallen asleep sitting up and been driven home by Pell, who returned smelling of rain and looking softer than he had in years—Orsen stayed behind to lock up.

She stood in the doorway a moment, looking back at the wreckage of the evening: mugs, napkins, the green glow of Pell's abandoned projection still crawling its damning arithmetic across the wall. Meridian Holdings. Two hundred thousand a year. A ghost woman counting.

On the whiteboard, in Tally's round handwriting, someone had written beneath the case heading, without ceremony:

**MAREN VOSS — WE'RE LISTENING.**

Orsen turned off the lights and let the dark have the room.

She was halfway down the stairs, keys in hand, when she remembered she'd left her phone charging in the conference room, and climbed back up—and that was when she saw, through the frosted glass of the front door, the shape of an envelope lying on the mat where none had been an hour ago.

No stamp. No return address. Her name typed across the front in letters too even to be human.

Inside, when she slit it open with trembling fingers under the streetlight: photographs. Glossy, high-resolution, perfectly lit photographs of ledger pages—the very pages Maren's coded numbers pointed to, the pages Pell had reconstructed from inference and math.

Pages that lived, as far as any record showed, in Halvard Finch's private archive.

Photographed from above. From the custodian's side of the desk.

Someone was handing her the truth, piece by perfect piece—and Orsen stood alone in the cold, understanding that a gift like that has only ever meant one thing.

The hunter was helping her follow the trail. Which meant the trail led exactly where he wanted it to go.
