## 1. Salt on the Threshold

The rain had been at the windows all night like a creditor who wouldn't take a hint, and when I came down to open the shop it was still there, patient and gray, working the glass the way water works everything in this town eventually.

Kavanagh's Books occupies the ground floor of a building that predates the harbor wall, on a street the tourists don't walk because it doesn't lead anywhere they want to go. That was the idea, once. I bought it with the last of my pension money and my wife's insurance, two currencies I've never entirely stopped grieving. Eleven years later I sell maybe four books a day, drink tea I let go cold, and know exactly which floorboard creaks under which customer. It's not a living. It's a habit with a roof.

I unlocked the door at half past eight because routine is the only religion I've kept, and stepped out to collect the morning paper from where it lay soaked against the step. That's when I saw the card.

It wasn't an envelope. It was one of those stiff little police cards, the kind they hand out to next of kin and nosy civilians alike, printed with the department's crest and a phone number nobody answers after four o'clock. Someone had pushed it halfway under the door sometime before dawn, and the rain had gotten to it first, softening one corner until it drooped like a wilted flower.

I stood there longer than I should have, letting the wet come up through my slippers, before I picked it up.

*Det. Sgt. R. Calloway requests the pleasure — * no, that's not what it said. Police cards don't request pleasures. It said: *Re: deceased female, recovered from the tideline below Harrow Point. Initial finding: accidental drowning. If you have information—*

The rest was the usual boilerplate. But the address of Harrow Point went through me like a fishhook through soft flesh, catching somewhere I'd rather it didn't.

Another one.

I took the card inside, set it face-down on the counter as if it might bite, and put the kettle on. While it heated I stood at the window watching the gray water of the harbor do its gray work, and I thought about how many times now the sea had handed this town a corpse and called it weather.

Three times that I knew about personally. Three times the official record said *accident*, and three times I'd sat alone in this shop at night with the files spread across the mystery section — which, given my stock, meant most of the store — and done the arithmetic no one else wanted to do.

The first was Tomas Reyes, five years back. A crab boat deckhand, forty-one, found floating off the breakwater with a blood alcohol level the coroner called "consistent with misadventure." Everyone nodded. Men drank; men fell; the sea collected them. But I'd known Tomas slightly — he bought westerns, always paid cash, always sober enough to count it — and I remembered that his boots were never recovered. A man who goes into cold water drunk still kicks. Boots come off, yes, sometimes. But Tomas had been found with his oilskins zipped to the chin, and a man zips his oilskins *before* he works, not before he drowns. More than that: his hands were unmarked, and anyone who has watched a man fight for his life in cold water knows the hands tell the story — torn nails, split skin, the desperate archaeology of gripping things. Tomas's hands were clean as a choirboy's. He hadn't fought anything. He'd been put into the water already finished.

I wrote none of this down anywhere official. I mentioned it once, carefully, to a sergeant who thanked me with the warmth of a man being handed a live eel, and after that I kept it between me and the dead.

The second was Edith Marsh, three years ago. Seventy years old, widow, church organist, found at the base of her own garden steps with a broken neck, a half-mile from where she was supposed to be. She'd gone out, the record said, to check on a neighbor's cat in the rain. Edith had arthritis so bad she used two canes on flat ground. Her garden steps were wet slate with no rail. And the neighbor's cat — I checked, because checking is the only muscle I have left that still works properly — had died the previous winter. There was no cat to check on. Somebody had lured her out, or carried her out, or both. I sat with that one for a long time, because Edith had bought poetry from me, and had once told me my shop smelled "like a place where thinking is allowed," which is the finest review I'll ever receive.

The third was young Danny Quill, eighteen months ago. Apprentice electrician, found in the flooded quarry pond off the old Harrow road, ruled a swimming accident in April water too cold for any sane swim. Danny couldn't swim. His mother had told anyone who'd listen, and everyone had nodded sympathetically and filed him under tragedy anyway, because the alternative was a question nobody in this town could afford to ask.

Nobody could afford it, you understand. Not literally. That's the thing about Port Merrow that took me eleven years to learn properly: grief here is a currency, and somebody has been spending it.

Danny Quill was the one that broke the pattern open for me, though it took months. Because Danny had worked a summer job two years prior — surveying, of all things, on the land above Harrow Point, before the development deal went through. And Edith Marsh had owned a parcel on the same headland that she'd refused to sell, right up until she died and her heirs sold it within the month. And Tomas Reyes — well, Tomas had crewed a boat that ran night deliveries to the Point in the year before the ground broke, deliveries that appeared on no manifest I could ever find.

Three accidents. One headland. One developer whose name was on every plaque in town by then, gilded and grateful.

Aldous Crane.

I never proved anything. Let's be clear about that, since clarity is the last luxury I allow myself. I assembled a shape in the dark, the way you can sometimes see a figure standing in a doorway by the light it blocks rather than the light it casts. I put the shape in a folder in the bottom drawer of my desk, under the spare reading glasses, and I told no one, because the one time I'd tried telling people — years before any of this, in a different life — it had cost me nearly everything, and taught me that a town will forgive a man almost anything except being right too early.

But that's getting ahead of the tide. This morning, the card on my mat was only the fourth death, and I didn't yet know it was going to be different. I didn't yet know it was going to wear a dead woman's clothes.

*

The kettle screamed and I made the tea and drank it standing up, which is how I drink everything now, as if sitting down might become permanent. The shop around me did its usual morning settling — the tick of the old radiator, the smell of paper and dust and the salt that gets into everything in this town, that gets into the books especially, blooming their pages with a faint tide-mark bloom no matter how careful you are. I've stopped fighting it. Salt is honest. It tells you exactly where it's been.

At nine I flipped the sign to OPEN, more from ritual than hope, and dealt with the day's first customer, Mrs. Penhale, who comes in every Tuesday to buy nothing and discuss everything. Today she wanted to talk about the storm, and the gulls, and her hip, and then — dropping her voice as if the shelves might repeat her — about the body.

"You'll have heard," she said. "Down below the Point. Young woman. Drifter, they're saying. Nobody's claimed her."

"I heard something," I said, which was true in the way that a seed is a tree.

"Third one in ten years down that stretch." She shook her head with the relish of genuine sorrow. "That water takes people. Always has."

"That water doesn't take people, Mrs. Penhale. People get taken."

She looked at me the way people look at me when I say things like that — a flicker of something, quickly closed, like a curtain drawn over a lit window. "Well," she said. "You were police, weren't you. Once."

"Once," I agreed, and sold her a paperback she didn't want, because that's the transaction we actually come for, and she left, and the bell over the door gave its small tired jangle, and I was alone again with the rain and the card lying face-down on the counter like a thing I was trying not to wake.

*

Here is what eleven years of retail solitude does to a former detective: it turns your mind into a room you can't leave. I spent the middle of the day shelving a box of donations from the estate sale of a man I'd vaguely known, and while my hands sorted thrillers from sermons, my head sorted the card.

*Deceased female. Recovered from the tideline below Harrow Point.*

Below Harrow Point. Where Tomas's boat had run its dark errands. Where Edith's refusal had been an inconvenience. Where Danny had surveyed ground someone needed surveyed by someone else. Where Crane's memorial garden now stood, all sculpted grass and commemorative benches, built — and I have never believed this was sentiment — directly over the geography of everything buried.

And *drifter*, Mrs. Penhale had said. *Nobody's claimed her.* Which meant no family pressing questions, no history in town, no one to notice if the details didn't quite add up. The sea's ideal victim: a person who arrived without a past, so the past could be written for her.

I turned the card over. Read it again. The sergeant's name meant nothing — Calloway, new to me, probably new to the job, probably assigned the case precisely because he'd have no reason to look sideways at it. The wording was standard. *Initial finding: accidental drowning.* Initial. As if the finding were a first draft, subject to revision, though we both knew findings in this town were never revised upward toward murder. Only downward toward peace.

I thought about calling it in. Offering my assistance, such as it was — a bookseller with a reputation like a damp stain. I even had the phone in my hand.

Then I set it down, because I heard the voice of Sergeant Okafor in my memory — the coroner, the one ally the years hadn't managed to starve out — saying what she always says: *Felix, if you come near one of my cases, I have to report it, and if I report it, it dies in a drawer. Stay home. Read a book.*

So instead I did what I do. I opened the bottom drawer, moved the spare glasses, and took out the folder that officially does not exist. Three names. Three dates. One headland. I laid it all out on the counter between the till and a stack of unsold Proust, and I added a fourth line in pencil:

*Unknown female. Tideline, Harrow Point. [Date].*

Four deaths in ten years, all within a mile of each other, all ruled by water. In any other town, that would be a coincidence. In Port Merrow, it was a signature. Somebody had been signing this coast for a decade, and the sea had obligingly provided the pen.

I sat with that until the light began to fail, and then I did the thing I do every evening and pretend isn't a ritual: I checked the tide tables pinned beside the door. Old habit. A detective learns the tides the way a priest learns Latin — the water gives up what it wants to give up, when it wants to give it up, and everything else stays down there keeping its secrets, sometimes for years, sometimes forever.

Low water tomorrow: 4:17 in the morning.

I don't know why that number snagged me. It was just a number, just the moon doing its arithmetic. But I stood there looking at it while the rain started up again against the glass, and I felt the particular cold that isn't the weather — the one that starts behind the sternum when a pattern you've been feeding in the dark finally opens its eyes.

The card was still on the counter. I picked it up, read it one last time, and my eye caught the detail I'd skimmed past all day, the one line of description the department had bothered to include, the kind of thing that goes in a file to make a file feel complete:

*Female, approx. 30–40 yrs. Recovered dressed in a dark green dress, good quality, and a wool coat.*

Something in me went very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when a floorboard creaks upstairs and you realize you're not alone in it.

Because I knew that coat. Not this coat — I'd never seen this coat. But six years ago, in a courtroom that ruined me, a witness had worn a coat cut exactly like it, dark wool, brass buttons, and I had spent three days watching her hands while she testified, and those hands had told me truer things than her mouth ever did.

Maren Voss. Dead six years. Drowned off the rocks below Harrow Point, they said, though they never found her — only the coat, on the stones, and a death certificate signed by a doctor conveniently beyond questioning now.

I stood in my failing bookshop with the rain coming down and the tide turning somewhere out in the dark, and I understood, with the calm that arrives just before the ground gives way, that the sea had finally washed up something it had been holding for six years.

Tomorrow at low water, I was going down to the mortuary.
