## 3. What the Tide Keeps

The mortuary at Greyhaven General sat in the basement like a secret the hospital kept from itself, down a corridor that smelled of disinfectant doing an inadequate job against something older and patient. I'd been in that corridor four times over the years — once for each of the quiet cases, and once for my wife, though that time I'd refused to go past the door. Some thresholds you only cross when you have no choice left.

Detective Sergeant Imogen Hale was waiting for me by the double doors, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who had already lost an argument with herself.

"You're not on this case," she said.

"No."

"You're retired."

"Also true."

"And yet here you are, at eight in the morning, looking like a man who slept in his coat." She sighed, which I took as the sound of the argument ending. "Corrigan said you could have ten minutes. He's lying, by the way. You'll get twenty, because he's soft, and because he owes you for the Alder Street business, and he knows it, and he hates knowing it."

"How is old Corrigan?"

"Old. Crotchety. Convinced the job's gone to hell since they gave everyone computers." She pushed the door open. "He'll be delighted to see you, which he will express by insulting your shoes."

My shoes were, admittedly, a disgrace. Grief does that to a wardrobe eventually — you stop replacing things because replacing them implies a future, and somewhere along the way you wake up and everything you own has holes in it and six years have passed like water through a grate.

* * *

Dr. Edmund Corrigan had been the county coroner since before I could legally drink, and he had the particular dignity of a man who had spent forty years being the last person to speak for people who couldn't speak anymore. He stood over the covered gurney with his hands folded, and when he saw me his eyebrows performed their usual pantomime of disapproval.

"Kavanagh. They let anyone in here now?"

"They let you in," I said. "Standards clearly slipped decades ago."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — the closest thing to a smile the building allowed. Then it went away, and he pulled back the sheet, and I understood why.

I've seen drowned people before. The sea is not gentle with what it takes, and it is less gentle with what it gives back. But there was something about this woman — young, maybe thirty, dark hair still holding its shape, face composed in that way the dead sometimes manage, as if they'd made peace with the indignity at the last moment — that made the room feel colder than refrigeration could account for.

"Cause of death?" I asked.

"Drowning. As advertised." Corrigan's voice had gone flat and professional, which is how I knew he was bothered. "No signs of struggle. No defensive wounds. Petechial hemorrhaging consistent with saltwater aspiration. She went into the water alive, Kavanagh. That much I can tell you honestly."

"But?"

He looked at me for a long moment. "But nothing. Everything about this body says accident. Everything except—" He stopped himself.

"Except the clothes," I said.

* * *

They'd bagged them separately, of course — procedure is procedure even when the world stops making sense — but Corrigan laid them out on the steel table under the lamp, and I put on gloves, and I looked.

A dress. Navy blue, small white flowers, the kind of thing sold in coastal towns to women who want to look like they belong to the postcard. A cardigan, oatmeal-colored, pilled at the cuffs. A scarf. Flat shoes, scuffed at the toes.

And I knew them.

Not the way you know a photograph. Not the way you know a description in a file. I knew them the way you know a voice on the telephone after years of silence — instantly, horribly, with your whole body at once.

Because I had seen that dress before. Six years ago, in a witness room at the Harrow Point inquiry, on a woman named Maren Voss, who wore it the day she testified about what she'd seen at the quarry, and who wore it again — or one exactly like it, God help me, I'd need to check, I would spend the rest of this case checking — in the last photograph ever taken of her, three weeks before the sea supposedly took her too.

"Maren Voss," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. Inside, something had begun to tilt, slowly, like a table losing a leg.

"The drifter?" Corrigan frowned. "No. We identified her yesterday. No ID on the body, but her prints came back overnight. Name of—"

"I know what the file says." I lifted the dress carefully, turned it toward the light. "Look at the seams, Edmund. Look at the hem."

He leaned in. He was a good coroner, which meant he noticed things, which meant within seconds his frown changed shape — from confusion to recognition to something like dread.

"The repairs," he said quietly.

"The repairs." There were three of them, small and neat, done by hand with thread slightly wrong in color — mended by someone who cared about the garment but not enough to match the cotton. I had seen those repairs six years ago. I had stared at them across an interview table while Maren Voss smoothed the hem nervously and told me she'd learned to sew from her grandmother, and I had thought, at the time, *there's a woman who keeps things going long past the point where keeping them going makes sense.*

"This isn't a costume," I heard myself say. "This isn't someone dressing a body for effect. Costumes are new, or they're theatrical — clean, pressed, chosen. This dress was *worn*. It was worn for years. It was washed until the flowers faded. It was mended three times by the same hand. Someone owned this life, Edmund. Someone lived in these clothes."

"Or kept them," Corrigan said. "Six years is a long time to keep a dead woman's dress."

"Yes," I said. "It is."

And there it was — the sentence underneath all the others, the one I'd been circling since yesterday morning when Detective Inspector Naylor's card lay on my doormat with its tidy little word *accidental* printed on it. If the dress was real, then either someone had kept Maren Voss's clothing for six years and dressed a stranger in it — which required intimacy, patience, and a reason — or the dress had never stopped being worn at all.

Neither possibility was comfortable. One suggested a killer with a long memory. The other suggested a ghost with a washing machine.

* * *

Corrigan poured coffee from a machine that predated both of us, and we drank it standing up, because neither of us wanted to sit down and make this official.

"Tell me about the woman," I said. "The actual woman. The drifter."

"Jane Doe became Sarah Ellery, thirty-one, no fixed address. Known to services in two counties. Petty stuff — shoplifting, mostly food. A caseworker described her as 'vulnerable but pleasant.'" He said it without inflection, but his hand tightened around the paper cup. "She'd been in town maybe a week. Seen at the shelter on Marsh Lane twice. Nobody knew where she came from or where she was going."

"Nobody's looking for her."

"Not yet. There'll be a notice. There always is." He set the cup down. "You're asking whether anyone will miss her enough to ask hard questions."

"I'm asking whether anyone chose her *because* nobody would."

Corrigan didn't answer right away. He looked at the body under the sheet, and I watched him weigh something — the truth against the trouble the truth would cause him.

"Felix." He used my first name, which he did perhaps once a year, and always as a warning. "I've known you a long time. I signed off on the Alder Street findings when every other pathologist in the county would have called it homicide, because you asked me to look closer and I did, and you were right, and it cost us both something. So hear me now, as a friend: walk away from this one."

"Why?"

"Because the file is clean. Because the prints match a real person with a real sad little history. Because the tide brought her in and the tide will take the questions out, and if you start pulling threads on a drowning that looks like a drowning, in *this* town, with *this* council and *these* benefactors—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because the last man who asked questions about Harrow Point ended up retiring early with a ruined name, and the woman whose clothes you're holding ended up in the sea. And you're not a policeman anymore, Felix. You're a bookseller with bad shoes and a dead wife and no standing whatsoever. Whatever this is, it will eat you."

I looked down at the dress in my hands. Under the lamp, the faded flowers looked almost like foam.

"Edmund," I said, "six years ago I sat across from Maren Voss and I believed her, and then she vanished, and the inquiry collapsed, and everyone decided she'd been unstable, unreliable, possibly complicit — and I let them decide it, because deciding it was easier than fighting. Her testimony ruined Crane's first application and my career died in the same fire, and I told myself for six years that those two facts were unrelated." I folded the dress with more care than it needed. "Now she's washed up on my doorstep wearing her own clothes, and you're asking me to walk away."

"I'm telling you what walking away costs. Not nothing. But less than this."

"Maybe." I handed the dress back. "But here's what I know about tides, Edmund. Everyone talks about what they take. Nobody talks about how they work — how they come back. Twice a day, every day, patient as arithmetic. Whatever went out with the last one comes looking for the shore again."

He studied me with those tired coroner's eyes that had seen every way a human being can stop.

"You're going to do this regardless," he said.

"I'm afraid I am."

"Then do it quietly. And Felix — the clothes. When you're done with whatever you're going to do, bring me the answer. Not the theory. The answer." He held out his hand, and we shook, and it felt like 1998 and the Alder Street basement all over again, two men agreeing to be troubled together.

"One more thing," he added, as I reached the door. "The shoes. Buy some new ones. If you're going to get yourself killed, at least die presentable."

* * *

I walked home the long way, along the seawall, because the mortuary had given me a fact and I needed air to think inside it.

Here is what I had, laid out as plainly as I could manage:

A woman was dead. Drowned, genuinely drowned — Corrigan doesn't lie about petechiae. A drifter named Sarah Ellery whom nobody would miss, found wearing the clothes of another woman who had been dead for six years, officially, unquestionably, with a certificate and a grave-less grave and a town's relieved consensus behind her.

And the clothes were real. Worn. Mended. *Kept.*

I kept turning it over like a stone, looking for the soft underside. If Ferrar — no, I didn't have a Ferrar yet; I didn't have any names, just a shape moving behind the curtain — if whoever did this wanted to stage Maren Voss's death, why now? Her death was already certified. The paperwork was settled, the town had grieved, the development had gone through. Staging a death that already happened was like robbing a house that had already been emptied.

Unless the point wasn't to create the death. Unless the point was to *renew* it. To refresh the town's memory, to put the image of poor drowned Maren Voss back in front of every eye in Greyhaven, so that anyone who might someday whisper *she's still alive, I saw her* would find the whisper already answered. *Dead six years, dear. Body washed up just recently, tragic, the same clothes and everything.*

Someone was maintaining a lie the way you maintain a seawall. Not building it. Keeping it repaired against the weather.

Which raised the question that made my hands cold despite the morning sun: maintained against what? Lies don't need upkeep unless something threatens them. And the only thing that could threaten the lie of Maren Voss's death was—

Maren Voss.

Alive. Somewhere. Watching the same news report I'd watched, seeing her own death performed again in borrowed skin, knowing that someone out there knew she lived and was spending bodies to keep the secret.

Or — the other branch, the darker one — dead after all, truly dead, and someone dressing corpses in her wardrobe for reasons I couldn't yet see.

I stopped at the seawall's end, where the rail was rusted through, and looked out at the water. It was doing its innocent act — glittering, mild, slapping the stones like a dog wanting attention. Somewhere under all that sparkle was a quarry, flooded forty years ago, that the town preferred not to discuss. Somewhere along that shore was a coat on rocks that had once been enough to close a file.

I thought about the mended hem. Three repairs, same hand, thread slightly wrong. Somebody had kept that dress going long past the point where keeping it going made sense.

I'd met a woman like that once.

I turned back toward town, and my feet, without consulting me, started toward Gull Row — toward the archive, toward the reclusive keeper of the town's paper memory, whose hands I'd glimpsed once at a council meeting and noticed, idly, were bleached the way hands get when they spend years near salt water.

Not suspicion. Not yet. Just a thread, slightly wrong in color, that I needed to pull.

The tide was going out. It would be back by evening.

So would I.
