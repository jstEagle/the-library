## 5. The Archivist of Gull Row

The municipal archive of Port Cael lived on Gull Row in a building that had clearly lost an argument with the sea and was pretending otherwise. Salt bloom climbed the lower courses of its stone like frost that never melted, and the front steps had been patched so many times they looked like a quilt stitched by someone who hated quilting. A brass plaque read *HARROW POINT HISTORICAL COLLECTIONS — EST. 1974*, which meant the archive was younger than the conspiracy I was hunting, though the paper inside it went back two centuries. Towns like this one keep their sins filed alphabetically.

I'd spent three days chasing deeds. The Harrow Point development — Crane's cathedral of condominiums and marina slips, the project that had made a dozen men rich and put four bodies in the water — had required land. Land requires titles. Titles require paperwork, and paperwork, unlike people, can be made to confess if you ask it enough times in the right order.

The police had asked none of those questions six years ago. The police had been busy not finding Maren Voss's body.

I pushed open the door and stepped into air that smelled of dust, old glue, and something faintly mineral — the smell of a building that breathed through stone. Inside, the archive was one long room under a low ceiling, shelves running floor to rafters, lit by green-shaded lamps that gave everything the complexion of a drowned man. There was a reading table with four chairs. Three were empty. At the fourth sat a woman with gray-streaked hair pinned up carelessly, reading a ledger by lamplight as though the rest of the world were a rumor she hadn't bothered to verify.

She didn't look up when the door closed. She didn't look up when my boots crossed the floorboards. It wasn't rudeness; it was the practiced deafness of someone who has decided that most visitors are weather.

"Ms. Brandt?" I said.

Odile Brandt raised her eyes. They were pale gray, the color of the harbor in February, and they took me in with a single unhurried pass — coat, hands, shoes, face — the way a customs officer reads a passport. Whatever verdict she reached, she kept to herself.

"The collection is open Tuesdays through Fridays," she said. "Nine to four. We close for lunch."

"It's ten past nine."

"Then you have nearly seven hours." She returned to her ledger.

"I'm looking for land records. Parcels along the north shore, 1998 through 2004."

"Deed books are on the east wall. Chronological. There's a magnifier in the drawer if your eyes are as old as your coat."

I liked her immediately, which annoyed me, because I hadn't come here to like anyone. I came here because of a pattern, and patterns don't care whether their components are pleasant.

* * *

Here is what I knew by then, laid out the way I lay out evidence at home, on the kitchen table, weighted down with teacups:

Three deaths, all ruled accidental, all quietly solved by me over the past five years because nobody else would do it. Tomas Reeve, the harbormaster, found floating off his own skiff with a head wound the coroner called "consistent with a fall." Petra Lindqvist, the surveyor, dead of a gas leak in a house where the valve had been turned with a wrench. And Danny Ostrander, the young clerk at the registry office, who drove his car into the flooded quarry on a night with no rain and no reason.

All three had touched the Harrow Point deal. Reeve had logged boat movements the night of the first murder. Lindqvist had surveyed parcels whose boundaries didn't match what Crane's filings claimed. Ostrander had processed documents — I still didn't know which ones — before he died with his lungs full of quarry water.

And now a fourth woman, nameless, drowned, wearing Maren Voss's clothes.

Maren Voss. The name still had weight in this town, the way a stone has weight after you've thrown it into deep water — invisible, but you feel the line go taut whenever you think about it. She'd been the key witness against Aldous Crane back then, prepared to testify about what she'd seen at the north shore property. Then she'd walked into the sea, or so the town agreed to believe. Her coat on the rocks. No body. A death certificate signed by Dr. Emory Hale, conveniently deceased himself these four years. My testimony in an unrelated matter had been shredded by Crane's lawyers shortly afterward, my reputation used as sandbagging around their client's foundations. I'd retired from the force at fifty-one with a bookshop and a bitterness I kept in a drawer next to the batteries.

Four deaths, one hand. And every thread ran through paper — filings, surveys, registries. Paper lives somewhere. In Port Cael, paper lived here, on Gull Row, under the lamp of Odile Brandt.

So I pulled deed books for three hours and watched her sideways, the way you watch a tide pool: patiently, without disturbing the surface.

* * *

What I noticed first were her hands.

She wore fingerless wool gloves indoors, which I might have attributed to the building's damp chill except that when she removed them to turn pages — carefully, always carefully, lifting each leaf by its corner like a nurse handling a burn victim — I saw the skin beneath. It was bleached. Not aged; bleached. The creases of her knuckles were white in a way that sun doesn't do and saltwater does, that long immersion does, that years of cold water leave behind like a signature. Divers' hands. Cold-water swimmers' hands. The hands of someone who had spent more time in the sea than any archivist reasonably should.

I thought of a coat on wet rocks. I thought of a death certificate signed by a man now beyond questioning.

I also noticed, within the first hour, that she checked the tide tables.

Not glanced — checked. There was a copy of the annual tide almanac on the shelf behind her desk, spine cracked white with use, and twice in my three hours she rose, crossed to it, and consulted it with the particular attention of someone confirming an appointment rather than satisfying curiosity. The second time, she wrote something small in a pocket notebook. Tide times change by roughly fifty minutes a day. Anyone who tracks them daily is either a fisherman, a smuggler, or a swimmer.

Or someone who needed to know exactly when the water at the flooded quarry would be low enough to walk its bed.

I turned another page of deed book 41 and felt the old machinery start up inside me, gears I'd sworn I'd sold for scrap. *Careful,* I told myself. *You've been wrong before. You were wrong about Maren Voss once already — wrong about what her testimony would cost you, wrong about everything since.* But being wrong had never once stopped me from looking.

* * *

At noon she closed the reading room. "Lunch," she announced, to me and to the shelves collectively. "You can stay if you don't touch anything. The building trusts me, not you."

I stayed. Of course I stayed. Through the doorway to her back room I could see a kettle, a single chair, a wall of card catalogs, and — this interested me — a corkboard with a map of the coastline pinned to it, marked with small colored pins clustered suspiciously around the north shore. Around Harrow Point. Around the quarry.

When she came back with tea — two cups, one set down in front of me without comment, which I chose to interpret as surrender rather than hospitality — I opened the conversation I'd been building toward all morning, the way you approach a dog you suspect bites.

"Odd question," I said. "Does the archive hold anything on Maren Voss?"

The flinch was small. Most people would have missed it. Her shoulders drew up maybe half an inch, her eyes went to the middle distance for exactly one second, and her teacup met its saucer with a click slightly too precise to be casual. If I'd had a camera rolling at thirty frames per second, I could have shown you the whole confession in slow motion.

"Why would you ask that?" Her voice was level. Level voices take work.

"She was in the papers again last week. The drowned drifter wearing her clothes. People talk."

"People always talk. It's why I prefer paper." She straightened a stack of index cards that did not need straightening. "There may be clippings. Newspaper collections are in the west alcove, by decade. You're welcome to look."

"That's not quite what I asked."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

We regarded each other across the reading table, two people conducting a negotiation in which neither had yet named the currency. Her gray eyes held steady, but there was something underneath the steadiness — not guilt, exactly. Fear, maybe. Or its cousin, vigilance. I know the difference because I've worn both expressions myself, in mirrors, for years.

"You knew her?" I asked, softer.

"I knew everyone in this town's records. That's not the same as knowing them." She gathered her gloves from the desk, began pulling them back on, finger by finger. "Mr.—"

"Kavanagh. Felix Kavanagh."

Something moved behind her eyes at the name. Recognition, yes — my face had been in the local paper often enough, mostly unflatteringly — but something else too. Something that looked almost like grief, arriving late to its own appointment.

"Yes," she said quietly. "I know who you are."

"And what is it you think I am?"

"A man who digs where digging hurts people." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "You solved those deaths — the harbormaster, the surveyor, the boy from the registry. Don't look surprised; I file the newspapers, Mr. Kavanagh, I don't merely dust them. You're very good at finding out how people died." She tied off the second glove. "You've never once seemed interested in how they lived."

The words landed harder than she could have known — or perhaps she knew exactly. Because that was the wound under my whole retirement, wasn't it? Five years of corpses and case files, a shop full of other people's mysteries bought and sold by the yard, and a house with one chair at the table. I'd gotten very good at the dead. The living had stopped returning my calls sometime around my wife's funeral.

"That's not fair," I said, and heard how thin it sounded even as I said it.

"No," she admitted. "Probably not. Fairness is above my pay grade. I keep records. Records aren't fair either — they only remember what someone bothered to write down." She stood, and the interview was over; I could feel the door closing in her posture. "Deed books are due back by four."

* * *

I didn't leave at four. I left at half past, after walking the west alcove and pulling the newspaper volumes for the year Maren Voss vanished. The clippings were all there, of course — the search, the coat on the rocks, the inquest, Crane's statement expressing sorrow, the photograph of Maren herself, dark-haired, sharp-eyed, caught mid-sentence at some public hearing, mouth open on words the transcript never got to record.

Someone had gone through this volume before me. I could tell because the pages around the Voss coverage were subtly cleaner than their neighbors, handled more, and because one clipping — a small item noting that Dr. Emory Hale had certified the death — bore a faint pencil dot beside the date. Archivists mark things. It's a tic. Whoever had marked this one had marked it the way you'd mark a debt.

I copied the date into my notebook, closed the volume, and stood a moment in the green lamplight listening to the building settle. Somewhere below the floorboards, the sea was working at the foundation stones, patient as a lawyer.

* * *

That evening I did something I'm not proud of, in the sense that I'm not proud of it the way a man isn't proud of checking his ex-wife's porch light. I walked Gull Row after dark.

Not to break in. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them anymore. I walked the Row because the tide was going out and I wanted to see whether Odile Brandt watched it.

She did.

Her window — the back room, above the card catalogs — showed lamplight until nine, and at nine-fifteen a figure crossed it and paused, and the pause had a shape I recognized. The shape of a person consulting a clock against a horizon. When I shifted position to see past the chimney pot, I caught her silhouette at the glass, motionless, facing the water. Facing south. Facing, if my sense of the coastline held, the drowned quarry two miles off, where Danny Ostrander's car had gone into the black water and where, I was increasingly certain, other things had been put besides cars.

I stood in the cold until my feet numbed, feeling like the worst kind of hypocrite. Here was a woman keeping vigil over the sea, and instead of asking what she'd lost, I was cataloguing her like evidence. *Salt-bleached hands. Tide almanac. Pencil dot. Flinch.* Four entries in a file that was starting to write itself, and every entry pointed somewhere I didn't want it to point, because the destination was a conclusion I'd been circling for three days like a gull over a wake:

Either Odile Brandt knew who killed Maren Voss.

Or Odile Brandt knew something better. Something about whether Maren Voss had ever been killed at all.

* * *

The next morning I came back at opening time with a pretext — probate records, 2001, the estate of Dr. Emory Hale — and a second cup of coffee bought from the cart on the corner, which I set on her desk without ceremony.

She looked at the cup. Then at me. Then at the cup again, as though it might be wired.

"Hale's estate went through probate in 2001," I said. "His widow still lives on Chapel Street. I want whatever the archive holds on him — correspondence, certificates, anything with his signature."

"Probate records are county," she said. "Not municipal."

"Then consider this a request for interlibrary cooperation."

For a moment I thought she'd refuse. Her hand actually moved toward the return slot, toward the little brass bell she could ring to summon some municipal authority who would take one look at my face and show me the door. But then her eyes went — briefly, involuntarily — to the newspaper alcove, to the west wall, to the place where a pencil dot marked a date beside a dead doctor's name, and something in her changed. Not softened. *Repositioned.* The way a chess player repositions when she realizes the game has been running longer than she thought.

"Hale," she repeated. "Why him?"

"Because he signed a death certificate for a body nobody ever found. Because he's dead now, which is convenient for everyone involved. And because I've started counting conveniences, Ms. Brandt, and I've run out of fingers."

The silence stretched. Outside, a gull screamed at something the gull alone considered unjust. And Odile Brandt — keeper of the town's paper memory, guardian of its alphabetical sins — looked at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere, an expression that was half warning and half plea, as if she were standing on a shore watching me wade out and trying to decide whether to shout a warning or let the water do its work.

"Dr. Hale's materials," she said finally, "are restricted. Sealed by request of the family."

"His widow told you to seal them?"

"His widow paid me to seal them." She said it flatly, and then, seeing my face, added something quieter, almost to herself: "Everyone pays someone, Mr. Kavanagh. That's the whole history of this town. You just haven't found the receipts yet."

"But they exist," I said. "The receipts."

She didn't answer. She pulled on her gloves, finger by careful finger, and turned back to her ledger, and the interview was over — but as I gathered my coat, she spoke once more, without looking up, in a voice pitched so low I nearly missed it under the gulls.

"The tide comes in at 6:40 tomorrow. If you're going to be foolish, Mr. Kavanagh, be foolish on an outgoing tide. Things wash further when the water's leaving."

I stopped in the doorway, heart doing something complicated against my ribs.

"Is that advice," I asked, "or a warning?"

She turned a page. "It's a tide table," she said. "Interpretation is above my pay grade."

But her hands, when I glanced back from the threshold — those bleached, careful, sea-marked hands — had gone still on the ledger, and she was watching me leave with the look of a woman deciding, at last and against every instinct she owned, which side of the water she wanted to stand on.

I walked home the long way, along the seawall, and by the time I reached the shop I had made myself a promise: tomorrow I would knock on the widow Hale's door on Chapel Street and ask her what her husband had been paid to certify.

And if the answer was what I feared — if Maren Voss had never touched that water at all — then the woman on Gull Row hadn't been guarding a killer's secret.

She'd been guarding a ghost. And ghosts, in my experience, only hide from people who intend to bury them twice.
