## 9. Someone Is Still Writing Her

The trouble with dressing a dead woman is that the dead are uncooperative models. They don't hold their arms where you need them, they don't breathe to fill out a bodice, and they have opinions about temperature that they express by stiffening at exactly the wrong moment. I had learned this the hard way, six years ago, on a cold slab of rock below the Harrow Point cliffs, and I had hoped — professionally speaking — never to learn it again.

Hope, like everything else in this town, was a development project that never broke ground.

Her name, as far as anyone would ever officially know, was Petra Ilves, forty-one, of no fixed address, last seen drinking cheap white wine at the Anchor & Chain and asking the wrong man the right questions. She'd been asking questions about Harrow Point, which was how she came to me, though she didn't know it. A drifter with a notebook and a nose for land titles is not a threat. A drifter with a notebook who has found the name Voss in a six-year-old deed transfer is a problem, and problems, in my line of work, get solved before they get interesting.

I had watched her for nine days. That's not cruelty; that's arithmetic. Nine days to learn her habits, her route home, the stretch of seawall where the railings went low and the rocks went high. On the tenth night I bought her the wine myself. She thought I was being kind. People always think that, right up until the moment kindness shows its other face.

* * *

Let me tell you how it's done properly, since nobody else in this business seems to know.

You don't drown a woman and then dress her. The clothes tell the story backward: if she went into the water clothed, the clothes must show it — soaked before death, abraded in the same sequence as the body, salt-stained in the pattern the tide would make. So you dress her first. You walk her to the water dressed as the person she needs to be, and you let the sea do the costuming.

The dress was the difficult part. It hung in a garment bag in my flat for six years, next to a coat and a pair of shoes, all of them kept the way an accountant keeps receipts. Maren Voss's clothes. Not copies — copies are for amateurs and costume departments. The coroner's man had held the original dress in his gloved hands three weeks ago and pronounced it genuine, worn, lived-in, and he was right. It had been worn. By her. For years, before. And then by the first one, the girl from the hostel whose name I made a point of never learning, because names are how you start keeping ledgers you can't close.

Aldous Crane believes I keep the clothes out of sentiment. Aldous Crane believes a great many comforting things; it's what his money buys him. I keep them because a prop used twice must match itself across time, and because throwing away evidence is the one sin this trade doesn't forgive. Also — and I admit this with the mild embarrassment of a man confessing to liking opera — there is something in the work itself. A staging done well is a sentence written so cleanly that no editor will ever question it. The sea writes in erasures; I write in affirmations. Between us we produce a text the town reads every morning over its eggs.

Petra Ilves weighed perhaps eight stone wet, and after Tuesday night she was very wet indeed. I had walked her along the low railing with my hand at her elbow like a gentleman seeing a lady home, and the conversation had been pleasant enough — she'd told me about Tallinn, about a brother, about how she'd come here because the coast reminded her of home except warmer. I let her talk. It's a courtesy. They're going to stop talking forever; the least you can do is listen while they still can.

Then the railing, then the rocks, then the particular silence that follows.

I won't describe the rest. Not because it haunts me — nothing haunts me, that's rather the point of me — but because descriptions are how amateurs get caught. You leave details in your head like fingerprints on glass. I do the work, I check the work, and I put the work away. There is a version of Nils Ferrar that goes home, showers until the water runs cold, and sleeps eight hours. He is the only honest version, and he clocks out at the waterline.

* * *

Here is what most people misunderstand about killing to keep a lie alive: the murder is the easy part. Anyone angry enough or frightened enough can end a life. What takes craft is the paperwork of death — making the town agree, making the record agree, making the future agree. A corpse is an argument. You have to win it.

Six years ago, the argument was simple. Maren Voss, key witness against Aldous Crane, walks into the sea off Gull Row. Coat found on the rocks. Body never recovered — inconvenient, but the doctor handled it, and the doctor was paid in the currency doctors understand, which is not money but relief. Death certificate signed. Case closed with a ribbon on it. The town got its grief, Crane got his development, and I got a retainer that has kept me in good tailoring ever since.

But a body never recovered is a door left open. And three weeks ago, someone walked through it.

Not the police. The police read the file and saw what the file said, which is the whole art of writing files. No — it was Kavanagh. Felix Kavanagh, retired, running his sad little bookshop on Cordwainer Lane like a man tending a grave he dug himself. I had catalogued him years ago: competent once, broken by the Voss case, defanged. A man who solves cold puzzles in private because the solving is the only warmth he has left. Harmless, I wrote in my own ledger. Harmless men are the ones you re-catalogue annually.

He held the dead girl's dress at the mortuary — I know because Coroner Ashe told me, and Ashe tells me things the way a dog brings sticks to the man who feeds it — and something in his face changed. Ashe described the change reluctantly, the way you describe weather you don't understand. Kavanagh had looked at that dress the way a man looks at a word misspelled in a document he's read a thousand times.

So Crane called me on Thursday, and his voice had the particular brightness it gets when vanity and fear shake hands. "Nils. It's happening again. Someone's putting her back in the water."

"No," I said. "Someone took her out. We're the ones putting bodies in."

Silence down the line. Crane does not enjoy precision; precision implies a past tense. "You know what I mean. She's surfacing. The dress, the — whatever Kavanagh's doing. Fix it the way you fixed it before."

"The way I fixed it before," I said, "was a single performance. One body, one certificate, one closed door. You're asking for a second show of the same production, and second shows invite comparisons." I let him sit in that. "But yes. I'll fix it."

Because here is the thing I did not say to Aldous Crane, the thing I keep in the locked drawer of myself alongside the garment bag: I have known where Maren Voss is for six years. Fourteen months into my employment, following a thread of stationery purchases and a librarian's pension application, I found her. Odile Brandt, archivist, Gull Row, salt-bleached hands, eyes that flinch at her own name. I stood outside the archive for one hour on a rainy afternoon and watched her through the glass, cataloguing the town's paper dead, and I understood two things at once.

First: she was no threat. She had built herself a tomb of card catalogs and was living in it voluntarily. Killing her would be the one act in this entire affair that served no purpose whatsoever.

Second: I could sell her. Any day I chose. To Crane, to whoever succeeded Crane, to the highest bidder in a town full of men who buy silence the way other men buy wine.

I did not sell her. Call it professionalism — a solved problem left solved is worth more than a new problem profitably created. Call it something else if you like; I've stopped auditing that drawer. But I took her existence off the market, and I told no one, and for six years the arrangement held: she stayed buried in plain sight, I stayed paid, and the sea kept its mouth shut.

Until Kavanagh started pulling threads, and Crane started panicking, and now the mathematics had changed. Because a panicked Crane might hire someone else to find her — someone less tidy than me — and an untidy man finding Odile Brandt means an untidy death, and an untidy death means questions, and questions mean the whole architecture comes down on both our heads.

No. If the secret had to surface, it would surface on my terms, at my speed, in my handwriting. Which meant, unpleasantly, that Felix Kavanagh had to be managed. And management, in my experience, begins with understanding what a man wants. Kavanagh wanted the truth. Fine. I would give him a truth — just not the true one. A man chasing a staged drowning needs to catch a staged drowning. Give him his villain-shaped hole and he'll stop digging toward the foundation.

* * *

Wednesday morning, before the fishing boats were back, I arranged Petra Ilves where the tide would find her: on her side, half in the kelp line below the old quarry path, arm extended toward the water like a woman reaching for something she'd dropped. The dress — Maren's dress, the real one, salt-stiffened now with two deaths in its seams — I smoothed down over her hips and let the foam worry the hem. Detail work. The reaching arm was the masterstroke; a body that washed ashore is passive, but a body that reached is a story, and stories are what coroners' juries actually weigh.

One error to avoid, and I avoided it: no second coat. The first time, the coat on the rocks was the whole exhibit — the body optional, the coat sufficient. This time the body was the exhibit, and adding a coat would be gilding. Repetition with variation, but variation in the direction of simplicity. Amateurs escalate. Professionals edit.

I checked the tide tables — I always check the tide tables; there is a copy in my desk drawer and, I suspected, one in hers, and isn't that a small comedy, the killer and the ghost consulting the same moon — and calculated she'd be found by the dog-walkers by seven, reported by seven-forty, and in front of Coroner Ashe by noon. Ashe would call me, because Ashe calls me, because six years ago I helped him through a season of his life he doesn't discuss, and gratitude is the strongest adhesive I know.

Then I did the last thing, the thing that mattered most. I drove to Cordwainer Lane and sat in my car across from the bookshop with a thermos of coffee, waiting for the lights, and when Felix Kavanagh appeared in his doorway with his cup and his gray face and looked down the street at nothing, I got out and crossed to him like a neighbor.

"Mr. Kavanagh." I offered my hand. "Nils Ferrar. I handle some of Mr. Crane's charitable affairs. I understand you attended the benefit."

"I remember you," he said. His handshake was dry and unhurried. "You watch rooms."

"It's a habit. Look — I'll be direct, because I'm told you appreciate that. There's going to be another drowning. Another woman, wearing — well. Wearing clothing that will interest you." I watched his eyes. They didn't widen; they narrowed, which was worse. "Mr. Crane didn't order it. I want that understood. But it's going to happen, and when it does, the town is going to say what the town said six years ago, and I thought — a man who reads as much as you do might want to be ahead of the story."

"Why?" he said. Just that. Why.

"Because I'm tired," I said, and the remarkable thing is that it wasn't even a lie. Tiredness is the most convincing mask there is; nobody checks behind it. "Because I've spent six years holding a lid on a pot, and the pot's boiling, and I'd rather it boiled in a direction I chose."

He studied me for a long moment, this ruined detective with his careful eyes, and I felt the thing I always feel around him, the thing I'd noted in my ledger and underlined: he wasn't harmless. He had simply been waiting. Men like Kavanagh don't break; they go dormant.

"You're offering to help me," he said.

"I'm offering to stand near you while you help yourself. There's a difference, and it matters legally."

And he almost smiled. Almost. "All right, Mr. Ferrar. Stand near."

I walked back to my car feeling the specific satisfaction of a stagehand who has moved the scenery without the audience noticing. He would go to the body. He would see the dress. He would conclude — because I would leave him just enough breadcrumbs — that Crane was staging deaths to reinforce the original lie, that the killer was sloppy or arrogant, that the trail led upward to the great philanthropist himself. All true, as far as it went. Truth is the best material for lies; it's already load-bearing.

What he would not conclude — not yet, not until I chose — was that the woman he was really hunting had been twenty feet from him in the archive on Gull Row, and that the man standing beside him offering directions was the only soul in the county who knew.

By noon the dog-walkers had found her. By one o'clock the sirens were singing down the coast road, and the town gathered at the police tape the way it gathers for everything — hungry, solemn, grateful for a grief it could share without cost. I watched from the sea wall, coffee in hand, tailoring immaculate, and I gave the scene one final editorial pass.

The dress was right. The reach was right. The tide was behaving.

Somewhere behind me, I knew, a bell above a bookshop door was ringing, and a tired man was setting down his cup and reaching for his coat.

Good, I thought. Write your chapter, Kavanagh. Every book needs a detective.

Just remember that I'm the one holding the pen.
