## 10. The Bookshop Burns Slowly

The fire started, according to the fire marshal's report, at approximately 2:47 in the morning, in the wiring behind the poetry section. I have always suspected that poetry would be the death of me, but I expected it to be slower and more dignified.

I got the call at three-twenty from a night porter named Wexler who runs the chandlery two doors down and has never once bought a book from me. He said, "Kavanagh, your shop's on fire," with the flat delivery of a man reporting weather, and then he hung up before I could ask anything useful, which is the way of all informants everywhere.

By the time I arrived the street was full of orange light and men in yellow coats doing their work with the unhurried competence of people who have done it a hundred times and will do it a hundred more. The smell hit me first — wet char, kerosene — no. Not yet. At three-twenty in the morning, standing on the cobbles in my coat over my pajamas like some Dickensian failure, all I knew was that half my life was burning behind a plate-glass window, and I stood there and watched it with my hands in my pockets because there was nothing else for them to do.

You'd think a man watching his bookshop burn would feel grief. What I felt, mostly, was embarrassment. It's a small shop. It has never made money. My wife used to say I ran it the way other men keep aquariums — not for profit but to have something alive to look after. And now the thing was dying in front of an audience, and I was standing in my slippers.

The firemen let it burn slow. That's what they told me later, anyway: it burned *slow*, which is a strange thing to say about a fire, as though the fire had manners, had patience, had all night and intended to use it. Electrical faults are quick things. They flash and they're done. This one sat down to dinner.

I didn't know that yet. At three-forty I only knew that the window over the poetry section — the good window, the one with the leaded panes that came with the building — went out in a soft collapse, like a sigh, and the light inside turned the color of the inside of a shell, and forty years of other people's words went up in a column of sparks that rose straight into the gray air over Harrow Point and scattered.

A young constable tried to move me back behind the barrier. I showed him my old warrant card out of habit, and he looked at it, and looked at me, and decided I was harmless, which is the most accurate assessment anyone has made of me in six years.

"Electrical," he said, by way of comfort. "Happens in these old buildings."

"Mm," I said.

It happens in these old buildings. Of course it does. Old buildings, old men, old cases. Everything in this town eventually gets explained by its age.

* * *

They let me go in at nine, when the smoke had thinned enough that the insurance assessor would stop complaining about liability. The water stood ankle-deep on the floorboards, black and faintly iridescent, carrying a scum of ash and the swollen ghosts of paperbacks. Half the shop was gone — the east wall, the poetry, the history, the little reading nook where Mrs. Aldous used to sit every Thursday and read romances she never bought. The other half was soaked and steaming gently, like something that had been crying.

I walked through it in borrowed wellingtons, cataloguing losses the way I once catalogued evidence. The first-edition Tennyson my wife gave me the year we married: gone. The photograph of her behind the till: warped but recognizable, her face blistered amber by heat, still smiling at whatever she'd been smiling at in 1998. I set it upright on the surviving shelf so it could watch me work. She'd have wanted to see this part. Elena never trusted luck, and she'd have taken one look at this fire and said, *Felix, darling, nobody's wiring does that.*

She was right, of course. She usually was, which is one of the many things I failed to appreciate while she was alive to be right at me.

The fire marshal found me around noon, picking through the poetry section's remains with a garden trowel. He was a broad, tired man named Okafor with soot in the creases of his neck and the expression of someone who has heard every possible reaction to losing a building and ranks them privately.

"Mr. Kavanagh." He crouched beside me. "You want to hear the preliminary?"

"I want you to say electrical fault so the insurance pays out, and then I want you to tell me what actually happened."

He almost smiled. "Point of origin's behind the shelving, second bay. Wiring in this building's cloth-insulated, pre-war, and honestly it's a wonder the whole street hasn't gone up. So on paper? Electrical fault. Clean report, everyone goes home."

"But."

"But." He glanced toward the door, then lowered his voice as though the ash itself might testify against him. "Cloth insulation doesn't smolder for forty minutes before it flames. This burned *slow*. Started low, crept along the skirting, took its time finding the shelf stock. Somebody fed it. We pulled residue from three points along the baseboard. Accelerant."

"Kerosene."

His eyebrows came up. "Lab'll confirm, but yes. How'd you know?"

Because I'd smelled it at three-thirty in the morning and told myself it was imagination, or the harbor, or grief playing tricks. "Lucky guess," I said. "Occupational habit."

"Retired detective," Okafor said. "Wexler mentioned." He stood, knees cracking. "Look — officially, this is electrical. Unofficially, if I were you, I'd think hard about who wants you reading by candlelight somewhere else."

"And unofficially, do you investigate arson?"

"Unofficially, we investigate everything. Officially, we need more than a smell." He put a card in my hand. "Call me if you find anything. Anything at all. Don't touch it, don't clean it, don't be clever. Just call."

I promised him all three things and meant none of them, which is also occupational.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing what the fire hadn't finished: going through the wreckage systematically, shelf by shelf, because grief is bearable in alphabetical order. Around four o'clock, working the far corner near the old coal chute — the one dry pocket in the whole shop, shielded by the chimney breast — I found the tide chart.

It was folded in quarters and tucked behind a loose brick in the chimney breast, which is a hiding place I did not know existed in my own building, which means someone else did. Someone had been in my shop, quietly, recently, and left something for me to find. Or lost something. With Ferrar's kind, those are often the same act.

I unfolded it on the counter, next to Elena's photograph, and smoothed it flat with both hands.

An admiralty chart of the coast, printed years ago, the paper gone soft and foxed with damp. Handwriting across it in blue ballpoint — not mine, not Maren's; a blunt, impatient script, all downstrokes. Depth soundings circled. A date from last month. And at the flooded quarry, two miles inland from Harrow Point, a mark: not an X, nothing so honest. A small square, drawn with a ruler, precise as a coffin lid.

Beside it, in the same blunt hand, a single word: *pump-house.*

I stood very still in the ruined shop, listening to the drip of water off the scorched rafters, and felt the thing in my chest that had been grief all day change its chemical composition into something with considerably more utility.

Fury is underrated, as motivations go. People talk about it like it's a loss of control, but done properly it's the opposite — it's clarity with a temperature. All morning I had been a victim, an insured party, a man in borrowed boots. Now I was a man holding a piece of paper that said somebody had marked my death on a map and filed it under infrastructure.

Because that's what this was. The fire wasn't punishment. Punishment is personal, and Nils Ferrar didn't do personal. The fire was *maintenance*. I had gone to the mortuary, and to the archive, and to Crane's charity night, and asked questions in my wry, harmless, retired-detective voice, and somewhere in the last week a man in a good coat had decided that Felix Kavanagh had become a line item requiring attention. The shop wasn't a message. Messages are for people you're negotiating with. The shop was simply the removal of a base of operations — and, if the old man happened to be asleep upstairs, a tidy solution to a secondary problem.

I sleep upstairs. They'd have known that too.

I looked at Elena's photograph. "Nobody's wiring does that," I told her, and her blistered smile agreed with me the way she always had — instantly, and slightly ahead of me.

* * *

Okafor came back at dusk, as promised, and I gave him the chart, because I am capable of following instructions when the instructions happen to align with spite. He held it by the edges like a relic and read the word *pump-house* twice.

"You know whose writing this is?"

"No. But I know whose errand it was." I told him about Crane, about Ferrar, about the drowned drifter wearing a dead woman's dress, and I watched him decide how much of it he believed. He believed roughly sixty percent, which for a serving officer presented with a conspiracy theory by a civilian is practically a religious conversion.

"This is evidence," he said. "Chain of custody starts now. You touched it?"

"Gloves were in the wreckage. I used a trowel and a plastic sleeve from the till."

"The till survived?"

"The till is indestructible. It's the most valuable thing I own."

He almost laughed, then remembered the shop around us and stopped himself, which I appreciated. Grief has rules; laughing in it feels like trespass even when the grief is mostly fury wearing a mourning coat.

"Kerosene, a planted chart, a fire that burned polite," Okafor said, folding the chart into an evidence bag. "This'll get me a task force request. It might get me laughed out of the room. You understand that."

"I've been laughed out of better rooms than yours."

"Yeah. Wexler says you used to be famous." He zipped the bag. "One more thing. Off the record. Whoever wrote on this chart — they wrote *pump-house*. Singular. Like they already know exactly which building they mean. That's not reconnaissance, Mr. Kavanagh. That's a man writing down something he's known for years."

After he left I sat on the counter in the darkening shop, feet dangling like a boy's, and thought about that. Known for years. Marked on a chart kept behind a brick in *my* chimney. Which meant either Ferrar had been careless — and Ferrar did not strike me as a careless man — or the chart had never been Ferrar's at all.

Someone else had hidden it. Someone who knew my shop, knew my habits, knew I'd tear the place apart shelf by shelf looking for what the fire had taken. Someone salt-bleached of hand, perhaps. Someone who watched tide tables the way other people watch the news.

Or someone who wanted me led to the pump-house by the nose, straight into whatever waits there, and was clever enough to know I'd trust a clue I found myself over any clue I was given.

Both possibilities ended at the quarry. That, I suspected, was the point. In my experience, when every road leads to the same address, it's less often fate than architecture — someone built the roads that way on purpose.

I locked up what remained of the shop — a formality, since what remained was mostly wall and memory — and walked home through streets smelling of wet ash, past the harbor, where the evening tide was coming in over the flats with that soft, patient, businesslike sound it makes, the sound of something that keeps excellent records and never loses a file.

Tomorrow I'd buy a torch, a rope, and a set of waterproofs. Tomorrow I'd learn what a pump-house is for.

Tonight I let myself be angry, properly, all the way down — for the Tennyson, for the photograph's amber blisters, for Mrs. Aldous's Thursdays, for the drifter in Maren Voss's dress, for six years of accidents with one handwriting on them. Anger keeps a man warm on the walk home. And tomorrow, at low water, I intended to go and see what the tide had been keeping for me at the bottom of that quarry — and whether the person who drew that little ruled square wanted me to find it, or wanted the quarry to keep me.
