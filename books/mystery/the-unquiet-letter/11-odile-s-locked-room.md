## 11. Odile's Locked Room

The trouble with living as someone else for six years is that you forget which face goes with which name. I had been Odile Brandt so long that when Felix Kavanagh pushed open the door of the Gull Row archive that Tuesday morning, my first instinct was to greet him with Odile's voice — dry, unhurried, a woman whose worst problem in life was damp in the basement stacks. It took real discipline to remember that underneath the cardigan and the reading glasses and the careful stoop I'd taught myself, there was another woman entirely, one who had drowned on paper and had every intention of staying drowned.

"Miss Brandt." He stood in the doorway with rain on his shoulders and something folded in his hand, and he did not smile. Felix Kavanagh was not a man who smiled his way into rooms. He entered them the way weather does.

"We open at ten," I said. "It's nine-forty."

"I'll wait."

"You'll get wetter standing there."

He came in anyway, dripping politely onto the coir mat I kept for exactly this purpose, and looked around at my kingdom: the shelves of ledgers going back to 1911, the map drawers, the smell of cold paper and camphor that I had come to love more than I loved most people. Six years of cataloguing other people's history while burying my own. There is an irony in that which I decline to find funny.

"You've reorganized," he said.

"The maritime section. It was a disgrace."

"It was fine last spring."

"Last spring I had lower standards." I took off my glasses, polished them on my sleeve, put them back on — a little theater of composure, a woman with nothing to hide except everything. "Was there something you wanted, Mr. Kavanagh? The council minutes are on the shelf where they always are. The parish registers are indexed by surname. If you've come about the Hollis estate again, the answer is still no, the deed was never amended, and your client should stop asking."

"It's not about the Hollis estate."

"No?"

He unfolded what he'd been carrying. A tide chart. Not even a good one — a commercial thing from the chandlery, the kind tourists buy and then lose. But across it, in grease pencil, someone had marked the flooded quarry at Harrow Point, and beside the mark, a date. Last Thursday. The day before half his bookshop burned down.

I looked at it for perhaps two seconds too long. I know how long two seconds is; I have spent six years measuring them.

"That's not mine," I said.

"I didn't say it was."

"You're holding it at me like a summons."

"I'm holding it at you like a question." He set it down on the counter between us, gently, the way you set down something that might go off. "The quarry hasn't been swum in thirty years. The water's black past four meters. Whoever marked this knew the tide tables matter there — and they do, Miss Brandt, more than anywhere else on this coast, because the quarry fills and drains through a fissure nobody bothered to map. You check tide tables. I've watched you do it. Every morning, first thing, before you open the post."

"Do I?" I said, and heard my own voice go pleasant and stupid, the voice of a woman discussing the weather. "Habit. Bad knees. I like to know if I can walk the shingle."

"In October."

"The shingle doesn't care what month it is."

We stood there in the quiet of the archive, rain ticking against the high windows, and I understood with terrible clarity that I was losing. Not because he had proof — he didn't, not yet — but because Felix Kavanagh had spent forty years listening to liars, and I could feel him cataloguing me the way I catalogue documents: provenance, condition, discrepancies. My hands were wrong. I caught myself hiding them under the counter and made myself bring them back out, salt-bleached and steady, a working archivist's hands, nothing more.

"Someone died wearing Maren Voss's clothes," he said.

"That was in the paper."

"A drifter, they said. Unidentified for three days, then identified through a hostel receipt." He turned the tide chart over. On the back, faintly, in pencil: a column of dates and times, tidal heights, the neat shorthand of someone who has checked the same figures hundreds of times. "This handwriting is careful. Left-slanted. The writer crosses their sevens."

"Half of England crosses its sevens."

"Yours don't." He nodded at the accession ledger lying open on the counter, at my entries in iron-gall ink, tidy as scripture. "You cross yours with a long diagonal stroke. So does whoever wrote this."

For a moment — I will be honest, since honesty is the one luxury I have left — I considered simply telling him. Right there, among the council minutes and the shipping registers, saying: *You're right, Inspector, or Detective, or whatever we call you now that they've taken your badge away; you're right, and I am the woman you helped bury, and I have been dead for six years and it is the most exhausting work I have ever done.* I imagined the relief of it, the way you imagine sleep when you've been awake too long.

Then I thought of Nils Ferrar's grey car passing Gull Row twice last week, slow, and I remembered what staying alive had cost already, and I did what I have always done. I lied.

"Mr. Kavanagh," I said, "I think grief has made you imaginative. A woman drowned. It's sad. Drowning is sad. But I file papers. I have filed papers for eleven years, and before that I lived in—"

"Before that you lived nowhere," he said quietly. "There's no record of Odile Brandt before eleven years ago. No school, no passport, no tax history. I checked. It's as if you were assembled out of spare parts in 2014." He paused. "Which is, incidentally, two years after Maren Voss went into the sea."

*Two years,* I noted, and some cold practical corner of my mind admired the gap. Two years of drift, of learning to be Odile in towns further up the coast, of practicing a new signature until it stopped hurting. He had done his homework. That was the trouble with Felix Kavanagh. He was the only man in this town who ever did his.

"People fall through cracks," I said.

"People do. You fell through one with excellent penmanship."

* * *

He didn't leave. That was the worst of it. A lesser man would have thrown his accusation and gone, satisfied, and I could have spent the afternoon breathing again. But Felix took off his coat, hung it on the peg by the door as though he owned a peg here, and said, "I'll wait while you close for lunch. Then I'd like to see the Voss materials."

"There are no Voss materials."

"Every public inquiry leaves a trail. Depositions, correspondence, planning objections. Maren Voss objected to the Harrow Point development — her letter is quoted in the committee record. Copies of submissions go to the county archive." He smiled without warmth. "You are the county archive."

He had me there, and he knew it, and I hated him a little for the elegance of it. Because yes — box 44-C, third shelf, behind the flood-damaged electoral rolls. I had catalogued it myself, five years ago, with hands that shook only on the last item. Her objection letter. Her signature. The last time anyone official had seen that handwriting before it started appearing, fresh, in a locked drawer beneath my desk.

"I'll fetch it," I said, because refusing would tell him more than fetching would.

I walked back into the stacks slower than I needed to, and in the dark between the shelves I let myself feel, for exactly the length of one breath, the full weight of what was coming. Then I straightened my spine, pulled box 44-C, and carried it out into the light like a woman carrying nothing heavier than paper.

He read. I worked. That was the arrangement of the next hour — he sat at the reading table turning pages with a care that surprised me, and I processed accessions at my desk above the locked drawer, and neither of us spoke, and the rain kept on, and the whole performance was so domestic that a stranger might have taken us for colleagues. Old colleagues. Something almost like friends, in a town where friendship is mostly just proximity plus time.

It was nearly noon when he said, without looking up: "Your drawer is open."

My pen stopped.

"The one under the desk. It's been open since I arrived. Half an inch." Now he looked up, and his eyes were kind, which was infinitely worse than if they'd been hard. "I wasn't trying to see. But I did. And there's a ledger in it, Miss Brandt, and the top page has today's date."

The room got very small. I have read enough confessions, catalogued enough depositions, to know that this is the moment people describe afterward as the floor giving way. It isn't like that. The floor holds. What gives way is the story you've been standing on.

"That's private," I said. "Council records, accessions in progress—"

"In whose hand?" He rose. He didn't come around the desk, didn't touch anything, and somehow that restraint was the loudest thing in the room. "Show me it isn't hers. Show me the hand that wrote those land purchases — Crane Holdings, Meridian Trust, the Ashcombe Group, seven shell companies buying up Harrow Point parcel by parcel over six years — show me that hand, and I'll walk out of here and never come back."

"You haven't seen any purchases. You saw a closed drawer from across a room."

"I saw enough." His voice dropped. "Maren Voss catalogued Crane's empire under false names, in a locked drawer, in a town archive, in her own handwriting — and she's been dead for six years. Either she's writing from the grave, Miss Brandt, or the grave is a fiction, and I have spent the last month pulling threads that all end in this room."

"And if they do?" The words came out of me before Odile could stop them, and they came out in the other voice — lower, harder, the voice I hadn't used aloud since the night I paid a dying man to write my death certificate. "If they all end here? What will you do, Mr. Kavanagh? Will you go to the police? Which police? Constable Dray, who takes Crane's charity checks? The coroner who signed off on a drowned girl wearing my clothes?" I stood up. My legs were steady. Six years of practice will do that. "Or will you write another of your quiet little solutions, the ones you're famous for among exactly no one — the ones where the guilty stay comfortable and the truth stays in a notebook in a bookshop that burns down?"

Something moved behind his eyes at that. Hurt, maybe. Or recognition.

"You know about the fire," he said.

"I read the paper."

"The fire was yesterday's paper. The kerosene finding isn't printed yet." He said it without triumph, almost sadly, and I understood I had just handed him a thread, and he was courteous enough not to pull it in front of me. "Whoever burned my shop wanted the tide chart. They'll want what's in your drawer more."

"They can't want what doesn't exist."

"Maren." He said the name softly, and it went through me like a fishhook. "I'm not your enemy. Whatever you think of me — whatever she thinks of me — I'm not your enemy. Ferrar is. Crane is. And they are closer than you think, because I found this chart in the ashes of my own shop, which means someone followed me to the quarry, which means they know the evidence exists, and when they finish with me they will come here, and you—" he glanced around at the shelves, the maps, the eleven years of her careful invented life, "—you are the only witness left who matters."

"I'm not a witness. I'm an archivist."

"You're the best witness I've ever seen," he said. "You've documented everything. Dates, parcels, names. You've built the case against Aldous Crane with your own hands, one entry at a time, for six years, and you've hidden it fifty feet from the police station because you couldn't bear to throw it away and couldn't survive to use it." He picked up his coat. "I'm going to ask you once, plainly, and then I'm going to leave you alone. Is Maren Voss alive?"

The clock on the wall ticked. Rain on glass. Somewhere below us the sea worked at the rocks, patient as a clerk.

And I — Odile Brandt, keeper of records, guardian of other people's truths, coward by trade and conviction — heard myself say:

"No."

Just that. One syllable, level as a shelf. And I watched him believe it and not believe it, both at once, the way you watch a man stand in a doorway deciding whether to step into weather.

"All right," he said finally. "Then you won't mind if I keep checking the tide tables myself."

He let himself out. The bell over the door gave its small dull note, and then the archive was silent, and I stood at my desk with my heart going like a trapped bird and the locked drawer half an inch open beneath my hands.

I should have burned it all that afternoon. The ledger, the letters, the confession draft with its careful left-slanted lines — six years of evidence, six years of a dead woman's testimony, feeding the stove until there was nothing left but a smell of ink and ash. Any sensible ghost would have.

Instead I sat down, opened the drawer, and added one more entry to the ledger, dated today, in a hand I no longer recognized as mine:

*Kavanagh knows. Decision required.*

And beneath the window, far down Gull Row, a grey car slowed to walking speed, and did not pass.
