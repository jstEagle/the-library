## 14. A Break-In, Tidily Done

The trouble began with a paperclip.

Ulyana Yarrow noticed it at 7:42 on a Thursday morning, before the kettle had finished its first complaint, before the grey light had fully committed to the day. The paperclip sat on the floor beside the filing cabinet — the tall green one, second drawer, H through L — and it was bent into a shape that suggested someone had worried it between finger and thumb for a long time. A nervous habit. *Her* nervous habit. She did it herself when reading difficult correspondence, worrying clips until they surrendered their temper.

She stood in the doorway of her own office with her coat still buttoned and her keys still in her hand, and she let the cold morning air move past her into the room, because something in the room was wrong and she wanted to give it time to confess.

The window was latched. The door had been locked; the key turned with its usual reluctant click. Nothing was overturned. Nothing was missing — not the silver letter opener Edmund Hallow had given her three Christmases ago, not the petty cash tin whose combination she'd never bothered to change because there had never been anything in it worth stealing, not the framed certificate from the Law Society that hung slightly crooked above the desk no matter how many times she straightened it.

Everything was exactly where it should be.

That was what frightened her.

Ulyana crossed to the filing cabinet and opened the second drawer. The folders inside were arranged alphabetically by client surname, then chronologically within each file, then — for matters with multiple instruments — by document type: wills, trusts, conveyances, correspondence. A system she had built over eleven years, refined like a seashell, worn smooth by daily use. It was not a system anyone could learn from a book. It had quirks. Hallow, Edmund was filed under H but cross-referenced under E for his middle name, Edmund Augustus, because early in their acquaintance he'd insisted his affairs be findable "under the name my mother used when she was cross with me." The cross-reference was a pale blue card, handwritten, tucked behind the H divider.

The blue card was there. But it had been moved half an inch forward, so that its corner protruded past the edge of the H divider instead of sitting flush beneath it.

Ulyana pulled it out. The ink was hers. The handwriting was hers. But the card was warm.

Not warm from the room. The office radiator didn't reach the cabinet; she'd complained about it for years. The card held the particular residual warmth of a hand that had held it recently, turning it over, reading it, understanding it.

She set it down very carefully on the desk, as though it might go off.

Then she did what she always did when the world misbehaved: she made tea, she sat down, and she took inventory.

* * *

It took four hours. She went through every drawer, every folder, every file, wearing cotton gloves she kept for handling old deeds, though these files were hardly precious — most of them were conveyances and probate paperwork as dry as August grass. She checked dates against her diary. She checked signatures against her own. She checked the order of documents within folders against the order she remembered, and where memory failed her, she checked against logic, because her system was logical even in its quirks, and logic leaves fingerprints.

By noon she had found seven anomalies.

A conveyance for the Pask family cottage — Nell Pask's cottage, though Ulyana hadn't yet connected the name to anything larger — had been refiled under P rather than resting in the pending tray where she'd left it Tuesday night. The pending tray was for unfinished business. Someone had finished it, or decided it wasn't business at all, and shelved it.

The Hallow estate file — the first will, the funeral, the letters testamentary — sat in its place, complete, correct. But the manila folder itself had been replaced. The original had a coffee ring on the lower right corner, a stain she'd made the morning after the funeral and privately resolved never to mention. The folder now on her shelf was clean. Same weight, same tab cut, same manufacturer's code stamped inside the spine — she checked — but clean. Someone had taken the stained folder away and put the contents into an identical one.

Why? Not to steal anything. To erase evidence that they had been there. A coffee ring is a timestamp. You can't unspill coffee, but you can throw away the cup.

And the seventh anomaly, the one she saved for last because she dreaded it: the drawer of miscellaneous correspondence, the one she kept unsorted — truly unsorted, a honest jumble of circulars, bar association mailings, and letters awaiting action — was sorted. Perfectly. Alphabetized by sender, then date, then subject line where one existed.

Including the confession letter.

Ulyana had kept Edmund's letter — *I did what the parish required. The ledger knows.* — in that drawer, folded into a plain envelope, wedged between a dental reminder card and a flyer for the harvest festival. She had told herself this was discretion. In truth it was superstition: she hadn't known where else to put a dead man's confession, so she'd hidden it in chaos, believing chaos was camouflage.

Someone had found it. Read it. Understood it. And then — this was the part that made her hands go cold around her teacup — put it back *in alphabetical order*, filed under H for Hallow, in a drawer that was supposed to have no order at all.

They hadn't taken it. They hadn't copied it — or if they had, she couldn't know. They had simply told her, in the quietest possible language, *we know where you keep your secrets, and we know how you think.*

* * *

Constable Brack arrived at two o'clock, summoned by a phone call Ulyana had debated making for two hours and finally made because not reporting a break-in would itself become a data point in someone's ledger somewhere.

He listened without interrupting while she described the anomalies. He walked the office slowly, hands clasped behind his back, pausing at the window latch, at the door, at the filing cabinet. He was a careful man, Brack. Careful the way a locked drawer is careful.

"No forced entry," he said at last. It wasn't a question, but she answered it anyway.

"None."

"Who has keys?"

"Myself. Ada Crane holds a duplicate for emergencies — she's done since I took over the practice. The locksmith. And presumably whoever cut copies before me; the lock is older than my tenancy." She watched him write nothing down. That was its own kind of answer. "You'll want to check who's requested key duplicates from Hollis the locksmith."

"I'll want to do a great many things, Miss Yarrow." He smiled — a small, professional warmth, the smile of a man who wanted her to feel looked after. "May I ask what, precisely, you believe the intruder was seeking?"

"The same thing everyone in this parish seems to be seeking," Ulyana said. "Paperwork."

Brack's pen hovered. "You've had other indications of interest in Mr. Hallow's affairs?"

"I've had a vicar advising patience, an elders' dinner I'm still digesting, and now a burglar with excellent taste in stationery. Yes. Indications."

He closed his notebook. "I'll have the lock changed myself — I know a man in Ashcombe, discreet, quick. And Miss Yarrow?" At the door he paused, and for a moment the courtesy thinned and she saw the calculation underneath, the way you see wire beneath plaster. "Offices get broken into when they hold things worth breaking in for. Sometimes the wisest course is to make sure they don't."

"Is that advice, Constable, or a warning?"

"It's community policing," said Brack, and let himself out.

* * *

She worked late. Of course she worked late — the office felt like a room in which someone had breathed, and she wanted to breathe back, to reclaim it, to touch every file the stranger had touched until her own hands overwrote theirs. But by nine o'clock the exercise had curdled. Every document now seemed to look at her sideways. She caught herself checking the blue cross-reference card for warmth a third time and stopped, disgusted with herself, disgusted with the elegant little trap her own mind had become.

Because that was the true violation, she understood now, standing in the lamplight among her alphabetized ghosts. Not the touched papers. Not the swapped folder. The intruder had broken in and rearranged her office into a perfect portrait of her own mind — her systems, her habits, her secret places — and hung it up where she'd have to look at it every day. Whoever it was knew her the way she knew herself. Better, perhaps, in one respect: they knew what her orderliness concealed.

There were perhaps four people alive who could have navigated her filing system unaided. Ada Crane, who had taught it to her. Herself. And — she thought of the dinner, of Mrs. Aldous's soft questions, of the way conversation at that table had circled her work like gulls circling a plough — whoever those people had told.

She locked up, walked home through streets gone blue with evening, and did not sleep so much as hover above sleep, and in the grey hour before dawn she got up and did the one thing she had been avoiding.

She wrote a list.

Not of suspects. Of everything the intruder could have learned, had they read thoroughly — and the paperclip, the warmth, the patience of the reordering all argued thoroughness. What did she hold that was dangerous? The second will, with its impossible beneficiary. The confession letter. Three years of parish legal work with its small oddities she'd filed and forgotten. Her notes on M.V., such as they were — a defunct children's home, burned records, a fire no one discussed. And one more item, added reluctantly, in smaller handwriting, as though the pen itself were embarrassed:

*Tomas. His arrival. His claim. What he is to me.*

If the intruder had read her calendar — and her calendar lived in the top drawer, and the top drawer showed no anomalies, which proved nothing whatsoever — then they knew that Tomas Reyes had come back to Thistlewick after eleven years, and why, and that Ulyana had spent two evenings with him in the back booth of the Lamb and Flag with his mother's name between them like a candle neither dared blow out.

Her private life, filed. Alphabetized. Assessed.

* * *

At half past eight, Ada Crane telephoned.

"I heard you had some excitement," her mentor said. Her voice was dry as old vellum, and as hard to read. "Brack rang me. He rings me about everything, you know. Habit of forty years."

"Does he." Ulyana kept her voice level. "Ada, did you give anyone a key to my office?"

A pause. On the line, Ulyana could hear the particular silence of a woman choosing her words with tweezers. "No one has a key to your office but you, me, and God, and I've never been entirely sure about God's discretion." A rustle of paper. "You should come see me today. There are things about this parish's paperwork that you ought to hear from me before you hear them from anyone else."

"That sounds ominous."

"That sounds like Thursday," said Ada. "Come at four. And Ulyana—" the dryness cracked, just barely, just enough to show the old woman underneath, the one who had taught her conveyancing and cross-examination and how to drink sherry without grimacing— "when you come, bring nothing. Say nothing on the walk. And if anyone asks where you're going, tell them you're having your eyes tested."

The line went dead.

Ulyana sat holding the receiver, listening to the dial tone hum its single patient note. Outside her window, Thistlewick was waking into another blameless morning: smoke from chimneys, the vicarage gate swinging, Mrs. Aldous's car gleaming outside the bakery like a well-fed cat. A town that kept its books balanced to the penny.

She looked down at her desk, at the list she'd written in the dark, and at the bottom of it, beneath Tomas's name, she found she had written one more line without remembering doing so — her handwriting, but not her intention, the way a sleepwalker's path is her own and yet not:

*Someone fluent in me.*

Four o'clock. Ada Crane, who held a key. Ada Crane, who had taught her that a good filing system tells you more about a person than a diary does. Ada Crane, who had said *bring nothing* in a voice that was afraid.

Ulyana put on her coat, locked her carefully reordered office behind her, and started down the hill toward her mentor's house — rehearsing, all the way, the lie about her eyes.
