## 39. Nell's Garden

The ground thawed in the third week of March, and Nell Pask was waiting for it the way other people waited for letters.

Ulyana found her at the edge of the old home's grounds, standing in a field gone to seed behind the burned-out footprint of the children's wing. Thirty years of grass had grown over the foundation, but if you knew where to look — and Nell always knew — you could still read the shape of it in the earth, the way a scar reads under a sleeve. She stood with her hands in the pockets of a coat too heavy for the season, and she did not turn when Ulyana's boots crunched the frost behind her.

"You came," Nell said. "I half thought you'd have had enough of this parish by now."

"I have had enough of it," Ulyana said. "That's rather the point of being here."

Nell laughed — a dry, cracked sound, like a gate that hadn't been oiled in years — and finally turned. Her face had been in the newspapers for four months now, in the courtroom sketches and the grainy archive photos the papers ran beside them, but up close it was simply an old woman's face, lined and weathered and lit with something that had waited a very long time for permission to exist.

"Four of them," Nell said, looking back at the field. "Martha. The twins, Ivo and Sam. And little Grace, who was only six." She said the names the way one says a rosary, each one given its full weight. "They buried them in the churchyard with a single stone, *in memory of the children*, as if they were a weather event. As if they were hail."

"I know."

"You know now." Nell's eyes were not unkind when she said it. That was the terrible thing about Nell; she had survived the fire and the silence and the thirty years, and she had come out of it without cruelty, which Ulyana could not say for anyone else in the parish, least of all themselves. "But knowing isn't the same as carrying. So we'll carry it where people can see. That's what the garden's for."

The plan had been Nell's from the start. Not a monument — the parish council, in its penitent new configuration, had offered a monument, granite and bronze and a plaque with carefully hedged language, and Nell had refused it in a single sentence: *"Stone is what they used to keep us quiet."* A garden, she said. A living thing. Something that had to be tended, year after year, by hands that would remember why. Something that would die back every winter and come up again every spring, so that forgetting would take actual effort.

Ulyana had offered, in the flat professional voice that had once been their armor, to handle the legalities. The land was parish property; the trust Edmund's estate had been forced to disgorge could fund it; the paperwork had taken Ulyana eleven days, and they had enjoyed every one of them, because for the first time in their career the documents said exactly what they meant.

But the planting itself — that was not paperwork. That was kneeling in cold soil with an eighty-year-old woman who had once pulled a child's coat from a burning doorway and been told, by men in good coats, that she was confused, that the fire was electrical, that it would be best for everyone if she stopped asking.

* * *

They came on a Saturday, and Ulyana had not expected them.

They came the way parishioners come to everything — in ones and twos at first, drifting up the lane with the self-conscious air of people who have not decided whether they are attending or witnessing. A woman Ulyana recognized from the food bank, the one Ruth's ministry had fed for years with money that had once been labeled *quieting* in a ledger balanced to the penny. A farmer who had sat three rows behind Ulyana at the probate hearing and had not looked at them once. The schoolteacher. The man who ran the garage. Families, some of them, with children who did not understand where they were and were told, quietly, to be gentle with the ground.

Ulyana stood by the wheelbarrow of trowels and watched them arrive and understood, with a sensation like a bone setting, what Nell had known all along: that the parish had not come because it had been shamed into it, though it had been shamed, thoroughly and publicly and on the record. It had come because thirty years of silence is a weight, and weight wants somewhere to go.

"They don't know what to do with their hands," Nell observed, beside Ulyana. "Look at them. They keep putting them in their pockets."

"Give them trowels," Ulyana said. "That's what the trowels are for."

And it worked, more or less. The woman from the food bank took a spade and began turning earth at the corner where the dormitory had stood, and the farmer took the wheelbarrow, and the schoolteacher knelt with her sleeves pushed up, and slowly, in the way that work does it, the crowd stopped being a crowd and became a crew. The frost came out of the soil in dark curls. Someone had brought a thermos and it went around without ceremony.

Ulyana planted where Nell told them to plant. Nell had drawn the layout on the back of a funeral order of service — Ulyana had recognized the paper, one of Edmund's, from the stacks that had come through the office — four beds in a square, one for each child, with a path between them wide enough for two people to walk abreast. "So nobody has to visit alone," Nell said, and did not explain further, and did not need to.

Martha's bed got roses, because Martha had been fifteen and had once, Nell said, stolen a rose from the vicarage garden and been caned for it, and Nell had never forgotten the look on her face — not pain, but fury, the fury of a girl who understood that beauty was being kept from her on principle. The twins got beans and sweet peas, because they had been the ones who climbed everything. And Grace, little Grace, who was six, got crocuses, because crocuses come up through the last of the snow, and Nell said Grace had been the sort of child who was always arriving too early, into rooms, into conversations, into the world.

Ulyana dug the holes for the crocuses with their hands, no trowel, because the bulbs were small and the soil was cold and it seemed like the kind of work that should hurt a little.

* * *

Ruth Ellery came at noon.

Ulyana saw her at the gate and their whole body went tight, an old reflex now, four months old and already worn smooth with use. Ruth stood at the lych-gate of the field — there was no gate anymore, the fence had long since fallen, but the posts were there, and Ruth stood between them as if the gate still hung — in her collar, holding nothing, and for a long moment she did not come in.

The work around the beds did not stop. But it quieted. Ulyana watched the woman from the food bank straighten up, watched the farmer set down his spade, and understood that everyone here was doing the same arithmetic at once: what Ruth had confessed, what Ruth had surrendered herself for, what Ruth had believed she was saving. The trial was still ahead of her. The plea had been entered; the charges of obstruction and conspiracy were being negotiated by solicitors Ulyana did not know, in rooms Ulyana was no longer welcome in. But the parish had already held its own proceeding, here in this field, in the silence of forty people deciding whether to keep digging.

Ruth came in.

She walked down the path between the beds — wide enough for two, and she walked it alone, which Ulyana suspected she understood as well as anyone — and she stopped at the fourth bed, Grace's bed, the crocuses, and she knelt. Not gracefully. She was in her sixties and her knees cracked audibly and she knelt anyway, and she put her hands in the soil the way the others had, and she began to dig.

Nobody spoke to her. Nobody spoke *against* her, either. She dug beside the woman from the food bank, whose money she had fed and whose trust she had spent, and the two of them worked a foot apart in the cold earth for the better part of an hour, and the only sound was the spades.

Later — much later, when the light had gone long and gold and the beds were turned and raked and half-planted — Ruth came to where Ulyana stood by the wheelbarrow, and she said, without preamble:

"I posted the letter because he asked me to. I want you to know I've never regretted that part. Only everything after."

"I know," Ulyana said.

"He thought the parish could survive the truth." Ruth looked out at the field, at the bent backs and the turned earth, at the children running along the path between the beds because children run everywhere and no one had the heart to stop them. "I thought it couldn't. One of us was wrong." She was quiet a moment. "It's a strange thing, being wrong in public. Everyone is very kind to you, and none of the kindness helps."

"No," Ulyana said. "It doesn't."

Ruth nodded, as if Ulyana had said something wise, and went back to the crocuses, and Ulyana stood by the wheelbarrow and felt the strange, unhealed thing in their chest that had been Tomas-shaped for four months and understood that Ruth's sentence was true in a way she had not meant it. Everyone was very kind, now. Ada sent a card at Christmas with a signature and nothing else. Brack nodded to them across the market square with the careful respect of a man who had handed over a sealed report and would spend the rest of his career living with what was in it. The disciplinary complaint had been withdrawn — quietly, without apology, the way it had been filed. Even the elders' families, the ones who remained, were courteous in the new way, the way people are courteous to weather.

None of the kindness helped. Because kindness was not what Ulyana had lost. Ulyana had lost the person who had known them before the ledger, before the choice, before the reading — and there was no parish, no garden, no spring, that gave that back.

* * *

They finished at dusk.

The bulbs were in — crocuses and daffodils and, along the path, a row of snowdrops that Nell handled herself, one by one, pressing each into the earth with two fingers like a woman tucking in children at bedtime. The roses were heeled in against the frost. The beds were raked and watered from a tank someone had trailered up, and the four corners were marked with stakes and string until the plants came, and the whole thing looked, in the failing light, less like a garden than like a promise drawn on the ground in pencil, waiting for the year to ink it in.

The parishioners drifted away in twos and threes, the way they'd come. Several of them shook Nell's hand. One woman — the schoolteacher — hugged her, briefly and fiercely, and Nell stood in the hug the way a person stands in warm water, not quite believing it, afraid to move in case it drained away.

Then it was the two of them, and the field, and the first stars.

Nell walked the path one more time, slow, her boots scuffing the fresh-raked soil, and Ulyana walked beside her because the path was wide enough for two and because Nell had built it that way on purpose.

"My mother worked in that kitchen," Nell said, nodding at a depression in the ground Ulyana would never have identified as anything. "She used to say the home was the best thing Mr. Hallow ever did and the worst thing, both at once, and I didn't understand her until I was sixty." She stopped at Grace's corner. "He loved us, in his way. That's the thing nobody wants written down. You can love a thing and fail it. You can love a thing and bury the proof of your failing in your own accounts, balanced to the penny, for forty years." She looked at Ulyana. "You'll write it down anyway."

"It's already written down," Ulyana said. "It's in the record. It's in the papers. It's in the transcript of the hearing, which anyone can read, and which the schoolteacher, I happen to know, has read twice."

"Good." Nell said it simply, without triumph, and Ulyana understood that this was what thirty years of waiting had refined her down to: not vengeance, not even justice exactly, but this — *good* — the plain satisfaction of a thing being said where it could be heard.

They stood a while longer. The cold came up out of the ground the way it does after sundown in March, patient and total.

"You'll come in the spring," Nell said. It was not a question. "When the crocuses come up. Someone should see them who knows why they're there."

"I'll come," Ulyana said. "I'll come every spring. That's rather the arrangement."

Nell smiled, and it transformed her face, and Ulyana thought: *this is what the parish never let her have. Thirty years, and no one to smile at.*

* * *

Ulyana walked home through the dark lanes with soil still under their fingernails, because they had not wanted to wash it off at the standpipe and had told themselves it was sentiment and knew it was something closer to penance, or proof.

The office was cold. Ulyana lit the lamp, and the room came up around them in its familiar shapes — the desk, the shelves, the filing cabinet that had started everything, still standing in its corner like a witness who never gets called. Ulyana had left the top drawer's contents unsorted since the autumn. It had begun as defeat, the day after the hearing, when they had been too hollowed out to alphabetize anything. It had become, without their noticing, a principle. The new files lay in the drawer in the order they had arrived, which is to say the order the world had produced them, which is to say chaos, which is to say honest.

There was a letter on the desk.

Ulyana stopped in the doorway with the lamp not yet turned down, and looked at it, and their heart did the thing it had learned to do in the past year — the small cold drop, the reflex of a person who has learned what letters can carry.

It was not a threat. It was not from the disciplinary board, or a client, or a solicitor, or the parish council, or — their hand had actually paused over it — from a person whose handwriting they would have known anywhere, though no such letter had come and Ulyana had stopped, mostly, waiting for one.

It was a plain envelope, postmarked two days ago, from a town Ulyana had never visited: a market town forty miles east, a name Ulyana knew only from a train timetable. The handwriting was careful, a woman's or a careful man's, and it pressed hard enough to be felt through the paper, the way handwriting presses when the writer has rewritten the letter several times before sending it.

Ulyana sat down. They did not open it at once. They sat in the lamplight with soil under their nails and the smell of turned earth still on their coat, and they thought about the field, and the four beds, and the path wide enough for two, and Nell's face in the schoolteacher's hug, and Ruth kneeling in the cold with her cracked knees and her unhelped kindness, and Tomas somewhere across an ocean not writing, and Ada's card with its signature and nothing else, and the whole long cost of it, itemized, paid, still being paid.

Then they opened the letter, because that was the vocation now, the one they had chosen over everything, the one the dead kept finding them for.

*Dear Mr. or Ms. Yarrow* — the writer did not know which, and had left both standing — *I am writing because I do not know who else to write to. In our parish there is a will that no one will read aloud, and a fire that no one will discuss, and I am told by everyone I trust that it would be kindest to let it be. I have begun to suspect that everyone I trust is the problem.*

Ulyana read it twice. Then they took a fresh sheet of paper, and uncapped the pen, and began, in their own careful hand, to write back: *Dear friend — the dead write often, and it is never too late to answer them.*

Outside, the frost was already forming on the field where the garden slept, four beds in a square, waiting for the crocuses.
