## 8. Dorian's Alibi

Tamsin

The Ashcombe estate announced itself the way old money always did: not with grandeur, but with the confidence that it didn't need to. Wrought-iron gates, gravel drive raked into perfect parallel lines, a façade of honeyed limestone that had been honeyed limestone since before Hollowmere had a name. Tamsin had been out here twice in her career—once for a drowned dog, once for Verity Ashcombe's husband, whose heart had quit in the middle of his morning ride and who had been, by all accounts, the luckiest man in the county to die before anyone asked him anything.

She parked at the base of the steps and sat for a moment, hands still on the wheel, watching the house watch her. That was the thing about the Ashcombe place. Every window was a mirror from the outside. You couldn't see in; you could only see yourself, small and distorted, looking up.

Ruth had wanted to come. Tamsin had told her to stay with the water-level reports. If this went badly, she didn't want both of them on Verity's radar. If it went well, she wanted Ruth's findings clean, uncontaminated by whatever happened inside this house.

She got out. The air smelled of cut grass and, underneath it, the reservoir—two miles east, but present anyway, the way a grave is present in a house where someone died.

The door opened before she knocked.

"You'll want the blue room," Dorian Ashcombe said. "Grandmother's in town until four. Charity luncheon. She's giving a speech about stewardship." His mouth did something at the edge—not quite a smile, not quite a wound. "I thought we'd be more comfortable without an audience."

"I didn't call ahead."

"No," he agreed, stepping back to let her in. "You didn't. That's rather the point, isn't it?"

* * *

Dorian

He'd known she would come today. Not because he'd planned it—well, not *only* because he'd planned it—but because Tamsin Voss was a woman who walked toward the thing she was afraid of the way other people walked toward the bar at a funeral. He'd watched her do it for three years. Watched her drive past the estate with her jaw set, watched her stand in the cemetery with her hands in her coat pockets, counting headstones the way other people counted sheep.

He'd left the front door unlocked on purpose. He'd told the staff to take their lunch in the village. He'd worn the grey sweater because she'd once, three years ago, in a conversation that lasted ninety seconds in the churchyard after a funeral, glanced at him in a grey sweater and then very carefully looked away, and Dorian Ashcombe had built entire strategies on smaller data than that.

She followed him into the blue room, and he poured the coffee he'd had ready since ten—black, no sugar, because he'd watched her at the anniversary committee meeting last spring drink it black and make a small face at the sweetness of everything else on the table.

"You knew I was coming," she said.

"I hoped. Knowing is such a vulgar word." He handed her the cup. "You're investigating a drowning. The drowned man is wearing your cardigan. You've spent the week looking like a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't want to finish. And this house is where the arithmetic lives. Of course you were coming."

"You make me sound predictable."

"You make yourself sound predictable. I just don't look away." He settled into the chair across from her, leaving the sofa between them deliberately unoccupied. A sofa was a suggestion. A chair was a boundary. She'd trust the boundary. "So. Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever you drove out here to ask me. You've got your coroner voice on. I can hear it. It's very professional and it doesn't suit you at all."

Tamsin set the cup down without drinking. "Where were you on the night of the seventeenth? Between eight in the evening and six the following morning."

And there it was. He'd rehearsed this moment in the shower, in the car, in the dark of his bedroom staring at the ceiling, and in every rehearsal he'd felt the same thing he felt now: a kind of giddy horror. Because the answer was true, and the truth was the most dangerous thing he owned, and he was about to hand it to her gift-wrapped.

"Here," he said. "Alone. Grandmother was in Bath. The staff had the evening off—I gave it to them, which Mrs. Pell thought was odd, because I never give anyone anything. I sat in this room with a bottle of the '61 port and I drank most of it and I did not sleep, and at approximately eleven-forty I walked down to the boathouse and stood on the dock for an hour looking at the water."

Tamsin had gone very still. It was the stillness of a woman recalibrating. He watched her do the math: an unprompted alibi, detailed, uncorroborated, from a man she hadn't yet accused of anything.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you were going to ask."

"I wasn't. Not yet."

"Then I'm ahead of schedule, which is the Ashcombe family motto, though we'd die before putting it on a crest." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and let the charm drop—not all of it, never all of it, but enough that she could see the mechanism underneath, the way you can see the works of a clock through glass. "Tamsin. Someone pulled a dead man out of the reservoir wearing your clothes. Someone is either framing you, or using you, or—worst case—telling the truth through a corpse. And you came to my house because my family is at the center of it, and you're right, and the fact that you're right is the only interesting thing that's happened to me in three years."

"You're not a suspect," she said, which was a lie, and they both knew it, and he loved her a little for the professionalism of it.

"No," he agreed. "I'm worse. I'm a witness."

* * *

Tamsin

There was a version of Dorian Ashcombe that Hollowmere knew well: the charming grandson, the one who'd come back from London after the father died, the one who poured wine at charity auctions and remembered everyone's children's names. Tamsin had always filed that version under *performance, unverified*. Everyone in this town performed. It was practically the municipal charter.

But the man across from her had just handed her an alibi she hadn't asked for, and the handing of it had a texture she recognized from her work. Guilty people gave you alibis like fortresses—thick walls, few doors, everything load-bearing. Innocent people gave you alibis like apologies, apologetic and thin. Dorian had given her something else entirely: a gift. Deliberate, wrapped, with a card.

*You were going to need this. Here it is. Now ask yourself why I knew that.*

"Walk me through it again," she said. "The seventeenth."

"I've told you everything."

"You've told me a story. Walk me through the evening."

Something flickered in his face—amusement, maybe, or respect. "Dinner alone, eight o'clock, in the small dining room. Mrs. Pell made lamb; I didn't finish it. I read. I drank. Around eleven I went out. The dock, as I said. Came back around midnight, sat up until three, went to bed. Woke at seven. Found out about the body when Ruth Okonkwo's cruiser went past the gates at speed, and I knew, before the radio said it, that it would be the reservoir." He paused. "I knew because it's always the reservoir. This town drowns its problems. It's our one consistent tradition."

"The anniversary dinner," Tamsin said. "You're on the committee."

"I'm the committee's wine budget. Grandmother is the committee."

"Your name's on the guest list. So is mine." She watched him. "First time in nine years my name's been on it. Someone added it."

"I know. I added it."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms do when something large has been said in a small voice. Tamsin kept her face still. Outside, a gardener's shears snipped rhythmically at something, absurdly domestic, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"You added my name," she repeated.

"Grandmother's list is a document of alliances. Everyone on it is either family, money, or useful. You've never been any of those, which has always been your most attractive quality." He turned his glass a quarter-rotation on the table. "But this year the town needs to see you at that table. Seated with us. Smiling. Because in six weeks, when whatever is coming arrives—and it is coming, Tamsin, you can feel it the way you feel weather in a bad knee—this town will need to decide who it believes. And if you're already family friend of the Ashcombes, then when you say what you're going to say, half of them will believe you on loyalty and the other half will dismiss you on it. Either way, everyone's attention is where it needs to be."

"On what I'm going to say. You know what I'm going to say."

"I know what you're going to *find*," he said. "The saying is your department."

* * *

Dorian

She was angry now. He could tell by the way she got quieter—Tamsin's anger was a receding tide, pulling back to reveal the rocks. He'd have to be careful. He'd have to give her the next thing soon, or she'd leave, and the next thing was the one he'd actually brought her here to deliver.

He'd thought about how to do it for days. He couldn't just tell her about the study. Telling her outright would be a betrayal of a different kind—the kind that got noticed, the kind his grandmother's world punished with precision. He had to let her find it. He had to hand her the thread and watch her pull it and never once be seen holding the spool.

"You're angry," he said.

"I'm working."

"You're angry *and* working. It's very efficient. My grandmother would admire it, which is why I'd never introduce you." He stood, crossed to the window, gave her his back—a calculated vulnerability; people talked to backs. "Can I tell you something unguarded? You'll want to put it in a report, so consider whether you can afford to have it."

"Try me."

"Three years ago, Elian Marsh stood where you're sitting." He heard her breath change. Didn't turn around. "Not this chair. That one. He'd come to the gala—crashed it, really, in a borrowed jacket, with a head full of whatever he'd seen or thought he'd seen, and Grandmother had him escorted out through the kitchen, and I followed, because I was twenty-four and curious and he looked at me like I was the only person in the room who might listen." He turned then. Her face was a closed door, but her hands had curled around the arms of the chair, and hands always told him the truth her face wouldn't. "He said, 'Your family kills people.' And I laughed, because that's what you do at twenty-four when someone says something unbearable, and he looked at me—God, he looked at me—and he said, 'You know I'm right. That's why you laughed.'"

Tamsin said nothing. The shears outside had stopped.

"Three weeks later he was dead," Dorian said. "Officially. And I have spent three years doing the arithmetic you're doing now, and I have never once gotten a number I could live with. So when a man with his face surfaces in the reservoir, I'll tell you what I did, Tamsin. I sat down and I made a list of everyone who could have arranged it. And my name is on that list. And my grandmother's name is on that list. And yours." He let that sit. "I gave you my alibi because if it's my grandmother, I need to know before she decides I'm a line item. And if it's you—" he smiled, and hated how easily the smile came, how it was armor first and expression second, "—then I'd rather hear it from you than from a jury."

"It's not me."

"I know. I checked."

"You *checked*."

"I checked," he said pleasantly, "the way you checked the water levels. Ruth Okonkwo has been very diligent. She should be careful. Diligence is noticeable."

* * *

Tamsin

He knew about Ruth's water-levels. Which meant he knew about Ruth's methods, her access, her schedule—or someone was telling him, which was worse, because the only people who knew were Tamsin and Ruth.

She stood. The chair scraped. "I think I'm done here."

"You're not. You haven't asked the real question yet." Dorian stayed by the window, hands in his pockets, the grey sweater doing its quiet, treacherous work. "You're wondering why I'm helping you. Write it down as self-preservation if it makes the report cleaner. But before you go—" and now his voice changed, dropped its polish, and for one unguarded second he sounded like a tired man in his late twenties who drank too much port, "—you should know about the study."

"Study."

"Grandmother's. Second floor, end of the east wing. It locks. It has always locked. The staff don't clean it—she does it herself, which Mrs. Pell finds so unnatural she mentioned it to me twice in one week, and Mrs. Pell has not volunteered information since the Ford administration." He shrugged, one shoulder. "No servant enters. No one enters. And in thirty years of living in or visiting this house, I have never once seen that door open. Not for cleaners. Not for the accountant who does everything else. Not during the fire, when she stood in the hall and let them carry out the silver and refused to let anyone go up."

Tamsin's pulse was a slow, cold knock. "Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're a coroner," Dorian said, "and you of all people know that a body tells you nothing until you open it up. Consider the study a body." He walked her to the door himself, unhurried, and at the threshold he paused, close enough that she could smell the coffee and, under it, the port he must have had at lunch, three days early, in a house where no one was watching. Which meant he drank like that all the time now. Which meant the alibi had been true in every detail, including the drinking.

"Tamsin," he said. "One more thing, off the record, off everything. The seventeenth—the night on the dock. I wasn't there because I'm melancholy, though I am. I was there because someone had called the house that afternoon asking whether the family still owned the east boathouse. They wouldn't give a name. The voice was—" he hesitated, and she watched him choose the word with real care, "—familiar. I went down to look. There was nothing. But someone was asking after that boathouse before your drowned man ever reached the water."

"You should have led with that."

"I should have led with a great many things." He smiled the smile again, and behind it, for the first time, she saw something that looked almost like fear. "So should you. Ask yourself who benefits from your name on that guest list, Tamsin. I put it there. But I'm not the only one who edits Grandmother's documents."

* * *

Tamsin

The drive back took eleven minutes and felt like an interrogation of herself. She replayed every sentence, catalogued every gift he'd handed her: the alibi, the gala memory, the study, the phone call, the warning. Five offerings from a man who claimed to be protecting himself. Either Dorian Ashcombe was the most cooperative suspect in the history of suspicious families, or he was building something—laying pieces on a board for her to move, so that when the game ended, her hands would be the ones on the pieces.

Ruth was waiting in the office with the water reports spread across her desk like tarot cards, and Tamsin could see from the doorway that something in her face had changed.

"Find something," Tamsin said.

Ruth turned a page toward her. "The reservoir's fed by two streams and a sluice. The sluice gate records—digital, timestamped—show it was opened manually for forty minutes on the night of the seventeenth. Someone let water in. Someone wanted the body to move, to surface somewhere visible." She looked up. "The gate's accessed from the east bank. Tamsin—the east boathouse path runs right past it."

Tamsin stood very still, hearing Dorian's voice again: *someone was asking after that boathouse before your drowned man ever reached the water.*

"Ruth," she said slowly. "How do you feel about breaking into a locked room?"
