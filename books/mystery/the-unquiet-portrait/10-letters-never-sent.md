## 10. Letters Never Sent

The boxes came down from the attic on the first cold morning of October, when the light through Tamsin's kitchen window had gone thin and honest—the kind of light that showed dust, and cracks, and everything else she'd spent three years not looking at.

She'd been putting it off since the funeral. Not her mother's funeral—her mother had been gone eleven years, buried on a gray Wednesday with half of Hollowmere in attendance because Edith Voss had been the sort of woman who remembered birthdays and blood pressure medications and the names of everyone's children, including the ones nobody talked about. No, the funeral Tamsin had been avoiding was smaller and quieter: the funeral of the last excuse she had for never opening these boxes. Ruth had moved the last of them down from the attic two nights ago, after the thing with the photographs in the chair, after Ruth started sleeping on the couch with her service weapon in the side table drawer, and had said, in the gentle way that was worse than nagging: *Might as well. You're up anyway.*

So Tamsin made coffee she didn't drink and knelt on the kitchen floor in the honest light and opened the first box.

Her mother had been a keeper. That was the word for it—*keeper*, as in lighthouse, as in someone who maintained a flame against the dark. Receipts from 1989. Tamsin's report cards, every one, even the second-grade disaster where a teacher had written *Tamsin is a very serious child* in red pen, which her mother had underlined twice and kept for forty years as though it were a compliment. Photographs in envelopes from the pharmacy, the negatives still tucked in their paper sleeves. A christening gown gone the color of weak tea. The ordinary sediment of a life, layered and compressed, and Tamsin moved through it with the same detached competence she brought to cataloguing the effects of the drowned—*one cardigan, blue, buttoned to the throat; one wristwatch, stopped*—except this time her hands weren't steady, and the inventory was her own childhood.

She was two boxes in, sitting on the floor now with her back against the cabinet, when her fingers found the tin.

It was a biscuit tin, the kind that had once held shortbread and now held sewing supplies in a thousand households. But it was heavy, and it rattled wrong, and when she pried the lid off she didn't find buttons or thread.

She found letters.

A dozen of them, maybe more, bound with the green ribbon her mother had used on Christmas presents, tied in the same neat bow she'd taught Tamsin to make when she was six. Each envelope was addressed, stamped even, the postmarks faded to ghosts. And every single one was addressed to the same place.

*Mr. A. Ashcombe, Ashcombe House, Hollowmere.*

Tamsin sat very still. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere upstairs Ruth's floorboards creaked, and then went quiet, as if the house itself were holding its breath.

Her mother had written to Augustus Ashcombe—Verity's late husband, dead fifteen years now, the patriarch whose portrait still hung in the town hall with its eyes following everyone who walked beneath it. And she had never sent a single letter.

Tamsin untied the ribbon. Her hands were shaking, and she noted that clinically, coroner-fashion, the way she'd noted her own pulse spiking at the reservoir: *subject experiencing tachycardia, pallor, tremor of the extremities. Cause of distress: pending.*

The topmost envelope was soft with age. Inside, the paper had yellowed, and the ink had faded to brown, but her mother's handwriting was unmistakable—slanted, careful, the handwriting of a woman who'd been a secretary before she'd been a widow and who believed that penmanship was character made visible.

*Mr. Ashcombe,*

*I have thought for a long time about whether to write this letter. I have decided not to send it, but I find I cannot decide not to write it, and so I will keep writing them until the need goes out of me like a coal.*

*I saw what happened at the quarry in the summer of '74. You know what I mean, because you looked at me across the churchyard on Sunday and you knew that I knew, and I looked back, and you understood that too. That is why I am writing.*

*I am not asking for anything. That will seem strange to you. Everyone asks you for something. I am telling you instead: I have a daughter. Her name is Tamsin. She is four years old. She has her father's chin and my stubbornness, God help us both. I am not going to tell you what I saw because telling you would be the same as threatening you, and I am not threatening you. I am only telling you that I am choosing to keep it, the way one keeps a coal in the ash, and that you should not test whether it still burns.*

*I will not speak. I want you to know that my silence is a choice and not a weakness, and that it is for her. Everything I do now is for her.*

*Respectfully,*
*Edith Voss*

Tamsin read it twice. The kitchen light hummed. The word *quarry* sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She knew the quarry. Everyone in Hollowmere knew the quarry—it had been the Ashcombe fortune's first root, before the timber, before the bank, before the town itself had grown up around the family like ivy around a wall. It had been closed in 1974, the same year. Closed suddenly, the records said, for *structural concerns*, and two men had lost their jobs, and one of them—the one who'd complained loudest about the safety conditions, the one whose name Tamsin had seen in an old newspaper clipping Ruth had spread across her desk two weeks ago, in the growing archive of the town's rot—had drowned in the reservoir the following spring. *Accidental.* The coroner at the time had been a man named Pruitt, and Pruitt had signed everything Pruitt was asked to sign, and then Pruitt had retired to Florida on a pension nobody could quite explain, and the quarry had stayed closed forever.

*Witnesses to Ashcombe scandals, all officially accidental.* Ruth had said it like a diagnosis. Fifty years of the same disease.

And now here was the cure's opposite: here was proof that the silence wasn't new. That it wasn't even, in its way, a corruption. That it was a tradition. That her mother had stood in this same kitchen—these same walls, though the wallpaper had been different then, yellow roses—and had weighed the same scales Tamsin had weighed three years ago with a bleeding man in her examination room, and had chosen the same thing Tamsin had chosen, and had chosen it *for her.*

Tamsin set the letter down on the floor with the care one gives evidence and untied the rest.

* * *

There were nine letters. She read them all, in order, the dates marching from 1974 to 1979, five years of unsent correspondence, and the story they told was the story of a siege.

The second letter was angrier. In it her mother wrote about the quarry man's widow, about how she'd brought her a casserole and sat with her and listened to her say *he was a strong swimmer, Mrs. Voss, he was a strong swimmer* until the words stopped meaning anything, and how she'd come home and written to Augustus Ashcombe: *I know what the water was for. I know what the water has always been for in this town. You did not invent it. But you are keeping it full.*

The third letter was quieter. The fourth was almost friendly, which was the most frightening thing in the whole tin—a letter in which her mother described running into Verity Ashcombe at the market, Verity with her new baby, Dorian's father, fat and red-faced in the papoose, and how Verity had said *Edith, we should have you to the house* and how her mother had written: *I understood, standing there among the vegetables, that the invitation was the same thing as the look across the churchyard. She was not inviting me to dinner. She was inviting me to be one of hers, or to understand what happens to people who are not. I smiled. I said perhaps someday. I have been smiling for five years, Mr. Ashcombe. I am very good at it now. My daughter thinks I am a cheerful woman.*

The fifth, sixth, and seventh letters tracked the slow calcification of that smile into something permanent. Her mother wrote about taking the job at the county records office—*you arranged it, I know you arranged it, and I took it, because it is steady and because it puts me in the room with the paper, and I have decided that if I must be silent I will at least be silent in the archive, where I can see everything and be seen by no one.* Tamsin read that sentence three times. *Silent in the archive.* Her mother had spent twenty-two years as the deputy registrar of Hollowmere County, a gray, unremarkable woman behind a gray, unremarkable desk, and Tamsin had never once wondered what she'd seen from there. What documents had passed under her hands. What she'd noticed and filed away in the coal-ash of herself.

The eighth letter was short. *My daughter is nine. She wants to be a doctor. She says she wants to help people, and when she says it her whole face changes, and I think: God forgive me, I have raised her to walk toward the hurt, and I have taught her nothing about what this town does to people who see it. I have decided that is the next thing I must do. Not tell her. Teach her to look without being looked at. It is the only inheritance I have that is worth anything, and I hate myself for it, and I will do it anyway.*

The ninth letter was dated 1979 and was the last. In it, her mother wrote: *I am done writing to you. Not because the need has gone out of me, but because I understand now that these letters were never for you. You will never read them. They are for me. They are how I keep the coal without letting it burn the house down. And I understand something else, which is that you have won—not because I have surrendered, but because I have built a whole life inside the silence, a good life, a real life, and my daughter is happy, and I find that I no longer wish to burn anything down that would take the house with it. This is what you do, you Ashcombes. You don't destroy people. You just make the silence comfortable enough to live in. I am living in it. It is warm in here. God forgive me, it is warm.*

The ribbon lay coiled on the floor like something shed.

* * *

Ruth found her there an hour later. Tamsin didn't hear her come down—Ruth moved quietly for a big woman, a habit from years of walking into rooms where things were wrong—and when Tamsin finally registered her, Ruth was standing in the kitchen doorway with two mugs of coffee, looking at the boxes, the tin, the letters fanned across the linoleum like a hand of cards nobody wanted to play.

"Found something," Tamsin said. Her voice came out sanded flat.

Ruth set the mugs on the counter, crouched down with a grunt, and read. She read for a long time. Ruth was a fast reader—she'd burned through the county archives in a week—but she read these letters slowly, the way you'd handle something fragile at a scene, and when she finished the last one she sat back on her heels and was quiet for a moment.

"She knew," Ruth said finally. "About the quarry. About—"

"About all of it, probably. She worked the records office for twenty years, Ruth. She was *in* the archive. She saw what I've been seeing for three weeks, and she saw it forty years earlier, with better eyes." Tamsin picked up the ninth letter, the last one, and looked at the warm-in-here sentence again. "And she chose it. She chose it and she made her peace with it and she raised me inside it."

"And then you became a coroner."

"And then I became a coroner." Tamsin laughed, and it came out wrong, a dry crack with nothing in it. "She must have died knowing. Eleven years ago. I'd been in the job four years by then. I'd already signed things for the Ashcombes—little things, then. Timing of death on a hunting accident. A blood alcohol report that got *adjusted* before trial. Nothing like—" She stopped. *Nothing like Elian.* The name wouldn't come out. It hadn't been coming out all week.

"You think she knew what you'd do," Ruth said. Not a question. Ruth didn't ask questions she'd already answered.

"I think she trained me for it." Tamsin pressed her thumb against the edge of the letter, hard enough to blanch the nail. "Look at the eighth one. *Teach her to look without being looked at.* That's what she did. That's exactly what she did. She taught me to be invisible in plain sight, to be the quiet competent woman in the room that nobody watches, to do the work and keep the face. And then when Elian was bleeding on my table and I had ninety seconds to decide who I was going to be—" Her voice caught. She pressed on. "I didn't decide anything. I just did what I was raised to do. The inheritance kicked in. I looked without being looked at, and I filed him among the dead, and I told myself it was mercy, and it *was* mercy, but it was also—" She gestured at the tin. "It was this. It was her kitchen. It was the coal in the ash. It's been in my family for fifty years, Ruth. The Voss women keep the town's secrets the way other families pass down china. And now somebody's put my name on the Ashcombes' guest list like I'm being formally welcomed into the collection."

Ruth took the ninth letter from her hand and read the last paragraph again, her lips moving slightly. Then she said, in the careful voice she used for families at viewings: "Your mother kept these for thirty years. Tied them with a bow. She could have burned them. People burn things all the time—easier, safer. She kept them."

"I noticed."

"You know what I think that means?"

"Tell me."

"I think part of her wanted them found. Not by Ashcombe. By *you.* Someday. When it was safe, or when it was too late, or when you'd need them." Ruth set the letter down and looked at Tamsin straight on, and there was something in her face Tamsin had rarely seen there—Ruth was the steadiest person Tamsin knew, and right now she looked almost frightened, and not for herself. "Tamsin. The photographs in Verity's study. The folder with your name on it. They've had you on a leash for three years, and now the drowned man's shown up wearing your cardigan and somebody's moving the furniture in your house, and you've been asking yourself all week what they know about you." She tapped the tin. "The question underneath the question—the one you're not asking—is whether your mother's silence and your silence are the same silence. Whether they own you the same way they owned her."

"They didn't own her," Tamsin said. "She said so. She said her silence was a choice."

"She said a lot of things to a man who was never going to read them." Ruth's voice was gentle and merciless, the way it always was. "She wrote nine letters to a dead man's mailbox and then she went to work and she smiled and she died, and the only person who ever knew she was at war is sitting on this floor. That's not freedom, Tamsin. That's a very well-kept grave."

Tamsin didn't answer. The honest light had moved across the kitchen while they talked, and now it lay in a long pale bar across the letters, across her mother's careful slanted handwriting, across the word *quarry* and the word *daughter* and the word *warm*, and Tamsin looked at her hands—her mother's hands, she realized, the same knuckles, the same flat strong nails, the same faint scar on the left index finger from a can lid in 1980—and understood that she had spent three years believing she'd invented her complicity, that it had been born in a single terrible night with a bleeding man and a blank certificate, that it was hers alone, original sin, her own private crime.

It wasn't. It had never been. It was a family heirloom, polished and passed down, and her mother had wrapped it in love the way you wrap anything fragile, and Tamsin had carried it her whole life without once looking at what was inside the box.

"I keep thinking about Mara," she said at last. Her voice was steadier now. Something had settled in her, the way sediment settles. "Mara Marsh, living under a new name, taking money orders from a brother who's supposed to be dead. She's carrying the same thing. The whole town's carrying it. Generations of it. *Silent in the archive, warm in the silence.*" She looked at the tin. "Verity Ashcombe didn't build this. Verity just maintains it. Like a lighthouse keeper."

"Then somebody's about to put her light out," Ruth said. "That's what the drowned man's doing. That's what the photograph on the far shore was. He's not trying to expose you, Tamsin. He's trying to burn the lighthouse down, and he doesn't much care who's standing inside it when it goes."

Tamsin gathered the letters, all nine, and instead of returning them to the tin she squared them and set them on the kitchen table, in the open, where the light would find them in the morning. Then, on impulse, she picked up her phone and photographed each one—top to bottom, front and back, her mother's handwriting rendered in cold digital blue. Evidence, some part of her said. History, said another. Inheritance, said a third, and she told that third part to shut up.

It was while she was photographing the fifth letter—the one about the records office, *silent in the archive, seen by no one*—that she noticed what she'd missed on the first four readings. On the back of the envelope, in the corner where a return address would go, her mother had written something small, in pencil, so faint it was nearly an impression in the paper rather than a mark on it. Tamsin tilted it toward the window. Held her breath, the way she did over bodies, the way she did when the thing she was about to see would change the paperwork forever.

Her mother had written a date. Not a letter date—a different date, later, in a shakier hand. And beside it, three words:

*He answered once.*

Tamsin turned the envelope over. The front was addressed to Augustus Ashcombe, Ashcombe House, Hollowmere—she'd read it a dozen times. But beneath the address, in the same late pencil, her mother had added a single line, and the line was not about Augustus at all.

*Verity read them. Verity knows I kept them. Verity keeps everything.*

Tamsin stood very still in her kitchen while the light died out of it.

She thought about a locked study no servant entered. She thought about a fresh folder with her own name on it, photographs of herself three years younger, forging a certificate in a room she'd believed was private. She thought: *they keep everything. It's how they own you. It's how they've always owned this town—not with money, not even with fear. With archives. With the certainty that somewhere in that house is a folder with your mother's handwriting in it, and your name, and every coal you ever kept.*

"Ruth," she said quietly. "How soon can we get a look at Verity Ashcombe's power grid?"

Ruth looked up from the letter in her hand. "Why?"

"Because," Tamsin said, "I want to know when the next storm is coming—and I want to be standing in that study when the lights go out."
