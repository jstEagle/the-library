## 21. Sentencing

The courthouse in Carrow County had a smell Tamsin had never noticed before she started spending her Tuesdays there: old paper and floor wax and something faintly sweet, like apples going soft in a cellar. She'd sat through eleven days of testimony in that smell. She'd listened to her own voice played back from a recording device on the state investigator's table—*I forged the certificate. I signed it. I knew the coffin was empty*—and heard how flat it sounded, how much like someone reading a grocery list. Grief and guilt, it turned out, both sounded like inventory.

On the morning of sentencing, frost silvered the courthouse windows and the radiators clanked like something trying to get out.

Elian Marsh wore a gray suit that fit him wrong. He'd lost weight in the county jail—not the dramatic wasting of the cellar months, but a settling, as if his body had finally agreed to stop bracing. His hair was longer than she remembered from before any of it, curling at the collar, and when he was led in he found her in the second row the way a compass finds north: instantly, without searching.

Tamsin didn't look away. That was part of the deal she'd made with herself, the one no lawyer had drawn up. Whatever happened in this room, she would watch all of it with her eyes open. She had spent three years keeping hers half-closed. It was half-closed eyes that let Hollowmere rot for fifty years.

Judge Okafor—a woman in her sixties with reading glasses on a beaded chain and a voice like a door closing gently—read the charges without ornamentation. Manslaughter. Unlawful disposal of human remains. Obstruction. The name of the dead man was read aloud too, because Ruth had insisted on it, had driven two towns over to find the drifter's estranged daughter and bring her to sit in the front row with a tissue folded small in her fist. His name was Callum Reeve. He was forty-four years old. He had liked fishing and had one photograph of himself smiling, taken by a stranger, which was somehow the saddest fact of the whole proceedings.

"Mr. Marsh," Judge Okafor said, "do you wish to address the court?"

He stood. The chains on his wrists made a sound like wind chimes in a hard gust.

"I do." His voice carried better than Tamsin expected. Jail had given it gravel. "I want to say first what I did to Callum Reeve's family. There's nothing I can build that replaces what I broke. I took a man who was dying and I turned his death into a tool, and I told myself that made it different, and it didn't. It made me a man who tells himself stories." He paused. His hands, cuffed in front of him, were very still. "I also want to say that Verity Ashcombe killed my sister. Not quickly. Not with her hands. But she did it, and the town helped her do it by not looking, and I want the record to show that I looked. That's all I ever wanted anyone in that room to do."

Tamsin felt Mara's hand find hers in the dark under the bench. Mara's grip was cold and certain, and Tamsin held on.

The sentence came down like weather: twelve years, reduced to seven for cooperation and for the documented pattern of Ashcombe crimes—the Empty Coffin Society, Ruth called it, though she'd only said it once, quietly, over bad coffee, and never again. Three deaths across fifty years, each one accidental on paper, each one with an irregularity only a coroner would catch. The state had finally caught them too. Verity Ashcombe, surviving her fall against her own display case with a shattered hip and a face rebuilt with pins, would be tried separately in the spring. Dorian was gone—Cayman Islands, someone said; Lisbon, someone else said—and the Ashcombe house on Merrow Hill stood empty behind its iron gates, windows dark, waiting for whatever towns do with houses like that.

Seven years. Elian would be fifty-one when he walked out.

As the bailiffs led him away, he turned at the door and looked at Tamsin one more time, and mouthed something she couldn't read—or chose not to. Some sentences you have to write yourself.

* * *

Her own hearing was shorter. Plea deal: obstruction and falsification of records, badge suspended rather than revoked, eighteen months of community service, most of it, in a gesture the county clearly found either poetic or punitive, at the Carrow County morgue assisting the regional coroner—an elderly, meticulous man named Dr. Fenwick who pronounced bodies with the unhurried courtesy of a man greeting neighbors.

"You're the one from Hollowmere," he said on her first day, not unkindly, sliding a clipboard across the steel table. "The one who wrote the certificate."

"Yes."

"Mm." He'd considered her over his glasses. "Then you know exactly where every mistake lives. Start with the intake forms. We'll see if you've learned anything."

She had learned everything. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. Every shortcut she'd ever taken, every signature she'd signed on faith or friendship or fear, had come due with interest, and now she catalogued the dead of Carrow County with a precision that bordered on penance. She verified. She double-verified. She photographed everything twice. When Fenwick asked her why, she said, "Because someone might need the truth later," and he nodded as if that were the only acceptable answer, which it was.

Ruth kept her badge. Ruth had followed her into the cellar, had put herself between Tamsin and a loaded gun, and the county knew a hero when it needed one. But Ruth wore it differently now—she'd said so herself, standing in Tamsin's kitchen the week after the hearings, turning her shield over in her palm like a coin she wasn't sure was still currency. "It used to mean I belonged here," she'd said. "Now it means I stayed anyway. Different thing."

The town watched them both, but differently now. That was the phrase Mara used, and Mara, who had grown up in Hollowmere watching people watch her family, knew the grammar of being observed. Before, they'd been invisible—Tamsin the quiet coroner, the Marshes the poor family nobody invited. Now they were visible in a new way: pointed at, discussed in the bakery line, thanked by strangers who couldn't quite meet their eyes. A woman stopped Tamsin outside the hardware store and pressed her hand and said, "My brother worked for them, years back. Nobody ever asked him anything." Then she hurried off as if she'd broken some rule.

Hollowmere was learning to look. It squinted. It flinched. But it was looking.

* * *

Mara came on a Thursday in late autumn, the first cold snap, breath fogging in the doorway of Tamsin's house—the same house that had been rearranged by careful hands a lifetime ago, whose photos all faced forward again, whose chair sat exactly where chairs should sit.

"I brought soup," Mara said, holding up a container like evidence. "It's not a bribe. It's just soup."

"It's always soup with you."

"It's always soup because soup is honest." She came in, hung her coat on the hook that had become hers without discussion, and went to the stove with the ease of someone who had done this twenty times, which she had.

They ate at the small table by the window. Outside, the reservoir lay flat and pewter-colored, holding its secrets with better manners these days. Mara talked about work—she managed a dental office two towns over, under the name she'd chosen, though lately she'd begun using Marsh again, testing it in her mouth like water. She talked about the trial coming in spring, about whether she'd testify, about the letter she'd gotten from Callum Reeve's daughter, who had written *I don't forgive him but I understand him and I hate that I understand him*, and Mara had read it forty times.

"Do you forgive him?" Tamsin asked.

Mara stirred her soup long after it needed stirring. "I forgave him in the cellar," she said. "Before I even knew what he'd done. I saw his face and my body decided before I did." She set down the spoon. "That's the thing nobody tells you about love. It doesn't wait for the facts. So then you spend the rest of your life deciding whether to overrule it." She looked up. "You didn't overrule yours. You arrested him with your whole heart breaking. I watched you do it."

"He asked me to take his full statement. All of it. Even my part."

"I know. He told me. He said it was the only gift anyone ever gave him that cost the giver something real."

Tamsin's throat tightened. She drank tea she didn't taste.

Afterward they washed dishes side by side, shoulders touching, and Mara said, casually, terribly, "I got a letter from the prison. He wants visits. Family can request the list." A pause, water running. "I said yes. I wanted to ask—" She shut off the tap. "Would you come? Not every time. Just. Sometimes."

Tamsin dried her hands slowly. Through the window the reservoir held the last of the light, and somewhere under that pewter surface, in the town's memory and the county's files and her own permanent record, a drowned man in a borrowed cardigan was still teaching everyone who'd look what looking costs.

"Every time," Tamsin said. "Not sometimes. Every time."

Mara smiled, and it was Elian's smile, remade gentler, and something in Tamsin's chest that had been clenched since a Tuesday in September loosened one notch—not all the way, maybe never all the way, but enough.

* * *

Dr. Fenwick retired in December. On his last day he handed Tamsin the intake clipboard and said, "Keep it. You'll want the paperwork straight when they offer you the position." And they did offer it—regional coroner, provisional, contingent on the tail of her community service, contingent on the spring trial going the way trials go. She signed her name carefully, letter by letter, the way she now signed everything.

That evening she drove out past the reservoir to the shore where, in another season, a tourist's camera had caught a figure who wanted to be almost-seen. The light was going gold and long. Ruth was already there, leaning against her truck, and Mara's car pulled in behind hers, and the three of them stood in the cold looking at water that was only water.

"Some portraits refuse to sit still," Ruth said, reading Tamsin's thoughts the way she always did, and Tamsin laughed—for the first time in months, really laughed—because the next chapter of Hollowmere was already stirring: the summons on her desk that morning, Verity Ashcombe's trial date set, and a witness list with a new name added at the bottom, someone claiming to have seen a man matching Elian Marsh's description on Merrow Hill the night the lights failed at the dinner.

Some stories, it seemed, weren't finished with any of them yet.
