## 13. Fire in the Stacks

Paloma Kavanagh had slept in the back room of Kavanagh & Sons for three nights running, and he would have been embarrassed to admit why.

It wasn't fear, he told himself. A man who had spent thirty years walking into rooms where bad things had happened did not frighten easily in his own. It was the diary. Nell Ashcombe's diary, wrapped now in a pillowcase and tucked under his mattress like contraband, radiating its small stubborn heat through the cotton. He kept telling himself he'd take it to a safe-deposit box at the bank. He kept not doing it, because the bank closed at four and the records office closed at five and by the time he remembered, the fog had come down over Verity Hollow the way it always did, thick as wet wool, and the walk home from anywhere felt like wading through cold soup.

So he stayed. He made himself tea on the hot plate, ate a tin of sardines over the sink because Marta would have scolded him, and read the diary again by lamplight until the handwriting swam.

The shop around him creaked its old complaints. The building was older than the town's pretensions — his grandfather had bought it from a chandler, and the shelves still smelled faintly of tar if you knew where to put your nose. Paloma knew every sound it made. The settling groan at midnight. The drip in the corner of the stockroom that no plumber could find. The way the front door sighed on its hinges when the wind came off the harbor.

Which was why, at some hour past two in the morning, when the door sighed and no wind had come, he opened his eyes and lay very still.

He heard footsteps. Soft ones. Then nothing — a long nothing, the kind of silence that has effort in it.

Paloma reached for the lamp and thought better of it. He slid out of bed in his socks, took the poker from beside the cold stove, and moved down the corridor between the stacks with the particular care of a man who knows exactly which floorboards betray him. He had catalogued those boards the way he'd once catalogued suspects: *third plank from the poetry section, sings; the one under the atlas case, silent as a judge.*

The shop was dark. The front door stood open six inches, fog curling in across the welcome mat like something being poured.

On the glass, taped at eye level, was an envelope.

He stood there a long moment, poker raised at nobody, feeling foolish and cold and wide awake. Then he took the envelope, locked the door, and carried it back to the lamp.

Inside was a single sheet of paper, typed, in the same flat bureaucratic voice as the funeral card:

*Some books are better left shelved.*

No signature. Beneath the typing, pressed hard enough to emboss, someone had drawn a small circle with a line through it — the same mark that had been chalked on the vault door at the cemetery, he realized now, though he hadn't understood it then. He turned the paper over. Nothing. He held it to the lamp. Nothing.

"Charming," he said aloud, to the empty shop, because saying things aloud to empty shops was one of the privileges of living alone that nobody warned you about.

He should have gone to Tomás that night. He knew that later, in the way you know all the roads not taken once the bridge is behind you. But Tomás had been strange for a week — evasive, apologetic in a way that didn't fit him, answering questions with questions — and Paloma, who trusted almost no one and had just learned he could trust even less than that, decided to sit on it until morning.

Morning never got the chance.

* * *

He woke to the smell.

Not smoke, not yet — smoke is polite, it announces itself. This was the smell underneath smoke, the hot-tar-and-burnt-paper smell of a fire already eating, and it came pouring through the gap under his door like a tide.

Paloma came off the mattress with the diary clutched to his chest before his mind finished the sentence his body had started. He shoved his feet into his boots without lacing them, grabbed the wool coat off its hook, and pulled the back-room door open onto a wall of orange.

The front of the shop was burning. Not smoldering — *burning*, properly, greedily, the flames standing up out of the history section like a congregation risen from their pews. Someone had done it right: accelerant, poured low along the aisles so the fire would climb the shelves shelf by shelf, book by book, a hundred years of paper turned to kindling in the space of a match.

He had perhaps ninety seconds. Maybe less.

Paloma did what any sensible man would do, which is nothing sensible at all. He went back for the ledgers.

They were in the stockroom, in the crate he'd hauled from the railway station — Merrick's accounts, the smuggled labor contracts, the whole rotten financial skeleton of the town's founding legend. He heaved the crate up, felt his back announce its formal objection, and went out the back door into the alley as the front windows blew inward with a sound like the sky clearing its throat.

He stood in the alley in his unlaced boots with a crate of dead men's sins in his arms and watched his life burn.

That's the thing nobody tells you about a fire. It isn't dramatic from outside. It's almost quiet, at first — a rushing, a roaring, but muffled, private, as if the building were trying to keep its dying to itself. The flames came up through the roof and the fog took them and bent them sideways, and the light of it turned the whole street the color of the inside of a shell. Somewhere a dog began to bark. Somewhere a bell — the volunteer brigade, slow as ever, bless them — began its ragged clanging up from the harbor road.

People came. Of course they came. In Verity Hollow, a fire at three in the morning is the best entertainment since the flood of '61, and half the town arrived in nightclothes and galoshes to stand in the wet street and watch Kavanagh & Sons go up like a birthday cake.

Marta Osei got there before the brigade did, hair under a scarf, still buttoning her coat wrong. She took one look at Paloma — soot-faced, bootlaces dragging, clutching a crate — and steered him by the elbow to her bakery cart at the corner, where she pressed a cup of terrible vending-machine coffee into his hands because it was the only hot thing she had.

"You slept in the shop," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I've been sleeping in the shop."

"For how long?"

"A few nights."

Marta looked at the fire, then at him, and her face did something complicated. "You knew," she said quietly. "You knew somebody might come for you."

"I knew somebody had left me a note."

"And you *stayed?*" She said it the way she said *you put salt in the dough* — with the full authority of a woman whose judgments were final and correct. "Paloma Kavanagh, you are the most intelligent fool I have ever fed."

He couldn't argue. He drank the coffee. It tasted like burnt pennies, or possibly like burnt everything; the whole street tasted of it now.

By dawn the shop was a black ribcage against the gray sky. The brigade had saved the neighboring buildings and nothing else. Paloma walked the perimeter while the last steam rose, and Fire Chief Aldana — a broad, patient woman who had known him since he was a boy stealing penny candies from the counter her mother used to run — fell into step beside him.

"Accelerant," she said, before he asked. "Kerosene, maybe, poured along the baseboards. Front door wasn't forced." She glanced at him. "Your lock's fine, Paloma. Whoever came in had a key or you left it open."

"I locked it. I heard the door after I locked it." He said it flatly, and watched her decide whether to believe him. She decided to, mostly, because Aldana was fair that way. "Someone let themselves in, Chief. With a key."

"Mm." She looked at the ruin. "You know, your grandfather sold my mother her first cash register. Off this very floor." She said it gently, the way people hand you small weights to see if you can carry them. "I'm sorry, Paloma."

"So am I," he said, and meant about forty different things.

* * *

It was mid-morning, gray light and the smell of char, when he found the boot print.

The rain had come at seven — a thin, apologetic drizzle that did nothing for the ashes and everything for the mud in the alley behind the shop. Paloma had gone out the back door a dozen times that morning, ferrying the salvaged crate, the diary, the typed note, his own scorched coat, into Marta's dry storeroom next door. On one of those trips, on impulse, he stopped and looked down.

There, pressed into the soft ground beside the back steps, was a boot print. Deep — a man's weight, planted, waiting. The tread was distinctive: a heavy work boot with a diagonal slash pattern across the heel, the kind issued by the constabulary's supply order, the kind half the town bought secondhand at Ferro's surplus shop.

But Paloma had spent thirty years reading prints the way other men read headlines, and this one told him more than its brand. It told him the man had stood here, facing the door, for some time. It told him he'd shifted his weight twice, heel to toe, the fidget of a man working up his nerve. And it told him — because the left print was deeper than the right, and slightly rolled at the outer edge — that the man favored his left leg.

Tomás Reyes favored his left leg. An old injury from the winter Paloma had gotten him his badge, a fall on the reservoir ice that Tomás laughed about and never had properly seen to.

Paloma stood in the drizzle looking at that print for a long time.

"No," he said finally, out loud, to the mud. "No, that's not — he wouldn't."

But the mud didn't argue, and neither did the evidence, and Paloma Kavanagh had built a career on the uncomfortable principle that evidence doesn't care whom you love.

He thought of the week of strangeness. The evasive answers. The way Tomás had looked at him over their beers at the Anchor — not guilty, exactly, but *loaded*, like a man carrying something breakable he couldn't set down. The oblique warning, delivered in that awful code drunk men think is subtle: *"Watch your back room, viejo. Some doors only lock from the inside."*

And the spare pair of boots. Tomás kept a spare pair in his locker at the station — everyone knew it, it was a running joke, the constable with two bad pairs of boots and one good pair he saved for Sundays. If someone had borrowed them —

If someone had *lent* them —

Paloma crouched, careful not to disturb the print, and photographed it with the cheap camera he kept in his coat for exactly these moments, the moments when the world handed you something fragile and dared you to hold it right.

The fire had taken nearly everything. Forty years of stock. His grandfather's counter. The first-edition shelf he'd never actually sold anything from because he liked looking at it. The armchair by the window where he'd sat every evening for twenty-two years after closing, reading, listening to the foghorn, believing — with the simple unexamined faith of a man in his own house — that the walls around him were the safest walls in Verity Hollow.

And yet the diary had survived. The ledgers had survived. Because he'd slept there, because he'd been paranoid enough to keep the one thing that mattered within reach of his own two hands.

Whoever set the fire hadn't known he was inside. That was worth noting. That was worth holding onto. Arson to destroy evidence, yes — but not murder, or not planned murder. His attacker had wanted the diary ash and had settled, badly, for a burned bookshop.

Unless the fire was also a message. *We can reach you while you sleep.*

Paloma straightened up. His knees cracked. Down the alley, the drizzle kept falling on the ashes, and somewhere beyond the wet rooftops the foghorn sounded its long patient note, the same note it had sounded the night Nell Ashcombe drowned on paper, the same note it would sound long after every one of them was dust.

He gathered the crate, tucked the diary deeper under his coat, and started toward Marta's storeroom — and stopped.

Because there, propped against the salvage pile where he hadn't left it, was a fresh copy of that morning's *Verity Hollow Courier*. Someone had folded it to page three and weighted it with a brick.

The headline: **CENTENNIAL CEREMONY PROCEEDS AS PLANNED, SAYS MAYOR HALE.**

And beneath it, circled twice in red grease pencil, a single sentence from the mayor's quoted remarks: *"Verity Hollow does not live in the past. We honor it, we close the book, and we move forward together."*

Close the book.

Paloma looked at the burned shell of his shop, then at the newspaper, then at the boot print filling slowly with rain.

"All right," he said softly, to the mayor, to the arsonist, to the whole quiet conspiracy of a town that had spent forty years treading water over a drowned truth. "You've had your quiet hour."

He picked up the brick, weighed it in his hand, and set it down gently on the mud — directly on top of the boot print, preserving it, sealing it like a signature on a confession he intended, very soon, to make someone answer for.

Then he went to find Tomás Reyes, and God help them both, he went to find the truth about whose boots had stood in his alley in the dark — because the fire had taken his shelves, his chair, his grandfather's counter, everything except the one thing Paloma Kavanagh had never been able to burn out of himself:

the itch of an unopened case, and now, sharper than the itch, colder than the fog —

the certainty that someone he trusted had stood watch while it burned.
