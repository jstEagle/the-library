## 20. Reinterment

The rain had stopped by morning, which the town took as a personal favor from Aldous Merrick himself, and by nine o'clock Verity Hollow was dressed in its funeral best. Bunting went up along Harbor Street — black bunting, then, at the council's last-minute correction, a compromise gray that looked like the town couldn't decide whether it was mourning or celebrating, which, Paloma thought, watching them string it from his shop window with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand, was exactly right.

The bookshop smelled of smoke and new paint. He'd repainted the back wall himself, badly, in a color the hardware store called "Antique Ivory" but which dried closer to the yellow of old newspaper. The shelves Tomás Reyes had helped him rebuild stood empty on one side, waiting for stock he hadn't ordered yet. On the other side, the survivors: three hundred books rescued from the fire, their spines smelling faintly of char, like they'd all been to the same campfire and come back with stories.

He touched the counter where the diary had sat through the worst of it, wrapped in his coat in the back room while the front of his life burned.

"You're not going," said the kettle, or rather, said nothing at all, but he heard the question anyway. He'd been talking to his kitchen appliances since the fire. Tomás said this was normal. Tomás said a lot of things were normal lately, in the tone of a man reassuring himself.

Paloma locked the door behind him anyway. Of course he was going. He'd spent forty years believing Verity Hollow's founding story because it was easier than checking the arithmetic, and today the town was going to say some of it out loud, and he intended to stand there and hear every word.

* * *

The cemetery had never held so many people. They came down from the hills and up from the harbor, and the ones who couldn't walk were driven in trucks and parked along the lane like patient livestock. Paloma recognized faces he'd known his whole life — Mrs. Okonkwo from the bakery, the Delgado twins now grown into two identical retired schoolteachers, old Henrik Sørensen who claimed to have been at the first centennial planning meeting and possibly the second. The whole town, gathered around an open grave that contained, as yet, nothing but air and expectation.

The stones were there, though. Someone — Beatrice Voss, Paloma suspected, though she'd have denied it under oath — had washed them and arranged them in a wicker basket lined with white cloth, so that the river stones from Merrick's coffin looked almost ceremonial, almost intentional, instead of what they were: forty years of evidence that the founder of Verity Hollow had never lain in his own grave.

Mayor Corliss Hale was not present to see it. Mayor Corliss Hale was in the county jail, having been arrested at six-forty the previous evening on the courthouse steps, where he'd gone, according to Tomás, to make a statement about "coordinated misinformation." The statement had lasted four seconds before the deputies reached him. His aide, Daniel Pruitt, had lasted somewhat longer — long enough, over the lighthouse radio broadcast, for half the town to hear him explain, in a voice like a man reading his own obituary, how Tobias Grange had drowned in 1984 and how the records clerk Wendell Marsh had drowned last month, both times because paper was more dangerous than water to men who understood paper.

Pruitt had confessed to both drownings. Hale had confessed to nothing, which, Paloma reflected, taking his place near the back of the crowd, was at least consistent. A man who could arrange a false death certificate, a false conviction, and a false coffin could certainly arrange his own innocence. Some people built their whole lives out of that material.

The judge's order had come through yesterday afternoon: the posthumous vacatur of Tobias Grange's conviction. Forty years after a jury had sent an innocent man to prison for a murder committed by the town's beloved founder, a judge in a nearly empty courtroom had signed a piece of paper saying, in effect, *we were wrong.* Paloma had read it twice. It was admirably brief. Courts, when they finally apologized, did it in as few words as possible, as if apology were a fee charged by the word.

Tobias Grange had died in prison in 1991. There was no one left to hand the paper to. That was the thing Paloma kept circling and failing to land on: justice, delivered this late, had no address. You could vacate a conviction; you couldn't vacate the years. Somewhere in the county archive there was a man's whole life filed under a case number, and today the town would try to take it back out of the file, and it wouldn't fit anymore. It never fits. That's what nobody tells you about exonerations. The man you're returning the life to has already spent it.

* * *

The ceremony began at eleven. Father Anselm — recovered from his faint at the exhumation, though he still paused slightly before the word "resurrection" in any context — opened with a prayer that managed, through careful phrasing, to ask forgiveness without specifying for what, which the crowd received gratefully, since specificity was the day's hardest currency.

Then Beatrice Voss stepped up to the microphone.

She wore her good coat, the charcoal one, and she stood very straight, the way she'd stood through every council meeting Paloma had ever attended, composure worn like armor polished daily. But he'd seen her in the records office two weeks ago, shame breaking through her like water through a dam, and he knew now what the straightness cost.

"My name is Beatrice Voss," she said. "I am the great-granddaughter of Edmund Voss, who helped found this town, and I am here to read a statement approved by the town council."

A pause. Wind off the harbor moved the gray bunting.

"Forty years ago, Tobias Grange was convicted of the murder of his business partner, Aldous Merrick. This conviction was wrong. Aldous Merrick was not murdered. Aldous Merrick killed Tobias Grange, and fled this town, and the leading families of Verity Hollow — my family among them — chose to protect his name rather than tell the truth."

The crowd made a sound Paloma had never heard a crowd make — not a gasp, not a murmur, something lower, collective, like the ground itself exhaling.

"We falsified documents. We paid for silence. We allowed an innocent man to be imprisoned, and we allowed the woman who witnessed the truth, Nell Ashcombe, to be erased from our records and hunted from our memory. We did this in the name of the town's good name, which is to say, we did it for ourselves." Her voice held. Only her hands, folded on the paper, betrayed anything: they were trembling, finely, like tuning forks. "There is no version of this apology that is adequate. We offer it anyway. To the family of Tobias Grange. To Nell Ashcombe, wherever she is. And to this town, which deserved better stewards than us."

She stepped back. For a moment nobody moved. Then Mrs. Okonkwo began to clap — two hands, slowly, deliberately — and the sound spread through the crowd the way fire had spread through his bookshop, except this fire warmed.

Paloma clapped too. He noticed his own hands weren't entirely steady either.

* * *

They buried the stones at noon.

It was decided — by acclamation, more or less, the way decisions happen in towns this size, which is to say someone suggested it and everyone else failed to object loudly enough — that the river stones should be reinterred with full honors, since they were, after all, the only honest thing the original coffin had ever contained. The gravediggers lowered the basket on ropes. Father Anselm said words about how God weighs hearts, not reputations, and Paloma watched the stones go down into the earth and thought: *forty years, and the truth weighed less than rocks, and rocks were enough.*

Henrik Sørensen wept openly and told anyone within earshot that he'd always had his doubts. Nobody believed him. Everybody let him have it anyway. Grief, Paloma had learned, makes room for small vanities.

Tomás stood beside him, arm in a sling from the shoulder wound, refusing all assistance with the dignity of a man who had been shot shielding a witness and intended to milk it for approximately the rest of his natural life. "You know what the worst part is?" Tomás said quietly, watching the grave. "I grew up on the Merrick story. Founder's Day every year. The pageant. I played young Aldous in the fourth grade. Had the little hat and everything."

"I remember," Paloma said. "You forgot your line and introduced yourself to the audience."

"Twice." Tomás smiled, then it faded. "You think the town will actually change? Or will we just get better at the story?"

Paloma considered the crowd dispersing toward the harbor, where the council had — with a courage that surprised him — voted to rename Founder's Day. The new name hadn't been chosen yet. There was talk of simply calling it Truth Day, which everyone agreed sounded like a government program, and talk of naming it for Tobias Grange, which everyone agreed felt right and also sad, and the argument itself, Paloma thought, was the healthiest thing Verity Hollow had done in a century. Towns that argued about their own names were towns that had stopped lying to themselves.

"Both," he said. "We'll change and we'll get better at the story. That's what towns do. What matters is whether somebody checks the arithmetic next time."

"Is that a detective thing to say?"

"It's a bookseller thing to say. Detectives check arithmetic. Booksellers keep the receipts."

* * *

The crowd thinned through the afternoon. By four, the cemetery held only groundskeepers and wind, and Paloma lingered the way he always lingered at endings, professionally unable to leave until he'd checked the room.

That was when he saw her.

Back row, past the last of the folding chairs, half in the shadow of the yew tree that had been old when Merrick was young. A small woman in a gray cardigan, silver hair pinned plainly, standing with the stillness of someone who had practiced being unnoticed for forty years and gotten very good at it. She wasn't hiding, exactly. Hiding was for people who feared being seen. She was simply declining to be named.

Their eyes met across the grass.

Nell Ashcombe didn't wave. She didn't smile. She gave him a single nod — the nod of one professional acknowledging another's work, of a witness confirming testimony — and Paloma returned it, and that was the entire conversation, and it was enough. The town had apologized to her by name, aloud, into a microphone, and she had come to hear it, and she had chosen to remain unnamed while hearing it, and both of those things were true and neither canceled the other.

*Absolution incomplete,* he thought. *But begun.*

She turned and walked away through the yew shadow, unhurried, and was simply not there anymore, the way she had been not-there for forty years, and Paloma found he wasn't worried about her. She had survived Merrick, the verdict, the town, the mayor's men, and the rising tide at Cutter's Point. Whatever came next for Nell Ashcombe, she'd meet it on her own terms, at her own hour, and probably with better posture than anyone else involved.

He stayed a few minutes longer at the fresh grave. The headstone would come later; the council was arguing about the inscription, another healthy argument. For now a temporary marker read only: TOBIAS GRANGE. INNOCENT. RESTORED.

Two words doing the work of a paragraph, Paloma thought. Restored, as if restoration were possible. As if you could give a man back 1979 through 1991. Still — the stone was honest, which was more than most stones in this cemetery could claim, and honesty, he was learning, compounds. Slowly, like interest. Like forgiveness.

He walked home through the blue end of the day, past the harbor, past the renamed-day banners, past Mrs. Okonkwo's window where she was already baking something celebratory, the smell following him halfway up the hill.

The bookshop waited with its one wall of bad paint and its smell of smoke and possibility. He unlocked the door, flipped the sign, and stood a moment in the quiet, listening to the building settle around him like an old dog lying down.

Tomorrow he'd order stock. Tomorrow he'd shelve the survivors and hang a sign in the window — he'd been drafting it in his head for days, turning the words over like river stones. Tomorrow the town would begin the long, unglamorous work of being sorry properly.

Tonight, there was just the shop, and the evening light coming through clean glass, and the sense — unfamiliar, suspicious, but persistent — that a case was closed.

He was reaching for the light switch when he saw the envelope.

It lay on the counter, square and sealed, where no envelope had been that morning. No note, no name, no postage. Just cream-colored paper, heavy stock, addressed in a fine, slanted hand:

*For P. Kavanagh — regarding a matter that is not yet a matter of record.*

Paloma picked it up. It was heavier than paper alone should be.

He smiled despite himself — the itch, the old hunger, already stirring like a body surfacing in water — and turned the lock behind him.
