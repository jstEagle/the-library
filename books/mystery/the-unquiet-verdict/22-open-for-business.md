## 22. Open for Business

The smell of fresh pine is, in Paloma Kavanagh's professional opinion, the smell of a lie being told well.

New shelves, new boards, new joists — all of it smelling of sawmills and optimism, laid over the char that still ghosts the cellar stairs if you lean close. He had argued for keeping the scorch marks on the doorframe. *Character*, he'd said. *Provenance*. The carpenter, a broad-shouldered woman named Odette who had rebuilt half of Verity Hollow after the flood of '89 and did not suffer fools, had looked at him the way one looks at a man who wants to keep a stain on his wedding shirt.

"Character," she'd said, "burns down. Pine holds." And she'd installed the new frame anyway, though she'd left one beam in the back room dark and scarred, because Paloma had stood under it for a long time one afternoon and she was not, in the end, a cruel woman.

So the shop smelled of pine and, faintly, of the lemon oil Paloma applied to the old shelves that had survived, and — if the morning was damp, as most mornings in Verity Hollow were — of the sea, which came up the hill like a debt collector, patient and certain of eventual payment.

The hand-lettered sign had taken him four tries.

CLOSED CASES CONSULTED, it read, in his own careful capitals, the letters slightly uneven because his hand had never fully recovered its steadiness since the night of the fire, or possibly since the night of the rocks at Cutter's Point, or possibly since 1979, when a young detective had taken a witness at her word and built a reputation on a verdict that was a beautiful, load-bearing lie. He'd tried other phrasings. *Investigations Undertaken* sounded like a plumber's van. *The Truth, Reasonably Priced* had made Tomás laugh so hard he'd had to sit down on a crate of unsold Agatha Christies. In the end, plain was better. Plain was honest. Plain was, at last, what Paloma was aiming for in all things, having spent sixty-eight years learning that the ornate version of any sentence, like the ornate version of any crime, was usually hiding something.

He had opened the door at nine. It was now eleven, and exactly one person had come in.

* * *

The one person was Mrs. Fenwick, who came in every day regardless of what the shop was or wasn't, because Mrs. Fenwick believed that commerce was a form of prayer and that her attendance constituted a tithe. She bought nothing, inspected everything, and today paused before the small display Paloma had assembled near the register: a copy of *The Unquiet Verdict of Verity Hollow* — no, he thought, that wasn't right either, the town had never gotten a book, only a plaque, and the plaque was still being engraved, the engraver having died mid-commission, which Paloma felt was the most Verity Hollow thing that had ever happened.

What was actually on the display was simpler. A framed photograph of a young man in a prison photograph, grainy and hollow-eyed: Daniel Okafor, convicted of a drowning he did not commit, dead eleven years in a prison infirmary, exonerated this autumn by a posthumous order that had taken the county four months and two resignations to produce. Beneath the frame, a card in Paloma's handwriting: *He waited forty years. We can wait to sell books.*

Mrs. Fenwick looked at it for a long moment.

"You're not going to sell that frame," she said.

"No."

"It's not for sale."

"It's not for sale, Mrs. Fenwick."

She nodded, satisfied, as though this had been a commercial transaction successfully concluded, and left. The bell over the door — new bell, the old one had melted — gave its bright, unbroken voice.

Paloma straightened the photograph a degree it didn't need and went back to cataloguing.

* * *

Cataloguing was the old ritual, and he had missed it the way a man misses his own heartbeat only when it falters. Unsold stock, alphabetized by a system no one else had ever understood and no one ever would: mysteries shelved under M regardless of author, because Paloma had decided in 1987 that mystery was a genre of geography and not of writer. Local histories under H for *Hollow*, except the two that insisted on calling the town Verity, which lived under V and resented their neighbors. A whole shelf of books on tide charts and coastal navigation, purchased in a fever of research in October and now, he admitted, unlikely to move.

There was a new shelf, though, that Odette had built to his exact specifications and that no customer had yet been permitted to touch. It stood behind the counter, within reach of his chair. On it: a tin box containing Nell Ashcombe's diary, tide-chart marginalia and all, now formally logged as evidence and formally returned to the finder, which was a legal fiction so comfortable that Paloma had decided not to examine its stitching. On it: a copy of the ledgers Elias Voss had mailed him, along with the note Elias had included — *I'm sorry it took a fire. I'm sorry it took me this long. — E.V.* — which Paloma had answered with a note of his own, and which had begun a correspondence of four letters now, each shorter than the last, each, Paloma suspected, the beginning of something. On it: a jar of river stones, one for each year of Daniel Okafor's sentence, collected by schoolchildren the week after the apology was read, because Verity Hollow's grief, when it finally arrived, arrived in the hands of its children, which was the only way grief that old could arrive at all.

And on it, in a small wooden case that had once held a doctor's instruments: Tomás's spare pair of boots.

Not the boots themselves. Paloma had returned those, along with an apology that had taken him three drafts and that Tomás had waved off before he'd finished the first sentence. Just the laces, coiled neatly, which Tomás had sent back with a note: *So you keep remembering that trust has two ends.* Paloma had looked at the laces for a long time. Then he'd put them on the shelf, because Tomás was right, and because a man who had spent his life collecting evidence against other people could stand to curate a little evidence against himself.

The bell rang again at half past two. Paloma looked up with his cataloguing pencil still in his teeth.

It was Beatrice Voss.

* * *

She looked smaller than she had in October. Or perhaps not smaller — *lighter*, the way furniture looks lighter after you've carried it up the stairs and set it down. She wore a gray coat that had been expensive in a decade when expensive meant something, and she carried her cane like a woman who had recently decided, on principle, to stop pretending she didn't need it.

"Mr. Kavanagh." She surveyed the shop with the expression of a woman inspecting a rival's garden. "It smells like a lumberyard."

"It's honest lumber, Beatrice. It hasn't learned to lie yet."

A sound escaped her that, in a younger woman, would have been a laugh. She crossed to the counter, set down her cane with a click that had finality in it, and looked at the shelf behind him — the one with the tin box and the jar of stones and the coiled laces. She looked at it the way people look at a grave they once visited out of duty and now visit out of something else.

"Elias is coming to Sunday dinner," she said. "First time since—" She stopped. Started again. "He's bringing a girl. He's terrified. I intend to be gracious, and I intend to be gracious *loudly*, so that everyone in town hears how gracious I am, and I intend to keep being gracious until it stops being an act."

"That's how most virtues get built," Paloma said. "Like a shed. You put up the walls first and figure out what goes inside later."

"Mm." She turned her gaze on him. Her eyes were the same pale gray they'd been in the council chamber, but the flint in them had been ground down to something softer and, he thought, more durable. "I came to say something, and then I'm going to leave, because I find that if I stay anywhere longer than ten minutes people start being kind to me, and I haven't yet developed a tolerance."

"Say it."

"You could have ruined us. All of us. Me, the Hales, half the founding families, the whole rotten architecture." She said it plainly, the way one reads a weather report. "You had us, that night on the radio, and every night after. You could have spent the rest of your life collecting the debt. Instead you took the mayor, the aide, and the actual killing, and you let the rest of us do our penance in private, which is the only kind that works." She lifted her chin. "I don't forgive you. I don't need to. But I'm going to buy a book, and I'm going to pay full price, and I'm going to tell Mrs. Fenwick it was overpriced, because that's the only language this town respects."

She bought a local history — one of the ones shelved under V — and paid with exact change counted out of a coin purse, and at the door she paused.

"Nell sent me a postcard," she said, not turning around. "No return address. It just said *the light is on*." A beat. "I've decided to find that comforting."

The bell spoke. She was gone.

Paloma stood a moment with the pencil in his teeth, then took it out and wrote in the ledger, under *Beatrice Voss, one (1) local history, full price*: *Paid in full. All parties. At last.*

* * *

The weeks since the reinterment had been, by any objective measure, quiet, and Paloma had discovered that quiet was a country he no longer spoke the language of.

The town buried the stones from the coffin — Aldous Merrick's actual remains having never existed to bury, a sentence that still made the priest wince when he rehearsed it — beneath a new marker that read, at the council's grueling, three-meeting-long insistence: *ALDOUS MERRICK, FOUNDER. HIS TOWN REMEMBERS WHAT HE WOULD HAVE FORGOTTEN.* Paloma had voted against it in his capacity as a private citizen shouting at a public meeting, on the grounds that it was both too generous and too clever, and had been outvoted, and had privately conceded it was the most honest epitaph in the cemetery, which was not a high bar but was a bar.

The apology had been read aloud by the new mayor — a schoolteacher named Ibarra who had run unopposed after the arrest, on the platform of *I have never burned anything* — and Nell Ashcombe had stood at the back of the crowd in a gray coat with her collar up, and Paloma had been the only one who knew to look for her, and he had not told anyone, and she had known he wouldn't.

She'd come to the shop once, in the second week, before the shelves were finished. They had drunk tea out of chipped mugs among the sawdust, and she had looked at the scarred beam Odette had left standing, and she had said, "You kept the burn."

"Odette kept the burn. I just stood under it looking significant until she gave up."

"Hm." Nell had turned her mug in her hands. "I'm staying at the point. The light's mine now, legally — some maritime easement nobody's bothered to revoke since 1911. I'll keep it. Somebody should." She'd looked at him then with those unrepentant, unblinking eyes. "You could visit. Lighthouse keepers historically take in strays."

"I'll visit."

"You won't. You'll mean to. That's all right. I'll leave the light on anyway." She'd finished her tea, stood, and at the door had added, without turning: "The verdict kept me alive, Kavanagh. But you know what kept me *human*? Forty years of writing the truth in a diary nobody would ever read. Turns out the record matters even when it's secret. Especially then." And she'd gone, down the hill, into the fog, upright as a mast.

Paloma had thought about that a great deal. It was, he suspected, the closest thing to a blessing he would ever receive from the profession, and he intended to frame it eventually, once he'd finished being unsettled by it.

* * *

At four o'clock the fog came up the hill, as it did, and the shop went soft-edged and golden, and Paloma made himself tea in the back room, under the scarred beam, and allowed himself ten minutes of the newspaper. The front page was the engraver's death — *MEMORIAL PLAQUE DELAYED; COUNCIL CONSIDERS INTERIM SIGN* — and Paloma read it with the deep contentment of a man who has learned that a town's scandals, like its tides, are best observed from slightly above sea level.

He was on his second cup when the bell rang.

"Shop's open," he called, "though I warn you the stock is ninety percent unsold and the other ten percent is Mrs. Fenwick's opinion."

No answer. He came out through the curtain, drying his hands on his apron.

The shop was empty. The fog stood in the doorway like a customer who had lost its nerve.

But on the counter — on the counter, dead center, square to the register — sat a sealed envelope.

* * *

Paloma did not touch it for a full minute, and he was proud of that minute. Old habits: he circled it once, reading it from every angle. Plain white envelope, good stock, heavier than standard. Sealed with wax — actual wax, dark green, unmarked by any seal he recognized. No address on the front. On the back, in small, precise, upright capitals, a single line:

*FOR THE MAN WHO CONSULTS ON CLOSED CASES. THIS ONE ISN'T CLOSED. IT'S ONLY BEEN QUIET.*

He stood very still.

The last time an envelope had appeared in his life uninvited, it had contained Elias Voss's ledgers and had cost a bookshop. The time before that, a funeral card with his own name on it, pinned to this very door with a tack, and he could still, if he let himself, feel the exact cold of that tack going in.

But fear, he had learned, was like the fog: it came up the hill on schedule, and it never actually took the town, and a man who rearranged his life around it ended up owning nothing but his caution.

He checked the door — locked, he'd locked it at four, out of habit older than the shop. He checked the fog, which contained, as far as he could tell, only fog. Then he took the envelope to the counter, set it under the lamp, and turned it over one more time, and that was when he saw it, the detail he'd missed because his eyes had gone straight to the words:

The wax was not merely green. Pressed into it, faint, deliberate, was the impression of a small key.

He knew that key. He'd held it in October, in a widow's parlor, over a sugar bowl. Room Nine. The railway station locker that had held a dead woman's diary and a living woman's photograph.

But the station had been condemned. The lockers had been emptied and auctioned in November — he'd read the notice himself, had clipped it, had filed it under R for *Resolved*.

Paloma Kavanagh stood in his warm, pine-smelling, rebuilt shop, in his pool of lamplight, with the fog pressed against the glass like a face, and he felt the thing he had spent sixty-eight years refusing to feel and forty days successfully not feeling at all.

The hunger. The itch. The unopened case, sitting on his counter, addressed to him by someone who knew exactly what it was.

"Closed cases consulted," he said aloud, to the empty shop, and heard the laugh in his own voice and let it stay. "Well. Nobody said anything about *staying* closed."

He locked the door — properly, both bolts, though the fog had no hands — turned the sign, carried the envelope to the back room, and sat down under the scarred beam with the lamp pulled close and the tea gone cold and forgotten.

He broke the green wax with the impression of the key.

Inside was a single sheet, and on the sheet, in the same precise capitals, a photograph — no, a *copy* of a photograph, the kind made on a photocopier with the lid not quite down, so that the edges faded into gray. It showed a row of railway lockers, numbers seven through twelve, the paint peeling. Locker nine stood open. And standing beside it, caught mid-turn, face half in shadow but unmistakable to a man who had spent an autumn learning every angle of that face —

The photograph was of Nell Ashcombe. Taken, by the timestamp stamped in orange in the corner, *three days ago*.

Beneath it, one more line, in those small upright capitals, and Paloma read it twice, and then a third time, and then he set the page down very carefully on the scarred wood, the way one sets down something that might go off:

*THEY KNOW SHE'S ALIVE. FRIDAY'S BOAT WASN'T THE ONLY ONE. — A FRIEND OF ROOM NINE.*

Paloma sat in the lamplight for a long moment, listening to the fog press against the windows, feeling sixty-eight years old and entirely, dangerously awake.

Then he reached for a fresh sheet of paper, and began, in his own careful capitals, to make a list.
