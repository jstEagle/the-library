## 3. What the Tide Keeps

Marisol had known the dead man's face before the tide finished giving him back.

She had known it the way you know a scar in a family photograph — half-remembered, half-denied, always at the edge of the frame. Tomás Ferreira. That was the name her father had used, and her grandfather before him, though she suspected neither name was real, because men like Tomás were never real. They were vessels. They carried things that could not be spoken aloud in Port Isla and they carried them across the water at night, and in exchange they were allowed to keep breathing.

She had seen him on the Tuesday before the body came ashore, standing at the rail of the fish market with his collar turned up against the wind, wearing a coat she now understood had never belonged to him. She had turned away. She had made herself small behind a barrel of ice and counted to a hundred, the way her mother had taught her when she was a girl — *if you see one of them, mija, you see nothing* — and when she looked again the rail was empty and the gulls were screaming and she told herself the relief she felt was just the weather.

Then the tide brought him to Gull Point, and he was wearing Wendell's coat, and there was no more counting.

* * *

She told him on the fourth night, in the back room of the coroner's office, with the ledger page spread flat between them under a lamp that buzzed like a trapped insect. The cipher had begun to yield — Marisol's grandmother had taught her the old substitution, the one the founding families used for grocery accounts and worse — and the numbers had started becoming names, and the names had started becoming wounds.

"I recognized him," she said. It came out smaller than she intended. "Before. I saw him alive, and I said nothing, and I let you think he was a stranger to all of us."

Wendell did not look up from the page. His pen had stopped moving, though. She watched the stillness move through him like a tide change.

"Tomás Ferreira," she went on, because now that the dam had cracked she could not stop the water. "My family used his family. For generations. They carried messages, debts, sometimes people. When my mother ran from my father, it was a Ferreira who rowed her to the mainland. When my uncle needed to disappear —" She stopped. "They were how Port Isla said the unsayable. And when I saw him here, wearing your coat, I knew it was a delivery. I knew someone had sent him to you. And I was afraid, Wendell. Not for you. For me. For what it would mean that the Quinteros had been part of this machinery for a hundred years."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere in the building, a pipe knocked twice, like a fist on a door.

"Say the unsayable now," Wendell said quietly.

She told him. All of it. The couriers, the routes, the little brass token her grandmother had kept sewn into her own coat lining — a token Marisol still had, in a tin, under her bed, because she had never been able to throw away anything that proved her family had been complicit in something she could not name. Wendell listened the way he did autopsies: completely, without flinching, taking each fact and laying it out with the others until the body of the truth lay whole on the table.

When she finished, he reached across the page of ciphered numbers and took her hand.

"You're the only person who ever saw me," he said. "I mean that the way it sounds. Before you, I was a name on a door. You looked at me and there was someone inside." His thumb moved over her knuckles. "Whatever your family carried, you carried me out of it. That's the only ledger I care about."

She wanted to believe it was that simple. She had loved him long enough to know that nothing in Port Isla was that simple.

* * *

The summons came the next morning, delivered by a boy on a bicycle who would not meet her eyes — a cream-colored envelope, hand-lettered, smelling faintly of some old-fashioned floral water, the kind of stationery that had outlived the empire that produced it.

*Mr. Wendell Villalobos is requested at Delacruz House. Four o'clock. Come alone. — O.D.*

Marisol read it three times. "Odile Delacruz doesn't request," she said. "She summons. My grandmother used to say the whole town was on a string, and Odile held the spool."

"I'm going."

"Wendell —"

"I'm going," he said again, and there was something in his voice she had never heard before, something that was not the mild, careful coroner she had first met over a body at Gull Point. "She has my history in her hands. Whatever I was before I was Wendell Villalobos — she can give it back or she can bury it deeper. Either way, I want to hear it from her mouth."

"Then I'm coming."

"Come alone," he said, almost gently, quoting the card. "If something happens, someone has to be outside it. Someone the string doesn't reach." He touched her face. "You're the only one it doesn't reach. Don't let it."

She let him go, and hated herself for it, and stood at her window until his truck was a speck on the coast road, and then she did the one thing she could do: she took the brass token from the tin under her bed and sewed it into the lining of her own coat, where her grandmother's had lived, and waited.

* * *

He came back at dusk, and she knew before he reached the door that something had broken in him — not badly, not the way bone breaks, but the way ice breaks on the harbor in late winter: a long groan, and then the whole surface is different forever.

He sat at her kitchen table and told her about Delacruz House — the dark rooms, the smell of camellias and salt rot, the old woman in the wheelchair by the window with the sea at her back like a held breath. He told her about the offer. His true name. His true parents. The circumstances of a birth forty years ago that Odile Delacruz had purchased and buried and kept, all this time, like a pressed flower in a book no one was allowed to open.

"She wants the page," he said. "The one from the coat lining. In exchange, I get to know who I was."

"And what did you tell her?"

"I told her I already know who I am." He said it steadily, but his hands were not steady, and Marisol took them in hers and held them flat against the table until they stilled. "I told her a name you buy is just a receipt. I told her the man she wants erased doesn't exist. He never did. There was only ever a boy, and a woman who bought his silence, and a town that agreed to forget."

"She'll burn it," Marisol said. "The page. Maybe more."

"Maybe more," Wendell agreed.

They did not sleep. They sat in her kitchen with the lamp turned low and the tide audible in the dark, coming and going, keeping its own ledger, and somewhere past midnight Wendell said, "If they come for the office, everything's in there. The decoded pages. My notes. The photograph of the stitching." He looked at her. "We could move it tonight."

"We could leave it," Marisol said. "And let it burn, and be free of it."

"And let the Ferreira boy stay a stranger forever? Let whoever comes next get disappeared as cleanly as I almost was?" He shook his head. "No. I didn't survive forty years of a borrowed name to hand the eraser back to them."

So they went, the two of them, through streets that smelled of low tide and woodsmoke, and they carried the pages out of the coroner's office in a fishing satchel, and Marisol locked the door behind them with her grandmother's key, which still fit, because in Port Isla everything fit if it was old enough.

* * *

She woke to the color orange.

It was coming through her curtains at three in the morning, the wrong color for that hour, the wrong color entirely, and she was out of bed and at the window before her mind caught up, and there it was: the coroner's office, burning. Flames stood out of the roofline like something being born rather than destroyed, and the smoke went straight up into the windless dark in a black column, and the heat of it reached her face across two streets.

Wendell was already beside her — she hadn't heard him move, hadn't heard anything over the sound of her own heart — and his arm came around her, and they stood together at the glass and watched forty years of a town's careful forgetting go up into the sky.

"The pages," she whispered. "Tell me the pages."

"In the satchel. Under your floorboards. They're safe." His voice was calm, almost wondering. "They burned my files, Marisol. My case notes. The photographs. Everything that proved how I work, what I found." He was quiet a moment. "They didn't burn a building. They burned my credibility. By morning, I'll be the coroner who lost his records in a fire, and no one will have to kill me at all."

Marisol looked at the fire, and at the man beside her, and felt the fear in her chest do something strange: it hardened. All her life she had counted to a hundred and made herself small and seen nothing. Her family had rowed the unsayable across the water for a hundred years, and it had bought them nothing but the privilege of silence.

"They made a mistake," she said.

Wendell turned to her.

"They think the ledger was the proof." She was already dressing, her hands steady now, her grandmother's token hard against her ribs where she had sewn it. "But the proof was never paper, Wendell. It's a tide chart. It's the date the Ferreira boy arrived. It's the fact that Odile Delacruz summoned you the day after we cracked the cipher, and her office knows it, and her driver knows it, and drivers talk." She looked up at him, and the firelight was in both their faces through the glass. "The tide keeps everything. It just waits for low water to give it back."

Below, the fire bells had finally begun to ring, and the whole town was waking, and Marisol took Wendell's hand.

"Low tide is at dawn," she said. "We should be on the flats when it turns."
