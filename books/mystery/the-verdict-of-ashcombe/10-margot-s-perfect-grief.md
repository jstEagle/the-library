## 10. Margot's Perfect Grief

The invitation arrived on cream card stock, hand-lettered in a script so controlled it might have been typeset.

*Margot Vane requests the pleasure of Mr. Casper Montrose's company for luncheon at Ashcombe House, Saturday next, one o'clock.*

There was no question mark anywhere in it. There was no question in it at all. It was not an invitation; it was a summons dressed in silk, and we both knew it. I had been asking questions about the Vane family for three weeks — quietly, in the way of concierges, through ledgers and registries and the soft interrogation of old men who remembered too much — and Margot Vane had decided it was time to look me in the eye.

I considered declining. I considered it for exactly as long as it took to understand that declining would tell her more than accepting ever could. A man who refuses to enter the house is a man who fears what's inside it. A man who accepts lunch is merely hungry.

I wrote my acceptance in my neatest hand and posted it before I could reconsider.

* * *

Ashcombe House sat above the town like a dowager who had stopped pretending to be poor. Grey stone, slate roof, chimneys like raised fingers counting the generations it had outlived. The drive curved twice before reaching the front steps — designed, I suspected, so that visitors would have time to compose themselves, and so that anyone watching from an upstairs window would have time to decide what kind of visitor they were about to receive.

A housekeeper opened the door before I knocked. She was perhaps sixty, with the flat, careful expression of someone who has worked in a great house long enough to know that the safest thing to see is nothing.

"Mr. Montrose. Mrs. Vane is expecting you."

She led me through a hall paneled in dark oak, past portraits of men whose jaws suggested they had never once been contradicted, into a morning room that caught the southern light. And there was Margot Vane, standing at the window with her back half-turned, wearing dove grey and a smile that arrived a full second before she did.

"Mr. Montrose." Her voice was low, warm, unhurried. "Thank you for coming. I've been wanting to meet you properly for some time. Everyone in this town speaks of you."

"Do they?"

"Oh, endlessly. *The man who notices everything.*" She said it lightly, as one quotes a nickname, but her eyes stayed on mine while she said it, and I understood that the phrase had been delivered with intent. "Eleanor adored you, you know. She used to say the Grand Meridian was lucky to have a concierge who read people the way other men read newspapers."

"Eleanor was generous."

"Eleanor," said Margot, gesturing me toward a chair, "was many things. Sit, please. Lunch will be a few minutes yet, and I thought we might talk first. Woman to — well." A small laugh. "Man who notices everything."

I sat. The chair was deeper than it looked, angled slightly away from hers, so that to hold a conversation one had to turn — and turning meant presenting one's profile, off-balance, unguarded. I had seated difficult guests in that exact configuration myself, once or twice, when I wanted them to feel slightly at sea without knowing why. It was a hotel trick. It was, specifically, *my* trick.

I turned my chair six inches toward her as I settled, neutralizing the angle, and watched her register the correction. Something flickered behind her composure — not annoyance. Interest. The look of a chess player whose opponent has declined the opening gambit she'd spent years perfecting.

"You have a lovely home," I said.

"It's Julian's, technically. Was Eleanor's. Will be —" she waved a hand, a small elegant gesture that dismissed the entire apparatus of inheritance, "— settled, eventually. Estates are like icebergs, Mr. Montrose. What you see above the waterline is never the trouble."

"No," I agreed. "It rarely is."

* * *

Luncheon was cold poached salmon, new potatoes, a white wine from a vineyard whose name I recognized because the Grand Meridian had stocked it until 2011, when the importer folded. Margot ate very little and drank less, though she refilled my glass twice with the practiced grace of a woman who understood that hospitality is also a form of surveillance: watch what a man reaches for, watch how much he takes, watch whether his eyes go to the door when he thinks you aren't looking.

I kept my eyes exactly where she could see them, and let her draw her own conclusions about a man who drinks moderately and looks at nothing twice.

Except that I looked at everything twice. That was rather the point of me.

The morning room walls held photographs — dozens of them, arranged with the studied casualness that takes hours to achieve. Family groups on lawns. Boating pictures. A christening. And every single one of them, I realized within four minutes, had been cropped or angled or hung in such a way that certain faces were lost. A shoulder where a head should be. A figure at the frame's edge, cut neatly in half by the mat. In the largest photograph — a garden party, perhaps fifteen years ago, everyone in summer whites — there was a gap between two laughing women where a third person had clearly stood. The grass beneath the gap was unshadowed. Someone had been removed not by scissors but by light, or by a print made from a negative that had been doctored, which is a slower and far more loving form of erasure.

"You're admiring our family history," Margot observed. It was not quite a question.

"The photographs are wonderful."

"Aren't they? Though I confess I've never been much of a keeper of them. Photos are how the past refuses to stay past, don't you find? One keeps them thinking one is preserving memory, and all the while memory is preserving evidence." She smiled at her own cleverness, and I smiled back, and across the width of the tea table we acknowledged each other in the only language available to us: two people complimenting each other with knives.

"I noticed some of them are cropped," I said mildly. "Fashion of the era, I suppose."

"Fashion," she agreed, "or tidiness. Families accumulate people the way houses accumulate furniture. Every so often one rearranges."

"And the pieces that no longer fit?"

"Go to the attic." She lifted her glass. "Or the lake."

I did not flinch. I had prepared not to flinch for two days, rehearsing in the mirror of my bathroom like an actor before a difficult scene, and the preparation held — barely. She saw the effort anyway. I could tell she saw it, because her smile deepened by a single degree, the way a duelist's does when the first blade touches cloth.

* * *

After lunch she walked me through the house. This was not hospitality; this was a tour of her defenses, conducted with the confidence of someone who has nothing left to hide because everything hidden has already been buried deep enough.

We passed the library, where Julian's father's books stood in regimented rows, spines uncracked. We passed the music room, its piano closed, its sheet music yellowing under dust covers. We passed a corridor of family portraits, and here Margot slowed, and here I earned the chapter's most valuable observation.

"That's Thomas," she said, stopping before a portrait of a lean man in his thirties with dark hair and an expression of pleasant, unfocused charm. "Julian's uncle. He drowned before most of the staff were born. You may have heard."

"He died at the lake house. 1994. It was in the papers."

"It was in the papers," she echoed softly. "Everything important always is."

I studied the portrait longer than courtesy required, and she allowed me to, and while I studied it I listened — because behind us, at the end of the corridor, the elderly housekeeper had appeared with a tray of coffee she had no reason to carry, and she had stopped moving the moment Margot began speaking, and she was not looking at the portrait. She was looking at the floor. The posture of a woman who has been instructed, explicitly and recently, that certain names are not to be mentioned in this house, and who is therefore holding herself rigidly still whenever those names come near the air.

Three weeks ago, Mrs. Okonkwo had told me — in the steam room of the Grand Meridian, where conversations go to be deniable — that the Vane servants had been given their instructions after Eleanor's funeral. *Nothing about 1994. Nothing about the lake. Nothing about the uncle.* Okonkwo had heard it secondhand from a girl who cleaned at Ashcombe House on Thursdays. I had filed it as rumor.

It was not rumor. It was policy, and the policy had a face, and the face was smiling at me over a portrait of a drowned man.

"He was handsome," I offered.

"He was charming," Margot corrected. "Handsome is a face. Charming is a weapon. Thomas never met a room he couldn't talk his way out of." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "Or into."

* * *

We ended in the garden, because she wanted me to see the garden, and I wanted her to think I didn't know why. Roses past their season. A sundial green with age. And beyond a yew hedge, glimpsed and then deliberately not-glimpsed, the path down toward the lake.

"Your ring," I said, as we walked. "May I ask about it? My mother wore one similar."

Margot extended her left hand without hesitation, turning it so the stone caught the light — a sapphire, old-cut, in a setting thirty years out of fashion. "Thomas's mother's. Eleanor gave it to me when I married into the family. She said the women of Ashcombe House wear their history on their hands."

"It's beautiful. Though I notice you wear it on your right hand."

The words were out before I had fully decided to spend them, and the moment they landed I understood I had made an error — or that I had made it deliberately, which amounts to the same thing in a duel.

Margot looked at her own hand as though noticing it for the first time. "Do I? How observant of you." She slid it across to the fourth finger of her left hand, where a widow's ring belongs, and the motion was smooth, unbothered, entirely without haste. "Force of habit. I wore it on the right for years, you see, before I was a widow. Old habits are the last things to die, Mr. Montrose. They outlive marriages, reputations, sometimes even—" she paused, selecting the word with visible pleasure, "—husbands."

But here was the thing, the small sharp thing I carried out of Ashcombe House like a splinter under the skin: county records show Thomas Ashcombe never married. No license. No registry entry. No banns. The woman who called herself his widow had produced a wedding that existed nowhere but in her own telling of it — and now she wore the proof of that wedding on whichever hand suited the day's audience.

Unless, of course, the marriage had been real and simply never recorded, which happens, occasionally, in families with lawyers and reasons.

And unless the ring itself was honest, which I doubted. I had spent twenty-two years handling guests' jewelry — logging it, guarding it, returning it — and I knew the weight of true age in gold. That sapphire's setting was old. The band beneath it was not. The band had been sized recently, and resized again, and the newest sizing was smaller than the one before it. Rings shrink when they change hands. Or when they change stories.

* * *

At the door, she gave me both her hands, the sapphire now properly widowed on the left, and held them a moment longer than farewell requires.

"You'll come again, I hope. Eleanor always said the Grand Meridian and Ashcombe House were the two hearts of this town. It seems foolish for the hearts not to know each other."

"Your hospitality is memorable, Mrs. Vane."

"I'm told I give good grief," she said, and laughed — a real laugh, sudden and unguarded, and somehow that laugh frightened me more than anything else in the house, because for one unguarded instant the charm dropped away and what looked out at me was not grief at all, nor even guilt, but something patient. Something that had been waiting thirty years and could wait thirty more.

Then the charm returned like a lamp being relit, and she released my hands.

"One more thing, Mr. Montrose." She spoke casually, watching the drive rather than me. "You've been asking questions around town. About 1994, about Thomas, about poor Bram Hodge and his conscience. I don't mind — truly, I don't. Questions are how towns digest their past." She turned, and her eyes were kind, and that was the worst part. "But I'd hate for you to hurt yourself digging where the ground is unstable. Eleanor is dead. Thomas is dead. Whatever the letters say, whatever the ledgers say — the dead are very good at staying dead in Ashcombe. We keep them that way. It's practically a civic service."

"And if one of them didn't stay dead?"

The kindness held. It held perfectly, the way glass holds — transparent, and one degree from shattering.

"Then I should want to meet him," she said. "Wouldn't you?"

* * *

The walk down the drive took eleven minutes. I counted them because counting steadied me, and because somewhere around minute seven I admitted what I had known since the morning room: she had invited me not to warn me off, but to measure me. To learn how much I knew by watching what I looked at. The photographs. The portrait. The ring. The servant frozen in the corridor.

I had given her a catalogue of my suspicions, gift-wrapped in courtesy.

And she had given me one thing back, whether she meant to or not. As the housekeeper had shown me out, I had glanced back down the portrait corridor — a concierge's habit, checking the room behind him — and seen Margot standing before Thomas's painting, motionless, her hand rising to touch the frame the way one touches a wound to confirm it still hurts.

Not the gesture of a woman looking at a stranger's picture.

The gesture of a woman keeping an appointment with a ghost she had personally introduced to the water.

That evening, back at the Grand Meridian, I pulled the 1994 guest registry from the archive room and laid it open beside the newspaper clippings, and I wrote in my private notebook, in shorthand only I could read:

*She knows I know. She doesn't know how much. Next move is hers — unless I make it first.*

The notebook went into the drawer. The drawer locked with a sound like a small verdict.

And then the telephone rang — the front desk, after midnight, which is the hour when telephones carry either emergencies or confessions — and the night porter's voice said, in the careful tone he saved for strange things:

"Mr. Montrose. There's a gentleman in the lobby asking for you by name. Says he's just driven up from London. Says—" a pause, the sound of a hand covering the receiver, "—says his name is Ashcombe."
