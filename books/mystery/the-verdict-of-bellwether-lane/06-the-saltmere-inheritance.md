## 6. The Saltmere Inheritance

The office of Pemberly & Vane had not, in its working life, been distinguished by anything so vulgar as ambition. It sat above the chandlery on Saltmere's high street, behind a door whose brass plate had oxidized to the color of old tea, and it smelled — when Elodie pushed it open on Thursday morning — of pipe smoke, law reports, and forty years of patient, unglamorous paper.

"Miss Amberdale." The man rising from behind a desk the size of a small dinghy was seventy if he was a day, with a face like a well-read map and a cardigan that had clearly outlasted several governments. "Gilbert Vane. Retired. Emphatically retired. I agreed to see you because Ivy Fenwick's mother once owed me six pounds and I never forgave her for it."

"That seems like a sound foundation for an interview."

"It's the only kind I have left." He waved her into a chair that groaned like a ship's timbers. "Brandy?"

"At ten in the morning?"

Vane looked at her over his spectacles with genuine pity. "My dear, you're investigating a drowning in a village where everyone swears everyone else is dead. It's past ten somewhere."

He poured two measures from a bottle that had, by the look of its dust, been waiting years for exactly this conversation. Elodie accepted hers. When in Saltmere, she was learning, one did as Saltmere did — which was mostly to say less than one knew and drink more than one should.

"I'll be direct," she said. "Tobias Fenwick drowned on Tuesday. Before he died, he'd been asking questions about the Bellwether estate. About a trust."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes — not surprise, precisely. More the weary recognition of a man who has been waiting decades for a knock at the door and is almost relieved when it finally comes.

"Did he now," said Vane.

"He did. And then he washed up wearing my clothes, which is a separate mystery I haven't the stomach for before lunch. So I want you to tell me about the trust. All of it. Because I've spent three days asking polite questions and receiving answers shaped like wet sand, and I'm told you're the last man in this village who can't lie convincingly anymore."

Vane laughed — a dry, rusted-hinge laugh — and lowered himself back into his chair.

"You've been talking to Mrs. Tallow at the bakery," he said. "She's right. Lying requires practice, and practice requires motive, and I lost all my motives around 2003." He sipped his brandy. "Very well. But we're going to do this properly. Fetch me that ledger, would you? The green one. Third shelf. Mind the spider; she's senior staff."

* * *

The green ledger was older than Elodie's career, its spine cracked, its pages soft as cloth. Vane opened it with the tenderness of a man handling a family Bible and turned to a page marked with a ribbon gone pink with age.

"The Bellwether Trust," he said. "Established 1911 by Edmund Bellwether, shipping magnate, self-made, insufferable, dead. He had four children and one great terror: that his fortune would be scattered to the winds by heirs who married badly, gambled stupidly, or died inconveniently. So he did what wealthy frightened men do. He hired a solicitor." He tapped his own chest modestly. "Well. My predecessor's predecessor. Same desk. Same spider's grandmother, probably."

Elodie leaned forward despite herself. There was something about ledgers — the way they made human folly legible.

"The terms were simple and vicious," Vane went on. "The whole estate — the house, the land, the investments, and by now a truly embarrassing sum of money — is held in trust until the *fixed date*. That's the legal term, 'the fixed date,' and it means what it says: a single day, written into the deed, on which the trust dissolves and whatever remains is divided among the surviving heirs. Equally. To the penny."

"And the fixed date is...?"

"March the first. Next year." He let that sit. "Eleven months away. After a hundred and fourteen years of waiting."

"One heir's death would redirect everything," Elodie said slowly. "You mean the shares don't pass down. If an heir dies before the date—"

"—their portion doesn't go to their children or their widow. It reverts. It's redistributed among the *surviving* heirs. Edmund Bellwether wanted to punish anyone who died early, which tells you most of what you need to know about him as a father. Dying, in his scheme, was a form of disobedience." Vane turned a page. "There are provisions, of course. Accidents are forgiven — deaths by misadventure don't trigger forfeiture, only redistribution. But redistribution, Miss Amberdale, is the whole game. Every death before March the first makes the survivors richer. Considerably richer. One heir dying might double another's take, depending how many are left."

"How many are left?"

Vane ran a blunt finger down the ledger's columns, names in faded iron-gall ink, some struck through with the quiet finality of a man crossing off groceries.

"Edmund had four children. Two lines have died out entirely — no issue, no claimants. That leaves two branches. The Fenwicks, descended through the second son. And the Quills, descended through the youngest daughter." He looked up. "I imagine you've heard the name Quill recently."

"Maren Quill," Elodie said. "Certified dead since 1998."

"Certified dead," Vane agreed, "and therefore holding no share. A dead heir's portion reverts to the living. Which means—" he drew a slow circle in the air with his brandy glass, "—as of 1998, there are exactly two living heirs. Tobias Fenwick. And whoever stands to inherit through him."

"Ivy."

"Ivy," said Vane. "By marriage, but marriage into a line counts under the deed — Edmund was nothing if not generous to spouses, having been one himself and found the experience profitable." He closed the ledger gently. "So you understand the arithmetic. If Tobias Fenwick dies before March the first, the entire Bellwether fortune — house, land, investments, the lot — passes to one person. His widow. Entirely. Unconditionally. On a fixed date eleven months away."

Elodie set down her glass. Her hand was steady, which surprised her, because inside she could feel the case shifting beneath her feet the way the tidal flats shifted — solid ground becoming channel, channel becoming ground.

"Fenwick drowned on Tuesday," she said.

"Yes."

"Three days ago."

"Four, if you count properly. Tide time runs differently here."

"And his widow inherits everything in eleven months."

"If she survives to collect," Vane said mildly. "Which, given the terms of the deed, is rather the point of the exercise. Edmund built a machine for making widows rich and heirs careful. For a century it mostly made lawyers comfortable." He refilled both glasses without asking. "And then, seven years ago, someone nearly drowned Tobias Fenwick, and the insurance company declined to pay, and I remember thinking at the time — I drafted a letter of support for his appeal, as it happens, never sent it — I remember thinking that a man who has almost drowned once, in a village where drowning pays, ought to move inland."

Elodie stared at him. "You knew about the near-drowning?"

"Everyone knew. Maren Quill pulled him out of the water herself, they said. Down by the flats. Saved his life with her own two hands, and then eighteen months later she was dead, and he married Ivy within the year, and the village decided that was grief moving quickly rather than anything worth examining. Villages are efficient that way. We bury things faster than the churchyard does."

He said it lightly, but Elodie heard the weight underneath — the practiced lightness of a man who had watched things get buried and kept his mouth shut because keeping his mouth shut was, professionally speaking, the job.

"Mr. Vane," she said carefully. "When Maren Quill died — you would have seen the paperwork. Death certificates cross a solicitor's desk."

"They do."

"Did hers cross yours?"

The pause was long enough that the spider on the third shelf seemed to hold its breath.

"No," Vane said at last. "It didn't come through this office. Which was itself unusual. In a village this size, everything crosses this office — births, marriages, deaths, disputes over fence posts, the lot. A Quill death certificate arriving from outside..." He shook his head. "I asked about it once. Politely. I was told the family preferred discretion. And then I was paid a retainer I hadn't earned, for services described only as 'ongoing,' and I understood that the question had been answered more thoroughly than my asking deserved."

"Paid by whom?"

"By the estate. By the trustees. By whoever holds the purse strings of a fortune waiting for March the first." He met her eyes over the rim of his glass. "Miss Amberdale, I took the money. I want that on the record, since you're the sort of person who keeps records. I was sixty-one, my knees were going, and the alternative was asking louder questions in a village where loud questions have historically been followed by quiet funerals. I'm not proud of it. But I am, at least, honest about my cowardice, which puts me ahead of most of my profession."

* * *

They talked for another hour. Vane produced documents the way other men produced photographs of grandchildren — the original deed, brittle and foxed; a codicil from 1954; a letter from Edmund Bellwether to his solicitor that read, in full, *"Ensure none of them profit from dying. Except the ones who deserve to. You will know which."* (Elodie read it twice and decided Edmund Bellwether sounded like a man who had been a delight at Christmas.)

Piece by piece, the shape of it assembled itself in her mind, and it was not the shape of a puzzle. It was the shape of a trap that had been springing, slowly, for a hundred years.

A fortune. A fixed date. A rule that rewarded death among the living. A witness pulled from the water who had saved a man's life and then become a corpse herself. A widow who grieved with suspicious precision. And now a drowned man wearing an investigator's clothes, as though someone wanted the whole rotten mechanism noticed — or wanted *her*, specifically, to notice it.

"May I ask you something impertinent?" Vane said, as she gathered her notes.

"You may. I'll simply refuse to answer."

"That's the spirit. My question is this: you came here asking about a trust. Most investigators would have started with the body and worked outward. You started with the money and worked inward." He tilted his head. "Why?"

Because the body wore my cufflinks, she thought. Because the file on my desk has a dead woman's name in it. Because somewhere between the archive and the churchyard this stopped being a case and became a debt.

"Because bodies float," she said instead. "Money sinks. And I've learned to dredge where the heavy things are."

Vane smiled — a real smile, brief and crooked, the first she'd seen on him. "My predecessor used to say something similar. He also ended up retiring early, very comfortably, and never discussing the Bellwethers again." He walked her to the door, pausing with his hand on the frame. "One more thing, gratis. The deed has a clause — an odd one, Edmund's personal addition. If any heir is proven to have *falsely* been recorded dead — if a death in the line is shown to be fraudulent — the entire trust voids. Everything. It goes to restitution, to the Crown, to charity. Nothing to the family at all."

Elodie stopped on the stairs.

"A fraudulent death voids the whole trust," she repeated.

"Every shilling. Edmund hated liars even more than he hated early deaths. Which means, Miss Amberdale, that if Maren Quill is alive — and I'm an old man, and I've watched the marsh cottage across the water for twenty-five years, and I have never once seen a light go out in it — then somebody in this village isn't merely hiding a secret." He folded his arms. "They're sitting on a fortune that could evaporate the moment the truth surfaces. Think about what a person might do to keep that truth underwater. Think about what they've already done."

* * *

Outside, the high street was doing its midday impression of sleepiness: gulls arguing over the chandlery roof, the bakery exhaling bread-smell, Mrs. Tallow's curtains twitching with the reflexes of a periscope. Ordinary. Harmless. A village pretending, with tremendous commitment, to be nothing but itself.

Elodie stood on the cobbles and felt the new arithmetic settle over her like cold water.

Fenwick had died four days before a trust worth — what? Millions? Tens of millions? — began its final countdown. His death redirected everything to one woman. And underneath it all lay a second, stranger fact: a woman certified dead for twenty-seven years, whose resurrection wouldn't just embarrass the village but *bankrupt* it. Whose survival was, financially speaking, the most dangerous thing in Saltmere.

Maren Quill wasn't just a ghost. She was a liability with a heartbeat. And someone had decided, four days ago, to start settling accounts.

Elodie turned toward the police station, toward the flats, toward the dragging operation that Constable Reeve had promised would begin at low tide — and found her thoughts snagging, stubbornly, on one loose thread. Fenwick had been asking questions about the trust before he died. Asking *someone*. Men who ask questions carry the answers with them — in notebooks, in letters, in the worn pockets of the men they used to be.

If Tobias Fenwick had learned the truth about Maren Quill, then the proof of it might still exist.

And if the tide was going out — she checked her watch, did the arithmetic, and broke into a walk that was nearly a run — then the flats were about to give back everything they'd taken.

The drag teams, she remembered with a lurch, were scheduled to start at three o'clock.

It was twenty past two.
