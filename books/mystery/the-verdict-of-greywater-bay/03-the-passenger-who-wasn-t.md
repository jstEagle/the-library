## 3. The Passenger Who Wasn't

The rain came in off the bay that Tuesday like it had a grudge, and Dominic Thornbury watched it through the dispatch window of Bayline Cabs with the particular satisfaction of a man who did not have to be out in it. His office was a glass booth bolted to the side of the garage on Cannery Row, lit amber against the dark, and from inside it he could see the whole wet geometry of Greywater Bay at night: the sodium lamps along the wharf, the ferry terminal's blue sign bleeding color into the puddles, the long black tongue of Harbor Road running out toward the gate that closed at eleven.

He had the radio low. He always kept it low — just enough to catch the drivers' voices when they checked in, never so loud that it drowned out the other channel he listened to, the one that ran silently beneath everything: the archive.

Ten years of fares lived in his head the way other men kept fishing tackle or war stories. Not verbatim — he wasn't a machine — but filed, cross-referenced, alphabetized by name and incident and grudge. The councilman who took cash rides to a house on Dune Street that wasn't his own. The pharmacist who wept every third Friday about her son in Portland. The lawyer who'd once, drunk, recited half a client list into the seat leather. Dominic had never used any of it. That was the covenant. They talked because the cab was dark and moving and he was furniture with a meter; he listened because listening was the only way he knew how to belong without being seen.

Until the silver-haired man had walked into his booth nine days ago and paid triple for a ride that hadn't happened yet, and said: *Use it to stop a murder on the twenty-first.*

Since then the archive had been open all hours. Dominic found himself pulling files at red lights, at three in the morning between dispatches, in the shower, over coffee gone cold. Three names had surfaced so far, three plausible victims orbiting the solstice like debris around a drain. He turned them over nightly the way a jeweler turns stones, looking for the flaw.

But it was not one of the three who broke the case open for him. It was Callum Reeve.

* * *

Callum had been riding the night shift for eight months. Ferry mechanic, mid-forties, soft-spoken, with hands that looked like they'd been assembled from spare dock parts — knuckles scarred, nails cut short, a permanent ghost of diesel under the skin. He rode home from the terminal most nights around twelve-thirty, after the last boat was tied down and the engines were buttoned up. Always the same route: left on Wharf, straight up the hill past the shuttered cannery, right onto Larch, drop at the small blue house with the rowboat hull overturned in the yard.

He tipped well. He sat up front. He talked the way tired workingmen talk — about the ferry's aging transmission, about herring season, about nothing. And he asked questions.

That was the thing Dominic couldn't stop turning over now, three days after the realization hit him somewhere between sleep and waking. Callum asked questions. He'd been asking them for months, and Dominic — archivist, cataloguer, man whose entire profession was noticing — had filed them as small talk because they arrived dressed as small talk.

*How late does the night cab run out to the Vann place? Edith still taking taxis since her hip went bad?*

*Does Reyes ever patrol Harbor Road after midnight, or is that dead coverage?*

*The harbor gate — it locks at eleven, but the cab company has a key for pickups past close, doesn't it?*

*What time does the last ferry unload on the twenty-first? Longest day of the year — they run late runs in summer, don't they?*

Dominic sat in his amber booth with the rain ticking the glass and replayed them all, one by one, laying each question beside the others until the pattern stood up out of them like a figure rising from dark water. Routes. Schedules. Coverage gaps. Lock times. A woman's mobility habits. The solstice date, offered casually, twice.

It was a map. Assembled piecemeal, across dozens of unremarkable rides, by a passenger who never once sounded curious — only companionable. Only tired. Only warm.

And it mirrored, almost exactly, the questions Dominic himself had been asking of his own archive since the prepaid fare: *who moves at night, who is exposed, where does the town go blind?*

Someone else was doing his work. Someone else had been doing it longer.

* * *

He could have gone to Reyes. He thought about it seriously, sitting there while driver after driver called in — *Three-Seven clear on Larch, nothing moving* — the radio litany of an empty town. But what would he say? *A mechanic asks friendly questions in cabs*? Reyes would look at him the way she'd looked at him on the phone last week when he'd hinted, too obliquely, at trouble coming: with polite exhaustion, waiting for the crazy part.

Besides — and this was the thought that shamed him even as he admitted it — going to the police meant opening the archive. It meant explaining how he knew what he knew, and everything he knew was contraband. Ten years of other people's midnight confessions, subpoenaed into daylight. Marisol would never forgive him. Half the town wouldn't either. The covenant would be broken, and for what — a hunch built from small talk?

So instead, Dominic did what he did best. He waited, and he watched, and he arranged a test.

* * *

Callum's ride came up on the board at 12:41 a.m., Thursday, the twelfth. *Terminal to Larch, regular.* Dominic dispatched it to himself, which he almost never did — he was the voice, not the wheel — but some tests you administer personally.

Callum climbed in out of the drizzle smelling of engine degreaser and coffee, nodded his easy nod, buckled up. "Quiet one tonight."

"Dead," Dominic agreed, pulling out onto Wharf. "Ferry come in clean?"

"Clean enough. She's held together with paint and prayer." Callum settled back, watching the streetlights slide across the windshield. "You hear they're extending the summer schedule? Late runs on the solstice. Festival traffic."

"First I've heard." A lie, gently told. "They keep us in the dark down at the office."

"You'd think they'd tell the cab company first." Callum smiled at the window. "You're the nervous system of this town, Dom. Everything routes through you eventually."

The phrase landed strangely — *nervous system* — and Dominic felt the hair on his forearms lift, though his voice stayed level. "Something like that. Speaking of which — the twenty-first. You working late?"

"Past midnight, probably. Why?"

"I'll be driving that night myself. Just wondering if our routes cross." Dominic let a beat pass, casual as weather. "You take the harbor road much after close? Gate's locked by then. I've always wondered how the mechanics get in and out."

It was a small probe, well disguised. Callum's answer should have been nothing. A shrug. A grunt.

Instead, Callum turned from the window and gave him a smile — warm, unhurried, arriving a half-second too late, like thunder after lightning already counted. "Side door's got a padlock with a combination. Everyone at the yard knows it. Why, you thinking of picking up extra harbor runs?" He laughed softly. "You should get out of that booth more, Dom. It's making you suspicious of everybody."

The words were friendly. The eyes behind them were not unfriendly either — that was the terrible thing. They were patient. And for one moment, no longer than a stoplight, Dominic had the distinct sensation of being read: of sitting across from someone who was consulting his own archive, and finding Dominic Thornbury filed neatly within it.

Then the moment passed, and Callum was just a tired mechanic again, asking whether the heater worked, complaining amiably about the price of brake pads, tipping five dollars on a nine-dollar fare at the blue house on Larch with the overturned rowboat.

"Night, Dom. Stay dry."

Dominic watched him unlock his door, go inside, and light a lamp. Then he sat in the idling cab in the rain and made himself say it plainly, in the privacy of his own skull, where no subpoena could reach:

*He knows I'm watching. Or he will, if I keep looking.*

Because here was the detail that had finally cracked it open, the one he'd turned over all the way home: eight months of rides, and Callum had never once asked him anything personal. Not where he lived, not whether he had family, not why a man with a dispatcher's memory drove cabs at night. Everyone asked eventually. Everyone wanted to know the man behind the voice.

Everyone except the one passenger who, apparently, already knew the answers.

* * *

Back in the booth at two a.m., Dominic pulled the logbook toward him and did something he had never done in ten years. He started a file on a passenger.

Not in the book itself — the book belonged to the company, and the company had already been questioned once, Marisol said, after some anonymous tip. On a blank page at the back of his own pocket notebook, the one no one had ever seen, he wrote: **C.R.** And beneath it, the dates, the routes, the questions, each one transcribed exactly as it had been asked, in order, across eight months.

When he finished, the entry filled two pages. He stared at it and understood that he was looking at a mirror image of his own recent work — the same method, the same patience, the same exploitation of the town's one blind spot: the fact that nobody watches the man who watches everybody.

Except somebody had. Somebody had looked straight at Dominic Thornbury, invisible for ten years, and seen not a wall but a tool. Every route he'd confirmed, every schedule he'd recited, every gap in coverage he'd mentioned offhand — it had all been harvested, ride by ride, by a man paying nine dollars plus tip for the privilege.

The silver-haired stranger had said *stop a murder*. Dominic had assumed he'd need to find the killer in the town's shadows.

He hadn't considered that the killer might be sitting in his front seat, smiling, learning him.

Dominic clicked off the booth lamp and sat in the dark with the rain, and far down the hill, at the blue house on Larch, the lamp in Callum's window was still burning — steady, patient, wide awake.
