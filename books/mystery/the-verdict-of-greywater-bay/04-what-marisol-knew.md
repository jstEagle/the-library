## 4. What Marisol Knew

Marisol Vega had been a dispatcher for eleven years, and in that time she had learned that the day shift was a different animal entirely from the night. Dominic's hours belonged to the town's secrets — the drunks, the lovers, the men leaving houses they shouldn't have been leaving at three in the morning. Her hours belonged to the town's machinery: the school runs, the pharmacy pickups, the old women with dialysis appointments who liked to talk about their grandchildren and their knees and, occasionally, about each other. Day shift was gossip in daylight, harmless as sea glass. Night shift was something else. Night shift was confession.

She had never envied him his confessions. She'd told him once, over burnt coffee at the changeover, that she didn't know how he carried it all.

"I don't carry it," he'd said. "I file it."

That was Dominic. Ten years of filing other people's lives into that vast, silent archive behind his eyes, and never once — not once — letting anything slip back out. She used to think it was coldness. She'd learned better. It was discipline, the kind you build when you've got nothing else to hold yourself together with, and she loved him for it the way you love a load-bearing wall: without thinking about it, until you imagine the house without it.

Which was why, on the morning after the solstice booking appeared in the system, Marisol stood in the dispatch office holding a printout and felt her stomach turn over like a boat in chop.

* * *

The booking itself was unremarkable to look at. Account number, prepaid, triple fare, pickup unspecified, date the twenty-first. The kind of entry that scrolled past a hundred times a day without snagging anyone's attention. But Marisol had been the one to process the payment, two days before Dominic ever saw it, because the client had called during her shift — a voice like dry paper, silver-haired, courteous in the way of people who have practiced courtesy so long it has worn smooth as a river stone. He'd asked questions no ordinary client asked. Which dispatcher worked nights. How long he'd been there. Whether records were kept digitally or on paper.

She'd answered because answering was her job, and only afterward, hanging up, had she understood what she'd done. She'd handed a stranger a map of Dominic Thornbury.

Now she watched through the office window as Dominic crossed the lot toward the day door, moving in his usual way — unhurried, precise, a man who had made invisibility into a craft. He looked tired. He looked, if she was honest, like a man carrying something that wouldn't file flat.

She met him at the coffee machine, printout folded in her hand like a knife she didn't want to show anyone.

"We need to talk," she said. "Not here."

Dominic raised an eyebrow. In ten years, Marisol could count their private conversations on one hand, and every one of them had been about her — her divorce, her mother's illness, the year she'd almost left Greywater Bay altogether. He gave counsel the way he filed secrets: completely, and without keeping any for himself.

"Break room's empty," he said.

"It isn't about the break room." She lowered her voice anyway. "It's about the twenty-first. The prepaid fare."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, guarded, gone. "What about it?"

"You've been pulling records." Not a question. She'd seen the access log herself, his login stamped across queries at hours when no legitimate fare needed looking up. "Three nights running. Cross-referencing accounts against route histories. I run the same reports for billing, Dominic. I know what snooping looks like, because I'm the one who has to explain it to the accountants."

He said nothing. That was answer enough.

* * *

She took him outside, around the side of the building where the dumpsters stood and nobody from the office bothered to smoke anymore since the new ordinance. The harbor glittered beyond the chain-link, deceptively pretty, the water doing its morning performance of innocence.

"The police were here," she said. "Tuesday. Two detectives. A woman named Reyes and a big quiet one who mostly took notes."

Dominic went very still. Stillness was his native language, but this was different — this was the stillness of a man hearing a floorboard creak overhead when he thought he lived alone.

"They asked about the booking," Marisol continued. "The prepaid one. Someone had called in an anonymous tip — said there'd be a crime on the solstice, said the taxi company's records would prove planning. They wanted everything. Account holder, payment trail, call logs, driver assignments going back six months."

"And what did you tell them?"

"What was I supposed to tell them?" She heard her own voice sharpen and hated it. "I told them what the system shows. Prepaid account, third-party processor, no name attached. Which is true. What I didn't tell them—" she stopped, started again. "I didn't tell them our night dispatcher has been treating the dispatch logs like his personal case board."

The words landed between them and neither picked them up.

"Dominic." She stepped closer, dropped to almost a whisper, though the lot was empty. "Do you understand what's in those logs? Not the fares. The *notes*. Your notes. Driver notes, call-taker notes, the flags we put on addresses. Mrs. Okonkwo's son picking up packages he shouldn't. The councilman's Tuesday-night standing fare to a house on Larkspur that isn't his. The fights people had in the back seat that drivers wrote down because they didn't know what else to do with them. Ten years, Dominic. If Reyes pulls those files — and she will, if she thinks there's a reason — she won't find one murder plot. She'll find half the town's private life, indexed by date."

"I know what's in them."

"Do you?" She searched his face, that closed, weathered face she'd read a thousand times across a radio console. "Because I don't think you do. I think you think you're being careful. I think you think you can pull threads quietly and put them back. But the police don't work quietly, and they don't put things back. They subpoena. They seize. They spread everything out on a table under fluorescent lights and make everyone come look at it."

"That's why I've been careful—"

"Careful." She laughed, and it came out bitter. "You want to hear careful? Careful is me telling Reyes the digital archive had a corruption event last spring and the pre-spring records are unreliable. Careful is me staying late tonight misfiling paper logs into boxes that won't be catalogued for months. Careful is me lying to a detective, Dominic, which is a thing I have never done in eleven years, and which I did yesterday afternoon while smiling at her."

He stared at her. For the first time since she'd known him, Dominic Thornbury looked genuinely off balance — a man whose filing system had just returned an error.

"You did that for me?"

"I did that because if they take the logs, they take everyone." Her voice cracked and she steadied it. "Mrs. Okonkwo. The councilman's wife, who doesn't know and maybe is better off. Every scared kid who ever called us instead of calling home. You think you're protecting Edith Vann or whoever it is you've decided is in danger. Fine. Maybe you are. But you're spending everyone else's secrets to do it, and nobody asked you to make that trade."

* * *

For a long moment neither of them spoke. A gull argued with something invisible on the roof. Down at the water, the early ferry loaded cars with the patient clank of chains.

"I have to know," Dominic said finally, quietly. "Whoever paid that fare knew things about this town. About the routes. About *me*. And they used my company, my system, to set it in motion. If I do nothing and someone dies on the twenty-first—"

"Then someone dies, and it's terrible, and it isn't yours."

"It became mine the moment they booked it through us."

Marisol closed her eyes. This was the wall she always hit with him — not coldness, exactly, but this fierce, misplaced accounting, this belief that proximity created obligation. She'd watched him carry passengers' griefs for a decade like unpaid fares accruing interest.

"There's something else," she said, because he deserved all of it, because loyalty that withheld wasn't loyalty. "The anonymous tip. The one that brought Reyes here. I heard the detective say something to her partner when she thought I was out of the room." She hesitated. "She said the caller had quoted the booking details word for word. The exact amount. The exact date. Details that only exist inside our system, Dominic. Inside the logs."

The color left his face by degrees, like a tide going out.

"So whoever tipped them," Marisol finished, "has already read the same files you're reading. Or written them." She pressed the folded printout into his hand — the booking record, the thing she'd carried around all morning like contraband. "Whatever you're doing, do it faster. Because you're not the only one going through our archives. And whoever else it is doesn't care who gets exposed when the whole drawer spills open."

She walked back inside before he could answer, because she was afraid of what he'd say, and more afraid of what he wouldn't. Behind her, in the lot, Dominic Thornbury stood alone with a piece of paper in his hand, reading it again as if it might rearrange itself — and somewhere past the breakwater, out where the gray water met the grayer sky, the twenty-first was already coming in with the tide.
