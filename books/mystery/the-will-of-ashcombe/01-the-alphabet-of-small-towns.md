## 1. The Alphabet of Small Towns

There were two hundred and fourteen condolence cards, and Tamsin Brightwater had every intention of cataloguing every last one of them before the week was out.

They sat in the archive beneath St. Aldhelm's Cathedral—a low stone room that smelled of vellum and cold air and, faintly, of the lavender sachets Edith slipped into the drawers when she thought nobody noticed—and they worked the way they always worked: left to right, top to bottom, alphabetically where alphabet could be imposed and chronologically where it couldn't. The cards were arranged on six long trestle tables in the nave above until the funeral was a week past and the town's attention had moved on to more pressing matters (the fate of the allotment society's prize marrows, mostly), and then Tamsin had carried them down in three careful boxes, one at a time, because carrying two at once invited disaster.

Card one hundred and seven lay open under the desk lamp. *To the family of the late Lady Ashcombe,* someone from Taunton had written, *with deepest sympathy. She was a great lady.* The handwriting slanted left. The ink was ballpoint, blue. There was a small grease spot in the corner, which suggested the card had spent some time in a handbag near a pasty.

Tamsin recorded all of this. Not the pasty—though they were tempted—but the sender, the date of postmark if legible, the general tenor of sentiment, and any request for reply. It was not glamorous work. It was, in fact, the least glamorous work imaginable, and Tamsin Brightwater had built an entire life out of it, brick by orderly brick, the way a wren builds a nest: small things, carefully placed, invisible from the street.

*Ashcombe, Edith Margaret, Lady — condolences received re: death of, 214 items, Oct–Nov.*

That would be the entry. That was always the entry. Tamsin had learned long ago that the secret to being forgotten was to be useful in exactly the amount required and no more. A person who filed things properly was furniture. A person who filed things improperly became a character, and characters got talked about, and talked-about people got asked to things—to suppers, to committees, to the front pews at funerals where everyone could see them and wonder who they were and why they were crying or, worse, why they weren't.

Tamsin did not cry at funerals. Tamsin stood at the back by the west door, next to the fire extinguisher and the stack of service booklets, and watched the town assemble itself like a jigsaw puzzle they'd memorized years ago but were never once allowed to touch.

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere overhead, through six feet of Norman masonry, the organist was murdering a hymn.

"Still down here, then?"

Edith came down the spiral stair the way she came everywhere—sideways, at speed, as though the building might change its mind about her if she gave it time to think. She was seventy-one and built like a doorstop, with a face that had been pretty in the way of weathered oak and hands that could still a fidgeting child at forty paces with one look over her spectacles. Thirty-eight years a verger. She knew every stone in St. Aldhelm's personally and had opinions about at least half of them.

"I'm cataloguing," Tamsin said.

"You're hiding," said Edith, "in a room with no windows, surrounded by dead people's letters. I'm told there's a word for that."

"Archivist."

"That's the one." She peered at the tables, at the neat rows of cards standing open like little paper tents, each one angled to catch the light at the same degree. "You've done the Ws already."

"I do the Ws first. They're the most interesting. Widow this, widow that. Half the town is a W."

"Mm." Edith picked up card one hundred and ninety-three—*Mrs. Wilkins, deeply grieved, psalm enclosed*—and read it twice. Then she set it down slightly crooked, purely to watch Tamsin's eye twitch, and sat herself on the edge of the desk with the ease of someone who had been sitting on that particular desk since before Tamsin was hired. "Bram's asking after you."

"Bram asks after me every day. He asked after me yesterday while telling me my guttering was a disgrace."

"He means come up for supper. He's made that thing with the sausages."

"The thing with the sausages is a casserole, Edith. We've discussed this. A casserole is not a personality."

"He's lonely since the hip." Edith said it lightly, but her hand smoothed the front of her cassock-alternative—she refused, on principle, to call it anything so papist as a cassock, and called it instead *my coat*, though no coat ever made had buttons like those—and Tamsin saw the truth underneath, which was that Edith was also lonely, and that Bram's supper invitations were really Edith's supper invitations laundered through a man too proud to issue his own.

"I'll come Thursday," Tamsin said. "I've got the Ashcombe correspondence to finish."

"Ashcombe, Ashcombe." Edith waved a hand at the ceiling, in the vague direction of the great house on its hill above the town. "Half the cathedral's been up there weeping into their hankies all week and you're down here filing their tears. You know she left the church a legacy? Two thousand pounds for the roof. Bram says it won't cover the north aisle, but he says that about everything. He said it about the Queen's Jubilee money and then bought a new wheelbarrow."

"She was generous," Tamsin said, in the tone of a person recording a fact rather than feeling one.

Edith looked at them for a moment over the spectacles. It was a look Tamsin knew well—the look that said *I see you sitting in your little paper fort, and I am choosing, for now, not to knock it down*—and then she pushed off the desk, straightened card one hundred and ninety-three with a flick of one finger (Tamsin's soul briefly left their body), and went back up the stairs sideways, calling down as she went:

"Thursday. Sausage thing. Bring nothing, you'll only label it."

* * *

The archive held eleven thousand documents older than the town's memory and roughly four hundred thousand newer than it, and Tamsin loved it with the specific, unsentimental love of a person who has decided that loving things is safest when the things cannot leave.

They had come to Ashcombe twelve years ago, answering an advertisement in a journal nobody read, for a position nobody wanted: parish archivist, part salary, part cottage, *suitable for a quiet applicant*. The interviewing committee had consisted of the then-dean, Bram, and a woman from the diocese who kept checking her watch. Tamsin had worn their one good jacket and answered questions in complete sentences, which apparently settled the matter, because the dean had shaken their hand before the woman from the diocese finished her second question.

Nobody had ever quite worked out what Tamsin was. Not man, not woman—or both, or neither; the town had run the calculations for a year or two and then, finding the sums didn't resolve, had simply filed Tamsin under *the archivist* and moved on. Tamsin had found this an enormous relief. *The archivist* was a role with clear duties and no expectations. One did not invite *the archivist* to dances. One did not ask *the archivist* why they lived alone. One brought one's water-damaged Bibles to *the archivist* and received them back clean, pressed, and annotated in pencil, and one went away satisfied, and that was the whole of the relationship.

It was, Tamsin told themselves, enough.

Most days they even believed it.

The afternoon light came down the stairwell in a slow gold bar and crawled across the floor toward the far shelves, and Tamsin worked through it, card after card. *With heartfelt sympathy. In fond remembrance. Requiescat in pace.* The town had turned out its whole vocabulary of grief for Lady Ashcombe, and the vocabulary turned out to be smaller than expected: perhaps thirty phrases, rotated, the way a choir rotates its anthems. Even the handwriting repeated—aunts teaching nieces, nieces growing into aunts, the same loops coming round again like the seasons.

At half four, boots on the stair, heavy and deliberate, and Bram filled the doorway the way he filled every doorway, which was entirely.

He was seventy-eight and had been sexton of St. Aldhelm's for fifty years, digging graves back when digging graves meant knowing the ground, and he still walked like a man listening to what was underneath him. His beard had gone the color of old lichen. He held his cap in his hands, which meant something was on his mind, because Bram wore his cap indoors and out and only removed it for church, funerals, and difficult conversations.

"All right?" he said.

"Cataloguing."

"So I heard." He came in, ducking under the lintel out of habit though the lintel had never once threatened him, and stood looking at the tables of cards with his head tipped, like a man reviewing troops. "Two hundred-odd, is it?"

"Two hundred and fourteen."

"Lady Ashcombe'd have liked that. Liked a number, she did. Kept accounts of everything. Eggs, letters, sins." He said it comfortably, without weight, the way you'd say a man kept bees. Then he shifted, and the cap turned once around in his hands. "Edith says you'll come Thursday."

"Edith says a great many things."

"She does. It's how she prays, near enough." Bram smiled, then didn't. He looked at the cards again, and Tamsin—whose profession was noticing what people did with their hands—noticed that his had gone still on the cap, gripping it, just for a moment, before loosening. "Strange business, a funeral," he said. "You spend a week getting the ground ready, and then it's done, and everybody goes home, and you're left standing there thinking: well. That's that, then. Twelve years I've known you, Tamsin, and I don't believe I've ever seen you at a graveside except working."

"I attend professionally."

"I know it." He nodded slowly. "Only—it struck me, standing there Tuesday. Whole town up on the hill, and I thought, there's folk in that crowd who've buried more than they've said." He stopped himself, visibly, the way a man steps back from a ledge. "Never mind me. Old man's wind. Thursday, then?"

"Thursday," Tamsin agreed, and Bram put his cap back on, and went up the stairs, and the archive was quiet again, and Tamsin sat for a while not writing anything, wondering what on earth he'd meant.

* * *

They finished the Ws as the light died. Card two hundred and fourteen was from the Ashcombe estate itself—printed, formal, thanking the town for its kindness—which Tamsin found a curious object: grief manufactured in advance, ordered in bulk, waiting in a drawer for the moment it would be needed. Someone had signed it in fountain pen. *Marcus Ashcombe.* The signature was enormous and confident and took up half the card, the signature of a man accustomed to space being made for him.

Tamsin entered it in the register, closed the book, and squared the cards into their boxes, and that was when they found it.

It was tucked inside box three, beneath the last of the cards—an envelope, cream, good stock, the kind sold by the stationer in Wells. No card inside. Just a letter, folded in thirds, and across the front of the envelope, in a small, precise, upright hand:

*For the Archivist. To be opened alone.*

Tamsin stood very still in the lamplight.

The postmark was smudged but legible, and Tamsin's eyes, trained on twelve years of postmarks, legible ones especially, read it twice to be sure.

The date was the day after the funeral.

The letter had been in the box since the cards came down from the nave—Tamsin was certain of it, certain the way they were certain of dates and shelf-marks and the exact angle at which card one hundred and ninety-three preferred to stand. Which meant it had been placed among the condolence cards sometime during the week the cards had stood in the nave, by someone with access to the cathedral, addressed not to the family, not to the dean, but to the one person in Ashcombe whose whole life was built on reading other people's mail and keeping quiet about it.

*To be opened alone.*

The organ upstairs had stopped. The bar of gold light had climbed off the floor and gone. Tamsin looked at the envelope for a long moment, and then, because they were who they were, they did the sensible thing: they logged it. *Unsolicited item, condolence collection, provenance unknown, addressee: archivist.* Date. Time. Initials.

And then, alone in the windowless room beneath the singing stones of St. Aldhelm's, with the town's two hundred and fourteen sorrows packed away around them like insulation, Tamsin Brightwater broke the seal, and began to read.
