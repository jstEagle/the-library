## 18. The Bell Foundry

The note came on Thursday, tucked into the return slot of the insurance office like a bill that had come due. Courthouse stationery, cream stock, the county seal embossed at the top. One sentence, in a hand so controlled it might have been typeset:

*The foundry, Saturday, seven o'clock. I have what Crane wrote. Come alone, or it burns with everything else.*

No signature. None needed. The paper smelled faintly of pipe tobacco and old money, and I stood there in my doorway holding it for a long time, watching the streetlights come on down Bellwether Lane one by one, the way they had every night of my life.

He was inviting me to my own funeral. I understood that. Any man who could read a claims file could read this: Voss had nothing to gain by giving me Crane's testimony and everything to gain by closing the last open account on his ledger. The drowned man had worn my coat; the second drowned man would need no costume at all. They'd find me in Miller's Creek and say grief had finally taken Astrid Whitlock, poor fellow, walking the banks where his parents died, and Judge Voss would preside over the inquest with his kind gray eyes and his voice like a hand on your shoulder, and no one—no one—would ever ask a question he didn't want asked.

I went anyway.

Not because I believed him. Because Dana Okafor believed me, and because Petra Hollis-Byrne had spent twelve years keeping a witness alive and deserved to know what happened to him, and because somewhere in that ruined building was either the truth or the end of the road, and I had spent my whole life unable to leave a file unfinished.

I told them both where I'd be. Dana wanted to come. I made her promise not to—not until eight o'clock, not unless she heard something worth hearing. "You're the law," I said. "Let me be the record first."

She didn't like it. She shook my hand anyway, hard, like a woman sealing a bet she intended to collect on.

* * *

The Hollis & Sons Bell Foundry had been dead longer than Elias Crane had been officially dead. It sat at the bend of Miller's Creek where the water slowed and went dark, a cathedral of brick and broken skylights, its great casting pit open to the sky like a wound that never healed. As a boy I'd been told never to go near it—the floor was unsound, the cranes were rotten, a child fell once—and like every prohibition in Millbrook, it had been less a warning than a fence around somebody's secret.

I parked half a mile out and walked in through the loading yard, past rusted ingot racks and a shipping manifest still nailed to a post, curled soft as cloth by forty winters. My breath hung in front of me. The creek talked to itself somewhere below, patient as a debt collector.

The doors on the river side stood open.

Inside, the light came down through the shattered skylights in long dusty columns, and in one of those columns, seated on a folding chair as if he'd been set there by a stagehand, waited Judge Harlan Voss.

He looked exactly as he always looked at the Founders' Day ceremony: silver hair combed with geometric precision, overcoat buttoned to the second button, hands folded on a walking stick he did not need. A battery lantern sat on the floor beside him, throwing his shadow up the far wall, enormous, across the ghost-outline of an old bell mold—a bell-shaped hollow in the packed sand, big enough to hold a man's secrets. Or a man.

"Mr. Whitlock." He said my name the way he said everyone's name, warmly, as if we were old friends who'd simply lost touch. "Punctual. Your father was punctual too."

"You knew my father?"

"I knew everyone in this town, Astrid. That was the job." He gestured to a second chair, placed opposite his own, facing him, like a deposition room with better lighting. "Sit. You'll want to be comfortable for this."

I didn't sit. He nodded as if I'd passed some small test, and took a tin flask from his coat pocket, poured something into its cap, and drank with the unhurried pleasure of a man who has already won.

"Do you know why bells were cast here?" he said. "Right on this spot, for ninety years? Because a bell is the only instrument that must be true all the way through. A crack anywhere and the whole thing is worthless. No patching a bell, Mr. Whitlock. You melt it down and start again." He smiled. "I've thought about that a great deal lately."

"Where's the testimony?"

"Direct. Also your father's way." He set the cap down on the arm of his chair. "It's here. Or most of it. Enough of it. Whether you leave with it depends entirely on how this conversation goes."

"And how does it go?"

"That," he said, "is up to you," and then, before I could answer, he began to talk.

* * *

He talked the way other men recite golf scores.

"The night of March ninth, 2012, I was driving back from Carverton. There was a man named Deke Rourke who kept books for certain interests of mine, and Deke had developed a conscience, and consciences are expensive. We met to discuss the terms under which his conscience would remain quiet. The discussion went poorly. I left angry, faster than I should have, on roads I knew better than God did." He turned the flask cap slowly on the chair arm. "Elias Crane was walking home from the feed store. He stepped into the headlights. I felt the impact. And I made a decision in roughly the time it takes to blink—that I had built thirty years of good works in this town, and I would not surrender them to a drunk pedestrian's bad luck."

"He wasn't drunk."

"No. But that's what the report says, because I wrote the report. Not personally—I don't write anything, Astrid, that's the first rule—but I dictated it, and Sheriff Pruitt typed it, and Dr. Ansel signed it, and by morning it was true. That's all truth is, in the end. Consensus with paperwork." He looked up at me with genuine, almost paternal interest. "Crane saw the car. Crane read the plate—he told the Hollis woman he'd known my car since he was a boy shoveling my walk. And Crane ran, because he thought I'd kill him, and he was right to think it. The Society got to him first. Sentimental old people. They hid him like a fugitive from a novel, moved him twice, fed him, kept his faith alive. Twelve years they kept that match burning."

"And when he decided to testify, you killed him."

"I arranged for a man to die wearing his name," Voss corrected mildly. "There's a difference, and the difference matters to men like you. The gentleman in the creek was a drifter out of Ohio named Wendell Moss, who resembled Crane in build and had no family and fewer questions. Alder told me Crane meant to talk—my nephew, whom I love, and who will spend the rest of his life believing he merely made a phone call. I located Crane's hiding place, I introduced myself to Mr. Moss as a benefactor helping a frightened man disappear, and I dressed him in clothes chosen to tell a story. Your story." He spread his hands, apologetic. "A corpse in Elias Crane's name closes the witness problem. A corpse in *your* coat opens a second question—why was Elias Crane killed in Astrid Whitlock's clothing? Grief, perhaps. Obsession. The town would have concluded what towns conclude: that the son of the crash victims had finally gone looking for the witness and it had ended badly. Two tragedies, one grave. Case closed by consensus. Paperwork."

"And the cufflinks."

Something flickered behind the gray eyes—the first crack in the glaze. "That was Moss's initiative. He robbed your brother's casket on his own, greedy little man, wanting payment in advance. It nearly unraveled everything. I confess it angered me." He said *angered* the way another man might say *inconvenienced*. "But anger is a luxury. I've had none since 2012. Only work."

"Why am I here?" I said. "If you've confessed to murder in an empty building, why not just drown me and be done?"

"Because drowning you is the *end* of the work, and I wanted—" he paused, searching, and found the word with evident satisfaction— "*company*. Do you know how long it has been since I spoke a true sentence aloud? Thirty years of benevolence, Astrid. Scholarships. The courthouse restoration. I buried your parents' claim under forty-one settlements routed through Meridian Holdings, and every year I attended the memorial service, and every year I wept, and every year it worked. I am the best thing this town ever had. Ask anyone. Everyone will tell you." He rose, slowly, using the stick now, and crossed to the edge of the casting pit, looking down into the dark where the crucifixes used to pour. "You're the only person alive who knows the whole shape of it. Your memory—it's quite famous, you know. Pruitt used to joke about it. *That Whitlock boy forgets nothing.* I've thought about that boy for twelve years. Every file you memorized was a nail I couldn't pull."

"So this is the part where you offer me a choice."

"This is the part where I explain that there isn't one." He turned back to face me, and for the first time his voice lost its warmth, not into anger but into something worse—clerical calm, the tone of a man reading terms and conditions. "You will walk to the creek bank with me. There's a stretch below the old sluice where the water is deep and the current goes under, and a man who falls there stays fallen. Tomorrow you'll be a sorrow, not a scandal. The testimony burns tonight regardless—there's kerosene in the mold, if you'd like to see it. And your files, Mr. Whitlock? Files can be discredited. A dead obsessive investigator with a head full of conspiracy—that's not evidence, that's a diagnosis. I've already spoken with Dr. Ansel's successor. Grief does strange things to a mind."

He picked up the lantern.

"Terror is loud," he said conversationally, starting toward me across the ruined floor. "Mine hasn't been. That's how I knew I was right. In twelve years I have not once lain awake. Can you understand what a gift that is? To be *certain*?" He stopped six feet away, close enough that I could smell the tobacco and the kerosene on his gloves. "Your brother understood, at the end. Your father too, I think. People want so badly to believe the town is good that they'll believe anything a good man tells them."

Behind him, up in the dark of the loft, something shifted—a board easing under a weight, a whisper of wool against old timber. Voss heard nothing. Men who are certain hear nothing.

I thought of Dana's headlights two miles off, waiting on eight o'clock. I thought of Petra's hands, which had rung the foundry bell once before, sixty years ago, for a debt come due. I thought of the archive in my head, forty-one files, unburnable, unpullable, every nail still hammered home.

"It's seven-forty," I said.

Voss glanced at his watch—an elegant, unnecessary courtesy—and in that glance the darkness above him moved, and the great doors at the loading yard rolled open against the night, and standing in the frame, backlit by a dozen sets of headlights, was the entire town of Millbrook.

Petra Hollis-Byrne stood at their head, and in her hands, held level like a rifle, was the foundry's old signal rope.

Voss turned. For the first time in twelve years, Judge Harlan Voss looked uncertain.

And high above us, gathering itself out of the silence, the cracked old bell began to ring.
