## 11. The Mayor's Perfect Memory

The Greywater Bay Grand did not host charity galas so much as it hosted the town's annual appointment with its own reflection. Every October, the ballroom filled with the same hundred and forty people who had been attending since before I was born, dressed slightly better than usual and drinking slightly more than advisable, all of them raising money for a cause that changed yearly but whose proceeds somehow always ended up repaving the harbor parking lot.

This year it was the Children's Sailing Fund. The year I started at the desk it had been the Lighthouse Restoration. Before that, presumably, children.

I worked the coat check that night, which was exactly where I wanted to be. A concierge sees guests at their most curated — arriving, departing, performing themselves. But a coat check sees what people carry when they think no one is looking: the flask inside the wool overcoat, the second phone, the apology gift wrapped in tissue paper and clutched like a grenade with the pin already out.

Mayor Edmund Corliss arrived at seven-fifteen precisely, because Edmund Corliss was a man who believed punctuality was a moral position. He handed me his coat — charcoal wool, immaculate — and pressed a twenty into my palm with the practiced warmth of a man who had been pressing twenties into palms for thirty years.

"Tamsin, isn't it? Good lad. Big night for the bay."

"Big night," I agreed, and watched him go, silver-haired and sunlamp-tanned, moving through the crowd like a man walking on water he'd personally negotiated a lease for.

Here is what I knew about Edmund Corliss by that point in my investigation, which is to say, by the point where I had stopped sleeping properly and started keeping notes under false names in a notebook I hid inside a box of guest stationery nobody ever used:

In 1994, he was nineteen, home from his first semester at a state college, and — according to the clipping Elowen had kept — one of four young people whose boat was moored at the north dock the night Cressida Vale went into the water. The other three were Ada Voss, then Ada Fenwick, working her first summer at this very hotel; Harbormaster Gil Renner, then just Gil Renner, nineteen and already handy with boats; and Nell Ashcombe, then Nell Pryce, sixteen years old and, per every photograph I could find of her, standing slightly apart from everyone else even then.

The police in 1994 called it an accident. The town called it a tragedy. The four of them, apparently, called it nothing at all, because in thirty years not one of them had ever publicly mentioned being on that dock, or that boat, or anywhere near that girl.

And now one of them might have killed Elowen Marsh to keep it that way.

* * *

The gala gave me something rare: all four pillars in one room, under chandeliers, drinking donated wine.

Ada Voss I could discount for the moment. She had made her play two weeks ago — the promotion offer, the gentle suggestion that some doors are better left unopened — and whatever she was, she wasn't patient enough to be my follower-in-the-fog. Patience, in my experience, is either a virtue or a tell, and Ada had neither.

Nell Ashcombe hadn't come. Retired teachers, I'd learned, send regrets and donations by check. But I'd caught her watching me twice that month outside the library, once with an expression I couldn't file under any heading I owned. Guilt, maybe. Or the particular terror of someone who has decided to do the right thing and is waiting for her courage to finish downloading.

Gil Renner stood by the bar with a club soda he wasn't drinking, doing what harbormasters do at parties: scanning exits. He'd barely spoken to me since the day I'd asked him about mooring logs and watched his face perform a small, controlled shutdown, like a shop closing early.

Which left the mayor.

Edmund Corliss held court near the string quartet, telling a story I suspected he'd been telling since the Clinton administration. I let him get to the punchline — something about a cod, a councilman, and a compromise — waited for the laughter to crest, and approached with two glasses of champagne and the expression of a man carrying good news.

"Mayor Corliss. Congratulations on the turnout."

"Tamsin!" He took the glass without looking at it, the way powerful men accept things. "You know, your mother worked events like this. Back when the Grand was the Grand." He said it warmly. He always said it warmly. That was the thing about Edmund Corliss — he weaponized warmth so smoothly you sometimes thanked him for the wound.

"She did," I said. "She talked about those nights. Though mostly the ones from her own era." I smiled. "You've been coming to these since you were barely older than me, haven't you? I heard you practically grew up on that dock."

Something crossed his face — quick, professional, gone. The look of a man whose calendar has just shown him a meeting he doesn't remember scheduling.

"The north dock, sure. Misspent youth. Why do you ask?"

"No reason. It's just — people romanticize it. Summer of '94, everybody says. Best summer the bay ever had." I sipped my champagne. "Except for poor Cressida Vale, of course."

The quartet sawed away at something in a major key. Around us, the town laughed and bid on silent-auction baskets of local honey.

"Cressida Vale," Corliss repeated, carefully. "That was a terrible thing. A drowning. Tragic."

"Drowning," I said. "Yes. That's the word they used."

He looked at me then, fully, and I understood why he'd been mayor for five terms. His eyes didn't flicker. They didn't need to. A man that controlled doesn't leak guilt; he leaks nothing at all, and the nothing is its own kind of message.

"It was a long time ago, Tamsin. The town grieved. We moved forward. That's what coastal towns do — we move with the tide." He raised his glass slightly, as if toasting the metaphor itself.

"That's a lovely line. Do you use it often?"

"I use what works." And here he did something interesting. He stepped closer, lowered his voice into the register politicians reserve for confidences, and said: "Your brother's friend — the Marsh woman. Denny's sister. I hear you've been... helping look into things. Off the books."

"Someone has to. The police closed the file in nine days."

"The police close files when there's nothing in them." He smiled. It reached his eyes. That was the frightening part — it genuinely reached his eyes. "But you're a thorough young man. Your mother was thorough too. Ask anyone." He patted my shoulder, twice, the way you'd steady a piece of furniture. "Thoroughness is a virtue. In moderation."

Then he moved off toward the auction table, leaving me holding my champagne and the distinct impression that I'd just been warned by a man who would deny warning me, in language so smooth it wouldn't leave a mark.

* * *

I didn't trap him at the gala. Not really. What I did was plant seeds and wait for the soil to do the work, which is most of what hospitality teaches you: you don't force the checkout, you make the guest want to leave.

The seeds went in over the next hour, one contradiction at a time, each delivered like small talk.

Seed one: I mentioned, apropos of the sailing fund, that I'd read old articles about the regatta of '94 — canceled that August, after the drowning. Corliss said, "Of course it was canceled, the whole town was in mourning." Except the article I'd read said the regatta went ahead, postponed one week, with a moment of silence. The town had sailed. Only the four of them had stopped going near the water.

Seed two: I asked whether he'd known Cressida well, since they were both summer regulars at the north end. He said, "Barely knew her. She was younger. Different crowd." Except Cressida's yearbook — which Denny had gotten from a cousin — had a photo of a beach bonfire with fifteen-year-old Cressida leaning on the shoulder of nineteen-year-old Edmund Corliss, both laughing, both clearly part of the same crowd, the same night, the same fire.

Seed three was the best one, and I saved it for last, when the wine had done its quiet work and the mayor's perfect memory had begun, very slightly, to improvise.

"You know what strikes me," I said, as the quartet packed up and the crowd thinned toward the dessert table, "is how consistent everyone's memory is. Thirty years, and everybody tells it the same way. Accident, storm, tragedy, tide came in. It's almost rehearsed."

"Shared memory is what makes a community, Tamsin."

"Oh, absolutely. It's beautiful, honestly. Like a story everyone agreed on." I paused, letting the silence do a lap. "Funny detail, though. The storm that night — the papers called it a squall that blew up around ten p.m. But the weather service log says the front didn't hit until nearly midnight. Two hours. That's a long time for a story to be wrong, isn't it? Almost like somebody needed the storm to start earlier than it did. Needed it to explain something."

For the first time all evening, Mayor Edmund Corliss set down his glass.

It was a small thing. Deliberate men don't put down their drinks unless the drink has become a liability — unless their hands have told them something their faces refuse to admit. He looked at me across the abandoned dessert table, past the pyramid of petit fours, and for maybe three seconds I saw not the mayor but the nineteen-year-old on the dock in 1994, calculating tides.

"There are a lot of logs in this world," he said quietly. "Weather logs. Mooring logs. Guest registers." He straightened his cuff. "Most of them are wrong, or incomplete, or misfiled. A man builds a career on knowing which records matter and which are just paper." Then the warmth returned, fully installed, professionally lit. "Enjoy the desserts, Tamsin. The pastry chef outdid herself. Tell her the mayor said so — she loves that."

And he walked away, collecting congratulations on the way out like tolls.

I stood there among the petit fours with my heart going like a dinghy in chop, aware of two things simultaneously. One: I had just told a possible murderer, to his face, that I'd been reading official logs. Two: I had enjoyed it, which said something about me I'd examine later, probably with a therapist, possibly with a lawyer.

* * *

The fog came in off the water around eleven, the way it does in Greywater Bay in October — not rolling, exactly, but assembling, street by street, until the lamplights turned into smudges and the whole town felt like a photograph left in the rain.

I walked home. I always walked home; it was twelve minutes along Harbor Road, and I liked the way the Grand shrank behind you and the sea got louder. That night I noticed three things in the first block, because noticing is the only skill I have and I exercise it compulsively:

One: the fog was thick enough to hide anything smaller than a building.

Two: my footsteps had company. Not echoes — echoes keep your rhythm. This was a half-beat behind, patient, matching me the way you match a stranger's pace on an empty platform.

Three: I had left the gala through the kitchen, alone, told no one my route, and was carrying a notebook full of accusations against four of the most beloved people in Greywater Bay.

I did what any sensible person does. I ducked into the all-night laundromat on Cannery Row, which was empty except for a dryer tumbling someone's towels, and watched through the window while the fog produced, slowly, a figure.

Tall. Coat collar up. Standing across the street beneath the dead streetlight that the town had been petitioning to fix since roughly the Obama administration, so still that I might have thought it was a post if posts breathed.

I gave it a full minute. It gave me back the minute, plus interest.

So I did the second sensible thing, which was less sensible: I pushed open the door, stood in the doorway under the fluorescent buzz, and said, "If you're going to follow a man, you should know he works at a hotel. We notice everything. It's the entire job."

The figure didn't move. Didn't speak. The fog moved for it, curling around its shoulders like something being offered.

"Was it the weather log?" I called. "Because I can show you the archive stamp. It's public record. Anybody could've—"

The figure turned and walked away, unhurried, into the white, and the fog closed behind it with the soundless finality of a door in a house where you're a guest.

I stood in the laundromat doorway for a long time, listening to the towels tumble.

Here's what unsettled me, and I mean truly unsettled, down where the professional calm keeps its life vest: the figure hadn't run. Hadn't hurried at all. Whoever it was had followed me for twelve minutes through a fog thick enough to commit any crime the bay had ever buried, and when confronted, had simply... left. As if the following hadn't been the point. As if I'd been allowed to see.

As if the message wasn't *I'm watching you*, but rather: *I could have done anything tonight, and instead I chose to let you know.*

Patience, I thought, walking the rest of the way home with my keys threaded between my fingers like brass knuckles for the optimistically minded. The enemy has patience.

At my door, on the mat, was an envelope with no stamp and no return address. Inside was a single sheet of hotel stationery — Grand stationery, the cream stock with the tide-line watermark — and on it, in handwriting I now knew better than my own, in Elowen Marsh's looping, certain hand, one line:

*The storm didn't come early. Someone lied about the time. Ask who benefits from two extra hours.*

I looked up the length of my dark street. Nothing. Fog, lamplight, the distant complaint of a buoy.

Whoever had followed me home had also delivered my mail.

And they wanted me to keep digging.
