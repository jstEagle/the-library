## 3. The House Remembers Her

Mrs. Tarnow met me at the servants' entrance the next morning with a ring of keys that would have opened a small prison and an expression that suggested I was about to be one of its inmates.

"You'll want the good boots," she said. "The east wing floods."

"I want Edith Hawley's room."

The keys went still in her hand. Not long — half a second, perhaps less — but I have built a career on half-seconds. People tell you everything in the time it takes them to decide what to hide.

"Mrs. Hawley's room is kept as it was," she said. "By instruction."

"Whose instruction?"

"Mrs. Hawley's."

I let that sit between us like a stone dropped into a well, listening for how deep it fell. Eleven years dead by every record in England, and her housekeeper spoke of her instructions in the present tense, the way one speaks of a living woman's preferences. Either Mrs. Tarnow was a sentimentalist of unusual discipline, or she knew something the death certificate didn't.

"Then I expect it's very tidy," I said.

"It's exactly as she left it." And there — beneath the flatness — something almost fierce. A woman defending a shrine, or a lie, or both.

* * *

Hawley House is the kind of house that money built quickly and grief finished slowly. Grey stone gone black at the joints, windows tall enough to admit weather as well as light, and everywhere the particular silence of rooms that have been closed rather than cleaned. The corridors smelled of cold ash and beeswax, and under that, faintly, the sea — not the postcard sea but the working sea, salt and rot and something mineral, as though the house had been drinking it for years through its foundations.

Mrs. Tarnow walked ahead of me at a pace calculated to discourage conversation. She pointed out nothing. She answered three questions I hadn't asked, which told me which questions she feared.

"The family doesn't come often," she said to a portrait of a man with sideburns and a grievance. "Mr. Alistair prefers London. Miss Vera comes for Christmas when her health allows."

"And you stay all year?"

"Someone must keep the house ready."

"Ready for what?"

Her shoulders did something small and controlled. "Ready," she said again, and unlocked a door at the end of the east passage.

I had expected dust sheets. Every preserved room in every dead person's house comes with dust sheets; it is the furniture equivalent of a mourning veil. There were none. The bed was made — properly made, corners tucked with hospital precision — and on the bedside table sat a book with a ribbon marker laid two-thirds through, a pair of spectacles folded on top of it, and a glass of water.

A glass of water. Not empty, not evaporated to a mineral ring, but *there*, catching the grey morning light, as if its owner had stepped out to see about the weather and would be back before it cooled.

"She left mid-sentence," Mrs. Tarnow said behind me. Her voice had changed — lower, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice they've walked on before and know the thickness of. "That's how we keep it. Mid-sentence."

"People don't leave houses mid-sentence, Mrs. Tarnow. They leave them mid-life. It's different."

"Not in this house."

* * *

I gave myself permission to touch nothing for the first ten minutes, which is both professional ethics and a trick: ten minutes of looking teaches you more than an hour of handling. Handling tells you what things are. Looking tells you what things mean.

Edith Hawley's bedroom meant order imposed on appetite. The room was large, south-facing, with a window seat that had clearly once been a favourite — the cushion bore the fossil impression of a habitual sitter — but the chair by the writing desk was the worn one now. Someone had migrated from idleness to industry in her last months here. The desk faced the wall rather than the view, which is the posture of a person who has decided that scenery is for people with time to waste.

The wardrobe stood open a hand's width. Inside, clothes hung in ranks so orderly they looked issued rather than owned — greys, blues, one wine-coloured dress that had been pushed, I noticed, slightly back from its neighbours, as though even the hangers had been asked to give it privacy.

On the vanity: hairbrush with silver hairs in it. A porcelain dish holding three buttons and a key too small for any door I could see. A photograph turned face-down.

I did not turn the photograph over. Not yet. Photographs are confessions, and I wanted to hear this room confess in its own order.

The book on the night table was a gardening manual, of all things, its ribbon marking a chapter on forcing bulbs out of season. *Forcing.* Making something bloom when every natural law said it should sleep. I wrote that down in my head, where I write most things, because paper can be searched and heads, so far, only occasionally.

And then the letter.

It lay in the centre of the writing desk, squared to the desk's edge with the same precision as everything else, a single sheet in a room that contained no other loose paper anywhere. No blotter clutter, no spare nibs, no ink bottle beyond the one standing open — open, mind you, eleven years after its owner allegedly drowned. Ink dries. Someone had been tending this room the way one tends an altar, replacing the perishables, keeping the fiction fresh.

The letter began:

*If you are reading this—*

And stopped.

Two words and a dash, in a hand that started confident and then simply quit, the pen lifting from the page mid-stroke. I bent close. The ink of those two words was darker than the ink in the bottle beside it. Older. Written years ago, and never finished, and never removed.

You may think me morbid, sitting down in a dead woman's chair to commune with her abandoned sentence. But consider my position. I had been summoned to read the will of a woman who died eleven years ago, by a document signed four years after her death, in a house kept ready for her return by a servant who spoke of her in the present tense. Somewhere in this arithmetic there was either a fraud or a ghost, and I have never met a ghost who could hold a fountain pen.

"If you are reading this," I said aloud, to the room, to the water glass, to the wine-coloured dress hiding in its corner of the wardrobe. "If you are reading this, then what, Mrs. Hawley? Finish it. You've had eleven years."

Behind me, Mrs. Tarnow made a sound. Small. Involuntary. The sound of a woman who has just heard someone else say the thing she says to herself at night.

* * *

We took tea afterward in the kitchen, because Mrs. Tarnow declared the drawing room "not aired," which I understood to mean *not safe*. The kitchen was the only honest room in the house — scrubbed wood, a stove with real heat in it, a cat named Bishop who assessed me from a chair and found me adequate.

"You kept her room exactly," I said. "For eleven years. That's devotion, or orders."

"Trousers, Miss Coldharbour. Both legs or neither."

"Were you fond of her?"

Mrs. Tarnow poured tea with the concentration of a bomb-disposal officer. "I was in her service nineteen years. Fondness isn't the word. Fondness is what you feel for a canary."

"What's the word, then?"

She set down the pot. For a moment she looked at the window, at the rain coming off the sea in ropes, and her face did something unguarded — grief, yes, but grief braided with guilt, which is a heavier weave entirely.

"Loyalty," she said finally. "The word is loyalty. And loyalty means you do what she asked even when—" She stopped herself with the visible effort of a woman closing a drawer on her own hand.

"Even when it looks like madness?"

"Even when people come asking questions." She turned the full weight of her attention on me, and I will grant her this: most people flinch first. She didn't. "Three times in eleven years someone has come to this house asking about Mrs. Hawley. A journalist. A genealogist. A solicitor's clerk. All of them left disappointed."

"And what happened to them?"

"They left," she said, "disappointed."

There it was again, that half-second pause, that well-stone falling further than it should. I drank my tea and smiled my professional smile, the one that opens doors by appearing too dull to be a threat.

"Mrs. Tarnow. The letter on the desk. 'If you are reading this.' Who was it addressed to?"

"No one. It was never finished."

"Everything unfinished is addressed to someone. That's what makes it unbearable. Who did she write to?"

The cat chose that moment to leave, which struck me as tactful. Mrs. Tarnow gathered his abandoned saucer of milk and held it against her chest like a shield.

"She wrote to whoever deserved it," she said. "That's all I know. That's all I'll say."

"And if I'm the one who deserves it?"

Something moved behind her eyes then — fear, or hope, or the place where those two become indistinguishable. She set the saucer down with a click.

"Then God help you," she said, "because no one else in this house will," and left me alone with the teapot and the sound of the sea working at the foundations, patient as a lawyer.

* * *

I broke my own rule at four o'clock. Rules are for cases that behave.

I went back up the east passage, past the flooded flagstones and the portraits of grievance, and let myself into the room that remembered its owner better than any grave. The light had gone the colour of old pewter. The water glass caught the last of it and threw a small trembling coin of brightness onto the ceiling, and I thought: *eleven years of afternoons, that coin has climbed these walls, waiting.*

I turned over the photograph.

It showed a woman on the window seat — this window seat, this cushion — laughing at someone outside the frame. She was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, dressed in the wine-coloured dress, and she was laughing with her whole face, the laugh of a woman who has recently decided something enormous and is giddy with the terror of it. On the back, in the same hand as the letter, one line:

*E.H., the day I stopped being afraid of him.*

Him. Capital H in my imagination, though the handwriting offered no such courtesy. I stood in the middle of that preserved, tended, mid-sentence room and felt the case shift beneath my feet the way shingle shifts under a wave — nothing visible moving, everything rearranged.

Because here is what I knew, and what I had known since the will touched my hands: Edith Hawley drowned in 1979. Officially. Certifiably. With a coroner's blessing. And here is what the room insisted: she had lived past that drowning into some new resolution, some day of stopping being afraid, some unfinished sentence addressed to whoever deserved it.

Dead women don't stop being afraid of anyone. Dead women aren't afraid of anything, which is the one mercy of the condition.

Which meant that somewhere between 1979 and the day this room was sealed mid-sentence, Edith Hawley had been alive — alive and laughing and writing letters — and every record, every signature, every solemn nod in this town had agreed otherwise.

I put the photograph face-up on the vanity, where she could keep an eye on the door. It seemed the least I could do. Then I took out my notebook — the real one, paper, because some things deserve to exist outside my head — and wrote a single line:

*She expected someone. She just didn't expect it to take eleven years.*

As I closed the notebook, my eye snagged on the desk again. The letter, squared to the edge. The ink bottle, capped now — I was nearly certain I'd left it open. And beneath the letter's corner, protruding a quarter-inch, the edge of a second sheet that had not been there that morning.

I drew it out with two fingers, the way one draws a splinter.

One line, in Edith Hawley's hand, fresher than the letter, fresher than anything in that room had any right to be:

*You're slower than I hoped, but you're here. Ask Pell about the boathouse — and don't trust the locks.*

Outside, somewhere down among the wet gardens, a padlock clinked against a hasp in the wind, or in a hand.
