## 18. The Photographs

I did not open the photographs in the ice house.

This was not sentiment, whatever Brightwater might have told you if you'd asked him—and he would have asked, because he asks everything, that is both his profession and his affliction. It was method. A room with a dirt floor and a century of damp is no place to handle the only proof a dead woman left behind, and my hands were shaking too badly to trust them with anything I couldn't afford to drop. I want it on record that the shaking was cold. The wind off the headland in October has opinions about exposed skin, and mine had been exposed for the better part of an hour while I knelt on a stone shelf prying at a biscuit tin like a woman defusing something.

So I put the lid back on, tucked the box inside my coat against my ribs where it sat like a second heartbeat, and walked down to the village with Inspector Brightwater matching my stride and asking nothing, which from him was its own kind of eloquence.

* * *

My lodgings were the back room above the chandlery, which sounds picturesque until you learn that the chandlery now sells bait and novelty tea towels, and that the landlady, Mrs. Quill, considers any guest who arrives before nine or after five to be morally suspect. Brightwater could not come up. That was the arrangement we'd arrived at somewhere on the cliff path without either of us saying it aloud: the photographs were mine until they became evidence, and once they became evidence they belonged to everyone, including the people we suspected of wanting them gone.

"You'll lock the door," he said on the stairs, not as a question.

"I'll wedge a chair under the knob as well. I've read novels."

"And you'll send for me the moment—"

"The moment anything happens, yes. You'll be across the lane drinking tea you don't want with Mrs. Quill, who will tell you about her hip."

"She's already told me about her hip. Twice." He paused on the landing, and for a moment the lamplight caught him sideways and he looked younger than his file said he was, and tired in a way that files don't record. "Casper. Whatever's in there—it changes things. Be ready for that."

"Things are already changed. I'm holding a will signed by a drowned woman. There isn't a version of tonight where things stay as they were."

"No," he agreed. "But there are versions where they get worse."

I let myself into my room and wedged the chair, because I had promised, and because the novels are right about chairs more often than people think.

* * *

The tin opened with a sound like a small sigh.

Inside, on top of the diary pages I'd already glimpsed and hadn't yet dared read properly, lay three photographs and a railway ticket, and beneath those, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, a man's signet ring that meant nothing to me yet and would come to mean a great deal—but that is later, and I am trying to tell this in order, which is difficult when your whole case has just turned itself inside out like a glove.

The ticket first, because tickets are honest in a way people aren't. British Rail, Little Hawley to Brighton, one way, third class. Dated the fourteenth of June, 1984.

Edith Hawley had been buried on the eleventh of June, 1984.

I sat with that for a long moment. Three days. She had stood at her own graveside—or rather, she had very sensibly declined to stand at her own graveside, since standing at it would have defeated the entire purpose—and three days later she had bought a third-class single to Brighton with cash, the way people do when they never intend to be traced. Third class. Edith Hawley, whose fortune could have bought the railway, riding to Brighton third class among the fish baskets and the holidaymakers, and I would have given a great deal to have seen her face at that station. I imagine it was magnificent. I imagine she wore mourning, because there is a kind of joke only the dead can make, and Edith struck me, even then, as a woman who understood the value of a well-placed joke.

Then the photographs.

The first showed a boarding house. Not grand—a narrow terraced place squeezed between two others on a seafront street, bay windows, a sign I could half read: *Penhale's, Vacancies*. Geraniums in a window box making a brave show against the salt wind. On the step, slightly out of focus because whoever took the picture had been hurrying, stood a woman in a grey cardigan.

I knew her face from the portrait in Hawley House, from the funeral photograph Vera kept turning face-down, from eleven years of official paper. But knowing a face from documents and seeing it alive are two different countries separated by an ocean you don't know exists until you cross it. In the portrait she had been composed, arranged, a magistrate's mother rendered in oils by someone paid to flatter. Here she was squinting into sunlight, one hand raised either against the camera or in greeting, her mouth caught mid-word, and she looked—there is no other word for it—*relieved*. She looked like a woman who had set down a weight so heavy she'd forgotten her own posture, and her spine had straightened all on its own.

The second photograph was the same step, different day. Edith again, older somehow though only months could have passed, seated now in a wicker chair dragged out onto the pavement, a cup of tea balanced on the arm. And beside her, leaning down, hand on her shoulder—

Alistair.

I set the photograph face-down on the bedspread, then picked it up again, because running from evidence is how cases rot. He was smiling. That was the detail that made my skin crawl, more than anything else in the tin: he was smiling, and the smile reached his eyes, and his hand on his dead mother's shoulder looked easy, proprietary, fond. A son visiting his mother. If you knew nothing, you would see nothing. You would see a good son taking the train down to Brighton to sit with the old lady in the wicker chair while she drank tea she liked better than the tea at home.

Except his mother had been dead for two years when that picture was taken. Except every soul in Little Hawley had wept at her grave and then agreed, collectively, silently, without ever meeting to do it, never to wonder why the ground felt wrong. Except the son in the photograph was visiting a woman whose death had made him rich, and whose continued existence made him—what? Careful. Watchful. A man who checks the post twice.

I counted what the two photographs told me together. Edith had lived at Penhale's through at least two seasons—the geraniums were summer-full in one, cut back and browned in the other. She had aged comfortably. She had been photographed by someone she trusted enough to turn her back on. And Alistair had known where she was—not stumbled on her, not tracked her; the ease in that hand said years of knowing—and had chosen, visit after visit, to leave her breathing.

That was the part that rearranged everything. I had come to Little Hawley prepared to find a murderer. Every instinct I'd honed on three quiet, uncredited investigations was aimed like a compass needle at Alistair Hawley, and the needle hadn't moved, but the map underneath it had. Because a man who knows his mother lives and lets her live is not a man protecting a corpse. He is a man managing a problem he prefers alive. Dead, she could be exhumed, discovered, resurrected by accident—a stranger's recognition, a seaside snapshot sold to a newspaper. Alive, in a boarding house in Brighton, she was a secret he owned. Alive, she could be visited. Warned. Controlled.

Or loved. I made myself write that possibility down too, because a good investigator writes down the possibilities she hates. Perhaps it had begun as her escape and become, absurdly, horribly, a kind of arrangement. Mother and son, bound by a secret instead of blood, meeting on a sunny step with tea. People build families out of worse materials. I have seen them do it.

The third photograph settled the question, and not kindly.

It was taken indoors, badly, the light yellow and the edges soft. Edith sat at a small table by a window, writing. Behind her, blurred by the cheap lens, a mirror reflected the rest of the room—and in the mirror, half-turned away, a man's figure stood near the door. Tall. Grey at the temples even then. His face was lost in the blur, but I didn't need the face. I had spent an evening across a dinner table from that silhouette, watching him carve a pheasant and mention my three solved cases by name, one after another, like a man laying cards down slowly to show me he held the whole suit.

He had been in her room. While she wrote. Standing at the door of the room where she wrote, in the boarding house where she had fled to be free of him.

And she had kept the photograph. She had kept all three, wrapped in oilcloth, sealed in a tin, hidden in an ice house above the flood line in a village she supposedly died in. She had kept the picture of the man at her door the way you keep a bite mark. Proof. Evidence assembled by a woman who understood, better than anyone, that someday someone would come asking, and that whoever came would need more than a feeling.

She had expected me. Or someone like me. The half-finished letter in her bedroom had said *if you are reading this, I am already dead twice*, and I had thought it the melodrama of a frightened woman. Sitting on my narrow bed with the photographs spread out and the rain starting against the window, I revised the assessment. It wasn't melodrama. It was a filing system. Edith Hawley had organized her own resurrection and left the index where a careful person would eventually look.

* * *

I should have sent for Brightwater immediately. I did not. I sat with the dead woman's evidence for the better part of an hour, and I will not pretend that hour was professional. It was the loneliest hour I had spent in Little Hawley, and I had spent some contenders.

Because here is what the photographs did, beyond flipping the case: they made her real. Eleven years of paper had given me a name, a fortune, a cause of death. An afternoon in her preserved bedroom had given me a half-finished letter and the smell of lavender going stale. But a woman squinting on a sunny step, relieved—that was a person. That was a person who had decided her life was unlivable and done the unthinkable thing about it, who had let a town bury an empty coffin over a stranger's body—no. I stopped there. I stopped there because the thought arrived fully formed, the way the worst ones do, wearing no clothes and knocking politely.

An empty coffin requires a body, or the coroner requires an explanation. There had been a body. There had been a burial, a stone, four days of official grief. Which meant that in June of 1979, when Edith Hawley drowned according to every record in the county, a woman had gone into the water—or been put there—and had never been named, never been missed loudly enough to matter, never been looked for by anyone with the standing to insist.

I looked at the third-class ticket to Brighton and saw, for the first time, not a triumph but a transaction. Edith had bought her freedom with someone else's grave. Perhaps she hadn't known. Perhaps Tarnow—I was already certain Tarnow was at the bottom of this somewhere; housekeepers always are, in the best sense—perhaps Tarnow had handled the arrangements and spared her the details. Perhaps. But the woman in the grey cardigan on the sunny step had known *something*, and she had carried it to Brighton and back, and in the end she had come home to die in the house that remembered her, and I could not help thinking that the tide everyone swore had taken her in '79 had simply been waiting, patient as tide is, to give her back.

I wedged the chair more firmly, put the photographs in order, and knocked on the wall twice—the signal Mrs. Quill thought was a plumbing complaint and Brightwater knew was a summons.

* * *

He came up the stairs quietly, which for a man his size is a professional accomplishment, and stood dripping just inside the door because he has manners even when the situation has abandoned all pretense of them.

"Well?" he said.

I handed him the ticket first. I watched him read the date, watched the arithmetic happen behind his eyes—he is quick, quicker than he performs—and watched him arrive at the same ledge I had been standing on alone for an hour.

"Three days," he said. "After the funeral."

"After the funeral. Second class of evidence, first class of implication." I gave him the photographs one at a time, the way you feed a fire. The boarding house. The step, the tea, the hand on the shoulder. He went very still at that one. Then the third, the yellow room, the mirror, the man at the door.

"That's him," he said. Not a question. "Even blurred. That's Alistair."

"It's Alistair. And here's what I can't stop looking at, Inspector—" I tapped the second photograph, the sunny one, the fond hand. "He's relaxed. Men who've just discovered their murdered mother alive are not relaxed. Men who've known for years, who've built their lives around the arrangement, who come down on the train with biscuits and gossip—they're relaxed. This isn't a man confronting a ghost. This is a man keeping an appointment."

Brightwater lowered himself onto the windowsill, which groaned but held, and stared at the photographs spread on the counterpane like a hand of terrible cards. "Then 1979 wasn't murder."

"Not of Edith."

The rain filled the silence for us. Outside, the sea was doing its slow work against the shingle, unhurried, thorough, the way it had been unhurried and thorough about everything in this town for eleven years.

"But somebody drowned," he said finally. "Somebody went in the water in June of '79, and the county buried her under Edith's name, and nobody—" He stopped. I watched him hit the same wall I'd hit, and I confess it gave me a small, shameful comfort to watch him hit it. "There'll be records. Unidentified female, washed ashore, that week. Coroner's file. If the coroner was friendly—"

"He was friendly. We know how friendly."

"Then the file will be thin. But thin isn't blank." He looked up at me, and there it was again, that thing between us that neither of us had named and both of us kept stepping around like wet paint: two people who notice everything, noticing each other noticing. "You understand what this does. If Edith ran—if she *escaped*—then Alistair isn't a murderer. Not yet. Not in '79. He's a conspirator. An accomplice. And men forgive themselves for accomplice. They build whole respectable lives on top of it, magistracies and dinner parties and names in the parish newsletter. It's the murder after that needs explaining."

"Then we'd better find out whether there was a murder after," I said, "before he decides we're the sort of problem that gets managed."

Brightwater gathered the photographs with a care that bordered on reverence and squared their edges against the bedspread. "First thing tomorrow," he said, "we find out who else went into the water in June of 1979. Because somebody did. Somebody young, by the look of the grave's appetite for silence. And Casper—" he paused at the door, rain-wet and serious, "—whoever she was, she's been lying under another woman's name for eleven years. Someone in this town put her there. And I don't think they've forgotten her. I think they're counting on everyone else having."

After he left I sat a long time with the lamp low, listening to the sea, thinking about a girl no one had ever bothered to name, waiting under a stranger's stone for someone to ask her name.

Tomorrow, I promised her. Tomorrow we start with you.
