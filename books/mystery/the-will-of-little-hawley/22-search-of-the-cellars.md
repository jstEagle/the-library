## 22. Search of the Cellars

There is a particular quality to a house that has been lied about for eleven years. I don't mean haunted—I've never met a ghost I couldn't explain with damp and guilt—but something in the way the rooms hold their breath. Hawley House had been holding its breath since 1990, and by Thursday morning I could feel it in my teeth.

Brightwater arrived at nine with two torches, a crowbar he pretended was for legitimate purposes, and the expression of a man who has decided that if his career is going to burn, it may as well be warm while it does.

"You slept," he said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite an accusation either. With Brightwater everything is a question wearing an accusation's coat.

"Barely. You?"

"I made tea at three in the morning and forgot to drink it." He handed me one of the torches. "Found it cold on the counter at six. Decided that was as good a sign as any."

"A sign of what?"

"That we're both past the point where sensible people would have left this town." He looked up at the house—the grey stone, the blind windows, the ivy doing its patient work of strangling the east wall—and exhaled through his nose. "Cellars?"

"Cellars," I agreed. "If Tarnow's right, if Edith came back in 1990 and never left, then she didn't leave *somewhere*. And Alistair isn't a man who drives to the coast to dispose of problems. He's a magistrate. He keeps things close. He keeps things *tidy*."

"Tidy," Brightwater repeated, tasting the word like something off a bad oyster. "Eleven years tidy."

"Eleven years exactly. The mortar will tell us."

I'd said it lightly, but my hand wasn't light on the torch. Because here is what nobody tells you about finding bodies: you spend the rest of your life knowing how easy it is. Not committing the act—God, no—but understanding, with terrible clarity, how a person might do it and sleep afterward, provided they were the sort of person who thought of murder as administration. Alistair Hawley struck me as a man whose correspondence was filed alphabetically. Men like that don't bury their mothers in the garden, under a rosebush, where every spadeful of earth is a confession. They put them somewhere with a door.

* * *

The cellar stairs were behind the kitchen, down a flight of stone steps worn into shallow smiles by two centuries of feet. Mrs. Tarnow stood at the top with a ring of keys she did not offer and would not put away.

"There's nothing down there but wine and spiders," she said.

"Then we'll be quick about the spiders," Brightwater said pleasantly, and went past her.

She caught my sleeve as I followed. Her grip was stronger than her hands looked. "Miss Coldharbour. Whatever you find—" She stopped. Started again. "She was kind to me. That's all I'll say down here. Whatever you find, remember somebody loved her first."

"Somebody loved her last, too, Mrs. Tarnow," I said. "That's rather the point."

She let go. I felt her eyes between my shoulder blades all the way down the stairs.

The cellar ran the length of the house, low-ceilinged, brick-floored, lit by our torches and two grates at ground level through which the rain made a sound like distant applause. Wine racks lined the left wall—oak, floor to ceiling, bottles sleeping on their sides in the dark with their dust coats undisturbed. On the right: a workbench furred with rust, a chest freezer humming to itself in the corner like something digesting, stacked deck chairs, a child's bicycle gone to lace, and the general sediment of a family's refusal to throw anything away.

And cold. Cold the way cellars are, but also cold the way churches are—deliberate, preserved, a temperature maintained by something more than stone.

Brightwater walked the length of the room slowly, sweeping his torch along the wall. I watched him the way I'd learned to watch him over these weeks: not his face, which performed competence beautifully, but his hands, which told the truth. They stopped moving when he reached the far corner.

"Casper."

I came to him. The wine racks ended four feet short of the north wall, leaving a gap—a dead space behind the last shelf, shadowed, smelling of stone and old air. Except.

"Smell it," he said quietly.

I did. Under the must and the tannin and the freezer's electric breath there was something else. Lime. Fresh lime, sharp and mineral, the smell of mortar that hasn't finished becoming stone. Mortar has a working life of perhaps a year before it stops announcing itself. This smelled like it had been mixed within the decade.

"Fresh," I said. "Relatively."

"Behind the racks." He crouched, played the torch along the baseboard of the shelving unit, and I saw what he'd seen: scuff marks on the brick floor, wide arcs, the sort of dragging marks a heavy oak rack leaves when someone has pulled it away from a wall and pushed it back. More than once. Dust everywhere else in this cellar lay in unbroken grey velvet, but here the floor had been swept—not well, not recently enough, but swept.

"Help me move it," Brightwater said.

We pulled together. The rack groaned, bottles chattering on their sides like teeth, and slid eighteen inches from the wall with a shriek of oak on brick that brought Mrs. Tarnow's footsteps to the top of the stairs and held them there, frozen.

Behind it: a wall. Brick, load-bearing, ordinary—except that it wasn't. The surrounding cellar walls were old brick, soft-edged, mortared with the pale crumbling lime of the 1800s. This section was newer brick, harder-fired, and the mortar between the courses was smooth, deliberate, *finished*, troweled by someone with patience and pride. Someone who had wanted it to look like wall.

"How recent?" Brightwater asked. His voice had gone flat, the voice he used for crime scenes, and I realized I'd never heard him use it for me.

I pressed my thumb near the bottom course, where the cellar's damp rose highest. The mortar gave slightly, cool and faintly yielding, like skin. "Within ten years. Possibly within five. It's been kept dry—that gap behind the rack, the rack itself acting as a screen—it's aged slower than it should have." I stood back. My heart was doing something unprofessional. "Alistair had this done after 1990. After she came back."

"After he found her," Brightwater said.

Neither of us said the next part. We didn't need to. The wall said it for us, in fresh lime and careful trowel-work: *she never left because she was never allowed to.*

* * *

"We need a warrant," Brightwater said, twenty minutes later, standing in the yard with the rain coming down like a decision already made. "Proper one. I can't open that wall on a smell and a hunch, however good the hunch smells."

"You can if you believe the evidence is being destroyed."

"It's been sitting there eleven years, Casper. It'll sit another forty-eight hours."

"Will it?" I looked back at the house. At the east window on the second floor, where a lamp had burned two nights ago in a sealed room. At the drive, empty now, but I remembered the car with the warm engine, the driver who was never quite where I turned to look. "Someone turned Edith's letter page, Brightwater. Someone is reading over our shoulders. The moment they think we've found the wall, that wall comes down and whatever's behind it goes into the sea, and the sea has always been very obliging to this family."

He was quiet. Rain dripped off his nose. "You think he knows."

"I think he's known since the night the lamp burned. I think he's deciding whether we know. And I think the difference between those two states is whether I'm found floating off the shingle next week or merely asked pointed questions at dinner parties for the rest of my life."

"So we watch it. Round the clock. I'll put Pell—"

"No." I said it too fast and saw him register the speed. "Pell locks doors for money. We've established that. Who else does this town have? Constable Dawes?"

"Dawes is thirty years past caring and forty years past seeing."

"Then we watch it ourselves. In shifts. Tonight, tomorrow, until your warrant lands." I heard myself and almost laughed. "Look at us. Two grown professionals camping in a wine cellar to guard a wall from a magistrate."

"Two grown professionals," Brightwater agreed, "who are going to catch pneumonia and be posthumously embarrassed." But he was already checking his watch, dividing the night into watches, and I understood that we had passed some border together without either of us marking it. There was no version of this anymore where he went home to his neat flat and I went back to London and we each filed the impossible paper away. The wall had seen to that. Walls do that—they make the space on either side of them into one room.

* * *

We took the first shift together, which was tactically indefensible and emotionally inevitable. He spread his coat over a stack of deck chairs; I sat on an upturned crate with my torch off, learning the dark. The cellar at night was a different country. The freezer cycled on and off with a sigh. Water ticked somewhere in the dark. Once, unmistakably, a bottle shifted in its rack with a small glass click, and we both came upright with our hearts in our ears, and then sat back down and didn't joke about it, because neither of us could find anything in it to joke with.

"Do you ever think about them," Brightwater said, around two in the morning, "the ones you solved? The three."

"Constantly."

"Do you ever want credit?"

I considered lying, decided he'd hear it, and told the truth instead. "Every day. And every day I remember what credit costs. The moment your name is attached to a death, you stop being a person who noticed things and become a person who *attracts* them. Grief turns on you. So does guilt. And the ones who did it—" I turned my torch briefly toward the bricked gap, off, on, off. "The ones who did it start thinking about you the way Alistair is thinking about me right now. I stayed invisible because invisible survives."

"And how's that working out?"

"The drowned man was wearing my coat," I said. "So. Poorly."

He laughed—softly, so it wouldn't echo—and then, in the dark, his hand found mine on the crate. Not a declaration. An anchor. We sat like that while the freezer sighed and the water ticked and the wall waited, and I thought: *this is what it costs, being no one. And this is what it costs, finally being someone.* Both bills were coming due at once.

At half five, footsteps crossed the kitchen above us. Slow. Deliberate. Pausing at the head of the cellar stairs long enough for both of us to hear the pause, and then moving on. A door closed somewhere in the upper house. Neither of us moved for a full minute.

"He knows we're here," Brightwater breathed.

"Yes," I said. "That was the point of the footsteps."

* * *

The warrant came through at noon the next day, signed by a district judge who owed Brightwater a favor from a case I suspected I'd never hear about. Brightwater collected a constable from the next town over—one who could still see—and a mason from further than that, because we agreed that whatever was behind the wall deserved to be opened by hands that knew how to be gentle with old structures.

Mrs. Tarnow watched from the kitchen doorway as we carried the tools down. She had dressed properly, as if for church. I understood: she was attending something.

The mason worked from the top course down, drilling out the mortar line by line, and the noise in that low brick room was enormous and holy, like the house clearing its throat after eleven years. Brick by brick the wall came apart, each one passed out and stacked, and the hole behind it breathed out air that had been shut in since before the millennium—air with lime in it, and stone, and underneath, faintly, sweetly, terribly, something else.

I knew that smell. I had hoped never to know it again.

Brightwater's hand found my shoulder. "Casper."

"I know."

The mason stopped. Looked at us. Looked at the black gap where the last bricks had been, where our torchlight went in and came back changed.

"Shall I go on?" he asked.

"Yes," said Brightwater.

"No," I said, at the same moment—and then, because only one of us was allowed to be a coward, "Yes. Go on."

The mason took out the final brick at the level of a human heart, and the light went in, and what it found there I will carry to my own grave, wherever and whenever that comes, and I only pray no one has to read a will over me twice.

Because there, in the dry dark where the water could never reach, folded with a care that broke something in me beyond repair, lay a woman's cardigan—grey wool, moth-nipped, buttoned—and beneath it, the shape the wall had been built around.

Edith Hawley had come home in 1990.

Her son had made sure she never left again.
