## 23. Edith

The mortar was wrong. That was the first thing I knew for certain, standing in a cellar that smelled of cold stone and old wine, holding a torch whose batteries were dying by increments, as if the dark were charging rent.

Everything in Hawley House was old and proud of it. The wine racks were Victorian. The flagstones were older. The damp was older than the damp. But the mortar sealing the alcove behind the third rack of claret was pale, gritty, and no more than a dozen years old. Somebody had bricked up a piece of this cellar in the early nineties and then gone upstairs and lived his life as a magistrate, a son, a mourner, a man whose mother had drowned.

"Fresh," Brightwater said. He had a penknife out and was worrying a corner of it the way a man worries a tooth. "Not fresh-fresh. But fresher than anything else down here by a century."

"Mrs. Tarnow said the cellars were never touched after the flood of '82."

"Mrs. Tarnow," said Brightwater, "has been lying to us with great tenderness for two weeks. I'm inclined to forgive her, but I'm not inclined to believe her about the masonry."

We had come down with a crowbar, a torch apiece, and the particular silence of two people who have stopped pretending they're here for an innocent reason. Brightwater had brought the crowbar from his own car, off the books, off the record, off the map. If we were wrong, we were trespassing in a magistrate's cellar with a housebreaking tool. If we were right—

I didn't finish the thought. I had been refusing to finish that thought since the ice house, since the tin box, since Tarnow's face when I'd asked her, gently, *when did Edith come back?* and she had looked at me the way people look at a door they've been keeping shut for eleven years and realized the lock was never going to hold again.

*She returned in 1990. For the diary. She never left.*

The penknife scraped. A crumb of pale mortar fell, then another, then a small grey avalanche, and Brightwater put his shoulder to the crowbar and the wall gave a sound I will hear for the rest of my life—a sound like a held breath finally released, brick grinding against brick, eleven years of silence coming apart in our hands.

"Steady," he said, though I don't know whether he meant the wall or me.

The first bricks came out whole. The second row crumbled. I pulled the third with my gloved hands, one at a time, setting them on the flagstones in a neat row, because some part of my mind insisted on order, on procedure, on being the professional I had spent fifteen years training myself to be, while the rest of me understood that I was opening a grave in a wine cellar and no amount of procedure was going to make that a normal thing to do.

The gap widened to the width of a hand. Cold air came out. Not stale air—cold air, cellar air, but carrying something underneath it, something sweetish and mineral and wrong, and I stepped back, and Brightwater went very still beside me, and neither of us said anything for a long moment because there was nothing left to say. We both knew. We had known, I think, since the shoe on the tideline. Since the letter that said *I am already dead twice.*

"We could stop," Brightwater said quietly. "Call it in properly. Warrant, photographer, the whole circus."

"He's had eleven years to arrange the circus," I said. "If we stop now, by morning that wall will be a hundred years old and we'll be two hysterics with a crowbar. No. We look. We know what we're looking at. Then we call it in."

He nodded once, and put his shoulder to the wall again, and I have never admired anyone more than I admired him in that moment, because his hands were shaking and he did it anyway.

* * *

The alcove was small—three feet wide, perhaps five deep, the sort of space a house might have for coal or kindling. It was not full. That was the detail that undid me, later, when I let myself be undone. It was not a mass grave, not a horror of the kind you read about. It was nearly empty. A folded blanket, gone to grey felt. A woman's cardigan, moth-eaten, its pearl buttons intact. And Edith Hawley.

I will not describe her in detail. Not because I am squeamish—I have seen the sea give things back, and the sea is not gentle—but because she has been described enough by circumstance, by the position of her bones, by the eleven years of dark. What I will say is this: she was lying on her side, knees drawn up, the posture of a person who had been cold and had tried to keep warm. Someone had laid her down with a degree of care. Someone had put the cardigan over her. And then someone had built a wall.

"God," Brightwater said. Just that. Just the word, said the way you'd say it in a church.

I crouched at the edge of the alcove and did not go in. It seemed important, in some way I couldn't have defended to a coroner, not to step inside her space. Eleven years she had been kept in this house. Eleven years her son had eaten dinner above her, given interviews above her, sat as a magistrate in judgment over other people's truths above her. The least I could do was not put my boot where her blanket was.

"Look," Brightwater said, and his torch found the far corner.

A tin box. The twin of the one in the ice house—the same brand, the same rust-bloomed seams, the same careful stenciling. Except this one was open, and beside it, wrapped in oilcloth that had done its work faithfully, was a leather diary with a broken clasp, and loose pages, and a fountain pen, and a small bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone the colour of weak tea.

"She came back for this," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened. "The letter said the rest of the proof was where the water couldn't reach. The ice house was half of it. This is the rest. She came back in 1990 to collect it, and he found her, and—" I stopped. The sentence had an end and I could see it clearly and I was not going to say it in her hearing, even now, even knowing what hearing amounts to for the dead.

"Read it," Brightwater said. "If there's anything that names him. I can't touch anything until this is a crime scene, but you can read. Reading isn't evidence handling. Reading is just—" he faltered, and I understood that he was giving me a job because a job is the only thing that stands between a person and collapse. "Reading is just reading."

So I sat on the cold flagstone with my back against the wine rack, in the cellar of Hawley House, with the wall broken open behind me and the torch propped against a bottle of claret that had outlasted its owner's owner, and I read Edith Hawley's diary, the pages that had been missing from the tin box, the pages she had died coming back for.

* * *

The handwriting changed over the years. That was the first thing I noticed—the 1979 entries were a firm, slanted hand, a woman who signed cheques and chaired committees and did not suffer fools. By the entries she'd written in exile at the seaside boarding house, the hand had loosened, grown rounder, almost cheerful in places. And then the 1990 entries, the last ones, were cramped and hurried, written in the dark, in a house where she had to be a ghost in her own rooms.

I will not reproduce the whole of it here. Some of it is hers, and private, and I have already taken more from her than anyone should take from the dead. But the parts that matter, the parts the law will want, went like this:

*Alistair came to the boarding house in June. I thought he had come to make peace. He brought flowers and a lawyer's smile, and he asked me to sign a paper confirming the 1983 codicil was made under duress. I told him the codicil was the truest thing I ever signed. He sat with his flowers and looked at me for a long time and said, "Mother, you are the only person alive who can ruin me, and I find I've grown very fond of being unruined."*

*I understood then what I should have understood in 1979. I thought I had escaped him. You cannot escape a man like that. You can only be elsewhere.*

And then, the last entry, dated the fourteenth of September, 1990:

*I came back for the diary because the ice house is not safe anymore—Pell has padlocked it, and padlocks are a language. I will take the pages and go to London, to a solicitor, to anyone. I have been dead eleven years and I am tired of being a ghost in my own house. If I am careful, if I leave before he comes for Michaelmas, I will—*

The entry stopped mid-sentence. The pen had been set down. There was no entry after it.

I sat in the cold and looked at that unfinished sentence for a long time. *If I leave before he comes for Michaelmas, I will—* She had been interrupted. Not by a dramatic moment, not by a struggle—the pen was capped, the diary closed, the oilcloth folded. She had been interrupted by footsteps on the cellar stairs, I imagine, and she had done what she had done for eleven years: she had hidden, and waited, and believed that patience would save her, because patience had saved her once before.

It hadn't saved her twice.

"Casper." Brightwater's voice, very low. "There's something else. Under the blanket edge."

I made myself look. He was pointing his torch at the space between the folded blanket and the wall, and there, half-tucked, was an envelope. Modern. The kind sold in any stationer's. And on it, in Alistair Hawley's handwriting—the handwriting I had seen on a hundred letters of administration, on the funeral instructions, on the thank-you notes he'd sent after his mother's memorial service—two words.

*Mother's things.*

It was an inventory. Dated November 1990. Alistair Hawley had made a list of his mother's effects—the cardigan, the diary, the letters, the pen—and he had dated it, and he had filed it, because he was a meticulous man, and the meticulousness that made him a good magistrate made him an excellent murderer. He had not wanted the papers. He had wanted the *silence*, and he had catalogued what he was sealing away the way a careful man catalogs a shipment, and then he had built the wall, and he had gone upstairs, and he had kept her.

"Eleven years," I said. I heard my own voice and didn't recognize it. "He kept her in the house for eleven years. He let us walk past her. He let *Tarnow* dust around her. He let me stand in his mother's bedroom and read her letter and he poured me sherry and asked after my firm."

"Casper—"

"He *knew*." I was on my feet now, and the fury had arrived all at once, the way it always does, the way it did over the ferryman and the archivist and every other quiet death this town had agreed not to look at. "He knew when I found the will. He knew when I found the shoe. He knew when I pulled the 1979 file. He has been watching me dig up his mother for a month and every time I got close he moved a piece—Tarnow frightened, Pell warned, Vera terrified, a dead man in my coat—and the whole time she was *down here*. Behind his wine. Eleven years, Brightwater. He made her a fixture. He made her a *shelf*."

"Yes," Brightwater said. He didn't try to calm me. That's one of the things I had already learned to trust about him: he never tried to calm me, he just stood next to the fury like a man standing next to weather. "And in about six hours he's going to be in a cell, and in about six months he's going to be at trial, and every piece of his meticulousness is going to be read aloud in court by a barrister with a good voice. But right now, Casper—right now we are two people standing in a hole we dug without a warrant, and if we don't get this done properly, he walks. So." He held out his hand for the diary, and I saw that his own hand was not steady, and I loved him a little for not pretending otherwise. "Phone. Now. And then we stand guard over her until the proper people come, and nobody touches anything, and we let the law do what the law does."

"Which is arrive late," I said, "and take credit."

"Occasionally," he said, "it arrives exactly on time, and it brings a blanket for the witness, and it stands in a cold cellar and doesn't leave. I've seen it happen." He was already dialling. "Stay with her. I'll call it in."

So I stayed. I sat down again on the flagstone, facing the alcove, and I kept watch over Edith Hawley, who had been dead twice and hidden twice and patient beyond all reason, and I told her—out loud, because the cellar was private, because it was the only confession I had to give—that we had her now. That the wall was down. That the diary was going to be read by people who were not her son, in a room that was not a cellar, and that the codicil she'd signed in 1983 was going to do exactly what she'd built it to do, even if it took eleven years longer than she'd planned.

*You engineered your own trial,* I thought. *You just didn't live to attend it. That's the one thing he took from you that I can't give back.*

The torch guttered. Above us, the house creaked through its old bones, and I thought of the lamp in her bedroom, the page turned, the sense of someone reading over our shoulders, and I understood it at last—not a haunting, not a warning. A summons. She had been trying to get us down here since the night we arrived. The will, the letter, the ice house, the shoe—she had left a trail like breadcrumbs, and she had waited eleven years for someone with a crowbar and a bad habit of not letting things stay closed.

Brightwater finished his calls and crouched beside me, and for a while neither of us spoke. The cold came up through the stone and into our coats. Somewhere above, a door closed—Mrs. Tarnow, I supposed, moving through the house she had kept for a dead woman, knowing at last that the keeping was over.

"Whoever's in the grave," Brightwater said quietly. It wasn't a question. We had both been circling it for days, and now, with the real Edith behind a broken wall, it stood in the cellar with us, unavoidable. "The woman they buried in 1979. The one the tide gave them instead."

"I know," I said.

"Because Alistair didn't kill her. He couldn't have—she drowned the same week Edith 'drowned,' and he was in London, we checked. Which means Edith didn't just fake her death. Someone *else* died for it. A girl nobody ever named, and Alistair has spent eleven years being blackmailed by his mother's ghost over a grave that isn't even hers." He rubbed his face. "The warrant's coming. Brightwater's reputation, what's left of it, is going on the line with it. But Casper—the moment we arrest him for this, he's going to fight. And his first move will be you. The drowned man. Your coat. He's been building that case for a month."

"I know that too."

"Then say it out loud, so we both know we're not pretending."

I looked at the alcove, at the grey blanket and the pearl buttons and the tin box with its terrible inventory. I thought about the archivist in my scarf, face-down on the shingle, and about a magistrate's voice over dinner saying my three solved cases like a man laying cards on a table.

"He's going to try to drown me," I said. "On paper first, and then, if paper doesn't work, the old-fashioned way. It's the only trick he knows. The tide does the work and the town signs the certificate."

"Then we'd best be faster than the tide," said Brightwater.

Headlights swept the cellar window above us—the first of them, the proper people, arriving late and taking credit, and welcome to every bit of it. I stood, and my knees complained, and I took one last look at Edith Hawley before the photographers came to do what I had done: bear witness.

*Hold on,* I told her. *The reading of the will is about to begin.*

And upstairs, faintly, through eleven years of stone, I would have sworn the house exhaled.
