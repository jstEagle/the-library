## 30. Brightwater Risks Everything

Inspector Brightwater did not knock. He never knocked; knocking was for men who had something to lose.

I opened my lodgings door at seven in the morning to find him standing on the step in a suit I'd never seen before—dark grey, sharply pressed, the kind of suit a man wears to his own funeral or someone else's trial. His tie was knotted with a precision that suggested he'd done it twice and hated it both times.

"You're dressed for church," I said.

"I'm dressed for war," he said. "It's the same tailoring."

He stepped past me into the sitting room without being asked, which was unlike him, and set his hat on the table with a care that was very like him. Then he stood there a moment, hands at his sides, looking at nothing in particular—the window, the cold grate, the teapot I hadn't yet filled—and I understood that whatever he'd come to say, he'd rehearsed it on the walk over and the rehearsal had failed him.

"Sit down," I said. "I'll make tea. You can be brave in your own time."

"I'm not here to be brave." He sat, though. That was something. "I'm here to tell you what I've already done, so you can't talk me out of it. You have a way of talking people out of things, Casper. It's not even an argument when you do it. It's more like weather."

I put the kettle on because my hands wanted occupation more than my ears wanted silence. "Go on, then."

"At eight o'clock this morning I filed to reopen the Hawley drowning. Formally. On the record. Under my own name." He said it the way a man reads out a sentence passed against him. "I cited the exhumation of the grave marked Edith Hawley as the object of the application. I've requested the Home Office pathologist rather than our own coroner, since our own coroner is—"

"Old friends with the magistrate."

"—old friends with the magistrate," he agreed. "I've also written to the Chief Constable, cc'ing nobody, so that when this goes wrong there will be exactly one signature at the bottom of it. Mine."

The kettle began its low complaint toward boiling. I didn't turn around right away. There are moments when a woman's face is better left unwatched, and I suspected mine was doing something unprofessional.

"Brightwater," I said finally, measuring tea leaves into the pot with more attention than they deserved, "do you know what happens to inspectors who reopen closed cases in towns where everyone agreed to close them?"

"They get promoted," he said. "To civilian."

"Exactly."

"And do you know what happens to women who keep pulling threads in towns that have decided the sweater is finished?" He leaned forward. "They end up on a shingle beach wearing their own stolen coat, with a note in their pocket bearing their own name. I've seen how this town treats people who notice things. I signed three of those notices myself."

That landed harder than he could have known—or perhaps he knew precisely, which was why he'd said it. I carried those three cases the way other people carry a limp: quietly, always, in bad weather most of all. Three deaths ruled accidental. Three deaths I had proved otherwise, in the privacy of my own notebooks, without ever taking credit, because credit is a lamp and lamps attract moths and some moths wear magistrates' robes.

"You were young," I said, setting his cup down. "You told me. Told to sign."

"I was twenty-six and grateful to be told anything." He wrapped both hands around the cup but didn't drink. "Do you know what I've been doing since the night we found Edith behind the brick? Since the photographs? Since the nameplate?"

"Losing sleep."

"Reading." He looked up at me then, and there was something in his face I hadn't seen before—not the sparring sharpness, not the careful suspicion, but a kind of settled resolve, like water finding its level. "Eleven years of files. Every case this force touched between 1979 and now. And do you know what I found, Casper? I found that every time Alistair Hawley's name came within a mile of an investigation, the investigation developed a sudden respect for ambiguity. The ferryman who drowned. The maid who vanished. The archivist who washed up in your coat. Ambiguity, everywhere, like damp."

The ferryman. I felt that one in my ribs. Thomas Grebe, dead eight months after reporting he'd picked up a living woman matching Edith's description—a report buried so deep in a logbook that only Pell's nameplate had ever led anyone back to it. My old pattern confirming itself: everyone who touched the truth drowns.

"So you're going to dig her up," I said. "The wrong woman in Edith's grave."

"The girl," Brightwater said. "Whoever she is. She's waited eleven years under a stone with another woman's name on it. That's two thefts, Casper—her life and her name—and I mean to give at least one of them back." He paused. "Alistair will fight it. His friend the coroner will fight it. Half the town will fight it, because half the town has spent eleven years not thinking about it and doesn't want to start. By Friday I'll likely be suspended. By Christmas I may be selling insurance in Colchester."

I sat down across from him. Outside, the rain had started again—that fine, patient Little Hawley rain that seemed less like weather than like the sea trying the windows.

"Why?" I asked. It wasn't the question I meant. We both knew it wasn't the question I meant.

He considered his tea. "Because for nine years I've had a file in my head that wouldn't close. Edith Hawley, drowned, case closed in four days, and me standing at the inquest with a list of small wrongnesses—no water in the lungs worth mentioning, no shoes recovered, a husband—" he caught himself, corrected, "—a *son* who answered questions before they were asked. I told myself the list was nothing. I told myself that for nine years. And then a woman walked into this town reading a will that shouldn't exist, and she noticed everything I'd noticed, and she wasn't afraid of it, and I thought: *there it is. That's what it looks like when a person refuses.*"

"Casper," he said, and then stopped, and I realized he'd used my first name for the first time, and that he knew it, and that he'd done it deliberately, the way a man steps off a ledge knowing the drop.

"Yes," I said, to save him the trouble.

"I'm not saying it well. I don't say things well. I say them in reports, in triplicate, with exhibits attached." He set down the cup. "So let me say it in triplicate. Exhibit one: I am reopening the case because it is right, and if it costs me my career, that is arithmetic, not sacrifice. Exhibit two: I am doing it today, before Alistair can move again, because the last person who helped you ended up in the sea and I intend to be inconveniently alive. Exhibit three—"

He stopped again. The rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere down the lane a gull made its opinion known.

"Exhibit three," he said, quieter, "is that somewhere in the last month I stopped wanting to solve this case *near* you and started wanting to solve it *with* you, and I'm given to understand those are different propositions, and I'd like to find out by how much. Not now. Not while there's a body in a cellar and a magistrate building gallows with your name on them. But after. If there is an after. I'm telling you now so that if tomorrow goes badly, you'll know it wasn't duty that put me on that step this morning. Duty got me up the hill. Something else brought me the rest of the way."

I have faced down murderers, magistrates, and one memorably hostile vicar, and I have never once been at a loss for words. I was at a loss for words.

What I managed, after a silence long enough to embarrass us both, was: "Your tea's going cold."

"I know," he said. "I'm given to understand that's traditional."

* * *

By ten o'clock the news had reached Hawley House the way news reaches small towns: instantly, inaccurately, and with everyone already holding an opinion.

Mrs. Tarnow met me in the hall with a face like a slammed door. "They're saying the inspector's dug up the churchyard."

"He hasn't dug up anything yet. He's applied to."

"That's not what Mrs. Ellery at the post office says, and Mrs. Ellery was there."

"Mrs. Ellery was not there. Mrs. Ellery was born knowing things that haven't happened yet; it's a gift."

But beneath my flippancy I was counting hours. An exhumation order, once granted, moved fast; once blocked, it moved never. Alistair would know by lunchtime. Alistair would have made calls by teatime. The question was whether Brightwater's paperwork could outrun the magistrate's friendships, and I have never in my life trusted paperwork to outrun anything.

Pell found me at the garden gate that afternoon. He came up the drive with his cap in his hands and his eyes on the gravel, a man approaching a dog he wasn't sure was friendly.

"Miss Coldharbour." He cleared his throat. "Thought you'd want to know. There's been a man round asking at the Anchor about you. Not police. Suit too good, shoes too clean. Asking when you come and go, whether you lodge alone."

"Did you tell him?"

"No, miss." He shifted his weight. "And I'll tell you why, since we're being honest these days and I find I don't care for it much. Mr. Hawley paid me to lock that boathouse and burn that boat, and I took his money, and I've hated myself quiet-like for thirty years. I kept the nameplate instead of burning it. Insurance, I called it. Cowardice with a lock on it, more like." He looked up then, and his eyes were wet and furious in equal measure. "I'm done being paid to look away. So when a clean-shoed man asks after you, I look at my boots and remember nothing. But you ought to know he's asking."

"Thank you, Pell."

"Don't thank me." He jammed the cap on his head. "Just don't walk home the same way twice."

* * *

Brightwater came for me at dusk, and we drove together to the station house, where he showed me the morning's work laid out on his desk like a hand of cards he intended to play all at once: the application, sworn statements from Vera and Pell and Tarnow, the nameplate photographed and logged, the diary pages copied in Edith's own hand naming her son.

"It'll take forty-eight hours for the order," he said. "Less, if the Chief Constable is feeling brave. More, if Alistair's friend finds a technicality. In the meantime—" he tapped the diary pages, "—this is the spine of it. Her handwriting, his name, dated 1984. No court in England looks at that and calls it ambiguity."

"And Alistair knows you have it."

"Alistair has known since the cellars." He shrugged into his coat. "Which means the next forty-eight hours are the dangerous ones. A cornered man with a title, a fortune, and a friend in the coroner's office doesn't wait for exhumations. He acts."

We stood a moment in the lamplight of the station, and I saw it plainly then—the thing he'd tried to say on my doorstep and had mostly said in exhibits. He was afraid. Not of Alistair, not of suspension, not of the sea. He was afraid the way a man is afraid when he has finally put down the thing he's been carrying for nine years and discovered his arms don't know what to do without it. And underneath the fear, steady as bedrock: he had chosen. Publicly, irreversibly, in triplicate.

"You understand," I said, "that once that order is granted, there's no going back. For either of us. They'll dig up that grave and find a stranger, and every soul in this town who slept well for eleven years will need someone to blame, and we're the only candidates with clean shoes."

"I've thought about it."

"Have you also thought about the fact that I'm already named a person of interest, my flat's been searched, and a dead man was wearing my coat? Because attaching yourself to me professionally is rather like attaching yourself to a lightning rod."

"Casper." He smiled—not the sparring smile, something older and less defended. "I've read your file. All of it, this week. Do you know what it says? Nothing. Nine years, three cases, and nothing—because you never took credit, never gave interviews, never left a fingerprint on any of it. The invisible woman." He shook his head slowly. "And then you came here, and you let yourself be seen, and it nearly killed you. So no—I'm not attaching myself to a lightning rod. I'm standing next to one and holding the earth wire. Somebody has to."

Outside, the rain had thickened. Through the station window the high street was a smear of lamplight, and beyond it, invisible but present as pressure, the sea worked at the shingle where a man in my coat had come ashore, and further out still, the tide turned over its long inventory of kept things.

The telephone on Brightwater's desk rang.

He answered, listened, and I watched his face do something complicated—relief and alarm arriving in the same post. He set the receiver down with exaggerated gentleness, the way one handles a thing that might go off.

"The Chief Constable," he said. "The order's granted. Exhumation Thursday morning, six o'clock, Home Office pathologist attending." A breath. "And Casper—one more thing. The call came through five minutes after a car was seen turning out of the churchyard road. A car with a magistrate's flag on the bonnet."

Thursday, then. Two days. Whatever Alistair meant to do about a hole being opened in his family's plot, he had two days to do it—and he had just been seen driving away from the churchyard itself.

Brightwater picked up his hat.

"Come on," he said. "Let's go see what a man does in a graveyard at night when he's spent eleven years keeping a secret underground."

I took my coat from the hook—the real one, this time, accounted for and on my own shoulders—and followed him out into the rain.
