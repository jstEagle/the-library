## 35. Confrontation at the Boathouse

I had always assumed that if I ever died badly, it would be in a filing room, crushed beneath a toppled cabinet of unclaimed estates, and that the coroner would write *misadventure* on my certificate with the same bored pen he'd used for everyone else. It had never occurred to me that my death might be staged as a rehearsal—water rising, a witness hidden, a confession coaxed out of a man who believed himself untouchable. But then, most of what had happened since I arrived at Little Hawley had never occurred to me, and here I was anyway, sitting in a rotting boathouse with a wax cylinder in my handbag and the tide coming in.

The plan was simple, which is how you know it was mine and not Brightwater's. His plans involve roadblocks and warrants and men named Sergeant. Mine involve patience and a well-placed rumor.

I had let the rumor fall the way you drop a crumb for a bird: casually, carelessly, to Pell's nephew over a pint at the Anchor, loud enough for the man in the corner who drank nothing and watched everything. *The recording's not safe in London,* I'd said. *I'm moving it tomorrow. One last night in the ice house, then it goes to a solicitor's strongroom where no one can touch it.*

A lie, of course. The cylinder had been copied twice already—one copy with Brightwater, one in a place Alistair Hawley would never think to look because it involved actual work: a bank deposit box under Vera's name, opened that morning while she gripped my arm so hard I still had the bruises. The original sat in my handbag, which sounds reckless until you understand its purpose. Bait is only bait if it's carried.

Alistair wouldn't come for copies he couldn't verify. He would come for the thing itself, from me, in a place where the ground was his and the water was his and there were no witnesses worth the name.

So I gave him the place. The boathouse. Pell's old territory, padlocked since spring, floor scrubbed too clean by a man paid to forget things. At half past nine I let myself in through the door whose lock Pell had shown me how to open with a flat iron, and I settled onto an upturned dinghy to wait, and I listened to the sea come for us both.

* * *

He came at ten, which told me something. A guilty man comes in the dark of two in the morning; a confident one comes at a civilized hour, when any encounter can be dressed as coincidence. Ten o'clock was the hour of a magistrate taking an evening stroll.

His torch found me before I found him. "Miss Coldharbour." The beam held steady on my face, courteous as a handshake. "Pell said the lock was broken. I see he was mistaken."

"Pell says a great many things," I said. "Most of them rehearsed."

He stepped inside and closed the door behind him, which I confess raised my pulse more than anything else he did. The boathouse is a single long room, timber-framed, open at the seaward end where the slipway slides into black water. The tide was three hours shy of high but already licking up the slip, and the sound of it filled the space the way guilt fills a house—gradually, then completely.

"You've made rather a nuisance of yourself," he said pleasantly. He was wearing a beautiful coat. I remember thinking it was a beautiful coat even as I understood I might be looking at the last expensive thing I ever saw. "First the will. Then the archives. Then poor Mr. Fenwick's effects—you went through them, I'm told, though the police seem oddly incurious about why a stranger came to Little Hawley asking questions about my mother."

"Mr. Fenwick," I said, "was an archivist. My mother taught me that the dead keep better records than the living. He was hired eleven years ago to authenticate a document. He finished the job too well."

"And now you have a recording." He said it lightly, testing the weight of it. "My mother's voice. Telling stories."

"Naming names," I corrected. "There's a difference. Stories end. Names go on file."

He smiled. It was a good smile; I'll grant him that. It was the smile of a man who had spent thirty years being liked in rooms where liking him was compulsory. "You know what strikes me about you, Miss Coldharbour? You talk like someone who believes paper protects people. I've read the same history you have. Paper burns. People drown. The tide has been very good to this family."

"Yes," I said. "I've noticed. Twice in one generation, and both times the drowned woman was inconvenient to you."

Something moved behind his eyes—not anger yet, but the approach of it, like weather seen far off at sea. "Careful."

"I'm always careful. That's rather my whole character." I stood, slowly, and set my handbag on the dinghy between us, and I watched his gaze drop to it the way a drunkard's drops to a bottle. "Edith Hawley signed a codicil in 1983. She disinherited you unless you confessed. Confessed to what, Alistair? Say it aloud. No one's listening."

The lie slid out of me smooth as oil, and somewhere in the dark above the boat racks, in the loft where the nets used to hang, Inspector Brightwater crouched with a recorder running and, I hoped, her heart in her mouth.

* * *

He didn't confess at once. They never do. First he circled, the way men do, telling me the story he'd told himself so long it had worn smooth as a pebble.

"She was ill," he said. "In 1990. Confused. She came back to the house in the night—Tarnow let her in, sentimental old fool—and she wouldn't stay. She wanted to leave again. Take the diary, take everything, start over a third time as if the first two times hadn't cost enough." He walked along the boat racks, trailing his fingers over the hulls like a man choosing a coffin. "Do you know what it costs, Miss Coldharbour, to be the son of a woman who won't stay dead?"

"I imagine it costs exactly what it cost her to be the mother of a man who wouldn't let her live."

He turned. The weather had arrived. "She was going to ruin me. The codicil, the diary, the ferryman—she'd kept every receipt of my sins for twenty years. If she'd reached London—" He stopped. Recalibrated. The magistrate's voice returned, reasonable, regretful. "It was an argument. She fell. The cellar steps are steep; you've seen them. I panicked. I've regretted it every day since."

"Eleven years of regret," I said, "and you bricked her into the wine cellar."

"I *kept* her." For a moment his voice cracked and I saw the boy underneath, the one Edith must have loved once, before love curdled into ownership. "She wanted to disappear. I simply... made it permanent."

"And the maid?" I asked quietly. "The girl in the grave? What did she cost?"

And that—that was the moment the mask didn't slip so much as come off entirely, because he looked at me with genuine puzzlement, as if I'd asked him the price of a fish, and said:

"She was meant for the grave either way. I merely chose which grave."

* * *

The water was at our ankles now.

I hadn't planned for that part—the speed of it. Spring tide, wind in the southeast, and the boathouse floor lower than I'd remembered from daylight. Brightwater had warned me. *The tide doesn't negotiate,* she'd said, and I'd nodded the way clever women nod right up until they're drowning.

Alistair felt it too. He glanced down, and I saw the arithmetic move behind his face, and I understood with terrible clarity that the confession was over and the sentencing was beginning.

"You're standing in the room where it ends," he said. "Fenwick ended here. Or near enough—he was so fond of asking questions I thought I'd give him a practical demonstration. And you'll end here, and they'll find you in the morning wearing your own clothes for once, and the coroner—an old friend—will write *misadventure*, and it will even be true. You did come here alone. You did ignore the warnings. Everyone will say you were obsessed."

"My bag," I said, nodding at the dinghy, keeping my voice level while the water climbed my shins. "Is empty. The recording left this afternoon. You've confessed to three deaths in front of a witness you haven't found, and all you've caught is a handbag."

For one full second he believed me—I saw it land, saw the whole architecture of his evening collapse—and then he didn't, and that second cost us both dearly, because he lunged for the bag anyway, and I threw the dinghy between us, and the room exploded into motion.

He was stronger. There was no dignity in denying it; he was sixty to my fifty-odd and built like a man who rowed for pleasure, and he got a hand around my wrist and slammed me back against the boat racks, and the hulls rang like bells. My elbow found the edge of a thwart and pain went up my arm white and total. The bag hit the water and sank, cylinder and all, and I heard myself make a sound that wasn't words.

"There," he breathed, close, triumphant, the water at our knees. "Now it's just your word against—"

"Mine," said the dark above us, "and mine."

Brightwater came down out of the loft with her torch blazing and her service whistle shrieking, and Alistair swung toward the light, and I brought my knee up with every year of quiet, invisible discipline in it. He folded. We went down together into the black water, and the last thing I registered clearly was his hand letting go of my collar, and the sea pouring over both of us, indifferent as scripture.

Then hands—strong, certain, Brightwater's—hooked under my arms and hauled, and the cold air hit my face, and I was coughing salt on the slipway with the recorder held above my head in a dripping fist, still running, the red light steady as a heartbeat.

"He confessed," she was saying, dragging me up the stones. "All of it. Coldharbour, I have all of it—"

But Alistair Hawley was not on the slipway.

The boathouse stood empty behind us, the water climbing the racks, and out beyond the slipway the tide ran fast and silver toward the open sea, and there was no sound of swimming, no sound of anything at all.

"He's gone into the water," Brightwater said, and her voice had gone strange. "Casper. He's gone into the *water*."

We stood together at the top of the slip, two soaked women and a recording, watching the black tide run, and I thought of the ferryman, and the archivist, and the girl in the wrong grave, and I heard myself say, in a voice I didn't recognize:

"The tide gives back what it takes. God help us—it's about to give him back."
