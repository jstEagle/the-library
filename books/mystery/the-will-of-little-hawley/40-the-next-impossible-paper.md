## 40. The Next Impossible Paper

The letter arrived on a Tuesday in March, which is the sort of day the universe chooses when it wants to be ironic without putting in any effort.

I was in my office at Coldharbour & Vane — my office again, my name back on the door, the suspension expunged from the record like a bad dream a firm agrees collectively to have never had — going through the ordinary machinery of probate. Two estates, one trust amendment, and a widow in Hampstead who wanted to leave her collection of decorative thimbles to a cat charity out of what I could only assume was spite. It was the kind of morning that makes you believe the world has rules.

Then Mrs. Okonkwo brought up the post, set it on my desk in her usual tidy stack, and there it was at the bottom: cream paper, hand-addressed, a postmark from a coastal town I'd had no reason to think about before breakfast.

*Gullmere.*

The handwriting was careful, old-fashioned, the loops of a person taught penmanship by someone who believed character lived in the downstrokes. And the enclosure inside — I knew before I opened it, the way you know a storm is coming from the taste of the air — was a will.

I read it twice. Then I did what any sensible woman does when confronted with an impossibility: I made tea, because the alternative was screaming, and screaming is unprofessional even alone.

The testator's name was Marguerite Voss. The date of execution was 1987. The date of death, according to the parish record helpfully enclosed on a second sheet, was 1976.

Eleven years dead before she signed her own will. Eleven years — the same span as Edith Hawley, which was either coincidence or a pattern, and I had spent enough of my life being punished for assuming coincidence to stop making that particular mistake.

The signature matched samples of Marguerite Voss's hand held in the county archive. The paper was right. The ink was right. The witnesses were real people who had really lived in Gullmere in 1987, one of them a schoolmistress, one of them a fisherman who had drowned — *drowned*, naturally; the sea keeps its own counsel about irony — in 1989.

A will that shouldn't exist. Signed by a woman the tide had supposedly taken eleven years earlier.

I sat with it for a long time while my tea went cold, and I thought about how, eight months ago, I would have put this letter in a drawer, told myself it was a clerical error or a cruel joke, and let some other town keep its secrets. Invisibility had been my discipline for fifteen years. Three deaths solved, three cases closed as accidents, three times I had watched the truth get buried under a polite consensus and said nothing, because saying something meant becoming someone, and being someone meant being a target.

Little Hawley had burned that discipline out of me the way fire burns a letter — thoroughly, and leaving only what couldn't be destroyed.

I picked up the telephone.

* * *

"You've seen it," Brightwater said. Not a question. He'd been expecting the call the way you expect weather.

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Because Gullmere rang us Tuesday last asking whether we'd ever had anything unusual. A constable there found the file odd and couldn't say why. Oddness migrates toward you, Casper. It always has. You're a lighthouse for things that should have stayed sunk." A pause, and then, drier: "Also your clerk telephoned my sergeant, because your clerk and my sergeant play darts and gossip like washerwomen."

"I'll have words with Okonkwo."

"You'll buy her a drink. She's the reason half of London knows you're respectable again." He let that sit, then: "When do you leave?"

"Tomorrow, probably. There's nothing here I can't—" I stopped, because the sentence I'd been about to finish was the old sentence, the invisible-woman sentence, the one that began *there's nothing holding me* and ended nowhere at all. "Tomorrow," I said again, differently.

"I'll drive," he said. "You navigate. You're insufferable in a passenger seat otherwise."

"I'm insufferable everywhere. That's not new information."

"No," he agreed, warm as a stove. "It isn't."

We hung up, and I looked around my office — the ledgers, the brass lamp, the window with its view of nothing more dramatic than Chancery Lane — and I understood that I was doing something I hadn't done in fifteen years. I was leaving a place *wanting* to come back to it. Wanting, specifically, to come back to a man who noticed everything and suspected almost nothing, which after Little Hawley felt like the rarest luxury in England.

* * *

Packing took an hour, which surprised me. I had assumed my life would take longer to fold into a bag.

Two changes of clothes, the good coat — not the one they'd buried a stranger in; that one had gone into evidence and then, at Inspector Brightwater's quiet insistence, into the incinerator, because I refused to wear a dead man's borrowed warmth — my notebook, the tin box. The tin box went everywhere now. Edith's photographs, her train ticket, the wax cylinder in its velvet nest. I had offered it to the museum in Little Hawley and they'd wanted it behind glass with a placard, and I'd said no, because Edith Hawley had spent eleven years behind glass in a bricked alcove and I was not going to be the one to arrange a second exhibition.

Some things you carry. That's the whole of grief, really. You just decide which things.

Mrs. Tarnow came to see me off, of course. She arrived at my lodgings with a basket that could have provisioned a siege — seed cake, a jar of her damson preserve, a scarf she'd knitted in a fortnight of sleepless testimony-giving — and stood in my doorway looking smaller than the woman who had helped stage a drowning and kept the secret for thirty years out of love.

"Gullmere," she said. "Coastal."

"Very."

"There'll be a housekeeper there. There always is. Someone who dusts a room that shouldn't be dusty, and says nothing, and grows old saying it." She handed me the basket and gripped my wrist, brief and fierce. "Find her first. Before anyone else does. Whatever else happens — find the one who kept the room."

"I will."

"And Miss Coldharbour." Her eyes were wet and level at once, which is a trick I still haven't learned. "You went into a cellar for me. Whatever's in Gullmere, you go in for her too. Promise me the housekeeper gets out alive this time."

I promised. It was the easiest promise I'd made in years, and the heaviest to carry.

Vera saw me at the station, because Vera now saw everyone off at stations; it had become her penance and her pleasure both, standing on platforms in a good coat, telling strangers too much. She pressed a flask of something medicinal into my hand and whispered, "If it's another son, shoot him," which was, from Vera, practically a blessing.

* * *

Gullmere announced itself the way all these towns do: a church spire first, then gulls, then the smell — salt and tar and something older underneath, the mineral breath of a place that has been losing people to the water since before it had a name.

Brightwater drove with his sleeves rolled and his jaw set in the particular way it set when he was rehearsing arguments with a coroner who wasn't present yet. We'd been over the file twice on the road. Marguerite Voss, widow, drowned 1976, case closed in three days — faster than Edith's four, which I chose to read as ambition rather than efficiency. The coroner who signed: one Dr. Emlyn Prowse, whose practice, conveniently, adjoined the Voss estate, and whose daughter, less conveniently, married into the family of the solicitor whose firm had drafted the impossible will.

"It's never just one rotten plank," Brightwater said, taking the coast road down. "It's a whole jetty. Everyone standing on it has a reason not to look at the water."

"That's the most poetic thing you've said to me."

"Don't get used to it. It frightens me."

The town itself was prettier than Little Hawley and knew it — whitewashed cottages, a promenade with iron railings painted the green of old bottle glass, a boarding house called the Mermaid's Rest with geraniums in every window. I looked at those geraniums longer than I needed to. Somewhere a photograph existed of Edith Hawley standing outside a place very like it, alive two years past her own funeral, squinting into a sun she wasn't supposed to see. Some cases you close. Others you just carry to the next town.

We took rooms at the inn — separate rooms, though the landlady's eyebrows conducted an entire editorial — and by four o'clock I was standing in the vestry of St. Aldhelm's with the parish register open in front of me, because before anything else, I wanted to see the burial entry with my own eyes.

*Marguerite Anne Voss, interred 14th September 1976. Officiating: Rev. T. Callard.*

And beneath it, in the same hand, added later, the note that made the hair on my arms stand up:

*Grave reopened 1988. Remains removed by order of family. Present location unknown.*

Someone had dug her up. Twelve years after burying her — two years after signing her will — somebody in the Voss family had ordered the coffin lifted out of consecrated ground and taken God knew where.

"Well," Brightwater said behind me, reading over my shoulder, his breath stirring the page. "That's new."

"That's not new. That's Little Hawley with better weather." I closed the register gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "They moved her. Which means either they knew she wasn't in the coffin—"

"—or they needed to make sure she stayed wherever they'd actually put her." He straightened. "Casper. The ice house above the flood line. Where would you hide a body in a town built on a cliff?"

I turned and looked out the vestry window, down the lane, past the geraniums, to where the headland dropped away and the old coastguard station stood — derelict, roofless, perched on rock above a tidal cave that the charts marked with a warning symbol and the locals, I'd already learned, marked with silence.

"The one place the water reaches twice a day," I said slowly, "and nobody would ever dig."

He was already reaching for his coat.

* * *

We didn't go that evening. Brightwater insisted — warrants, torches, the local inspector who deserved to be woken politely rather than presented with facts — and I conceded because he was right, and because I had learned, at considerable cost, that the tide keeps its own schedule and does not negotiate.

So instead we walked the promenade as the light went copper, and I did the thing I'd promised Mrs. Tarnow: I asked after housekeepers.

The third name was the one. *"Oh, the Voss place? Nobody's kept it since old Nell Fennimore. Strange one, Nell. Still lives up past the church. Still keeps the garden, they say, though there's no one left to see it."*

Nell Fennimore answered her door at dusk, seventy if she was a day, with flour on her hands and a look in her eyes that I recognized the way you recognize your own face in dark glass. She looked at me, then at Brightwater, then at the register of my profession written all over my questions, and she said, before I'd spoken a word:

"You've had a letter, then."

My heart did something complicated. "How could you know that?"

"Because I posted it." She stepped back and opened the door wide. "Thirty-eight years I've waited for someone to come who wouldn't be bought, or frightened, or related to him. Come in. Put the kettle on yourself — my hands aren't steady — and I'll tell you about the night Marguerite Voss didn't drown."

Inside, the house smelled of bread and beeswax, and on the mantelpiece, in a frame gone soft with age, stood a photograph of two young women arm in arm on this very beach, laughing at something outside the frame.

One of them was Marguerite Voss.

The other, unmistakably, sixty years younger, was Nell Fennimore.

She caught me looking and nodded once, unsurprised, unsurprisable.

"Aye," she said. "Her room's still kept. Forty-nine years, and I've dusted it every week. And there's a letter upstairs, in her hand, that she made me swear to send only when someone came asking who the water couldn't reach." Her chin lifted, and beneath the flour and the years I saw the girl in the photograph, and the housekeeper in the preserved room, and every keeper of every sealed door in every coastal town in England.

"So. Are you the one? Or do I wait another thirty years?"

Outside, far below the cliff, the tide was coming in over the cave mouth, patient as paperwork, and somewhere in the dark between the church and the water, I had the distinct, cold, exhilarating certainty that we were being watched by someone who had already heard we'd arrived.

I set down my bag, and this time I didn't check the exits first.

"Yes," I said. "I'm the one. Start at the beginning — and don't leave anything out, because whoever dug up that grave is going to want this letter back, and I intend to make that very difficult."
