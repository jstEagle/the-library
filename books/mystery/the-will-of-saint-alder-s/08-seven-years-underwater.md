## 8. Seven Years Underwater

The Voss estate had a room nobody talked about.

Elias found it on his third day in the house, the way you find anything in Saint Alder's—by accident, by following a draft. He'd been tracing the cold air to its source, a habit from childhood winters when he'd map the house's leaks like wounds, and it led him past the linen closet on the second floor to a door painted the same white as the wall behind it. No handle worth noticing. A keyhole so small and clean it looked decorative, as if the door existed only to be admired and never opened.

But the draft came from under it, thin and persistent, carrying the smell of old paper and something faintly chemical—mothballs, or the ghost of them—and Elias stood in the hallway with his hand flat against the wood for a long moment before he went looking for the key.

He didn't find a key. He found a hairpin in his grandmother's bathroom drawer, wedged between bottles of medication with names longer than their purposes, and it took him eleven minutes of kneeling on the hallway floor, feeling like a burglar in the house that had raised him, before the lock gave with a sound like a held breath released.

The room was small. A single window, shuttered, the slats painted shut. A desk against one wall, a filing cabinet against another, and shelves of boxes stacked with the neatness of a person who believed order was a moral position. His grandmother's handwriting was on every box: dates, ranges of dates, single words in her upright, unornamented script. *Taxes 2009–2013. Correspondence. Household. Legal.*

And on the bottom shelf, alone, a box labeled simply: *The Year We Do Not Discuss.*

Elias sat down on the floor with the box in his lap and did not open it right away. That was the thing about grief and guilt—they arrived together in this family, twins, inseparable, and he could feel both of them now sitting down beside him in the dust and the dark like relatives at a funeral who don't know where to put their hands.

Seven years ago, Edith Voss had died—or everyone in Saint Alder's had agreed she had. Not this Edith, the one whose house he was sitting in, but the other one, the story: Edith Voss, murdered in her own home, and the handyman, Denny Okafor, convicted of it, and the witness, Ruth Calloway, drowned in Miller's Pond before she could testify twice. Elias had been nineteen. He'd come home for the trial because his father had insisted, because *family shows up*, because Martin Voss understood appearances the way some men understand engines—completely, mechanically, without ever asking what they were for.

He remembered the courtroom. He remembered Denny Okafor's face, which was not the face he'd expected. He'd expected, without knowing it, a monster, and instead there was a tired man in an ill-fitting suit who kept looking at the jury the way you look at weather—as something happening to you that you cannot argue with. And he remembered Ruth Calloway's name being read into the record, the coroner's findings, the pond, the spring thaw, the accidental drowning, case closed, and how his father had put a hand on his shoulder during all of it, heavy and warm, and said, quietly, "It's over now."

It had sounded like comfort. Sitting on the floor of the sealed room seven years later, it sounded like something else entirely.

* * *

He started with the newspaper clippings, because clippings were safe, clippings were the official version, and he wanted—needed—to see how thoroughly the official version had been assembled before he let himself look at anything else.

There were more than he expected. The *Saint Alder's Courier* had covered the trial in installments, front page three days running, and someone—his grandmother, presumably—had saved every one. But it was the annotations that stopped him. Pencil, in the margins, in that same upright hand. Beside the article about Ruth Calloway's drowning: *No footprints reported on the east bank. Who walks into a pond in April without leaving prints?* Beside the coroner's finding of accidental death: *Dr. Havel signed this in eleven minutes. I timed it.* Beside the verdict: *Denny was in the greenhouse. I saw him myself at 9:40. The call came at 9:15.*

Elias read that last line four times.

*I saw him myself.*

His grandmother had been in the house the night of the murder. Of course she had—it was her house, her murder, her attempted murder, allegedly. But the annotation wasn't testimony; it was arithmetic. It was a woman doing sums in the margin of a story everyone else had agreed to stop checking, and the sums didn't come out even, and she had known it, and she had filed the knowledge away in a locked room under a label that admitted everything by refusing to.

*The Year We Do Not Discuss.* She had discussed it constantly. She had just done it alone.

Under the clippings were letters. Copies—carbon paper, the kind of copies you make when you want proof but not confrontation. The first was addressed to Judge Merrick Weller, dated six weeks after the verdict, and it was polite in the way a blade is polite. *I write regarding certain inconsistencies in the timeline presented at trial...* The response, also copied, was two sentences long and thanked her for her concern and reminded her that the matter was settled. The next letter was to the district attorney. Then to the state attorney general. Then, remarkably, to a legal aid society in the capital, on behalf of *Mr. Dennis Okafor, whom I believe to be innocent of the crime for which he was convicted.*

She had written for years. Elias counted the carbons: eleven letters across four years, each one answered with less than the last, until the final reply was a form letter and after that there were no more replies at all, and no more letters either, just a gap in the file, four years wide, and then a different kind of document began.

Receipts. Taxi receipts.

He recognized the letterhead immediately—the same one on the stack Juniper Nystrom had shown him two nights ago at the dispatch office, that pale, careful man with his logbooks and his questions, who had asked Elias, with a directness that had startled him, whether he knew anything about a nurse named Ruth. At the time Elias had thought the question strange. Now, holding receipts made out to *R. Calloway* for fares between a cottage address on Route 9 and the Voss estate, dated three years after Ruth Calloway drowned, he understood that nothing in this town was strange. Everything was simply arranged. Arrangement was the local industry.

* * *

He took the box to the kitchen because he couldn't breathe in the sealed room, and spread the documents across the long table where the family had eaten Thanksgiving dinners in the years when the family still ate together, and worked through the night with the lamps on and the rain starting against the windows around midnight.

The picture assembled itself the way pictures do—not all at once but in pieces that refused, individually, to mean anything, until suddenly they meant everything.

His father's fingerprints were everywhere, once you knew to look for them.

Not literal fingerprints—Martin Voss was too careful for fingerprints. What he left instead was timing. A phone call to Sheriff Dunmore logged at 6:12 a.m. the morning after the murder, before the body in the pond had even been reported found—how did Martin know to call? A deposition in which Martin placed himself at the estate all evening, corroborated by a housekeeper who, Elias learned from a payroll stub tucked in the file, had received a bonus that month equal to half a year's wages. A letter of reference Martin had written for Dr. Havel, the coroner, when Havel later applied for a county position—a reference dated eight months *before* the drowning, which meant Martin had been cultivating the man's gratitude well in advance of needing it.

You didn't plan favors like that unless you already knew what you would need the favor for.

And then there was the money. A ledger page, in Edith's hand again, listing payments to an account Elias didn't recognize, monthly, beginning the month after the trial. He wrote the account number on the back of his hand like a teenager and spent two hours on the phone with a bank officer who initially refused to tell him anything and then, when Elias said the words *estate of Edith Voss* and *probate attorney* in the same sentence, became suddenly, expansively helpful. The account belonged to a shell entity. The shell entity paid, among other things, the property taxes on a cottage on Route 9, the private medical insurance of one R. Calloway, and—for seven years, every month, without fail—a prisoner's commissary fund at Halden Correctional Facility.

Denny Okafor's commissary fund.

Elias sat back from the table. The rain had thickened into something serious, drumming on the roof, running in ropes down the dark windows, and the kitchen felt very far from anywhere, a lit island in a drowned world, which was, he supposed, exactly what this house had always been.

His grandmother had known. She had known Denny was innocent, had fought for him with letters until the letters ran out of road, and then she had done the only thing left to her: she had paid. She had kept the man alive in prison on commissary deposits and kept the woman alive in hiding on cash fares, and she had locked the evidence of all of it in a room labeled with a lie, and she had lived like that for seven years, eating dinner at this table, hosting the garden society, being Saint Alder's beloved matriarch, while two people she couldn't save rotted—one behind walls, one behind silence.

And his father? His father had built the walls.

Elias tried to construct the alternative reading. There had to be one; sons always build one; it was practically a biological function. Martin had panicked. Martin had been protecting the family. Martin had genuinely believed Denny guilty and Ruth's disappearance coincidental. Elias turned each of these over like stones and found nothing underneath any of them but wet ground. The phone call at 6:12 a.m. killed the last stone. You don't call the sheriff about a drowning before anyone knows there's been a drowning—unless you're reporting it, and Martin's call, per the dispatch log copy in the box, had not been a report. It had been, in the sheriff's own notation, *"Voss re: handling."*

*Handling.* As though it were a delivery. As though it were freight.

Elias got up and stood at the window with his forehead nearly touching the glass and watched the rain and thought about being nine years old, riding in his father's truck, and passing Miller's Pond, and his father saying, without being asked, "That's where that woman drowned," and Elias feeling the special delicious horror children feel near death, and his father glancing over and smiling slightly, satisfied, a man who had told a story well. Even then. Even at nine, Elias had registered something wrong in the smile and hadn't known what, and had spent thirty years assuming the wrongness was his own.

Shame, he thought, is the inheritance nobody lists in the will. It passes silently, at death or before, from the guilty to whoever loves them, because love is a channel and shame flows downhill.

* * *

In the morning he drove to the county records office, because there was one document the box didn't contain and he needed to see it with his own eyes: the trial transcript. Or failing that, the exhibit list. Or failing that, anything at all with Denny Okafor's name on it that wasn't filtered through his family's hands.

The records clerk was a young woman with chipped nail polish and earbuds in, who looked up with the enthusiasm of someone interrupted mid-video, but the request—trial records, State v. Okafor—landed on her like a change in the weather. She pulled out the earbuds.

"Okafor," she repeated. "Like, the handyman? From way back?"

"Seven years ago."

"Huh. My mom used to talk about that. Everybody kind of stopped talking about it, though." She said it lightly, but her eyes had gone somewhere else, and Elias recognized the look—he'd seen it on faces all week since he'd come back to town, the flicker of a door closing behind the eyes, a whole town of doors closing in sequence, politely, in unison. "Files are downstairs. Nobody's requested them since—" she checked something on her screen, frowned, checked again—"huh. Since never, actually. Since they were archived."

Nobody had ever asked. In seven years, not one person in Saint Alder's had gone downstairs and asked to see the file. Elias thanked her and descended the stairs into a basement that smelled of concrete and cold, past rows of gray shelving, to a box with a faded label, and carried it to a scarred table under a caged bulb.

The transcript was incomplete—pages missing, exhibits absent, the court reporter's certification stamped but the record itself full of gaps, like a mouth missing teeth. But the gaps told their own story. Pages 114 through 131 were gone: the entirety of the testimony of the state's forensic examiner. Page 87, torn out cleanly. And in the margin of page 92, beside a line of Denny Okafor's cross-examination—*"I was in the greenhouse. Mrs. Voss will tell you. Mrs. Voss saw me"*—someone had written, in pencil, in a handwriting Elias did not know: *She never testified. Why didn't she testify?*

Why indeed. Edith Voss had witnessed the crime, per her own marginalia, and had never taken the stand. The prosecution's star narrative had proceeded without her, and no one in that courtroom had asked why, and no one in this town had asked since, and the question had waited seven years in a basement for a stranger to come downstairs and read it.

Elias photographed every page with his phone, hands not quite steady, and was repacking the box when the clerk appeared at the bottom of the stairs, moving faster than before, her face carefully blank in a way that was worse than alarm.

"Sir? There's—someone called down. Asking if anyone had requested the Okafor files." She paused. "They asked me to take your name."

"Whose office?"

"I didn't get that. They hung up when I asked." She shifted her weight. "Look, I'm not supposed to say stuff, but... my mom used to clean out at the Voss place. Years ago. And she always said the same thing, that the son—" She stopped herself, visibly, physically, like a woman catching a stair rail before a fall. "Never mind. Forget it. Your name's not required, actually. I checked. It's not required."

She went back up the stairs. Elias stood alone under the caged bulb with the box half-packed and his heart going hard, and understood that the clock had started. Somewhere above him, in a warm office or a warm house, a phone was ringing or had rung, and word was traveling through the closed doors of Saint Alder's the way it always traveled—fast, silent, downward, like water finding the cracks.

He drove back along Route 9 with the rain easing and the fields steaming, and at the bend where the road met the reservoir he passed the turnoff to the cottage—the one on the taxi receipts, the one the property taxes flowed to—and slowed without meaning to, and in the rearview mirror caught a shape at the end of the gravel drive: a woman standing among bare trees, watching his car go by, motionless, as if she'd been watching cars go by for seven years and knew exactly which ones mattered.

He didn't stop. He wasn't ready, and neither, he suspected, was she. But his phone buzzed against the console as he drove, and he glanced down at the screen at the red light outside town, and saw a message from a number he didn't have saved, from a name he was only beginning to know:

*Juniper Nystrom. Dispatch. We need to talk tonight. I found out who mailed the confession letter — and it wasn't Edith.*
