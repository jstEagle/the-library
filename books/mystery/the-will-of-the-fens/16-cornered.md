## 16. Cornered

The summons came at half past seven in the morning, delivered not by telephone but by a knock—three measured raps on the door of Kestrel's room at the Marsh King, spaced with the deliberate patience of a man who wanted to be heard thinking.

Kestrel opened the door still dressed from a sleepless night, the journal's contents turning over and over behind their eyes like stones in a tumbler. Sergeant Dane Ferris stood in the corridor with his hat in his hands, which was somehow worse than if he'd worn it. His face was arranged into an expression of professional regret so carefully constructed it might have been taxidermied.

"Miss Villalobos." He said it the way he said everything—flat as the fen horizon, giving nothing back. "I'm going to need you to come down to the station."

"Now?"

"Now would be best."

"What's this about?"

Ferris looked past Kestrel's shoulder into the room, and Kestrel understood, with a cold settling in the chest like river water finding a seam in a boot, that he was checking. Checking whether the journal was visible. Whether anything had been left out, carelessly, for him to see. His gaze snagged for half a second on the wardrobe, then returned.

"Tampering with a heritage site," he said. "The cannery. It's a protected structure under the centennial preservation order. Somebody broke in there two nights ago. Started a fire."

"I nearly died in that fire."

"That's not what I heard." He put his hat on now, finally, and the brim shadowed his eyes. "What I heard is that a person matching your description was seen on the property before the fire started. Carrying things out." A pause, calibrated. "We can do this the easy way or the other way, Miss Villalobos. Either way, we're doing it."

* * *

The Saltmere police station occupied what had once been the harbormaster's office, a squat brick building at the edge of the quay where the fen water came closest to town. It smelled of old paper, damp stone, and the sweet rot of the marsh that seeped through every wall in Saltmere like a rumor. Ferris led Kestrel past the front desk without processing them—no booking, no paperwork, no officer reading rights off a card—and into a small interview room with a table bolted to the floor and a window set high enough to show only sky.

That was the first wrong thing. No paperwork meant no record meant no clock. This wasn't an arrest; it was a shelf. Somewhere to keep a person while the rest of the house was tidied.

Ferris sat across the table and produced a folder, though he never opened it. It sat between them like a prop in a play rehearsed too many times.

"You've been busy," he said. "Centennial interviews. Guest registers. The cannery." He ticked each item off with a finger resting on the tabletop, not quite touching it. "You know what I find interesting? Everything you've touched has gotten worse. Tansy dead. The cannery burned. Now Silas Fenwick drowned in water shallow enough to stand up in."

"You think I did those things?"

"I think trouble follows you like gulls follow the fish boats." He leaned back. "I think a stranger comes to town, and within a month our archivist is in her grave and our gravedigger is in the fen, and I think a reasonable man asks why."

"A reasonable man might also ask who benefits," Kestrel said quietly. "From all of it staying buried."

Something moved behind Ferris's eyes—not guilt, nothing so useful as guilt, but the flicker of a man hearing a door he'd hoped was sealed creak open somewhere down a long corridor. "This isn't about Ada Quill," he said. "That girl ran off in '94. Ran off or drowned herself; either way, she chose to leave. What this is about is a protected heritage site and a fire, and whether you're going to cooperate."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you stay here while we sort it out." He stood, smoothing his uniform shirt with both hands, a gesture Kestrel had watched him perform a dozen times—the gesture of a man straightening himself against something. "Could be a day. Could be longer. Heritage matters are complicated."

He knocked twice on the inside of the door. It opened from outside—he had someone waiting, of course he did—and he left without looking back.

* * *

The hours went by the way hours go in a room with one window: slowly, and then all at once gone. Kestrel counted what they knew and what they didn't, laying the facts out on the scarred table of the mind the way they'd once laid out a guest's lost luggage inventory, item by item, weight by weight.

Ferris had moved early and moved fast, which meant he was frightened. Frightened men made mistakes, but frightened men with power made other people's mistakes for them first. The charge was manufactured—that much was obvious even to a stranger—but its shabbiness was the point. It didn't need to survive a trial. It only needed to survive long enough for whatever remained of the evidence to be destroyed: the storage room's ashes sifted, the peat bank at Silas's watching-place dug over, the journal—

The journal.

It was hidden in the hotel. Third-floor linen closet, behind the stacked spare mattresses, wrapped in a pillowcase Kestrel had chosen precisely because it was unremarkable. Marisol didn't know. Wren didn't know. If Ferris searched the Marsh King—if he'd already searched it, the way his eyes had gone to the wardrobe in Kestrel's room—it would be found, and then thirty years of the truth would burn twice, once in ash and once in silence.

And there was no one to tell. That was the arithmetic of the cell: Kestrel had spent a lifetime being self-sufficient because there had never been anyone else, and now the habit had become a cage with the door standing open. All they had to do was get word out. All they had to do was trust somebody.

Through the high window, the sky went from pewter to the color of old bone. Footsteps passed in the corridor at intervals—a patrol, unhurried. Once, a kettle whistled somewhere far off, absurdly domestic, and Kestrel found their hands were shaking and pressed them flat against the table until they stopped.

Fear was a concierge's oldest colleague. Fear sat down beside you when a guest screamed at midnight, when a powerful man's lie needed managing, when the whole machinery of a place turned its attention on you. You didn't beat fear. You gave it a job.

All right, Kestrel told it. Here's yours.

* * *

The bellhop came at four o'clock with a tray Kestrel hadn't ordered.

His name was Pim Osei, seventeen years old, all elbows and careful manners, and he was the only soul in Saltmere besides Marisol who treated Kestrel as though belonging were a settled question rather than a negotiation. He pushed the tray through the door the duty officer held open—tea, bread, butter, an apple—and said, in the bright carrying voice of a boy performing normalcy, "Compliments of Miss Marisol. She says eat something, you look terrible."

"I look fine."

"You look like the east wing after the pipe burst." He set the cup down, nudged the plate, and his eyes flicked up—quick, questioning, terrified and eager at once. *Tell me what you need.* He'd been a bellhop long enough to know that guests in trouble rarely say what they mean, and long enough to want to be the kind of person who could read it.

Kestrel spoke to the tea, low, shaping each word like something breakable. "Tell her the third-floor closet needs fresh linens. Tell her the ones behind the mattresses are musty—she should bring them to me here, personally, tonight, before nine. Tell her—" and here Kestrel lifted the apple, turned it once in their fingers, and set it back down, "—tell her the apple's from the orchard at Quill Cottage. She'll understand the rest."

Pim's throat moved. "Musty linens. Before nine. Personally."

"And Pim—" Kestrel met his eyes. "Don't carry the message anywhere but to her. Not past the desk, not past anyone. Straight up the stairs."

"Yes, miss." And then, quieter, with a boy's fierce sudden loyalty: "We've been wondering when you'd ask."

The door closed. Kestrel sat very still and listened to their own heart, which was doing the work of a drum at a funeral march, and thought: *that's done, then. Whatever happens now, that's done.* Trusting someone, it turned out, felt less like relief than like stepping off a boardwalk plank in the dark and waiting to learn whether the ground was there.

* * *

They came for the search at seven.

Not Ferris himself—two constables Kestrel didn't know, county men by their accents, with a warrant signed by a magistrate whose name meant nothing. They went through Kestrel's room at the hotel with methodical courtesy while Kestrel sat in the holding cell listening to the radio in the duty office drift through the wall: shipping forecasts, a bit of music, the ordinary weather of a world that didn't know what was happening inside its own small rooms.

They found nothing, because there was nothing to find. The journal had been out of Kestrel's possession since the night of the fire, moved within the hour, hidden where no searcher looking for a fugitive's secrets would think to push aside spare mattresses in a linen closet. But sitting in the cell, hearing drawers open and close three floors away through the bones of the building, Kestrel understood exactly what the search was for. Not the journal. Time. Every hour Kestrel sat shelved was an hour Ferris could spend at the peat bank with a spade, at the cannery ashes with a sieve, at the archive with a box of matches.

At twenty minutes to nine, footsteps in the corridor again—but different. Lighter. Faster. A key turned, and the duty officer leaned in, bored, embarrassed. "Visitor. Ten minutes. Family only, she says." He shrugged as if to disclaim responsibility for whatever family meant.

Marisol Vega filled the doorway of the holding cell the way she filled every room she entered—completely, and with an expression that dared the world to comment. She was sixty, built like the hotel she'd run for thirty years: broad, warm-lit, load-bearing. In her arms she carried a neat stack of folded linens.

"They said I could bring you clean things," she announced to the duty officer, loud enough for the whole station. "This is a disgrace, keeping a guest of mine in a cell on nonsense charges. I'll be writing to the council." She swept past him and knelt by the cot, and her hands shook as she unfolded the top sheet—shook, Kestrel saw, not with fear but with the effort of keeping something enormous contained.

"The linens," Kestrel began.

"I know what the linens are." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Third floor. Behind the mattresses. Do you have any idea what you're asking me?" Her eyes were wet and furious. "My father's initials are in that book. M.V. I read it, Kestrel. I sat on my kitchen floor at six o'clock this evening and I read every page, and I kept seeing him at forty years old, signing his name to God knows what, and I kept thinking, *he raised me in the house that lie built.*"

"Marisol—"

"Be quiet and let me finish." She smoothed the sheet over the thin cot blanket with unnecessary force. "I have spent my whole life protecting that hotel because it was all I had of him. And now I find out what he was protecting instead." She sat back on her heels. In the fluorescent light her face looked suddenly, terribly tired. "You know what I decided, sitting on that floor? I decided my father made his choice in 1994. Tonight I get to make mine."

She took Kestrel's hand and pressed something into it, folding the fingers closed around it: a brass key, small, hotel-stamped. Room 12. Ground floor, end of the corridor, the room nobody rented because the window faced the fen wall and guests called it gloomy.

"The journal's in the mattress in room twelve," she whispered. "Under the ticking, sewn back in. I did it myself an hour ago. If they search the hotel again, they'll find it there—or they won't, depending how lucky they are. But tomorrow morning, first light, Wren Quill is coming through the fen path to the staff door, and you—" her grip tightened, "—are going to tell me exactly what to do with it, because I am not letting that sergeant burn one more page of the truth in this town. Not one more."

Kestrel looked down at the brass key in their palm, warm from her hand, and felt something shift in their chest that had been locked for thirty years and a lifetime besides. Fear was still there—it would always be there—but it had changed shape, the way ice changes to moving water. It wasn't a wall anymore. It was a current, and it was going somewhere.

"Room twelve," Kestrel said. "First light. And Marisol—when Wren comes, don't let her take the fen path by the drowning pool. Take the boardwalk along the reed beds. There are planks missing near the third marker, and I don't intend to lose anyone else to that water."

Marisol almost smiled. "Look at you. Even in a cell, taking care of everybody's route home."

"Somebody has to know where the soft ground is."

She gathered her empty basket, patted the folded linens one last time as though they were children, and stood. At the door she paused, her back to Kestrel, her voice steady now, ironed flat with purpose.

"Nine o'clock tomorrow, the county investigators arrive for the Fenwick inquiry. Wren's already written to them—she showed me the letter. Real police, Kestrel. Not ours." She glanced back over her shoulder, and in her eyes was the defiance of a woman setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd mistaken it for her skeleton. "Whatever happens in the next twelve hours—whatever he does—we hold. You hear me? We hold."

The door clanged shut. The lock turned. Kestrel lay back on the cot with the brass key hidden in their fist and listened to the fen wind worrying at the high window, and somewhere across the dark water, in a gloomy room at the end of a quiet corridor, the truth lay sewn inside a mattress, waiting for morning.

Twelve hours. Outside, the tide was already turning.
