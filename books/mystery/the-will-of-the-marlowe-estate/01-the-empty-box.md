## 1. The Empty Box

The radio at Halloran Taxi ran on two frequencies: the one that mattered and the one that didn't. Channel one carried the fares—Mrs. Okonkwo to the dialysis clinic at 6:40 sharp, the night manager at the Kwik-Stop home at three, the drunk from the VFW who always tipped in quarters he'd counted twice. Channel two was the police band, which Odile kept on low volume out of habit, the way other people kept a porch light burning. Not because she expected anything. Because the town of Marlowe's Ferry had been quiet for so long that silence itself had started to feel like a sound she ought to be listening for.

At 1:17 a.m., the silence broke.

*Dispatch to all units, we've got a detail at Riverside Memorial, section nine. Traffic control only. Nobody crosses the tape.*

Odile looked up from the fare ledger, her pen hovering over an entry she'd already made. The clock above the corkboard said 1:17. The coffee in her mug had gone the color of the harbor in November.

*Riverside?* That was Officer Pell, young, breathless even over static. *What's going on out there at this hour?*

*Court order,* came the flat reply from Sergeant Amos, who had been on the force since before Odile could drive and treated the radio like a personal inconvenience. *Exhumation.*

The word sat in the cab office like a cat that had walked in off the street and refused to leave. Odile set down her pen.

There was exactly one grave in section nine of Riverside Memorial that anyone in Marlowe's Ferry would bother exhuming. There was exactly one grave in this whole town with a granite obelisk taller than the chapel steeple, with a bronze plaque worn smooth by eighty years of schoolchildren's fingers, with a name that was on the water tower and the bank and the sign coming in off Route 9 and, if you believed the mural in the post office, on the very founding of the place itself.

Edmund Marlowe. 1918–1994. Father of the Ferry.

Odile had driven past that obelisk maybe four thousand times. She'd picked up mourners from its shadow every Founder's Day, when the town laid wreaths and the high school choir sang and somebody always gave a speech about vision and grit and the man who built the ferry landing with his own hands. She'd never once thought about what was under it. Graves were graves. You put people in them and the ground did the rest.

*Who ordered it?* Pell again.

*Land survey dispute. Some question about where the original parcel line runs. County wants the plot verified.* A pause, papers shuffling somewhere in the dark of the sheriff's office. *It's a paperwork thing, Pell. Don't make it a thing.*

But it was already a thing. Odile could hear it becoming a thing—the way Pell's breathing changed, the way another unit, Delacroix out by the truck stop, keyed up just to say *copy* in a voice that meant *did you hear that too?*

She got up and turned channel two up until the static hissed like rain.

* * *

The night passed the way nights do when something is wrong: slowly, and then all at once.

At 2:03, a state archaeologist's van went through the intersection at Mill and Water, though no unit called it in and Odile only saw it because the cab office window faces the street and she had stopped pretending to work. At 2:40, someone from the coroner's office arrived—a woman Odile didn't recognize, carrying a case that looked too small for anything but documents and too heavy for documents alone. At 3:15, the radio went quiet in a way that felt deliberate, like a door being closed gently so as not to wake someone.

At 3:22, her father's old chair creaked behind her, and Odile nearly came out of her skin.

"Easy." Gus Halloran stood in the doorway in his robe, gray stubble, reading glasses pushed up into white hair that never lay flat. He was seventy-one and had sold himself the business twenty years ago for a dollar, and he still came downstairs most nights around three because, as he put it, a dispatcher who falls asleep is just a person sitting in a room. "You look like you seen a ghost."

"They're digging up Edmund Marlowe."

Gus lowered himself into the second chair, the one with the broken spring he'd never fixed because fixing it would mean admitting someone else used it. He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a delivery truck groaned past on its route to the bakery, headlights sweeping the ceiling.

"Well," he said finally. "Ground's been telling us something was off for years. That corner of the cemetery floods every spring. Always figured it was the clay."

"That's not why they're digging."

"No," Gus agreed. "That's not why." He rubbed his jaw. "Your grandfather dispatched the night Edmund died. Did I ever tell you that?"

"You tell it every Founder's Day."

"I tell the version for Founder's Day." He smiled without much humor in it. "The real version is shorter. It rained. The whole town wanted rides to the funeral and nobody wanted rides home. And there was one fare that night your grandfather never could account for. Wrote it in the book and couldn't say where it went. Drove him half crazy till the day he died."

Odile's pen was in her hand again before she knew she'd picked it up. "Which fare?"

Gus waved a hand, already halfway back to sleep in the broken-spring chair. "Nineteen ninety-four. You'll find it. Book's all still there. Lord knows why we keep 'em, but we keep 'em."

He was snoring within ten minutes. Odile sat with the radio hissing softly and the ledger open in front of her and the strange cold feeling that her family's cluttered little archive—which she maintained out of stubbornness and tax paranoia, thirty years of dispatch books in cardboard boxes in the back room—had just become the most interesting object in Marlowe's Ferry.

She didn't go get the 1994 book. Not yet. Something in her gut said wait, the way you wait at a green light when you can't see why but you can feel it. She waited.

* * *

At 5:50 a.m., the first shift driver, Ray, came in smelling of cigarettes and cold air, took one look at her face, and said, "What happened?"

"I don't know yet."

At 6:12, the radio woke up all at once, units talking over each other, and Odile caught fragments—*section nine*, *get the county prosecutor out here*, *nobody talks to anybody*—and then Sergeant Amos's voice cutting through, tired as she'd ever heard it:

*All units stand down. Scene is now a criminal investigation. That's all. Stand down.*

Criminal. Not administrative. Not clerical. Criminal.

At 7:04, the WQMF morning broadcast, which played on the little radio by the coffee maker because drivers liked the weather, interrupted itself mid-forecast.

"—breaking news out of Marlowe's Ferry this morning, where officials exhumed the remains of town founder Edmund Marlowe overnight as part of a property boundary dispute—and found the coffin empty. I'll say that again, folks: after eighty-one years in the ground at Riverside Memorial, the casket of Edmund Marlowe contains no remains. County officials are calling it—"

Ray dropped his coffee. The mug didn't break; it just spun on the linoleum, shedding brown in a slow spiral, and neither of them moved to stop it.

"—calling it, quote, 'a matter of active investigation,' and have sealed the cemetery. More as we—"

Odile turned it off.

For a moment the office was perfectly still. The clock ticked. Somewhere outside a gull screamed over the harbor, the same gull, probably, that had screamed over the harbor every morning of her life. The mural across the street showed Edmund Marlowe standing on the ferry landing in his wool coat, pointing toward the future, and the future he pointed at was the pharmacy, which had closed in March.

"Eighty-one years," Ray said. "You mean to tell me that box has been empty since before either of us was born?"

"That's what they're saying."

"So where—" He stopped. She watched him arrive at the same place she'd been sitting since 3 a.m. "So where's he been?"

Nobody answered, because there was nobody to answer. Just the two of them and the snoring ghost of Gus Halloran upstairs and thirty years of dispatch books stacked in the back room like sediment, like tree rings, like the town's own memory waiting to be read.

* * *

By eight o'clock, the town had come apart quietly, the way old fabric comes apart—one thread, then a seam, then the whole shoulder giving way.

The diner on Front Street had its TV on through the window, and a knot of people stood outside watching it rather than going in, as if eating eggs during a scandal were somehow disrespectful. The bank flew no flag. The historical society, Odile noticed driving Mrs. Okonkwo home from her appointment, had taken down its Founder's Day banner early this year—or had it been taken down? The brackets were still on the lamppost, fresh screw marks shining.

Everyone she drove wanted to talk about it. Everyone talked about it the same way, too—in the third person, at a slight remove, as if the empty coffin were weather.

"Can you imagine," said Mr. Ferro from the hardware store, climbing into the back seat. "Eighty-one years. My father shook the man's hand. Shook the hand of a man who wasn't even—well. Who knows."

"Maybe it's a mistake," said the woman from the library. "Maybe they dug in the wrong spot."

"In section nine?" Mr. Ferro said. "Under the biggest rock in the county? What are they gonna say, they dug up the wrong Edmund Marlowe?"

Everyone laughed at that, briefly, the way people laugh at funerals—because the alternative was worse.

Odile drove and listened and said almost nothing, which was her lifelong specialty. Thirty-eight years in Marlowe's Ferry and she had perfected the art of being furniture: the dispatcher who knew every address, every schedule, every secret hour of everyone's life, and whom nobody had ever once considered knowing anything back. Invisible. She had told herself it was a virtue. This morning, pulling away from the hardware store with Mr. Ferro's change rattling in her apron pocket, she understood for the first time that invisibility was also a kind of debt. All those years of seeing everything. Sooner or later, the seeing came due.

Her last fare of the morning was a young deputy she'd known since he was delivering newspapers, riding back from the courthouse. He sat in front, which nobody did, and stared straight ahead.

"Miss Halloran," he said, when they were nearly at the station. "You keep the old dispatch records here, right? At Halloran Taxi?"

"We keep everything."

"Yeah." He paid, tipped too much, and got out. Then he leaned back down to the window, and his voice dropped. "If somebody official asks you for those books—like, officially—you might want to make copies first. Of everything. Before anybody asks."

He straightened up and was gone through the station doors before she could ask what he meant.

* * *

Noon. The office was empty. Ray slept in the break room; Gus was upstairs. Rain had started, soft and steady, the kind that made the harbor disappear into itself.

Odile went into the back room.

The dispatch books lived on steel shelving her grandfather had welded himself, one box per year, spines labeled in three different handwritings across three generations. She pulled 1994 and carried it to the front desk, set it under the lamp, and opened it.

October. The handwriting was her grandfather's—small, exact, unhurried, each entry a little monument to a man who believed a record was a promise. She read down the page. Fares to the church, the airport, the hospital. Ordinary. The rain of that week appeared in the margins in his notes: *wet roads, wipers bad on car 2.*

Then the fourteenth of October. The night Edmund Marlowe died, according to every textbook, every plaque, every speech.

Fare 446: Mrs. Pruitt, Maple Street to St. Anne's, vigil service. Paid.

Fare 447.

The entry stopped her the way a missing stair stops you in the dark. The pickup location was written as *Riverside Mem., rear gate.* Time: 11:48 p.m.—nearly midnight, hours after the family had gone home from the deathbed, according to everything the town believed. Destination: a single line drawn through the space where the address should be. Drawn firmly, deliberately, more than struck through—obliterated. Payment: cash. Driver initials: E.H. Her grandfather, Elias Halloran.

And beneath it, in the margin, in pencil so faint she had to tilt the book into the lamplight to catch it, five words in her grandfather's hand:

*Asked me not to record.*

Odile sat very still. The rain ticked against the window. On the wall, the framed photo of her grandfather in front of car 1, 1961, watched her with the mild, patient face of a man who had carried something for fifty years and finally set it down where someone might find it.

Not lost, then. Not an accounting error that drove him half crazy. An omission. A hole he'd left in the record on purpose—and marked, so that someday, someone paying attention would know the hole was a message.

Fare 447. Picked up at the rear gate of the cemetery on the night the founder died, destination erased, payment untraceable, at an hour when the dead don't usually take taxis.

She reached for the phone to call no one, realized there was no one to call, and pulled the 1995 box off the shelf instead. Then 1996. If it happened once, it might have happened twice. If it happened twice—

In the 1997 book, on a raw night in February, there it was again. Fare 447. Pickup: the lane behind the old Marlowe boathouse. Destination: redacted. Cash. Initials: E.H.

Odile Halloran, invisible for thirty-eight years, sat alone in the yellow light of the dispatch office with her heart going hard and slow, like footsteps on stairs, and understood that the ground under Marlowe's Ferry had just shifted—and that she was the only one in town holding a map of where it used to be.
