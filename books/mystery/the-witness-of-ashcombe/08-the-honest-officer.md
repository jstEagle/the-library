## 8. The Honest Officer

The rain had been falling on Ashcombe for three days without conviction, a thin grey insistence that made the streets shine like pewter and drove everyone indoors. Sable Halloran watched it from the window of the Copper Kettle, the café on Wren Street where he had arranged to be seen rather than to see — though of course, with him, the two were never entirely separate.

DS Imogen Reyes was late, which surprised him. He'd studied her habits the way he studied everything: quietly, from angles she wouldn't notice. She took her coffee at seven-forty each morning from the machine in the station, black, no sugar, and she walked to work along the towpath when the weather permitted because she said — once, to a colleague whose gossip traveled faster than any police report — that she liked to hear the water before she heard the paperwork.

Today there would be no towpath. Today she came through the café door at nine-fifteen precisely, shaking rain from a coat the colour of wet slate, and Sable understood immediately that she knew this was not an accident of geography. He had left the message himself, hand-delivered, unsigned: *I have something you should look at. The Kettle, 9:15. Come alone or don't come.* He hadn't expected her to come alone. He had hoped.

She scanned the room the way he would have — exits first, faces second, the corner table by the window last — and found him sitting exactly where he wanted her to see him: visible, unhidden, hands wrapped around a cup of tea going cold. An old man's posture. Deliberately so.

"Mr. Halloran." She didn't sit. "You know I'm not supposed to take meetings like this."

"I know you're not supposed to." He gestured at the chair opposite. "But you're here."

"I'm here because I've spent four years wondering what you actually do, and because if I don't find out, someone less careful will." She sat, finally, but kept her coat on, her bag strap looped around one wrist. Everything about her said *temporary*. "So. Talk."

Sable turned his teacup slowly on its saucer, buying a moment, studying her the way a locksmith studies a lock. Imogen Reyes was thirty-eight, maybe forty, with the kind of face that gave nothing away on principle — dark eyes set deep, a mouth that had learned early the economy of words. She'd come to Ashcombe six years ago from somewhere south, some city with real crime, and the town had never quite decided whether to be flattered or insulted by her competence. She was the only officer in the station who had ever returned his calls. She was also the only officer who had ever looked at him with something other than either suspicion or superstition, and both of those, in their way, were easier to manage than respect.

He had rehearsed this conversation three times in the night, lying awake while the rain worked the gutters. Each version had failed him. The problem was arithmetic: tell her too little and she'd walk out; tell her too much and everything he'd built — the ledger's safety, Nell Marsh's secret, whatever fragile thing Tomas had trusted him with — would collapse into official channels and official channels, in Sable's long experience, were where truth went to be embalmed.

"You'll remember," he said, "the Harrow Lane fire. Eleven years ago."

Her expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a small recalibration, a weight shifting. "Officially, electrical fault."

"Officially." He let the word sit between them like a coin on a table. "And the drowning at Miller's Bend, eight years back. And old Mrs. Calloway's fall down her own stairs, five years ago. All officially what they appeared to be."

The café noise seemed to recede. Reyes had gone very still, the stillness of someone listening with her whole body. "Those are closed cases."

"They are."

"And you're telling me—" She stopped herself. Her jaw tightened. "You're telling me they weren't what they appeared to be. And you're telling me this now, in a café, over cold tea, eleven years after the fact. Why?"

Because I need to know what you'll do with it, he thought. Because I'm about to hand you something far heavier than three old ghosts, and I can't carry it to you blind.

"Because I want to ask you a question," he said aloud, "and I want you to answer it honestly, which is more than I'd expect from most people in your profession."

"That's a hell of a preamble."

"It's an earned one." He leaned forward slightly. "If I brought you evidence — real evidence, documented, verifiable — that a crime was committed in this town decades ago, and that certain respected families had spent fifty years making sure no one ever looked at it, what would you do?"

Reyes looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened against the window, blurring the street into watercolour. When she spoke, her voice was lower, stripped of its procedural armour.

"What kind of crime?"

"A death."

"Whose?"

"A young woman. In 1975."

Something flickered across her face then — fast, quickly mastered, but Sable caught it, and catching it changed everything. Not surprise. Recognition. Somewhere in the station's filing cabinets, in some box nobody had opened since the Ford administration, there was a file with Elsie Marsh's name on it, and Imogen Reyes had at some point read it, or tried to.

"You already know the name," he said quietly.

"I know there's a file that's thinner than it should be." Her hands had come off the table; she sat back, arms crossed, and for the first time she looked less like an officer conducting a meeting and more like a woman carrying something heavy up a long hill. "I pulled it two years ago. Routine follow-up on a records audit. The original report is four pages. Four pages for a drowning — no witness statements beyond the responding constable, no post-mortem photographs attached, no next-of-kin interview. The coroner signed off in nine days. I flagged it. My inspector told me the audit was complete and thanked me for my thoroughness." Her mouth twisted. "That was the end of it. Officially."

*Officially.* The word again, passed back and forth between them like contraband.

"So you understand," Sable said, "why I'm asking before I show anything. I've watched this town bury things for a long time. I buried three of them myself — the ones I told you about. I did it because the system you work inside couldn't hold them. A man walked free on the Harrow Lane fire because a witness recanted after someone bought his silence. Mrs. Calloway's nephew inherited before the body was cold. I could prove none of it in a courtroom, so I proved it to myself, and I stopped sleeping well, and eventually I stopped trying." He paused. "You're the only person wearing a badge in this county I'd trust with a live grenade. But trust has to be tested, and testing means risk, and I'm too old to lose gracefully."

Reyes was quiet for a while. The rain filled the silence. Then she said, "Ask your question."

"If I give you this — the whole of it, when the time comes — will you take it up the chain? Or will you work it yourself?"

"That depends."

"On what?"

"On whether the chain is clean." She met his eyes, and hers were steady in a way that cost her something; he could see the cost. "I've wondered for six years why I was posted to a town with a crime rate that wouldn't embarrass a village fete, and why every time I pull a thread, someone senior tells me to put the scissors down. If your evidence touches anyone above sergeant, Mr. Halloran, then no — I won't take it up the chain. I'll work it myself, off the books, exactly the way you did. Which apparently makes me a hypocrite and you a prophet, and neither of us should enjoy that."

Sable felt something loosen in his chest that he hadn't known was clenched. It wasn't relief, exactly. It was closer to recognition — the rare, disorienting experience of finding another person standing on the same narrow ledge.

"There's more," he said. "There's a possibility — only a possibility — of a crime that hasn't happened yet. One that might be prevented."

Her stillness returned, deeper this time. "Prevented how?"

"That's what I'm still working out. I can't say more yet. Not because I don't trust you." He almost smiled. "Because I don't yet understand it myself."

"That's not reassuring."

"No. But it's honest, and you asked for honesty." He slid a folded piece of paper across the table — not the ledger, not even a fragment of it, just a single date written in his own hand: *14 June. Centenary gala.* "When the time comes, I'll bring you everything. Until then, keep this date in mind, and if anyone asks why you met me today, tell them the truth: an old bookseller wanted advice about a first edition."

She took the paper, read it, and her brow creased. "The gala. Everyone who matters in this town will be there."

"Yes," said Sable. "That occurred to me too."

She stood, shrugging the slate coat onto her shoulders, and paused beside his table. For a moment her voice dropped to nearly nothing, beneath the café's murmur.

"For what it's worth — the Marsh file. I never filed my flag as closed. It's in my desk drawer, not the system." She looked down at him, and there was something raw under the composure, something that might have been anger worn smooth by years of swallowing it. "Whoever she was, somebody in this town decided she didn't count. I've hated that for two years without knowing her name. So don't test me too long, Mr. Halloran. I'm on the ledge already."

Then she pushed through the door into the rain, and was gone.

* * *

Imogen Reyes walked the long way back to the station, though the direct route was four minutes shorter, because she needed the four minutes and because walking in rain meant no one could see her face properly.

An old bookseller. That was what everyone called him, and she had let herself believe it for six years — let herself believe the man who'd retired to Ashcombe with a shop full of other people's discarded lives was simply what he appeared. But she'd been a detective long enough to know that appearance was the first lie every careful person told, and Halloran was the most careful man she'd ever stood within arm's reach of. His tea had gone untouched. His questions had arrived pre-loaded, sequenced, each one opening a door he'd already measured. And those three cases — Harrow Lane, Miller's Bend, Calloway — he'd named them like a man laying cards face-up, watching which ones made her flinch.

They had all made her flinch. She'd had doubts about all three, filed away in the same drawer as the Marsh flag, in the place where Imogen Reyes kept the things her job required her to pretend she didn't know.

*A crime that hasn't happened yet.*

She turned the phrase over as the towpath — flooded, impassable, ironically — gave way to the high road. It was either the rambling of an old man who'd read too many mysteries, or it was the most frightening sentence anyone had ever said to her, and she had learned in six years that Sable Halloran did not waste sentences.

At the station, she hung her coat to drip, nodded to the desk sergeant, and climbed to her desk by the window. She unlocked the bottom drawer. Inside, beneath a folder marked *TRAINING RECORDS — RETAINED*, lay the Marsh file copy and her own handwritten flag, two years old now, the ink gone soft at the creases: *Records incomplete. Request full coroner's packet. Denied — Insp. Whitcombe.*

Whitcombe. Who had signed off on the Calloway inquest. Who had approved the closure of Miller's Bend. Who was, as of last month, chairman of the Ashcombe Centenary Committee's security sub-group.

Reyes sat very still, the rain ticking against the glass behind her, and felt the shape of something enormous begin to assemble itself in the dark — not a suspicion yet, not even a theory, just the terrible gravitational pull of a pattern.

On her blotter, the paper Halloran had given her caught the grey light. *14 June.*

Nine days away.

She picked up her pen, and beneath the old flag, in fresh ink, she wrote: *If he's right, we're already too late. Work faster.*
