## 7. Burn Notice

The smell reached Sebastian before the sight did—ink and salt and something sharper underneath, the chemical bite of a struck match that had been snuffed too soon. They stood in the doorway of their own office at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, coffee cooling in one hand, and understood, with the terrible clarity of a photograph developing, that someone had been here.

The filing cabinet stood open like a rib cage. Drawers pulled out and upended, manila folders fanned across the floorboards in a paper tide. The framed certificate from the state investigators' association—the one thing Sebastian had ever hung on a wall—lay face-down beneath the window, its glass starred with a single deliberate crack. Someone had wanted it broken. Someone had wanted everything touched.

Sebastian did not move for a full minute. They let the room print itself.

*Desk drawer forced from the left side, pry mark at the joint. Lamp knocked but not shattered—set down again, or caught. The second drawer of the lateral file emptied entirely; the third half-empty. Water glass on the sill, ring of condensation dried to a ghost. They waited. They sat. They worked by daylight, unhurried, because they believed no one would come.*

That was the part that made Sebastian's hands go cold around the paper cup. Not the theft. The *patience*. Whoever had done this had known the office sat empty most evenings, had known Sebastian kept late hours only when a case ran hot—and this case had been running hot for eleven days now. The intruder had read the rhythm of Sebastian's life the way Sebastian read everyone else's, and had found it wanting.

They set the coffee down and began the inventory aloud, voice low, as if speaking the losses might hold them in place.

"Vasquez, Elena—claim file, 2019. Gone." The folder containing the original payout documentation, the beneficiary designations, the coroner's photographs Elena's mother had never been allowed to see. "Creed Holdings—annual statements, 2018 through 2023. Gone." Every page Sebastian had copied from the county records office, tracing the slow migration of money through shell companies with names like seabirds: Petrel Marine, Cormorant Trust, Grey Heron Estates. "Correspondence index, alphabetical, C through H. Gone."

The alphabet of secrets, halved. Creed's section ripped out at the spine. Harlan's medical records from the coastal clinic—stolen under a pretext Sebastian still hadn't identified—already missing before this. And now Daniel Creed's insurance history, his lapsed policies, his signature on documents dated within a week of Elena's drowning.

Half the archive, gone. But not all. Not the letters. Those Sebastian had moved two nights ago, after the second envelope arrived smelling of brine with Elena's dive watch rattling inside it like a dead insect in a box. Those lived now in a place no ransacker would think to look, because no one who knew Sebastian would believe they'd ever trust anything to a church.

* * *

Marisol arrived an hour later, drawn by a phone call Sebastian hadn't meant to make. She came through the door the way she came through every door—shoulder-first, already talking—and then stopped dead in the wreckage, and the talking went out of her like air from a lung.

"Dios mío," she said. Then, quieter, turning slowly: "This is because of us. Because of Elena."

"This is because someone is afraid," Sebastian said. "Fear means we're close."

"We?" Marisol picked her way across the scattered papers, crouched, lifted a page at random—a decade-old claim about storm damage to a shrimp boat—and held it like evidence at a trial. "You keep files on everybody in this town, Nakamura. I always figured you for a vulture. Quiet little man with quiet little folders." Her jaw tightened. "But somebody thinks you're something else. Somebody thinks you're dangerous. So which is it?"

Sebastian looked at her—at the flour dust still on her forearm from the morning's empanadas, at the resemblance to Elena that lived mostly in the set of her mouth—and told the truth for the first time in five years.

"I'm a witness. That's all I've ever been. I see things and I keep them, and I told myself keeping them was harmless. Neutral." The words tasted like rust. "Elena drowned on a Tuesday in October. On the Wednesday before, I saw a car parked at the north seawall with its lights off. Two men inside. I noted the plate. I filed it. I did nothing, because doing nothing was how I survived this town, and I was a coward, and I have been paying for it in installments ever since."

Marisol didn't soften—that wasn't in her—but something in her stance shifted, weight moving from judgment to alliance. "The plate. You remember it?"

"4-Kilo-7-1-9. Registered to Petrel Marine, LLC. A Creed holding company." Sebastian watched her absorb it. "I've remembered it for five years. I could recite it at my funeral."

"Then we're past *we're close*," Marisol said. "We're inside the house." She glanced around the ruined office, and for the first time since the inquest, Sebastian saw fear move across her face—not for herself. For them. For this strange, brittle thing growing between the Vasquez table and Sebastian's empty rooms. "They took your files. What happens when they figure out the files aren't the only copy? What happens when they figure out it's all in your head?"

Sebastian had been asking themselves the same question since dawn. There was only one answer, and it fit the shape of the cracked glass under the window perfectly.

"Then I stop being a keeper," they said, "and become a target."

* * *

The third letter came Thursday, delivered not by post but by hand—slipped under the office door sometime in the small hours, because Sebastian had taken to sleeping on the cot in the back room, and had heard nothing. That nothing frightened them more than any sound could have.

The envelope was cheap, the kind sold in boxes of fifty at the drugstore. Inside, a single sheet, block capitals pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

**YOU KEPT EVERYTHING. NOW GIVE IT BACK. THE REST OF YOUR FILES AND WHATEVER ELSE YOU'VE MADE. BURN THEM WHERE THE WATER CAN SEE. SATURDAY, LOW TIDE, GREYWATER BAY, NORTH SEAWALL. COME ALONE OR THE VASQUEZ WOMAN LEARNS WHAT HAPPENS TO PEOPLE WHO DIG.**

Below the words, no signature. Just a small, precise drawing in ballpoint: a watch face, hands frozen at 3:40, a crown rendered in careful detail.

Elena's watch. The one that had come back to Sebastian in the second envelope, water-stopped, band snapped. The time Elena's lungs had filled. Whoever drew it had seen it—or had held it, long ago, in the dark, while a woman fought the cold grey water.

Sebastian read the letter four times. On the fourth reading, the fury arrived—not the hot flare of panic but something colder and far more durable, a pressure building behind the sternum like deep water against a hull. They had spent thirty years making themselves invisible. Invisible people were safe. Invisible people watched and recorded and kept the town's sins in alphabetical order and were left alone because they threatened nothing.

But invisibility, Sebastian understood now, had never been protection. It had been camouflage—and camouflage only works until something crosses your scent. The moment Sebastian had opened the first confession, they had become visible. Worse: they had become *useful*. The threat against Marisol proved it. This was not the letter of a dying man seeking absolution—Harlan Creed's shaky, looping hand was nothing like these brutal capitals. This was the handwriting of someone cleaning a ledger. Someone who needed the files burned not because they contained truth, but because truth, in Sebastian's memory, was indestructible.

You cannot steal what lives in a head. But you can burn what it loves.

Sebastian sat very still in the wrecked office as evening came down over Greywater Bay, the water beyond the window going the color of gunmetal, and let the fury settle into architecture. Saturday. Low tide. The north seawall—the same seawall where a car had once waited with its lights off. They want me there alone, files in hand, ready to feed the fire. They want the last copies gone and the witness standing in plain sight with nothing left to trade.

They think I'm coming to surrender.

Sebastian rose, crossed to the safe behind the displaced bookshelf, and began—line by line, page by page, in a fresh notebook bought that afternoon with cash—to rebuild the stolen archive from the flawless vault of their own recall. Petrel Marine. Cormorant Trust. The dates, the amounts, the signatures. If Daniel Creed wanted a fire, Sebastian would bring him paper. Every page of it true.

Outside, the tide was already turning, and somewhere across the black water, low tide Saturday was waiting like an open grave.
