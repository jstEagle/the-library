## 9. Greywater Bay at Low Tide

The note came by hand, tucked under the door of the office sometime before dawn. No envelope this time, no stamp to trace, no postmark to interrogate. Just a single sheet of cream paper in that second handwriting—the cold one, the one that had promised fire and then delivered it.

*The files you copied are worthless without the originals. The originals are where she died. Come alone at low tide, or the sister learns what her brother-in-law really kept in his basement.*

Low tide was at 4:17 p.m. Sebastian knew that the way they knew everything—absorbed once, held forever, from a tide chart pinned above Marisol's stove with a magnet shaped like an oyster shell. They read the note three times and did not burn it, though their hands shook enough that the letters blurred and reassembled themselves, sharp again on the third pass, indelible.

*Come alone.*

Sebastian looked at the phone for a long time. Then they dialed no one, wrote nothing down, and drove to Greywater Bay with the windows down and the wind tearing at their hair like it wanted to warn them.

* * *

The bay at low tide was a different country. Five years of visiting it, photographing it, cataloguing its moods, and Sebastian had never seen it stripped bare like this—mudflats stretching gray and glistening toward the channel, tide pools holding small trembling worlds of their own, the pilings of the old Creed pier exposed to their barnacled waists. The water had pulled back like a witness stepping away from something shameful, leaving everything exposed.

Elena Vasquez had drowned here on a night when the tide was coming in, not going out. That detail had never fit, and Sebastian had known it never fit, and had said nothing, because saying something would have meant admitting what they'd seen from the seawall that night: two figures on the pier, one tall, one slighter, and then only one walking back.

They stood now on the mudflat below that same pier, and the memory played behind their eyes with the merciless fidelity of film. Elena's dive bag over her shoulder. Her laugh, carrying across the water—she'd been laughing. And the tall figure following her out along the planks, unhurried, familiar in his bearing even at that distance, though Sebastian hadn't let themselves recognize him until months later, assembling the truth from fragments the way a jeweler assembles a setting around a stone.

"You came."

Daniel Creed stepped out from beneath the pier's shadow, and the evening light caught him full in the face, and he was smiling. Not the cold amusement Sebastian had catalogued in his father's parlor—something warmer than that, almost tender, which made it infinitely worse. He wore a good coat and boat shoes inappropriate for the mud, and he picked his way across the flats toward Sebastian as if he were strolling his own grounds.

"Alone," he observed, glancing past them at the empty shoreline. "Good. I half expected the police. You've been so careful all these years, Mr. Nakamura. I couldn't quite believe you'd break the habit."

"The note said you'd hurt Marisol."

"The note said a great many things." Daniel stopped ten feet away, close enough that Sebastian could see the fine stitching on his collar, the faint dampness at his temples. He was nervous. Underneath the smile, he was nervous, and that was the first true thing about him. "Harlan writes such florid confessions, doesn't he? All that guilt, all those long sentences. I told him nobody would believe a dying man's letter. But you—you're a professional. You follow evidence."

"Evidence," Sebastian repeated. Their voice sounded strange to them, flattened by the wind off the flats.

"Which is why we're here." Daniel gestured at the bay with an open hand, a host presenting a view. "You've been reconstructing my files from memory—I know, it's remarkable, truly—but memory has gaps, doesn't it? Even yours. So let me fill them in. Consider it a professional courtesy between people who keep records."

Sebastian said nothing. They had learned, across five years and two hundred interviews, that silence was a net, and you cast it wide and waited.

Daniel cast himself into it gladly.

"She was supposed to sign the release," he said. "The insurance payout, the trust structure, all of it—it required her signature, and she refused. Do you understand? She stood in my father's parlor—*that* parlor—and said she didn't want the money, she wanted the truth about the dive charter, about who'd been skimming the crew shares, and she would go to the state marine board if she had to. My father begged her. I offered her triple." His smile flickered, and for a moment something young and wounded moved behind it. "She just looked at me. Like I was something she'd scraped off her boot."

"And so you followed her here."

"I followed her here," Daniel agreed softly. "And Harlan—" He stopped. The tide pools caught the last of the light and threw it back in broken coins. "My father loves this town more than he loves God, and he loved God mainly for forgiving him. He couldn't let her talk. But he couldn't do it himself either, so he asked me to make sure she changed her mind, and I—" He spread his hands, an apology, a shrug, a confession. "The water does the accounting. That's what I told myself. The bay takes what the bay takes. Ask anyone in Greywater; it's practically scripture."

"It isn't scripture," Sebastian said. "It's a place where a woman died while you watched."

"I didn't watch." For the first time his voice cracked, and the crack revealed the thing underneath—not remorse, Sebastian realized, but offense. "I helped her out of her gear first. I want that understood. She went into the water clean. It was almost gentle."

The words landed in Sebastian's chest like stones dropped down a well. They thought of Elena's watch, dredged up after five years and mailed like a taunt—no, not mailed by Daniel. By Harlan. The dying man's penance, each artifact a stone rolled away from the tomb. They thought of Marisol's hands shelling oysters while she talked about her sister's laugh. They thought of the alphabet of secrets they had built, every resident filed under a letter, every sin cross-referenced, and how the file marked **C** had always been too thin, because Sebastian had flinched from finishing it.

"I have it all now," Sebastian said quietly. "The ledger. The transfers through Creed Holdings. Your accounts. Harlan's letters. The watch. Enough to bury you."

"Enough to bury us both," Daniel corrected pleasantly. "That's rather the point of this meeting. You saw us that night, Mr. Nakamura. From the seawall. Don't look surprised—my father figured it out years ago, which is why he's spent five years being charming to you at every function in town. You think he liked your company?" He laughed, short and ugly. "He was keeping his witness close. And now you'll keep mine. Because if I fall, I take you with me. Accessory after the fact. Obstruction. Five years of silence, documented in your own meticulous little files." He took a step closer, mud sucking at his shoes. "So here is my offer, and it is generous: the originals stay buried, the copies burn, and you take a very long vacation somewhere the mail doesn't reach. Or—"

"Or?"

"Or I tell the Vasquez family exactly what their new pet investigator has been sitting on since the night their daughter drowned."

The bay seemed to hold its breath. Somewhere out on the flats, a heron lifted off, slow and prehistoric, and the sound of its wings was the loudest thing in the world.

"That's what you don't understand," Sebastian said. "I already told them."

Daniel's smile died by degrees, like a light on a dimmer.

"Two weeks ago," Sebastian went on, and their voice steadied as they spoke, the way it always did when the truth finally had weight enough to stand on. "At their table. Over Sunday dinner. Every word of what I saw, everything I failed to report, everything I'm guilty of. Marisol cried. Tomás put his hand on my shoulder and didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything to say. And then Marisol said—" Here Sebastian's throat closed briefly, and they let it. "She said, *then finish it.*"

"You're lying."

"I don't lie. I remember. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference between us."

Daniel moved then—fast, graceless, fear dressed as fury—and Sebastian, who had spent a lifetime watching and none acting, discovered in the space of a heartbeat that watching teaches you things too. They saw the shoulder drop, telegraphing the shove, and turned with it, and the two of them went down together into the cold mud of the flats, and the world became taste and struggle and the bell-note of a struck piling.

Then hands were pulling Daniel off—old hands, trembling, ferocious.

* * *

Harlan Creed had come down the pier path leaning on a cane and left it in the mud, and now he stood over his son with the tide coming back in around all their feet, and he looked older than death itself and twice as determined.

"No," he said. One word. He had been a man of ledgers and silences for eighty years, and he spent his remaining authority on that single syllable.

"Papa—"

"You will not touch him. You will not touch another soul in this town." Harlan's breath whistled. "I sent the letters. I sent the watch. I meant to die with it done right—confession, restitution, the boy spared if he'd kneel. But you can't stop, can you. You were reaching for the man's throat just now, in front of God and the bay." He turned to Sebastian, and his eyes were clear as tide water. "Everything I wrote in those letters is true. Sworn. Witnessed by the only court that matters." He nodded at the water rising around them.

And that was when Marisol Vasquez came down onto the flats.

She had not come alone—Tomás flanked her, and behind them, half a dozen faces Sebastian knew from the oyster bar, from the inquest, from the pews of St. Brigid's—but she might as well have been alone, for all the notice she gave anyone else. She walked through the returning shallows in her work boots, straight to the spot where the water deepened over the old channel, the exact coordinates Sebastian could have recited to the decimal, and she stopped there, and she looked at Daniel Creed kneeling in the mud with his father's hand fisted in his collar.

"My sister laughed," Marisol said, "the last time anyone heard her alive. Did you know that? She laughed, and then she went quiet, and then the water went quiet, and this town went quietest of all." Her voice did not rise. It didn't need to. "Five years I waited for the law to wake up. Now I see it was waiting for us."

"Marisol." Harlan bowed his head. "Do what you must with me. But my son—"

"Will stand trial," Marisol said. "On evidence that will hold. That's the justice I choose today." She looked at Sebastian then, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "But first, Mr. Nakamura. Tell them what you remember. All of it. Right here, where the record should have been made five years ago."

The tide crept in around Sebastian's ankles, cold as conscience, and they opened their mouth to speak—

—and far up the shore road, red and blue lights began to spin, silent at this distance, someone having called the law at last.
