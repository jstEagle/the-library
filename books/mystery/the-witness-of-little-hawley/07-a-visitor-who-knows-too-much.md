## 7. A Visitor Who Knows Too Much

The rain came to Little Hawley on the third of October, a thin grey drizzle that had been falling, it seemed, since the beginning of time. Tamsin Ravensworth watched it bead and slide down the archive's high slit window while she waited for the kettle in the little back room to boil, her hands wrapped around a chipped mug that read *Cathedral Fayre 1987* in flaking gold letters.

She had slept badly. The diary sat where she had left it, in the locked drawer of her desk, but she could feel its presence the way one feels a letter unopened — the weight of Edith's small, slanted hand pressing up through oak and brass lock. She had read the early entries twice more before bed, then once again at dawn with cold toast going stale beside her, cross-referencing dates against the police file until the numbers swam. The entries stopped on the fourteenth of June, 1974, mid-sentence almost, in the middle of an ordinary week: a complaint about scales practice, a grumble about Mrs. Aldous's arithmetic drills, a plan to pick elderflowers by the mill stream on Saturday.

*Saturday,* Tamsin thought now, watching the rain. *She never got to Saturday.*

The kettle clicked off. She was pouring water over the leaves when she heard the outer door — the heavy one at the head of the stone stairs, the one that groaned like a ship in weather — swing open, and footsteps descend: unhurried, deliberate, the tread of someone who had made an appointment with themselves long ago and intended to keep it.

By the time Tamsin reached the reading room, the woman was already standing at the counter, dripping gently onto the flagstones.

She was perhaps eighty, perhaps older, dressed in a good wool coat the colour of wet slate, a silk scarf knotted at her throat, a handbag held in both hands before her like a hymnal. Her hair was set in careful white waves, and her face was smooth in the way of faces that have been arranged rather than aged — composed, courteous, faintly amused, as though the whole world were a mildly clever joke she had heard some years ago and still appreciated.

"Miss Ravensworth," the woman said. It was not a question. "I hoped I'd find you here. You're always here, they say."

"I'm here most mornings," Tamsin said. "How can I help you?"

"My name is Margaret Fenn." The woman extended a hand — cool, dry, the grip brief and precise. "I was at school with Edith Marlowe. St. Saviour's Primary, before it closed. We shared a desk for two years." She smiled, and the smile did not reach anywhere near her eyes, which were pale grey and very still. "I saw the notice in the parish magazine. About the fiftieth anniversary appeal. Memories, photographs, that sort of thing. I thought — well. Fifty-one years is a long time to leave a child unaccounted for."

Tamsin felt the familiar tightening behind her ribs, the archivist's reflex: interest sharpening into attention, attention into caution. Visitors came with memories the way moths came to glass — endlessly, hopefully, uselessly. But there was something in this woman's stillness that was not hopeful at all.

"You knew Edith," Tamsin said carefully. "Then you'll understand why I have to ask — the school records from St. Saviour's were transferred to the county archive in 1991, but the pupil registers for 1968 through '74 are incomplete. I've been through them myself."

Something flickered across the woman's face — so fast Tamsin might have imagined it. A shutter drawn and released between one breath and the next.

"Of course you have," said Margaret Fenn. "They say you're thorough."

*They say.* Tamsin filed the phrase away. "Won't you sit down? I'll make tea."

"Thank you, no. I shan't stay long. My daughter collects me at eleven." The woman settled onto the edge of a chair anyway, spine straight, hands folded. She looked around the reading room with an expression Tamsin could not quite name — appraisal, perhaps, or inventory. Her gaze lingered on the locked cabinet against the far wall, on the iron gate visible through the inner doorway, on the ledger shelf with its numbered spines. "It's smaller than I imagined. All those years of Little Hawley, kept in one little room under the floor."

"Most towns keep less," Tamsin said. "You mentioned the anniversary appeal. Did you bring something? A photograph, perhaps?"

"A photograph." The woman considered this. "No. I brought something better, if it's what you want. I brought a name."

Tamsin set down her mug. "A name?"

"The man everyone was too frightened to say out loud, back then. Gerald Cade." She pronounced it carefully, savouring it, a word learned by rote. "The caretaker at St. Saviour's. He used to let the girls into the vestry when the choir master wasn't looking. He gave us sweets. He walked Edith home sometimes, when it rained." The pale eyes fixed on Tamsin with terrible mildness. "He died in 1989, poor man, alone in a bedsit in Norwich. But he was the last one who saw her. Everyone knew it. Nobody said it."

Tamsin kept her face still, though her mind was moving quickly, laying the claim against what she knew. Gerald Cade appeared in the 1974 file — she had read his statement only yesterday, or what remained of it. A caretaker's statement, two paragraphs, taken down by Sergeant Hobbes. *Saw nothing, heard nothing, went home at six.* And then — she remembered the oddness of it now, the wrongness she had noted and shelved — the statement was signed in ink darker than the rest of the page, as though added later, or written by another hand entirely.

"That's very interesting, Mrs. Fenn," she said. "May I ask how you remember it so clearly, after fifty years?"

"How does anyone remember?" The woman smiled again, that arranged smile. "Some things stick. A girl goes missing and the town closes its mouth like a purse-string. You grow old holding your tongue. Then one day you read a notice in a parish magazine and think — well. Someone ought to say it before we're all in the ground."

"And you chose me to say it to."

"You keep the records, don't you?" Margaret Fenn rose, smoothing her coat. "Records are all that's left of any of us, in the end. I should imagine you know that better than anyone." At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back — and for a moment the composure slipped, or seemed to slip, and Tamsin saw beneath it something quick and watchful and cold, the look of a bird assessing a window. "You'll look into Gerald Cade, then?"

"I look into everything," Tamsin said. "It's my job."

"Yes," the woman said softly. "So I'm told. Good morning, Miss Ravensworth."

Her footsteps climbed the stairs, unhurried, deliberate. The great door groaned shut above. Rain filled the silence after, ticking against the slit window, running in threads down the ancient glass.

* * *

Tamsin stood a long moment in the empty reading room, listening to the building breathe.

Then she moved, quickly now, the unease in her chest resolving into purpose. She pulled the visitor register from its drawer — a plain bound volume, cream pages, ruled lines, every soul who crossed the archive threshold recorded in her own neat hand or their own uncertain ones. She found today's date.

*Margaret Fenn* — signed in a firm, confident script, the letters upright and regular, the kind of handwriting taught with a copperplate copybook and a ruler across the wrist.

Tamsin stared at it. There was nothing wrong with it. That was what was wrong with it. In fifty years of signatures in this book — the scrawled, the shaky, the proud, the illiterate, the hurried — she had never seen a signature so *finished*. So rehearsed.

She went to the shelves and took down the box marked ST. SAVIOUR'S PRIMARY — TRANSFERRED RECORDS, and carried it to the table, and began, methodically, to go through it. Admission registers. Class lists. Dinner money ledgers. The fragile carbon copies of reports sent home to parents. Edith Marlowe's name appeared where it should, in the register for 1969 onward, in the class photograph of 1973 — second row, third from left, a thin solemn girl with a crooked parting. Tamsin let herself look at the face for a moment, the living ghost of the voice in the diary, then turned the page.

She searched the registers for 1966, 1967, 1968 — the years a schoolmate two years Edith's senior would have been enrolled. She searched for *Fenn*. She searched for *Margaret*. She searched the dinner ledgers, the absence books, the transfer-out lists, the prize day programmes with their columns of printed names.

Nothing. No Margaret Fenn. No Fenn at all, in any year, in any list, in any hand.

She tried variations. *Fenner. Fenwick. M. Fenn.* She went back further, to 1955, forward to the school's closure. She checked the confirmation rolls kept in the cathedral's own records, the Sunday school attendance books, the choir mothers' rota. If Margaret Fenn had ever passed through any door of any institution in Little Hawley, she had left no trace upon the paper.

Tamsin sat back. The rain had thickened; the light in the reading room had gone the colour of pewter. Somewhere overhead a door closed, and the cathedral shifted on its bones.

There were explanations, of course. People moved. Records burned. Families fled debts and scandals and simply vanished from the parish paper trail. An elderly woman's memory of her own schooling might be mistaken — though not, Tamsin thought, about sharing a desk for two years. Not about sweets and vestries and walking home in the rain. Those details had the texture of things studied, not lived. Recited, not remembered.

And one other thing. Small, and growing larger the longer she turned it over.

*I saw the notice in the parish magazine.*

Tamsin had drafted that notice herself. She had written it in September, submitted it to the parish office, and been told — told plainly, by the parish secretary, a woman incapable of concealment — that the magazine was full, that the notice would run in the November issue. It had not yet appeared. It was sitting, right now, in a tray on a desk upstairs, waiting for typesetting.

Margaret Fenn could not have seen it. No one could have. Unless they had access to the parish office. Unless they had read the draft.

Unless someone had sent her.

Tamsin rose slowly, her joints stiff, her mouth dry. She looked at the register again, at that too-perfect signature, and understood — with a clarity that arrived not as a thought but as a physical sensation, cold water poured down the spine — that she had just been interviewed. That the questions had flowed one way only, and she had answered them all: yes, she was thorough; yes, she would look into Gerald Cade; yes, the records were all downstairs, all in one little room, all beneath the floor.

And Gerald Cade. Dead nineteen years, unable to contradict a single word, convenient as a coat on a hook. *Steer her there,* the visit had said, in a voice too polite to speak aloud. *Point the diligent Miss Ravensworth at a corpse, and let her dig. Let her waste her weeks. Or better — let her publish the name, and be wrong, and be finished.*

The hunter realizing she is hunted: it was not a metaphor. It was the simple truth of the morning. Somewhere beyond the rain, someone knew exactly what she had found in the diary drawer, knew she had begun to pull threads, and had chosen — for now — to play with her rather than end her. To test how thorough she really was. To learn what she knew by watching what she chased.

Tamsin took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote, in her own hand, the record no one could edit without leaving a mark she would recognize:

*3 October. Visitor giving name Margaret Fenn. Claims schoolmate of E.M. Directs suspicion toward Gerald Cade (dec. 1989). Name absent from all St. Saviour's records, 1955–1991. Claimed knowledge of parish magazine notice not yet published. Signed register in practiced hand. Left 10:52.*

She blotted it, folded it, and slid it inside the cover of the burial ledger — the oldest one, the sealed one, the one nobody opened — reasoning only half-consciously that if her domain could be desecrated, it would be hidden best among things no one thought to disturb.

Then she climbed the stairs, unlocked the great door, and stepped out into the rain to ask the parish secretary, very quietly, who else had seen the notice that did not exist.

Behind her, in the dark of the archive, the diary lay in its locked drawer, and somewhere in its later pages — she was certain of it now, certain as bone — Edith Marlowe was waiting to tell her what she had seen in the crypt, and who had put it there.
