## 8. The Edited File

The microfilm reader lived in the corner of the archive like an old dog that had outlived its usefulness but not its loyalty. It was a Rees-Martin model from the seventies, all cream enamel and Bakelite knobs, and it hummed when it warmed up — a low, patient note, B-flat, Tamsin had decided years ago, though she had never verified this with a tuning fork because some fictions were kinder than facts.

She fed the first reel in with hands that were steadier than she deserved.

*Hawley Gazette & Advertiser, October 1974.* The film was brittle at the edges, spotted with brown decay the archivists called foxing, as though the past were a pelt slowly going to ground. The reader's bulb threw her shadow huge against the stacks behind her, and she worked, as always, in the small hours before the cathedral opened, when the only sounds were the hum of the machine and the tick of the building above her, stone cooling and contracting like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

The front page for the fourteenth of October was exactly what she remembered from her earlier surveys. *CHOIRGIRL MISSING — POLICE APPEAL FOR WITNESSES.* A photograph of Edith Marlowe, grainy as a watermark: a thin face, a stubborn chin, hair cut bluntly at the jaw by someone who had clearly done it themselves with kitchen scissors. She was wearing her choir robe in the picture, or the collar of it; the rest of her dissolved into grey.

Tamsin had read this page perhaps forty times in eleven years. She knew it the way she knew the Psalms.

Which was why, on the forty-first reading, she saw it.

The date line beneath the masthead read *Monday, October 14th, 1974.* But the appeal inside — *police are anxious to trace the girl, last seen on the evening of Friday the eleventh* — referred to events of the previous week, and the article's own body text spoke of searches "entering their fourth day." Fourth day. From the eleventh to the fourteenth was three days. A paper did not print a fourth-day search on the third day. Papers were many things — careless, sensational, hungry — but they did not make arithmetic errors about their own deadlines, because deadlines were the one religion journalists kept faithfully.

She backed the film up and ran it again. The same. *Fourth day.*

Her pen was in her hand before she had decided to pick it up. She wrote the discrepancy on a slip of archive card in her small, exact hand — the hand, said a treacherous voice in the back of her skull, that looked so much like the handwriting in the later pages of the diary — and reached for the next reel.

*October 21st, 1974.* Front page: *NO TRACE OF EDITH.* Below the fold, a longer piece, bylined by a reporter named G. Fenwick, whom Tamsin knew slightly from the Gazette's centenary supplement — dead now, she believed, or long retired to somewhere with warmer weather. The piece was a profile of the search effort, quoting the vicar, quoting the police superintendent, quoting a neighbour who described Edith as "a quiet little thing, but sharp."

Tamsin read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way she read documents suspected of forgery.

There was a paragraph missing.

She could not have said how she knew — that was the maddening, marvellous, terrible thing about a lifetime among records: you developed an ear for silence the way a bell-ringer developed an ear for a cracked note. The article moved from the second paragraph, which described the police cordon at the lychgate, to the fourth, which quoted the neighbour. Between them should have been a third. The column measure was wrong — the type sat a half-line high against the facing column, the way type sits when a compositor has been handed copy late and told to fill. And there was a white scar down the left margin of the page, a vertical streak where the emulsion had been scratched away — deliberately, evenly, with a blade, after development. Not damage. Surgery.

Someone had removed a paragraph from the master print before it went to microfilm. Fifty years ago or yesterday; the film could not tell her which. But someone had stood exactly where she was standing, or somewhere very like it, and had decided that the town of Little Hawley did not need to know what G. Fenwick had written in his third paragraph.

Tamsin sat back. The reader hummed its faithful B-flat. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and unwelcome.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty archive, because saying things aloud made them real and she needed this to be real. "Right."

* * *

The police file lived in Box 1974/C, on the third shelf of the restricted range, and Tamsin had the only key to the restricted range, which was a fact she repeated to herself several times over the following hour, the way a woman repeats a prayer that has stopped working.

She carried the box to the long table under the lamp and laid out its contents in the order she had catalogued them eleven years ago, when she first took custody of the transferred file: forty-one items, she had recorded, plus two photographs sealed in mylar. Forty-one items. She had counted them herself, because counting was the first sacrament of her profession and she had never once in her career trusted anyone else's arithmetic.

She counted again.

Thirty-seven.

She stood very still. The lamp buzzed. Somewhere overhead, a door closed — the verger arriving early, perhaps, or the wind worrying the south porch — and the sound fell down through the floors and died.

Four pages gone. Not misfiled — she checked, of course she checked, she pulled every adjacent box and shook it gently like a Christmas stocking, and nothing fell out. Not borrowed — there was no issue slip, no withdrawal card, nothing in her own meticulous register, and Tamsin Ravensworth's register did not lie; it was the single honest witness of her entire adult life. Four pages, simply *absent*, and the absence itself arranged so neatly — the remaining sheets re-collated, the brass fastener refastened, the folder sitting square — that a casual eye would never know anything had ever been there.

But Tamsin's eye was not casual. She had catalogued this file eleven years ago, item by item, and — thank God, thank God for the compulsions of a careful woman — she had *described* each item in her finding aid. She fetched the ledger volume and set it beside the file, open to the entry dated March 2014, her own younger handwriting, confident and unshadowed:

*Item 12: Witness statement, Marjorie Cutler (neighbour), 3 pp., signed, dated 12 Oct 1974.*
*Item 13: Witness statement, Rev. S. Halloway (vicar), 2 pp., signed, dated 13 Oct 1974.*
*Item 17: Statement re: sighting near lychgate, name redacted at transfer, 1 p., undated.*
*Item 18: Continuation sheet, Det. Sgt. R. Mowbray, re: door-to-door enquiries, west side of Church Lane, 2 pp.*

Items 12, 13, 17, and 18. Gone. All four. And the pattern of the removal was itself a sentence: the neighbour, the vicar, the unnamed sighting near the lychgate, and the door-to-door of Church Lane. Someone had not removed pages at random. Someone had removed a *geography*. Everything touching the churchyard's western approach, everything touching the lane that ran down past the sacristan's door—

Tamsin stopped. Her pen hovered over the card.

The sacristan's cottage stood at the top of Church Lane. It had stood there since 1908. In 1974 it had been occupied by the cathedral's sacristan, a man named Pell — the father, if the family resemblance meant anything, of Ambrose Pell, the benefactor whose money kept the roof on and whose charm kept everyone else off balance.

She wrote the connection down anyway. Writing things down was how she refused to be frightened of them.

Then she turned to Item 9, because Item 9 was still present, and Item 9 was the statement of the verger, Harold Tice — the same Harold Tice who now lay dying in Meadowbank care home, and whom she intended to visit tomorrow regardless of what the morning held.

Item 9 was two pages long. Tamsin had read it a dozen times over the years. She knew its texture: ballpoint, blue, the cheap kind that skipped, leaving pale tracks through the letters like veins in marble.

Today the ink was black.

Not faded-to-black, not blue-ink-aging-dark, which has a particular brownish patience to it. This was black ballpoint, wet-looking even now, the strokes confident and unskipped, and the handwriting — the handwriting was *different*. Rounder. A schoolroom italic, tidy and unhurried, the hand of someone who had learned penmanship properly and never lost the habit.

The words, however, were the words she remembered. Almost. She laid her finding aid beside it and compared, line by line, the transcription she had made in 2016 against the page in front of her.

*"I saw nothing unusual on the night in question,"* said both versions. But the original — her 2016 transcription, made from the document as it then existed — continued: *"I did hear the crypt gate at approximately 11 o'clock, which I took to be Mr Ellery locking up late."*

The page before her ended at *"night in question."*

Full stop. New sentence, in that round schoolroom hand: *"I locked the crypt myself at ten as usual and heard nothing further."*

They had not merely removed pages. They had *rewritten testimony*. They had taken a dying declaration — for that is what it was, an old man's account of the last hour he could vouch for — and had replaced it with a forgery that contradicted it, and had left the forgery in the file wearing the dead man's name, and had trusted that no one would ever hold the two versions side by side.

Because no one was supposed to have a 2016 transcription. No one was supposed to keep finding aids that detailed. No one was supposed to be Tamsin Ravensworth.

A coldness moved through her that had nothing to do with the undercroft's damp. It settled in her chest and made itself at home.

* * *

She worked until the light changed above the stairwell — grey to pearl to the thin gold of a Little Hawley morning, sifting down through the grille that let the world know the archive existed without letting the world in. She photographed everything: the scratched microfilm, the shortened file, the forged statement, her own old transcriptions, the dates, the discrepancies, the arithmetic of the fourth day. She photographed the boxes' spines, the shelf, the lock on the restricted range. She built, photograph by photograph, a fortress of evidence, because evidence was the only architecture she had ever believed in.

And all the while a single question turned in her like a key in a lock:

*Who has access?*

The answer was short enough to memorise. Herself. The dean, who had never once come downstairs in her tenure. The verger, for deliveries. And the fellows of the restoration trust — the trust chaired, funded, and in practice *owned* by Ambrose Pell, whose donations had paid for the dehumidifiers, the shelving, the very lamp that now burned over her evidence.

He had walked these aisles. He had stood at this table. He had perhaps run his thumb along Box 1974/C and smiled at whatever he found there, the way a gardener smiles at a bed he has weeded.

Tamsin thought of his hand on her elbow at the Dean's Dinner, warm and paternal. *You're doing such important work down here,* he had said. Important work. Yes. He had known precisely how important. He had been auditing it for fifty years.

The fury arrived then, and it was not the hot kind. It was cold and total and very quiet, the fury of a woman standing in her own house and finding every mirror turned to the wall. Eleven years she had guarded this place. Eleven years she had believed that order was a bulwark, that a well-kept record was a promise made to the future, that her meticulousness was a form of love. And all the while, in the dark, someone else had loved this collection too — had tended it, curated it, pruned it — had made of her archive a garden of lies and let her believe she was the gardener.

Her domain. Desecrated. And she had dusted around the wounds for a decade without seeing them.

She pressed her palms flat against the table until the wood bit back, and she made herself a vow in the language of her profession, which was the only dialect her anger spoke fluently: *I will reconstruct every page you took. I will find your ink. I will date your hand. And I will enter it all in the register, in my name, where no one — not you, not the bishop, not fifty years of your careful gardening — will ever be able to edit it out.*

Above her, faintly, the tower clock struck eight.

Below her, in the restricted range, Box 1974/C sat square and silent on its shelf, four pages lighter than history, waiting — as she now understood it had always been waiting — for someone to notice.

And as she climbed the stairs toward the ringing of the morning bell, Tamsin realised she had forgotten, in all the fury and the forensics, the simplest and most chilling deduction of all: the visitor register from last week was still open on her desk, and the woman who had signed a name that existed nowhere — the woman who had steered her toward a dead man — had sat at this very table, in this very chair, while the forged file lay six feet beneath her feet.

Whoever was tending this case had not sent a stranger.

They had sent someone who knew the archive. Who knew the register. Who knew *her*.

Tamsin paused on the bottom step, the gold light full in her face, and understood that the next document she needed was not in any box at all.

It was in the memory of a dying man named Harold Tice — and she would be at Meadowbank care home before the morning was out.
