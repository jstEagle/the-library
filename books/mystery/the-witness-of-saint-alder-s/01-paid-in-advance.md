## 1. Paid in Advance

The red light on the microphone was the only honest thing in the room.

Ulyana Thornbury looked at it the way she used to look at the sea as a child — with respect, and a small private fear that it might one day swallow her whole. It was 11:47 on a Thursday night, and she was recording the final episode of a season that had, by every metric her producer could find, failed.

"Welcome back to *The Witness*, the podcast where we ask what one voice can do against fifty years of silence." She said it in her radio voice, warm and low, the voice that had once made three hundred thousand strangers feel like they were sitting across her kitchen table. "I'm Ulyana Thornbury. And tonight, we close out our season with an episode I never wanted to make."

She paused. In the old days, the pause was a tool. She'd learned it from her mother, who could hold a silence at the dinner table so long that Ulyana's father would confess to anything just to break it. Now the pause was just... a pause. The gap where an audience should have been.

"Two years ago, I told you the story of Marcus Bell," she continued. "A man convicted of killing his business partner on the Norfolk coast. I told you about the tire tracks, the ferry ticket, the neighbor who heard shouting. I told you I was certain."

The kettle in the kitchen clicked off. She let the sound stand in for a breath.

"I was wrong. The ferry ticket was his brother's. The neighbor recanted. And last spring, after eleven years inside, Marcus Bell walked free because of DNA evidence I had spent two episodes explaining away." She kept her voice level. That was the discipline now — level, always level, like walking on ice you'd already fallen through once. "I retracted. I apologized. I paid back the award money, which hurt less than the letters did. Some of you wrote to say you'd never listen again. Some of you wrote worse than that. Both groups were right to be angry."

She looked down at her notes, though she didn't need them. She knew this script the way you know a scar.

"So here is my final episode of the season. No case. No theory. Just me, telling you what I've learned, which is this: certainty is a drug, and I was an addict, and the withdrawal nearly killed me. If *The Witness* comes back — and I honestly don't know if it will — it will come back slower. Smaller. More afraid, which is to say, more careful. Because the opposite of getting it wrong isn't getting it right. It's being willing to sit in the not-knowing for as long as it takes."

She reached for the mouse to stop the recording, and that was when the doorbell rang.

At quarter to midnight. In the rain.

Ulyana sat very still, the way you do when your house makes a sound it shouldn't. Then she laughed at herself — a short, unlovely laugh — saved the file, and went to answer it, pausing only to grab the cricket bat she kept by the coat rack, which her sister had given her as a joke and which she had never once needed until, possibly, now.

* * *

The man on her doorstep was sixty-something, dry under an enormous black umbrella, and dressed like a funeral that had been upgraded to a wedding. Grey suit, grey tie, grey hair combed with geometric precision. He held a briefcase the way priests hold scripture.

"Miss Thornbury." He pronounced it *Thorn-burry*, correctly, which put him ahead of most people. "Forgive the hour. I'm Ambrose Quill. I'm instructed to deliver something to you personally, and personally was emphasized."

"Deliver what?"

"A retainer." He opened the briefcase with a small ceremonial air, as though the hinges were part of the liturgy. "My client wishes to engage your services."

"My services." She leaned against the doorframe, bat still in hand but angled casually, the way you angle a thing when you want it forgotten. "Mr. Quill, I'm a podcaster. My services are talking into a microphone and occasionally ruining my own life."

"My client is aware of your work. All of it." Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile. "Including the retractions. He considers them relevant qualifications."

"That's a first."

"He believes," Quill went on, unruffled, "that someone who has been publicly wrong, and has examined exactly how she was wrong, is better equipped for this task than someone who has never been wrong at all. The latter category, he notes, consists mostly of liars."

Ulyana studied him. Rain came off the umbrella in a fine silver fringe. Somewhere down the street a fox screamed, and Quill didn't so much as blink, which told her he was from a world where midnight deliveries and screaming foxes were both simply weather.

"And what is the task?"

Quill drew out a heavy envelope, cream-colored, sealed with actual wax, which struck her as either very old-fashioned or very deliberate. He held it out. She didn't take it.

"Who's your client?"

"Instructed not to say."

"Then I don't know who I'm working for, or why, or—"

"You'll know the task," Quill said mildly. "That's the arrangement. Five thousand pounds, half in advance, the balance on completion. You may refuse, of course. Most people do, at first." He glanced past her, into the hall, at the cricket bat. "I'd have guessed tennis, somehow."

"It was a gift."

"They usually are."

She took the envelope. It was heavier than paper had any right to be. Inside, she could feel the crisp edges of banknotes, and beneath them something flatter, stiffer. A card, maybe. Or a photograph.

"The terms are inside," Quill said. "Along with the instruction. One instruction. My client asks only one thing, Miss Thornbury, and it is this—" he paused, and for the first time the lawyerly smoothness thinned, and underneath it she saw something tired, something almost sorry, "—prove that Edith Marsh is alive."

The name landed somewhere in her chest and sat there, unfamiliar and cold, like a coin dropped down the back of the sofa.

"Edith Marsh," she repeated. "I don't know that name."

"No," Quill agreed. "Almost nobody does. That's rather the point." He tipped the umbrella back, considered the rain, and stepped off the porch onto the path. "You have until the end of the month to accept. There's a number in the envelope. Burner phone, don't bother tracing it, a friend of mine tried and it embarrassed us both."

"Mr. Quill." He stopped but didn't turn. "Why me? Really. Not the speech about being wrong. Why me?"

He did turn then, rain streaming off the umbrella's edge between them like a bead curtain.

"Because my client said you'd ask that question," he said, "and that the asking was proof he'd chosen well. Good night, Miss Thornbury. Do read the letter before you decide anything. It's the handwriting that matters."

* * *

She read it at the kitchen table with the mic still warm in the next room and the cricket bat lying across two chairs like a patient in triage.

Five thousand pounds in fifties, banded neatly. A burner phone, still in its packaging. And a single sheet of good paper, typed except for one line at the bottom, written by hand in faded blue ink:

*I am alive. Find me before the tide turns. — E.M.*

Ulyana sat back. The house ticked around her, settling, ordinary. On the wall above the stove hung the framed printout of her first episode ever — a hundred thousand downloads in a week, her sister's handwriting on the mat: *told you so.*

She thought about Marcus Bell's face at the retrial. Not angry. Worse — puzzled, like a man checking his pockets for something he was sure he'd had.

She thought about the phrase *before the tide turns*, which was either poetry or a deadline, and she had learned, expensively, that deadlines hidden in poetry were the dangerous kind.

And she thought about the handwriting itself. She wasn't a graphologist, but she'd interviewed enough liars to know that hands tell truths mouths won't. This writing was old-fashioned, upright, careful — the loops precise, the pressure even. Except in one place. The *E* in *E.M.* was pressed so hard into the paper it had torn through slightly, as if the person signing had meant it as an act, not a name.

"Prove Edith Marsh is alive," she said aloud, to test it. The kitchen absorbed it without comment.

She got up, made tea she wouldn't drink, and opened her laptop. Her search history was a graveyard of cases; she typed the name into the newest grave.

Saint Alder's. Disappearance, 1974. Sole witness: Edith Marsh, aged nineteen. Testimony withdrawn. Case unsolved. Village partially lost to flooding the same winter.

And then, three results down, a parish record transcription, digitized by some local history society, listing burials:

*Marsh, Edith, 1955–1974.*

Ulyana stared at the screen for a long time. Dead fifty years. Gravestone and register agreeing, which in her experience was rarer than you'd hope and more convincing than you'd like.

So the client was either mad, or cruel, or playing a game whose rules hadn't shown themselves yet. Five thousand pounds to prove a dead woman lived.

She should refuse. Every instinct honed by two years of penance said refuse, walk away, stay small and slow and safe.

But the torn-through *E.M.* stayed in her mind like a splinter. Somebody had signed that line with their whole body. Somebody had paid five thousand pounds in cash to send a stranger hunting a ghost — and had known, Quill had said, that she'd ask *why me?*

Ulyana picked up her phone, photographed the letter, the money, the burner phone, and uploaded all three to the cloud. Old habits. New ones too.

Then she looked up Saint Alder's on the map. It sat on the coast like a comma the sea had never gotten around to finishing — roads ending mid-street, a church standing in what the satellite image rendered as a grey-green smear of marsh and shallows.

The tide, according to the tide table she found with two clicks, turned on the last day of the month.

Spring tide.

She closed the laptop, and in the quiet that followed she heard herself say, in her radio voice, the sentence she would later understand was the first line of a story she had no idea she was already inside:

"Well then. Let's go wake somebody up."
