## 15. The Client Unmasked

The burner phone woke Ulyana at six minutes past midnight, buzzing against the nightstand like an insect trapped under glass.

She'd been asleep for perhaps forty minutes — the shallow, salt-flavored sleep of a woman who had spent the evening photographing parish ledgers and uploading them to three separate cloud accounts, because a note reading *some witnesses stay buried* has a way of rearranging your relationship with your own mortality. She lay still in the dark of the rental room, listening. The sea did its patient work against the seawall. Somewhere below, the landlady's dog sighed.

The phone buzzed again.

She picked it up on the third ring. "You have terrible timing."

"You have terrible security," said the voice, and Ulyana's whole body went cold, because she knew it now. She should have known it days ago. She *had* known it, some animal part of her, every time Daniel Voss leaned on the chandlery railing with his tea going cold beside him, watching the water with the particular patience of a man who has been waiting fifty years for something to surface.

"Hello, Mr. Voss," she said.

A pause. Long enough that she could hear him breathing, hear the faint creak of whatever chair he sat in above the chandlery. "When did you know?"

"Tonight. Maybe an hour ago." She sat up, switched on the lamp, and pulled her notebook across the duvet. "The retainer came through a firm called Penhale & Marsh. Solicitors' letterhead, very proper, five thousand pounds in used notes. Except I rang the Law Society this afternoon on a hunch, and there is no Penhale & Marsh. There never was. There was a Penhale & Voss — a two-man practice in Saint Alder's that dissolved in 1976, when its junior partner went to prison. The senior partner, Mr. Penhale, died in 2019. His letterhead stock didn't die with him. It went into his estate sale. Guess who bought a box of old office supplies at the estate sale, Mr. Voss?"

Another silence. When he spoke again, the careful flatness had gone out of his voice, and what was underneath was worse — something raw and old and terribly tired. "I wanted you to trust it. A lawyer's envelope opens doors. A convict's envelope gets thrown away."

"And the photograph? Edith Marsh's handwriting, recent, on the back of a tide table?"

"Mine," he said simply. "I've been practicing her hand for four years. I had samples. Everyone in this town kept something she wrote — shopping lists, hymn margins, a recipe for sloe gin I stole from the vicarage when no one was looking. Four years, Miss Thornbury. Do you know how long four years is, when you're copying the loops of a dead girl's letters?"

Ulyana closed her eyes. The lamp made a small warm island around her, and beyond it the room was all shadow and salt damp, and somewhere out there the sea was counting down to the spring tide.

"Why?" she said. "That's the part I can't make fit. You hired me to prove Edith Marsh is alive. Fine. Cruel, but fine — you wanted me to dig up the town's lie. But then the second call. 'The murder hasn't happened yet.' 'It will occur on the spring tide.' 'Find her before the water.' I thought I was working for someone trying to *prevent* a killing. Was that theater too?"

"No," Voss said. "That part is true. That part is the only true thing I've said to you."

"Whose murder, Daniel?"

"Hers," he said, and the word fell into the room like a stone into a well. "Edith Marsh died in 1974. Not her body — her body is walking around Saint Alder's right now, cataloguing photographs and being kind to schoolchildren and being called Miss Aldous by people who love her. But Edith Marsh, the witness — she was murdered the morning after the flood, when she walked into the police station and took it all back. Someone killed her testimony, and everyone in this town helped bury it, and they've been watering the grave for fifty years." His breath caught. "On Saturday, at the spring tide ceremony, it dies properly. Officially. They're unveiling a plaque, did you know that? *In memory of those the sea reclaimed.* Reclaimed. As if the sea did it. As if the sea skimming the flood relief fund and drowning the auditor who found out was an act of God."

Ulyana's pen had stopped moving. She stared at her own handwriting without seeing it. "So when you said find her before the tide—"

"I meant it. Find her. Make her testify. Publicly, before the whole town, before the water comes in. That's the only thing that can kill the lie instead of letting the lie kill her twice more." He paused. "You've met her, haven't you. Miss Aldous. You've sat in that chapel with her among the drowned photographs, and you've felt it. I watched you leave the heritage center last Tuesday. You stood in the street for a full minute afterward, just standing there, like a woman who'd heard a song from her childhood played slightly wrong."

Ulyana said nothing. Her throat had gone tight.

"I'm not a monster," Voss said, and it sounded like a man pleading a case he'd lost decades ago. "I want you to understand that. I spent nineteen months inside on the strength of a testimony that evaporated overnight, and another thirty years being the man from Saint Alder's who did *something* to somebody, nobody quite remembers what, but look how he flinches. I lost my trade, my name, my mother — she died while I was inside and they wouldn't let me out for the funeral. And the woman who put me there has spent fifty years being beloved. Being *gentle*. Being the keeper of the town's memories, for God's sake, while mine rotted in a cell." His voice cracked and steadied itself with visible effort. "But I didn't hire you to hurt her. I hired you because you were the only person in England with a reason to dig, and a reputation for digging, and nothing left to lose. I read about your case. The one you got wrong."

The old shame rose in her like floodwater. "That's low, even for you."

"It was necessary. I needed someone who understood what it costs to be wrong about a person. You got a man wrong once, Miss Thornbury. So did this town. The difference is you spent years trying to un-do it, and they spent fifty years pretending it never happened." He let that sit. "I chose you because I thought — if anyone will understand why Edith owes me the truth, it's her."

"She doesn't owe you her consent," Ulyana said quietly. "Whatever she owes history, you don't get to drag a seventy-something woman to a seawall and—"

"I'm not dragging anyone. Yet." Something shifted in his voice, and Ulyana heard it with a lurch: the sound of a decision already made, wearing patience as a disguise. "Saturday, the tide ceremony. The whole town on the square. Edmund Harrowe arriving in his car with his oxygen like a Roman emperor at the games. The vicar saying his words about the sea. And if Edith Marsh won't stand up and say what she saw—" He stopped.

"Say it," Ulyana said. "Finish the sentence. I need to hear you finish it."

"If she won't say it," Voss said, each word placed down carefully, like a man crossing ice, "then I'll say it for her. All of it. Her name, what she saw, what she retracted, who paid for the retraction. In front of everyone. And if this town wants to silence me the way it silenced her, they'll have to do it in front of a crowd and a podcast microphone, because I've learned one thing in fifty years, Miss Thornbury — shame only works in the dark."

"That's not testimony," Ulyana said. "That's an execution. You'll burn her life down. The woman she became — the archivist, the person everyone loves — you'll destroy her to resurrect a dead girl neither of them wants to be."

"The dead girl is *me*," Voss said, and now the rawness was fully open, no disguise left. "Don't you see? I am the dead girl. I've been dead since 1975. This isn't vengeance. This is a resurrection, and resurrections are never gentle." A long breath. "You found her. You did what I paid you for. Now there's one more job, and it's not paid, and you can refuse it. Keep her away from the square on Saturday. Take her out of Saint Alder's, drive her anywhere, put her on a train. If she's gone when the tide comes in, I'll... reconsider."

"And if she's there?"

"Then the water decides," he said, "the same as it decided in 1974," and the line went dead.

* * *

Ulyana sat for a long time with the phone in her lap, listening to the sea.

Then she got up, dressed in the dark, and drove.

The streets of Saint Alder's at one in the morning were a study in wet stone and sleeping gulls. The chandlery's upper window showed a thin line of lamplight; she didn't stop. She drove past Harrowe Hall's gates, past the dark chapel, past the seawall where the water slapped and sucked at the stones like something worrying a bone, and she thought about the shape of the thing she'd been carrying for two weeks without recognizing it.

Because here was the truth she kept circling: she had liked Daniel Voss. That was the humiliation of it. In a town of rehearsed answers and shaking vicar's hands, he had been the only person who told her things straight — *ask who benefited from my silence* — and she had built him, in the private workshop of her heart, into the wronged man, the innocent, the one decent ruin in a landscape of polished lies. She had wanted his vindication. She had half-planned her final episode around it.

And all along he'd been the client. All along the anonymous money and the mysterious urgency and the *find her before the spring tide* had been a man setting a stage, and she had been the stagehand, hauling scenery for a play whose ending was already written. *Prove Edith Marsh is alive.* Not so she could live. So she could be *used*.

Ulyana pulled over near the harbor and sat with her hands on the wheel, and let herself feel it fully: the betrayal, hot and specific, and underneath it the older, colder recognition — *you of all people should have checked who was paying you.* She had built a career on the question *who benefits?* and she had forgotten to ask it about the one check that mattered, because the payer had sad eyes and a broken life and she had pitied him, and pity, she knew now, was just credulity wearing a kinder coat.

Her phone buzzed. Not the burner — her own. An email. Automated, from the cloud service, confirming tonight's upload: 214 files, complete.

And beneath it, a new message, sent eleven minutes ago, from an address she didn't recognize. No subject line. Just a scan of a single sheet of old paper, yellowed, creased into quarters, water-stained along one edge.

A letter. In a young woman's hand — the hand she'd seen on the tide table, the hand she'd matched against Miss Aldous's catalogue cards. Dated August 1974. Three weeks after the flood.

*If anything happens to me, I want it written down somewhere that I saw what I saw. I am not brave. I am going to take it back tomorrow and I will hate myself for it, but there are thirty children at St. Brannoc's and he said he would drown them in their beds, one for every word I spoke, and I believe him, because I watched what he did to the man at the seawall.*

*I am sorry. Whoever finds this — I was fourteen, and I was so afraid, and I am sorry.*

Ulyana read it four times. Then she looked up through the windshield at the dark bulk of the flooded chapel on its rise above the waterline, one window still lit — impossible, at this hour — a small gold rectangle in the black stone.

Miss Aldous was awake. Miss Aldous had her email address, because Ulyana had given it to her, two weeks ago, over tea, in the innocent course of asking for archive access.

Which meant Miss Aldous had been listening to the same midnight conversation somehow, or had reached the same conclusion on her own, or — and this possibility arrived in Ulyana's chest like a swallowed stone — had decided, tonight, that if the truth was coming for her either way, she would rather hand it over herself. On her own terms. To the one person in Saint Alder's who might still choose mercy.

The light in the chapel window went out.

Ulyana started the car. Saturday was three days away, and the spring tide was coming in regardless, and somewhere between the sleeping town and the waiting sea, a fourteen-year-old girl's apology had finally, after fifty years, found someone to deliver it to.

She drove toward the chapel with her headlights off, slow on the wet road, her heart hammering out the only prayer she knew: *let me be wrong about one more person — please — let me be wrong in the right direction this time.*
