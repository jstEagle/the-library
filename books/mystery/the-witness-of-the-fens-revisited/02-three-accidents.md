## 2. Three Accidents

The archive after dark was a different country. By day it belonged to the tercentenary committee, to volunteers with white gloves and laminated badges, to the hum of the dehumidifier and the polite clatter of the tea trolley Dean Fenn insisted on sending down every afternoon at three. But at night, when Sable Oyelaran turned the key in the iron door and the cathedral's stone silence settled over the stacks like snow, the place became hers alone — forty thousand documents breathing their slow breath of vellum and dust, and she the only living thing among them.

She had not meant to open the files. She told herself she was pulling them for Harrow's inevitable request — the police would want everything touching the crypt, every record of disturbance, every note of previous work near the vault — but her hands knew better. Her hands went past the tercentenary boxes, past the conservation reports, down to the bottom drawer of the steel cabinet she had privately named *the drawer of small wrongs*, and lifted out three folders that had lived there for years like stones at the bottom of a pond.

She spread them on the reading table beneath the green-shaded lamp. She did not need to open them to know what was inside. She had memorized them the way other people memorize grief.

* * *

The first folder was thin, and it was Cyril Pask's.

Cyril had been the verger for thirty-one years, a small man with a whistling breath and a genius for keys. He could open any lock in the cathedral blind, by feel, the way a blind man reads a face. On the fourth of November, nine years ago, he had fallen from the crypt stair — the narrow spiral that descended from the north transept into the cold dark where Aldous Crane slept in his lead-lined coffin. He had fallen, the coroner said, backward. Down twelve steps. A man of sixty-eight, sure-footed as a cat on those stairs he'd climbed twice daily for three decades, falling *backward*.

Sable closed her eyes and she was there again: the morning after, standing at the top of the stair while the paramedics carried the stretcher up into the light, watching a smear of blood on the eleventh step — the eleventh, not the twelfth — and thinking, with a cold clarity she had never learned to forgive herself for, *he was struck from above, or he was struck below and pushed, and either way someone stood where I am standing now.*

There had been a discrepancy. Of course there had been. There always was, in Fenmarsh, if you knew how to read the ledgers the way Sable read them — not as records but as sentences, with grammar and hesitations and lies between the lines. The service register for that week recorded a requiem mass said for Cyril Pask on the sixth of November. But the burial ledger — Cyril's own hand, his last entries, made days before his death — showed an interment scheduled for the fifth that no one had ever performed. A burial entered and then erased, the ink scraped back so carefully you would miss it unless you tilted the page against the light, which Sable had done, alone, at two in the morning, three weeks after they buried Cyril himself.

Someone had been meant to go into the ground that week. And then Cyril died, and the entry vanished, and no one asked why.

She had reported nothing. She had been twenty-six years old, newly permanent in her post, hired by Dean Ambrose Fenn himself, who had shaken her hand and said, *You have the eye, Miss Oyelaran. The eye is rare.* And she had looked at the scraped ink and thought: who am I, against a town, against a coroner, against a dean? Who am I to say this was murder when everyone kind and reasonable tells me it was an old man's misfortune?

She had put the folder in the drawer. That was the cost of being right without proof: you learned to file your certainties away like contraband.

* * *

The second folder was thicker, because drowning leaves more paper.

Edith Marsh, warden of the fen sluices, found in the River Leam eleven years before Cyril fell — found downstream of the weir, three days after she was last seen at the cathedral's harvest supper, sober, cheerful, complaining about the price of eel nets. The coroner ruled misadventure: she had walked the fen path home, slipped, gone into the water. It happened. The fens took people; it was practically municipal policy.

But Sable had grown up half-feral on these fens herself, fostered out to a family in Marrow Drove, and she knew what the river did with its dead. She had pulled the tide tables for that week — she still kept them, folded into the folder like a pressed flower — and the mathematics were simple and terrible. Edith had gone into the water, according to the finding, somewhere above the old mill cut. But the mill cut drained *against* the tide that night; anything entering there drifted upstream into the reedbeds, not down to the weir. For Edith to be found where she was found, she must have entered the river below the sluice gates — below the sluice gates, at night, alone, in October, in her good shoes.

Or she had never entered the water at all where anyone saw her enter it.

And there was the ledger. There was always the ledger. In the year of Edith's death, the alms distributions — small sums, shillings and sixpences, paid quarterly to the fen wardens in recognition of their keeping of the drains — stopped. Not tapered. Stopped, mid-column, the final payment initialled by a hand Sable had never been able to identify, and beside it, in the margin, one of those flourishes: a looping pen-stroke like a bird's wing, repeated twice, then never used again in that ledger's lifetime.

She had shown the tide tables to no one. She had been twenty-nine, and she had learned by then what happened to archivists in Fenmarsh who noticed things: nothing happened to them, which was itself the punishment. You simply carried your knowledge alone, year upon year, until it grew heavy enough to bend your spine.

* * *

The third folder smelled faintly of smoke even now, though the fire had been sixteen years ago and the papers inside were only copies.

The Mowbray cottage, on Cutler's Drove, burned on a Sunday night in February. Thomas Mowbray, retired schoolmaster, seventy-one, had died in it. The fire brigade called it an electrical fault in the front room; Thomas had fallen asleep in his chair by the bar heater, as he did every winter night. His daughter had said so at the inquest, weeping, grateful almost, that it was quick.

Sable had catalogued Thomas's effects when the cathedral acquired his library — he'd left his books to the archive, poor man, the only bequest he had to give. Among them she found his commonplace book, and in the commonplace book, in his neat schoolmaster's hand, a list of dates going back decades, each date followed by initials. She had spent four months, off and on, working out what the dates were. Feast days. Every one of them feast days from the old kalendar — Candlemas, Lammas, the feast of Saint Withburga, local, obscure, the saint whose bones were said to lie beneath the cathedral floor. And beside each feast day, initials. Initials that matched, when she checked them against the parish registers, names. Names of people who had died. All accidental. All, like Cyril and Edith, connected in some threadbare way to the cathedral — verger, warden, tenant of cathedral land, bell-ringer, sexton.

Thomas Mowbray had been keeping count. And then Thomas Mowbray had burned.

The ledger discrepancy for Thomas was the smallest and the strangest: in the terrier — the great survey of cathedral lands and holdings — a parcel of fen behind Cutler's Drove changed hands the week after the fire, transferred out of a holding that had been in the same name for a hundred and forty years, into the cathedral's own freehold, witnessed by two signatures Sable had traced to men long dead. Dead men witnessing live transactions. Someone had reached back into the past to make the present legal.

* * *

Three folders on a green baize table. Three accidents. Three discrepancies she had never reported, because each one alone was nothing — a scraped line of ink, a tide table, a flourish in a margin — and together they were only a pattern, and patterns, Detective Inspector Harrow had told her once, with the weary kindness of a man defusing the same conversation for the third time, patterns are what obsessive minds make out of coincidence.

She heard his footsteps on the stair before she heard the knock. Heavy, unhurried, the tread of a man who wanted her to know he was coming.

"Miss Oyelaran." DI Harrow filled the doorway the way weather fills a horizon — grey coat, grey eyes, a face built for patience rather than sympathy. His gaze went to the table, to the three folders laid out like a tarot spread, and something flickered behind his eyes that might have been pity. "The desk sergeant said your light was on. I'm here about the coffin."

"I know why you're here." She stood, smoothing her skirt, aware of how it must look — the archivist alone at midnight with her conspiracy files. "I have something for you. About the crypt. About all of it."

He came no further into the room. "An empty coffin is a prank, Miss Oyelaran. A nasty one, timed for the cameras. Students, most likely, or some group with a grievance about the tercentenary money. We're pursuing several lines of—"

"Cyril Pask," she said.

Harrow stopped.

"The verger who fell on the crypt stairs nine years ago. The stairs that lead to the vault they just found empty." She turned the first folder toward him, opened it, laid her finger on the photograph of the scraped ledger line. "A burial was scheduled that week and erased. Erased with a scraper, carefully, by someone who knew the ledgers. And now the founder's coffin is empty, and everyone is surprised."

"An old man fell downstairs—"

"Backward. On stairs he climbed twice a day for thirty-one years. And eleven years before that, Edith Marsh drowned on the wrong side of the tide. And sixteen years before *that*, the one man in this town who was keeping a list of deaths tied to the old feast days burned asleep in his chair." Her voice was steady. She had rehearsed this steadiness for years without knowing it. "I'm not asking you to believe me, Inspector. I'm asking you to hold these until you've finished being surprised."

For a long moment Harrow said nothing. He looked at the folders the way a man looks at water he has been assured is shallow. Then he closed the first one gently, almost tenderly, and pushed all three back across the table toward her.

"You catalogue things," he said quietly. "It's what you're for. And when a mind like yours can't find an order in the world, it makes one. I've seen it before. Good people. Careful people." He picked up his hat. "I'll take whatever bears on the coffin. The rest of this—" he nodded at the drawer of small wrongs, not unkindly, "—the rest of this is grief looking for a shape. Get some sleep."

His footsteps faded down the stair. The door did not quite close behind him — it never did; the frame had swollen years ago — and through the gap Sable watched the cathedral's floodlit west front glow against the dark, serene and certain, worshipped by ten thousand people who had never once wondered what lay beneath it.

She sat back down. She drew the third folder toward her, Thomas Mowbray's, and turned to the last page of the commonplace book, the page she had never been able to explain. One final date, in a shakier hand than the others, written perhaps a week before the fire. No initials beside it. Only a single line:

*If I do not finish this, ask the ledgers. They remember what we are made to forget.*

Beneath it, a reference. A shelf mark. Her shelf mark — the numbering system she herself had devised, six years ago, for the tercentenary collection.

Thomas Mowbray had been dead eight years when she invented it.

Sable sat very still in the pool of lamplight, her heart beating slow and enormous, and understood that the next thing she touched would change everything.
