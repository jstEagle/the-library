## 4. The Dean's Kindness

The dean's study smelled of beeswax and old leather, the way it had on the day he'd hired her — nine years ago now, when Sable Oyelaran had been a twenty-four-year-old with a conservation certificate, a suitcase, and no history anyone cared to ask about. Dean Ambrose Fenn had looked at her across this same desk and said, *Everyone deserves a place where their past doesn't precede them.* She had thought it kindness. She still wanted to think it kindness.

"Sit, sit." He waved her into the chair by the window, where afternoon light came through leaded glass in amber panes. On his desk, between a paperweight of Fenmarsh fen-agate and a photograph of the cathedral choir, sat a folder she hadn't seen before. Her name was written on the tab in his careful, clerical hand. *O. Oyelaran.*

"You've heard about the grant," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I heard something at the bursar's office."

"The Whitcombe Research Fellowship." He slid the folder toward her. "Endowed, endowed properly — three years, your own budget, an assistant if you want one. The tercentenary has made the chapter realize what an asset we have in you. What the archive has in you." He smiled, and the smile reached all the way to his eyes, which was the trouble with Ambrose Fenn: he was never performing warmth. He simply had it, generously, the way some men had height or a good singing voice.

Sable looked down at the folder. Inside were figures that would have paid off her flat, funded the climate control the archive had begged for since before her arrival, bought proper acid-free housing for the whole parish collection.

"This is because of the coffin," she said quietly.

His expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a shutter drawn a half-inch, no more. "This is because of your work. Which the coffin has, admittedly, made visible." He laced his fingers together on the desk. "The town is frightened, Sable. You've seen it. A founder's grave opened after three hundred years and nothing inside but mud and — " he paused delicately — "a shoe. People need steadiness. They need the church to be steady."

"And they need me not to ask questions."

"They need you not to *perform* questions." His voice stayed gentle. That was the worst of it; he never raised it, never threatened. "I've read your memo to Inspector Harrow. The ledger discrepancies. The verger's fall, the drowning, the fire." He said the words without flinching, naming them like items on a shopping list. "You have a gift for pattern, my dear. It's why I hired you. But patterns are also how we frighten ourselves. Three sad accidents in two hundred years, in a town built on water and peat — accidents are what the fens *do*. If you go looking for murder in them, you'll find it, whether or not it's there. And then you'll be the archivist who cried conspiracy at the tercentenary. Is that what you want your name to mean here?"

She looked at him for a long moment. He met her gaze with perfect, patient openness.

"You're offering me a grant and a warning in the same breath," she said.

"I'm offering you a future," he said, "and asking you not to throw it away on ghosts." He rose, came around the desk, and rested a hand briefly on her shoulder — the same gesture he'd used when her mother's death notice finally reached her, six years ago, unsigned and unexplained. "For your own good, Sable. That's all any of this is. For your own good."

Walking back through the cloister, she held the folder against her chest like a shield, or like evidence. The stone flags were cold even in June. Somewhere above her, the tercentenary scaffolding creaked against the tower, and she found herself thinking, uncharitably, that the dean had never once asked her what she'd found in the ledgers. Not what she suspected. Not whom. Only that she should stop.

Kindness, she thought, that tells you exactly where to stop looking is not kindness. It is a fence.

* * *

DI Harrow ate his lunch in the car, as usual, a meal-deal sandwich going stale on the passenger seat while he reread Sable Oyelaran's memo for the fourth time.

He'd been dismissive in her office and he knew it. That was policy more than conviction — you didn't tell a civilian archivist *yes, I believe our town's founding family was murdered across three centuries* on the strength of marginalia and gut feeling, not when the chief constable was chairing the tercentenary committee and the cathedral was the biggest employer in the district. But he hadn't thrown the memo away either. It lived now in the door pocket of his car, dog-eared, and every time he stopped at the lights on Fenn Bridge he caught himself thinking about tide charts.

Because that was the thing. The drowning — Ellery Voss, 2011, pulled from the drain-cut below the sluice — had never sat right with him either, back when he was a constable and too junior to say so. The body had been found downstream of the weir, and everyone had nodded along with *fell in above, drifted down*, but Harrow had grown up on these fens. You didn't drift through the sluice gates in August. The flow ran backward on the spring tides. Anything in that channel went *up*, not down, unless someone put it there.

He'd said nothing at the time. Eleven years of saying nothing.

Now he sat outside the deanery with the engine ticking cool, watching the cathedral through the windscreen, and told himself he was there for routine canvassing. Tercentenary security assessment. Perfectly normal.

A woman came out of the cloister door — tall, dark-skinned, walking fast, clutching a folder. Oyelaran. She crossed the close without looking left or right, went into the archive annex, and shut the door behind her with a click he could almost hear from thirty yards.

Harrow got out of the car and stood a moment in the graveyard shade, looking up at the tower. The scaffolding wrapped it like bandages. Somewhere in his chest, an old unease turned over and opened one eye.

*Three accidents,* she'd written. *Each within sight of the crypt. Each followed by a quiet correction in the parish records.*

He was a skeptic by trade and temperament. But he knew what it looked like when a town agreed not to look at something, because he'd been part of the agreement for eleven years.

He got back in the car and drove to the harbourmaster's office to request eleven-year-old tide tables, telling himself it meant nothing.

It meant everything, and he knew it.

* * *

That night the archive annex was hers alone, as it always was after seven. Sable worked late because the building was honest at night — no visitors, no clergy drifting through with small talk, just her lamps and the breathing of old paper.

She was rehousing the 1724 ledger — the cipher ledger, though she'd told no one that — when she noticed the light under her office door.

Her office was inside the annex. The corridor light was on a timer. She had not switched it on.

She stood very still, one hand flat on the ledger's linen cover, and listened. No footsteps. No voice. Just the faintest sound, once, of a drawer sliding home on oiled runners — runners she herself had waxed last winter.

There was no second way out of her office. Whoever was inside would have to pass her to leave.

Sable set the ledger down without a whisper of sound and stepped out of the stacks into the corridor, and the moment her shadow fell across the threshold of her own doorway, the figure inside moved — fast, silent, professional. It went past her. Not at her; *past* her, shoulder turning at the last instant so that it brushed her sleeve rather than struck her, and then it was down the corridor and through the fire exit, and the alarm chirped its single polite chirp, and the night swallowed it whole.

She stood in the empty corridor, heart slamming, and made herself count to sixty before she moved.

Nothing was taken. She checked twice, then a third time, hands shaking only a little. Her files on the three deaths — untouched. Her working notes on the cipher — untouched, or replaced so perfectly she couldn't tell. The drawer that had closed was the bottom-left one, the one where she kept — she went cold — the tercentenary display catalogue, the 1724 ledger's condition report, and a photocopy of the marginal flourishes she'd made on a whim three days ago.

On a whim. In daylight. At the copier by the front desk, where anyone might have seen.

She sat down slowly in her chair, in the room that smelled of her own tea and her own dust, and understood that the search had not been a failure. It had been a message. *We know what you know. We can reach what you reach. We chose, tonight, to take nothing — and you should think hard about why.*

Or — and this thought arrived unwelcome, wearing the dean's gentle voice — perhaps it was simpler than that. Perhaps someone had merely confirmed that she had copies. That burning or stealing the originals would avail nothing now.

Either way, the lesson was the same. She was being read, the way she read ledgers: carefully, patiently, margin to margin.

Sable locked the annex, walked home through the sleeping close with her keys threaded between her fingers, and did not sleep. At three in the morning she gave up, lit her desk lamp, and spread the photocopy of the cipher across the kitchen table.

The flourishes repeated on the feast days. She'd established that much. But tonight, staring at them with the raw clarity of the sleepless, she saw what she'd been too hurried to see before: the repetitions weren't uniform. Every seventh entry carried an extra stroke — a small descending tail, like a signature's final flourish, like a hand signing off.

Seven entries. Seven feast days. She counted forward from the earliest struck-through name, marking each seventh, and the sequence marched through the ledger year by year, decade by decade, until —

Until it arrived at the most recent feast day in the volume. Martinmas, last November.

And after that final marked entry, the tail-flourish appeared once more, alone, in the margin beside a record she had catalogued a hundred times without ever truly reading: a birth registration, decades old, entered by the cathedral itself.

Mother unnamed. Father blank.

Surname: *Oyelaran.*

The lamp hummed. Outside, the fens lay black and still beneath a moon the colour of bone.

Sable sat motionless for a long time, her own name swimming in the lamplight, and understood that whatever the searcher had come looking for, they had already known what she was only now beginning to learn:

The ledger had been waiting for her all along.
