## 5. Blood in the Register

The genealogical charts lived in the north bay of the archive, in a cabinet Sable had catalogued herself during her first year and never opened again. They were beautiful things — vellum sheets the size of tablecloths, inked with the patient vanity of a family that had believed it would last forever. The Crane tree began with Aldous himself, drawn as a sapling at the top margin, and descended through nine generations of branches rendered in brown iron-gall ink that had eaten into the skin of the page until the names seemed scarred there rather than written.

She unrolled the first chart across her worktable and weighted its corners with lead book-snakes.

Outside, Fenmarsh was rehearsing its tercentenary. She could hear the brass band stumbling through the same eight bars on the cathedral green, over and over, a phrase that kept dying and being resurrected. Somewhere a man was shouting about bunting. The town was preparing to exhume its founder in three days' time and celebrate him, and Sable sat alone with the arithmetic that would undo all of it.

She had spent the previous evening copying out the cipher's struck-through names onto index cards, one to a card, laid out in date order across her kitchen floor while her tea went cold beside her. Twenty-three names. She had slept badly among them, or rather she had not slept at all, and by dawn she had done what she always did when the ledgers spoke too clearly: she had come to work early, before the cleaners, before Dean Fenn's secretary arrived with his post, and she had taken down the family trees.

Now she set the cipher list beside the first chart and began, name by name, to match them.

The verger came first. Thomas Ellery, struck through in the 1724 ledger's margins in that small, deliberate hand, dated the week of his fall from the crypt stair. She found him on the fourth branch down — *Ellery, Thomas, verger to this church* — an odd entry for a family tree, she'd always thought, a servant recorded among the bloodline. Now the oddness rang like a struck bell. A servant did not belong on a genealogy unless he belonged to the family. Unless he *was* the family, wearing a servant's coat.

Her pen hovered. Then she wrote, on a fresh card: **Ellery = Crane?**

The drowning came next. Mercy Kettle, 1781, fished from the fen drain below the sluice gate, ruled misadventure. Struck through in the ledger within days of her death. On the chart, Sable found a gap where a daughter should have been — the 1770s branch thinned abruptly after two solid generations of fecund Cranes, as though someone had pruned it. In the parish burial register, Mercy Kettle appeared under her married name. In the baptismal register of 1758, there was no Mercy Kettle at all.

There was, however, a *Mercy Crane*, baptised privately, at home, the entry witnessed only by the curate. And in the marriage register, the woman who became Mercy Kettle had signed her name with a small looping M that matched, stroke for stroke, the M in the private baptism.

Sable sat back. The band outside had stopped; the silence felt enormous.

*They weren't strangers,* she thought. *Every name in the cipher is a Crane. The accidents aren't murders of witnesses — they're prunings.*

She worked through the morning without noticing the hours pass, the way she had not noticed time pass since she was a girl reading in the children's home library, that particular trance of hers that teachers had called a gift and social workers had called avoidance. The fire victim, 1849 — a lodger named Joseph Pike who died in the toll-house blaze — resolved into Joseph Crane-Pike, a hyphenated entry in a dissenting chapel register that the established church had no reason to consult. The fall from the mill race, 1862. The fever that took four members of one household in a single winter, 1877, leaving one infant alive who appeared, thereafter, in the records under a different surname entirely.

By noon she had twenty-three cards arranged in a fan across the worktable, and every single one of them connected to the tree. Not one of the dead was an outsider. The cipher wasn't a list of people who had learned the secret.

It was a list of Cranes who had been removed.

* * *

The vertigo came on slowly, the way floodwater comes into a fen field — not all at once but everywhere at once, so that by the time you notice, your boots are already wet.

She stood at the cabinet and pulled the later charts, the Victorian ones, executed in a finer hand with gilded capitals and little painted shields. The nineteenth-century Cranes were prosperous, numerous, celebrated: aldermen and benefactors, their portraits hanging now in the cathedral cloister, their names carved on the lychgate and the schoolhouse and the drinking fountain on the green. She traced the line down through the decades with her fingertip, and the fingertip kept finding what the ink tried to hide.

A marriage entered twice, once in the parish register and once, faintly, in pencil, in a different hand, then erased so thoroughly the vellum shone where the graphite had been ground away. A son recorded as born in 1884 whose mother would have been fifty-one. A death in India that corresponded to no shipping record, no regiment, no grave — a man who simply ceased, on paper, to exist, while a boy of about the right age appeared two years later in a boarding-school register under the Crane name, fees paid, the guardian listed as *the Dean and Chapter of Fenmarsh*.

The Dean and Chapter. Again and again, whenever the bloodline thinned, whenever a record needed sealing or a child needed inventing, the same institution surfaced like a body in the drain. Not the mayor. Not the family itself. The cathedral.

Sable walked to the window. Below, the band had resumed its eight bars. Two women were testing the ropes of the maypole on the green, laughing. A man in a hi-vis vest directed a lorry reversing toward the cathedral steps, where the empty coffin — though nobody yet knew it was empty, though the exhumation was still officially tomorrow — would lie in state.

Three hundred years, she thought. Three hundred years of this town arranging itself around a name. The festival. The motto cut into the market cross. The schoolchildren who still learned the Crane founding legend by heart, the little rhyme about the reeds and the light. All of it built on a lineage that had run out of blood before Queen Victoria died, and on a succession of strangers quietly poured into the mould ever since, each one told he was the heir, each one perhaps believing it, each one buried under a stone bearing a name that belonged to no one living.

And the town had killed to keep it. That was the part she kept circling back to, unable to make it sit still in her mind. Not once. Over and over, across two centuries, whenever a true-blooded Crane surfaced — a servant who knew too much, a disinherited daughter, a cousin returned from overseas — the ledger's cipher hand had struck the name through, and within the year the parish registers recorded another accident. Drowning. Fire. A fall. The fens were generous with accidents. The fens had always been generous.

She thought of Thomas Ellery going down the crypt stair in the dark, and made herself hold the thought.

* * *

It was the sealed box that finally broke something open in her.

She found it in the legal deposit section, where the cathedral's own administrative papers were interred — a tin document case, rusted at the clasps, labelled in faded copperplate: *Chapter Acts, 1893–1901 — CLOSED*. Closed papers were rare here; the archive ran on openness the way the tide ran on the moon. She checked the catalogue. The restriction note read: *Not to be opened without consent of the Dean.* No expiry. No reason given. The seal signature beneath was that of Archdeacon Wilfred Fenn — Dean of Fenmarsh, 1889 to 1911.

Fenn. The dean's own predecessor. Ambrose Fenn had been named for him, she realised; the family resemblance in the portrait downstairs, the same long jaw, the same hooded eyes, was not coincidence but dynasty. The guardianship of the secret was itself hereditary.

She should have waited. Procedure said wait; her whole professional life was procedure, and procedure was the armour she wore against exactly this kind of moment. But the band outside played its eight bars again, and somewhere in her chest the dread and the thrill — those old unwelcome twins — rose together, and she broke the clasps with a flat screwdriver from her desk drawer and heard the rust give way like a held breath released.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone brittle as autumn leaves, lay a single bound volume: an adoption register, unofficial, private, kept by the Chapter itself. Nineteen entries between 1866 and 1904. Each entry recorded a child brought into Fenmarsh — some infants, some older — and placed, usually with a widow or a childless couple of good standing, under a new surname. And beside each entry, in the archdeacon's hand, a second name. The name the child had been born with.

Crane. Every one of them. Or near enough — Craynes, Crain, one Crame, spelling drifting the way names drift when they are being hidden. Children of the scattered true line, gathered up wherever the Chapter's correspondence could find them, renamed, resettled, and slotted into the town's story like replacement panes in an old window — close enough in colour that no one standing outside would ever see the mending.

The final entry was dated March 1904. A boy, aged six, born in Ely, mother deceased, father unknown. Born name: Crane. Given name: Fenn.

Adopted by the Archdeacon himself.

Sable closed the volume and held it against her chest and stood very still in the cold of the north bay, listening to her own heart. So it was not only a lie the Chapter maintained. It was a lie the Chapter *fed*. The deans had not merely guarded the empty name — they had made themselves its heirs, generation upon generation, adopting the blood they claimed to protect until the boundary between guardian and beneficiary dissolved entirely. Ambrose Fenn was not the last of the keepers.

He was the last of the Cranes. By purchase. By paperwork. By everything except blood.

And if the register's logic held — if the Chapter had spent two centuries gathering stray Crane children and renaming them — then the practice had not necessarily ended in 1904. The tin box was old, but institutions are not. Institutions repeat themselves the way the tide repeats itself.

She looked down at the last page of the adoption register, at the blank lines that followed the boy Fenn's entry, and saw that they were not entirely blank. There was a twentieth entry, begun and abandoned — a date in a later hand, 1970-something, and the first letters of a word, pressed hard enough to emboss the page, then scratched out with a blade.

But the embossing remained. She tilted the page against the window light, and the dented letters rose out of the paper like bones under silt.

*Oyel—*

The band outside stopped mid-phrase. In the silence, Sable Oyelaran stood holding a book that contained the first half of her own name, written in a stranger's hand, forty years before she was born.
