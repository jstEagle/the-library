## 10. Read Aloud

The tercentenary morning came in silver and grey, the fens steaming gently under a sun that had not yet decided whether to commit. Sable stood at the edge of the cathedral green and watched the town assemble itself around a lie three hundred years old. Bunting in the Crane colours — deep green and bone-white — hung from the lampposts. A brass band was tuning somewhere behind the market stalls. Children waved little flags printed with the founder's profile, that stern, bearded face that had looked down from every classroom wall in Fenmarsh for as long as anyone could remember.

She had not slept. Her hands, tucked into the pockets of her cardigan, still smelled faintly of the flooded passage — cold water and old stone and something older still, the mineral patience of the earth beneath the cathedral. In her satchel, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, lay the ledger.

Aldous Crane's actual remains lay below them all. She had found them at dawn, in the hidden chamber off the crypt, arranged with terrible care: a skeleton seated in a plain oak chair, hands folded, a wax-sealed letter on its lap addressed in the founder's own hand — *To be read aloud, when the town is ready to hear it.* The crypt above held only silt and a child's shoe, a decoy staged so long ago that the deception had become scripture.

And Dean Ambrose Fenn had spent his whole life making sure no one read that letter.

She saw him now on the cathedral steps, resplendent in vestments, greeting the mayor with the warmth of a man who believed he had already won. Last night he had offered her everything — the archive, the name, the inheritance, a place in the story instead of at its margins. He had locked the crypt behind him with the gentleness of a father tucking in a child, and she had smiled and asked for one night to decide.

He had believed her. That was the saddest part. He had believed her because he wanted a Crane heir so badly that he had been willing to manufacture one, and when a true one appeared — orphaned, untraceable, registered by the cathedral itself — he had spent months deciding whether to crown her or bury her.

The murders had decided it for him. The verger who had seen too much. The drowning that the river was meant to explain. The fire. Three names struck through in a cipher two centuries old, and the newest hand to strike through them had been his.

Sable moved through the crowd like a woman walking through her own memory. Mrs. Petch from the archive volunteers pressed a paper cup of tea into her hands. The mayor's voice boomed something about heritage and continuity. And everywhere, everywhere, the name *Crane*, spoken with the easy reverence of people who had never once questioned what they were worshipping.

The ceremony began. Inside, the cathedral was full — the nave packed, the side chapels standing-room only, the great east window throwing coloured light across a congregation that had come to watch a coffin be reinterred with honours. The empty coffin. Sable took a seat near the back, the satchel on her lap, her heart beating so loudly she was certain the woman beside her could hear it.

The service moved with liturgical smoothness. Hymn, lesson, address. Then the dean ascended to the lectern for the tercentenary address, and beside him, on a small table draped in white, sat the tercentenary display — the parish ledgers she herself had catalogued, arranged in chronological splendour for the public to admire.

Including the 1724 ledger. The founder's ledger. The confession.

She watched his eyes flick to it — once, quickly, the way a man checks a locked door — and she understood the plan completely. Somewhere in the service, perhaps during the final hymn when all eyes would be closed or lifted, the ledger would be removed. A fire in the vestry. An accident. A tragic loss of irreplaceable records, mourned for a generation. The cipher would burn, and with it the names, the struck-through lineages, the final instruction. And the letter on the dead man's lap would remain a curiosity no one would ever connect to anything.

The dean began to speak. His voice was beautiful. That was the thing about Ambrose Fenn — everything about him was beautiful, considered, kind. *For three hundred years,* he said, *this town has kept faith with its founder.*

Sable rose.

It was not dramatic, her standing. That was the point. She simply stood, as any parishioner might, and walked up the side aisle with the unhurried gait of a woman who had spent her life among records and had learned that the truth does not need to shout. It only needs to be read.

The dean saw her and faltered — one syllable, a hairline crack in the marble of his voice. She reached the front, set the satchel on the lectern's lower shelf, and drew out the ledger in its oilcloth.

"Dean Fenn," she said, and her voice carried further than she expected, the way voices do in buildings built to carry prayer. "Before we reinter the founder, I think the founder should be allowed to speak."

A rustle went through the congregation. The dean's hand moved, very slightly, toward the ledger on the display table — and stopped, because three hundred pairs of eyes were watching, and because Detective Inspector Harrow had just appeared at the west door with two officers, moving not toward the altar but toward the stair that led down to the crypt.

"Madam Oyelaran," the dean said gently, "this is not the moment—"

"It is precisely the moment." She opened the ledger to the page she had marked. "The flourishes in these margins are not decoration. They are a cipher, keyed to the feast days, maintained by every cathedral archivist since 1724. It records the Crane line. Every birth. Every death. Every heir." She paused. "Every true-blooded Crane. The last of whom died in 1899."

The word *died* landed in the silence like a stone in still water.

"The heirs who came after were adopted. Quietly. Sealed records, authorised by this cathedral, generation after generation — because the town needed the name more than the name needed the truth. And the founder knew it would come to this. Because the founder—" her voice caught, steadied, "—the founder never left the crypt at all."

She turned the ledger to the final page and began, in a clear and level voice, to read Aldous Crane's confession. *I write this knowing it will not be read in my lifetime, and perhaps not in any lifetime, for towns do not forgive their founders for being mortal...* The words of a dying man who had watched his own legend begin and had hidden his body where only the truth-seekers would ever look, and had left instructions that the ledger — his ledger, his cipher — be read aloud when the town was ready.

She read about the founding and the fortune and the fear of extinction. She read about the first substitution, made with love and desperation in equal measure. She read the instruction: *Let the ledger be the coffin. Let the truth be the body. When they are ready to bury me honestly, let them read this aloud.*

And in the middle of the reading, below them, in the crypt where Aldous Crane had sat in his oak chair for three hundred years waiting for exactly this, Harrow found the dean's private stair unlocked and descended into the dark — and found, at the bottom, a paraffin can and a bundle of documents that had no place in any archive, and understood that the man who had guarded the lie had been preparing, this very morning, to end it the only way left to him.

The officers brought him up through the vestry door just as Sable reached the final line. The congregation turned as one. Ambrose Fenn stood between two constables, and his face — she would remember this all her life — was not furious. It was exhausted. It was the face of a man putting down a weight he had carried too long.

"The ledger," he said quietly, to her, across the nave. "I only ever wanted to keep it safe."

"I know," Sable said. "That's what makes it a tragedy, and not just a crime."

They led him out. Harrow paused at the west door and looked back at her — the archivist and the detective, the skeptic and the believer, each holding half of a truth the other had needed — and gave her a single nod, which from Harrow was a eulogy, a commendation, and an apology all at once.

* * *

They asked her, afterwards, about the inheritance.

The mayor, the solicitors, a distant Crane cousin from Cambridge who materialised within the hour like a vulture with a briefcase — they all wanted to know what she intended to do. The name was hers by blood. The estate, such as it remained. The seat on the tercentenary trust. The story could be salvaged, they said; a living heir was better than a dead legend; the town could survive the truth if the truth had a respectable face.

Sable stood in the archive that afternoon, in the room that had been her whole world long before she learned it had been her cradle, and gave her answer.

"My name," she said, "is Sable Oyelaran. It was given to me by people I never got to thank, in a life that was arranged without my consent, and it is the one thing in this entire affair that is genuinely, verifiably mine. I'm keeping it."

"And the Crane estate?" the solicitor asked.

"Put it in trust for the archive. Fund the cataloguing properly. Let the ledger be displayed — unciphered, annotated, with the confession printed beside it. Let the town read what it has been praying to." She looked out the window at the cathedral green, where workmen were already dismantling the bunting. "I didn't come to Fenmarsh to inherit a legend. I came to catalogue it. I'd like to finish the job."

That evening, Harrow found her reshelving. He stood in the doorway of the stacks with his hands in his coat pockets, looking like a man who had rehearsed a speech and decided against it.

"The drowning case is closed," he said. "All three. The Crown Prosecution Service has the dean's statement. He's cooperating." A pause. "He asked that you be told the crypt chamber is to be preserved. He's paying for the conservation work himself."

Sable set a folio down. "He would."

"The founder's remains — the town council wants to know your wishes. Reinterment in the coffin, presumably. With honours."

"Read the confession over him," she said. "All of it. Aloud. And bury him under his own name — his real one, the ordinary one, the one from before the legend. He waited three hundred years to be a man instead of a monument. Let him have it."

Harrow almost smiled. Then he reached into his coat and produced a slim folder. "One more thing. The adoption records. Your mother's name was sealed by order of the cathedral, but the dean's cooperation means the seal is lifted." He set it on the table between them. "You don't have to open it today. You don't have to open it ever. But it's yours now. No one else's."

Sable looked at the folder for a long moment — at the plain manila cover, the cathedral stamp, the string tie that had held a stranger's secret shut for thirty years. Her whole life, she had been the keeper of other people's records. She had catalogued the dead and filed the living and never once been able to find herself in the index.

She untied the string.

Outside, the last of the bunting came down, and the fens went quiet in the way they did at dusk — vast, patient, keeping what they kept. Inside the archive, under the lamp, Sable Oyelaran read her mother's name for the first time, and began, at last, to catalogue herself.
