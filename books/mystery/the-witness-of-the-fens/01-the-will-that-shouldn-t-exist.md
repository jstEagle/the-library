## 1. The Will That Shouldn't Exist

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Sebastian Whitlock would later consider the first wrong thing about it. Tuesdays were for probate correspondence and the quarterly reconciliation of trust accounts; Wednesdays were for clients; Thursdays were for the parish council, which he attended out of a sense of duty so old it had fossilized into habit. Nothing of consequence was supposed to happen on a Tuesday.

It came in the second post, wedged between a water rates notice and a flyer for the Fenny Stowbridge Autumn Fayre (Welly Wanging, 2 p.m., All Welcome), and it sat on his doormat looking, if anything can be said to look guilty, guilty. The envelope was heavy cream stock, the kind that costs more per sheet than some people spend on lunch, and his name was written across it in copperplate so precise it might have been engraved: *Mr. S. Whitlock, Solicitor.*

Sebastian stood in his narrow hall with the post in one hand and his umbrella dripping on the mat—he had walked home from the office through a fen drizzle that wasn't quite rain but never quite stopped either—and felt the small, cold prickle at the back of his neck that he had learned, over fifty-one years, to take seriously. It was the same prickle he'd felt when a client's smile didn't reach their eyes, or when a document was *too* tidy.

He carried the letter into his kitchen, set the kettle on—because whatever else happened, there would be tea—and examined it under the good light above the stove.

No postmark. No return address. The stamp was correct, current, affixed square and true. Someone had taken this to a post box personally, which meant someone wanted him to know they had taken it personally.

He opened it with the bone-handled letter knife that had been Aldous Fenwick's, because Sebastian was a man who kept things where they belonged, and read it standing up, which he almost never did.

*Dear Mr. Whitlock,*

*You are instructed to attend Marsh House on Friday next, at low tide, for the reading and execution of the last will and testament of Edith Crane, deceased.*

*You will come alone. You will bring your seal.*

*Yours faithfully,*

There was no signature. There was only, beneath the valediction, a small embossed circle pressed into the paper—a notary's seal, worn nearly smooth by decades of use, but legible still to anyone who had spent twenty years watching it descend onto documents across a scarred oak desk:

*A. FENWICK, NOTARY PUBLIC.*

The kettle began its slow climb toward a whistle behind him. Sebastian did not hear it. He was looking at the seal the way a man looks at a photograph of someone at their own funeral.

Aldous Fenwick had been dead for four years. Sebastian had arranged the funeral himself. He had chosen the hymns—Fenwick had left instructions, filed alphabetically under F, naturally—and he had watched the coffin go down into ground so wet the gravediggers had joked grimly about needing an anchor. He had catalogued Fenwick's effects, settled the estate, closed the office on Cutter's Lane, and carried home two boxes of personal papers that he had never opened, because opening them would have meant admitting that the desk was empty now, and the chair, and the dry voice that used to say, *File it properly, boy, or don't file it at all.*

And yet here was his seal, pressed into cream-laid paper, summoning Sebastian to execute the will of a woman who had been legally dead since before the last general election.

"Right," Sebastian said aloud, to the kitchen, in the tone he used for difficult clients. "Right."

The kettle screamed. He made the tea without tasting any of it.

* * *

Marsh House stood—or leaned, depending on one's generosity—at the drowned end of the fen, three miles past where the road gave up pretending and became a raised track between black ditches. Everyone in Fenny Stowbridge knew about Marsh House the way everyone knows about a bad tooth: you don't look at it directly, but you're aware of it constantly, and it shapes how you chew.

Edith Crane had lived there alone for years before she vanished. Sebastian had been thirty-nine then, newly made partner, still arranging his pens by nib width. He remembered the disappearance the way the town remembered it—in fragments, carefully sorted. The police search. The dogs going quiet at the edge of the reed beds. Judge Harrow's measured statement to the *Ely Standard*. The vicar's memorial service, held without a body, where Cade the brewer had paid for the flowers and everyone had agreed it was a lovely gesture. Twelve months later, the declaration of death in absentia. Sebastian himself had handled a piece of the paperwork—a small thing, a transfer of a fishing right, initialed and filed. He could still feel the pen in his hand.

She had simply gone. That was what everyone said. *Simply gone*, as though the fens were a door and she had walked through it, and the decent thing—the *town* thing—was to close it quietly behind her.

Sebastian drank his tea, then poured it out and made fresh tea, because the first cup had gone cold while he sat staring at nothing, and a man should have standards even when the floor is tilting.

He took out the file. Of course he took out the file; he was Sebastian Whitlock, and when the world cracked open, his instinct was to reach for the folder labeled CRANE, EDITH — ESTATE (DECLARED) and hold it like a rail in a storm.

The file was thin. Suspiciously thin, he thought now, turning the few sheets over. A certified copy of the declaration. The fishing-right transfer. A single page of notes in his own younger handwriting: *No next of kin located. House contents: see Harrow J., inventory held.* See Harrow J. As though the inventory of a missing woman's house were a matter for the local magistrate rather than her solicitors. As though he had asked no questions because there were no questions to ask.

He had been thirty-nine and ambitious and grateful, and Aldous Fenwick had patted the file shut and said, "That's all tidied away nicely," and young Sebastian had glowed like a schoolboy given a gold star.

The kettle clicked off. Outside, the drizzle went on with its fen patience, wearing the world down grain by grain.

* * *

He did not sleep well, which was itself remarkable, because Sebastian Whitlock slept the way other men balanced ledgers—completely, and every night. But at half past two he found himself downstairs in the study with the letter again, holding it under the lamp, tilting it against the light.

Watermark: fine. Paper: expensive, old stock—he'd seen reams of it in Fenwick's bottom drawer, bought in bulk sometime in the nineties, the firm's letterhead cut from the same sheets. And the seal—

He got out his magnifying glass, because he owned a magnifying glass and saw no reason to pretend otherwise, and studied the impression. Pressure marks consistent with a hand press, the old brass-and-ebony one that had sat on Fenwick's desk to the right of the inkwell. Sebastian had sold it with the office contents. Or believed he had. He made a mental note to check the sale inventory, then made a second note to be ashamed of the first note, because the alternative explanations were worse than a clerical error.

Because here was the arithmetic that would not balance. Either someone had stolen a dead man's seal and was using it—which was forgery, fraud, and a criminal offence stacked like pancakes. Or someone had access to the seal legitimately, which narrowed the field to approximately nobody, because Fenwick had kept it locked in that drawer and given the key to no one, not even Sebastian, not even at the end. Or—

Or the third possibility, which Sebastian declined to examine closely, the way one declines to examine a noise in the night: that the seal had never really gone anywhere, and neither, in some sense that made his teeth ache, had the hand that used it.

"Dead men don't notarize documents," he told the study, firmly. The study, being lined with law reports going back to Victoria, declined to argue.

He looked at the letter again. *You will bring your seal.* His own commission as notary, inherited along with the practice, hung framed above the filing cabinets like a modest knighthood. Whoever wrote this knew the shape of his working life down to the instruments of it. Knew that he would recognize the Fenwick mark instantly. Knew, moreover, that he would come.

That was the part that settled over him like the drizzle: the certainty of it. *You will come alone.* Not *please attend*, not the soft courtesies of instruction letters. An imperative, issued with the calm of someone holding all the cards and shuffling them slowly, for effect.

Sebastian had spent his whole professional life being the man people trusted with their secrets, and he had discovered long ago that such men become invisible. People confessed around him as though he were furniture. He had heard confessions of adultery, embezzlement, one memorable case of bigamy conducted simultaneously in two parishes, and once, from old Mrs. Pettifer, an admission that she had poisoned her husband's roses out of spite and would do it again. None of it ever went further than the filing cabinet. That was the service. That was the price. You became the town's memory and lost your own voice somewhere in the stacks.

And now something in those stacks had written him a letter.

* * *

Thursday evening, the phone rang. Sebastian answered on the second ring, as always.

"Whitlock." Judge Harrow's voice, unhurried, gravelled pleasantly by forty years of sentencing. "I'm told you've had an interesting letter."

Sebastian's pen stopped mid-stroke. He had been drafting a will for the Hendersons, a cheerful couple leaving everything to each other and then to cats, and the contrast between that document and the conversation now arriving in his study was almost comic.

"I receive a great deal of correspondence, Your Honour."

"A cream envelope." A pause, delicately weighted. "Regarding Crane."

The fire popped in the grate. Somewhere outside, a moorhen complained at the dark.

"I couldn't discuss a client's affairs, Judge. You know that better than anyone."

"Of course, of course." The voice smiled; you could hear it, the way you can hear a hand smooth a tablecloth. "I only wanted to say—man to man—that whatever you find out at that house, some things are best handled quietly. The town has been at peace a long time, Sebastian. Peace is worth more than paper. Your mentor understood that. I'd hoped he'd taught you."

"He taught me to file things properly," Sebastian said. "Alphabetically."

Harrow laughed, a short bark with nothing in it. "Friday, then. Low tide. Dress warm—the east wind off the channels gets into the bones." And he rang off, having conveyed, in ninety seconds, that he knew about the letter, disapproved of it, expected compliance, and considered all of this perfectly natural.

Sebastian sat for a long time afterward, listening to the tick of the carriage clock and the whisper of the fen wind testing his window latches. Then he rose, took down the tin box from the top shelf—Fenwick's old tobacco tin, repurposed, containing the practice seals, the spare keys, the small metal furniture of authority—and laid out his notary seal beside the letter, side by side under the lamp, the living instrument and the ghost of one.

Two seals. Two men. One of them dead beyond all appeal.

"You will bring your seal," he murmured. "Whose, I wonder, did they mean?"

He slept, eventually, badly, and dreamed of reed beds moving in a wind that touched nothing else, and woke on Friday morning to a sky the colour of dishwater and a decision already made, the way decisions in Fenny Stowbridge were always already made—you just hadn't been informed yet.

He packed his bag: the letter, the thin file, a torch, a flask of tea, and, after a moment's consideration, the bone-handled letter knife, which was absurd, and which he packed anyway.

At the door he paused, keys in hand, and looked back at his hall—ordered, dusted, alphabetized, safe—and felt the peculiar sensation of a man stepping off a map.

"Low tide," he said to the empty house. "Apparently we do this at low tide."

The house said nothing. The fens, three miles east, were already waiting.
