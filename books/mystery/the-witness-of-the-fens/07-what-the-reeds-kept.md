## 7. What the Reeds Kept

The diary gave me coordinates the way a dying man gives directions: reluctantly, and only in fragments.

I had spent most of the night at my office with the thing open under two lamps, cross-referencing Edith Crane's final entries against the Ordnance Survey of the fen drains that I kept—God help me—in a folder labeled *Waterways, Historical (Obsolete)*. This is the sort of folder a man accumulates when he has spent twenty years filing a town's secrets instead of living his own life. I have folders for everything. *Boundary Disputes, Ecclesiastical.* *Grudge Holdings, Miscellaneous.* Once, in a moment of self-awareness I have never repeated, I started a folder called *Sebastian, Personal* and it contained, after four years, a single receipt for a pair of gloves.

The coordinates were given as distances from landmarks that no longer existed. "Three hundred paces south of where the chapel bell used to hang," she wrote. "Where the water goes when they take it away." The chapel bell had been removed in 1961. The water went wherever the Drainage Board told it to go, which was nowhere, on principle.

But Aldous Fenwick had taught me to read old maps the way other men read faces, and by three in the morning I had triangulated her wandering prose down to a stretch of the old Sedgefleet Channel—a drain cut through the peat in 1890, abandoned in 1974, and left to silt into a brown ribbon of nothing between two fields that belonged, according to my own files, to Judge Harrow.

Of course they did.

* * *

The morning was the kind the Fens do best: flat grey light lying on flat grey land, with a wind coming off the washes that found every gap in a man's coat and introduced itself personally. I parked the car on the drover's road, put on boots I hadn't worn since a client's boundary dispute in 2011, and set out along the top of the channel bank with the diary in a plastic bag inside my coat, because some part of me already understood that this book was the most dangerous object in the county.

The reeds had taken the channel back. They stood ten feet high on either side of the dry bed, gold-grey and whispering, and walking between them felt like walking down a corridor in a house where everyone is listening but no one will answer the door. My breath made small clouds. Somewhere ahead a moorhen clattered out of cover like a door slamming.

"Three hundred paces south of where the bell used to hang," I said aloud, just to hear a voice, even mine. The reeds took it and gave nothing back.

Edith's last entries had been getting stranger—not madder, exactly, but more compressed, as though whatever she was writing toward had gotten too large for the page. The entry for what should have been the day after her disappearance read, in full:

*"They watch from the reeds. Not the men—the watching itself. It has learned to stand still."*

I had read that sentence perhaps forty times. In daylight, with mud on my boots, it read differently. Less like madness. More like a woman describing something she could see and I could not yet.

At roughly the three-hundredth pace—I counted, because counting is what I do instead of praying—the channel bed changed. The silt gave way to older ground: brick rubble, the corner of a culvert, and then, half-swallowed by a tussock of rush, a shape that stopped me where I stood.

A shoe. A child's shoe, leather gone stiff and the colour of weak tea, one lace still knotted. Small. The size of shoe a boy of eight or nine might wear, might have worn, might have been wearing on a night in October twelve years ago when he walked out of the world and into a folder marked *Missing Persons, Unresolved*, and then out of that folder too, eventually, because folders are only ever as honest as the men who keep them.

I did not touch it. That surprised me. Twenty years of estate law had taught me that evidence is a thing you bag and label, but standing over that small knotted lace I understood that I was not here as a lawyer. I crouched in the mud like a man at a graveside that had no stone.

"I'm sorry," I said to the shoe. It seemed the correct audience.

Then, because dread is a poor companion but an excellent employee, I got to work.

* * *

*They tell me I am Edith. The woman with the kind hands says it every morning, Edith, good morning, Edith, and I say it back like a lesson. Edith. It fits the way a borrowed coat fits—warm, but the pockets are all wrong. I keep reaching for things that aren't there.*

*I remember water. I remember that everyone was happy and then everyone was quiet, and quiet after happiness is the loudest sound there is.*

*I wrote in this book before. I know because the handwriting is mine. But the girl who wrote those pages knew why she was hiding, and I only know that I am hiding. There is a difference and the difference has teeth.*

*The kind one says: write what you can. Even the pieces. Pieces are how the light gets in.*

*I don't trust that saying. It sounds like something a clever person tells a broken one so the broken one will hold still.*

*But I am holding still. And I am writing. And somewhere under the borrowed coat, the pockets are filling up.*

* * *

The bracelet turned up forty minutes later, and it nearly didn't turn up at all.

I had widened my search in slow arcs from the shoe, parting the reed stubble with a walking stick, working the way Aldous taught me to work a document—systematically, without hope, because hope makes you skip lines. The burnt hospital bracelet was caught in the root mat of a rush clump, fused into a curl of blackened plastic. Most of it had melted, but the printed band survived in patches, and I held it up to the flat white sky and read what was left:

*...ST ELMO'S PAEDIATRIC... CRANE, T... DOB .../.../1998...*

Crane. Not Edith Crane. A child with her surname, born in 1998, treated at St Elmo's—the cottage hospital in Marsh Deep that had closed the same year Edith disappeared, its records shipped off to a regional archive and, if my memory of the transfer paperwork served—and it did serve; my memory always serves, it simply chooses its moments—*if my memory served*, the records had been flagged for destruction after seven years.

Seven years. The destruction order would have come due five years ago. Had anyone checked? Had anyone signed?

I had signed. I remembered it now with a horror that arrived not like a wave but like a bill: the routine certification of obsolete medical records, forty-one boxes, my signature at the bottom assuring the archive that the contents were valueless. I had certified the erasure of a child named Crane and driven home and complained about the traffic.

I sat down on the culvert bricks, because my legs had opinions of their own, and let the wind explain things to me for a while.

Here is what I knew, laid out in the order I refused to lay it out in: Edith Crane vanished in October, twelve years ago. Three weeks later—or so the dates claimed—her diary continued in another hand. A child's shoe sat in a drain on Harrow land. A burnt hospital bracelet bearing the name Crane lay beside it, burned the way things get burned in a grate or a brazier, not the way things get burned by accident. And the town I had served faithfully for twenty years had a Founders' Dinner coming up in nine days, at which three men would raise their glasses to their ancestors while I stood by the wall being invisible, which was, I had long ago concluded, my function.

The diary said someone watched her from the reeds. The reeds, it now appeared, had been watching back for twelve years, keeping what the town would not.

I got up. My knees cracked in stereo. Somewhere out over the washes a curlew began its bubbling cry, that sound the Fen people call the whaap, the one that means, depending on your mood, either *spring is coming* or *you are alone forever*. I chose spring.

"Right," I told the channel, the shoe, the bracelet, the listening reeds. "Right."

It is not much of a battle cry. But I am a solicitor. We work with what we're given.

* * *

*The kind one came tonight with a candle and asked me if I remembered the dinner. I said: everyone was happy. The kind one said: go on. I said: and then the boy wasn't there anymore, and everyone stayed happy anyway, and that was the wrong part. That was the part with teeth.*

*The kind one cried. I didn't know a grown person could cry that quietly. It sounded like rain on the far side of a wall.*

*I don't know whose boy he was. I know his shoes. I know they were good shoes, new, the leather still stiff, the kind a mother buys for a day she wants to go well.*

*The kind one says soon. Says the house is safe, says the judge thinks—*

*There the candle guttered and the kind one blew it out fast, faster than the draft needed, and I heard them go away down the corridor counting their own footsteps, which is a thing people do when they are afraid of being followed by themselves.*

*The judge thinks what?*

*I will ask tomorrow. Tomorrow I will be brave. Tonight I will write the pockets full.*

* * *

I photographed everything—the shoe in situ, the bracelet, the culvert, the channel looking north and south—with the grim competence of a man building a case he hasn't yet admitted to himself he's building. Then I wrapped the bracelet in a handkerchief and, after a long argument with myself that I lost, took it. The shoe I left where it lay. Some things belong to the place that kept them safe; taking the shoe felt like robbing a grave, and besides, I had a sudden superstitious certainty that I would need to bring witnesses here, proper witnesses, people whose presence couldn't be explained away, and the shoe would need to be found by eyes other than mine.

The wind had risen while I worked. The reeds bent and hissed in long combers, whole fields of them bowing eastward and rising again, and standing there among them I thought: *she stood here.* Edith Crane, or the woman who had been Edith Crane, had stood in this drained corridor of nothing with the watching all around her, and had run, and had been hidden, and the town had filed her under *Gone* and moved on to next year's dinner.

And Aldous. God, Aldous. His hand in her diary, three weeks past the end. Either he had written those pages to fake her survival—which meant he'd known she was dead, known where, known how—or he had written them because she was alive and couldn't write, and he was keeping the record going until—

Until what? Until he died, which he had done, eight years ago, comfortably, in his chair, with a glass of the Judge's whisky in his hand and me across the desk learning how to file a town's sins alphabetically. He had never said a word. He had left me a practice, a set of principles, and a notary seal, and not one syllable of warning that the foundation under all of it was a drowned boy and a buried woman.

Unless the warning was the diary itself. Unless leaving it in the safe of Marsh House, behind a combination I realized with a cold start I had recognized instantly—Aldous's birthday backwards, the same code he'd used on his office safe for thirty years—was the longest letter he ever wrote me.

"You absolute old devil," I said to the reeds, and meant it fondly, which made it worse.

I walked back along the bank with the bracelet in my pocket pressing against my hip like a small hot coal. At the car I paused and looked back at the channel: a brown line through the gold reeds, going nowhere, holding everything.

Nine days to the dinner. Three men at the head table. One shoe in the mud.

And between me and all of it, one housekeeper who had looked me in the eye yesterday and told me, with the serene confidence of a woman reading from a script she'd rehearsed for twelve years, that Edith Crane had never existed beyond a missing-person file.

Mrs. Tallow had lied to me. That was interesting. What was more interesting was *how* she had lied—fluently, thoroughly, with the polish of long practice—but with one flaw, one hairline crack that I had missed yesterday and heard ringing in my ears the whole drive home: she had told me the east wing was closed for damp. Closed for damp. In a house where the rest of the windows stood open to rot, that one wing shut tight, that one staircase swept.

Nobody sweeps a staircase for damp.

I started the engine. Whatever Mrs. Tallow's first story had cost her to tell, I had a strong feeling her second story was going to cost considerably more—and that she had been waiting twelve years for someone rude enough to ask for it.
