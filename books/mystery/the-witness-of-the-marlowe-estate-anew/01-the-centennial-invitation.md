## 1. The Centennial Invitation

The fog came off the water in slow gray sheets, and Edmund Vasquez drove through it with one hand on the wheel and the other on her recorder, narrating the way she always did when a place first opened itself to her.

"October fourteenth. I'm twenty miles outside Marlowe's Crossing, and the GPS has given up twice. The road narrows after the last gas station—the last anything, really—and the trees close in like they're passing me hand to hand. If you're hearing this, it means the podcast survived another season. It means somebody out there still wants to know what happened to Abigail Marlowe."

She paused the recording at the crest of the hill where the town announced itself: a scatter of slate roofs and brick chimneys below, a white church steeple rising through the mist, and beyond it all the long dark line of the orchards that had made the Marlowe family rich a hundred years before anyone alive could remember.

One hundred years ago this week, Abigail Marlowe had died. That was the official version, anyway. Heart failure, aged thirty-four, at the height of her powers—the woman who had founded the town, endowed its school, planted its famous orchards, and then, with almost insulting neatness, died just as the town's centennial preparations began. Her funeral had been the largest gathering in county history. Her grave had been tended every single day since by a rotation of volunteers who called themselves the Keepers, though nobody Edmund had interviewed by phone could quite explain what they were keeping.

Now, for the first time in a century, the coffin was coming up.

Edmund had pitched the story eleven times before anyone bit. *The Witness of the Marlowe Estate* was what she'd renamed the podcast after her old feed collapsed in a rights dispute—a name that felt right even then, before she knew why. She had forty thousand listeners, down from two hundred thousand, a savings account down from comfortable to careful, and a producer named Dana who had said, with the gentleness people use for bad news, *"Maybe it's time to think about something else, Ed."*

Then the town of Marlowe's Crossing sent her an invitation. Not an email—an actual letter, heavy cream stock, embossed with an apple blossom. *The Marlowe Estate Centennial Committee requests the honor of your presence.* They'd read her back catalog. They'd heard her episode on unsolved deaths of prominent women. They wanted her there, they said, to witness history.

Nobody invites a true-crime podcaster to their founder's exhumation unless they want something. Edmund knew that. She'd spent six years learning how often what people wanted was control of the story.

She also knew she had no choice but to say yes.

* * *

The town materialized out of the fog all at once, the way places do when you've been dreading them. Main Street was brick-paved and lamplit even at noon, the lamps Victorian reproductions with glass the color of weak tea. Every storefront looked occupied and prosperous: a bookstore, a chandlery that hadn't been necessary for fifty years but sold candles anyway, a bakery with apple fritters cooling in the window. Bunting hung from the second-story cornices in deep green and cream. A banner stretched across the street between two buildings:

**MARLOWE'S CROSSING — 100 YEARS GRATEFUL**

*Grateful*, Edmund thought. Not proud. Grateful. Like the town owed something. Like it was still paying a debt.

She parked in front of the Harrow Inn, the only lodging the committee's letter had mentioned, and before she'd gotten her bag out of the trunk, a woman was crossing the street toward her at a pace just short of running.

"You must be Ms. Vasquez." The woman was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, wearing a wool coat the color of moss and a smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of her face seemed to decide about it. "I'm Cordelia Ash. Centennial Committee. We've been watching for your car since ten o'clock."

"Watching for—" Edmund shifted the bag on her shoulder. "That's a long watch. My ETA kept changing with the fog."

"We didn't mind." Cordelia Ash's eyes were pale gray and very steady. "We don't mind waiting for things that matter. Here, let me take that—you'll want the corner room, third floor. Best light. We remembered you do your editing in daylight."

Edmund had never told anyone on the committee that she edited in daylight. She'd mentioned it once, three years ago, in an episode about her own process, buried in minute thirty-eight of a conversation about archival research.

"That's thoughtful," she said, and heard her own voice do the thing it did when her instincts lit up: go smooth, go warm, give nothing away. Six years of interviewing people who might be lying had taught her that the best camouflage was friendliness.

"We're so glad you came." Cordelia touched her elbow, lightly, a gesture that was either grandmotherly or proprietary. "This is going to be a wonderful week. You'll see. Everything here works, when you let it."

*When you let it.* Edmund filed the phrase away and followed her into the inn.

* * *

The Harrow Inn smelled of woodsmoke and apples, which Edmund would learn was simply the smell of Marlowe's Crossing; it got into everything, her coat, her hair, the pages of her notebook. The innkeeper, a broad cheerful man named Hollis, refused her credit card. "Committee's covered you," he said. "Room, board, the lot. You're doing the town an honor being here."

"I'm doing my job," Edmund said. "You're paying me in fritters."

Hollis laughed too loudly, and Cordelia didn't laugh at all.

By evening, Edmund had counted the ways the welcome unsettled her, because counting was how she kept fear useful instead of paralyzing:

One. Everyone knew her name. Not just Cordelia and Hollis—the teenager stocking shelves at the general store nodded at her like they'd met. An old man sweeping the church steps said, "Evening, Ms. Vasquez," without looking up.

Two. Nobody asked her questions. In every other town she'd ever reported from, she'd been an object of suspicion or curiosity or both. Here, she was received like weather—expected, accommodated, not especially interesting. As if her arrival were less an event than a fulfillment.

Three. When she'd asked Hollis, casually, over the pot pie he insisted on feeding her, what the townsfolk expected to find in the coffin, his ladle had stopped moving for exactly one second. One second, no more. Then: "Whatever the good Lord left us." And he'd changed the subject to the quality of the pastry.

Four—and this was the one she kept circling back to, lying on the third-floor bed with the good light gone blue outside the window—everyone was kind to her in the same way. Identical warmth, identical phrasing. *So glad you came. Wonderful week ahead. You'll see how we do things here.* Three dozen conversations, one script. She'd interviewed cults before. She knew what shared language sounded like when it wasn't accidental.

She got up, set her recorder on the desk, and pressed record.

"Day one notes," she said quietly. "Marlowe's Crossing is the most welcoming place I have ever been, and I cannot shake the feeling that the welcome is load-bearing. Like a hand on your shoulder that would become a grip if you turned the wrong way. Tomorrow morning, nine a.m., they open the grave. A hundred years of secrecy, and they invited a stranger with a microphone to watch. Either this town has nothing to hide—"

She stopped. Outside, on the empty street below her window, someone was standing under the tea-colored lamp, looking up at the inn. At her window specifically, she thought, though the fog made certainty impossible. A man in a long coat, motionless, hands folded in front of him like a man waiting for a service to begin.

"—or it has something it wants managed," she finished, and reached for the camera.

By the time she got the lens focused, the street was empty. No footsteps. No door closing anywhere along the block. Just the fog folding closed behind the space where he'd stood, the way water closes over a stone.

* * *

Morning came bright and cold, the fog burned off to reveal hills stitched with orchard rows, trees bent and gnarled with age, most of them still bearing fruit no one seemed to harvest. Edmund walked to the churchyard early, wanting to see the grave before the crowd, before the cameras, before whatever ceremony the committee had planned.

The Marlowe plot sat apart from the other graves on a low rise, ringed by a wrought-iron fence painted black and maintained so well it might have been installed last year. Inside: one headstone. Granite, modest, worn soft at the edges.

**ABIGAIL MARLOWE**
**FOUNDER**
**1890 – 1924**
***SHE PLANTED WHAT ENDURES***

Below the fence, fresh-cut grass, dew still on it. And flowers—dozens of them, arranged with a precision that made Edmund's skin prickle. White chrysanthemums in perfect concentric rings around the stone, each bloom facing outward, each stem cut to the same length.

A hundred years of daily tending. She'd thought it was a figure of speech.

"They're beautiful, aren't them."

Edmund turned. A young man stood at the gate—mid-twenties, wire-rimmed glasses, a canvas satchel slung across his chest, holding two cups of coffee. He had the apologetic posture of someone who'd rehearsed an introduction and was already regretting it.

"Sorry—that was creepy, appearing out of nowhere. Theo Brandt. Historical society. Well, assistant archivist, which means I'm the entire staff under Dr.—under Silas Wren." He offered one of the coffees. "You're Edmund Vasquez. Obviously. I've listened to every episode. Twice. The Larkspur Hollow series changed how I think about local history, honestly, the way you treat records as testimony rather than fact—"

"Theo." She took the coffee. It was exactly how she took it. She decided not to ask how he knew. "How many people are on the tending rotation?"

His smile flickered. "Rotation?"

"For the flowers. Someone cuts these stems to match. Someone trains new Keepers. That's infrastructure, not devotion."

Theo looked at the grave for a long moment. When he answered, his voice had dropped into something more careful. "There's a list. Families take weeks in turn. Ash, Wren, Harrow, Vane, Brandt." He tapped his own chest. "My grandfather did it until he couldn't kneel anymore. Now I do it. Everybody does it, eventually. It's just—what you do here."

"And nobody thinks that's strange? Tending the grave of a woman who's been dead a century, every single day, like she might notice?"

Theo pushed his glasses up. Behind the lenses, his eyes were quick and unhappy, and Edmund recognized the look: it was the look of someone standing next to a locked door they'd never been allowed to open, telling themselves they didn't want to.

"Can I tell you something off the record?" he said.

"Everything's off the record until I press record. That's the deal."

He glanced back toward the church, where cars were beginning to gather, where men in dark coats were unloading something long and padded from a truck—the exhumation equipment, arriving with the gentleness of pallbearers.

"When I was nine," Theo said, "I asked my grandfather why we tend the grave if she's not in it."

The fog had come back off the water, thin and low, curling between the headstones. Somewhere across the churchyard, a door banged, voices rising, the town assembling for its ceremony.

"What did he say?" Edmund asked.

"He said, 'Because she can hear us.'" Theo gave a small, strained laugh that wasn't a laugh at all. "And then he made me promise never to say that sentence out loud again. So. You know. Welcome to Marlowe's Crossing, Ms. Vasquez. Nine o'clock. Try to stand near the front—they'll want you to see everything."

He hurried off toward the church, coffee sloshing, and Edmund stood alone inside the iron fence with her recorder running silently in her pocket, capturing the wind in the orchards and the creak of the old gate and her own heartbeat, which had picked up the way it always did—not with fear, exactly, but with the animal recognition that the hunt had begun.

*SHE PLANTED WHAT ENDURES.*

In ninety minutes, they would raise the coffin. And Abigail Marlowe—whoever she had been, wherever she actually was—was about to hear from her at last.
