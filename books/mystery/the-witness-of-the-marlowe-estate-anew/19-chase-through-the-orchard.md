## 19. Chase Through the Orchard

I heard the truck before I saw it—a diesel rattle tearing up Marlowe Hill Road at an hour when nothing in this town had moved faster than a porch swing in a hundred years.

I was still at Cordelia's kitchen table, the ledger page spread between us under a single lamp, when the sound came. Cordelia's head snapped toward the window like a bird dog's.

"That's Silas's truck," she said. "He never drives after dark. He says the fog plays tricks on old men."

"Where does that road go?"

"Past the chapel. Past the Ash property." She was already standing, already reaching for her coat. "And then it dead-ends at the orchard."

The orchard. Abigail's orchard. The place where, according to the diary we'd spent three nights reconstructing, a woman with a bruised face and a borrowed traveling coat had slipped out between the trees while her husband slept, and begun the hundred-year lie that held this town together like mortar.

"He's going to burn it," I said. "Whatever's left. The diary pages he didn't get, the ledger originals—everything."

"Edmund." Cordelia caught my arm at the door. Her grip was stronger than a woman her age had any right to. "The deputies are twenty minutes out. Theo called them from the diner."

"Twenty minutes is a long time to burn paper."

I didn't wait for her answer. I took my recorder off the table, checked the battery the way I'd checked it before every interview for six years, and ran for my car.

* * *

The gate to the Marlowe orchard was older than anything else in town except the story itself—wrought iron gone soft with rust, the M of the family monogram half-eaten away. It stood open. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the smell: kerosene, faint but unmistakable, threading through the cold apple-and-rot sweetness of November trees.

I left my car at the gate with the hazards on and went in on foot. My breath came out in white plumes. The frost had come hard two weeks ago, killing what the harvest hadn't taken, and the rows of trees stood black and bare against a sky the color of dirty wool. No moon. Just the fog pressing down from the ridge, thickening as I walked deeper, until each row became a corridor with no visible end.

I hit record.

"Eleven forty-two p.m., November fourteenth," I whispered. "Marlowe orchard. Following Silas Wren on foot. If something happens to me, this file is backed up in three places and Theo Brandt knows where all of them are."

It wasn't entirely true. It was true enough.

Ahead, through the trunks, I saw light—not firelight yet, just a flashlight beam swinging low across the ground, moving with purpose. He knew exactly where he was going. A man searching would sweep the beam wide. Silas Wren was walking a line as straight as if the trees themselves had shown him the way.

I followed one row over, parallel, keeping the trunks between us. The ground crunched with frozen fallen apples, and twice I froze when he froze, crouching in the dark with my heart slamming against my ribs, waiting to see if the beam would swing my way.

On the third freeze, I understood why he'd stopped. He wasn't listening. He was reading.

The flashlight was pointed down at something in his hands, and even from thirty feet I could see the shape of it—a flat tin box, the kind farm families used for seed receipts and land deeds. He'd known where it was buried. Of course he had. His family had been burying things in this orchard since 1924.

"Silas," I said.

My voice came out steadier than I felt. The beam snapped up, found me, pinned me like a specimen.

"Miss Vasquez." He didn't sound surprised. That was the worst part. He sounded tired. "You should have stayed at Cordelia's table. She makes good coffee. Better company than this."

"What's in the box?"

"You know what's in the box. You've been knowing for days." He set it down on a tree stump without hurrying, and unscrewed the cap of the kerosene can with hands that did not shake. "You've been knowing since the diary. Since the ledger page. You know everything except the only thing that matters."

"Which is?"

"Why." He looked at me across the dead rows, and in the spill of his own flashlight his face was every year of its ninety-one. "Why a town would choose a lie for a hundred years and call it love. You've written your little episodes about it. You've said the word *myth* like it's a disease. But you've never once asked what the truth would have cost them."

"It cost Daniel Reyes his life," I said. "It cost Cordelia's brother. It cost—" I stopped myself before Theo's name, because saying it out loud in this cold made it too real. "Every person who ever asked a question here paid for it. Don't stand in a graveyard of apple trees and tell me about cost."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not anger. Grief, maybe, or its well-worn impersonation.

"Daniel Reyes was a tragedy," Silas said quietly. "Walter Ash was a mistake my father never forgave himself for. And you will put their names in your mouth because they're useful to you. But Abigail—" his voice cracked open, just slightly, like ice on a pond, "Abigail is the only reason any of us existed at all. She built this town out of nothing but her own survival. The least we owed her was silence."

"She owed herself the truth," I said. "She wrote it down. She hid it in her own coffin. She spent fifty years in that second grave waiting for someone to dig her up and let her say it. You didn't protect her, Silas. You gagged her for a century."

For a long moment there was no sound but wind moving through bare branches, a sound like the whole orchard whispering at once.

Then he picked up the kerosene can.

* * *

I don't remember deciding to run. I remember the tin box leaving the stump, and then I was moving, crashing through the row, shouting his name, and the world became motion and frost and the white slash of his beam swinging toward me.

He was ninety-one years old and he was faster than he had any right to be, or maybe I was slower than I'd ever been, lungs full of freezing air, boots slipping on frozen apples, the fog swallowing everything past ten feet. I lost him between the fourth and fifth rows. I lost him completely. And then I heard the liquid hitting wood—the particular hollow drumbeat of kerosene poured onto dry old timber—and I understood he wasn't running from me at all.

He was making a perimeter.

"Silas!" I turned in a circle, beam-less, blind, my own recorder clutched against my chest like a child. "You pour that and you prove every word of my series. Arson on top of everything. Is that the legacy? Is that how you shepherd them?"

His voice came from somewhere to my left, unhurried, almost gentle. "You think I care what your series proves? Your series dies tonight with everything else. The diary copies, the ledger photographs, the recordings—all of it lives in boxes and basements and one very clever boy's hard drive, I know. But paper burns, Miss Vasquez. Memory burns better. In ten years no one under sixty will believe you. I've watched it happen before. In 1987, Walter Ash had proof too."

"And you killed him for it."

A pause. Long enough that I thought he might deny it, might give me the clean version, the shepherd's version.

"I was thirty-four years old," Silas said, "and my father handed me the matches and told me it was my turn now. Do you understand? It doesn't begin with a decision. It begins with an inheritance."

I pressed record harder against my palm, as if pressure could make the tape hold more. "Say that again. Say it clearly. For the file."

"Oh, I know it's recording." Almost a laugh. "I've known since you got out of your car. I let you follow me, child. Why do you think I drove with my lights on?"

The cold went through me then, colder than the night, colder than the fog. Every step of this had been measured. The truck at eleven o'clock at night, loud enough to wake Cordelia's street. The gate standing open. The straight line through the rows.

"You wanted me here," I said.

"I wanted a witness." The beam appeared again, far down the row, sweeping slowly, deliberately, so I could track him. "That's all a historian ever wants. Someone to see the last of it before it goes. You came here to take our story apart, and instead you're going to watch me give it back to the dark, properly, the way it should have been given a hundred years ago. There's a difference between hiding a thing and laying it to rest. I'm doing the second one. You'll understand someday, when you're old, when you're the one holding the matches."

"I won't," I said. "That's the whole point of me."

I started walking toward his voice. It was the stupidest thing I have ever done and the only thing I have ever been certain of. Because he was right about one thing: someone had to witness it. But not the way he meant. Not the lie laid gently back into the ground. The whole rotten beautiful truth, on tape, in my voice, in his.

"Stay back," he said, and now there was steel in it, the first crack in the gentleness. "I mean it, girl. This ends tonight either way. With the fire or without it. The only question is whether you leave this orchard."

"The deputies are coming," I said. "Theo called them. Cordelia called them. Half the town under forty is awake right now, Silas, and none of them ever took your oath. You're not guarding a secret anymore. You're guarding a corpse that everyone's already seen."

The pouring stopped.

In the sudden silence I heard it—the small metallic click of a lighter's wheel, once, twice, catching on the third try. A bloom of orange light rose at the end of the row, painting the bare branches gold from below, and for one suspended second the whole orchard looked the way it must have looked in 1924: alive, lit from within, holding its breath.

Then the flame caught the kerosene and ran.

* * *

Fire moves through a dead orchard fast. I learned that in the worst possible classroom. The flames went up the trunk of the nearest tree like a hand closing, leapt the gap to the next, and suddenly there was heat on my face and a roar in my ears and the fog above turning orange, and Silas Wren was standing inside it.

Not fleeing it. Standing in it, at the edge of the burning row, the tin box nowhere in sight—already fed to the flames, I realized, already ash—and his arms hanging at his sides like a man at a graveside.

"SILAS!" I was screaming now, coughing, eyes streaming. "Get out! Get OUT!"

He turned his head and looked at me through the fire, and I swear to God, I swear on every episode I've ever published, he smiled. Not triumph. Relief. A man setting down a weight he'd carried since he was thirty-four years old.

"My grandmother used to bring me here," he called, over the roar. "Told me this was where it all began. Told me the trees remembered. I used to think she was being poetic." He shook his head slowly. "They do remember. That's the trouble with this town, Miss Vasquez. Everything remembers. We were the only ones who forgot how to listen."

The row between us was fully alight now, a wall of it, and the heat drove me back three steps, five, my heel catching on a root and sending me down hard into the frozen mud. Through the flames I watched him turn and walk away—not running, walking, upright, into the next row, into the fog, a silhouette dissolving like ink in water.

I got up. I don't know how. My palms were bleeding and my throat was raw and somewhere behind me the fire was eating a hundred years of evidence, and I got up and I ran—not after him, not at first. Toward the edge of the blaze, where the rows were still dark, where the frost still held.

Because I'd seen something. In the second before the fire took the row, when the light flared highest—I'd seen the ground beside the stump. Seen the shallow depression, the disturbed earth, the corner of something pale showing where the tin box had sat.

He'd burned the box.

But the box had been sitting on the stump, and stumps don't hold paper, and Silas Wren was a historian to his marrow. Historians make copies. Historians leave the original where the founder left it, because to a man like Silas, the original was sacred—even the original he meant to destroy. Especially it.

I dug with my bare hands in the frozen ground at the base of the stump, mud packing under my nails, blood from my palms mixing with the dirt, the fire roaring hot against my back, and my fingers struck metal. A smaller tin. A tobacco tin, rusted shut, wedged deep beneath the roots where a woman's hands had pushed it in 1924 and a man's hands had failed to find it tonight because grief had made him careless, or maybe—maybe—because some part of him had needed it to survive.

I wrenched it free, shoved it inside my coat against my chest, and ran for the gate as the first sirens finally, finally crested Marlowe Hill Road.

Behind me the orchard burned, row after row lighting up like candles, and ahead of me the fog was full of red and blue light, and against my heart the little tin rattled with whatever Abigail Marlowe had considered worth burying twice.

At the gate, headlights swung toward me. Cordelia's car. Theo leaning out the passenger window, face gray with terror, shouting my name.

I opened the tin right there in the road, in the smoke, with the fire painting us all orange.

Inside, folded around a wedding ring I recognized from a photograph of a second grave, was a single sheet of paper in a hand I now knew better than my own—and at the bottom, in fresh ink, in handwriting that was not hers, someone had added a date.

Tonight's date.

Silas had read it before he burned the rest. And he'd left it for me on purpose.
