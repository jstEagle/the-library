## 7. The Bookshop Trap

The realization came on a Tuesday, in the hour between the last customer and the closing of the shutters, when the light through Salt & Page's front window went the color of weak tea and Theo Fairweather was doing what Theo Fairweather did every evening of their retirement: reading.

Not for pleasure. Never that anymore. They read the way a man probes a sore tooth— compulsively, wincingly—rereading the first letter for the hundredth time since Bram Ottery's body had been pulled from his own garden pond. The letter lay flat on the counter beneath the brass lamp, its cream paper gone soft at the folds from handling, and Theo had spread beside it three pages of their own handwriting: fragments of the 2003 investigation reconstructed from memory, set down over two sleepless nights in the flat above the shop.

They had been comparing the two documents the way one compares tide charts to wreckage, looking for the pattern beneath the surface noise. And there it was. It had been there all along, staring up at them with the patient insolence of a thing that knows it will eventually be found.

*The deceased's left shoe was recovered some four yards seaward of the body, suggesting post-mortem displacement by tidal action.*

Theo read the sentence again. Then again. Then they set down the letter very carefully, as though it might bite, and picked up their own reconstruction, and found the phrase they had written from twenty-year-old memory:

*Left shoe recovered approx. four yards seaward — tidal displacement, post-mortem.*

Four yards seaward. *Post-mortem displacement.* Not the sort of language an ordinary person used. Not even the sort of language most policemen used off the page. It was report-language, note-language, the compressed bureaucratic poetry of a scene-of-crime write-up—and the letter hadn't paraphrased it. The letter had *quoted* it. Word for word. Order for order.

Theo stood very still in the failing light while the rain worked at the window like something trying to get in.

Because here was the thing, the thing that turned the blood cold and then hot: those words had never appeared in any newspaper. They had never been spoken in any courtroom. Ada Quill's transcripts—the public record, such as it was—contained only the sanitized summary read aloud at the inquest, and the inquest had returned its tidy verdict of misadventure before the tide tables were even consulted. The detail of the shoe, the precise distance, the forensic inference—it lived in exactly one place.

The case notes. The file that no longer existed. The file that had been sealed, and then quietly destroyed, on the signature of a superior whose name Theo still could not make themselves say aloud.

Which meant the person who wrote this letter had not merely known about the investigation. They had *read the notes*. Had sat across a desk from them or stood behind a shoulder, had absorbed the dry music of official prose well enough to reproduce it decades later from memory. And there were perhaps six people alive who had ever seen those pages. Theo was one. Halloway was another. Bram Ottery had been a third—

Bram Ottery, who was dead.

Theo's hand closed slowly around the edge of the counter until the knuckles ached. The shop around them seemed to contract: the shelves leaning in with their drowned-ship smell of salt and old glue, the bell above the door hanging silent, the whole small kingdom of secondhand books suddenly feeling less like a refuge than like a room with one exit.

*Someone has been reading over my shoulder for twenty years.*

Or someone had been reading the notes themselves. Either way, the conclusion was the same, and it arrived with the clean, terrible clarity of a wave finally finding the crack in a sea wall: the letters were not confessions. They were *bait*. Each one a hook dressed in penitence, cast toward whoever still remembered enough to bite. And Theo—who had received the first, who had gone to the archive, who had walked the shoreline below the estate matching details against memory like a fool laying out their own itinerary—Theo had been biting beautifully.

* * *

They did not sleep that night. Instead, they did what they had done for thirty years as a detective and had almost forgotten how to do as a bookseller: they made a plan.

By lamplight, on the back of a 1978 Penguin edition of *The Moonstone* whose spine had already given up, Theo drew the shop. The front door with its bell. The narrow aisle of fiction. The counter, the lamp, the little back room where the returns piled up. Then they began, methodically, to turn a failing bookshop into an instrument.

A ledger came down from the office shelf—one of the old-fashioned ones, cloth-bound, meant for accounts Theo no longer bothered keeping. On its first page, in a hand steadier than they felt, they wrote: *Visitors.* Underneath, columns: date, time, name if known, description if not, what was browsed, what was said.

It was, they admitted to the empty shop, a thin trap. A net woven from observation and habit. But nets did not need to be strong; they needed only to be *patient*, and Theo had learned patience the hard way, in interview rooms and stakeout cars and twenty years of silence. Every person who crossed the threshold would go into the book. The postman. The woman from the bakery who liked naval histories. Children sheltering from rain. Especially anyone who asked questions. Anyone who lingered near the counter. Anyone whose eyes went, even briefly, to the pile of mail.

And because the letters came by post, Theo changed their relationship with the post. They took to arriving at the shop before dawn, before the van came, so that no envelope would sit unwatched in the slot. They noted the times of delivery to the minute. They handled each piece of mail with gloves now—not out of any hope of fingerprints after all this handling, but because the ritual reminded them they were hunting again, and being hunted, and that both required discipline.

On the fourth day, they added a second measure, one that felt more like surrender than strategy: they told Eleanor.

Not everything. Not yet. A telephone call to the estate, brief and businesslike, voice carefully level: *I think the letters are connected to the original notes. I think whoever is writing them had access to things only investigators saw. Until I know more, don't come to the shop alone, and don't answer any letter—don't touch it, don't burn this one, just bring it to me.*

There had been a pause on the line long enough for Theo to hear the sea behind her, somewhere far away at Marlowe House, and then Eleanor had said, quietly, "You sound like him again."

"Theo?"

"No." A breath. "The detective. I'd forgotten him. He had a voice like a locked door."

And then she had hung up, and Theo had stood holding the receiver, hearing *locked door* echo in the empty shop, understanding that she had not meant it as an insult—or had meant it as several things at once, which was worse, which was always how Eleanor spoke to them, layers within layers like the strata of the cliffs.

* * *

The bell rang on Saturday afternoon, in that dead hour when the rain had stopped but the sky hadn't decided anything, and Theo looked up from the ledger with their heart already going too fast—because they had learned, in five days of watching, the rhythm of their own doorway, and this entrance was wrong. Too slow. The bell had been allowed to finish its whole sentence before the door followed it.

Eleanor Marlowe stood in the frame with the grey light behind her, wearing a coat the color of wet slate and an expression arranged into casualness so carefully that it could only be a disguise.

"I'm destroying your ambush," she said, "by announcing myself. I assume it's an ambush. You've got that look."

"What look?"

"The one you used to get in the study at Marlowe House when you thought nobody was watching you work. Like the entire world was a crossword you intended to finish before dinner." She came in, shaking rain from her collar, and her eyes moved over the shop—the ledger, quickly, before Theo could angle it away; the gloves folded on the counter; the lamp burning in daylight. "I've come to buy a book," she said. "That's the pretext. I'm told one commits to a pretext fully or not at all."

"Who told you that?"

"You did. Nineteen years ago, the night we climbed out onto the roof of the orangery and you explained why you were going to lie to my mother about where we'd been." She smiled, and it cost her something; Theo saw the cost in the fine tension at the corners of her mouth. "You said a lie holds better if you build true rooms inside it."

Theo's chest did something complicated and unwelcome. Twenty years, and she kept the architecture of them filed away—every joist, every silly load-bearing sentence. "Did you find it?" they managed. "The book you came for?"

"I haven't looked yet." She drifted along the fiction shelf, trailing one gloved finger along the spines without reading them, and Theo understood that she wasn't browsing; she was giving them both somewhere to stand. "I burned mine, you know. My letter. Before I knew what it was. I stood in the grate in the blue drawing room and watched it go, and only afterward did I think—that might have been evidence. That I might have just helped destroy the only honest document in this entire miserable affair." Her finger stopped on a spine. "So I'm here under false pretenses, Detective, and we both know it, and I'd like to stop pretending before it chokes me. Tell me what you know."

So Theo told her. Not all of it—they held back the name they couldn't say, Halloway, held it behind their teeth like a stone—but the shape of it: the quoted phrasing, the case notes, the arithmetic of the living witnesses shrinking by one. Eleanor listened standing very still among the books, and when Theo finished she did not do what Theo expected, which was to go pale or go sharp. Instead she laughed, once, softly, horribly.

"Of course," she said. "Of course it's the *notes*. Vivienne used to complain about them—did I ever tell you? She said the police wrote about her death before it happened, all that dreadful tidy language, *the deceased*, *recovered*, *displacement*—" Her voice caught on the word and she mastered it. "She'd have hated this. Being a quotation."

"Eleanor." Theo came around the counter. Close, now—close enough to see that she was tired in a way that makeup couldn't reach, tired down to the bone, and that her hands in their gloves were trembling very slightly against the shelf. "Why did you really come?"

For a moment she didn't answer. The shop ticked and settled around them; somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked; outside, the gulls argued over something dead on the tideline. When she spoke, her voice had dropped its armor entirely, and it was the voice from twenty years ago, from the orangery roof, from the last night before duty had come between them like a drawn curtain.

"Because I can't be in that house alone anymore. Because every room remembers her, and the letter writer knows the house, knows the *family*, knows things—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because when you telephoned, and I heard that locked-door voice, I realized I'd spent twenty years waiting for you to use it. Isn't that absurd? All that anger, all that silence, and underneath it I was simply waiting for you to come back and be the one person who would take this seriously. Who would take *me* seriously." Her chin lifted, defiant even in surrender. "So. Arrest me for loitering. But I'm not leaving until you show me the ledger and tell me where I fit in your trap."

Theo looked at her—at the woman they had chosen the force over, and lost anyway, because the force had been rotten from the inside; at the last living witness; at the person the fifth letter, whenever it came, would come for. Every instinct honed across three decades said: *keep her at arm's length, keep her safe, keep her out of it.* And every one of those instincts had been wrong before, catastrophically, in ways that had let a servant hang himself in shame and a killer keep his roses pruned for twenty years.

"It's not a trap yet," Theo said at last. "It's a net with holes in it. But there's one thing that would close it." They hesitated, then reached under the counter and brought out the first letter, laid it flat between them like a card turned face-up. "The letters are bait. Which means the writer expects me to follow them—to the archive, the shore, the witnesses. They're steering me. So the only move that isn't in their script is for the bait to become the hunter." They met her eyes. "I need someone the writer doesn't expect to see sitting in this shop, day after day, watching who comes through that door. Someone with a reason to be here that no one will question."

Eleanor looked at the letter, then at Theo, and something moved through her face—fear, yes, but beneath it a fierce, waking alertness, the look of a woman stepping out of twenty years of fog.

"You want me," she said slowly, "to haunt your bookshop."

"I want you to help me catch a murderer. And I want—" Theo stopped. Swallowed the rest of it, or tried to. "I want you where I can protect you. Both things are true. I won't pretend otherwise anymore."

The silence stretched. Rain began again against the glass, soft as fingertips. And Eleanor reached out and turned the ledger toward herself, and uncapped the pen, and in the visitors' column, in a hand as elegant and unmistakable as her mother's, she wrote: *E. Marlowe. Reason for visit: none whatsoever.*

"There," she said. "Your first honest entry." She set down the pen and looked up, and for one suspended moment the shop, the case, the twenty years—all of it receded, and there was only the two of them and the smell of salt and old paper. "Now show me the rest of the file, Detective. Because yesterday morning, before I drove here, I found something on the gatepost of Marlowe House."

Theo went cold. "Found what?"

"A cream envelope." Her eyes held theirs, steady and afraid. "Postmarked yesterday. And Theo—it wasn't addressed to me."
