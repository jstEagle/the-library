## 11. The Servant's Daughter

Ruth Pell had walked past Fairweather's Books perhaps four hundred times in the twenty years since her father's death, and had gone inside exactly once—on the day of his funeral, when she'd bought a paperback of sea poems she had never read, because it seemed the sort of thing a grieving daughter ought to do. She still had it. It sat on the shelf above her kitchen sink with its spine cracked white, a bookmark of grief she'd never had the courage to finish.

Tonight she stood across the street under the awning of the shuttered chandlery, watching the light in the bookshop window go amber and then gold and then, as Theo Fairweather moved through the shop turning off lamps, dim to a single pool of brightness near the counter. Rain had started again, the fine sideways kind that Marlowe Bay specialized in, the kind that got under collars and into bones and made the whole town smell of kelp and old wood. Ruth pulled her coat tighter and told herself, for the third time, that she could still walk away.

Her father's notebook was in her bag. It had been in her possession for twenty years, and for nineteen of those years it had been in the same biscuit tin at the back of her wardrobe, beneath a folded christening gown that had belonged to a daughter she'd never had. She had not opened the tin in over a decade. She had not needed to. The contents had stayed exactly where she'd put them the night after the funeral, when she'd gone through her father's effects with the numb efficiency of the newly fatherless, and found, taped behind the loose panel of his wardrobe, a notebook that should not have existed.

Thomas Pell had been a careful man. That was what everyone said at the funeral—*careful, quiet, dependable*—the eulogy words of a servant, a man whose entire life had been an exercise in not being noticed. He had served the Marlowe household for thirty-one years, first as a footman and then as Lady Vivienne's personal valet-adjacent factotum, a word Ruth had only ever heard her father use, always with a small private smile, as though the title were a joke between him and the house. He had polished silver, laid fires, driven the estate car, and kept his mouth shut about everything he saw, which over thirty-one years in that house had been a very great deal.

They said he had hanged himself out of shame. That was the official story, the one the inquest had accepted, the one the town had swallowed with its supper: Thomas Pell, suspected in the death of Lady Vivienne Marlowe, had taken his own life rather than face the disgrace. *Guilty conscience*, the papers said. *The servant did it, and couldn't live with what he'd done.*

Ruth had been twenty-two. She had known, with the absolute certainty of a child for a parent, that her father had not done it. She had known it the way you know your own name. And she had known something else too, something she had never told a living soul, not the inquest, not the police, not the priest, not her husband: her father had not been a man who despaired. Her father had been a man who *planned*. And in the last month of his life, he had been planning something.

She had understood that only later, only when she'd finally worked up the courage to read the notebook all the way through, years afterward, on a night when her marriage was failing and she'd had too much wine and nothing left to lose. The early pages were ordinary—laundry lists, wine inventories, the small bookkeeping of service. But in the final third, the handwriting changed. It tightened. It went careful. And it began to record things that had nothing to do with laundry.

*V. asked me to post the second letter herself. I did not ask what was in it. She paid me extra and told me to forget the postmark date.*

*The study safe was open again Tuesday. She does not know I saw. There were papers on the desk I have never seen before. Letters. Many letters, in her hand, all addressed to the same person.*

*She said to me: "You of all people should understand, Thomas, that a name is only worth what it costs to keep it." I did not answer. One does not answer.*

*She is afraid. I have served her eleven years and I have never seen her afraid.*

Ruth had read those pages perhaps fifty times over the years, and each time she arrived at the same place: the last entry, dated four days before Vivienne Marlowe died.

*She told me tonight who it is. She made me swear on my mother's grave I would tell no one, and then she laughed and said my mother's grave was in the churchyard where everyone could see it, so perhaps I should swear on something no one could dig up. She was drinking. She is frightened and drinking and she thinks money will fix what she has done. I write it here because I am afraid too, and a man who cannot say a thing aloud must at least write it down: if anything happens to me, look to the initials. She said the initials and then she said, "You silly man, you knew him for years, you've brushed his coat." I did not know then. I know now. God forgive me, I know now.*

The entry ended there. No name. Only the initials, which her father had not written down—had not dared to write down—and which Ruth had spent twenty years not knowing, carrying the notebook like a stone in a tin like a wardrobe like a life.

But she knew something else now. She had known it for three days, ever since the *Marlowe Bay Courier* had run its piece about the anonymous confession letter, ever since old Mrs. Trelawny at the post office had gossiped it into a town-wide certainty before the ink was dry. Someone was confessing. Someone was killing. Bram Ottery was dead in his garden pond, and the whole town was pretending that was an accident, and Theo Fairweather—the detective, the one who'd left, the one her father had once spoken of with a complicated warmth, *a good one, that one, wasted on us*—was asking questions again.

And Ruth had realized, standing in her kitchen with the biscuit tin open on the table and her heart going like a trapped bird, that the notebook was no longer a stone. It was a fuse. If the confessor was working through the witnesses, working backward through everyone who had touched the case, then her father was on the list—had been on the list for twenty years, had been first on the list—and Ruth Pell was holding the only document in the world that could tell the truth about what her father had actually known.

She could burn it. She had thought about burning it, standing there with the tin lid in her hand. Burn it, and stay invisible, and hope the tide of deaths rolled past her the way the 2003 investigation had rolled past her, dismissing her father as a guilty suicide and never once looking at his daughter.

But her father had not raised her to burn things. Her father had raised her to write things down.

* * *

The bell above the bookshop door made its small broken sound—half chime, half rattle—and Theo Fairweather looked up from behind the counter with the wariness of a man who had spent three weeks learning that visitors came in two kinds: the curious and the dangerous, and that the dangerous ones smiled more.

Ruth made herself smile. She was not sure it worked.

"Miss Pell." Theo's voice was careful. Of course it was. Everyone in this town knew her name and what it had been attached to. "It's been—"

"Twenty years. I know." She crossed the shop slowly, her eyes moving over the shelves without seeing them, the way you move through a church. The shop smelled of paper and salt and pipe smoke, and beneath it all, faintly, of fear. She could smell fear on people lately; she'd developed the talent the way you develop a limp. "I'm not here to buy a book, Mr. Fairweather."

"Theo, please. And I'd guessed about the book." Theo set down the pen they'd been holding. Up close, Ruth could see what the newspaper photographs didn't show: the tiredness around the eyes, the vigilance, a person who had been sleeping badly and listening harder. "Can I make you tea? The kettle's honest, even if the stock isn't."

"No." Her voice came out sharper than she'd intended, and she made herself soften it. "No, thank you. I'd rather say it while I still can. If I sit down and get comfortable I'll lose my nerve, and I've spent twenty years losing my nerve, and I'm rather tired of it."

Something moved in Theo's face—not quite a smile, something older and sadder. "Then say it."

Ruth set her bag on the counter. Her hands were shaking. She had rehearsed this on the walk down, three versions, five versions, a dozen openings, and every one of them had evaporated, and so she simply did the thing her father had taught her without ever meaning to, which was to put the evidence down plainly and let it speak.

The notebook was small, bound in cracked green leather, its edges furred with two decades of wardrobe dark. She set it on the counter between them the way you set down a bird with a broken wing.

"This was my father's," she said. "Thomas Pell. You'll remember him."

"I remember him." Theo did not touch the notebook. Their eyes had gone very still, the stillness of a person standing at the edge of something. "I remember everyone from that case. He was—Lady Vivienne's man. He was found—" They stopped. Ruth watched them choose the gentler word and admired and resented it in equal measure.

"He was found hanged in the boathouse," Ruth said. "You can say it. Everyone else does. *Hanged himself*, they say, which is a lie with better manners. He didn't kill himself, Mr.—Theo. And he didn't kill Lady Vivienne. And I have spent twenty years knowing both of those things and saying nothing, because I was twenty-two and terrified, and because whoever drove him to that boathouse—" her voice caught, and she hated that it caught, and she pushed through it, "—whoever it was, they were still walking around Marlowe Bay with their face washed and their collar clean, and I had to go on living here, serving them at the bakery counter, taking their change at the post office. So I said nothing. And now Bram Ottery is dead in his pond, and I think my saying-nothing days are over."

Theo's hand had come to rest flat on the counter, not touching the notebook, close to it. "Why now? Why bring it to me?"

"Because of the letters." Ruth met their eyes. "Because someone out there is working through the witnesses, and my father is on that list whether he's been dead twenty years or not, because they'll want to know what he knew, and there's exactly one person alive who might know what he knew, and she's standing in your shop getting rainwater on your floor." She swallowed. "And because I read the paper. I know you're asking questions. And my father used to say you were the only honest one in the building. He said it the way you'd say something you were ashamed of—that the force had one honest person in it and it was a shame about the rest."

That earned her a real look, brief and wounded and grateful. "He said that?"

"He said it to my mother, and my mother said it to me, and I have kept it for twenty years like a coin I couldn't spend." Ruth's hands had stopped shaking now that the thing was on the counter; it was always like that, she'd found—the terror was in the carrying, not the giving. "Open it. The last third. The handwriting changes."

Theo opened the notebook.

Ruth watched them read, and it was like watching a person stand in shallow water and feel the ground go out from under them one grain at a time. Theo's eyes moved down the page, and back up, and down again, and their breathing changed, and one hand rose to press against their mouth in a gesture so involuntary, so nakedly human, that Ruth felt her throat tighten. This was not a detective reading a document. This was a person watching twenty years of careful, dutiful certainty come apart in their hands.

"The letters," Theo said quietly. "She was writing letters. Repeated letters. To one person." They looked up. "This is extortion. This is—" They stopped, recalibrated, and Ruth watched the detective come back into their face like a tide coming in. "The victim had teeth."

"That's what I thought you'd say." Ruth almost laughed, and it came out cracked. "Everyone remembers her as the poor murdered lady. The saint on the postage stamp. But my father's pages—read the middle ones, the safe, the papers on the desk—she wasn't a victim, not in the beginning. She was running something. She was squeezing someone inside that house for years, and my father carried her letters and posted them and kept his mouth shut because that was his whole life, keeping his mouth shut, and it killed him in the end anyway."

"The last entry." Theo had turned to it. Their finger hovered over the page without touching the ink, the way you hover over a wound. "*You've brushed his coat.*" They read it aloud, barely voicing it, and Ruth saw the words land on them like weather. "He knew. At the end, he knew who it was, and he didn't write the name."

"He was a careful man," Ruth said. "It's what got the eulogies said. He wouldn't put a name in ink. But he wrote *him*, Theo. *Him.* And he wrote *initials*, plural, which means—" she made herself say it, the thing she had turned over a thousand nights, "—which means the initials weren't a name he didn't know. They were a name he couldn't believe. *You knew him for years.* He was shocked. He was shocked by someone he'd served, someone close enough to brush his coat, and the initials were two letters, because he says initials, and then he says *him*—"

"Halloway." Theo said the name flatly, without drama, the way you'd set down something heavy. "Inspector Halloway. H. And—" Ruth watched them arrive at it a half-second before she did, watched their face change, "—and if there's a second H, if the fifth letter's initial isn't the inspector at all—"

"Then your killer is someone else who was close enough to brush my father's coat." Ruth's voice had gone very quiet. "Someone inside the house. Someone my father served tea to."

The rain had thickened against the windows. Somewhere down the street a gull complained into the dark, that raw Marlowe Bay sound that never entirely stopped, the town's pulse. Theo stood motionless behind the counter with the notebook open between them like a body on a table, and Ruth understood suddenly that she had just changed the shape of everything—that the case she had carried out of her father's wardrobe had been a different case all along, that the murdered woman had been an extortionist, that the grieving family had been a hunting ground, that the servant who died had been the only honest witness and had been silenced for it, and that she, Ruth Pell, had just handed the last living piece of him to the only person in Marlowe Bay who might do something with it.

"Keep it," she said.

Theo looked up sharply. "Ruth—"

"Keep it. Copy it, photograph it, lock it in a bank vault, I don't care. But it can't come home with me. Not anymore." She was surprised at how steady her voice was. "Whoever is killing witnesses—if they come for me, they'll search my house, and I won't have it. And if they come for me anyway, then at least it'll be for nothing." She managed something that was almost a smile. "My father spent his life being invisible so that other people could keep their secrets. I'd like to spend a little of mine making sure his last secret does some good."

Theo closed the notebook with both hands, gently, the way you'd close a coffin. "I'll copy every page tonight. You'll have your set by morning. And Ruth—" they hesitated, and the hesitation was the most honest thing in the room, "—I'm sorry. For 2003. For all of it. We looked at your father and we saw what we were told to see, and I was young and I believed the file, and the file was a lie someone built. If I'd been better—"

"You were the honest one in the building," Ruth said. "My father said so. That's why I'm here, and not at the police station." She picked up her bag. At the door she paused, her hand on the frame, and looked back at the figure behind the counter—this person the town had decided was finished, this keeper of a dying shop, this last honest one—and she said the thing she had come to say and had not known until now that it was the true thing:

"The initials, Theo. My father was shocked by someone he'd brushed the coat of. Think about everyone who was *inside that house* in 2003. Not the police. The household." She pulled her collar up against the rain waiting beyond the door. "And then think about who's still alive."

The bell made its broken sound behind her, and she stepped out into the rain, lighter by one notebook and heavier by one truth, and behind her she knew that Theo Fairweather was already reaching for a pen, already writing down names, already arriving—she could feel it through the glass, through the rain, through twenty years—already arriving at the name that would change everything.

*Harrow.*

The valet. The drowned man.

The man whose body had never been found.
