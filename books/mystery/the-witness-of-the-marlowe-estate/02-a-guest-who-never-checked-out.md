## 2. A Guest Who Never Checked Out

The girl arrived at half past nine on a Tuesday, which was the hour Hazel Joubert had long ago designated for the small, survivable misfortunes: a leaking radiator, a lost reservation, a guest who had died quietly in Room 14 and required the utmost delicacy. She was polishing the brass bell on the front desk when the doors swung open and the October wind came in wearing a camel coat.

"I need a room," the girl said, "and I need you to tell me everything you know about my grandfather's funeral."

Hazel set down the cloth. "I'm afraid I don't follow, madam."

"Vivian Marlowe." She said the name the way other people said *checkmate*. "Edmund Marlowe was my grandfather. And on Saturday, when they dug him up for the centennial ceremony, his coffin was empty. So either the founder of this town has been playing an extremely long game, or somebody in Marlowe's Crossing knows something they shouldn't." She laid a black-gloved hand on the counter. "The undertaker won't speak to me. The constabulary refers me to the coroner, the coroner refers me to the council, and the council refers me to my father, who has stopped returning my calls. You, however—" her eyes flicked up, sharp and gray, "—run the only hotel in town. Which means you've heard everything for the last thirty years. So. Everything you know."

Hazel had spent three decades perfecting the art of the unremarkable face. She deployed it now, all soft apology and professional regret. "I'm terribly sorry, Miss Marlowe, but I was not employed at the hotel at the time of Mr. Marlowe's funeral. I know only what the newspapers printed."

"You started in 1985," Vivian said. "The funeral was in '84. Close enough."

"You've done your homework."

"I've done *something*," the girl said, and there was something under the sharpness then, something that moved and breathed. Grief, maybe. Or fear. "My grandfather is missing, Ms. Joubert. Whatever else he was—and I've read what he was, don't worry, I'm not naive—somebody took him out of that grave, and everybody in this town is pretending it's a clerical error. I'd like a room. The quietest one you have. And I'd like you to stop looking at me like I'm a problem to be managed."

Hazel gave her Room 9, the one under the eaves where the pipes sang in cold weather, and watched her climb the stairs with a suitcase that looked heavier than luggage has any right to look. Then she sat down at the desk and did not move for a long time.

Because here was the thing the girl could not know: Hazel had been asking those same questions once. She had buried them so deep and so carefully that she had almost—*almost*—forgotten where the grave was.

* * *

The inquest had been held in the spring of 1984, in the county courtroom with the water-stained ceiling, and Hazel had attended every day of it because the Bellamy's night porter, Edda Solano, had asked her to. "Sit where I can see you," Edda had said, wringing her hands in the corridor. "In case I have to say something true."

The fire had taken the Marlowe Textile Works on the eleventh of January, a Tuesday, in the dead hours before dawn. It had taken with it the factory's bookkeeping office, three floors of records, and one night watchman, a Mr. Albert Dunn, who had perished at his post—or so the official account ran. The insurance was substantial. The grief of Edmund Marlowe, as reported in the *Marlowe's Crossing Courier*, was immense.

The inquest was meant to be a formality. Everyone understood that. The coroner, a soft-voiced man who played golf with Gerald Marlowe every Saturday, asked his questions in the tone of a man confirming the time of a dinner reservation. Electrical fault, he suggested. Old wiring. A tragedy of the kind that visits old buildings.

And then a young woman in a gray coat had stood up in the gallery and said, in a voice that shook but did not stop: "I saw a car in the loading yard at midnight. Two men got out. They went in through the office door with a can of something. I smelled it after. And Mr. Dunn was supposed to have the night off—someone paid him to swap shifts, and he told me so, and then he was dead in the building."

The courtroom had gone very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when a floorboard betrays you.

"Name," said the coroner.

"Ruth. Ruth Adeyemi." She was maybe twenty-five, a clerk at the factory, and she stood with her hands folded like a schoolgirl reciting, as though she had rehearsed the words until they wore smooth. "I was walking home past the yard. I see it every night from the lane."

"Did anyone else see this car?"

"No, sir."

"Were you drinking that night?"

The question landed like a slap. Hazel had watched the girl's chin come up. "No, sir. I don't drink."

The coroner had smiled gently, the smile of a man closing a door. The Marlowe family solicitor had then taken eleven minutes to establish that Ruth Adeyemi had recently been passed over for a promotion, that she had a grievance, that grievance made witnesses unreliable, that darkness made them more so. By the time he finished, the gray coat had stopped mattering. The verdict came down that afternoon: accidental death by electrical fire. Albert Dunn, unfortunate. Edmund Marlowe, bereaved. Case closed, sealed, and shelved.

Hazel had watched Ruth Adeyemi walk out of the courthouse alone, into a rain that had started without anyone noticing, and she had seen the car idling across the street—two men in it, not moving, just watching. She had seen the girl see it too. She had seen her pull her coat collar up and walk the other way, fast.

That was the last time Hazel saw her. Or thought she had.

* * *

Because there had been one more sighting, and this was the thing Hazel had buried.

Three nights after the inquest, Hazel had been coming back late from her sister's in Kettering—she was not yet the concierge, then, only a woman between lives who had taken a room at the Bellamy because it was cheap and the landlady was going deaf. She had come through the service entrance at half past midnight and found the back hall full of people: Edda Solano, still in her porter's coat, and a young woman in a gray coat with a bag clutched to her chest, and old Constable Aldous Pike, who was not yet old, holding the door.

Nobody saw Hazel. She had a lifetime's practice at not being seen, even then. She had stood in the dark of the linen room with her heart going like a clock and watched Edda press money into the girl's hands and Pike say, low and urgent, "Not the main station. The freight stop at Milford. There'll be a car waiting. You were never here, and when they ask—and they will ask—I'll handle the paperwork." And Edda had said, "God forgive us," and the girl had said, in that voice that shook but did not stop, "God has nothing to do with it."

Then the service door closed, and Hazel had stood alone in the linen room among the folded sheets, understanding three things at once and none of them fully: that Ruth Adeyemi was not dead, that the constable was hiding her, and that Hazel herself had just become a person who knew something dangerous.

She had told no one. Not out of courage—out of the opposite. She had watched what the town did to a young woman who spoke the truth in daylight, and she had concluded, at twenty-six, that the safest place to keep a thing like that was inside yourself, in a room with no windows, and never go in. And she had kept it there for forty years, dusting around it, until Saturday morning when a coffin came up out of the ground light as a lie and the whole room flooded open.

* * *

Vivian Marlowe came down to the lobby at eleven that night, barefoot, in the hotel's spare robe, carrying a cup of tea she had made herself in the guest pantry because she hadn't wanted to ring the desk.

"I can't sleep," she announced, as though this were Hazel's fault, and perhaps it was; the pipes in Room 9 had been singing.

"Would you like something stronger than tea?"

"I'd like you to tell me why you went pale this morning when I said the word *funeral*."

Hazel looked at her over the desk. Forty years of invisibility sat in her chest like a stone, and on top of it, newly, the memory of a gray coat in the rain.

"I didn't go pale."

"You did. I watch people for a living—I'm an auditor, before you ask, forensic. Pale is pale." Vivian set down the cup and folded her arms. "Here's what I've got so far, and I'll trade you: the exhumation was ordered by the heritage committee, but the paperwork was filed by someone on the council who then tried to have the date moved up twice. Somebody wanted that coffin opened *now*, before the centennial. That's not sentiment. That's a countdown. And the undertaker—Mr. Fenn? He was at my grandfather's funeral in '84. He was young then, an apprentice. And when I mentioned the funeral to him today, he looked at me the way you look at a number that doesn't reconcile."

Hazel felt the old room inside her open another window. "What did he say?"

"He said, 'Some funerals are for the living, miss.' And then he closed the door." Vivian leaned forward. "So I'll ask again, and this time I'm asking as a person, not a Marlowe. What happened in this town in 1984?"

The grandfather clock in the corner struck eleven, each note falling into the silence like a stone into a well. Hazel thought of a young woman in a gray coat, walking fast away from an idling car. She thought of Edda Solano's hands wringing in a corridor. She thought of the weight of forty years of dust on a door she had sworn never to open.

She thought: *she's asking my questions. She's asking them with my voice.*

"I knew a girl once," Hazel said slowly, "who testified at an inquest. She saw something she shouldn't have, and she said so, and the town made her sorry for it. And then she disappeared. Officially, she died—there's a record, I'm told, a death certificate. But I happen to know that three nights after the inquest, our night porter drove her to the Milford freight stop at midnight, and the constable arranged it, and she got on a train and was never seen again."

Vivian had gone very still. "The witness. There *was* a witness—Ruth Adeyemi. I found the inquest transcript in the county archive. It's the only part of the file that's been flagged as reviewed and resealed. Someone has been *maintaining* it, Ms. Joubert. For forty years. You don't maintain a closed file unless you're afraid of what's in it."

"Or unless you're protecting what's in it," Hazel said, before she could stop herself.

Vivian's eyes narrowed—not with suspicion, Hazel realized, but with the particular intensity of an auditor who has just heard a figure that doesn't reconcile, and knows it reconciles somewhere else. "You knew her. The porter. The constable. You know all of it. You've known for forty years and you've said nothing, and now a coffin comes up empty and you're still—" She stopped. Drew a breath. When she spoke again her voice had changed, quieter, stripped of the armor. "My grandfather may have burned a man alive for an insurance payout. And my family has been lying about it my entire life, and I've spent thirty years being proud of my name. I need to know, Ms. Joubert. Not for the town. For me."

Outside, the wind worried at the eaves, and the pipes in Room 9 sang their thin cold song. Hazel looked at this girl—this sharp, sleepless, grieving girl who had walked into her lobby and begun, without knowing it, to dig in the same grave Hazel had buried her own courage in.

"Ruth Adeyemi was declared dead," Hazel said, "and someone went to a great deal of trouble to make that true on paper. Which means either she's been hiding for forty years—" and here Hazel's voice did something she did not authorize it to do, something that trembled, "—or someone made very sure she could never come back."

Vivian stood up. "Then we find out which."

"*We*," Hazel repeated.

"I watched you decide something just now," Vivian said. "Right at the end there. You decided to help me. Don't waste it being embarrassed about it." She gathered her tea. At the foot of the stairs she paused. "The constable. Pike. Where does he live?"

"The cottage on Fen Lane," Hazel said. "But Miss Marlowe—Aldous Pike is not a man you ambush. He's a man who ambushes *back*, very politely."

"Good," said Vivian Marlowe, and climbed the stairs to her singing pipes, and Hazel sat alone at the desk with the stone in her chest gone strangely light, and did not sleep at all.

In the morning, she would take out the guest registers. All of them. Thirty years of names, dates, and small lies, and she would begin the slow, dangerous work of reconciling the accounts of Marlowe's Crossing.

But that night, as she locked the front door, she noticed one thing she could not yet explain: the register for the week of the fire, January 1984—the one week, of all weeks, that should have been the most ordinary—was missing from the shelf. Not misfiled. Not damaged.

Removed. And the spine where it should have stood was dusted, recently, by a careful hand.
