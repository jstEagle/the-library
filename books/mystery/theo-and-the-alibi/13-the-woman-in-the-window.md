## 13. The Woman in the Window

The lighthouse had been hers for eleven months, and Marisol Vega still could not decide whether it was a home or a sentence.

She stood at the north window of the cannery loft with a chipped mug of coffee going cold in her hands, watching the fog do what fog did in Grayhaven—arrive without apology, swallow the harbor, and leave the world the size of a room. Below her, the old cannery's corrugated walls wept rust in long orange streaks that looked, from this height, like something bleeding slowly upward. The town had died before she was born. The fish had left, then the people, then the money, until all that remained was the smell of brine and diesel and the great skeletal hulk of the processing floor where she now lived among Denny's careful arrangements: a cot, a hot plate, a curtain made from a tarpaulin, a bookshelf built from fish crates.

And the window. The window was hers.

From it she could see the whole curve of the bay, the breakwater, the fishing boats going out at dawn like patient animals, and—half a mile down the coast, on its bald granite knuckle—the lighthouse. Not a working light anymore; the Coast Guard had automated it decades ago and then abandoned even that. But Denny kept the lamp glass clean and painted the tower every other summer, white with a red band, because he said a lie held better when you maintained it. The lighthouse was the lie. It appeared in her diary entries the way a face appears in dreams. *The light turns even though no one tends it,* she had written once, and meant herself.

This morning there was a stranger on the rocks below it.

Marisol saw the figure before she understood what she was seeing—a dark coat, a hood against the damp, moving along the seaward path with none of the aimless wandering of tourists. Tourists came to Grayhaven in July and took photographs of the cannery because it looked picturesque, ruined, romantic. This person moved like someone following instructions written by a dead woman.

*My* dead woman.

The coffee trembled slightly in the mug. Marisol set it down on the windowsill and pressed her palm flat against the cold glass instead, as if she could feel through it, as if touch were a kind of proof.

The stranger stopped at the base of the tower, took out a phone or a small camera, and photographed the lighthouse. Once. Twice. Then turned slowly, deliberately, and photographed the cannery.

Not the cannery as scenery. The cannery as *subject*. The lens lingered on the loading door, on the row of blind windows on the second floor, on—Marisol was almost certain—the very window where she stood. She stepped back out of instinct, heart slamming, absurdly certain that glass could carry her image across half a mile of salt air like a whisper.

Then she laughed at herself, quietly, because the laugh came out wrong, and sat down hard on the cot.

*They always find you eventually.* That was what she'd told Rosa, three years ago, in a kitchen that smelled of cilantro and fear, the night they invented a drowning. *People like the Calloways don't forget. They just wait until you've started to believe you're safe.*

* * *

She had been good at believing it, for a while. That was the terrible thing. She had been so good at it that some mornings she woke up and reached for a life that no longer existed—reached for her phone on the nightstand, for the ferry schedule, for the particular gray light of Puerto Sombra through a window she'd shared with Tomás for six years before his brother's shipping ledgers ruined everything. Her hands remembered doors. Her body still turned left out of habit toward a street that would kill her.

Denny had found her those first weeks in the state of a woman who has been taken apart and not yet put back together. He never asked her to be brave. That was the first kindness anyone had shown her since the subpoenas began. Everyone else—the prosecutors, the marshals who'd lost their nerve, the lawyers who spoke about her safety in the passive voice (*witnesses are sometimes relocated*)—everyone else wanted her courage. Denny only wanted her alive, and he seemed willing to settle for a version of alive that looked like a woman sitting motionless in a loft above a dead cannery, drinking bad coffee, writing in a diary no one was supposed to read.

The diary. She looked at it now where it lay on the crate-shelf, spine cracked, cover swollen from damp. The real one. There were others—decoys, planted, drowned, recovered—but this one had never left her side except into Denny's hands, and it contained everything: the ledgers Tomás had copied before he died, the names, the dates, the night the *Calloway Star* docked with a hold full of nothing and a manifest full of medicine. And, lately, something else. Something she hadn't planned.

Because a diary is a strange instrument when you are alone. It stops being evidence and becomes a voice. And a voice, denied any listener, will invent one.

That was how it had started, months ago—entries addressed to no one, then to someone, then, shamefully, to whoever might someday find the pages. *If you are reading this, you already know I am supposed to be dead. Please water nothing and trust no one with a firm handshake.* She'd told herself it was a discipline, a way of keeping her mind sharp, practicing the story. But she knew better. It was loneliness wearing the costume of tradecraft.

And now someone was standing under her lighthouse taking photographs, and the first feeling that rose in her—not fear, not yet, fear arrived late the way weather off the sea did—was *hope*, and hope frightened her more than anything the Calloways could send.

* * *

She went downstairs.

She didn't decide to; her feet decided, carrying her across the loft's splintered boards, down the iron staircase bolted to the wall by men who'd died before her grandmother was born, into the processing floor where the light came down through holes in the roof in dusty silver columns. Denny was at the loading door, running a cloth along the barrel of the shotgun the way other men ran cloths along banisters—an absent, domestic gesture. He heard her before she reached the bottom. He always did.

"Someone's at the light," she said.

He didn't stop polishing. "Tourist."

"It's February."

He set the cloth down then. Crossed to the wall of one-way glass he'd installed behind a false panel—Denny's cathedral of small paranoias—and looked through the scope mounted there. Marisol watched his shoulders read the stranger the way she'd watched them read weather for eleven months. They dropped a quarter inch.

"Not local," he said. "Coat's too good. Shoes are wrong for the rocks." A pause. "Taking pictures of the cannery."

"Of us."

"Maybe of the building." But his voice didn't believe it either. He lowered the scope and stood a moment with his hand flat against the false panel, the way a man touches a door he isn't sure he wants to open. "Could be Hale's people."

"Hale's people don't photograph lighthouses. Hale's people burn things." She heard her own steadiness and marveled at it. Fear, when you lived inside it long enough, became a kind of furniture. You arranged your life around it. "It's an investigator, Denny. Someone's reading my diary."

"The diary's in the bay."

"One of them is." She watched him turn, and in the gray light from the roof-holes his face was all planes and exhaustion, fifty years old though he wasn't yet forty-five. "You knew this day would come. You told me yourself. Paperwork is a tide, you said. Everything washes up eventually."

"I said that about receipts." He almost smiled. Almost. "I didn't think I'd have to say it about me."

* * *

She climbed back up while he made his circuits—locks, wires, the alarm thread across the loading door that sang a hair-thin note only they could hear. Upstairs, the window again. The stranger was gone from the rocks, but the absence felt temporary, tidal. They would come back. People who photographed like that came back.

Marisol sat on the cot with the diary on her knees and did the arithmetic she had done a hundred times and would do a hundred more.

Option one: run. Tonight, while the fog held. There was a truck behind the cannery with plates Denny rotated monthly, cash in coffee cans, a route memorized in both their heads—north to the ferry at Kessel Point, then a name that wasn't hers in a town that had no post office. She had run before. She knew exactly what it cost. You paid in increments so small you didn't notice the bleeding: a name here, a birthday there, the way you signed your own thoughts. Until one morning you wrote in your diary and realized you no longer knew which name the words belonged to.

Option two: stay and let Denny handle it. He would handle it. He had handled the marshals' retreat, the staged scene at Puerto Sombra, the boat, the coat, the terrible gentle fiction of a body the sea politely declined to return. He would find the stranger and lose them, misdirect them, feed them a Grayhaven full of closed mouths. And the stranger would go away, or not, and Marisol would remain a woman in a window, alive the way furniture is alive—present, useful, unseen.

Option three.

She didn't write option three. Option three had been forming for months in the margins of the diary, in entries addressed to nobody, in the way she caught herself leaving the lamp in the tower lit some nights longer than necessary, like a signal, like a question. *Is anyone out there? Does anyone know I'm still—*

She opened the diary to a fresh page. The pen hesitated. Outside, the fog was lifting off the harbor in rags, and the lighthouse stood white and red on its rock, tended by no one, turning for no one, faithful to absolutely nothing and therefore, somehow, to everything.

She thought about Paloma Lockridge—or whatever the stranger's name was; she'd seen the file once, early, in the marshal's office, the name of the verification investigator assigned to confirm her death, a name she'd forgotten on purpose because forgetting was survival. She thought about how the stranger had stood on the rocks, and how they had photographed the cannery the way she imagined a person reads a difficult letter: completely, without skipping.

Whoever it was had read to the end. Eleven days past the end. Past the date the sea had been given as hers. Which meant they had noticed what everyone else had agreed not to notice—that the diary kept breathing after the drowning. That somewhere past the last official page, a dead woman's handwriting had continued, patient and precise, describing lemon drops and a scar and a light that turned although no one tended it.

A trap? Of course it might be a trap. Hale's people were capable of anything; she had read the ledger entries Tomás had died copying. But traps were built to catch, and this felt like something else. This felt like attention. Like being *read*.

When had she last been read? Not looked at, not processed, not protected like cargo. Read.

She began to write.

*To whoever is standing under my lighthouse with a camera:*

*I saw you. Of course I saw you—I see everything from this window, that is the one gift of a life like mine, the seeing. You took two photographs of the light and one of the cannery, and you stood on the rocks the way people stand when they are checking a detail against a memory, which means you have details, which means you have the diary, which means you are either the end of me or the beginning of something I gave up hoping for.*

*I want you to know I considered running. I always consider running; it is my evening prayer. But I am tired of being a rumor in my own life. I am tired of writing letters to the dark.*

*So here is what I will do, stranger. I will leave the lamp burning tonight, later than usual. If you are the kind of person who notices things—which you must be, or you wouldn't be here—you will notice that too. And if you come closer, come honestly. Come with the diary. I want to meet the one person alive who read a drowned woman all the way to the end and believed her.*

*If you are one of theirs—if Hale sent you—then burn this page and know that I wrote it anyway, knowing, because I am done letting them take my voice along with everything else.*

*The light turns at nine. I will be watching.*

She read it over twice. Then, because hope required witnesses or it curdled into madness, she carried the diary downstairs, past Denny's alarm threads, and set it on the table by the loading door where he would find it, open to the new entry, and understand that she had already decided.

Let him argue. Let him stand guard over her caution another eleven months, another eleven years. She had spent three years being protected out of her own existence, and the stranger on the rocks had undone it in four minutes with a camera, simply by looking at her lighthouse as if it mattered.

Upstairs, the fog finished lifting. The harbor opened like a hand.

At nine o'clock, Marisol Vega climbed the tower stairs of a lighthouse that officially did not work, and lit the lamp herself, and left it burning long past midnight—a small, deliberate flame on a dark coast, addressed to whoever was looking.

Two miles inland, on the ridge road, a parked car with rental plates noted the light, and stayed, and did not drive away.
