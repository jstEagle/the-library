## 10. The Bank That Forgot

**Bram**

The key was brass, old-fashioned, with a paper tag gone amber at the edges: *Merchants Trust & Savings — Box 114*. I had held it in my palm for three days like a stone I couldn't decide whether to skip or keep.

There was no Merchants Trust & Savings in Harrow's Bend. There hadn't been since 1987, when the chain collapsed and its branches were sold off like furniture from a dead man's estate. I knew this because I had called every financial institution within forty miles, and every one of them had told me, with the patient courtesy reserved for cranks and widowers, that they had never heard of such an account.

"You're telling me," Iris said, sitting across my desk with her coat still on, "that my grandfather left me a key to a bank that doesn't exist."

"I'm telling you the bank doesn't exist. I'm not yet prepared to say what else doesn't."

She turned the tag over in her fingers. She'd been doing that all morning — worrying it like a loose thread. "My mother used to say Voss men kept their promises in boxes. I thought she meant it as an insult."

"It may have been both."

I had a map of the town from 1974 spread open on my desk, the kind the county printed for tourists who never came. And there it was, on the corner of Wexler and Ninth: a small rectangle labeled *Merchants Trust & Savings*, wedged between a pharmacy long closed and a building that now housed a nail salon.

The branch hadn't been demolished. It had been forgotten, which is a different thing entirely, and in my experience a far more deliberate one.

* * *

**Iris**

The building looked like a set someone had struck but never stored. Limestone front, brass letters worn to ghosts above the door — you could still read *MERCH* if you squinted. The windows were papered over from the inside, yellowed to the color of old teeth. A realty sign leaned against the wall, its phone number two area codes out of date.

"Property records say it sold in 1991," Bram said. He'd driven us out in his car, which smelled of leather and pipe smoke and something older underneath, like a house that remembered other people. "To a holding company. Hollis & Vane Holdings."

"Let me guess. You can't find out who owns Hollis & Vane."

"I can find out anything," he said, "given enough time. Time is the one thing we may not have." He said it lightly, but his eyes went down the empty street before he said it, checking.

We went around back, where a fire door hung on its hinges like a bad tooth. Bram tried the handle, and when it didn't give, he took out a slim case from his jacket — not lockpicks exactly, he assured me, *documents tools*, which is lawyer for lockpicks — and had it open in under a minute.

"You've done this before."

"I've done this never," he said. "I've merely read about it extensively. There's a difference, legally speaking, that I'm choosing to lean on."

Inside, dust lay over everything like snow that had stopped falling mid-century. Our flashlight beams crossed in the dark and made shapes on the walls. The lobby was intact — teller counters, a queue rail, a rubber stamp tray fossilized in place. It wasn't decayed so much as *paused*, as if the whole bank were waiting for someone to come back and flip the sign.

"Why would anyone buy this place and leave it?" I whispered, though there was no one to hear us.

"Because someone wanted it exactly as it was," Bram said. His beam swept the wall behind the teller line, where the safe-deposit vault door stood open — the door itself massive, drilled open long ago by whoever emptied the boxes. "And because whatever they wanted to protect, they wanted protected by neglect. Nobody questions a ruin."

* * *

**Bram**

Box 114 was on the third row, at eye level, and it was the only one whose little brass door still bore its number plate. The others had been pried or stripped, but 114's door swung open at the turn of Iris's key — the actual key, fifty years old, fitting a lock that should have been rusted shut.

It moved like it had been oiled recently.

I felt the hair rise on my forearms and said nothing. Some observations are better kept until you're outside.

The box itself was empty. Not dusty-empty — *clean*-empty, wiped, the metal bright where hands or cloth had passed. Except.

At the back, flat against the rear wall, lay a single photograph, face-down, the size of a postcard.

Iris reached for it and I caught her wrist. She looked at me, and in the flashlight's spill her face was furious and frightened in equal measure, and I understood suddenly that we had crossed some line neither of us had named — that this was no longer probate, no longer curiosity, no longer even investigation. This was two people standing inside a locked room that someone had recently oiled, reaching for the thing someone had recently left.

"Gloves," I said. "Please."

She pulled a pair of nitrile gloves from her coat pocket — nurse's habit — and handed me a second pair without being asked. We lifted the photograph together, which was absurd, two adults handling a five-by-seven print like it was a live grenade, but neither of us suggested doing otherwise.

Four men stood on the courthouse steps, squinting into a sun that had set half a century ago. Suits, hats, the stiff formality of men posing for prosperity. On the back, in fountain pen: *Founders' Day, June 1959.*

Edmund Voss stood second from the left. I recognized him from the funeral photograph Winnifred Aldous had shown us — thinner here, younger, his smile not quite reaching his eyes. To his right, a heavyset man with a mayor's sash. To his left, a police captain with a mustache like a hedge.

And at the end, tallest of them, hand resting familiarly on Voss's shoulder —

His face had been scratched out. Not torn, not inked over. Scratched, with something sharp and patient, deep enough to score the emulsion down to the paper, again and again, until the features were a white wound.

But the rest of him remained. The height. The shoulders. The way he stood, slightly apart, as if the photograph belonged to him and the others were guests in it.

"That's four," Iris said quietly. "Voss, the mayor, the captain — and him."

"Yes."

"Someone didn't want us to know his face."

"Someone," I said, "wanted us to know everything *except* his face. Which is a different message entirely." I turned the photograph over again. Beneath the date, in smaller script, almost hidden by the shadow of the pen stroke: *He keeps the books now.*

* * *

**Iris**

We stood in that dead lobby for a long time after, and I don't remember deciding to sit down on the cold marble floor, but at some point I was sitting, my back against the teller counter, the photograph in my lap under the flashlight like a campfire.

Bram sat down next to me. He didn't ask if I was all right, which I appreciated more than I can say. He just sat, and let the silence be what it was.

"My grandfather scratched out that face," I said finally. "Or had it done. Before he sealed the box."

"Probably."

"Which means he knew who it was. Knew, and hid it anyway. Hid it so well that fifty years later his own granddaughter has to break into a haunted bank to find a picture with a hole in it." My voice cracked on the last word and I hated it. "Why hide it? Why not just name him?"

"Because naming him would have gotten the box opened by the wrong person," Bram said slowly, working it through the way he worked through clauses, aloud, testing each joint. "A name is searchable. A face, once seen, can't be unseen — but only by the person who sees it. He left us a riddle instead of a target. If the wrong person found this box, they'd find nothing usable. If the right person found it—" he gestured at the two of us, at the absurdity of us, "—they'd already know who they were looking for. Or they'd know where to look."

"The plaque," I said. "Winnifred's files. The cipher."

"The cipher names transactions, not people. But the dates, the donations — cross-referenced with the founding families, there's really only one man alive today who fits the height, the era, the standing, and the pattern of leverage." He paused, and I watched him decide whether to say it. "Judge Pike funded the restoration of this very block, Iris. Hollis & Vane Holdings' registered agent shares an address with a firm Pike's family endowed in 1962."

The flashlight trembled in my hand, just once. "The beloved judge."

"The beloved judge."

I laughed — a short, ugly sound that bounced around the marble. "Of course. Of course it's the judge. It's always the most respectable man in the room. My mother used to say the devil doesn't hide in hell, he hides on the bench."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have liked Winnifred."

That surprised a real laugh out of me, small and startled. And then, without either of us planning it, we were leaning together, shoulder to shoulder, two strangers sharing warmth in a tomb of a bank, and it felt less like comfort than like strategy — like the first honest thing either of us had done.

"This stopped being legal work somewhere around the second letter," I said. "Maybe the first. I just want to say it out loud so we both stop pretending."

Bram was quiet a moment. "I've spent thirty years believing every death could be filed neatly," he said. "Cause, date, signature, done. Edmund Voss broke that. Whoever scratched this face is trying very hard to finish the job." He looked at me, and in the dark his eyes were tired and absolutely steady. "So no. Not legal work. I don't know what to call it. But I'm in it, whatever it is. For the duration."

"For the duration," I agreed.

We got up. We photographed the box, the oiled hinges, the empty rows, the number plate. As we left through the fire door, I looked back once at the lobby, at the queue rail waiting for customers who would never come, and I had the sudden, certain feeling of being watched — not from the street, but from the past, from behind that scratched white wound, from a man tall enough to rest his hand on my grandfather's shoulder like he owned it.

* * *

**Bram**

In the car, engine running for the light it gave us, Iris held the photograph up to the window and studied the unscratched faces one more time.

"Bram," she said. "Look at Voss's hand."

I leaned across. In the photo, Edmund Voss's right hand hung at his side — and tucked between two fingers, half-hidden by his coat sleeve, was a small dark object. A key. Or something shaped like one.

"He's holding a key in the picture," she said. "In the founders' photo. In front of everyone. Nobody noticed because nobody was looking."

"Or because only one person was ever meant to look." I sat back, my mind moving fast now, the way it did when a will's clauses began to click into alignment. "Iris — Winnifred said the trust's founding charter lists four original trustees. Three signed publicly. The fourth signature was witnessed but the trustee's name was entered only by initials."

"H.P.," she said.

"H.P.," I confirmed. "Harlan Pike. Who was twenty-nine years old in 1959, six foot two, and whose family endowment shares a registered agent with the company that bought this bank and never let it change."

Iris lowered the photograph to her lap. Outside, the streetlights of Wexler and Ninth buzzed on, one by one, throwing our shadows long across the windshield.

"So we go to the police," she said.

"We go to the police," I said, "and we say *the Honorable Harlan Pike*, and every officer in that building remembers who paid for their new firing range." I put the car in gear. "No. First we go see Winnifred. If anyone in this town kept the originals — the charter, the trust deeds, the fourth signature — it's her. And if Pike knows what we found in this box—"

I didn't finish the sentence. I didn't have to. We both heard it: the clock under everything, ticking faster now.

I drove toward the archive with the headlights cutting the dusk, and in the mirror I watched the shuttered bank shrink behind us, its brass letters catching the last light — *MERCH* — like a mouth opening to say a name it had been keeping for fifty-two years.

Winnifred answered on the second ring when Iris called ahead. Her voice was strange — tight, breathless.

"Get here quickly," she said. "And children — come the back way. Someone's been asking about my files. Someone official."
