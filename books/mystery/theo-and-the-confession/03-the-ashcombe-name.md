## 3. The Ashcombe Name

The name appeared on the third page of the impossible will, and Kestrel Pemberton read it the way a man reads the name of a disease he has only ever heard about from other people — with a distant, professional sympathy and a private, unprofessional hope that it would never concern him personally.

*Ashcombe.*

He set down the document, took off his glasses, and polished them on his tie, which is what he did instead of sighing. Outside his window, London went about its morning with its usual indifference: a bus sighed at a stop, a pigeon made an argument with another pigeon, someone dropped a stack of folders two floors down and swore with real feeling. The world, in other words, had not stopped. Kestrel rather wished it would have. It would have made the moment feel properly weighted.

Because everyone in his line of work knew the Ashcombe name, and knowing it was a little like knowing the name of a storm. You didn't expect it to come through your window.

The Ashcombe estate was old money that had done what old money does when left alone long enough: it had thickened, darkened, and settled into the foundations of the county like a vein of ore. The patriarch, Alistair Ashcombe, had died a year and eleven days ago — Kestrel checked the date twice — of what the death certificate called *natural causes*, in the way that all certificates call everything natural, as though death itself were ever anything else. Heart failure, the coroner had written. A man of seventy-four, gout, a fondness for brandy, a heart that had been complaining for a decade. Nothing to see.

And yet here was a will dated three years in the future — a document that could not, by any honest arithmetic, exist — and threaded through its provisions like wire through a cake, the Ashcombe estate. A trust Kestrel had never heard of. A bequest to a woman whose name he didn't recognize. A residue clause that redirected funds in a way that made his lawyer's teeth ache, because it was *elegant*, and elegance in estate law is usually the fingerprint of someone very good at their job.

Someone inside the system had drafted this. Someone who knew the forms, the filing rhythms, the exact shade of bureaucratic inattention that lets a strange document sleep in a registry for years without anyone asking it questions.

Kestrel put the will back in its folder, put the folder in his safe, and then sat for a long moment with his hands laced on his desk, feeling the particular cold that comes not from fear but from the moment before fear, the way the air goes still before a change in the weather.

Then he did what he always did with a problem too large for his desk: he made tea, and he made a telephone call.

* * *

The Ashcombe manor, when Kestrel finally drove out to see it, was the kind of house that had given up pretending.

It stood at the end of a lane lined with chestnuts, a great grey sprawl of a building with more chimneys than it could reasonably defend and ivy climbing the east wing with the slow confidence of something that knew it would win. Two of the windows on the upper floor were boarded. The fountain in the forecourt held rainwater and one indignant moorhen. It was, Kestrel thought, a house in the process of being digested by its own history, and he felt an immediate, uncharitable kinship with it. He, too, was a structure maintained mostly by habit.

The woman who opened the door — herself, without a housekeeper, which told him something — was perhaps sixty, tall, dressed in dove-grey with the kind of understated precision that costs more than flash ever does. Her hair was silver at the temples and her eyes were the pale, unbothered grey of a winter sky, and Kestrel, who had spent thirty years reading people the way other men read weather reports, registered two things at once: she was beautiful in the way that certain knives are beautiful, and she had been expecting him.

"Mr. Pemberton," she said. Not a question. "You're earlier than I expected. Come in before the moorhen does. She's been trying to move in all week."

"You were expecting me," Kestrel said, stepping over the threshold into a hall that smelled of beeswax, cold stone, and — faintly, beneath it — dust.

"I was expecting *someone*," Vivian Ashcombe said, leading him down a corridor hung with portraits of men who looked as though they had all died disappointed. "The question of who, and when, and what they'd be carrying, was the interesting part. You came in a hired car. That's either caution or poverty, and your shoes say poverty is not the answer." She glanced back at him, and the corner of her mouth moved. "Your shoes are very well made, Mr. Pemberton. Don't look so alarmed. I was married to a man who noticed shoes. Some habits survive the marriage."

She showed him into a morning room where a fire had been lit against the damp, and on the table between two chairs there was a tea service already laid for two.

Kestrel looked at it for slightly too long.

"You'll have to forgive the theatre," she said, pouring without asking whether he took milk, and getting it right. "A widow's life is very quiet. One amuses oneself."

* * *

"I'll come to the point," Kestrel said, when he had been given his cup and his hostess had arranged herself in the chair opposite with the ease of a woman who had spent a lifetime arranging herself. "I've recently encountered a document. A will. It's dated three years from now."

He watched her hands. He had learned long ago that faces lie fluently and hands only ever lie badly.

Vivian Ashcombe's hands did not so much as pause around her teacup. But they went, very slightly, *still* — the stillness of an animal that has heard a sound in the hedge and is deciding whether it's the wind.

"How curious," she said. "A will from the future. Do you practice law or science fiction, Mr. Pemberton?"

"Both, some weeks." He smiled, because it cost nothing and because a smiling man is a man people underestimate, and being underestimated was the only inheritance Kestrel had ever actively cultivated. "The will concerns the Ashcombe estate. A trust I haven't been able to trace. Beneficiaries who are, as of this morning, all alive. And drafting conventions that suggest the hand of someone trained at one of perhaps four firms in England."

"What an unpleasant thing to find," she said. Her voice was warm and lightly amused, a voice made for drawing rooms, and Kestrel noticed that she had not asked him the obvious question — *how did this document come to you?* — and that not asking it was, in its way, an answer.

"Unpleasant," Kestrel agreed. "And strange. And it made me wonder about your late husband's own will. And his death."

"His death was his heart," Vivian said. "It had been threatening to resign for years. Alistair never listened to it. He didn't listen to me either, for that matter, which may be why the heart and I had so much in common." She sipped her tea. "There were doctors. A coroner. A very nice man from the insurance company who cried a little, which I found touching. Everything was natural, Mr. Pemberton. Everything was in order. Everything was *signed*."

The last word arrived with a small, precise weight, like a coin placed exactly on a scale.

"Signed by whom?" Kestrel asked.

"By everyone who signs things," she said pleasantly. "That's rather the nature of signatures, isn't it? They make things official. And once a thing is official, it becomes very rude to keep asking about it." She set down her cup and turned those winter-grey eyes on him fully for the first time, and Kestrel felt the sensation — familiar from cross-examinations, never comfortable — of being looked *through* rather than at. "Tell me, Mr. Pemberton. Who sent you? Not who hired you. Who *sent* you. There's a difference. The people who hire lawyers want documents. The people who send them want to know what the documents will cost."

"I retained myself," Kestrel said. "A client of mine has an unusual problem, and the trail led here."

"Ah. A client." She smiled, and it was a lovely smile, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "How I envy you that. Having clients. Having a *practice*. A little office somewhere with files in it, and the files stay where you put them, and the law behaves like the law." She looked out the window at the ivy advancing on the east wing. "This house has been in Alistair's family for two hundred and six years, Mr. Pemberton. Do you know what that means? It means that for two hundred and six years, someone in this family has owed someone else something unspeakable, and paid it, and called the payment *tradition*. Alistair used to say the estate wasn't land. It was a *ledger*." Her voice did not change at all. "A joke, of course. Alistair was fond of jokes."

"Was he," Kestrel said. "Was he fond of them a month before he died?"

The stillness came back. Longer this time. Outside, the moorhen made its opinion known about something.

"You're better at this than you look," Vivian said at last, and there was — Kestrel would turn the phrase over for days afterward — something almost like approval in it. "You look like a man who does conveyancing for retired dentists. And here you are, asking a widow whether her husband's jokes had a shelf life." She rose, crossed to the window, and stood with her back to him, and when she spoke again her voice had shed its drawing-room warmth and become something quieter and harder, like stone under the moss. "I'll tell you what I told the coroner, because it happens to be true. Alistair Ashcombe died of his heart. What killed his heart is not the sort of thing that goes on certificates."

"Mrs. Ashcombe—"

"In the last month of his life," she went on, as though he hadn't spoken, "my husband burned papers in the study. Three nights. I could see the light under the door. He was a man who never burned so much as a shopping list — he kept forty years of diaries in the library, dull as gravel, every one. And then, in his last month, he burned things and drank more than usual and said, twice, the same strange sentence." She turned. "He said, *It's all written down, and that's the problem.*"

The fire ticked. Somewhere in the house a door closed, though Kestrel was fairly sure they were alone in it.

"And then he died," Kestrel said.

"And then he died," Vivian agreed, "naturally, and officially, and everything was signed." She came back to her chair and sat, and the drawing-room warmth returned to her voice like a lamp being relit, so smoothly that Kestrel might have imagined the other voice entirely, if he hadn't spent thirty years making his living on the difference between the two. "Now. I've told you a story, Mr. Pemberton, and you've told me nothing, and I find I mind that less than I expected. So here is my offer. Come back and see me when you know more. Come back when you've found the man with the briefcase — you'll know the one I mean when you find him — and tell me what he looks like, and I'll tell you what Alistair was afraid of." She stood, ending the audience the way a judge ends one. "And Mr. Pemberton? Whatever your client paid you, it wasn't enough. It's never enough. That's rather the theme."

* * *

The drive back was long and wet and gave Kestrel ample time to conduct the argument with himself that had been running in the background since the impossible will had crossed his desk.

The sensible position — and Kestrel was, by profession and temperament, a sensible man — was that he had a frail elderly client with an eccentric fear, a forged document of unknown origin, and a widow who spoke in riddles because grief made people strange. Nothing that required him. Nothing that couldn't be filed, billed, and forgotten. He had built his entire career on the principle that the wise lawyer is the one history never has occasion to mention.

But.

But a will dated three years in the future. But a widow who had laid out two teacups before he arrived and who had said *the man with the briefcase* as though the phrase had been waiting in her mouth for a year. But *it's all written down, and that's the problem*, and a man burning papers in the dark, and then a heart, and then a signature, and then silence.

Kestrel was not a romantic. He did not believe in coincidences, however, and the absence of belief in coincidences is, for a lawyer, a kind of romanticism all its own.

By the time the city lights came up out of the rain, he had made his decision, which was to make no decision, which is to say: he would follow the documents. Documents were safe. Documents were his native country. And the next document on his list was the one thing he could check quietly, without telling anyone, without being anyone: the registry records for every death with even a marginal connection to the Ashcombe name.

It was, he would reflect later, the last quiet evening he would have for a very long time.

Because when he pulled the first file — a cousin of the late patriarch, dead fourteen months ago, cause of death: natural — he found the certificate, and it was perfectly ordinary, and perfectly in order, and perfectly signed.

And beneath it in the registry box, misfiled with the bland innocence of a thing hidden in plain sight, was a second certificate for the same death. Same name. Same date.

Different cause.
