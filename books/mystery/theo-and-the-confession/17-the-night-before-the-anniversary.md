## 17. The Night Before the Anniversary

The message came the way the important ones always did: small, damp, and smelling faintly of pigeons.

It was tucked into the morning post among a gas bill and a letter from a firm of accountants whose name I did not recognize and would later wish I'd paid more attention to. A single card, cream-colored, no envelope. On it, in a hand that shook like a man writing in a moving vehicle, or a man writing while his hands were afraid:

*St. Anselm's. The side chapel. After dark. Come alone. Come as a mourner.*

No signature. He didn't need one. There is a particular quality to Edmund Voss's handwriting — a kind of aristocratic apology in every letter — that I had come to know the way one knows a voice on the telephone. And beneath the card, folded twice, was a pressed lily. White, brittle, the kind sold in bundles of six outside churches for funerals and weddings and the occasional apology.

I sat at my kitchen table with the card and the flower and my tea going cold, and I considered, as I had considered every day since his flat stood empty, what it means to be asked for help by a man who paid you in advance to prevent his own murder.

Marguerite would have said don't go. Marguerite had, in fact, been saying don't go to me for two weeks in increasingly creative grammatical constructions. *Don't go* had evolved into *I'd advise against going*, which had evolved into the lawyer's final form, *I note for the record that I advised against going.* She had a gift for covering herself the way other people covered furniture.

But Marguerite wasn't holding a pressed lily. Marguerite hadn't spent three nights decoding a dead mentor's shorthand only to arrive at a cipher that required a key neither of us possessed. Marguerite hadn't watched an old man's name sit in the ledger's final column, penciled in, dated for tomorrow night — the Ashcombe anniversary dinner — with the tidy patience of an accountant booking a future expense.

I put the card in my breast pocket, where it sat over my heart like a small, cream-colored accusation, and I went to work, and I did no work at all, and at half past six I took a cab toward St. Anselm's.

* * *

St. Anselm's is the kind of church that cities grow around and then forget. Wedged between a wine merchant and a building whose purpose has changed four times in living memory, it has a tower that leans a degree or two — not enough to alarm anyone, enough to suggest the building had long ago made its peace with imperfection. The side chapel is dedicated to Saint Jude, patron of lost causes, which struck me as either a coincidence or a very dry joke on someone's part.

I had done as instructed. I came alone — Marguerite believed I was reviewing files at home, a lie I told her with the smoothness of a man who has recently had a great deal of practice. I came as a mourner: dark coat, dark scarf, the expression of a man carrying a grief he doesn't wish to discuss. It was, I reflected, not much of a performance. I had been carrying exactly that expression for a month.

The nave was dim and nearly empty. A woman in a green coat sat three rows from the front, praying with the efficient urgency of someone with a train to catch. A verger stacked hymnals with the tenderness some men reserve for grandchildren. Candlelight did its ancient work on the stone, making everything look both older and more forgiving than it was.

The side chapel was behind a screen of carved wood. I went through it and found him.

Edmund Voss was sitting in the front pew of Saint Jude's chapel with his back very straight, the way men sit when slumping has become dangerous. He had lost weight — the good coat hung off him like a borrowed opinion — and his hands, those careful, manicured, triple-paying hands, were folded in his lap and trembling in a way I suspected was no longer entirely voluntary. There was a bruise along his jaw, yellowing at the edges, the color of old parchment. His eyes found mine and something in them both broke and mended at once.

"You came," he said. "I wasn't certain. I gave you every reason not to."

"You paid in advance," I said. "I've never yet abandoned a retainer."

The ghost of a smile. "That," he said, "is the finest quality I know of in a lawyer, and the rarest."

I sat beside him. Up close he smelled of woodsmoke and unwashed wool and, beneath it, the faint chemical sweetness of a hospital. I didn't ask. There would be time for the asking, or there wouldn't.

"They took me," he said, before I could. "After I left the flat. Two men, very polite, very apologetic. They drove me to a house in the country and explained to me, over several excellent meals, that I was an old man with an overactive imagination, and that if I persisted in my delusions I would be gently, lovingly, permanently institutionalized. My own family signed the papers, Kestrel. My nephew wept while he did it. I think the tears were genuine. That's the thing about them — they never ask anyone to do anything they don't already want to do."

"Where have you been since?"

"Escaping." He said it simply, the way another man might say *retiring*. "It took eleven days. I'm slower than I was. But I have spent eighty-one years being underestimated, and it remains the most reliable advantage available to a man of my age." He turned the bruised jaw toward me. "The last stair was slippery."

"The chapel," I said. "Why here?"

"Because it's the one place in this city where Ashe cannot follow me without being seen." He nodded toward the screen. "The verger, Mr. Dunmore, has sat in that pew every evening for thirty years. He watches the door the way a lighthouse watches the sea. And because—" his voice did something complicated— "because I was married in this church. Fifty-three years ago. My wife believed Saint Jude was sentimental nonsense and prayed to him anyway. I thought, if I was going to be frightened, I would prefer to be frightened somewhere she had been happy."

I found, to my surprise, that my throat had gone tight. I am not a sentimental man by profession. But there is a kind of courage that looks, from the outside, exactly like a tired old man in a cold church, and I have never learned how to witness it without cost.

"The ledger," I said. "The final column is in a different hand. A shorthand — Marguerite's mentor's shorthand, we think, the same cipher as the rest. We can read the names and the dates. We can't read the terms. The conditions. Whatever it is Ashe needs from tomorrow night."

"Ah." Voss closed his eyes. "Yes. That would be my fault. Or my doing. Depending on how you keep accounts."

* * *

He told it in the manner of a man who has rehearsed a confession for a long time and is determined not to enjoy the relief of it.

"I was the cipher," he said. "Not the author — that was Aldous Crane, Marguerite's mentor, God rest his compromised soul. But a cipher is useless without a key, and keys must live somewhere. Crane kept his in his head and died with it, conveniently. I was his second. I was the redundancy. He taught me the substitution the year I turned fifty, over brandy, as a joke between professionals. *One day, Edmund, you may need to read what we've written.* I laughed. I was fifty and foolish and flattered to be trusted."

"And the key?"

"Is not a word or a number. Crane was too clever for that. It is a habit." He opened his eyes. "The ledger's dates are all written in Crane's private calendar — a liturgical one. He was a churchgoer, did you know? Quietly. Ashamed of it, almost, as though faith were another of his vices. Every date in that ledger is anchored not to the civil calendar but to the feast days. Saint Jude, the twenty-eighth of October. The anniversary dinner is set for tomorrow because tomorrow is the eve of Saints Simon and Jude — the founding date of the Ashcombe trust, sixty years ago. Crane thought it funny. He thought everything was funny; that was his tragedy. The numbers you've been reading are offsets. Count the days from the nearest feast day and the true dates emerge. And the terms—" his trembling hands steadied, deliberately, the way a man steadies a rifle— "the terms are read by taking every third word of the entry, beginning with the third. Crane's little joke again. *Thirds.* He always said the law was one-third truth, one-third paper, and one-third nerve, and only the nerve ever mattered."

I sat very still while the architecture of it assembled itself in my head. A month of Marguerite's careful decoding, and the whole thing had been waiting on a church calendar and a counting game. "The terms," I said. "What do they say? For tomorrow. For you."

Voss looked at the altar, at the small red lamp burning there with its patient flame.

"That I die at the dinner," he said. "Not violently — they've learned their lesson, violence makes paperwork. That I am taken ill. That a doctor — there is always a doctor — attends me in the study, and that a certificate is signed before my body reaches a hospital. That my nephew, who has been promised a house he cannot afford, witnesses my *lucid and voluntary* revision of my will, drafted by Ashe, which leaves the Voss holdings to the trust. And that you, Kestrel—" here he turned to me, and his eyes were dry and terribly clear— "are named in it as the supervising counsel. My *choice* of lawyer. They want your signature, not your silence. Your silence they could simply buy. Your signature makes the whole machine legitimate. It's very elegant. I almost admire it."

The candle flame bent in some small movement of air. I thought about the invitation that had arrived addressed to me by name, and understood it at last — not a threat. A summons. I had been invited to my own complicity.

"They'll kill me if I refuse," I said. It was not a question.

"They'll do something worse. They'll make you respectable." Voss's mouth twisted. "I've watched it happen to better men. You'd have the house, the partnership, the erasures. And in ten years you'd be Aldous Crane, teaching some bright young thing the cipher over brandy, and telling yourself it was only paper."

* * *

We sat in the quiet of Saint Jude's chapel for a long moment, two frightened men and one small red lamp.

"Give me the key," I said finally. "The feast days, the thirds. All of it. I'll write it down—"

"You'll do no such thing." He said it with the first real firmness I'd heard from him. "You'll keep it where Crane kept his. In your head. That is the entire point of a key, Kestrel — it must be carried, not stored. If they find it written down, they find it. If they find it in your head, they find a lawyer who knows nothing they can prove." He paused. "Besides. There is one more thing. The reason I asked you here tonight and not tomorrow morning."

He drew something from inside his coat: a thin envelope, soft with handling. Inside was a single sheet of paper — not the ledger's paper, older, and folded around a small brass key, the kind that opens a solicitor's strongbox.

"Crane's contingency," Voss said. "He was a monster, but he was a careful monster, and careful monsters keep insurance. This key opens deposit box 214 at the Chancery Safe Deposit Company. What's inside, I don't know. Crane told me only this: *if the ledger is ever read, the box will finish the sentence.*" He pressed it into my hand. His fingers were cold. "I was meant to be the redundancy. Now you are. That's what happens to people who get close to this thing, Kestrel — they get promoted."

I looked down at the brass key in my palm. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"Tomorrow night," I said, "at that dinner—"

"You will not be there." His voice cracked like a gavel. "I paid you to prevent a murder, not to attend one. You'll take the key, you'll open the box, and you'll be far away when they pour the wine—"

"Edmund." I said his name, and he stopped. "You hired me because I'm discreet. Let me tell you what a month of discretion has taught me. There is no version of tomorrow in which I stay away. If I run, they sign your certificate with some other lawyer's pen. If I stay, then at least when they raise their glasses, someone at that table will know exactly what they're drinking to." I closed my hand around the key. "You paid in advance. Consider this the invoice."

He looked at me for a long time. Outside, somewhere in the dark, a bell rang the quarter hour — St. Anselm's, leaning, imperfect, keeping its time anyway.

"You are either the bravest man I have ever retained," Voss said at last, "or the biggest fool. I find, at eighty-one, I can no longer tell the difference, and I no longer believe there is one." He rose, slowly, using the pew's edge, and stood straighter than his body had any right to allow. "Then we are agreed. Tomorrow night, the dinner proceeds. Let them set their table. Let them pour their wine. Let them pencil my name in their ledger one final time."

"And?"

"And tomorrow, Mr. Pemberton," said Edmund Voss, and for one moment the frailty fell away from him like a coat, "we give them something to read."

He pressed his hat to his chest — the old-fashioned gesture of a man taking leave of a church, or beginning one last argument — and I saw, over his shoulder, in the doorway of the chapel, a figure in a dark coat pause, glance in, and move on.

I did not recognize the walk.

But I recognized the briefcase.
