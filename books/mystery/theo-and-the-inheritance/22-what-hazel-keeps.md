## 22. What Hazel Keeps

The badge came back to me in a padded envelope eleven months after I mailed it away, with a form letter from the state board thanking me for my service and informing me that my certification as coroner of Marrow Creek had been formally revoked, effective the previous spring. They'd enclosed a second envelope, pre-addressed, in case I wished to appeal.

I used it for kindling.

Not out of bitterness. Out of arithmetic. An appeal would take two years, a hearing, a lawyer I couldn't afford, and a panel of strangers deciding whether telling the truth was a fireable offense. I already knew how that vote would go. I'd watched nine families learn the same lesson over four decades: the system isn't broken. It's working exactly as designed, and the design was drawn up in 1911 by a man named Ambrose Hollis on the back of a land deed.

So I lit the letter in the sink of my mother's old kitchen—the kitchen where I'd grown up believing she'd chosen to leave me—and watched the ash curl into the drain like something finally being released from custody.

What surprised me wasn't losing the badge. What surprised me was how little I missed it.

* * *

The office sits above the old pharmacy on Corben Street, two rooms with good north light and a radiator that knocks like a polite ghost. The sign went up in October, hand-painted by Ruthie Calloway, the diner cook's daughter, who turned out to have steady hands and a grief she'd been carrying since she was seven without ever knowing what to call it.

**OYELARAN DEATH VERIFICATION**
*Independent review for families with questions.*

Ruthie wanted to add *No question too small.* I told her every question is too big until someone answers it, so we left it off.

Business was slow at first, which I expected. Marrow Creek doesn't like to be reminded that it has dead people with unfinished sentences attached. But then word traveled the way word does—through church basements and diner booths and the long memory of women who keep receipts—and by spring I had three cases. By summer, eleven. Families from two counties over, driving in with shoeboxes of photographs and hospital paperwork and the specific, terrible hope of people who were told *accident* and never believed it.

I don't investigate anymore, not exactly. Investigation implies suspicion, and suspicion implies an adversary, and what I do now is quieter than that. I verify. I read a death certificate the way Theo reads a headstone—looking for the sentence underneath the sentence. I request records through channels that no longer require Sheriff Brandt's permission, because Brandt is in federal custody awaiting trial and his replacement, a tired honest woman named Okafor transferred in from Carthage County, signs whatever lawful request crosses her desk with the relief of someone handed a shovel after years of digging with her hands.

Most of what I find is nothing. A mislabeled fall. A heart attack recorded as drowning because nobody wanted to order the autopsy. Ordinary failures, ordinary grief. I write careful letters explaining what the record shows and what it doesn't, and I charge what people can pay, which is sometimes money and sometimes eggs and once, memorably, a 1987 Buick that runs when it wants to.

But twice this year I've found something. Twice I've written letters that ended with the words *I recommend you contact the state attorney general,* and both times the families did, and both times the machinery that ground my mother's family flat ground a little differently afterward. Not justice. Justice is a word for courthouses. But correction. Correction I can do.

Theo does the rest.

* * *

He's seventeen now, taller, still growing into his hands the way a puppy grows into its paws. His father came back in the winter—not home, exactly, but back—renting the room above the feed store and showing up on Sundays to help Theo maintain the cemetery grounds under a work-release arrangement that Judge Pelloran worked out with a patience I still don't fully understand. Some Sundays the two of them work all morning without speaking, and some Sundays they speak the whole time, and Theo says both kinds count.

"He's teaching me the old plots," Theo told me last month, spreading plot maps across my desk like a general deploying troops. "The ones from before the ledger. Dad knows where everybody actually is. Turns out there's a whole second history under the first one."

"There usually is."

"That's what you always say." He grinned. "You should put it on the sign."

His apprenticeship isn't official—there's no license for what I do, which is either the tragedy or the freedom of it—but he keeps our files now, cross-indexed the way he cross-indexed the cemetery records, and he's better at spotting patterns than I am. He found the duplicate payment in the Pruitt file that cracked open case number six. He noticed that the county archive's fire report listed accelerant residue in a pattern inconsistent with electrical origin, which became exhibit A in the arson charge against the man Brandt hired, which became the thread that unraveled Brandt himself.

The boy who reads stones reads paper just as well. Sometimes I think he reads people better than I do, though I'd never tell him. His head is already heavy enough with what he carries.

Because he carries it. That's the thing nobody warned either of us about. We won—we got Marguerite Hollis convicted on nine counts of conspiracy to commit murder, got the trust dissolved, got the protection racket named in a state report that ran four hundred pages—but winning didn't put anything down. It just set everything down gently, in order, where we could see it. Grief doesn't close like a case file. It stays open on the desk forever, and you learn to work around it, and some days you don't.

Marguerite died in March. Heart failure, in the medical wing of the state facility, alone except for a nurse who said her last words were *the grounds need attention.* No service. Her surviving relatives declined to claim the body, so the county buried her in potter's field, and when the notice crossed my desk—for verification, routine—I signed it and felt nothing I expected to feel. No triumph. No pity either. Just the strange flat fact of a woman who spent ninety-one years pruning a town and was pruned herself by something no trust could buy off.

Theo asked me once if I hated her. I thought about it honestly, which is the only way I know how to think about things anymore.

"I hate what she made," I said. "She was just the hands."

"And the town?"

"The town is what we make next."

* * *

My mother's grave is on the east slope, third row, under a sycamore that drops its leaves early every year like it's eager for the season to change. The original marker—*BELOVED WIFE AND MOTHER, TAKEN TOO SOON*—is gone. In its place stands a stone we cut from the same gray granite as the others, because Theo insisted she shouldn't stand out; standing out was never what she wanted.

It reads:

**ADELAIDE OKAFOR-OYELARAN**
*1951–1994*
*Teacher. Gardener. She asked questions.*
*Her daughter finished them.*

We moved her in June, legally this time, with permits and a judge's signature and Theo's father standing at the edge of the hole with his hat in his hands, saying the only prayer he remembered from his grandmother, which was in Yoruba and which he half-remembered wrong, and which was therefore perfect. She'd been where the record said she was after all—in the end, the 1994 move was ordered but never carried out, the gravedigger paid and then spared the act by his own cowardice, a mercy neither of us knew how to feel about. So we didn't move her far. Just deep enough to lay the corrected documents beneath the casket liner: the true findings, the ledger pages, the letter I wrote her when I was thirty-four and never sent, addressed to a woman I'd been told abandoned me and who had instead been taken, asking questions, right up until asking questions stopped being safe in Marrow Creek.

Her daughter finished them. That part is true. The rest of the stone is true too, and if it's plainer than most markers, well. Plainness is a kind of honesty, and I've developed a taste for it.

I go on Thursdays. I bring coffee I don't drink and pull weeds I don't need to pull, and sometimes I talk to her about the cases, and sometimes I just sit, and the sycamore does its early falling, and the town moves around us below—smaller now, poorer, but awake in a way it hadn't been in eighty years. There's a memorial garden going in behind the courthouse, nine beds, one for each name. Ruthie manages it. She says the marigolds won't behave themselves, and I tell her that's fitting.

* * *

Last Tuesday a woman came to the office whom I didn't recognize, mid-fifties, holding a manila folder against her chest the way you hold something that might be taken from you. She sat down across from me and Theo and didn't open the folder.

"My brother died in 2009," she said. "Carthage County. They called it a single-vehicle accident. He didn't own a car."

Theo looked at me. I looked at the folder. Outside, the radiator knocked its polite knock, and somewhere below us the pharmacy bell rang for a customer, ordinary as breathing.

"Tell me his name," I said.

And she did.

And I reached for a pen.
