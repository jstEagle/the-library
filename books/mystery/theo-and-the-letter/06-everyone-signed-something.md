## 6. Everyone Signed Something

The registry office in Greywater Bay kept hours that suggested it had never once been hurried: nine to noon, one to four, closed Wednesdays. Oscar arrived at 8:40 on a Thursday and waited on the steps with his coffee going cold, watching the fog lift off the harbor the way it did every morning here — reluctantly, like something being asked to leave.

Inside, the clerk was a woman of about sixty with reading glasses on a beaded chain and a cardigan the color of oatmeal. She looked at him for a long moment before speaking.

"You're the insurance man."

"Routine claims review," Oscar said. "Harbor policies. Boring stuff."

"Mm." She turned to the shelves behind her without another word, which he understood was the local dialect for *I know exactly who you are and I've decided to let you find out what I know at my own pace.*

He asked for corporate filings. She brought him a box.

Greywater Bay, population 4,100, had been incorporated in 1911 by four families whose names were still on everything — the Cannery Row sign, the school gymnasium, the bronze plaque by the pier. Wren. Crane. Halloway. Mercer. Four names carved into the town like initials into a tree, growing more prominent as the bark swelled around them.

Oscar worked through the filings methodically, photographing pages with his phone, letting his memory do what it always did — hold everything, perfectly, whether he wanted it or not. It was a gift people envied until they saw what it cost. He could not skim. He could not forget. Every page he read lived in him forever, filed and cross-referenced, waiting.

At 11:20, he found it.

*Greywater Holdings, Ltd.* Incorporated March 1999 — three months after Samuel Adeyemi's conviction was upheld on appeal. A holding company with no employees, no premises beyond a registered agent's address, and no stated purpose except "investment."

Its shareholders were listed in the original articles. Forty-one names.

Oscar sat very still.

Wren — eleven shares. Crane — fourteen. Halloway — nine. Mercer — eight. And then the rest, scattered across the founding families' branches like roots under a lawn: cousins, in-laws, second marriages. The fishmonger. The dentist. Two retired teachers. The woman who ran the bakery where he'd bought a roll yesterday and been told, warmly, how much everyone had loved Elias Wren.

Forty-one shareholders. Forty-one signatures on the articles of incorporation, dated ninety days after a dockworker went to prison for a crime the evidence never supported.

He pulled the later filings. Annual returns, dutifully submitted, showing dividends paid out every single year — modest sums, five figures, nothing flashy. Discreet. Compounding. Twenty-five years of quiet money flowing to forty-one households in a town that had forgotten, on purpose, why the money existed.

Hush money didn't have to be large. It only had to be regular. A tide of its own.

Oscar photographed every page twice.

* * *

Dana Voss knew he'd found it before he said a word.

She came to the hotel that evening, out of uniform, in a canvas jacket, carrying a paper bag from the chowder place as if this were a social call. She set the bag on the table between them and didn't sit.

"Registry closes at four," she said. "You were the last signature in the book."

"Public records."

"Nothing's public here. There's just records nobody asks about." She looked at the window, at the dark harbor beyond it. "You found Greywater Holdings."

It wasn't a question, so Oscar didn't answer it. He poured her coffee she hadn't asked for and waited. That was a trick he'd learned from watching interrogators better than himself: silence is a door, and most people walk through it eventually.

Dana walked through it faster than most.

"I was nineteen when Adeyemi was arrested," she said. "My father signed the articles. I didn't know what they were until I made constable and pulled the old case file out of curiosity, and Crane — Aldous Crane, he was lead investigator then — he called me into his office and explained, very kindly, that curiosity was a career-limiting quality in Greywater Bay." Her jaw tightened. "He wasn't cruel about it. That's the worst part. He was *kind*. Like a doctor explaining a terminal diagnosis."

"And you stopped being curious."

"For twenty years." She finally sat, and the chair seemed to take weight off her shoulders and put it somewhere worse. "Then Elias drowned, and you showed up, and I watched you ask questions nobody's asked since '98, and I thought — either this man dies too, or something changes. And I'm tired, Okafor. I'm tired of being the kind of person my father raised."

She reached into the canvas jacket and set a sealed envelope on the table. Manila, government-issue, stamped PERSONNEL — CONFIDENTIAL along the edge in faded red.

"What is it?"

"Crane's file. From when he was our chief investigator. Medical leave in 2001 — psychiatric evaluation, after a suspect in a drowning case died during questioning. Internal review cleared him. The review was conducted by—" she almost smiled, and it was a terrible smile, "—Aldous Crane. But the evaluator's notes are in there. Original notes. The ones that got summarized away."

"Why give me this? You know what happens to you if anyone finds out."

Dana looked at him for a long moment, and Oscar recognized the expression — he'd seen it in mirrors, in the months after his suspension, in the faces of people deciding whether the truth was worth what it cost.

"Because you're going to publish it anyway," she said. "Whatever you find. You're not the type to bury it, and God knows this town has buried enough. So I'd rather be standing next to you when it comes out than hiding behind my father's name when it does." She stood, buttoning the jacket. "Belonging here has a price, Okafor. I've been paying it since I was nineteen. Consider this my last installment."

At the door she paused, hand on the frame, not turning around.

"The third letter will come soon. Whoever's writing them — they'll want to meet you. When it comes, don't go alone, and don't go where they choose." A beat. "And Okafor — the envelope. Read the evaluator's notes tonight. Page six especially."

Then she was gone, and the fog was coming back in off the water, patient as everything else in this town.

* * *

Page six.

Oscar read it three times, though once would have been enough — his memory didn't blur, didn't fade, didn't grant him the mercy of forgetting. Dr. Eileen Marsh, consulting psychologist, handwritten notes, dated November 14, 2001:

*Subject displays rigid need for narrative control. Does not falsify records — instead curates them, omitting until the record tells the story he requires. Describes investigations as "authorship." When pressed on the Halvorsen drowning, subject became calm — unnaturally calm — and stated: "The water is the perfect witness. It never contradicts you, and it never testifies twice."*

*Recommendation: removal from active casework involving water-related deaths.*

Cleared anyway. Promoted, actually. Retired with honors in 2011, a plaque in the station house, a name on the gymnasium donor wall between Wren and Mercer.

Oscar set the page down and looked out at the black harbor, and let the whole architecture assemble itself in his head, floor by floor, the way it always did — the shell company paying forty-one families to stay quiet about a wrongful conviction; the retired investigator who authored cases rather than solved them; a stranger's body wearing Elias Wren's name; and letters, arriving on schedule, scripted by someone who understood that a confession could be bait as easily as an admission.

Someone was still writing this story. Someone who believed the water was the perfect witness.

His phone buzzed against the table.

An envelope icon. An email, no sender, routed through a relay that would take a warrant to unwrap. One line, centered, in a font that mimicked typewriter keys:

*You've been reading other people's files, Mr. Okafor. Tomorrow, low tide, 6:12 a.m., come alone to the lighthouse, and I'll show you mine. Bring page six. — M.W.*

Oscar stared at it for a long time.

*M.W.* Marion Wren.

And beneath the signature line, almost an afterthought, a single sentence that turned his blood to cold seawater:

*P.S. Constable Voss should stay home. Last person who helped an outsider needed rescuing from the water, and the water doesn't always oblige.*

He looked at the clock. 11:47 p.m. Low tide, 6:12 a.m. Six hours and twenty-five minutes to decide whether the third letter was a confession, a trap —

— or both.
