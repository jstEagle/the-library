## 7. The Third Letter Is a Summons

The envelope was waiting for him at the front desk of the Saltline Hotel, and Oscar knew before he touched it.

It wasn't the handwriting — though the handwriting had become familiar now, that careful, schoolmaster's copperplate that belonged to no one and everyone. It was the timing. The second letter had arrived four days ago, and in those four days he had walked the shoreline, found the tarp fragment, watched a shed burn to wet ash, and said nothing to anyone except Dana Voss. He had been careful. He had been quiet. And still the letters came, as if the writer lived inside his days, taking notes.

"You've got an admirer," the desk clerk said, sliding it across. "Third one this week somebody's left for you. Town's starting to talk."

"What do they say?"

"That you're either an insurance man or a priest." She smiled the rehearsed Greywater Bay smile. "Hard to tell the difference sometimes. Both of them come to count what you've lost."

He carried the envelope upstairs, sat on the bed with the lamp on against the grey afternoon, and opened it with the letter opener he'd bought at the hardware store after the second letter, because Dana had told him — deadpan, not quite joking — that he should stop using his fingers on things people wanted him to touch.

The paper inside was the same heavy cream stock. The same ink. But the letter was longer this time, and it began differently.

*Mr. Okafor.*

*You have been reading a confession. It is time you knew whose it is.*

*My name is Marion Wren. I was married to Elias Wren for nineteen years, and I buried him in a box that does not hold him, in front of a town that sang a hymn it did not mean, and I have been the widow they wanted me to be ever since. Grief is a costume this town hands you at the door. I wore mine well. They stopped watching me by the second week.*

*So I will tell you what I did, and you will believe me, because you have already verified everything else.*

*I helped stage his drowning.*

The words sat on the page with the terrible calm of a signed statement. Oscar read on, his photographic memory laying each sentence down beside the others — the first letter, the second, the case file he could still see whole, page by page, in the dark behind his eyes.

*Elias found the papers first. The Adeyemi papers. His father had kept them — a dockworker's son keeping receipts, that was Elias all over — and when he understood what the founding families had been paying each other to bury since 1998, he did what he always did with a thing he couldn't lift alone. He went to the water. He said the sea keeps honest books. He said we'd put the truth in a bottle and let the tide testify.*

*That was the plan. Not a death. A disappearance with an audience. Elias would be gone, and the papers would surface, and the town would have to look at what it had signed. I was to be the grieving widow who found the letters he left behind. I wrote the first confession under his direction, word for word, because he said a confession is the only document no one audits. People believe guilt. It's the one currency that never gets questioned.*

*But the man who drowned in Greywater Bay was not Elias.*

*I need you to understand that, Mr. Okafor. I stood on that pier and I watched the water take a stranger, and I did not know his name until the dental records came back wrong, and by then it was too late to say so, because the moment that body surfaced, our plan stopped being ours. Someone else had written a scene into our play. Someone who knew the tide table better than Elias did — better than anyone should — and who put a man in the water where my husband was supposed to swim.*

*The second letter was true. I was coerced. Not with threats. With corrections. Every draft I sent, something came back amended — a date, a detail, a fact only the investigation could know — until I understood that whoever was holding the pen knew the file better than I did, and knew it because he wrote it. You've felt it too. I could tell by the way you walked the shoreline at low tide, alone, at the hour the first letter predicted. You are being fed, Mr. Okafor. We both are. The only question is which of us is meant to be eaten.*

*Meet me at the old lighthouse on Fenwick Point. Thursday. The tide turns at 6:40; come at slack water, when the channel is calm enough for the boat. Come alone. If you bring the constable, or anyone from the mainland, I will know before you reach the point, and you will never hear from me again — and neither will the truth about the man in Elias's grave.*

*He has a name, Mr. Okafor. I found it in Elias's papers. The dead man in my husband's coffin has a name, and a mother in Accra who has been writing to this town for twenty-five years about a different missing man, and no one has ever written back.*

*Come at slack water. Come alone. And Mr. Okafor — if a fourth letter arrives before Thursday, in my handwriting, changing anything I have written here, then you will know I am no longer the one holding the pen.*

*— M. W.*

Oscar sat with the letter for a long time after he finished it, the lamp buzzing, the sea making its slow argument against the seawall below the window.

Then he did what he always did. He closed his eyes and let the file assemble itself — every letter, every line, side by side in the white room of his memory.

The first letter had been a confession, precise as an autopsy. The second had amended it: *someone else held the pen.* The third named the pen's holder as the writer herself, and named the coercion as something gentler and worse — collaboration, then correction, then capture. Each letter had been true in the way a funhouse mirror is true. Each one had moved him exactly where the writer wanted him: to the shoreline, to the pier, to the burned shed, to the growing conviction that the case was waiting for him specifically.

And now to a lighthouse, alone, at slack water, on the word of a woman who had just admitted — in her own hand, in her own name — to staging her husband's death.

*The confession was bait all along.*

He said it out loud to the empty room, and the room agreed with him, the way empty rooms do.

Because here was what his memory kept returning to, the splinter in the whole elegant story: the first letter had been postmarked the day after Elias Wren's funeral. A grieving widow, deep in a staged disappearance, mailing a confession she'd written under her husband's direction — to him. To Oscar Okafor. An insurance investigator on forced leave, sorting cold cases in a regional office four hundred miles away. Not the police. Not the coroner. Not the *Greywater Bay Sentinel*. Him, by name, in an office no one in this town should have had reason to know existed.

Unless someone did know. Unless someone had read the same training manuals Oscar had, the ones written by a lead investigator named Aldous Crane, the ones that taught a whole generation of investigators that the confession is never the end of the case — it's the instrument. That you don't catch a town in a lie with evidence. You catch it with a story it can't resist telling about itself.

*He wrote the playbook,* Oscar thought, and his hands went cold. *Whatever is happening here, someone is running it out of a manual I was trained on.*

He thought about the dental records. The stranger in Elias's grave, a man whose name was supposedly in papers Elias had kept — papers that had not appeared in any search, any seizure, any inventory Oscar had seen. Papers that existed, so far, only because a letter said so. The mother in Accra. The twenty-five years of unwritten replies. It was all true, he suspected. That was the horror of it. Crane — if it was Crane — never lied. He just arranged the truth into a corridor and let you walk down it, admiring the walls, all the way to the room with the lock on the outside.

Dana Voss. He should tell Dana. The thought arrived and was immediately, carefully set aside — because the letter had named her, had said *come alone,* and because he remembered the way she'd passed him the personnel file without a word, the fear in her that was older and larger than her loyalty. He didn't doubt her. That was what made it dangerous. Crane would know he didn't doubt her. Crane would know that the one person Oscar trusted was the one channel he'd never check.

*Come at slack water.*

Oscar looked at the window. Below it, the bay lay flat and pewter-coloured, the tide somewhere in the middle of its long exhale. Thursday was two days away. Fenwick Point was visible from his window, a black finger of land with the lighthouse on it, white paint gone the colour of old bone, its lamp room dark for thirty years.

He took out a fresh sheet of the hotel's stationery and, for the first time in his career, wrote a letter of his own. Not to send. To leave. He wrote down everything the third letter had told him, everything he believed it had withheld, and the one thing it had given him that he was certain the writer hadn't intended: the admission that Marion Wren was reachable, frightened, and almost certainly not free. Then he folded it into the lining of his suitcase, under the cardboard, where a man who knew every playbook ever written would never think to look — because it wasn't in any playbook. It was just a man's memory of his own father, who had grown up on a street where a family named Adeyemi had once lived, and who had taught him that you write things down because paper outlives the people who want you quiet.

* * *

Thursday came in grey and windless, the sea so calm it looked rehearsed.

Okafor left the hotel at five, walked the coast road north with the letter in his inside pocket and nothing else on him but a flashlight, a tide table, and the small stubborn hope that he was walking into this with his eyes open. The path to Fenwick Point was overgrown, the garrison's old fence rusted into lace. The lighthouse rose against the darkening east, and beneath it, at the base of the rocks, a small boat was already tied — and beside the boat, wrapped in a coat against the cold, a woman stood watching him come.

Marion Wren was smaller than the photographs in the file. Her hair was grey at the temples, her face the face of someone who had been rehearsing composure for so long that it had worn through in places. She did not smile.

"You came alone," she said. It wasn't a question. It sounded like relief, and it sounded like regret, and Oscar — who had spent two weeks learning to hear what this town said underneath what it meant — heard both.

"Slack water," he said. "As instructed."

"Not by me." She looked out at the flat, glassy channel, and when she turned back her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "Mr. Okafor, before we go any further, I need you to know that everything in my letter was true. Every word. And that's why I'm afraid. Because the man who wrote the first two letters never let me tell the truth. He only ever let me tell the parts of it that served him." She glanced up at the dark lamp room above them, then back at him, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "He's already here, isn't he? You must have known. He's always one step ahead, because—"

The light in the lamp room came on.

It swept once across the water — a slow, deliberate circle, the old lens catching the beam and throwing it out over the bay like a finger pointing at everything at once — and in its glare Oscar saw, silhouetted in the gallery rail above them, the figure of a tall man in a long coat, watching them with the patient attention of someone who had written this scene long ago and had only been waiting for the tide.
