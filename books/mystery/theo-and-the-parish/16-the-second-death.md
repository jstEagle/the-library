## 16. The Second Death

The knock came at half past six in the morning, and I knew before I opened the door.

Not because of the hour—though no one in St. Aldwyn's knocked on a door at half past six with good news—but because of the quality of the silence underneath it. The village had a morning sound: gulls off the estuary, the milk van's rattling circuit, Mrs. Halloway's gate hinge that she'd been meaning to oil since 1987 and never would. That hinge was silent. The whole street was silent in the specific way a body is silent when the breath has gone out of it.

I opened the door to a boy of maybe fifteen in a scout uniform, white around the mouth.

"You're the insurance lady," he said. "They said fetch you. It's Mrs. Halloway. She's fell. On the lighthouse path."

*Fell.* Even grief-struck, even terrified, he said it the way they'd all been coached to say it—or had coached themselves, which was worse. In St. Aldwyn's, accidents were not discovered. They were *pronounced*, like hymns.

"I'll come," I said, and got my coat.

* * *

The lighthouse path ran along the headland above the coastguard station, a ribbon of packed earth between gorse and a low stone wall, and at its midpoint the ground dipped where rain had cut a channel years ago. She lay just past the dip, on her back, one arm flung out toward the sea as if she'd been reaching for something on her way down. Her shopping bag had burst. Tins of soup were scattered across the grass like spent shell casings. Her handbag sat upright three feet away, strap neatly coiled.

That was the first thing wrong. People who fall do not set down their handbags first.

The second thing was the mud. There had been rain overnight—heavy, the kind that turned the path to grease—and the ground around her was churned with footprints. But the prints around her body were shallow, evenly spaced, the tread of someone walking *carefully*. Someone who had come out after the rain, stood over her, arranged the scene, and walked away without hurrying. The only deep, desperate prints—the kind a falling woman makes—were nowhere at all.

The third thing I didn't see until I knelt beside her, and then I wished I hadn't looked, because looking is what I do and I couldn't stop doing it.

Her fingernails. Two of them on the right hand were split to the quick, and there was grey wool caught under the third. Not her wool—she'd been wearing a waxed coat, no scarf. Grey wool, coarse-spun, the kind that comes from a cardigan worn by a man who keeps his hands in his pockets.

She had fought. Briefly. And then she had stopped fighting, because she was seventy-eight years old and whoever held her was not.

"Shouldn't touch her," the boy whispered behind me. "PC Drayton's coming."

I stood up slowly. Down the slope, half-hidden by the coastguard station's gable, Theo was watching from the doorway—a still figure in a dark coat, and when our eyes met across two hundred yards of morning, he did not look away and he did not come closer, because coming closer would kill him. His face told me everything his voice couldn't: *I know. I know what this means. I know whose fault it isn't and whose death it is.*

I turned back to Mrs. Halloway and made myself memorize her—not the arrangement, the police would photograph that—but *her*. The flour dust still on her cuff from yesterday's baking for the dinner. The reading glasses folded in her breast pocket. The small ink stain on her left index finger, permanent as a birthmark, forty years of postmarking stamped into her skin.

She had posted letters for Theo for three weeks. She had begged me, with tears in her eyes, not to report him. And now she was dead on a path she'd walked every day of her life, staged as a fall, and the letters were gone.

I knew they were gone before anyone searched the house. I knew it the way you know a word has been erased from a page you've read once. Her cottage smelled of her—lavender and toast—and her writing desk stood open, and the drawer where she kept her correspondence was empty except for a single rubber band, coiled like a question mark. Every letter from Theo. Every envelope in his careful hand. Taken.

Whoever killed her had known about the letters.

Only four people alive knew about the letters. Theo. Me. Mrs. Halloway.

And the killer.

* * *

By noon the village had arranged itself into mourning the way it arranged itself into everything—efficiently, identically, everyone saying the same words in the same cadence. *A tragedy. She was so steady on her feet. The path can be treacherous after rain.* PC Drayton wrote *accidental death, pending coroner* in his notebook with the weary relief of a man grateful not to have a murder on his patch. The doctor—old Dr. Penhale, half-deaf, fond of his lunch—signed the certificate by two o'clock.

I stood in the churchyard while they carried her in and listened to the parish agree, unanimously and instantly, that nothing had happened.

That unanimity was the loudest sound I'd ever heard.

Because here was what my memory kept returning to, unbidden, merciless: the dinner committee list, pinned to the noticeboard beside the hymn numbers, which I had photographed eleven days ago along with every other board in the parish. Nine names. Nine people with keys to the church, access to the foundation stone's ceremony, and—now—one fewer member than the evening before.

Voss. Chairman, naturally.

Dr. Penhale.

Mrs. Tregowan, who ran the flower rota.

Colonel Ashworth, retired, whose family name appeared in the 1979 restoration accounts.

The Ellery sisters, Edith and Maud.

Mr. Sarkissian, who kept the bells.

Young Tom Voss—Edmund's nephew—who did the heavy lifting.

And Mrs. Halloway herself, ex officio, keeper of the post.

Nine names. One of them dead. And somewhere among the remaining eight, a person who owned a grey cardigan and knew how to walk softly in mud.

I went home and laid the photographs out on my bed like a spread of cards, and I did what I always did, what I was built to do: I remembered. The committee meeting minutes from March, photographed in passing. The seating plan draft. The roster for anniversary-week duties. And there—there, in the corner of a page I'd taken almost idly, a note in Mrs. Halloway's own handwriting, appended to a supply order: *E.V. asked again re: old letters. Told him post is private.*

*Asked again.*

Edmund Voss had been asking the postmistress about old letters. Before Theo vanished. Before any of this, or near enough. Which meant the letters weren't evidence he'd stumbled onto—they were evidence he'd been hunting for decades, and Mrs. Halloway had been standing in front of the vault the whole time, smiling, stalling, keeping the parish's secrets the way she kept everyone's mail: safely, silently, without judgment.

Until she decided one secret deserved daylight. Until she started posting letters for a dead man.

I sat on the edge of my bed with the photographs fanned around me and felt something I hadn't felt since I was nine years old, since the first time I understood that remembering everything was supposed to be a gift and mostly felt like a sentence: rage. Not the clean professional anger of an investigator at a botched scene. This was hot and personal and it had my own fingerprints all over it. Because I had found the diary. I had asked Mrs. Halloway questions in the post office with the door open and the village listening through the walls. I had let her confess to me in her kitchen over cold tea, and I had promised her—I had *promised* her—that I wouldn't report him yet, and I had believed that promise was protection. For him. For her.

I had painted a target on the one person in St. Aldwyn's who had been kind to me without wanting anything back, and I hadn't even known I was holding the brush.

* * *

Theo came to me after dark, through the churchyard, the way ghosts are supposed to travel. He didn't use the door; he stood beneath my window and breathed my name once, and I let him in because the alternative was watching him stand in the rain like a penitent, and I didn't have the strength.

He looked ten years older than he had at the coastguard station. Grief will do that, even to a man rehearsing invisibility.

"She posted my mother's letters," he said, before his coat was off. "From 1979. Did you know that? When everything happened—when my father died and the theft was pinned on the Ellerys' gardener and nobody would speak to us—Mrs. Halloway was the only one who wrote. Every week. She was nineteen. She worked in the post office even then." He sat down heavily on the edge of my chair, as if his legs had resigned. "Forty years of letters. She kept them all. She showed me, the night before I—before I disappeared. She said, *someone should finally read these.*"

"And now they're gone," I said. "He took them."

"He?" Theo looked up. "You're certain it's a man?"

I described the footprints. The depth, the stride, the weight distribution. The grey wool under her nails. He listened the way he listened to music—with his whole body, eyes closed—and when I finished he nodded slowly.

"The wool," he said. "There's a cardigan. Grey, shawl-collared. Edmund Voss has worn it to every committee meeting since October. I saw it from the gallery." He opened his eyes. "But Juniper—it doesn't matter if we *know*. No one will say it. They signed the certificate by teatime. You heard them. *A tragedy. Steady on her feet.* This parish buried a murder in 1979 and called it a heart attack; burying a second one is practically tradition."

"Then we don't need them to say it," I said. "We need the ledger. Everything else—the letters, the hymn numbers, the hush money, your father—it all ends at the foundation stone. And the stone opens Saturday night, during the toast, in front of two hundred people." I heard my own voice go flat and hard, a stranger's voice. "He killed her to keep it shut. So we open it. We open it while he's standing at the top table holding a glass of champagne, and we make him watch."

Theo was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window.

"There's something else," he said finally. "Something I've been turning over since this morning. How did he know about the letters? Truly? Mrs. Halloway told no one—she swore it. You told no one. I told no one." He looked at me with an expression I couldn't read, and it took me a moment to understand that it wasn't suspicion. It was fear *for* me. "Juniper. Someone searched your room last week. You told me yourself—drawers moved, mattress lifted, nothing taken. What were they looking for?"

"My notes," I said slowly. "My photographs. My—" And then the cold arrived, spreading outward from the center of me like ice across a pond. "My reconstruction. The hymn sequences. I keep them in my head, but I wrote the mapping down once, just once, working papers, dates against numbers, and I hid them—"

"Where?"

"In the lining of my suitcase. Under the false bottom." I was already moving, dragging the suitcase from the wardrobe, peeling back the lining with fingers that had stopped being entirely mine.

The papers were there. All of them. Untouched, unmoved, exactly where I'd hidden them.

Which meant either the searcher had never found them—

—or the searcher had found them, read them, understood precisely how close I was, and put them back, because a dead investigator asks questions and a live one who thinks she's hidden simply walks, unknowing, toward the stone.

I stood there with my own handwriting trembling in my hands, and outside, far off across the dark fields, the church bell rang a single hour-note. Eleven o'clock. Somewhere in this village, a man in a grey cardigan was folding stolen letters into a fire, or filing them away as leverage, or reading them at last—forty years of a dead woman's loyalty going up in smoke—and tomorrow he would smile at me across the flower arrangements and ask whether I'd be attending the anniversary dinner, because of course I would, everyone would, the whole parish together, one last time.

"Juniper," Theo said quietly. "Saturday night. Whatever happens—we don't separate. Promise me."

I looked at the photographs spread across my bed: nine names, eight survivors, one stone waiting under the chancel floor with forty years of sin folded inside it like a letter no one was meant to read.

"I promise," I said. "But Theo—there's something you need to see first. The toast. I decoded it tonight, before the knock came. The toastmaster's script, the one printed in the program drafts." I handed him the page, my finger under the final line. "Look at the numbers in the closing verse. It's not just the key to the stone."

"It's the order the founders paid in," Theo whispered. "It's a receipt."

"No," I said. "It's a guest list. And one of the names on it is still alive."
