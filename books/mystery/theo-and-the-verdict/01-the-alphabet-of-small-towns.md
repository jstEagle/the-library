## 1. The Alphabet of Small Towns

Bellhaven kept its secrets the way a riverbed keeps stones: not hidden, exactly, just worn smooth enough that no one bothered to look closely anymore. I had lived there eleven months and I had catalogued four hundred and six of them—not the secrets, the claims. Insurance claims, that is, though on long evenings the two categories blurred in ways my employer would not have appreciated.

My name is Isandro Dravenmoor, and I am the kind of person who counts things. I counted the claims because counting was how I made the world hold still. A claim is a small, bounded grief: a burned kitchen, a flooded basement, a fender folded like paper. You assign it a number, you assign it a value, you file it, and it stops bleeding. I had built a life out of other people's bounded griefs, and I had convinced myself, most days, that this was the same as living.

The office was above the old hardware store on Corliss Street, a room with a radiator that knocked twice at the top of every hour like something asking to be let out of a wall. I kept the files in alphabetical order by claimant surname, then chronological within each letter, because a town is an alphabet that reads itself to you if you let it. A is for Ashford, whose family name appeared on deeds the way watermarks appear on paper—everywhere, invisible, structural. B is for Bellhaven itself, incorporated 1891 by men whose portraits hung in the county annex with the varnished confidence of people who never expected to be questioned. C is for Corliss Street, Corinne's diner, the county road that ran out of town past the quarry and did not stop until it reached somewhere else.

I had memorized the alphabet of the town before I ever unpacked my second box. This is what I do. This is what I have always done, since I was a child who could not forget a single thing said to me, a single face, a single afternoon, and learned that the only defense against a memory like mine is order. Everything filed. Everything numbered. Everything in its place, so that nothing could ambush me at two in the morning wearing its exact original light.

Eleven months in Bellhaven and I had filed 406 claims. The four hundred and seventh arrived on a Tuesday in October, in an envelope that smelled of smoke and river water, and it did not stop bleeding.

* * *

The fire had happened nine days earlier, at the old Bellhaven Elementary on Marsh Road—the school that had been closed since the district consolidated, standing empty for years with its windows boarded like a face deciding whether to look surprised or resigned. The fire had eaten the east wing, the gymnasium, and most of the roof before the volunteer department drowned it. The cause was undetermined, which in claim language is a door left open, and through that door I was hired to walk.

The school district's insurer wanted to know if the fire was arson, because if it was arson, they paid nothing, and if it was negligence, they paid everything, and the difference between those two words was roughly the annual budget of a small town's remaining ambitions. I drove out on a gray morning with rain starting, the kind of thin cold rain that makes a place look like a photograph of itself.

The school sat back from the road behind a chain-link fence with a padlocked gate that someone had opened with a bolt cutter sometime in the previous decade and never bothered to repair. I stepped through the gap where the fence met the gatepost, the way everyone in Bellhaven apparently did, and stood for a moment on the cracked asphalt of the old playground, reading the scene the way I read everything: completely, involuntarily, in permanent ink.

The fire had started in the east wing. I could see it in the burn patterns even now, even drowned—the V-shaped charring on the doorframe of the old boiler room, the way the paint had blistered and curled away from the point of origin like pages turning. Accelerant, probably. The smell was still there under the wet, that sweet gasoline ghost that lingers in a burned building the way a name lingers in a mouth after the person has left the room. Someone had wanted this fire. Someone had driven out to an empty school on a night in October and given it a match, and the question my employers paid me to answer was whether that someone had a reason worth a payout.

But that was the claim. The claim was never the whole story. I had learned that in eleven months and in eleven years before that. A claim is the shape grief leaves in the world when it passes through, and if you press your thumb into the shape, sometimes you can feel what made it.

I walked the east wing with my flashlight, cataloguing. Room 4, water-stained ceiling, desks stacked like ribs. Room 5, the fire's edge, a black tide mark across the ceiling tiles. Room 6—Room 6 had been a classroom once, and the fire had taken its roof but spared its far wall, and on that far wall someone had painted a mural, years ago, of a tree with children's names written on the leaves in a hundred different hands. The paint had run where the fire hose hit it, so that the names wept down the wall in long colored streaks, and I stood there longer than the scene required, memorizing it, because that is the curse of me: I memorize everything, including things I would rather not keep.

The basement access was through a door off the main hall, a steel door swollen shut with water. I put my shoulder into it the way the adjuster's manual suggests and the way experience suggests is a lie, and it gave with a groan of rust, exhaling a breath of air that had been shut in since the fire—cold, mineral, the smell of a drowned place.

The basement had taken a foot of hose water and held it. Debris floated in a gray skin across the surface: insulation, a mop head, the swollen corpse of a filing cabinet lying on its back with its drawers open like a man surrendering. My flashlight moved across the water and the water moved back, and in the corner, where the floor dipped toward an old floor drain, something pale lay against the wall, half-sunk, furred with silt.

I should have logged it as debris. That is what the manual says. Debris is debris; the claim is the fire; the fire is a number. I have built my whole life on the gospel of numbers.

I waded in anyway.

The water came up over my boots, shockingly cold, holding my ankles the way cold water does, as if the basement would prefer I stayed. The object was a book. A ledger, maybe, or a notebook, bound in something that had been leather once and was now a kind of fossil of leather, swollen to twice its size, its cover furred gray-green with mold. It had been wedged behind the floor drain cover, deliberately—wedged, I would later understand, the way you wedge something when you want it found by the right person and no one else, or when you want it found by no one and the universe disagrees.

I pried it loose. It came apart slightly in my hands, the way drowned things do, sighing, and the smell that came off it was not the smell of the basement. It was paper and ink and mold and underneath that something faintly sweet, like the ghost of a perfume, or like the gasoline smell upstairs, but older, patient, a smell that had been waiting.

I got it up the stairs and out into the gray rain and laid it on the hood of my car, and I stood over it in the dripping quiet of Marsh Road and did not open it for a full minute, which for me is a kind of discipline bordering on heroism.

The cover had a label, the old classroom kind, a strip of masking tape gone amber with age, and on it, in ballpoint, in a hand that slanted forward like someone always in a hurry to arrive: a name.

*Marisol Vega.*

I knew the name. Everyone in Bellhaven knew the name the way everyone knows the name of the hole in the town's teeth—it comes up in conversation and the conversation flinches around it. The teacher. The one who vanished. Twenty years ago this coming June, Marisol Vega, thirty-one years old, fifth grade, had walked out of the world and left nothing behind but a missing-persons file that the sheriff's office had closed in eleven weeks with the word *runaway* written across it like a period at the end of a sentence nobody believed.

I had heard the name four times in eleven months. Once from Corinne at the diner, who mentioned it and then changed the subject with the practiced smoothness of a woman changing lanes. Once from a claimant, an old man on Delano Street, who said *that was a bad summer* and would not say why. Once in the county annex, where I had seen the file number in an index—*Vega, M., 1998, closed*—and felt the small cold hook of it and let it go, because it was not my case, because nothing was ever my case, because my cases had numbers and payouts and endings.

And once from Sheriff Dale Pruitt himself, retired now, at the hardware store, buying paint, when a younger man behind him in line made a joke about the old schoolhouse being haunted and Pruitt had said, in a voice like a door closing, *Schools aren't haunted. People just don't like remembering them.* And then he had looked at me—looked at me the way you look at a stranger to see if the stranger heard—and I had smiled the neutral smile I keep for exactly such moments, and he had left.

I remember everything. I have never once been able to decide whether it is a gift. On Marsh Road, in the rain, holding a drowned book with a dead woman's name on it, it felt like the other thing. It felt like being the only person in a dark room who can see, and understanding that this has never once in my life meant that I was safe. It has only ever meant that I was alone with what I saw.

* * *

I took the book home, because taking it to the office felt wrong in a way I could not then articulate—some instinct, some animal sense that the book should not sleep in the same building as the files, that it was not a claim, not yet, not ever, maybe.

My apartment over the pharmacy was small and exact. I laid the book on newspaper on my kitchen table under the good lamp, and I put on gloves, and I began the work of opening it, which is a violence, and I knew it was a violence, and I did it anyway. Curiosity is not a gentle thing. It is a crowbar with a conscience, and the conscience always loses.

The pages were fused in blocks where the water had glued them. I separated them with a palette knife and a hair dryer, one at a time, hours of it, the radiator knocking its twice-an-hour knock, the rain coming down on the window like the town trying to tell me something in a language made entirely of static. The paper had survived better than it had any right to—the basement had been cold, the water clean, the book wedged out of the direct flow. Ink had run on some pages into blue ghosts. Other pages were almost perfect.

The first entry was dated August 28, 1997. The handwriting was the handwriting from the masking tape, the forward-slanting hurry-of-a-hand, and it began, in a teacher's tidy cursive that loosened as it went, as if the writer were learning, page by page, to stop performing:

*First day of school. Twenty-six students. Tommy Reyes put a frog in Dana Pruitt's desk and swore on his mother it wasn't him, and I let him keep the frog because his mother works doubles and I know what swearing on her costs him. Note to self: buy a terrarium.*

I read it twice. I read everything twice, though the first reading is always already permanent. And I noticed what a normal person would not have noticed, what a normal person would have been mercifully spared noticing: the texture of the entries. They were not a diary. I did not know yet what they were—I would not know for weeks, and the knowing would rearrange everything—but even that first night, in my small exact apartment with the rain coming down, I registered that the entries were too regular, too dated, too *addressed*, as if they were being written to someone. A letter that never says to whom is still a letter.

I turned pages. September: a field trip planned, a permission slip saga, a boy who cried at recess and wouldn't say why. October: the quarry. I remember the word *quarry* appearing on a page dated October 14, 1997, in a sentence I would return to so many times in the coming weeks that it wore a groove in me:

*Went out to the quarry with the class today for the geology unit. The men there were not happy to see us. One of them watched us the whole time and I didn't like his face. Note to self: never let the children out of your sight out there.*

The rain kept on. The radiator knocked. I turned pages with the palette knife, one at a time, a surgeon of other people's paper, and the dates walked forward through a school year—Thanksgiving, a snowstorm, a valentine's-day classroom party described with such specific tenderness that I had to set the knife down for a moment and look out the window at the dark, at the town, at the lit windows of strangers, and feel the particular loneliness of my profession, which is the loneliness of a man who knows everything about the shape of other people's grief and nothing about how to be inside it with them.

Then I reached the page that ended my evening, and my eleven months of order, and possibly my life as it had been arranged up to that point.

The entry was dated March 3, 1998. Ordinary. A spelling bee. A broken window. A sentence about the boiler making a new noise.

Marisol Vega had disappeared on June 6, 1998.

I checked the date three times. I checked it the way I check everything, which is to say I will know it until I die: March 3, 1998, and then March 9, and then March 22, and then April, the entries continuing in the same hand, the same ink, the same forward-slanting hurry—April entries, May entries, June entries, pages written in a hand that belonged to a woman who had been missing for months by the time they were written, pages that described grading papers and buying a terrarium and a man parked across the street from her house at night with his lights off, pages that should not exist, could not exist, and yet lay under my palette knife in my kitchen in Bellhaven, wet-ruined and patient, like a letter that had finally, after twenty years, arrived at its address.

The last page I opened that night was dated June 30, 1998—twenty-four days after the town had decided she was gone. The entry was four lines long. The ink had run at the bottom, as if the page had been held under water while the words were still wet, and the last legible sentence was this:

*If you are reading this, then I was right about the drain.*

I sat in the lamplight with the drowned book open in front of me and the rain on the glass and the town asleep around me, all four hundred and six of my filed claims in their cabinet across town, every grief in Bellhaven numbered and bounded and still.

Except this one. This one had been waiting twenty years in a basement behind a floor drain, keeping its own alphabet, and it had just spelled my name.
