## 9. Night Visitor

The rain came in off the reservoir around eleven, a soft gray hush that turned the streetlights outside my office into smudged halos, and I sat with my coat still on because I'd told myself I was only stopping in to file two things and go home. That was a lie I told myself often enough that I'd stopped believing it. The truth was that Bellhaven at night felt less like leaving and more like abandoning, and I had never been good at abandoning anything—not a claim, not a thread, not a town that had swallowed a woman whole and called it a runaway.

I filed the two things. Then I stayed.

The office was one room above the old hardware store, rented month to month from a landlord who never asked questions because he never came by. A desk, two filing cabinets, a corkboard I'd begun to cover with index cards in my small, exact hand: *Pruitt — hands shake. Corinne — amnesia too clean. Ink — period accurate. Paper — period accurate.* The diary itself lived in my bottom drawer, wrapped in a cotton cloth I'd bought at the pharmacy because it seemed to deserve better than a manila envelope. I had taken to touching the drawer when I passed it, the way you touch a doorframe coming and going, a small superstition of custody.

At a quarter past midnight I locked up, took the stairs down into the wet, and walked the four blocks to my apartment through rain that smelled like iron and cut grass. I remember the walk precisely, because later it would matter. The hardware store's sign buzzing. A cat under the awning of the pharmacy, orange, one ear notched. The porch light at the Kessler house flickering its slow arrhythmia. Nothing out of place. Nothing I would think of again until it was too late to be comforted by how ordinary it had all been.

* * *

I knew something was wrong before I understood it, which is the way my memory always works—first the shape of the wrongness, then the details arranging themselves beneath it like bones under skin.

The shape was this: the stairwell smelled like cigarette smoke, and I had never once smelled cigarette smoke in that stairwell.

I stood on the bottom step with my keys in my hand and let the smell tell me what it knew. Not fresh—hours old, soaked into the plaster the way smoke soaks into everything, a ghost of a ghost. Someone had stood in that stairwell for a long time. Long enough to smoke more than one cigarette. Long enough to be patient.

I went up anyway. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only thing I've ever had in abundance: I was afraid, and I went up anyway, and I have never decided whether that was courage or compulsion or just the particular loneliness of a man whose memory won't let him pretend a thing didn't happen. If I turned around, I would remember turning around. Forever. The choice was between fear and the archive of my own cowardice, and I chose the door.

It was unlocked. I had locked it. The lock was old but honest, and I had heard its click at a quarter past midnight the way I hear everything, permanently.

The office was dark except for the streetlight through the window, and in that gray light I took inventory the way I take all inventories—instantly, completely, against a version of the room I could not lose.

The desk: undisturbed. The lamp, the blotter, the coffee cup with its ring of cold, the pen laid parallel to the blotter's edge because I am the kind of man who lays pens parallel. The corkboard: every index card in its place, pinned exactly where I'd pinned them, and this was somehow worse than disorder. The filing cabinets: closed, their thin locks scratched but not defeated—no, wait. Not scratched. *Polished.* The brass had been rubbed bright around the keyholes, the way a lock shines when it has been opened many times by someone skilled, and the shine was old, and I understood with a slow cold turning in my stomach that the shine had been there for weeks and I had never looked.

The bottom drawer was open.

The cotton cloth was folded—*folded*, neatly, in thirds, the way a mortician folds a sheet—and the drawer otherwise empty.

The diary was gone, and then it was not gone, because when I crossed the room and looked down at the desk, the desk that had been undisturbed, there it lay in the center of the blotter, open, its pages spread like a bird that had been shot out of the air and arranged. I had inventoried the desk. I had inventoried it *completely*, and there had been no diary on it, and now there was a diary on it, and both of those facts were true, and one of them was a message: *I was here while you stood in your stairwell deciding whether to be brave. I was here while you walked past the pharmacy cat. I can be anywhere in the sequence of your evening and you will never know which frame I was standing in.*

My hands were steady. I noticed that with a kind of clinical detachment, the way you notice your own heartbeat in a moment of crisis—*still beating, interesting, carry on.* I did not touch the diary. Not yet. First I did what I do, the only thing I have ever been good at. I read the room.

* * *

The intruder had been careful, and the carefulness itself was information. Nothing had been searched so much as *read*. My claim files were in their exact order—alphabetical, then chronological, my system, respected, even improved upon: two folders I'd left slightly out of sequence had been re-slotted correctly, a courtesy that curdled me more than any vandalism would have. Whoever had stood in this room for hours had not been looking for something. They had been *auditing* me. Checking what I knew. Calibrating.

The corkboard cards: untouched in position, but when I leaned close I saw that the pins had been turned, each one rotated a quarter-turn clockwise. Every pin. A pointless, patient, deliberate act, done with nothing but time and a certain contempt. It said: *I read every word you wrote about us, and then I straightened up after myself, because I am tidy, and you are being watched by someone tidy.*

And the window. The window was latched—I checked, my fingers finding the cold brass latch seated home—but the dust on the sill held a half-print, the outer edge of a boot heel, size ten or eleven, pressed into the gray film where someone had braced a foot on the way out. Out, not in. He had come through the door like a civilized man and left through the window over the hardware store's flat roof, down the fire escape that hadn't been used since the store closed, and the heel print was fresh enough that its edges had not yet crumbled.

I stood in the middle of my office in my wet coat, dripping onto the floorboards, and I let the fear arrive all the way. I had been afraid before in this case—the diary's impossible dates, Pruitt's shaking hands, the too-clean amnesia of Corinne Hale—but all of that fear had been about the past, about a woman twenty years gone, about the dead and the vanished. This was different. This fear had my address.

*Then I opened the diary.*

* * *

It lay open to a page I knew. Entry 61, early March of the last year of Marisol Vega's life, an entry I had read four times and transcribed twice, an entry about nothing: a school assembly, a broken radiator, the way the light came through the east windows of her classroom in the late afternoon and turned the dust to gold. Ordinary. The most ordinary page in the book.

Except that it was not the page I had read.

The words were the same—I checked them against my memory, every word, and they were the same, all of them, the assembly, the radiator, the gold light. But the *page* was different. The paper was a half-shade darker. The ink sat differently on the fiber, a hair heavier, the loops of the *g* slightly more closed. It was a forgery. A perfect forgery, executed by someone who had studied Marisol's hand the way I studied everything, and substituted for the original, and left it open to this page specifically, in this room, for me.

And in the margin, in ink that was not hers and was not trying to be, in a small, precise, upright script that looked like it had been written by a filing cabinet, someone had added a single line:

*You keep excellent files, Mr. Dravenmoor. It would be a shame to become one.*

I read it eleven times. I could not stop reading it; that is the affliction and the gift of me, that a thing once seen is seen forever, that the sentence burned itself into the back of my eyes with its exact slant, its exact pressure, the tiny hesitation in the downstroke of the *b* where the writer had lifted the pen. *It would be a shame to become one.* A file. A closed file. A folder in someone else's cabinet, alphabetized, dated, resolved.

I sat down in my chair, which creaked the way it always creaked, and the ordinariness of the creak was almost unbearable. The rain had thickened against the window. Somewhere below, the hardware store sign buzzed on, indifferent, selling nothing to no one at one in the morning.

Think, I told myself. It is what you are for.

He had taken nothing. That was the first fact, and facts are the rungs of every ladder I've ever climbed out of a dark place. Nothing taken—no files, no cards, no diary itself, which he could have taken, which any thief would have taken, which *I* would have taken. He had left the diary. He had left it open to a forged page. Which meant the diary's value to him was not in its possession but in its *content*, and its content was now, as far as he was concerned, under his editorial control. He had already shown me he could revise it. Tonight he had shown me he could revise it *in my own office*, between the moment I locked the door and the moment I climbed the stairs, in a window of time so narrow that I kept turning it over in my memory looking for the gap where he had fit, and finding none, and finding none, and the finding-none was its own kind of horror. Either he had been inside before I locked the door—waiting in the dark while I sat ten feet away filing papers, breathing the same room—or he had a key, or he had picked the lock in silence in the time it took me to walk four blocks, and all three of those possibilities meant the same thing: the walls of my methodical life were made of paper, and he had been reading them for a long time.

The polished keyholes. The shine on the brass that I had never looked at. Weeks. He had been coming and going for *weeks*, reading my files as I built them, watching the investigation assemble itself the way you watch a crop come in, and he had let it grow because—why? Because he wanted to know what I knew. Because he was deciding, all this time, whether I was a problem or a tool.

And tonight he had decided to introduce himself.

Why tonight? I sat with that question the way you sit with a cold stone in your hands. What had changed? What had I done in the last seventy-two hours that crossed some line in his ledger? I ran the days backward through my memory, frame by frame. The visit to Pruitt—the sheriff's shaking hands, his too-loud insistence on *runaway, runaway, runaway*. The interview with Corinne Hale, her amnesia like a door painted on a wall. The ink tests. The paper. And yesterday—yesterday I had pulled the county records on the old schoolhouse property, the fire claim that had started all of this, and I had requested, through the annex, a certified copy of the original deed chain, and the request had gone through the county records system, and the county records system had a name, and the name was—

I stopped. I did not know the name. I had spoken to a clerk on the phone, a dry voice, helpful, forgettable, a man who had said *I'll pull that for you, Mr. Dravenmoor, might take a day or two, things are filed deep down here.* Filed deep down here. The annex. The county records annex out past the fairgrounds, the long low building I had driven past a dozen times without a second look.

It was nothing. It was a clerk. Every county has a hundred clerks, and a request for a deed chain is the most ordinary paper in the world.

But the stone stayed cold in my hands.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat in my office until the rain stopped and the window went from black to the color of dishwater, and I did three things.

First, I wrote out the forged page's differences from memory—every deviation in the hand, the darker paper, the heavier ink—on a card, and then I burned the card in the ashtray with a match and watched it curl, because I understood now that anything I wrote could be read, and some things needed to exist in exactly one place: the place behind my eyes that no one could search. My memory was no longer just a condition. It was the only secure filing cabinet in Bellhaven, and I had better start using it like one.

Second, I examined the diary itself, and this was the hardest. I turned the forged page and checked the binding, the thread, the wear of the cover, and everything was as it had been, which told me he had not disassembled the book—had perhaps not even needed to open it beyond that page, because he already knew what was in it, had always known, had maybe read it more times than I had. The diary was not a discovery anymore. It was a *channel*. Someone had made sure it reached me; someone else could reach into my office and annotate it. It was less a dead woman's voice than a telephone line, and tonight the line had been used to call me.

Third, near dawn, I did the thing I had been not-doing all night. I looked at the margin note again—not the words, which I had memorized, but the hand. The small, precise, upright script. The hesitation in the *b*. The way the *r* terminated without a flourish, clipped, efficient, a hand that had written ten thousand labels and file headings and had long ago sanded the personality off itself. It was not the hand of a man who had killed in passion. It was the hand of a man who had *filed* in passion, if such a thing existed, a man for whom the record was the weapon and the filing was the crime.

And underneath the fear, in the gray hour when the pharmacy cat would be curling under the awning and the Kesslers' porch light would be flickering its arrhythmia, I found the other thing that the night had left in me, the thing I was almost ashamed of: he had made a mistake. He had come to frighten me, and he had frightened me, and he had also—because he could not help himself, because a man who edits the record cannot leave a page unedited—shown me his handwriting. He had put his hand, literally his hand, into my custody. The note was a threat, and it was also an exhibit. Somewhere in this county there was paper covered in that script—forms, headings, marginalia, the bureaucratic sediment of twenty years—and every piece of it was a fingerprint, and I would find the sediment.

The diary lay open on my desk to Entry 61, the assembly, the radiator, the gold light. I looked at the forged page a last time, and then I closed the book, and my hand rested on the cover in the cold dawn light, and I understood that the message had a second layer, the way everything in this case had a second layer. Of all the pages he could have forged, all the pages he could have left open—the quarry, the man watching her house, the entries that shouldn't exist—he had left open the most innocent page in the book. The assembly. The gold light. A page about nothing.

Unless it wasn't about nothing. Unless the choice of page was the message beneath the message, and the man who curates evidence had curated this too: *Look here. Not at the entries that frighten you. Here. The answer is in the ordinary. The answer is in the page you skipped because it was boring.*

I opened the book to Entry 61 and read it again, slowly, the way you reread a letter from someone who has died, and this time I did not read it as a teacher's diary.

I read it as a cipher.

The radiator that clanked at intervals. The assembly where the principal's microphone cut out twice. The light through the east windows at 3:15. Numbers. The page was full of numbers, and I had read past them four times because they were the furniture of an ordinary day, and ordinary days were the one thing I had trained myself to see through.

The rain had stopped. The street below was waking—a truck, a dog, the hardware store sign buzzing for an audience of no one. I took out a fresh card, and then I put it back, and instead I sat with my hands flat on the desk and began, silently, to count.
