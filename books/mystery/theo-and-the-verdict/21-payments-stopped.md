## 21. Payments Stopped

The diner was still smoldering in my mind when I spread the paper across my desk that night, but I'll get to that. First I have to tell you about the arithmetic, because the arithmetic is what kept me upright. Grief unmoors a person; arithmetic holds them to the earth like a tent stake. I had dates. I have always had dates. Other people misplace their Tuesdays the way they misplace umbrellas, but every date I have ever seen is still nailed to the inside of my skull, bright as brass tacks, and that night I began pulling them out one by one and laying them in rows.

Tommy Reyes had given me the payments the way a man gives up a tooth—reluctantly, in pieces, with blood. Cash envelopes, mostly. The first had come in the autumn of the disappearance year, delivered by a lawyer with a school-board letterhead and a handshake like a wet rag. Then monthly, then quarterly, then—after the first few years—irregular. Tommy had never been good with calendars, he said, but he'd kept every envelope. Twenty years of envelopes in a Rubbermaid tub under his bunk in a truck-stop dormitory in Amarillo, each one marked on the back in his own blocky hand with the date it arrived. He'd let me photograph them all with the flat, exhausted obedience of a man who had already decided the memory was going to kill him whether or not he fed it.

I had the photographs. I had the dates. And now, sitting in my office with the radiator ticking like a slow clock and the lamp turned down to a coin of amber light, I had the obituaries.

Bellhaven's paper, the *Bellhaven Courier*, had never been a good newspaper, but it had been a faithful one in the way that small-town papers are faithful: it recorded everything and understood nothing. Births, harvests, church suppers, drownings. The county library kept the archives on microfilm, and I had spent the previous afternoon in that basement room with its smell of cold dust and warm machines, cranking the reel handle with my left hand and writing with my right, page after page, because the microfilm reader's print function had been broken since roughly the Nixon administration and the teenage librarian had shrugged at it the way her mother probably had before her.

So: the dates. Let me lay them out the way I laid them out, because the order matters, and the gaps matter, and if you are the kind of person who reads a thing like this to its end, you will want to see the shape before I name it.

Envelope one: October 14th, the disappearance year. Envelopes monthly through the following spring, then quarterly. I will not bore you with all sixty-some of them, but I will tell you about the lapses, because the lapses are the story.

The first lapse: payments stopped for five months, from March to August of the fourth year. They resumed in September, with a double envelope and a typed note—*sorry for the delay, administrative*—that Tommy had kept because it looked official and official things frightened him into keeping them.

The second lapse: eleven months, beginning in the winter of the eleventh year, ending the following November.

The third lapse: seven months, in the seventeenth year.

And the fourth lapse—the one that had put the cold hand on the back of my neck in Amarillo—had begun the previous month. Tommy's last envelope had come in the spring. No note. No explanation. Just silence, arriving on schedule for the first time in twenty years.

Four lapses. Now the deaths.

* * *

The first death was Earl Sutcliffe. You wouldn't know the name unless you'd lived in Bellhaven longer than I had lived anywhere, and even then you'd know it the way you know the names on old plaques—as texture, not fact. Sutcliffe had run the bait shop on the reservoir road. I found him in the Courier's fourth-year August issue, and the photograph showed a lean man with a mustache the size of a paintbrush, holding up a catfish with the pride of a man who has caught something that fought him.

Drowned. August 19th, the fourth year. Fell from his own boat, the article said, at night, alone, on a reservoir he had fished every weekend of his adult life. A strong swimmer, the article said. A careful man, the article said, quoting his sister. The water was cold that time of year, the article said, and cold water surprises even careful men.

August 19th, the fourth year. Five months into the first lapse. Payments resumed September 3rd.

I want to be honest with you about what I did when I saw that. I did not gasp. I did not stand up. I sat very still in the microfilm room's plastic chair and felt the room get larger around me, the way rooms do when something in them becomes true. Then I wrote the date on my legal pad, drew a line under it, and kept cranking.

Because here is what my memory does, and here is why it is both the only tool I have ever trusted and the thing I would trade away on some nights for an hour of peace: I do not have to go looking for the connections. I only have to stand still and let them come to me. The dates were already in my head—the obituary dates from this afternoon, the envelope dates from Amarillo, the weather reports I'd read idly while researching the quarry's water levels, the county road maintenance schedules I'd skimmed two weeks ago for reasons I couldn't then have named. All of it filed, all of it cross-indexed, all of it waiting like a library after hours, every book awake.

The second death was Delores Fenn. I remembered her name from the case file—she had been the school district's attendance clerk back when Marisol vanished, one of the last people to sign off on Marisol's end-of-year records. In the Courier's eleventh-year winter issues, there she was: killed in a single-car accident on Route 9, January 30th, the eleventh year. Black ice, the article said. She'd driven that road thirty years. Her daughter was quoted saying her mother didn't drive at night if she could help it, but she'd had a meeting in Harwick that evening, an odd meeting, an unscheduled one. The article did not say what the meeting was about. The article was not curious. Small-town papers are faithful, but they are faithful the way dogs are faithful—to whoever feeds them.

January 30th, the eleventh year. Eleven months into the second lapse. Payments to Tommy resumed the following November.

The third death took longer to find, and finding it frightened me more than the other two, because the third man had almost no obituary at all. Half a column inch. *Ray Duquesne, 54, died June 2nd of the seventeenth year, in a fall from the water tower on Kessler Hill. Authorities do not suspect foul play.* No photograph. No survivors listed. No quotation from anyone who had loved him, which meant either that no one had, or that no one was willing to say so in print.

Ray Duquesne. I turned the name over. It took me most of a pot of coffee and one walk around the block in the dark before it surfaced, and when it did, it came up whole, the way my memory always gives things up—complete, contextualized, timestamped. Ray Duquesne had been a county road crew foreman. Ray Duquesne had been on the crew that resurfaced the quarry access road the spring before Marisol disappeared—the spring of the field trip, the spring of whatever she had seen between the two men and the body bag. Ray Duquesne had been interviewed, briefly, in the original missing-persons file I'd pulled from Pruitt's boxes. He'd told the deputy he'd seen nothing unusual out that way. His statement ran four lines. Every other witness in that file had gotten a full page.

June 2nd, the seventeenth year. Seven months into the third lapse. Payments resumed the following January.

* * *

I stood up from the desk at some point—maybe midnight, maybe later—and went to the window. Bellhaven lay under a sky the color of wet slate, the streetlights making their small orange islands down Main Street, the water tower on Kessler Hill a black exclamation point against the last light in the west. From my window I could see the water tower. I stood there and looked at it for a long time.

Four lapses. Three deaths. And the arithmetic, which I will now give you plainly, because I earned it and you should have it:

Every lapse in Tommy Reyes's payments was followed, within months, by the death of a witness. Not a suspect. Not an enemy. A witness. A man who fished the reservoir. A woman who kept attendance records. A road foreman who had signed a four-line statement. And each time, when the witness was dead, the payments resumed—generously, apologetically, *administrative delay*—as if some ledger somewhere had been balanced. As if a cost had been incurred and covered and the account reopened.

I made myself say it in plain words, standing at that window, because plain words are a kind of handrail: someone had been paying Tommy Reyes for twenty years to stay vague. And when the payments paused—when the account went unattended, when the machine that dispensed silence hiccuped—other silences had to be manufactured the hard way. Then the machine was fixed and the money flowed again and the town went on believing in cold water and black ice and men who climbed water towers at night for no reason anyone could name.

Three accidents in twenty years, each one parked neatly inside a lapse like a car in a garage.

And the fourth lapse was happening now.

* * *

I went back to the desk and did what I do when dread arrives, which is to give it a job. Dread is useless as a feeling and excellent as a fuel; the trick is the conversion. I took a fresh sheet of paper and drew four columns. Names. Dates of death. Lapse intervals. And in the fourth column, the question I had been avoiding since Amarillo: *Who else was paid?*

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. Tommy had been paid to stay vague. Corinne Hale had admitted—cracked open in her back office like a geode, all glitter and damage—that she had been paid to forget. Two witnesses, two payment streams. And payment streams, in my experience, do not run alone. Money in a town like Bellhaven is water: it finds the low places, it seeps everywhere, it leaves a mineral ring you can read if you know what you're looking at.

Corinne. The thought of her had a taste, that night—copper and coffee. She had agreed to go on record. She had said the words *I'll sign whatever you need* with her hands flat on the diner counter as if pressing down a lid. And her payments—she had told me this with a laugh that had no floor under it—her payments had stopped too. Last month. Same month as Tommy's. She'd said it like a joke: *Guess I'm off the payroll, sugar. Twenty years of hush money and they fired me by silence.*

I had laughed with her. God forgive me, I had laughed.

I looked at my four columns and the fourth column was nearly empty and the empty part of it was screaming. If the pattern held—if the lapses were the dangerous seasons, the open windows through which the cold got in—then Corinne Hale was not a witness who had been paid to forget. She was a witness whose payments had stopped, which meant she had been moved, in whatever ledger governed these things, from the column marked *maintained* to the column marked *outstanding*.

Outstanding. The word has two meanings and I sat there holding both of them like two ends of a wire.

I picked up the phone to call her, and put it down. It was past one. What would I say—*Corinne, don't be an accident*? And worse, the thought I could not shake: if I called her, if I went to her, if I was seen going to her, I might be the thing that moved her from one column to the other. The killer—I was past pretending it was an abstract force; someone, some *body*, with hands and a car and a filing system—read my movements the way I read the diary. Chapter sixteen of my own private nightmare: my notes photographed, my deductions anticipated. Every step I took toward a witness might be a step I was walking them into.

I did not call. I told myself it was strategy. I have told myself a great many things at two in the morning and I have believed most of them until morning corrected me.

* * *

Morning did not correct me. Morning made it worse, the way mornings do when they arrive with weather.

It came up out of the southeast overnight, a low gray ceiling with rain in it, and by seven o'clock the light in my office was the color of dishwater and the windows had gone soft with condensation. I had slept maybe two hours at the desk, cheek on the payment ledger, and I woke to the smell of wet asphalt and the sound of a siren—not the urgent kind, the routine kind, the one-note wail of a volunteer department heading somewhere it went often.

I was at the window before I was fully awake. The truck went by on Main Street, red lights smearing in the rain, and it did not turn toward the highway or the reservoir road or Kessler Hill.

It turned toward the diner.

I don't remember deciding to run. I remember the stairwell, and the cold, and my breath, and the way the rain hit my face like a handful of thrown gravel, and Corinne's diner at the end of the block with its blue vinyl glow and its neon coffee cup that had buzzed every night for thirty years—and the coffee cup was dark. The whole sign was dark. There was smoke coming out of the building in a long gray rope that the rain kept trying to braid and failing, and there were neighbors in bathrobes on the sidewalk, and there was Corinne.

Corinne was sitting on the curb across the street in a man's overcoat over her nightgown, hair plastered to her face, watching her life burn with an expression I had seen once before, on Tommy Reyes, in a truck-stop booth in Amarillo: the expression of a person watching a debt come due that they had always known about. Not surprise. Recognition. The fire was not a shock to her. It was a sentence she had been reading toward for twenty years, and now she had reached the end of the page.

I crouched in front of her. Rain ran off my chin onto the concrete between us.

"Corinne."

She didn't look at me. The fire department was unrolling hose with the unhurried competence of men who knew the building was already lost. The heat reached us across the street in pulses, like a heart.

"I was going to sign," she said. Her voice was flat as the curb. "I told you I would. I meant it."

"I know."

"They know I meant it too." She turned her head then, and her eyes were dry in all that rain, which seemed impossible and was not. "That's the thing, sugar. They don't punish you for talking. They punish you for *meaning* it. There's a difference, and they can tell."

The diner's front window went out with a sound like a held breath released, and the neon coffee cup's dead tube caught the firelight and glowed orange for one second, one bright ghost of the light it had made every night of my time in Bellhaven, and then it went dark again for good.

I knelt there in the rain with the ledger of dates burning in my memory—fourth year, eleventh year, seventeenth year, now—and I understood that I was no longer reading a pattern in a newspaper. The pattern had come off the page. It was standing over me in the weather, and it had just told me, in the only language it spoke, exactly how much my questions cost, and who was expected to pay.

Three witnesses in twenty years. Each one parked inside a lapse.

Corinne's lapse was now.

And I had sat in my office at one in the morning, holding the phone, choosing strategy over a phone call, telling myself a story about not leading the killer to her door.

The rain came down. The diner burned. And somewhere in Bellhaven, in a warm dry room, someone was updating a ledger of their own—crossing off an account, noting a balance, deciding what the next payment would be, and to whom.
