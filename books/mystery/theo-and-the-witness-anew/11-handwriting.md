## 11. Handwriting

The graphologist's name was Ruth Okafor, and she lived eleven miles out of Marrow Creek on a gravel road that didn't appear on the dispatch maps because it didn't need to. Nobody flagged down cabs out there. Nobody flagged down cabs anywhere near her place, which was probably the point. Retired people who spent forty years reading the secrets people pressed into paper tended to live where the paper couldn't find them.

Isandro had called her from the office at 6:40 in the morning, the hour when Marta came on shift and the coffee was still honest. He'd kept the call short. *I have some handwriting I need looked at. It might matter. It might be nothing.* Ruth Okafor had listened, said nothing for four full seconds—a long time on a phone line—and then said, "Bring it by ten. And don't tell me what I'm supposed to find. People always want to tell the expert what to see. It's how they lie to themselves."

So he hadn't told her. Not on the phone, and not in the car on the way out, and not when she opened the door of her house—a low brick ranch with wind chimes made of old typewriter keys—and looked at him with eyes that had probably ended more arguments than any judge in the county.

She was smaller than her voice. Seventy-some, silver hair cropped close, wearing a flannel shirt and house slippers, holding a mug that said PROPERTY OF NO ONE. She looked at Isandro the way he imagined he looked at a dispatch log: reading him before he spoke.

"You're the taxi man," she said. "Isandro Blackwood. I know your voice. You used to call in the school runs for the Voss girl's class, years back. You had a way of saying addresses like they were eulogies."

"I didn't know anyone listened."

"Everyone listens, Mr. Blackwood. Almost no one answers." She stepped back to let him in. "Come on, then. Show me the ghost."

* * *

Her kitchen table had been cleared for work. A lamp with a daylight bulb. A jeweler's loupe on a chain. A magnifying sheet. A ruler, a protractor, a pad of blank paper, and a pencil sharpened to a needle. It looked less like a workspace than an operating theater, and Isandro felt the same cold sweat he'd felt at sixteen, waiting outside a hospital room, not knowing which side of the door the news would come from.

He laid out the diary with the care of a man handling something that might detonate. He'd wrapped it in a pillowcase overnight. He'd slept—badly, in four broken shifts—with it on the nightstand, because it felt safer in his house than in his office, where someone had already come looking once, and because he was honest enough with himself to admit the other reason: he didn't want to be more than a room away from Lena Voss's voice.

"Before you open it," Ruth said, "tell me the question. Not the story. The question."

Isandro looked at the diary's cover—faded cloth, a water stain shaped like a closed eye—and asked the only question that had kept him upright for three days.

"Is it her? The entries after the date she disappeared. Are they really hers?"

Ruth pulled out a chair and sat. "And if I say no?"

"Then I've wasted your morning and I go back to my logs, and the town keeps its story, and I keep my job, and everything stays filed where it belongs."

"And if I say yes?"

Isandro found he didn't have an answer ready. He'd spent fifteen years cataloguing other people's secrets precisely so he'd never have to answer a question like that. He said, finally, "Then someone in this town has been lying for fifteen years, and it isn't her."

Ruth Okafor nodded once, like a doctor confirming a symptom. "Open it."

* * *

She worked the way he filed: in total silence, with total attention. She turned pages with the flat of a finger, never pinching the paper. She used the loupe. She used the magnifying sheet. She wrote nothing for the first twenty minutes, which made Isandro's hands itch for something to hold, so he held his coffee and watched her read a dead woman's handwriting the way he read a night shift—looking for the entry that didn't belong, the fare that didn't add up, the small wrongness that meant everything.

The diary was open to the last week before Lena Voss vanished. Her handwriting there was loose, confident, a little showy. Loops that swung wide. A slant that leaned forward like a woman walking fast into wind. Isandro had read these pages four times now and knew them by heart: grocery lists braided into observations, a joke about the pastor, a line about her son—*Theo drew the whole town from memory today. Every street. He says he's going to be a mapmaker so no one can ever be lost.* Isandro had read that line eleven times. He was not prepared to examine why.

Then Ruth turned past the disappearance date, and the room changed.

Isandro felt it before he understood it—the way you feel a cab take a corner too fast. The handwriting on the far side of the date was still recognizably Lena's. The letterforms were the same. The *T* still crossed with that little downward hook. The *y* still descended in that long, elegant tail. But everything was *smaller*. Compressed. The words ran together like passengers shoved into a crowded car. The margins, which she'd used freely before, were now untouched, as if the lines themselves had been drawn as a cage and she'd stayed inside them.

Ruth held the loupe over a passage for a long time. Then another. She turned pages slowly, past the entries Isandro had found three weeks deep, past the final entry with the pickup address, and then back again, and then she sat back and took off her reading glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose.

"Say it," Isandro said. His voice came out flat. Dispatcher's voice. The voice you use when the news is bad and someone has to carry it steady.

"I'll say it properly, because you'll want it properly." She pulled the pad toward her. "The hand is the same. I compared forty letterforms across both sections—your *L*, your *V*, your *T*, the way she forms her *th* ligature, the pressure pattern on her downstrokes. A forger can copy shapes. What a forger almost never copies is rhythm, and what a forger *cannot* copy is the micro-tremor pattern of genuine writing under genuine strain. This is Lena Voss. Both sections. Same hand, same instrument—same pen, in fact; the ink chemistry shifts with age and use but it's continuous. Nobody handed this diary to a forger with a different pen and a different afternoon. This is one woman writing across time."

Isandro let out a breath he'd been holding since the phone call. It lasted one second before Ruth held up a finger.

"That's not the finding. That's the *premise*." She tapped the page with the pencil tip, not quite touching the paper. "The finding is *how* she wrote. Look here." She turned to the pre-disappearance pages. "This is her natural hand. Confident baseline—see how the line of writing drifts up and down with her mood but stays loose? Generous spacing. She takes up room. This is a woman who believes the page is hers."

She turned to the post-disappearance section. "And this is a woman writing in a moving vehicle. Or a small space. Or in the dark. Or all three. The baseline is rigid—she's bracing the page against something. The letter size drops by nearly forty percent and stays there. The spacing between words collapses. She's writing fast and small because she doesn't know how long she'll have, and she's writing *neat* because she's terrified of being illegible to whoever reads this later. Do you understand what that combination means, Mr. Blackwood? A person writing a diary for herself gets sloppy. A person writing a *message* gets precise. She knew someone would read this. She just didn't know who, or when."

"Duress," Isandro said. The word tasted like copper.

"Duress," Ruth agreed. "Sustained duress. This isn't one bad night. This is weeks of it. Look at the pressure—here, and here, and here." She indicated the downstrokes with the pencil. "The pen is digging. She's pressing hard enough to emboss the next three pages. People press hard when their hands are shaking and they're fighting the shake. And look at the *i* dots." She leaned in. "In her natural hand, the dots are placed with a little flourish, slightly right of the stem—casual, almost thrown. In the later entries, they're placed dead center, directly over the stem, every single time. That's a person concentrating on the mechanics of writing because concentrating on the mechanics is the only thing keeping her from thinking about where she is. I've seen it in hostage letters. I've seen it in people writing wills the night before an execution they didn't deserve."

The kitchen was very quiet. One of the typewriter-key wind chimes clicked outside, a single flat note, like a key turning in a lock.

"She was alive," Isandro said. It wasn't a question, but Ruth answered it anyway.

"She was alive for at least three weeks after this town decided she was dead. She was alive, she was captive, she had limited and probably stolen moments to write, and she was writing for a reader she couldn't name." Ruth looked at him over the glasses. "And here's the part you already know, taxi man, because I can see it sitting behind your face like a fare who hasn't paid. She wrote an address in the last entry. A pickup address. She wrote it the way you people write addresses—complete, exact, formatted for someone whose whole life is addresses."

Isandro's throat was dry. "The house burned down in the eighties."

"Mm." Ruth closed the diary gently, both hands, like closing a casket. "Then she wasn't writing to the house. She was writing to someone who'd know what the house meant. Someone who'd know that an address can point at a place even after the place is gone." She stood and carried her mug to the sink. "I've testified in forty-one cases. I've been wrong twice, and I remember both. I'm not wrong about this. The question you came here with has an answer. It's just not the answer you came here hoping for, because you were hoping for the lie. The lie was quieter."

* * *

He drove back toward Marrow Creek with the diary on the passenger seat, seatbelted in, which was absurd, and which he did anyway.

Hope curdling into urgency—that was the feeling, and he knew it precisely, the way he knew the precise moment a night shift turns: not at dawn, but at that dead hour when the last drunk has been delivered and the first baker hasn't called and the radio is just static and your own breathing. He had spent three days letting himself believe the diary might be a hoax, a cruelty, a mistake—anything that would let the town's story stand, because the town's story was load-bearing. Fifteen years of it. *Lena Voss ran off. Lena Voss drowned herself out at the quarry. Lena Voss is a sad story we tell so we don't have to tell the true one.*

But there was no hoax. Ruth Okafor had put her forty-one cases on the scale and weighed the ink, and the ink said: *alive*. Lena Voss had been alive, weeks after the search parties stood down, weeks after the sheriff closed the file, weeks after the Hollis family took in her son and the town exhaled and went back to its suppers. Alive and writing in a cramped hand with dead-centered dots, pressing hard enough to emboss the pages, formatting addresses for a dispatcher who wouldn't be born into this case for another fifteen years.

Which meant the question was no longer *what happened to Lena Voss.*

The question was *who needed her dead, and who kept her.*

Isandro took the long way in, past the quarry road, past the turnoff for the burned house—empty lot now, chain-link and weeds, the town's oldest lie sitting in plain view like a headstone with no name. He drove it slow. He was reading the route the way Lena had written it: not as a place, but as a message.

His phone buzzed against the dash. Marta, from the office.

*"Isandro. You need to get in here." Her voice was doing the thing it did maybe once a year—the flat, careful voice, the one that meant trouble with a name attached. "A man came by asking for the 1998 logs. Said he was from the county records office." A pause. "He wasn't from the county records office. I checked. There's no such request on file, and nobody from the county drives a car with Aldous Hollis's plate number on it."*

Isandro pulled over. The quarry road stretched out ahead of him, gray and patient.

*"Did he take anything?"*

*"He didn't get the chance. I told him the archives were off-site pending digitization. I bought you the morning." Another pause, longer. "Isandro, what did you find?"*

He looked at the diary on the seat beside him. Lena's cramped, brave, embossed handwriting, three weeks of stolen minutes pressed into paper by a woman who knew someone would read it and refused to be illegible.

*"Lena was alive," he said. "And I think somebody's finally worried we know it."*

He hung up and pulled back onto the road, toward town, toward the office, toward the morning—and in the rearview, two miles back, a gray sedan that had been with him since Ruth Okafor's gravel road turned off too.
