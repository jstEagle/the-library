## 8. The Second Set of Hands

It started, as these things always do, with something too small to notice.

A Tuesday. Rain needling the clerestory windows of the archive office while I worked through the quarterly audit — the ritual I'd performed twice a year for twenty years, every volume weighed against its ledger entry, every ledger entry against my memory. St. Aldhelm's kept its dead the way it kept everything: in triplicate, in ink, in my handwriting.

The discrepancy was a single digit.

Register of Burials, 1998–2004, folio 212. The interment of one Marcus Marsh, recorded as having taken place on the fourteenth of November. My memory said the eleventh. Not because I had any particular reason to remember Marcus Marsh — he was nobody's favorite, by all accounts, a solicitor who died young and was buried quietly — but because the eleventh was the day after my mother's birthday, and archivists remember dates the way sailors remember weather. It's not sentiment. It's calibration.

I checked the original interment docket. Fourteenth. I checked the sexton's day-book from that week. Fourteenth. Three sources, all agreeing, all in different hands, all older than my doubt.

And yet.

I sat with the register open for a long time, rain ticking against the glass, and felt the specific vertigo of a man who has spent two decades trusting his own instruments and has just heard them tick out of time. The page looked right. The ink looked right. The hand — my hand, twenty years younger and more confident — looked right.

That was the problem. Everything looked right. A forgery that looks wrong is a mistake; a forgery that looks perfect is a message.

* * *

I did what I always do when the ground shifts under me: I made an inventory.

Not of the registers — of myself. Twenty years of handling habits, catalogued like specimens. The way I sharpen a nib to a fine point for headings and let it blunt for body text, so that even within a single entry the line-weight tells you where you are. The way I cross sevens — never eights. The way I date entries at the top left, always, even when the printed form puts the field on the right, because the forms were designed by a man who never had to scan three hundred pages looking for when something happened. The way I file by death-date rather than surname, because the alphabet is arbitrary and mortality is not.

These habits are mine the way fingerprints are mine. I have never written them down. You don't write down your own gait.

By Thursday I had pulled forty-one volumes at random and compared them against secondary records — dockets, day-books, correspondence, the cathedral's financial minutes. I found six discrepancies. Six, in forty-one volumes, extrapolated across the full collection, came to something north of fifty alterations scattered through twenty years of the town's memory.

None of them were large. That was what made my hands go cold. A stolen relic announces itself; a rewritten date does not. Here a burial moved three days. There an initial added to a witness line — *R.C.* appended to a statement that had borne no initials before. Elsewhere a name spelled slightly differently, so that a search for *Voss* would miss an entry filed under *Vosse*. Small edits. Surgical. Each one invisible unless you already knew what the truth looked like — which meant each one was placed for a reader who would come looking, later, with questions.

Or placed to steer away a reader who might come looking.

I spread the six across my desk and looked for the pattern, and the pattern was there, and it was worse than tampering. It was etiquette.

Every altered volume had been returned to the shelf correctly — spine aligned, fore-edge flush, in sequence. But not in *my* sequence. I shelve by death-date within decade blocks, and I have a private rule, unspoken even to myself until that moment: anything post-1900 goes back with the spine tilted a degree off plumb, because the shelf boards sag toward the east wall and a plumb spine will gap in five years. Sloppy-looking. Deliberate. Mine.

Five of the six volumes stood plumb. Perfectly, textbook plumb — the way the diocesan manual says to shelve them, the way I taught two people to shelve them against my own instinct, because the manual is what you teach beginners before they earn the heresies.

Two people. In twenty years, I had trained exactly two assistants in this archive. One was Brother Edric, who had left filing for the bells a dozen years ago and whose hands now shook too badly to hold a nib steady. The other—

The other was sitting at the reading table downstairs, cataloguing accession slips in a hand I had praised for its neatness.

* * *

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left, and because what I did next deserves recording honestly, in the spirit of the drawer I will someday fill with this whole affair.

I did not confront anyone. I am an archivist. Confrontation destroys evidence.

Instead I spent Friday evening doing something I had never done: I read my own collection as an adversary. If I wanted to bury a truth in St. Aldhelm's — if I were the person who had just watched me find Ulyana Voss's thinned file and knew I would keep pulling threads — where would I steer me? What would I want me to find, and what would I want me to miss?

The answer arranged itself with terrible elegance. The alterations clustered around two names like iron filings around magnets. *Marsh.* And *Voss*. Every edit either burnished Verity Marsh's late husband's record into blandness or frayed the trail around Ulyana Voss — dates smeared, witnesses initialled or de-initialled, spellings bent so the name dissolved under search. Someone was curating a story. Someone had been curating it for months, patiently, one microsecond at a time, using my shelves and my system and my own teaching.

And the handling — God, the handling. The volumes had been opened flat with a weight rather than bent back, the way I insist. Pages turned from the top corner, never the bottom, to spare the gutter. The replacement ink was iron gall, properly aged with a breath of tannin, matched to the oxidation curve of the surrounding text — a technique I had demonstrated exactly once, to exactly two people, in a lesson about how forgers are caught by their impatience.

Whoever this was hadn't merely learned from me. They had learned to think like me. Somewhere in the dark of those stacks, my own methods had been picked up and worn like a coat, and every careful, reverent touch on those falsified pages was a small intimate betrayal — a kiss planted in my handwriting.

I put out the lamp and sat in the dark and let myself feel it, because feeling it was faster than pretending I wouldn't. Then I did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

Brother Edric's hands shook. He couldn't hold a nib to a fine point anymore; I'd watched him fail to sign the visitors' book at Michaelmas. Which left one pair of hands in this town trained to forge me flawlessly.

One pair of hands, belonging to someone who had access, patience, and — I realized, remembering the shell account opened with my own office address, remembering the gray-eyed stranger who had paid me to prove a murder wouldn't happen — possibly a second identity entirely.

* * *

Saturday morning I came in early, before the assistant's shift, and did one more thing. I took the six altered volumes, photographed every touched folio, and then — breaking my own first commandment, the one carved somewhere behind my sternum alongside *thou shalt not laminate* — I removed the current accession log for the reading room and locked it in my desk instead of returning it to its shelf.

If my student wanted to check their work, they would come back for it. And when they found it missing, they would know their teacher was awake.

Then I went upstairs, took out a fresh sheet of the good rag paper, and began, in my own hand, a complete list: every alteration found, every volume affected, every inference drawn, dated and signed and witnessed by no one. An honest record of dishonest records. If I vanished, if the archive burned, if the next reader was steered — this page would survive to say otherwise.

Halfway down the second sheet, my pen stopped.

Because I finally saw it — the thing the pattern had been telling me all along, the thing I'd been too insulted to hear. The alterations weren't hiding a crime. They were *preparing* one. Dates nudged, witnesses initialled, names blurred: someone was building a paper landscape in which a future event would look inevitable, documented, long-foreseen. The stranger's commission letter had sketched a murder in hypothetical prose. These files were sketching it in ink.

Fiction made flesh needed a paper skeleton. And I was looking at the bones being set, fracture by fracture, in my own handwriting.

Downstairs, faint through the floorboards, I heard the outer door open. Early. Too early.

My assistant wasn't due for another hour.
