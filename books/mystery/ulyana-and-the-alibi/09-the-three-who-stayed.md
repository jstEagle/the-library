## 9. The Three Who Stayed

The envelope had no stamp. That was the first thing Ulyana noticed, standing in her doorway with the morning paper still folded under her arm — an envelope with no stamp and no return address, which meant someone had walked up her path, past the gate she'd started locking in November, and posted it through the slot by hand.

Her name was written on the front in block capitals. Not her handwriting. Not anyone's handwriting, really — letters cut from newsprint and glued down, the kind of thing a child did for a ransom note, except there was no demand inside. There was only a page.

She knew the page before she opened it fully. She knew it by its weight, its faint foxing at the corners, the way the ink had gone brown at the folds. She had written it twenty years ago at a kitchen table while Verity Marsh made tea none of them drank and Brother Edric stood at the window with his back to the room like a man keeping watch for something that was never going to come.

*My name is Ulyana Voss and I am writing this so that it exists somewhere other than inside me.*

Someone had cut it apart. Whole sentences excised, words snipped out with scissors so that what remained read like scripture after a censor's pass:

*My name is ___ ___ and I am writing this so that it ___ somewhere other than ___ ___.*

She stood on her own doorstep until her tea went cold in her hand, reading the gaps where her own words used to be.

* * *

Twenty years ago there had been four of them in the crypt, and one of them had not walked out under his own power.

People said Father Anselm's death was a fall. A man of sixty-two, prone to dizzy spells, descending worn stone steps alone at night — the coroner had barely needed ten minutes to write *accidental*. The town had needed even less time to stop talking about it. Towns are efficient that way; grief is a currency they spend quickly and forget the exchange rate.

But Ulyana had been there. She had been twenty-six and newly hired to catalog the cathedral's vestments, and Verity Marsh — then Verity Aldous, wife of the cathedral's treasurer — had asked her to stay late on a December evening to help move a crate of candles. Edric had been there because Edric was always there; he lived in the bell tower the way a spider lives in a web, present in every corner without ever quite being seen. And Anselm had come down the steps white-faced and shaking, saying things Ulyana hadn't understood then and had spent twenty years refusing to understand now.

There had been shouting. There had been a scuffle near the old iron gate. And there had been the sound — the sound she still heard when the house went quiet, which was why her radio played all night, every night — of a body going down stone steps the way bodies do, all wrong angles and terrible finality.

Afterward, Verity had knelt in the candle wax and taken each of their hands in turn, and her voice had been so calm it was almost tender. *No one saw anything. No one was here. We were never here.* And then, looking at Ulyana last, holding her hand a moment longer: *You're new. You have nothing to lose. That makes you the most dangerous person in this room.*

Ulyana had written the confession two days later because she couldn't sleep, because the words kept assembling themselves behind her eyes like a filing system demanding entry. She'd sealed it, hidden it, told no one — but Verity had known. Verity always knew. That was the price of the tea and the calm voice: Verity had made her read it aloud, once, in that same kitchen, and then had helped her choose the hiding place, and had said, *Now we both know where your honesty lives,* and had smiled, and Ulyana had understood that she would never be free again.

Three of them stayed. Anselm didn't count anymore; Anselm was the reason they stayed.

Verity Marsh, who had buried a husband the following spring and turned widowhood into a career, who sat on the town council now and chaired the restoration fund and had a photograph in the parish newsletter shaking hands with the bishop. Verity, whose grief everyone praised, and whose grief Ulyana knew to be the most rehearsed performance in the county.

Brother Edric, who rang the bells, whose hands had begun to shake these last years — Ulyana had seen it at the Christmas service, watched him fumble the rope, watched him look down at his own fingers as if they belonged to a stranger. Dying men shake like that. Dying men also confess like that, eventually, if you wait long enough.

And herself. Ulyana Voss, who had spent twenty years being careful, and who had received a letter three weeks ago from a stranger asking questions about the winter solstice, and who had gone white in front of an archivist named Tamsin Crane because some part of her had recognized, finally, the sound of interest being charged on an old debt.

* * *

The second page came on Thursday.

Same envelope, same block capitals cut from newsprint. This time she found it in her letterbox at work — the small brass box outside the vestry office where the choir left sheet music returns. Someone had been inside the cathedral close. Someone who moved through those buildings the way she did, unremarked, expected.

This fragment was from deeper in the confession. She could tell because the paper was softer, handled more. Her own younger voice, amputated:

*I heard him say ___ name before he fell. I have never said ___ aloud. If I say it now, twenty years too late, I want it recorded that ___*

She sat down on the vestry steps and pressed the paper flat against her knee and made herself read it three times. *I heard him say ___ name before he fell.*

Anselm had said a name. She had written it down. She had never spoken it, not in twenty years, not even to herself in the dark — she had filed it away the way Tamsin Crane filed things, in a drawer that locked, and she had thrown away the key, or believed she had.

Whoever was cutting up her confession knew the whole document. Not fragments remembered, not rumor — the text itself, word for word, including the parts she had shown to no one. Which meant either they had found the original, or they had been in Verity Marsh's kitchen twenty years ago, listening.

Ulyana looked up. Across the close, the tower rose into gray sky, and beneath it the archive windows glowed with electric light, and she thought: *the archivist has my file.* She had seen it herself, thin and wrong, recently thinned. Someone had been pruning her out of the record before the record could speak.

* * *

The third envelope arrived on Sunday, and this time there was a demand.

It came with the collection plate — passed hand to hand down her pew during the morning service, an ordinary brown envelope among the folded banknotes, and she nearly dropped it when she felt the cut edges through the paper. She carried it out of the service untouched and opened it in the porch, cold air at her back, the congregation singing behind her about the mercy of God.

Inside: another fragment, and beneath it, in the same stolen alphabet:

*YOU KEPT A COPY. WE ALL KEPT COPIES. HERS IS GONE. WHERE IS YOURS?*

Ulyana read it twice. *Hers is gone.* Verity's copy — Verity had kept a copy of everything, that was her religion, insurance against all four of them — Verity's copy was gone. Taken. And whoever had taken it was now collecting the rest, the way a creditor collects, the way interest accrues on money nobody remembers borrowing.

She thought of the stranger with the questions. She thought of Tamsin Crane's careful face and careful hands. She thought of the solstice, three weeks past now, and the body that had been found in the crypt — not hers, though she had spent that night packed and ready by the door, certain it would be. Father Anselm's crypt, Father Anselm's death, repeated like a sentence being served twice.

And she understood, standing in the porch with the singing swelling behind her, that the envelopes were not threats. They were bookkeeping. Someone was settling accounts from twenty years ago, and they had begun with the dead man's crypt and they were working outward, name by name, page by page, and the next name after hers —

She didn't finish the thought, because across the close, coming out of the tower door into the thin light, she saw Brother Edric pause on the steps, press one trembling hand flat against the stone, and look directly at her window. Not at the cathedral. At her. As if he knew exactly what she held in her hands.

As if he had sent it.

Or as if he knew who had — and was trying, with whatever strength his failing heart had left, to warn her that the fourth envelope was already written, and that it had her name on it in full, uncut, spelled out at last.
