## 18. The Night Before

### Corinne

The trick to making an offer a man can't refuse, Corinne Voss had learned over sixty years of getting what she wanted, was never to let it sound like an offer. Offers could be declined. What you wanted instead was a conversation that had already ended before the other person realized they'd been in one.

So she arranged the archive meeting herself — not through Pell, whose nerves had gone to water, and not through the dean, who would only wring his hands and quote governance policy. She came at four o'clock on the last afternoon before the equinox, when the light through the archive's high windows went amber and long, and she brought a leather portfolio and a bottle of the good sherry, because Juniper Whitlock struck her as the sort of person who noticed details, and she wanted them to notice the right ones.

"Archivist." She set the portfolio on the reading table with a soft, expensive sound. "You'll forgive the intrusion. I know how you feel about your filing order."

"You've been told about my filing order?" Juniper didn't look up from the ledger they were annotating. Their pen moved in small, precise strokes. Corinne had watched that pen for two days now, through the vicarage window, through Pell's reports, through the particular quality of silence that followed Juniper down the cloister walks. A careful person. Careful people were either the easiest to buy or the hardest, and there was no way to know which until you named a figure.

"I'm told you alphabetize the dead," Corinne said pleasantly. "Voss under V. My father under V. My brother now under V. It must be a crowded shelf."

"It's a complete shelf." The pen stopped. Juniper looked up, and Corinne gave them the full weight of her attention — the smile she'd used on planning committees and hostile journalists and, once, memorably, a bishop. "Mrs. Voss. Was there something you needed from the records?"

"There's something I need *not* to be in the records." She opened the portfolio. Inside, arranged like a tasting menu: a letter of intent from the Halloway Trust pledging two hundred thousand pounds toward the digitization of the cathedral archives; a second document, drafted by her solicitor, establishing a personal stipend — generous, indexed, discreet; and a third page, which she turned face-down without showing him. Leverage kept its power best folded.

"The trust is real," she said. "The stipend is real. And this—" she tapped the hidden page once, lightly, "—is also real, but we won't discuss it, because discussing it would be vulgar, and I think you and I are neither of us vulgar people."

Juniper closed the ledger. "What is it you want me to do?"

"Nothing." She let the word sit. "That's the beauty of it. Tomorrow morning, the wardens' committee meets to consider cancelling the equinox opening. Structural concerns. Air quality. A death on the premises, after all — people find that distasteful. If the opening is cancelled, well." She spread her hands, a woman shrugging at weather. "The deed becomes moot. Your friend Maren inherits a hole in the ground nobody wants, and everyone goes home. All I need from you is your professional opinion, delivered to the committee, that the crypt should remain sealed pending review. One sentence. You've spent twenty years giving this town accurate sentences about its dead. Give it one more."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the trust withdraws, the stipend never existed, and—" she finally turned the third page around, and watched his face as he read it, "—the police receive a very interesting photograph of a certain confession letter, along with a note suggesting the archivist withheld evidence for weeks. Obstruction, they call it. It has such an ugly ring."

She saw it land. She'd expected anger, or fear, or that flinty pride clever people wore like armor. Instead Juniper Whitlock simply went still — the stillness of someone setting something down carefully so their hands will be free.

"Maren," Juniper said quietly. "You said protection. Protection from what?"

Ah. There it was. Corinne felt the familiar warm click of a lock turning. "From nothing at all, if tomorrow goes the right way. From a great deal, if it doesn't. Edmund's daughter has made herself conspicuous — lying to detectives, sneaking about the churchyard with a dying sexton. Conspicuous people attract attention, archivist. I'd hate for her to attract the wrong kind."

"You're threatening her."

"I'm *insuring* her." Corinne rose, smoothed her skirt, and poured two small glasses of the sherry anyway, because rituals mattered even when refused. She slid one across the table. "Think of it however you need to. But give me your answer by eight o'clock tonight. After that, events have a way of arranging themselves."

She left the glass untouched on the reading table, gleaming in the amber light, and walked out through the stacks without looking back. Looking back was for people who doubted.

She did not doubt. She had buried a father and a brother to keep what was hers. She could certainly bury an archivist's scruples.

### Juniper

The sherry sat on the table like a small amber accusation for a long time after she left.

Juniper didn't drink it. They carried it to the sink in the archive's back room and poured it out, watching it spiral down the drain, and thought: *this is what twenty years of neutrality comes to. A woman offers to buy your silence with money and threats, and the most radical thing you can imagine doing is pouring a glass of wine away.*

They sat back down at the reading table and took out the copy of Tobias Marsh's draft — the original was gone, stolen from the hymnal, but Juniper had transcribed it word for word the night Tobias gave it to them, in their own hand, in their own cipher of abbreviations that no thief would recognize as testimony. They read it again. *I saw her go down the steps the night Edmund died. I saw her come up alone. I have been dying of it ever since.*

Tobias lay in St. Aldhelm's Hospital with a fractured skull and a brain swelling against bone, and the doctors spoke in the passive voice — *he was found, he sustained, it remains to be seen* — and Juniper knew, the way you know things you've filed yourself, that Corinne's events were already arranging themselves.

Eight o'clock. She wanted an answer by eight o'clock.

Juniper looked around the archive — at the shelves they'd built, the ledgers they'd rebound, the long alphabetical patience of the dead — and understood, with a clarity that felt almost like grief, that the answer had been written years ago. That every year of obedient silence had been a payment on a debt, and the debt had just been called.

At seven-forty they wrote two things. The first was a short letter to Dean Halloway, stating their professional opinion that the equinox opening should proceed as scheduled, that the crypt's structural integrity was sound, and that any attempt to cancel it would constitute, in the archivist's judgment, interference with a legal instrument of record. They signed it. They left it on the dean's desk, where he would find it in the morning, too late to argue and too public to bury.

The second thing they wrote was smaller. A note, folded once, addressed to Maren Voss in care of the Bell Inn. *If anything happens to me, look in the 1897 parish register, third binding, spine cracked. Everything is there.* They slipped it into the evening post themselves, standing in the cold on the cathedral steps while the lamps came on across the close, and felt the strange lightness of a man who has finally mailed the truth.

Then they went back inside to finish cataloguing, because the work was the work, and because some stubborn clerkish instinct insisted that if the world was going to burn, it should at least burn in order.

It was nearly eleven when they heard the key turn in the archive door.

Not unlock. *Lock.* The heavy iron key, the one Pell kept on his ring, grinding home in the mortice with a sound Juniper had heard ten thousand times from the safe side of the door and never once from this one.

They crossed the stacks quickly, quietly, and tried the handle. It didn't move. Through the little wired-glass window in the door, the reading room beyond was dark, and the corridor beyond that was dark, and somewhere far off the cathedral clock began to strike the three-quarters.

"Pell," Juniper said aloud, to no one. But Pell shook too much to hold a key steady, and Pell, whatever else he was, had broken — had wept into his hands yesterday and said *I'll testify, God help me, I'll testify* — and this did not feel like Pell. This felt like arrangement. This felt like events.

Juniper turned back to the stacks and smelled it before they saw it.

Smoke. Faint, acrid, wrong — not candle-smoke, not incense, but paper. Burning paper, down among the shelves where a century of the town's memory stood in ordered rows.

They found it in the V section. Of course the V section. A small fire, exactly as promised — a neat nest of crumpled funeral registers wedged between the shelving and the wall, flames no higher than a hymnal, eating upward with terrible patience toward the ledgers above. Whoever set it hadn't wanted the archive destroyed. They'd wanted it *threatened*. A fire discovered in time, blamed on faulty wiring, an old building, a careless archivist working late — and any documents Juniper might have claimed to possess would be ash, and any archivist claiming them would be a fool or a liar or, best of all, an arsonist covering his tracks.

Unless the archivist burned with it. Then it was simply a tragedy. Grief, condolences, a memorial plaque. Corinne Voss attended lovely funerals.

Juniper didn't panic. Twenty years of filing had taught them that panic was just disorder wearing a loud coat. They pulled the nearest fire extinguisher — the red canister by the north door, inspected quarterly, tagged in their own handwriting — and put the nozzle to the base of the flames and squeezed.

The extinguisher coughed, sputtered, and produced a thin sad sigh of powder and nothing more. Empty. Or emptied. The tag in their own handwriting fluttered in the dead spray, dated last month, signed off, certified. Someone had discharged it and hung it back in its brackets and let Juniper walk past it every day.

The fire had climbed to the bottom shelf. Heat pressed against Juniper's face. Above the flames, the Voss registers — Aldous, Edmund, three generations of filed silence — began to brown and curl at their edges.

*Think,* Juniper told themselves, and the archive answered, because the archive always answered: the window in the map room, painted shut in 1989 according to the maintenance log, but the log lied sometimes, everything lied lately except the dust. The dumbwaiter shaft behind the periodicals, disused, wide enough for a body if the body was determined. The sprinkler risers that had never been connected to water because the restoration fund — Pell's fund, Corinne's fund — had been bled dry years ago.

The smoke was thicker now, tasting of lead paint and old glue. Juniper pulled their jacket over their mouth and went at the map room window with the fire axe from its bracket, swinging with the whole flat of their shoulders, and the first blow bounced, and the second cracked the casing, and on the fourth blow the painted-shut sash burst outward into the cold night air and the smoke leaned toward the opening like a living thing grateful for the door.

Glass shattered somewhere below — the cloister, the flowerbeds. Juniper leaned out into the freezing dark, lungs raw, eyes streaming, and shouted until their voice tore: for the watchman, for anyone, for the town that slept a hundred yards away on top of its secrets.

And below, at the edge of the lamplight where the cloister met the dark of the churchyard, a figure stood quite still, looking up. Not fleeing. Watching. A woman's silhouette, unhurried, hands folded, head tilted with something that even at this distance and in this light looked terribly like satisfaction — the look of a woman confirming a receipt.

Then the silhouette turned, and the dark took it, and somewhere beneath Juniper's feet, down past the floors and the foundations, past the stone stairs and the iron door, the crypt waited with its hundred years of held breath — and tomorrow at dawn, unless the town found its courage overnight, the doors would open, and whatever was sealed down there would finally have to be said out loud.

Juniper gripped the broken sill, coughing smoke into the cold, and made themselves one promise through the burning in their throat:

Dawn would come. And they would be standing in front of those doors when it did — with the ashes of the archive on their hands, and nothing left to lose, and everything, at last, to say.
