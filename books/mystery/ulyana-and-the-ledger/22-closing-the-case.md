## 22. Closing the Case

The file weighed less than August expected.

She had carried heavier things across the archive floor a thousand times — ledgers, deed boxes, the great leather-bound registers of baptisms that took both arms and a prayer to lift. But this folder, thin as a hymnal, seemed to pull downward with its own private gravity. Inside it: photographs of a crypt she would never again open without bracing herself, a seating chart annotated in two hands, an autopsy report she had read exactly once, and the confession, twenty-two pages in a careful, schoolroom script that looked so much like her own handwriting it had made Vance go quiet for a full minute when they first compared them.

*The Brightwater–Vance case, ref. SA/2019/Crypt,* she wrote on the closure slip in her best archival hand. *Closed. All materials accounted for.*

All materials accounted for. She almost laughed. As if paper could account for any of it.

Outside the archive's tall windows, February was doing what February did to St. Aldhelm's — turning the stone the colour of old milk, sending gusts down the close that rattled the yew trees like impatient fingers. The cathedral was emptying after morning service. Somewhere above her, the organist was running through the same four bars over and over, the way he always did when he thought no one minded. August minded, ordinarily. Today she let it be.

She slid the folder into its box, labelled the box, shelved it in the section reserved for closed cases — the ones that stayed closed — and stood there with her palm flat against the spine for longer than any professional act required.

Her brother was behind three inches of steel and glass eleven miles away, at HMP Ferndale, and she was going to see him this afternoon.

* * *

Vance drove them. August had stopped being surprised by this somewhere around November. The detective inspector had developed a habit, unremarked upon and never explained, of appearing at the archive on the days August visited, offering a ride in the tone of someone discussing parking permits. Today she wore civilian grey — a coat August suspected cost more than the detective's salary should allow — and drummed her thumb against the steering wheel through the whole of the ring road.

"You don't have to come," August said, for the fourth time since autumn.

"I know."

"He won't talk while you're there."

"He doesn't have to." Vance took the roundabout with the economy of a woman who resented every unnecessary degree of steering. "I'll read the paper in the car. I've gotten very good at reading papers in cars. It's practically all I do now that my one open case is your fault."

"My fault."

"Your archive. Your family. Your—" She waved a hand vaguely, encompassing the whole catastrophe. "You know what I mean. You're the reason I have a career-defining murder and a permanent low hum of dread about cardigans."

August looked out the window at the hedgerows going by, brown and patient. "You could have requested a transfer."

"I could have," Vance agreed, and said nothing else, which from Sorrel Vance was a whole paragraph.

The prison rose out of flat farmland like something the landscape had tried to swallow and failed — brick and wire under a sky the colour of dishwater. At reception, August surrendered her bag, her keys, the small penknife she'd carried since university and had never once used for anything but sharpening pencils. A officer with tired kind eyes led her down corridors that smelled of floor polish and boiled vegetables, and into a visiting room where the tables were bolted to the floor and the light came from strips too high to see out of.

He was already seated at the third table.

They had decided — or rather, August had proposed and the governor's office had approved — that she would not call him anything. Not the name on his birth amendment, not the name he'd been raised under, not *brother*, which still sat in her mouth like a coin she hadn't decided whether to spend. He had accepted this without argument. He accepted most things now with a strange, settled patience, as though the arrest had finally given his life a filing system.

"August," he said anyway, softly, because rules were for other people.

"Hello, Daniel."

She had started using his legal name in December. He'd flinched the first time; now he inclined his head, almost gratefully, as if each use of it laid another brick in the person he was trying to become.

He looked better than he had at the dinner. Everyone said so — his solicitor, the chaplain, even Vance, who had reviewed his custody photos with the detached eye of a woman cataloguing evidence and noted, in her flat way, that he'd gained weight, that the grey had gone out of his skin. Six months of regular meals and no access to archives. He sat with his hands folded, and August saw their shape clearly for the first time — the same long fingers she saw every day at the ends of her own wrists.

"I finished the carpentry course," he said. "They let me make a bookcase. For the library here. I measured everything twice."

"Of course you did."

That earned the ghost of a smile. "I wanted to ask—" He stopped. Started again. His voice was careful, a man walking on ice he himself had cracked. "The registry. Did they finish? The restoration?"

"They did." She kept her hands still on the table. This was the ritual now, theirs: he asked about the records, she reported on the records, and neither of them named what the records actually were. "Every razored page reconstructed. The 1984–89 volumes rebound. Your entry is legible again — both entries. Yours and mine. Same night, same ink, different columns."

"Different columns," he repeated, tasting it.

"One said *foundling*. One said *stillborn*. Neither one said the truth, which is that there were two children, and the ledger lied about both of us." She paused. "I amended mine. In the formal register, with a cross-reference. It's public now. Anyone can read it."

His eyes came up. There was something raw underneath the composure — six months of it had taught him composure, but not yet immunity. "And what does it say?"

"That I was born Ulyana Brightwater-Kessler. That I was placed privately through the diocesan foundling scheme in 1981. That my record was altered by persons unknown, with the diocese's cooperation, in exchange for a donation to the roof fund." She recited it evenly, the way she'd rehearsed it in the shower, in the queue at the post office, standing before the actual page with her pen uncapped for forty minutes before she could touch it to the vellum. "That I am not ashamed of any of it."

"And the other column?" His voice had gone very quiet. "Does anyone amend mine?"

August had thought about this more than she could say. At night, mostly. In the bath, in the archive after hours, standing in front of the crypt door with her key in her hand.

"I think," she said slowly, "that yours has to be written by you. That's the difference between us, Daniel. You were raised on a stolen version of events, and you built a life out of grievance because it was the only material anyone gave you. But the amendment is still available. It's always available. It just has to be in your own hand."

He looked at his folded fingers for a long time. When he spoke again, the carefulness had cracked slightly. "The clerk. Mrs. Okafor. I think about her every day. I want you to know that. Not because it helps her family — I know it doesn't — but because everyone assumes people like me don't think about it, and I do. Every day. Twice some days."

"I believe you."

"Do you?" And there it was, the real question, surfacing at last after months of circling it. "Do you actually?"

August considered lying, and rejected it — partly out of principle, partly because lying to him had been the whole architecture of the disaster. "I believe that you're sorry. I don't yet believe you understand what you destroyed. I think those are different things, and I think you'll get there, and I think it will take years, and I think you should serve every one of them."

Something in his shoulders loosened, oddly, at the honesty — as though the alternative, gentle falsehood, had been the thing pressing on him hardest.

"That's fair," he said. "That's more than fair. Thank you for not—"

"Performing forgiveness?"

"For performing anything."

A bell rang somewhere; the visit was ending. They stood, and there was an awkward moment in which they both seemed about to attempt something — a handshake, an embrace — and both settled instead on nothing, which felt correct. Two archivists of their own separate histories, respecting the integrity of the record.

At the door he turned. "August. The crypt. Have you—"

"I locked it last week," she said. "Properly. New mechanism, new keys, logged inspection. It's fine."

"It was never the lock I worried about."

"No," she agreed. "But it holds now anyway. Both meanings."

He smiled — really smiled, briefly, and for one unguarded second he was just a man who shared her jawline and her habit of measuring twice — and then the officer walked him away, and August stood alone in the corridor of boiled vegetables until her legs agreed to carry her back out into the dishwater light.

* * *

Vance was leaning against the car, newspaper folded under her arm, and she didn't ask how it went, which August appreciated beyond the capacity of ordinary language to express.

"Chip shop on the way back?" Vance said instead.

"It's eleven miles out of our way."

"So it is." She opened the driver's door. "Get in."

They ate chips in the car park of the chip shop, which sat between a garden centre and a defunct petrol station, steaming up the windscreen, and this — August realised, watching gulls patrol the tarmac with the entitlement of cathedral clergy — was what found family actually looked like. Not the candlelit warmth of the anniversary dinner where Vance had gripped her arm and whispered *don't look at him yet, look at me, keep breathing*. This. Grease on paper. Silence that wasn't empty.

"The bishop wants to give you a commendation," Vance said eventually. "Some kind of public acknowledgment at Easter. Service to justice, or whatever the brass plaque will say."

"I'll decline."

"I told him you would. He wants to give it to me instead, and I'm declining too, so we can be insufferable together."

"Why are you declining?"

Vance chewed, considered, swallowed. "Because we caught him by lying to him. Beautifully, I'll grant. The forged file was a masterpiece of your particular sickness. But I don't put plaques on traps." She glanced sideways. "Also because if anyone gets a plaque, it's the verger, who spent nine years terrified and still handed over the dead-drop address with both hands shaking. Has he heard back from the diocese about the pension review?"

"Approved. Full arrears." August smiled slightly. "He cried in the vestry. Then alphabetised the candle inventory, which is how he cries."

"Good man." Vance balled up the chip paper with sniper precision into the footwell bin. "One more thing, and then I'll stop being a police officer for the rest of the afternoon. The Okafor family. Her sister wrote to the diocese — she's asking whether the original 1984 ledger page, the true one, could be displayed. Publicly. With Daniel's name on it and the donors' names and all of it. She said—" Vance hesitated, uncharacteristically. "She said her sister spent her life guarding records nobody was allowed to see, and she thinks the only fitting memorial is a record everybody can."

August sat with that for a moment. Through the windscreen the gulls wheeled over the garden centre, screaming their small eternal grievances at a sky that owed them nothing.

"Tell her yes," August said. "Tell her I'll build the display case myself. Climate-controlled. UV-filtered. And tell her—" Her throat tightened unexpectedly. "Tell her the archive owes her family a debt it can't repay, but it can stop hiding."

"I'll write to her tonight." Vance turned the key; the engine coughed awake. "Right. I'm off duty as of now, which means I'm allowed to say that your brother's carpentry looks terrible in the custody photos and I'm glad you went."

"It's the finest bookcase in HMP Ferndale."

"I don't doubt it. He probably measured twice."

"Three times," August said. "We do three."

Vance laughed — a real laugh, rare as a solar event — and pulled out onto the road toward the spire of St. Aldhelm's rising over the hedgerows, grey stone catching what little light the afternoon had left.

* * *

The cathedral was nearly dark when August returned. Evening service had ended; the vergers had gone home; only the cleaning lights burned in the nave, pooling gold on the flagstones like coins dropped and forgotten. She let herself into the crypt stairwell with her own new key, feeling the familiar cool air rise to meet her — stone-smelling, candle-adjacent, the breath of the building's oldest room.

She didn't need to go down. The inspection was done, logged, signed. Everything below was exactly where it should be: the tombs, the ossuary niches, the small alcove where a woman had once lain wearing August's own cardigan, arranged like an exhibit in a gallery whose curator had lost his mind.

But she went down anyway. Some rooms require revisiting on your own terms, or they keep the visit scheduled for you.

She stood where the body had lain and looked at the space, which held nothing now but shadow and the faint mineral chill of centuries. Six months ago she had opened this door for an annual inspection and found her ordered world razored open like the registry pages. She had spent those months learning what she should perhaps have known all along: that an archive is not a fortress. That a curated past is a story, and stories get edited, and the editor is sometimes you, and sometimes it's someone who loved you in the only broken way they'd been taught.

Her brother had wanted to be seen. That was the sentence she'd finally written across the whole case, the marginal note that explained all the others. He had staged crimes from her files backward because forward had never once, in his entire life, pointed toward him. Reversal was the only direction from which anyone arrived at his existence. Even his murders had been a kind of citation — *see here, see the source, see the ledger that erased me* — footnotes written in the worst possible ink.

August took out the small brass key, hers alone now, and locked the crypt door. She tested it — twice, because they did three, and twice was enough when you knew exactly what the lock held. Not horror, anymore. Just truth: bones, stone, a history with its wounds sutured but visible. A room that had held a stranger wearing her clothes, and now held only the memory of her, which was a thing no lock had ever been built to contain and none needed to.

She climbed the stairs, closed the door at the top, and stood a moment in the quiet nave, listening to the building settle around her like an animal folding itself for sleep.

On her desk upstairs, the closure slip waited for her signature. She signed it, dated it, and then — after a pause, because precision mattered, because the record mattered, because she had learned at terrible cost what happened when records were left silent — she added a final line beneath *Closed*:

*Amended and balanced. See also: registry, foundling, 1981–84. See also: Brightwater, Ulyana; Brightwater-Kessler, Daniel. All names restored to the page.*

She blotted it, filed it, and switched off the lamp.

In the dark, the archive breathed around her — ten thousand boxes, a thousand years of names, every one of them waiting to be read honestly. And for the first time since a locked door had opened on a nightmare in February, August Brightwater stood among them feeling not like their keeper, nor their prisoner, but simply their author: one voice among many, writing the next line in her own hand.

Somewhere below, the crypt held its peace. Above, the cathedral held its light. And the ledger — her ledger, their ledger, the whole stubborn human ledger of who belonged to whom — lay open at last, every column filled, every name accounted for.

All materials accounted for.
