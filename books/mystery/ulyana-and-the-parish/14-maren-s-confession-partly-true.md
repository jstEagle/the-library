## 14. Maren's Confession, Partly True

The fog came up the valley that morning like something with intent, pouring over the ridge in a slow grey flood until Hollowmere's rooftops vanished one by one and only the church spire remained, a black needle stitching sky to earth. Ulyana watched it from Maren Vale's kitchen window while the kettle worked itself toward a whistle it never quite reached—the stove was old, and the flame beneath burned low and blue and patient, like everything else in that house.

Maren had made tea without asking how Ulyana took it. Two cups, both chipped at the lip, set on a table scrubbed so many times its grain had gone pale as bone. She sat across from Ulyana with her hands folded around her cup as though warming them at a fire that wasn't there, and she waited. She was very good at waiting. Ulyana understood now that she had been practicing for ten years.

"You said you'd tell me about the will," Ulyana said at last, because silence was a language she knew too well and would not be drawn into by an expert.

"I said I'd tell you what you needed to hear." Maren's eyes were the colour of the fog itself, pale and depthless. "Whether they're the same thing is between me and God, and He and I have an arrangement."

"Which is?"

"He forgives me slowly. I forgive Him quickly. It balances."

Outside, a rook called from the yew tree by the gate, once, twice, and then thought better of it. The kitchen smelled of woodsmoke and dried lavender hung in bundles from the ceiling beams, and beneath both—faint but unmistakable, the way a grave smell lives under flowers—the cold mineral scent of the mill pond, which lay a quarter mile off and which seemed somehow to have followed Maren home and settled into her walls.

Ulyana wrapped her hands around her cup. "Mrs. Vale. Maren. I've read the will four times. I've compared the hand to every sample of Edric Vale's writing in the parish archive. It's his hand, or close enough to survive scrutiny, and yet it names Daniel as beneficiary, and Daniel has been dead for ten years, and no solicitor in three counties ever saw this document before Tuesday." She made herself stop, made her voice drop to the register she used with hostile witnesses. "You told Tamsin you wrote it. So before I decide whether I'm holding evidence or a confession, I need you to say it plainly."

Maren looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed—not cruelly, not warmly either, but with a kind of exhausted tenderness, the laugh of a woman who had rehearsed this conversation against a wall for a decade and found the real audience smaller and quieter than imagined.

"Plainly," she repeated. "Lawyers. Edric was the same. *Say it plainly, Maren. Give me the plain truth.*" She drank, wincing at the heat. "All right. Plainly, then. My husband did not write that will. I wrote it. I traced his hand from his old letters until my wrist ached for a week. And I sealed it and hid it where he kept his real papers, because I knew that when he died—and he was dying, Ulyana, the doctors gave him a year and God gave him eight months—someone would come for a lawyer, and someone would come for you."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only one in this parish who reads everything." Maren said it simply, without flattery. "The council men skim. Dane skims. Even Rowan Pike reads like a man looking for exits. But you—you read the way priests pray. Line by line. Believing something is underneath." She set down her cup. "I needed someone who would notice that a dead man's name was sitting in a living man's will, and instead of striking it out, would *wonder*."

* * *

Ulyana had prepared arguments on the walk up from the village: that forgery was a crime regardless of motive; that anything Maren said could be used against her; that Ulyana was already under suspicion for obstruction and could not afford to be complicit in another. She had rehearsed them the way she rehearsed cross-examinations, in the fog, aloud, startling a pheasant near the stile.

She got none of them out. Because Maren, watching her open her mouth, raised one finger—an old woman's small imperious gesture, a schoolmistress's gesture, a mother's gesture—and said:

"Before you lecture me, girl, let me tell you about the night my son died. Or didn't."

The kettle finally whistled, thin and wavering, and neither of them moved to take it off the flame. It screamed itself hoarse and sank back into a simmer, and Maren began to speak in the level voice of someone reciting coordinates, each fact placed precisely, nothing wasted.

"You know the official story. Daniel hanged himself in Hallowmere Prison on the ninth of March, ten years ago, in the early hours. Bedsheet over the cell door bar. Found at the six o'clock count. Buried in the prison plot because we couldn't afford to move him and weren't allowed to anyway, pending—" her mouth twisted— "*pending inquiries.* You know all that because it's in the file. What isn't in the file is that Inspector Halloway came to this house two days later, alone, out of uniform, smelling of gin and grief, and sat in that chair you're sitting in, and said: *Maren. Do you want your son or do you want justice? You cannot have both, not both living.*"

Ulyana's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"There had been a body," Maren went on. "There always is, in these arrangements—that's what makes them work. A prisoner transferred through, no family, no name anyone pressed hard about. Halloway had signed the transfer papers himself. He'd been carrying the arrangement in his breast pocket for months, he told me, like a stone, waiting to see if he'd ever have the stomach for it. Daniel was going to hang himself whether I agreed or not—the alternative was that he'd do it properly, in despair, some bad night, because he'd stopped eating and started talking about seeing Tom Weller's face in the window glass." Her voice did not break. That was the terrible thing, Ulyana thought. Ten years had burnished her grief into something smooth and load-bearing, a beam the whole house rested on. "Tom Weller. The man Daniel was convicted of killing. The man I will swear to my grave he did not kill."

"Do you believe that? Truly?" Ulyana asked, because it was her trade to ask, and because some part of her needed to hear it said plainly, as demanded.

Maren leaned forward. The lavender bundles stirred above her in a draught from the ill-fitting door.

"My son came home from the war of this parish's making with a limp and a stutter and a gentleness that people mistook for weakness. He cried at lambing season. He once carried a wounded crow nine miles in his coat to get it to a vet who charged him a fortnight's wages and told him it was a fool's errand. That boy could not have strangled Tom Weller in the vestry and then walked home past the vicarage whistling—which is what the prosecution said he did, whistling, can you imagine, they put it in the *summing up*, the whistling, because it sounded like callousness if you'd never heard Daniel whistle, which was rare, and tuneless, and usually a sign he was frightened." She sat back. "So yes. I believe it truly. And Halloway believed it, which mattered more, because Halloway had been first through the vestry door that night, and Halloway had seen things in that room that never made it into any report. Things that pointed away from my son. Things he buried, or was made to bury—I still don't know which, and neither does he anymore, I think. His conscience and his career have been strangling each other for ten years and lately the career has been losing."

"The body in the prison," Ulyana said carefully. "It was never independently identified."

"No. I asked. Oh, how I asked. I wrote letters for years—prison board, coroner, Home Office. Every reply was the same polite brick wall. And then I understood that the wall was the answer. If there had been a proper identification, a photograph, dental records, anything a mother could hold—then someday, if Daniel ever surfaced, someone might compare. The wall wasn't neglect, Ulyana. The wall was maintenance." She smiled, and it was the most frightening thing Ulyana had seen in weeks, worse than the dead stranger on the parish steps wearing her scarf. "They built it to keep the world out. They didn't understand they were also building it to keep my son in. In hiding. In a life that wasn't his. For ten years my Daniel lived forty miles from here under another man's name, doing labour no one asked questions about, and I visited him twice a year as his 'aunt,' and I sat across a table from my own child and called him by a stranger's name and watched him learn to answer to it."

The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled. Ulyana became aware that her tea had gone cold in her hands and that she was gripping the cup hard enough to hurt, and she made herself loosen her fingers one at a time.

"And two years ago," she said quietly, "he vanished again."

Maren's composure moved for the first time—a flicker behind the eyes, a shutter caught by wind.

"Yes."

"Tell me."

"He changed." The word cost her; Ulyana watched it being paid out. "Slowly, at first, the way rot works in timber—from inside, so the surface looks sound until you lean on it. After the fourth year he stopped asking about the case. Stopped asking if there was news. I thought it was healing. It wasn't. It was—*hollowing*. He'd sit with his hands flat on the table like a man waiting for a search. He'd flinch at bells. And then one visit he looked at me—he looked *through* me, Ulyana, that's the honest way to say it—and he said, 'Auntie, do you ever think some debts are better paid than disputed?' And I knew. I knew whatever Halloway had buried in that vestry had followed him. Or he'd gone digging for it himself. Either way, he'd found the edge of something, and something on the other side of that edge had noticed him finding it."

"Did he say who?"

"He said a name once. Half a name. He said, 'It goes higher than the constable, Mum—it goes to the table.'" Maren's jaw tightened. "The table. The parish council table. Where Cornelius Ashe has sat for thirty years with his hands folded like a bishop. And then, two years ago this autumn, Daniel missed a visit. Then another. The lodging house said he'd gone. Left no forwarding address. Took nothing but his boots and a canvas bag." Her hands, folded on the table, had gone white at the knuckles. "His boots. He loved those boots. He'd resoled them himself. A man doesn't leave his boots unless he means never to be recognized walking anywhere again—or unless he's made to leave them."

* * *

"So the will," Ulyana said, when she could speak steadily again. "Your beacon."

"I needed him to come home." Maren's voice dropped to almost nothing, and the fog outside seemed to lean closer to listen. "Dead men can't be threatened. Dead men can't be followed. If Daniel was alive and running, the only way to give him shelter was to make the whole parish believe he was already in his grave—believe it louder than ever. So I forged Edric's will and left Daniel everything. Not money—we have nothing—but the *name*. The estate. The standing. A dead man resurrected on paper, in public, in front of witnesses, with a lawyer reading it aloud." She met Ulyana's eyes. "Because I knew if Daniel heard of it—if he was alive, if he could hear anything—he would come. A son doesn't let his mother stand alone in a parish house while strangers argue over his ghost. Whatever else they broke in him, they didn't break that."

"And instead," Ulyana said, "a stranger died on the steps wearing my clothes."

The silence that followed had texture, weight, the silence of a held breath ten years long.

"Yes," Maren said. "That I did not plan. That I—" For the first time, her certainty cracked, and Ulyana saw beneath it not calm but a vast, disciplined terror, held down the way one holds down a trapdoor with one's whole body. "I drew a beacon, girl, and something else answered it. Something that knows my son's story well enough to wear it. That body on the steps—Rowan tells me the man limped. Left leg, the same as Daniel. Same height, near enough. Someone dressed a corpse in my son's death and served it to this parish like a course at dinner." She stood abruptly, went to the window, stood with her back straight as a gravestone. "Either my Daniel is out there watching his own resurrection be murdered—or someone wanted us all to believe he'd come home, so that we'd stop looking for whoever made him disappear."

"Or," Ulyana said, very softly, "the person who killed that stranger wanted *me* to carry it. The clothes were mine, Maren. The scarf was monogrammed. That message had one addressee."

Maren turned. The fog-light made her face strange, half-lit, ancient.

"Then we're both bait," she said. "You for the killer. Me for my son. And the question, lawyer, the plain question you came here to ask—is whether you can sit at this table knowing I committed forgery, knowing I conspired with a police inspector to fake a death, knowing I've lied to every court and clerk in this county for ten years—" her chin lifted— "and still help me find out what happened to my boy. Because I won't apologize for any of it. Love makes criminals of better women than me, and I'd do every piece of it again, twice, in winter."

Ulyana looked down at her cold tea, at the chipped cup, at the pale worn grain of the table between herself and this woman who had broken a dozen laws with a mother's hands and slept, apparently, like the just.

Every instinct she had been trained to honour said: withdraw. Report. Distance yourself. The careful life, the invisible life, whispered its old litany—*you'll lose the license, you'll lose everything, you've barely anything left to lose as it is.*

And underneath the litany, fainter but growing, another voice—the one that had cracked open in her the night she'd refused Cornelius Ashe's bribe, the one that had warmed at bad stew among outcasts—said: *she forged a will to call her son home. You were summoned to read it. Perhaps you were chosen after all.*

"I'm not helping you cover a crime," Ulyana said slowly. "But I am telling you that a will naming a beneficiary legally dead may be defective—and that defects can sometimes be cured by proof of life." She looked up. "If Daniel Vale walks into this parish alive, the law will want to know where he's been. And so will whoever killed a man to make us think he hadn't. Which means, Mrs. Vale, that we need to find him before they do."

Maren crossed the room and took Ulyana's cold hands in her hot ones, and her grip was fierce and trembling and grateful all at once.

"There's one more thing," she said. "One thing I haven't told even Rowan. The night before the reading—the night before that poor devil died on the steps—someone left a note in my door. No name. Just five words, in handwriting I haven't seen in ten years and would know blindfolded." Her eyes were wet and blazing. "It said: *Mother, burn the will. Please.*"

Ulyana felt the floor tilt beneath the house, beneath the parish, beneath ten years of graves.

Daniel was alive. Daniel had been *here*—

And someone had gotten to him first.
