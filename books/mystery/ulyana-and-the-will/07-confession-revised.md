## 7. Confession, Revised

The second letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. All the worst news of my life has arrived on Tuesdays—the fire report, the probate notice, the cancellation of my first podcast deal. Tuesdays are the Mondays of people who've already given up on Mondays.

I knew before I opened it that it was from the same hand as the first two. The confession signed with my mother's name had come in cream paper; the unsigned promise of "the rest of the truth" had come in white. This one was gray, like weather, and my name was written across the front in that same careful, old-fashioned cursive—each letter built rather than written, the penmanship of someone who learned to write when writing still cost something.

Inside, one page.

*Lior —*

*You will have noticed by now that the first letter was not true. My mother did not write it. She could not have; she has been dead for thirty years, and she was never the sort to confess to anything, even alive.*

*I forged it. I am sorry for the forgery but not for the purpose. You were never going to come home otherwise. You built a career out of other families' fires because yours was too hot to touch. Someone had to make you touch it.*

*Ask your aunt what happened on the last Tuesday of October. Ask her why the attic is locked. Ask her who taught her to lie so gently that you'd call it love.*

*Then ask yourself what you'll do when the answer breaks her.*

*— A friend of the truth, if not of yours*

I read it four times. On the fourth reading, my hands were steady enough to notice the detail that mattered most: *she has been dead for thirty years.* Not "died in the fire." Dead. As though the fire were incidental. As though the writer knew something about the manner of her dying that no coroner's report had ever contained.

I sat in my car outside Marguerite's house—the house, the Brightwater house, the set-dressed museum of my childhood—and watched the kitchen light burn yellow through the frost on the windows. Somewhere inside, my aunt was probably making tea she wouldn't drink, the way she always did, brewing a pot as an excuse to stand still.

I had spent six weeks asking questions I thought were mine. Standing there, envelope trembling in my lap, I understood for the first time that they weren't mine at all. Someone had handed them to me, one at a time, like a parent feeding string to a kite.

The question stopped being *who started the fire?*

It became *who wanted me here, holding the match?*

* * *

Vera

He came back on Thursday. The podcaster. My brother, though he doesn't know it yet, and may never, and God willing the paperwork holds.

He sat across the table in the hospice break room with his recorder between us like a small black bird, and he asked me about the fire, and I lied to him the way I lie to families every day—with kindness, with competence, with the gentle voice I use to tell people their mother isn't in pain anymore.

But he's better at this than I gave him credit for. He didn't push. He just let the silence do the pushing, and silence is the one thing I can't medicate away.

"You said last time," he finally said, "that the Brightwater house had 'a smell you don't forget.' Most people would say smoke. What did you mean?"

And there it was. The trapdoor under the floor I'd been standing on.

Because I hadn't meant smoke. I'd meant lilies. My mother kept lilies in the east wing, and the year I turned nine, the year before everything, she took me into that wing and showed me a portrait of a woman with my eyes and told me, in the only act of honesty of her life, that the woman was my grandmother and that I had a father and a brother on the other side of that portrait's frame, and that I must never, ever say so.

"Smoke," I said. "Everyone means smoke."

He nodded slowly, and I watched him file the lie away, and I understood that I had just made things worse, because now he knew I was lying, and a man like him doesn't stop at knowing—he documents.

After he left, I sat in my car in the hospice lot and thought about the letters. Both of them. The forged confession and whatever came after, because I could see in his face that something else had come after.

Whoever was writing them knew about me. That was the part that made my hands shake against the steering wheel. The first letter drew him home; fine, cruel, effective. But the second letter—the second letter pointed him at the attic, at the will, at the seams of the whole arrangement, and the only people who knew where those seams were were Marguerite, Desmond Hale, Tomas, and me.

Four people. And I hadn't written any letters.

Which left three.

* * *

Lior

Marguerite made tea she didn't drink. I sat across from her in the kitchen where she'd bandaged every scrape of my childhood, and I laid both letters on the table between us like evidence, which is what they were, and watched her face do something I had never seen it do in forty-one years of knowing her.

Nothing.

Marguerite Brightwater's face had always been a public utility—warmth on demand, grief appropriately lit, joy slightly overexposed. Now it went dark. Blank. The face of a woman standing in a doorway deciding whether to run.

"Lior," she said, and her voice was the voice from my childhood, the one that meant *eat your vegetables, wear your coat, don't ask about that room,* "you need to stop this."

"I know," I said. "That's what worries me. Everyone who wants me to stop keeps being someone with something to hide."

"I have nothing to hide."

"Aunt Marguerite." I turned the first letter toward her. "Someone forged a confession in my dead mother's handwriting and mailed it to me. Someone else—or maybe the same someone—told me to ask you about the last Tuesday of October. So I'm asking. What happened on the last Tuesday of October?"

"That was the fire," she said. Too fast. Thirty years too rehearsed.

"The fire was the twenty-ninth. The last Tuesday of October was the twenty-sixth. Three days before." I had checked. Of course I had checked; checking dates is the only prayer I know. "What happened three days before the fire?"

And Marguerite Brightwater, who once drove four hours through an ice storm to hold my hand when I had appendicitis, who lied to social workers with a smile to keep me, who opened every door in that house for every stray child in the county—my aunt put her hand flat on the table to steady herself, and then she put her other hand over her heart, and then she went down.

Not dramatically. That's the thing I can't get out of my head. No gasp, no performance. She just folded, quietly, like a letter being closed, and slid sideways off the chair, and I caught her before her head hit the tile, and she weighed almost nothing, and I realized I had no idea how long it had been since anyone had weighed her at all.

I called the ambulance. I held her wrist and counted her pulse and talked to her the way she used to talk to me—nonsense, comfort, *stay with me, stay with me*—and somewhere in the middle of it, kneeling on the kitchen floor of the house where every lie of my life had been furnished and decorated, I finally saw the shape of the thing I'd been walking around for six weeks.

The letters weren't addressed to me. Not really. Cream paper, white paper, gray paper—they were all just packaging. The letters were aimed past me, through me, at the people around me. Every question I asked landed on Marguerite like a blow. Every record I pulled rattled Desmond Hale's office. Every interview scraped against Tomas's thirty years of loyal silence. And Vera—

Vera, whose eyes are my eyes, whose lies are structured exactly like Marguerite's, kind and load-bearing.

I wasn't the investigator. I had never been the investigator. I was the weapon—someone else's—being swung at the family that raised me, and the person swinging it had known exactly which bones were weakest.

In the ambulance, Marguerite woke briefly. She looked up at me, and her eyes were clear and terrified, and she said, in a voice sanded down to almost nothing:

"Don't open the attic, sweetheart. Whatever he tells you—don't open the attic."

*He.*

She closed her eyes again before I could ask. But the pronoun hung in the air between us all the way to the hospital, bright and terrible as a flare: *he.* Not *they.* Not *the letter-writer.* He.

There were only three men who had been close enough to the family to know where the bodies—in every sense—were buried. One was my groundskeeper, who had wept genuine tears during our interview. One was my lawyer, who had loved my mother and, I was beginning to suspect, loved me.

And one was whoever had taught my mother to sign her own name in a hand she'd abandoned thirty years ago.

At the hospital, while they ran tests I couldn't afford to think about, I sat in the corridor under fluorescent light and took out my phone and looked at the photograph I'd taken of the gray envelope's postmark before I'd even opened it, because I am, whatever else is true, very good at my job.

The postmark wasn't local. It wasn't from the city where Vera lived, or the town where Tomas had retired.

It was from Ashford. Forty miles from the Brightwater estate. The town where Desmond Hale had kept his practice for fifty years—and where, according to the probate records I'd photographed in week three, he maintained a second office he had never listed anywhere, in a building he owned outright, on a street called Atticus Lane.

I don't believe in coincidences. I've interviewed enough killers to know that coincidence is just motive wearing a disguise.

My aunt was unconscious down the hall. My half-sister was lying to me by phone twice a day. And somewhere in Ashford, behind an unlisted door, the person who had forged my mother's hand and aimed me like a rifle at everyone I loved was waiting to see if the shot would land.

I got in my car and drove toward him.

I just didn't yet know whether I was driving there to arrest him—or to thank him.
