## 6. The Annuity Trail

### Hazel

The bank in Marlow's Crossing kept its records the way I kept mine: badly, but with conviction.

Theo drove us out in his brother's borrowed sedan, a car that smelled of pipe smoke and old hymnals, and for two hours we watched the cathedral country flatten into hedgerows and feed stores while I rehearsed my cover story against the window glass. Archivist was a useful word. It sounded harmless. It sounded like someone who would never set fire to anything or ask inconvenient questions about dead women who weren't dead.

"You're muttering," Theo said.

"I'm practicing."

"Practice quieter. You'll scare the teller."

I shot him a look. He smiled at the road, that particular smile of his—sideways, unhurried, as though he had all the time in the world and intended to waste it beautifully. Two weeks ago he'd told me about the sealed letter and Judge Crane, and something between us had shifted from friendship into the territory where trust stops being comfortable and starts being load-bearing. We were carrying each other now. It made me careful and it made me reckless in equal measure.

The annuity trail had begun with arithmetic, which is how all true things begin. Vane's estate ledger showed quarterly disbursements to "M.C." through the Merchants' Trust of Marlow's Crossing until twenty years ago, when they stopped—not because the payments ended, but because the payee did. *Deceased*, the final entry read, in a clerk's tidy hand. No date of death. No certificate number. Just the word, dropped into the ledger like a stone into a well.

People who die leave paperwork. People who are erased leave only the hole where paperwork should be.

The Merchants' Trust occupied a granite building on the square, and inside it a teller with a mustache like a startled hedgehog listened to my story—that I was tracing an inheritance for a client—and produced, after forty minutes of rummaging, a box of payment vouchers.

"The disbursements went to St. Ambrose Convent," he said. "Care of the Mother Superior. For a boarder."

"A boarder," I repeated.

"That's what it says. 'Board and care of M.C.' Twenty-two years of it." He frowned at the last voucher. "Then a letter came instructing us to cease. Signed by—" he squinted— "A. Crane, attorney for the estate."

Of course it was. The judge's fingerprints were on everything; he simply never left them himself. He used attorneys the way other men used gloves.

"Is there a forwarding address?" Theo asked. "For the boarder?"

The hedgehog mustache twitched. "Son, St. Ambrose closed nine years ago. Nuns scatter like sparrows when the money dries up." He paused, then lowered his voice as if the vault itself might gossip. "Though there was one sister stayed on till the end. Sister Perpetua. Kept the grounds herself, near enough. Died this past winter at the county home over in Ashford."

### Theo

I have spent thirty years reading silences—the rest between notes, the breath before a phrase, the pause a congregation makes when the sermon turns personal. So I knew, watching Hazel work the county home's front office, that she was frightened. She hid it the way she hid everything: behind competence so polished it looked like armor.

Sister Perpetua had died in February, but the home kept her effects in a cedar chest pending claims from an order that no longer existed to claim them. The administrator, a soft-spoken man named Dill, needed persuading. Hazel persuaded him. I mostly stood by the window holding my hat and looking, I hoped, like a church musician—which is to say, harmless and slightly damp.

What unsettled me was not the subterfuge. It was how good she was at it. There is a version of Hazel Lockridge that could have been anyone—a detective, a forger, a spy—and every mile we drove toward Grayhaven, I felt that version standing just behind her shoulder, patient as a shadow. I loved her curiosity. I was beginning to understand that her curiosity had teeth.

Dill led us down a corridor that smelled of disinfectant and boiled cabbage to the cedar chest. Inside: two habits, a rosary worn smooth as river glass, a Bible, and a bundle of letters tied with black thread.

"She wrote to herself, near the end," Dill said. "Or to somebody. Never sent them. Sister Agnes who shared her room said she'd get confused sometimes—thought she was back at St. Ambrose, keeping watch over somebody." He hesitated. "She used to say a name in her sleep. Miriam. We assumed it was family."

Hazel's hands went very still over the letters. Stillness, in Hazel, is what other people's trembling looks like.

"May we?" she asked.

Dill looked at the letters, then at her face, and whatever he saw there made him nod once and withdraw to his office, closing the door with the tact of a man who has decided not to know things.

The letters were written in a hand that deteriorated across twenty years—from firm copperplate to a spider's scrawl. Most were prayers. Some were inventories: *the roof leaks again, the east wing is sold, the pear tree came down in the storm.* And then, third from the bottom, dated four years ago:

*Miriam came to me in the night before she went away. She said, If anyone ever asks, tell them I am where the light keeps turning. She made me promise to forget her, and I promised, and I have failed, because God does not let you forget what you promised Him to forget. She is alive. Whatever the papers say, she is alive, and I have carried this alone too long.*

Below it, in a shakier hand, a later addition:

*Grayhaven Point. The lighthouse. Forgive me, Miriam. A dying woman's promises rot fastest.*

Hazel read it twice. Then she sat down on the corridor bench, quite suddenly, the way a building settles.

"She's alive," she said. Not to me. To the letters, maybe. To the whole twenty years of erasure stacked between Edmund Vane's empty coffin and this cedar chest.

"Yes," I said.

"And Crane thinks she's dead. He stopped the payments. He buried her on paper." Her eyes came up, and I saw the thing underneath the fear, the engine under the hood: hope, sharpened to a point. "If she's alive, she's a witness. If she's a witness, the trial isn't Marsh's anymore. It's hers."

"It's ours," I said, "for the next six days. Then it's the court's."

She almost smiled. "You've been talking to Marsh."

"Somebody in this partnership has to count the days."

* * *

### Hazel

We drove toward the coast through the tail of a storm, wipers slapping, the road running out ahead of us like a sentence being cut short. Six days until trial. Five until we reached Grayhaven, if the map didn't lie. Somewhere behind us, Crane was presiding over arraignments in his courtroom, wearing his robes and his reputation, believing—as he had believed for twenty years—that Miriam Cade was a closed account.

I thought about what it costs a woman to stay dead. Twenty years of no name, no letters, no face in any photograph. I knew something about invisibility myself—I had haunted that cathedral archive for a decade, solving deaths nobody wanted solved, signing nothing. But I had chosen my ghosthood. Hers had been imposed, and enforced by threats I was only beginning to measure, and still she had survived it. That took a will like bedrock.

"What if she won't talk?" Theo asked, somewhere past the county line.

"Then I'll ask anyway."

"And if asking isn't enough?"

I watched the rain crawl sideways across the fields. "Then I'll show her the letters. Crane's handwriting on the order to stop her payments. His name in Vane's ledger margins. Four deaths that all happened within a season of someone getting close to her." I paused. "And then I'll tell her about the coffin. About the stones. Somebody put those stones there, Theo, and I don't think it was sentiment. I think it was a message: *we know what we buried, and it wasn't him.*"

He was quiet for a long moment. The storm thinned. Ahead, the land gave way to salt marsh, and beyond the marsh, gray water, and beyond the water—

"There," he said.

Grayhaven Point rose out of the sea mist like a knuckle: black rock, white foam, and the lighthouse standing at its tip, paint peeling, gallery rail rusted—but the lamp room intact, and, unmistakably, a thread of woodsmoke rising from somewhere at its base.

Somebody was keeping the light.

We left the car at the end of the track and walked the last half mile against a wind that seemed personally offended by our arrival. Theo took my hand without asking, which was unlike him, and I held on, which was unlike me. At the foot of the tower stood a low keeper's cottage with a repaired roof and a garden gone wild but recently weeded, and a door that opened before we knocked.

The woman in the doorway was seventy-odd, straight-backed, dressed in a fisherman's sweater fifty years out of fashion. Her hair was white and cropped short. Her eyes went from Theo to me and settled, unblinking, on my face, and I understood in one cold instant that she had been expecting us for twenty years—or expecting someone, some day, some knock—and that whatever she had decided to do when the knock finally came, she had decided long ago.

"You're the archivist," she said. "From Vane's town. The nun wrote that you'd come, if you were any good." She stepped back from the doorway, and her voice, when it came again, was dry as old paper and twice as brittle. "Well. You found me. Come in before the wind does, and mind the third step—it lies."

Behind her, on the cottage wall, hung a framed photograph gone silver with age: a young woman in 1920s dress, laughing, arm in arm with a man whose face I knew from the founder's portrait in the cathedral vestibule.

Edmund Vane. And beside him, unmistakably, the woman who had just let us in.

Miriam Cade looked at me looking at the photograph, and something moved behind her eyes—grief, or fury, or both wearing the same coat.

"Sit down," she said. "And before you ask me a single question, you're going to tell me exactly how Edmund's coffin came to be full of stones. Because I've waited twenty years for someone to explain that to me—and I promise you, Miss Lockridge, whoever answers it wrong walks back out that door."
