## 11. Eleven Days, Then Ten

The alarm at Brightwater & Associates had been silent for three years. Astrid had inherited it with the office—a gray box bolted above the door that no one had ever tested, because in Grayhollow nobody broke into anything. The town's crimes were older and quieter than burglary. They were filed away.

So when Astrid unlocked the front door on the morning after the eleventh day became the tenth, the first thing they noticed was not the shattered window. It was the smell of rain coming from inside.

Glass lay across the reception carpet in a fan, glittering like something celebratory. The window itself had been broken from the outside, cleanly, low, where the pane met the frame—someone who knew which pane was out of sight of the streetlamp. The filing cabinets stood open but untouched, drawers pulled wide and left empty-handed. Astrid's desk had been searched with a patience that felt almost respectful: papers squared back into their stacks, pen returned to its tray, blotter realigned to the millimeter.

Only one thing was missing.

Astrid knew it before crossing the room, before touching anything, before the part of them trained for this—the part that catalogued and preserved and never touched first—could raise its hand. The second will. Edmund Voss's impossible document, dated four days after his death, notarized by a stamp that had been destroyed years ago, naming a beneficiary who dissolved into a trust Astrid themselves had drafted. Astrid had kept it in the fireproof drawer, third cabinet, under K for the client whose name they couldn't find.

The drawer hung open. Empty except for the dust ghost where the folder had lain.

Astrid stood in the middle of their own office and felt the strange vertigo of being robbed by someone tidy. Whoever it was had taken nothing else. Not the cash box in the supply closet—Astrid checked, absurdly, and found it fat and undisturbed. Not the laptops. Not the original deeds, some of them worth more than the building. Just the will. Just the document that named *the arrangement*.

They wanted it gone, then. Not read. Gone.

Astrid called the sheriff's department at 7:42 in the morning, and Ilsa Brandt answered on the second ring, as if she'd been holding the phone.

"I know," she said.

"You know."

"Dispatch got an anonymous call about the window last night around eleven. Unit drove past. Reported nothing visible." A pause with weight in it. "I'm on my way."

She arrived in twenty minutes, out of uniform jacket, which she never did for property calls, and she walked the office the way Astrid imagined she walked crime scenes: slow, hands loose at her sides, eyes doing the work her feet refused to hurry. She crouched at the broken window and studied the frame without touching it.

"No pry marks," she said. "Glass cut or scored first. Gloved." She looked up. "You keep anything valuable here?"

"The will is missing."

Ilsa's jaw tightened. "Which will?"

"The one that shouldn't exist. The one I told you about."

"I remember what you told me." She rose, brushing nothing off her knees. "Anything else?"

"No. That's what's strange. Cash box, electronics, all here. Someone wanted one piece of paper."

"Or wanted you to think so." She said it mildly, but Astrid heard the edge under it, the old habit of a woman who had learned that in Grayhollow every fact came dressed as another fact.

"You don't believe that."

"I believe," Ilsa said, taking out her notebook, "that I have to write down something. So: forced entry, one item taken, value—" She stopped. Looked at Astrid. "What do I put for value?"

Astrid almost smiled. "That depends on whether you're asking what it's worth or what it costs."

Ilsa wrote something. Astrid didn't see what.

* * *

She worked through the morning with a thoroughness that would have been reassuring if it hadn't been so reluctant. Astrid watched her from behind the office's interior glass, the way they'd watched her across a dozen town meetings over three years, and tried to separate what they saw from what they wanted to see. That was the discipline, wasn't it? The whole profession was a discipline against wanting. Documents didn't care what you hoped. Signatures didn't flatter. But Ilsa Brandt standing in the gray morning light of Astrid's ransacked office, asking careful questions in a voice kept deliberately flat—that made a liar out of every neutral thing Astrid looked at.

"I need your whereabouts for last night," Ilsa said eventually. "Routine."

"Home. Alone. Asleep by ten, awake at two, asleep again by three. You can check my electric meter if you doubt me; the kettle went off twice."

"I don't doubt you." She said it too fast, and then, catching herself, too slowly. "And the night Edmund died? Where were you?"

The question landed wrong. Not the content—the angle. Property break-ins didn't reach backward to the night a dead man died. Astrid let the silence sit between them long enough that Ilsa's pen stopped moving.

"Why are you asking that?"

"Timeline. Standard."

"It's been nine days, Ilsa. If the timeline mattered, you'd have asked on day one."

Ilsa closed the notebook. For a moment she looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep—the specific exhaustion of a person carrying something heavy up a long flight of stairs and pretending it's a briefcase. "Humor me."

"I was home. Alone. Same answer as last night, same kettle, same meter." Astrid stepped closer. "Why do you ask like you already know where I was?"

"I don't know where you were."

"No," Astrid agreed quietly. "But you know where *you* were. And you've known since you walked in that I'd ask, because you've been rehearsing not telling me since the day we found Edmund's letters."

Something moved through Ilsa's face—there and gone, a fish under ice. "Astrid."

"Where were you the night he died?"

"That's not relevant to a broken window."

"Then it costs you nothing to say."

The office clock ticked. Outside, a truck downshifted on the county road, the ordinary sound of a town going about its ordinary morning, a town that remembered nothing and grieved in unison and paid its hush money through a lawyer's filing cabinet. Ilsa looked at the broken glass on the carpet as if it might testify for her.

"I was on patrol," she said finally. "North route. Logged at the station."

"North route doesn't pass Voss's house."

"No," Ilsa said. "It doesn't."

"And the log—"

"The log says what the log says." She put the notebook away. Her voice had gone official, which in Ilsa was the same as going cold. "I'll have a glazier contact for the window by noon. Don't touch the frame. And Astrid—" She paused at the door, hand on it, not turning around. "Whoever took that paper wanted it destroyed, not used. Think about what that means."

"It means they're one of us."

"It means," Ilsa said, "that you should be careful what you file next."

Then she was gone, and Astrid stood alone among the opened drawers, doing the arithmetic they'd been avoiding for nine days. Ilsa had lied—or at least bent something—and people only bent things when the truth had a shape they couldn't afford. She'd asked about the night Voss died unprompted. She'd known the anonymous call time before checking. She'd come out of uniform for a broken window in a town where windows didn't break.

The ally was becoming a suspect, and the suspect was becoming a wound, and Astrid had eleven days—no. Ten now. Ten days until the statute expired and whatever confession the second letter promised arrived to make all of this somebody's problem instead of everybody's secret.

Ten days. And somewhere in Grayhollow, a person with gloves and a glass cutter and immaculate manners was walking around with Edmund Voss's last lie folded in their pocket.

* * *

Astrid spent the afternoon doing what Astrid did best, which was turning fear into procedure. They photographed the office before the glazier came. They inventoried the drawers, confirming the thief's restraint, item by item, building the negative space of the theft: everything present, therefore everything considered, therefore the thief had known exactly what they wanted before they came. Which meant the thief had known where Astrid kept it. Which meant the fireproof drawer, third cabinet, filed under K—

Under K. Astrid stopped mid-inventory.

Nobody files a will under K unless they know the cipher. Unless they know that in this office, K was never just a letter. The second will had been filed where the ledger's architecture said it belonged, and whoever took it hadn't searched for it—they'd *looked it up*. They had read the filing system the way Astrid had, walked the alphabetical spine of Grayhollow's secrets, and pulled the volume they needed.

The thief wasn't hiding the arrangement. The thief was *curating* it.

Astrid sat down at the desk that had been so politely searched and thought about hands. Hands that had watched them write for three years. Hands that cut glass without prying. Hands that logged a north route that led nowhere near anywhere that mattered. The shortlist was short enough to hold in one closed fist, and every finger of it hurt to close.

At dusk, Astrid locked the office, stood a moment in the parking lot watching the light go amber over the water tower, and made two decisions. First: from tonight, the decoded ledger pages would live somewhere no alphabet could point to. Second: tomorrow they would stop waiting for the truth to arrive by mail.

The second letter had promised *the rest on the eleventh day*. The eleventh day had passed at midnight. Nothing had come.

Which meant either the promise was broken—or the eleventh day wasn't counted from the letter's arrival.

Astrid counted backward on their fingers, standing in the cold, and arrived at a date that made their breath fog and hang in the air like a question: the night Edmund Voss died.

Ten days left. And the calendar, it seemed, had been running longer than anyone knew.
