## 1. The Alphabet of Small Sins

The night dispatcher's chair at Grayhaven Taxi & Livery had a spring that poked through the cushion in exactly the wrong place, and Wendell Delacroix had long ago decided this was by design. A comfortable chair invited sleep. Sleep invited mistakes. Mistakes, in his line of work, were unforgivable—not because anyone would notice, but because he would.

It was 11:47 on a Tuesday in October, and the radio hissed its low, patient static, the sound Wendell thought of as the ocean's smaller cousin. Outside the dispatch window, Harbor Street lay wet and empty under the sodium lights, the fog rolling in off the water the way it always did after ten, softening the edges of everything until Grayhaven looked less like a town and more like a rumor of one.

Wendell opened the ledger.

Not the official ledger—the official ledger sat on the shelf above the radio, a fat green book with TAXI LOG stenciled on the spine, into which he entered fares with dutiful, forgettable precision. *Pickup: 14 Gull Street. Dropoff: Ferry Terminal. Fare: $8.50.* Three years of that. Enough entries to wallpaper a mausoleum.

The other ledger lived in the drawer beneath the cash box, wrapped in a rubber band, and it had no stencil on its spine because it had no spine at all—just a stack of index cards in a shoebox that had once held a pair of boots he'd never worn. On each card, in his small, upright hand, was a confession.

He hadn't set out to collect them. That was important to him, though there was no one to explain it to. It had simply happened, the way silt happens to a harbor. People talked to taxi drivers—or rather, they talked *around* taxi drivers, forgetting that the man behind the partition had ears and, more dangerously, a memory. And Wendell, who had arrived in Grayhaven three years ago with two suitcases and no history anyone could verify, had discovered that listening was a kind of company.

*Card 114. B., retired schoolteacher, Tuesdays:* confessed she'd failed her husband's brother in the eleventh grade out of spite and had been sending anonymous money orders to his widow ever since. Filed under **R** for Reparations.

*Card 87. The florist on Cannery Row:* kept her dead mother's ashes in the walk-in cooler among the carnations because "she liked the cold." Filed under **M** for Mothers, Inappropriate Storage Of.

*Card 203. A councilman whose name Wendell spelled only as C-4, for discretion:* had been seen leaving the Blue Anchor with a woman not his wife, which was nobody's business except that he'd voted to cut the ferry schedule while drunk on sentiment and gin, and Wendell felt the two facts belonged together somehow. Filed under **P** for Public Service, Compromised.

The system was alphabetical, but not by name. By sin. Wendell had found early on that sins sorted themselves more cleanly than people did. Envy, Theft, Cowardice, Kindness Misplaced—the last one a surprisingly thick section. He'd started it the night old Tomas Vane, three sheets to the wind, had confessed that he'd been the one who reported the poachers up the coast, not out of civic duty but because they'd laughed at his boat, and the resulting fines had bankrupted a family with four children. Tomas had wept in the back seat. Wendell had driven him home the long way, past the lit windows, so no one would see.

That was the service he provided, though no one had asked for it and no one paid for it. He held things. He filed them. He never spent them.

There was a difference, Wendell believed, between keeping a secret and owning one. The owners used theirs—traded them, dropped them like bait, wore them as armor. The keepers just made sure nothing got lost. Someone had to be the tide chart of a town's soul, marking where the hidden things sat and how deep the water over them ran.

He was aware, in the honest corner of his mind he visited only after 2 a.m., that this was a fancy way of describing loneliness. But the corner could think what it liked. Wendell had built himself a life with load-bearing walls of routine, and routine did not require witnesses.

At 12:15, the radio crackled. "Dispatch, this is unit six."

"Go ahead, six." Ray Okafor, the only driver who worked past midnight voluntarily. Ray was seventy-one and claimed insomnia had made him and the night shift "business partners."

"Got a fare at the Anchor. Mrs. Pell. She wants the scenic route again."

"The scenic route" meant past the Marsh house on Bluff Road, where Elias Marsh's truck still sat in the driveway under a tarp, nine days after his funeral. Half the town had taken the scenic route at least once since. Grief in Grayhaven was a spectator event; people didn't know what else to do with it.

"Approved," Wendell said. "Tell her the fog's thick tonight. She'll want the slow way anyway."

"Copy. Hey, Wendell."

"Yes?"

"You eat anything today?"

Wendell glanced at the half-eaten roll on the desk, purchased at noon, now fossilized. "A balanced meal," he said.

Ray snorted and signed off. This was another thing about Ray: he committed small acts of surveillance on Wendell's behalf, asking after meals and sleep as if Wendell were a lighthouse that might go dark without maintenance. Wendell filed Ray's concern under **K** for Kindness, Unsolicited, and pretended it didn't matter more than the rest of the box combined.

* * *

At 1:30, the phone rang—the landline, not the radio, which meant a private fare, which meant, usually, a confession in transit.

"Grayhaven Taxi."

Silence first. Then breathing, careful and controlled, the breathing of someone rehearsing.

"I need a ride," said a voice Wendell didn't recognize. Young, maybe. Or young-sounding, which wasn't the same thing. "Tomorrow. No—no, I can't say when. Can you—" A pause, long enough for the fog to press against the window. "Can you do a standing booking? Like, on call? Cash."

"We can do that. Name and pickup location?"

Another silence. This one had a different texture—Wendell had learned silences the way musicians learn rests, and this one was the kind that came before either a lie or a leap.

"Just—" the voice said, and then stopped. Started again, quieter. "Do you know what happens to things the water takes? Do they stay taken?"

Wendell's pen hovered over the blank card. "In my experience," he said carefully, "the water is a poor archivist. Things come back. Usually at inconvenient times."

A sound that might have been a laugh, or its ghost. "Yeah. Okay. Yeah." The voice steadied itself around something. "Forget the booking. Forget I called."

Click.

Wendell sat with the receiver in his hand a moment longer than necessary. Then he pulled a fresh card from the box, wrote the time, the number from the caller ID—a payphone, he noted, the one outside the laundromat, which had been scheduled for removal in June and somehow survived—and after some deliberation filed it under **U** for Unfinished.

He told himself it was nothing. Drunk dialers, pranksters, insomniacs like Ray looking for a human voice. Grayhaven produced all three in reliable quantities.

But the card went in the box face-up, not face-down. He noticed himself doing it and didn't correct it.

* * *

By 3 a.m., the fog had swallowed the streetlights entirely, and the dispatch office existed in a cone of yellow lamplight like the last lit room in the world. Wendell updated the official log, reconciled the cash drawer, and allowed himself the ritual he never skipped: reading back through three random cards, chosen blind, to make sure the archive was in order. It was a strange sort of prayer. *Forgive us our trespasses,* the cards seemed to murmur, *we have filed them alphabetically.*

Card 41. Card 156. Card—

The radio woke. Static, then a voice, thin and warped by distance or weather or both.

"Dispatch."

Wendell reached for the mic. "Go ahead."

"Dispatch, I need—" The signal shredded, reassembled. "...channel... can anyone hear..."

"This is dispatch. Identify your location."

More static. Then, impossibly clear, as if the speaker had leaned close to the transmitter: "You file them, don't you? All the little sins. I've seen the box."

Wendell's hand went still on the desk. The lamp hummed. Somewhere down the block, a dog began to bark and thought better of it.

"Who is this?" he said. "Identify your unit."

Nothing. Just the static, rising and falling like breath, like surf, like something patient waiting offshore for the tide to turn.

Then, at the very edge of audibility, almost lost in the hiss:

"Check the logs. Three years back. Check what I logged."

And the channel went dead—not silent, dead, the flat carrier tone of an open mic with no one behind it, stretching on and on until Wendell's own heartbeat sounded louder than the radio.

He sat in the poking chair and did not move for a long time. The fog pressed at the glass. The shoebox sat in its drawer, three years of other people's secrets, suddenly feeling less like an archive and more like evidence.

Slowly, deliberately, Wendell reached for the green logbook on the shelf, opened it to the first page, and began to read.
