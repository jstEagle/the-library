## 16. The Night Before

The cannery hall had been closed for eleven years, but the key was in Ines's pocket by seven o'clock, because Marguerite had lifted it from the municipal lockbox with the untroubled competence of a woman who had, apparently, been stealing things her whole life and simply hiding it well.

"You're enjoying this," Ines said, watching her work the padlock on the loading dock door.

"I'm committing a crime," she corrected. "Enjoying it would come later, if it works."

The lock gave. The door groaned open onto darkness that smelled of salt rust and old varnish, and beyond it, the vast belly of the Cannery Hall — where the town had once packed sardines by the thousand and now packed its secrets by the dozen, once a year, at long tables under the high windows.

Ines stepped inside and felt the room take his measure. Forty years of dinners had happened here. Forty years of the same twelve families, the same toasts, the same phrase — *the glass stays full* — spoken over wine that, at least twice that he knew of, had carried death in it.

"Big room," Marguerite said, clicking on her flashlight. "Twelve tables. One head table. That's where Delphine sits."

"You've been here before?"

"Every municipal function since I took the job. The winter fair. The harvest auction. Delphine Roux's ribbon-cutting for a roundabout nobody wanted." She swept the light across the floor. "But I've never looked at it like this. Like a stage I'm planning to crash."

* * *

They worked for an hour, and the work felt, strangely, like the best evening Ines had spent in years.

Marguerite paced out the sightlines. Ines followed with a pencil and the back of a delivery invoice from his own shop, sketching. She walked the route a waiter would take from the kitchen — a long galley along the east wall, half-hidden behind a partition that hadn't moved since the sardine days. He marked the exits: the loading dock, the front doors, the fire door behind the kitchen that stuck and had to be shouldered, which she knew because she'd once chased a stolen bicycle through it.

"Service entrance is here," she said, tapping the galley. "Staff come in at six, dinner's served at eight. If you're going to be in that room, you're a waiter. White shirt, black trousers, and you carry things without dropping them."

"I ran a bookshop for thirty years. I've carried stacks of Tolstoy up a ladder. I can carry a soup tureen."

"Tolstoy doesn't splash."

"Marguerite." He set down the pencil. "Say the rest of it. You've been circling it since the dock."

She was quiet a moment. The flashlight beam settled on the head table, empty, its twelve chairs pushed in neat as a jury.

"The rest of it is that I'm supposed to be at that dinner," she said. "Officially. Delphine invited me last week — very gracious, very public. The chief of police, honoring the town's oldest tradition. It was a courtesy and a cage, both. If I sit at that table, I'm a witness to whatever happens and a symbol that the town's law approves of it. If I stay away without cause, I tip my hand."

"And instead you're here, breaking into the venue the night before, helping a retired bookseller plan—" he gestured at the invoice, the penciled arrows, the whole absurd architecture of it, "—an ambush."

"An intervention," she said. "Ambushes are for enemies. These are my neighbors. Some of them brought casseroles when I moved here. Madame Ferrand's sister taught me to make clafoutis." She laughed, but it came out thin. "I keep thinking about that. About how you can know a person for years and only ever have met the version of them the pact allowed you to meet."

Ines looked at her — the flashlight halo, the tired eyes, the badge she'd left at home tonight on purpose, because bringing it would have made this official and official was the one thing it could not be.

"What you're doing," he said carefully, "could cost you the badge. Not a reprimand. The badge. If Delphine's lawyer frames tonight right, you didn't investigate, you conspired. You broke into a municipal building with a civilian and planned to confront suspects without warrants, without the prosecutor, without—"

"Without asking permission from the woman who ordered Felix Arnaud drowned," Marguerite said. "Yes. I've done the arithmetic, Ines. I do it every night when I can't sleep, which lately is most nights." She turned to face him fully. "Here's what I keep coming back to. The day I took the oath, I swore to protect this town. Not the town's comfort. Not the town's story about itself. The town. Felix Arnaud was the town. A scared old man who did one brave thing — he reported an empty coffin — and the town killed him for it in a retention pond. If protecting the town costs me the badge, then the badge was costing me the town, and I know which one I swore to."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere in the rafters, water dripped with the patience of old buildings.

Ines found he had no cleverness left. Thirty years of detective work had taught him every rhetorical trick, and none of them were equal to her standing there in the dark, having already decided.

"Then we do it right," he said. "As right as it can be, done wrong."

* * *

They spread the decoded ledger pages out on one of the long tables — the actual pages, photocopied twice, one set already in a sealed envelope in Marguerite's glovebox addressed to the county prosecutor, to be mailed at six a.m. regardless of what happened at dinner. Insurance. She had insisted, and Ines had loved her a little more for insisting, because it meant she was planning for the possibility that they'd both be in cells by morning.

"Walk it through again," she said. "The moment. When do you speak?"

"During the toast." Ines laid his hand flat on the table diagram. "That's the whole design, isn't it? Forty years of the same ritual. The decanter comes out, Odile pours, everyone stands, and they say the phrase. It's the one moment all twelve of them are doing the same thing at the same time, thinking the same thought. Their armor is the ritual. So you don't attack the armor. You *become* it." He tapped the first page. "I read the name. Just the first one. 'Henri Roux, 1963, the quarry.' And then I keep pouring wine and I let the room do the rest."

"They'll deny it."

"Of course. Denial is the first hour. But the pages will be in front of them — I'll set a copy at every place setting under the bread plate, and when the toast comes, everyone finds out everyone else has been holding the same paper all evening. Nobody can quietly pocket theirs. Nobody can pretend they don't know what the others know. That's the whole trick, Marguerite. A pact is just a secret with twelve locks on it. You don't pick the locks. You make all twelve keys turn at once."

She stared at him. "You've been thinking about this like a detective for thirty years and it shows."

"I've been thinking about it like a bookseller, actually. It's a limited edition. You make the readers feel they'd be fools to miss it."

The laugh broke out of her before she could stop it, and then she was laughing in earnest — the exhausted, giddy laugh of two people at the bottom of a long fall who have just realized they're holding hands. Ines laughed too, and for a moment the cannery hall was just a big cold room with two foolish people in it, and the fear was still there, but it had to share the space.

* * *

They walked the kitchen last. It was a cavern of dead steel, and Marguerite's flashlight made ghosts of the counters.

"Staff park on the Rue Basse side," she said, all business again. "You'll need a reason to be there. Delphine's office hired the catering through a Saint-Malo agency — I checked. They send whoever they send; nobody looks closely. I can get your name on the roster by noon."

"You can?"

"I can make a phone call to a woman who owes me for not impounding her oyster boat in March. I can also," she added, "lose my job for falsifying a vendor roster, which is a sentence I never expected to say aloud."

"Marguerite Osei," Ines said, "corruptor of oyster law."

"Keep it up and I'll corrupt you right back onto the menu."

He smiled, and then stopped smiling, because there was something he had to say and the kitchen was as good a place as any — private, ugly, honest.

"If it goes wrong," he said, "if Delphine gets the pages destroyed, or Odile stands up and lies, or the room closes ranks like it closed ranks for forty years — you have to walk out the front door and be the chief of police. You can't be in it with me. The moment it breaks bad, your only move is to be the law that arrived afterward. Promise me."

"I don't—"

"Promise me. Because I've done the arithmetic too, and I can survive losing. What I can't survive is you losing *for* me. I'm a retired bookseller with a bad knee and a dead man's code. You're the only honest institution this town has left. One of us has to still be standing tomorrow."

The flashlight trembled — just slightly, just enough. She set it down on the steel counter so he couldn't see her hands.

"You know what's obscene?" she said quietly. "I spent four years being careful with you. Careful at the market, careful at the funeral for your wife, careful every time you came into my office — because a chief can't be seen to have favorites, and because I didn't know yet what I felt, and because the town watches. And now, the one week being seen with you could ruin us both, I find I don't care who watches at all." She picked the flashlight back up. "So no. I won't promise to walk away. I'll promise to walk out the *front door*, loudly, at the first sign of trouble, and be so conspicuously the chief of police that no lawyer in the department can touch me. That's my version. Take it or take it."

Ines took it.

They stood there in the dead kitchen of a dead cannery, and he took her hand, and neither of them said anything for a while, because some things are said better by the grip than the mouth, and they had a long night's work left and a longer day tomorrow.

* * *

They were nearly done — pages copied, routes marked, the roster call scheduled for nine a.m. — when Ines stopped at the head table one last time.

Twelve chairs. He counted them the way he'd counted everything for weeks, out of habit, out of dread.

"Marguerite. The pact had twelve signatures."

"Eleven," she said. "The Rouxs never signed. Delphine's father refused, and he was the first accident."

"Right. So why are there twelve chairs at the head table, forty years running, for a dinner with eleven families?" He looked at her. "Someone's been setting a place for Auguste Vidal all this time. Or—" and the cold moved through him as he said it, the old detective's cold, the one that arrived just ahead of the truth, "—for whoever holds the pact now. Whoever pours."

Marguerite's flashlight found the twelfth chair. At its setting, though the hall had been closed for a year, someone had recently laid a napkin. Folded. Crisp. Fresh.

And beside the plate, where a card would announce a guest of honor, someone had placed a single wine glass — turned upside down.

An old superstition, Ines knew. A glass inverted at a place setting meant one thing across every kitchen and every table in the south of France:

*This seat is kept for the dead.*

Or, his mind corrected, as the flashlight beam caught the faint ring of moisture still drying on the polished wood beneath it —

*for someone who intends to be here tomorrow, and does not intend to leave.*
