## 17. Statue of Limitations

The note had been sitting in Petra's breast pocket for three days, folded into eighths like a secret that couldn't decide how loud to be.

*If you're reading this, they won. What I built to outlast them is where I put everything else — in plain sight, paid for by the town, admired by fools.*

Petra had read it perhaps two hundred times. On the bus. In the shower, holding it at arm's length through the steam like a man reading scripture underwater. At three in the morning, when the ceiling of their apartment offered no answers and the radiator clanked out its one-note opinion on the matter.

*What I built to outlast them.*

The founding families would say Wren built the town itself. The historical society would say he built the mill, the bank, the school with his name chiseled over the door in letters you could read from the highway. But those things were built *by* committees and *for* posterity, and Aldous Wren, as far as Petra could tell from eleven years of his paper trail, trusted committees about as far as he could throw the bank.

He trusted stone, though. Stone and bronze and the vanity of towns that like to look at themselves.

Petra was drinking terrible coffee outside the county records annex when it landed — not as a thought but as a physical event, somewhere behind the sternum.

"Outlast them," they said aloud, to no one. A pigeon gave them a look of professional skepticism and moved along.

Wren had built exactly one thing designed to outlast everyone: the memorial. The statue. Forty feet of granite and patinated bronze on Monument Hill, erected — according to the dedication plaque — "by a grateful town," which was a phrase that meant the founding families had held a fundraiser and argued about the catering.

A statue of Aldous Wren, standing forever over the town he'd secretly been poisoned in, containing — if Petra's hunch was worth the coffee it was riding on — the only honest document he'd ever signed.

Petra checked their watch. Then they did something they hadn't done since law school: they ran.

* * *

Monument Hill was less a hill than a suggestion of one, a grassy knoll at the end of Founders Avenue where the statue stood with one hand extended in perpetual benediction over the parking meters. Up close, the bronze Wren was greener than any living thing in the county, his face softened by decades of weather into something kinder than photographs suggested. Birds had opinions about his shoulders and expressed them freely.

And around his ankles — Petra's stomach dropped before their brain caught up — was scaffolding.

Chain-link fencing, orange mesh, a sign stenciled in municipal font:

**NOTICE: DEMOLITION AND SITE REDEVELOPMENT SCHEDULED. STRUCTURE TO BE REMOVED [DATE]. QUESTIONS: OFFICE OF PUBLIC WORKS.**

The date was tomorrow.

Petra stood there breathing hard, coffee sloshing, doing the math. Tomorrow morning. Crews would arrive at seven, maybe six-thirty. They'd wrap the bronze in tarps, cut the figure off its pedestal, and haul forty feet of Aldous Wren to some municipal lot where he'd lie on his side among traffic cones until someone decided he was scrap.

Whatever was inside him would go with him. Or be found by a workman with a pry bar and no reason to hand it to a lawyer.

"No," Petra said to the statue. "No, you stubborn old man, you did *not* build your last will and testament into a piece of public infrastructure scheduled for demolition."

The statue said nothing, which Petra chose to interpret as agreement.

They walked the fence line slowly, cataloguing. The scaffolding covered the lower third — legs, coat, the base. Above that, the figure was bare bronze against the sky. A cavity large enough to hold documents wouldn't be in the pedestal; pedestals get opened all the time, for plaques, for time capsules, for the occasional teenager's dare. It would be in the body. Somewhere a sculptor could hide a void without weakening the armature. The torso. Possibly the head.

Which meant climbing.

Petra Nakamura was fifty-three years old, had a bad knee that forecast weather better than the National Weather Service, and had not climbed anything more ambitious than a stepladder since the Clinton administration. They looked up at the scaffolding, then at the statue, then at their phone.

One call. There was exactly one person left who might answer.

Brandt picked up on the fourth ring. "This number doesn't exist anymore, legally speaking."

"I need a favor."

"You need a lawyer. You *are* a lawyer, technically, pending review." A pause. Petra could hear her walking, the squeak of her shoes on station tile. "What kind of favor?"

"The kind that involves trespassing."

Another pause, longer. "Nakamura, my captain already asks me why I talk to you. If I get caught helping a suspended attorney climb a monument the night before demolition—"

"Then don't get caught. I'm asking you to be a witness, not an accomplice. If I find what I think is in there, it needs to be found by someone with a badge, or at least adjacent to one, or the founding families' lawyers will have it suppressed before lunch."

Silence on the line. Petra let it sit. Brandt had spent three weeks risking her career in increments so small they barely registered on any personnel file — a photocopy here, a records check there. This was different. This was a ladder and a flashlight and a felony-adjacent evening.

"If this is another empty coffin," Brandt said finally, "I'm going to write you up myself."

"It's not an empty coffin."

"How do you know?"

"Because Wren didn't stop at telling us they won." Petra looked up at the green face, the outstretched hand, the patient stone patience of a murdered man who had planned further ahead than anyone gave him credit for. "He told us where to find the receipt."

* * *

They came at nine o'clock, after the park lights clicked off on their municipal timer. Brandt brought a flashlight the size of a loaf of bread and a folding ladder she'd "borrowed" from the evidence garage, which Petra elected not to ask about.

"This is insane," Brandt said, cheerfully, setting the ladder against the scaffolding. She'd been cheerful ever since the coffin came up empty, Petra had noticed — the particular brightness of a person who has stopped pretending the world is orderly and finds it a relief.

"Completely," Petra agreed, and started up.

The scaffolding was sound, at least; the demolition crew had built it properly, steel poles and cross-braces, platforms at ten feet and twenty. Petra climbed with both hands and a running internal monologue about estate law being a sedentary profession for good reasons. Below, Brandt kept the flashlight beam low and swept the perimeter of the fence every few seconds, casual as a woman checking her mirrors.

At the twenty-foot platform, Petra came level with the statue's waist and stopped.

Up close, the bronze was pitted and beautiful, streaked with a century of rain. And there — at the seam where the coat met the torso, hidden in the drapery folds the sculptor had cut deep enough to shadow even in daylight — was a line that didn't belong. Too straight. Too deliberate. A rectangle of slightly different patina, maybe eight inches wide, outlined by a bead of solder old enough to have gone green itself.

"Brandt."

"Yeah."

"I think I see it."

She was up the ladder faster than a woman her age had any legal right to be. She crouched beside Petra, played the flashlight across the seam, and went very quiet.

"That's a door," she said.

"That's a door."

"In a statue."

"Aldous Wren commissioned this monument himself. Paid for it personally — I checked the probate filings months ago and thought it was vanity." Petra ran a thumb along the seam. The metal was cold and gritty. "It wasn't vanity. It was a dead drop with a hundred-year lease."

"You brought tools?"

Petra patted their jacket. A flat pry bar, purchased that afternoon from the hardware store by a person who had never bought a pry bar before and had asked the clerk three questions too many. "I brought tools."

The solder gave reluctantly, in stages, each crack of it loud as a gunshot in the dark. Brandt flinched at the first one and put herself between the platform and the fence line, watching the road below. Petra worked the pry bar with the patience of their profession — gently, gently, you don't break the thing you're trying to open — until a plate of bronze the size of a hardcover book swung inward on a hinge someone had oiled within living memory.

Inside: a lead-lined cavity, dry as a sermon. And wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, a bundle.

Petra's hands were shaking. They noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain starting.

"Take it out slow," Brandt said quietly. "Slow."

The bundle came free easier than it should have — as if it had been waiting to be lifted. Petra set it on the platform, untied the cord, and peeled back the oilcloth.

Three items.

A leather-bound document, sealed with red wax stamped with the Wren crest. A smaller envelope, addressed in a strong slanted hand: *To whoever is reading this — you are either very clever or very late. Possibly both.* And beneath them, a folded newspaper clipping, yellowed to the color of weak tea.

Petra broke the seal on the document first. Their hands knew this ritual — the weight of a will, the smell of old vellum — but nothing had prepared them for the first line.

*I, Aldous Wren, being of sound mind and deteriorating body, do hereby revoke all prior instruments.*

"Brandt," Petra whispered. "It's dated four days before he died. Before the fake death. Before any of it."

She leaned in, flashlight steady now, and they read together in the small circle of light while the town slept below them, ignorant, its streetlights glowing like embers.

The language was pure Wren — precise, furious, and darkly funny in the way of a man who had audited his own murder in progress. He left everything — the fortune, the land, the controlling shares, the trusts the founding families had been bleeding for a decade — to Marisol Wren, granddaughter, estranged, "who alone of my blood ever asked me a question I didn't want to answer."

Then the codicil. Petra read it twice because the first time felt like vertigo.

*To the executor: my death will be reported as natural, and it will be a lie. I am being poisoned, slowly, by men who eat dinner with me and smile. I have documented what I can. I charge my executor with full disclosure — publish this instrument, publish the enclosed medical findings, and let the town know what its founders are. I would rather be remembered as a murdered man than honored by murderers.*

Below his signature, witnessed by two names Petra didn't recognize — and then, in a postscript added in shakier ink:

*P.S. — The doctor has the second copy. If mine does not survive, his will.*

The doctor. The one who'd fled town a week after signing the death certificate. The one whose car fire Brandt had traced to Petra's own cold case file.

"He hid it in the one place they'd never look," Brandt said softly. "Inside their own shrine."

"He counted on their reverence." Petra looked up at the bronze face above them, weathered gentle, hand still extended in blessing. "They loved him too much to take him apart."

Below, on the platform, the newspaper clipping waited. Petra unfolded it carefully, afraid it would crumble. It was a society page item, decades old, photograph and all: the groundbreaking for the statue. And there in the front row, shaking hands with the sculptor, young and smooth-faced and already wearing the archivist's cardigan of the soul —

Corwin Hale.

His father beside him. His father, whose name appeared in the margin of Wren's codicil, in the list of men who ate dinner with him and smiled.

"Hale wasn't just protecting the conspirators," Brandt said slowly. "He was protecting his father. He's been guarding this thing his whole life — making sure nobody ever opened it, making sure nobody ever *had* to."

"And now they're demolishing it." Petra repacked the bundle with hands that had mostly stopped shaking, which was worse somehow, because steadiness meant this was real. "Tomorrow at seven, a crew with torches and a crane takes him apart. If we hadn't come tonight—"

"But we did."

"Yes." Petra tucked the bundle inside their coat, against their chest, where it sat warm and impossible. "We did."

They climbed down in silence. At the bottom, Brandt folded the ladder and looked at Petra across the dark grass.

"You know what happens now. The moment this surfaces, it's not a fraud case anymore. It's murder, conspiracy, forty years of it, and every family in this town with a founder's name is going to come at you with everything they have. Your suspension becomes disbarment. Maybe worse."

"I know."

"And Hale—" Brandt hesitated. "If he figures out you got here first, tonight, before the demolition—"

"He'll know the statue was the last card, and he'll know someone played it." Petra touched the coat pocket, the shape of the bundle beneath the wool. "Which means between now and Founders' Day, I'm not just a target. I'm the only copy."

They walked down Monument Hill together, two shadows among the parking meters, and behind them the statue kept its vigil — lighter now, Petra thought, by the weight of one honest document, its long watch finally, nearly, over.

Tomorrow, the cranes would come. Tonight, somewhere across the sleeping town, Corwin Hale might already be counting the hours until the bronze came down — and wondering, perhaps, why the night felt too quiet.

Petra reached the sidewalk, turned once, and looked back up the hill.

"Hold on," they murmured to the green-faced man on his pedestal. "One more day. I'll make it count."

The statue said nothing. But for the first time in eleven years, Aldous Wren's silence sounded less like death, and more like a man waiting to speak.
