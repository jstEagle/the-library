## 10. The Passenger Who Paid Cash

The man who followed me home didn't come back the next night, and somehow that was worse.

I'd left the porch light on until two in the morning, sitting in the dark of my kitchen with the blinds slatted just enough to see the street. Nothing moved except a raccoon with the confident gait of a taxpayer. By dawn I'd talked myself into half a dozen explanations — a lost driver scoping routes, someone casing the neighborhood for catalytic converters, a coincidence wearing a sedan's body. But explanations are like fares at three a.m.: you take the first one that shows up because the alternative is standing alone on the curb forever.

So I did what I always do when a pattern won't resolve itself. I went to work and started reading.

* * *

Kestrel Taxi's dispatch floor at six a.m. is a cathedral of small noises: the tick of the heater, the hum of the radio base station, Nell's ancient coffee maker performing its arthritic rites in the back room. The morning shift drivers checked out their cars, grumbled about the cold, and rolled out into a town still gray with sleep. I stayed at the console with a fresh pot of coffee going stale beside me and pulled up the trip archive.

Here's something most people never think about: every cab company keeps records. Not because we're diligent — because the tax man and the insurance companies make us. Every fare, every pickup and drop-off, every cash ride logged by hand or meter, stretching back years in filing cabinets and older software than some of my drivers. People forget they exist in that database. They tell cabs things they'd never tell priests, and then they assume the cab evaporated afterward, taking their secrets with it into the wet dark.

Cabs don't evaporate. Cabs keep ledgers.

I wasn't looking for anything specific. That's the honest answer, though it sounds evasive. After the pharmacy record and the pension draft — Aldous Wren's ghost drawing breath and money somewhere inside a twenty-mile circle — I'd developed an itch I couldn't scratch directly. You can't search a database for *dead men*. But you can go sideways. You can look for the moment the itch started, which was the night someone tailed me home from the county records office, and before that, the night I found the pension draft, and before *that* —

Before that there was a fare.

It surfaced the way these things do: not as a memory but as a physical sensation first. My hands remembered the wheel. My ears remembered a voice, low and sanded down, saying four words through the partition.

*"Past the mill, please."*

I sat up straight enough that my chair complained.

* * *

It was October. I remember because the maples along Corbett Street were doing their annual arson impression, all that orange and red, and the woman at 44 Corbett had put a scarecrow on her porch dressed in one of her dead husband's flannel shirts, which struck me as either grief or humor and probably both. Slow Tuesday shift. The kind of night where the radio mostly carries static and the occasional drunk calling from the Kestrel Tavern asking for Danny, and Danny hasn't driven for us since March.

The call came in at 9:47 p.m. Pickup outside St. Ambrose, the Catholic church on Delancey — not the front, but the side door by the rectory, the one people use when they don't want to be seen coming out of a church at night. Confession hours. I noted it without noting it, the way you do. A town reads itself to you if you let it, and St. Ambrose's side door at quarter to ten on a Tuesday says somebody needed absolution more than they needed a ride.

The old man was already waiting when Marcus pulled up — no, wait. It wasn't Marcus. It was me. I'd taken the fare myself because the board was empty and I like driving sometimes, like putting my hands on the actual town instead of just its voice. I remember this now with terrible clarity, the way you remember a word that's been on the tip of your tongue for months.

He got in the back. Old, older than old — the kind of old that looks carved rather than aged, weathered down to structure. Tall once, folded smaller now. He wore a canvas jacket gone soft as cloth can get, and a watch cap pulled low, and he paid cash from an envelope, counted out exact, which nobody does anymore. Exact change is a dying language and he spoke it fluently.

"Past the mill," he said. "Hollis Mill. Just past it. There's a pull-off."

I said sure, and I pulled away from the church, and he said nothing else.

That was the thing. That was the thing I've been turning over ever since it came back to me whole. People talk in cabs. It's a law of nature, like water finding low ground. They talk because the driver isn't a person to them, exactly — he's a fixture, a confessional on wheels, a pair of ears attached to a meter. In eleven years I have heard marriages end, affairs begin, crimes confessed, prayers offered, and one memorable monologue about the sexual politics of the garden club delivered with such venom I nearly ran a stop sign. Silence in the back seat is rare. Silence for twenty minutes is practically geological.

But this man was silent the way stone is silent. Not uncomfortable. Not brooding, even. Just *present*, the way a landmark is present, and I caught him twice in the mirror looking out the window at the town sliding past with an expression I couldn't read then and can read now, God help me.

It was homesickness. No — it was worse than homesickness. It was the look of a man taking inventory.

And his hands. I remember his hands because when he counted the cash, the envelope slipped and I reached back to steady it, reflex, courtesy, whatever, and our hands almost touched under the dome light. His were ruined. Not old-ruined, not arthritis-ruined. *Burned.* The skin across both palms and the backs of his fingers was smooth and tight and wrong, grafted-looking, shiny as candle wax, pulling at the knuckles so his fingers couldn't fully close. He saw me see them. He drew them back into his sleeves like a turtle withdrawing, and the silence got heavier, and neither of us said a word about it for the rest of the ride.

Past the mill. Past Hollis Mill, which has stood empty on the river bend since the fire — boarded, tagged, slowly being eaten by ivy and teenagers. I took River Road because it's the only way past, and as we came around the bend the mill rose up black against the blacker sky, its broken windows catching the headlights for half a second, flashing like eyes.

"There," he said. "The pull-off. Thank you."

I stopped. He got out. He stood there in the cold with the mill looming over him and didn't move toward it or away from it, just stood, and I watched him in the mirror longer than I should have because something about him snagged — the way a coat snags on a nail, barely, just enough to stop you. Then he turned and walked toward the treeline behind the mill, and I drove away, and the fare went into the ledger like ten thousand other fares, and I forgot him.

Except I didn't. That's what I understand now. I filed him somewhere below forgetting, in the basement of the mind where things wait, and he waited nine weeks, and he came back up the stairs the moment I learned that Aldous Wren might be alive.

Because here's what the basement knows that the front office doesn't: burned hands. A fire killed four workers at Hollis Mill thirty years ago. An old man with burn-scarred hands asking to be taken *past* that mill — not to it, past it, to a pull-off where he could stand in the dark and look at the place that made him — that's not a fare. That's a wound with legs.

* * *

I pulled the October log. Paper, because our digital system only goes back four years; everything older lives in the filing cabinets in the back room, in triplicate, in Nell's fanatically neat handwriting. I found the entry at 9:47 p.m., October 14th. Pickup: St. Ambrose, Delancey entrance. Drop-off: River Road pull-off, mile marker 3. Fare: $18.50, cash. Passenger name field: blank, as it usually is. Driver: B. Marchetti.

Eighteen fifty. I stared at that number like it might confess. Cash, no name, no phone number called in from — wait. How had the call come in? I closed my eyes and reconstructed the shift. The board had been quiet. The request hadn't come through the main line; I remembered that now, remembered the slight oddness of it, the way you notice a knock at the wrong door. It had come in on the after-hours line, the direct number that rings at the console instead of going through the answering service. Somebody who knew the direct line. Somebody who'd used it before, maybe, or gotten it from someone who had.

Or — and this thought arrived cold and settled in like winter — somebody who wanted the call to come to *me*. Who knew I'd be on shift. Who knew I'd take the fare myself on a dead Tuesday night.

You can't know that. Nobody can know that. And yet.

I sat with it while the coffee went cold and the morning drivers drifted back in for fuel and complaints. Then I did the thing I do instead of panicking, which is make lists. On the back of a shift sheet I wrote:

*St. Ambrose side door, 9:40 p.m. Tuesday. Confession hours.*
*Knew the direct line.*
*Paid exact, from an envelope.*
*Burned hands, both, old burns.*
*Wanted to see the mill. Didn't want to be seen seeing it.*
*Said nothing else.*

Five facts and a shape. The shape was starting to look familiar, and I didn't like the resemblance. Because there was another anonymous voice in this story already — the one that had phoned in the exhumation tip, routed through *my* dispatch line, phrased like a cab call, aimed at *me* like a letter addressed in handwriting I almost recognized. Someone in this town knew how I worked. Knew that Bram Marchetti solves things from behind a radio, and decided I was the instrument to use.

Had the same hand dialed both calls? Had the man with the burned hands ridden in my cab in October and then, weeks later, set the whole exhumation machine moving with a phone call? Or was I flattering myself, connecting dots because connecting dots is the only prayer I know?

There was one way to start finding out. The mill.

* * *

I told Nell I was running a personal errand at lunch and she gave me the look she gives me, the one that says she counts my lies the way I count her refills and we've agreed not to audit each other. I took my own car, which felt strange, like a priest driving a getaway route.

River Road in daylight is a different country. The mill sits on the bend above the river with its back to the water, five stories of brick going soft at the corners, windows boarded or blown, the loading dock collapsed into a wooden shrug. The county condemned it years ago and then, in the way of counties, forgot to do anything else. A chain-link fence surrounds it, mostly decorative, breached in four places I could see and probably forty I couldn't.

The pull-off where I'd dropped the old man is a gravel shoulder just past the property line, wide enough for two cars, littered with cigarette ends and one sad mattress. I parked and got out and stood where he'd stood.

From there, the mill fills your vision. You can see the east wing — the wing where the fire started, according to everything I'd read in the last week. You can see the loading dock, the old foreman's entrance, the row of second-floor windows that used to light the sewing floor. Four workers died in there on a November night in 1994, behind doors that had been chained against theft, while the owner watched from the office window. Ida Voss had signed those death certificates. She'd told me that much, in her kitchen, with her hands wrapped around a teacup like it was the last warm thing in the world.

And now an old man with burned hands comes back after thirty years and stands on this gravel and looks at those windows.

I walked the fence line. At the third breach — a peeled-back section near the northeast corner, screened by sumac — the chain-link showed fresh damage. Bright metal where the wire had been cut, no rust yet, no ivy grown across the gap. Weeks old, maybe less. Beyond it, in the weeds, a path had been beaten flat. Not deer; deer don't walk in straight lines and don't step over boards. Human traffic, repeated, recent.

I shouldn't have gone in. Let the record show I knew that. The building was condemned, the floor was a rumor, and whoever cut that fence might still be around. I went in anyway, because curiosity in me isn't a feeling, it's a load-bearing wall.

Inside, the mill was cathedral-dark, dust hanging in the shafts of light from the broken skylights like the building was exhaling decades. My footsteps raised ghosts. And then, on the ground floor of the east wing — the fire wing — I saw it.

A room that had been swept. Actually swept, the dust pushed into deliberate piles at the walls, the debris stacked, the broken glass gathered into buckets. A camp stove, cold. A cot with a wool blanket folded at its foot with military precision. A shelf of canned goods arranged by size. A battery lantern. A Bible, spine cracked with use. And on the brick wall above the cot, taped in a neat grid, photographs — old ones, softened by time, and newspaper clippings, yellowed and folded sharp.

Four photographs. Four faces. Young men and women, laughing or solemn in the way of cheap portraits, each one captioned in careful block letters: *RUTH HOLLIS WORKER, DIED NOV 12 1994.* Names I knew now from the memorial wall downtown. The four who burned.

Below them, a fifth clipping, newer, from the *Kestrel Courier* of three weeks ago: *EXHUMATION ORDERED IN WREN CASE AFTER ANONYMOUS TIP.*

And beneath that clipping, written directly on the brick in pencil, in the same careful block letters, a single line:

*HE IS STILL HERE. SO AM I.*

The dust motes turned in the light. Somewhere upstairs, a pigeon muttered. I stood in a dead man's living room — because Elias Brandt, the fifth name on the fire's death roll, the worker nobody charged and nobody mourned because everyone thought he was ash, had made his home inside the wound — and I understood two things at once, the way you understand lightning and thunder as one event.

The man in my cab had been Elias Brandt, coming home to look at the graves of his coworkers. And the person who'd tipped the exhumation, who'd routed it through my dispatch line, who'd been watching me learn all this — wasn't hiding from me at all.

He was recruiting me.

Behind me, at the mouth of the stairwell, a floorboard spoke.
