## 12. Edmund's Offer

The note arrived on a silver salver at four o'clock, which told me two things before I'd read a word. First, that Edmund Ashcombe wanted me to know he could summon silver salvers the way other men summoned taxis. Second, that he'd learned my hours — when I came on shift, when I took my tea, when the lobby emptied of the kind of guests who gawk. A man who studies your schedule before he speaks to you is either courting you or casing you, and Edmund Ashcombe did not strike me as the romantic type.

*Drinks, eight o'clock, the Meridian bar. I'll be the one buying.*

No signature necessary. There was only one man in this city who signed his presence simply by occupying it.

I read the card twice, set it down beside the register, and considered my options, of which there were precisely one. You don't refuse an Ashcombe in his own hotel — and it was his hotel, whatever the paperwork said about holding companies and anonymous directors. I'd felt that truth in the walls for days now, the way you feel weather coming in a bad knee. The Meridian had been watching me longer than I'd been watching it.

"Expecting trouble?" Nora asked, passing behind the desk with a tray of glasses she was carrying purely for the pleasure of looking busy. She'd worked this hotel since Marguerite Ashcombe was a girl, and she read faces the way I read luggage.

"Expecting a drink," I said.

"From him?" She didn't say the name. She didn't need to. "Then eat something first. Men who buy you drinks want something, and it's easier to give nothing back on a full stomach."

I ate. It seemed wise to honor the counsel of the only person in the building older than its secrets.

* * *

The bar at eight was half-empty and fully lit, because Edmund had asked for the corner table by the window where the harbor showed itself off, and the staff had obliged by turning up every lamp within thirty feet. He sat alone with a bottle of something amber and two glasses, and when he saw me coming he spread his hands like a man revealing a winning hand he hoped you'd find charming rather than ruinous.

"Winterbourne." He pronounced all three syllables of it carefully, as though the name were an heirloom he was considering purchasing. "Sit. Please."

I sat. He poured without asking, which was its own small message: *in my house, we don't wait for permission.*

"You know," he said, settling back, "I've stayed in forty hotels this year. Geneva, Singapore, a place in Buenos Aires where they iron the newspaper." He said it without boasting, which somehow made it worse — the boredom of a man for whom the world's luxury had become wallpaper. "And none of them has a concierge who looks at you like an audit."

"That's not a compliment I'm equipped to return," I said. "You look at people like acquisitions."

"Do I?" He smiled. It was a good smile, maintained the way a garden is maintained — evidence of effort, no evidence of joy. Up close, the charm had seams. There were shadows under his eyes that no tailor could press out, and his hands, wrapped around the glass, were very still. Grief does that to some men. So does greed. From the outside, I've learned, they're nearly identical: the same stillness, the same appetite wearing mourning clothes.

"I'll come to it, then," he said. "You're not a man who enjoys foreplay."

"The drink is fine," I said. "The conversation can be too."

He laughed — a real one, brief and surprised out of him, and for a moment he looked younger and less armored, and I understood why the lobby adored him. Then the armor came back down, plate by polished plate.

"My sister died twenty years ago," he said. "Drowned. The police called it suicide, closed the file, sent everyone home. My father never recovered. My mother followed him inside three years. And now the estate is being sold, the house is being gutted by strangers with clipboards, and every few months some freelance archivist or hungry journalist digs up the Tideline case and makes it fresh meat again." He turned the glass a quarter-turn on the tablecloth. "You can imagine how I feel about fresh meat."

"I can imagine several things," I said. "One of them drowned in our harbor last week."

Something moved behind his eyes — quick, controlled, gone. Not surprise. Recalculation. "The archivist. Terrible business. The police seem to think you knew him."

"The police think a great many things. It's what keeps them busy."

"And do you? Know him?"

"I cataloged his effects. That's different from knowing him." I let that sit. "Though he did carry something interesting. A key. Brass. Old initials on it."

The quarter-turn of the glass stopped. If I hadn't been watching for it, I'd have missed it; if I hadn't spent twenty years watching people hide things behind glassware, I'd have missed it anyway. Edmund Ashcombe knew exactly what key I meant, and the knowledge cost him something.

"A family piece, possibly," he said lightly. Too lightly. "The Ashcombes collected brass the way other families collect debts."

"Your family collected both, from what I hear."

For a moment the charm dropped entirely, and what looked out at me was cold and patient and much older than his face. Then he chose, visibly, to laugh it off, and the laughter was worse than the coldness. Coldness I could work with. Charm was a tool, and tools have purposes.

"All right," he said. "Let's be adults with money. I'm told that's rare in this city." He leaned forward. "My father hired people, over the years. Investigators, mostly. After Marguerite died he became — obsessive. Certain the file was wrong. Certain someone knew something. He paid men to dig, and they dug, and they found nothing, and he died believing the world had conspired to keep a secret from him." His voice caught on the last word, just slightly, and I noted it the way I note everything: automatically, and then, a beat later, with actual feeling. Because it sounded true. That was the trap of Edmund Ashcombe — the grief was real. It was simply not the whole animal.

"When he died," Edmund continued, "I found receipts. Payments to a Detective A. Winterbourne. Your father."

The name landed in my chest like a coin dropped down a well. I kept my face still. Twenty years of practice at keeping my face still.

"He was a police cryptographer," I said. "Consulting work wouldn't be unusual."

"No," Edmund agreed. "It wouldn't. But consulting men leave files, Winterbourne. Notes. Decoded fragments. And my father's lawyers tell me that anything Aldous Winterbourne touched might still exist somewhere — in an attic, a storage box, a widow's keepsake drawer." He spread his hands again, that winning-hand gesture. "His widow being your mother, God rest her, and his effects being, presumably, yours."

"And you'd like to buy them."

"I'd like to *preserve* them," he said, with the smoothness of a man who had rehearsed the correction. "Family history. Closure. I'm selling the house, the land, the boats — everything but the past, and the past I can't sell until I know what's in it. Name a figure. Be unreasonable. I'll still pay it."

There it was. Greed and grief in the same tailored suit, each borrowing the other's voice. I thought of my father's storage box under the bed of my childhood, the smell of camphor and burnt paper, the half-charred pages I'd already begun to feed through the cipher key like a man feeding coins into a machine that printed danger. I thought of the payments in that ledger — dated after Marguerite's death, flowing like a quiet tide toward initials I couldn't yet bring myself to read.

"I'll consider it," I said.

Edmund studied me, and I watched him decide whether to push. He decided against it, which told me he had other levers, and preferred to save them.

"In the meantime," he said, refilling my glass unasked, "a kindness. Since you're clearly going to dig regardless — you have your father's eyes, and his eyes were always digging — let me save you some wasted shoveling." He paused, and here his voice changed, dropped its polish, went somewhere rawer. "Marguerite was pregnant when she died."

The bar noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. "That's not in any file I've seen."

"It wouldn't be. My father bought the discretion of every doctor south of the river. She was eight weeks along. She told no one — no one but me, the week before, and only because she was frightened and I was the only sibling she had." His jaw tightened. "She was going to run away. She said so. She said she'd found someone who'd help her disappear." He looked at me then, directly, and the grief in his face was so naked it was almost indecent. "So you tell me, Winterbourne, the great noticer — does a woman eight weeks pregnant, planning an escape, pack her coat and walk into the tide? Or does the tide have help?"

I had no answer I was willing to give a stranger. I had several answers I was unwilling to give myself.

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, which annoyed me.

"So am I." He finished his drink and stood, dropping a fold of notes on the table that would have covered a month of my salary. "Think about my offer. Anything your father left — papers, keys, notebooks, even his old habits. Especially his habits." At the word *habits* his gaze flicked, once, involuntarily, toward the front desk — toward the register, the guest cards, the machinery of the Meridian — and back. It lasted less than a second. I filed it where I file everything, in the drawer that doesn't lock.

"One more thing," he said, pausing. "The bellhop. Tam, isn't it? The one the police questioned about your closet." He smiled apologetically. "Small hotel. Small talk travels. If I were you, I'd keep him close. Debts make men useful, and useful men make enemies." 

He said it like concern. It landed like a threat with a bow on it.

"Goodnight, Mr. Ashcombe."

"Edmund," he corrected warmly. "We're practically partners, you and I. Both heirs to the same mess."

He walked out through his lobby, nodding to the porter, charming the night manager, leaving warmth behind him like a wake. I sat with the untouched second drink and turned over everything he'd given me, because a man like Edmund never gives anything without wrapping it in a price tag.

A woman planning to flee, eight weeks pregnant, helped to disappear by someone. My father, paid — by the grieving father, supposedly — to consult on the case. And a dead archivist carrying a key stamped with my father's initials, drowned in the harbor outside a hotel owned, if my instincts were right, by the very family whose tragedy kept paying dividends.

*Especially his habits.* He knew about the cipher. Which meant someone had told him, or someone had seen enough to guess — and there were perhaps three living people who'd glimpsed what my father's habits produced. One of them was feeding gulls on the promenade. One of them was me. And the third had checked into room 412 under a false name and never checked out except horizontally.

I walked back through the service corridor, past the closet with its polite little lock, past the dumbwaiter nobody used, and let myself into the records room where the old registration cards slept in their boxes like a morgue for names. Room 412. The false name. The forged handwriting — mine, or near enough to mine to chill me.

And beneath the forged signature, in the space for *referred by*, a notation I'd overlooked twice: a small, neat *C.R.*, written in ink twenty years older than the card had any right to carry.

I stood very still in the dark among the paper ghosts, and I heard Nora's voice in my head: *eat something first; it's easier to give nothing back on a full stomach.* Too late for that. My stomach had dropped through the floor, and what lay underneath it was a question I'd been refusing to ask since the first fragment decoded:

What if the initials in the ledger weren't a payment at all?

What if they were a lifeline?

I locked the records room behind me and went upstairs to burn the midnight oil over half-burned pages, because tomorrow the tide would bring in whatever it brought in, and I intended to be reading when it did.
