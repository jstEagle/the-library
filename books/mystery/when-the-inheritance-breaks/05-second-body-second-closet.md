## 5. Second Body, Second Closet

Sorrel Cade had been awake for thirty-one hours when the call came through, and she was standing in her kitchen holding a mug of tea she had forgotten to drink.

"Second one," said DS Ferro on the line, and the words landed in her chest like a dropped stone. "Mill Lane allotments. Woman in her seventies. And, ma'am — you're going to want to see what she's wearing."

"What is she wearing?"

There was a pause, the kind officers take when they're choosing which impossible thing to say first. "A plum-coloured coat. Fur collar. Kid gloves, still on her hands. There's a hat pinned to her head with a jet brooch."

Cade set down the mug because her hand had begun to shake, very slightly, in a way she refused to acknowledge. "That's Edith Marlowe's wardrobe."

"You know it?"

"I've spent four days reading a twenty-year-old trial transcript." She was already reaching for her coat. "Marlowe sat on the jury. She wore that outfit every day of the proceedings — it's in the press photographs. Don't touch anything. I'm ten minutes out."

* * *

The allotments at Mill Lane were the kind of place that looked peaceful in daylight and wrong at any other hour — rows of stripped bean poles, a shed with a rusted padlock, frost silvering the turned earth. The body lay between two plots, arranged on a folding chair that someone had carried half a mile across frozen ground to get there.

Edith Marlowe herself was alive, Cade confirmed within minutes; they'd woken her in her bungalow two streets away, where she'd slept through everything, her wardrobe door standing open like a mouth. The dead woman was Margaret Ellery, seventy-four, retired school secretary, no connection to Marlowe that anyone had yet found. No connection except the clothes.

And the pose.

Cade stood at the edge of the taped-off square and made herself look properly, the way she'd trained herself to look: not at the horror but at the composition. The woman sat upright in the chair, spine straight, hands folded in her lap atop a small leather-bound book. Her chin was lifted. Her gaze, even in death, seemed directed at some fixed point six feet away and slightly above eye level — as if she were listening to testimony.

"She's not slumped," Ferro said beside her. "Rigor would explain some of it, but not all. Someone positioned her. Arms like that, hands like that — deliberate."

"There's a book in her lap."

"Forensics are photographing it now. Looks old. Leather cover, no title."

Cade didn't need forensics. She'd read the trial record until the pages felt like her own handwriting. She knew what book a juror held in 2004 while the evidence was laid out before her. She knew the oath, the posture, the instruction from the judge: *You will keep an open mind and attend carefully to each witness.*

"She's posed as a juror," Cade said. "Seated in the jury box. Listening."

Ferro glanced at her. "Listening to what?"

"To the case." Cade heard her own voice go flat and careful, the voice she used when a thought frightened her and she wanted to starve it of drama. "This isn't a murder scene. It's a courtroom. We're standing in a reconstruction."

* * *

Back at the incident room, she pinned it up the way she'd once seen Theo Dravenmoor do it in a lecture she'd attended as a probationer — the famous detective, younger then, chalk-dusted and certain, explaining how a killer's logic could be mapped like a route. She'd taken notes. She still had the notebook somewhere. She'd admired him enormously, back when admiration was cheap.

Now she wrote on the whiteboard:

**VICTIM ONE** — unidentified male, marsh road. Grey overcoat, scarf. *Theo Dravenmoor's clothing.* Found posed near the spot where the first Dravenmoor victim was discovered in 2004.

**VICTIM TWO** — Margaret Ellery, Mill Lane. Plum coat, gloves, hat, brooch. *Edith Marlowe's clothing.* Posed as a juror, seated, holding a Bible or oath book.

Underneath, after a moment's hesitation, she wrote:

**THE TRIAL IS BEING RESTAGED.**

She stepped back and looked at it, and the room seemed to tilt around the sentence.

Because here was the thing that had been nagging at her since the marsh road, the thing she hadn't yet said aloud in front of anyone whose report might travel upward: the first scene hadn't just echoed the old murders. It had matched them. Not approximately — exactly. The distance from the gatepost. The angle of the body relative to the drainage ditch. Details that were never published, never released to the press, present only in the case file, the trial transcript, and the memories of people who'd been there.

Someone wasn't copying the file. Someone was *performing* it — and performing it with a precision that suggested they'd rehearsed. Suggested, in fact, that they'd been rehearsing for years.

"Ma'am." PC Anwar, young, earnest, holding a printout. "We ran the Ellery connection. Nothing direct. But —" he hesitated — "her daughter worked as a cleaner at Pell & Vane, the solicitors' office in Hollowmere, back in the nineties. Left in '99. Probably nothing."

"Probably," Cade agreed. "Get me everything anyway. And find out where Margaret Ellery's clothes came from — her own wardrobe, I mean, what she was wearing under the costume. If our killer dressed her the way we think, there'll be a gap between what she actually owned and what she was found in. That gap tells us how long she was held."

Ferro looked up sharply. "Held? You think she was kept alive?"

"The coat fits her badly. Look at the photos — shoulders too wide, sleeves rolled. Marlowe was a bigger woman than Ellery. Our killer took time to dress her in someone else's clothes, pin the hat, fold the hands. You don't do that to a body you've just found by chance on an allotment. You do it somewhere private, somewhere you can work undisturbed." Cade tapped the board. "Which means somewhere local, secure, and empty. A house, most likely. A big one."

She didn't say the name that rose in her mind, because there was no evidence yet, only a shape. Dravenmoor House had stood shuttered for twenty years. Everyone in Hollowmere knew it. Everyone had a story about it.

* * *

By evening she had Edith Marlowe sitting in an interview room with a blanket around her shoulders and a constable's handkerchief going grey in her grip. She was eighty-nine and sharp as broken glass, and grief for a stranger she'd never met sat oddly on her alongside something else Cade took a moment to identify: recognition.

"You want to ask about my clothes," Marlowe said. "Everyone keeps looking at my coat rack like it's going to confess."

"I'm sorry to put you through this."

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." Marlowe folded her hands — the same folded hands, Cade realised, as the dead woman's, and had to look away. "I've waited twenty years for someone to come back about that trial. Nobody ever did. They closed it up like a grave and told us all to get on with our lives."

"Tell me about the clothes. When did you last see them?"

"Last night, hanging in the wardrobe, where they've hung since the trial. I kept them out of — oh, call it vanity. Call it superstition. I wore that suit for eleven days of the Dravenmoor proceedings and I decided I'd earned keeping it." Her mouth thinned. "Whoever took it also took the brooch my husband gave me and my kid gloves, and they left everything else. My good jewellery was in the same wardrobe. Untouched."

"They weren't robbing you."

"No. They were *costuming* me." Marlowe said it without flinching, and Cade understood that this woman had already done the arithmetic alone in her kitchen, and had arrived at the same terrible sum. "I sat on that jury, Inspector. I listened to every word of it. And this morning a policeman described to me how that poor woman was found, and I'll tell you what struck him as odd and hasn't struck anyone else yet."

"Tell me."

"The hands." Marlowe held hers up, knuckles together, fingers laced. "Folded just so. Because that's how Judge Halloway instructed us to sit while we listened — well, he didn't instruct it, but the foreman did, day one, a fussy little man named Osgood who fancied himself in charge. Hands folded, eyes forward, no note-taking until breaks. Twelve of us sat like that for eleven days." Her voice cracked, just once, and steadied. "Somebody has studied us. Somebody knows how we *sat*, Inspector. That's not in any newspaper."

No, Cade thought. It wasn't in any newspaper. It was in the trial transcript only in the driest possible terms — *the jury retired at 4:40 p.m.* It was in nobody's file at all.

It was in memory. Someone's very long, very detailed memory.

"Mrs Marlowe. During the trial — did anything happen that never made it into the official record? Anything at all. A visitor, an odd remark, a juror who changed his mind?"

Marlowe was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a car passed; its headlights swept the interview room wall like a slow searchlight.

"One thing," she said finally. "And I've never told a soul, because telling it would have meant admitting we broke our oath, and I was raised better than that." She leaned forward. "On the ninth day, one of the jurors — I won't say who, she's dead now, God rest her — came to deliberations and said she couldn't convict. Said she'd seen something. She wouldn't say what, and we talked her round, or wore her down, I'm still not sure which, and in the end she went along with the verdict and hated herself for it, I could see it in her face every day after." Marlowe's eyes were dry and fierce. "If your killer is restaging that trial, Inspector, you should ask yourself what happens when they reach the part where a juror wanted to say no."

* * *

Cade drove back through Hollowmere in the dark, past the bookshop with its lights off, past the church, past the turning for Dravenmoor House, and let the town tell her what it always told her: nothing, pleasantly, in accents of brick and slate. Twenty years ago this place had produced a double murder, a fast arrest, a celebrated local detective, and a conviction so tidy that everyone had been able to stop thinking about it. Tidiness, Cade had learned, was rarely innocent. Tidiness was usually manufactured.

At the station, the forensic report on the oath book was waiting. Leather-bound, mid-century, gold-tooled. It belonged to Hollowmere Parish Church — stolen, along with a velvet kneeler, from the vestry sometime in the past week.

Not a Bible, then. A prop. The killer had gone out and sourced the correct furnishings, the way a theatre company sources set dressing. Cade spread the crime-scene photographs across her desk and forced herself to see them as stage directions rather than horror.

Victim one: the witness on the marsh road. Costume: the investigating officer — because it had been Theo Dravenmoor, twenty years ago, who'd walked that road and found the first body and begun the case that made his name.

Victim two: the juror. Seated, attentive, hands folded, oath book in lap.

It was a cast list. It was being filled in order. And if the production followed the structure of the original trial — and everything so far said it would — then the roles remaining included the solicitor, the judge, the witnesses, the family, the accused.

The defendant's chair.

Cade stood over the photographs for a long time. Then she picked up the phone and called the prison records office, because there was one question she needed answered before she could sleep, and it was this: Daniel Croft, convicted 2004, died in custody eight years later — she'd read that much. What she needed to know was whether anyone had ever claimed his body.

The answer came back at nearly midnight, in the flat tone of a clerk reading from a screen. No next of kin had come forward. The prison had handled the cremation. The ashes had remained uncollected for the statutory period and were disposed of per policy.

No one had claimed Daniel Croft. Which meant that whoever was staging this trial had no obligation to him either — or that they considered their obligation to be of an entirely different kind.

Cade wrote one more line on the whiteboard before she left, beneath the cast list, where she'd see it first thing tomorrow:

**WHO IS PLAYING THE DEFENDANT?**

Her instinct answered before her reason could stop it, and the answer had grey hair and a bookshop and a famous name, and she hated herself a little for thinking it.

But the coat fibres were still at the lab. And the lab would report by morning.

* * *

They reported at 6:12 a.m., and Cade read the email twice with the particular cold attention she reserved for findings that confirmed what she feared.

Grey wool fibres, consistent with the overcoat recovered from victim one — Theo Dravenmoor's overcoat — had been recovered from the plum coat worn by Margaret Ellery. Transfer evidence. Contact between the two garments. Recent, according to the preliminary assessment.

Either Dravenmoor's stolen clothing had been present when Ellery was dressed.

Or Dravenmoor himself had been.

Cade closed her laptop and sat in the blue-grey light of the incident room, watching the cleaners move between the desks, and thought about the old detective who had once taught her, in a bright lecture hall, that the neatest explanation is the one built for you.

The frame was beautiful. That was the problem. Every piece of it fit Dravenmoor like his own overcoat — which, of course, it literally was. And the person building it knew precisely how investigators thought, knew which threads they'd pull and in what order, knew the grammar of evidence the way Marlowe's killer knew the grammar of a courtroom.

Someone who had read every report. Someone who had studied the case the way Cade herself had studied it — only longer, and closer, and with a purpose.

She pulled the original 2004 witness list toward her and ran her finger down the names, one by one, casting them against the dead: the marshal road, the jury box, the remaining chairs. Near the bottom of the page, in the flat administrative type of a vanished era, was a name she'd skimmed three times without pausing, because the file listed her as deceased — a cousin of the murdered family, disinherited, drowned abroad, a footnote in someone else's tragedy.

Ada Voss.

Cade wrote the name on a sticky note, looked at it for a long moment, and stuck it inside her notebook, over the page where her old lecture notes began.

Then she picked up the phone to arrange an arrest warrant for Theo Dravenmoor — and hesitated, pen hovering, because the other thing the fibre report had told her, buried in paragraph four, was this: the transfer fibres had been found on the *outside* of Ellery's coat, pressed into the fabric in a pattern consistent with the garment having been folded around something. Or someone having carried both coats, folded together, in the same bag.

An innocent detail. Possibly. Or possibly the signature of a killer tidy enough to leave nothing else behind — and careless enough, suddenly, to leave this.

Nobody was that careless by accident, Cade thought. Carelessness that neat was a message.

She put the phone down without dialling, and reached instead for her coat. If she was going to arrest Theo Dravenmoor, she intended to look him in the eye first — and watch, very carefully, whether he already knew what was coming.
