## 15. Return to Dravenmoor House

Cade said no. She said it three times, in three different registers — professional, personal, and finally the flat voice she used when she'd run out of arguments and was down to fear.

"You're not a detective anymore, Theo. You're a witness. Possibly a suspect. If you walk into that house and something happens to you—"

"Then you'll know where I am." He zipped his coat. The grey one. He'd thought about leaving it on its hook forever, but it was his coat, bought with his money, and he was done letting other people wear his life. "You'll know exactly where I am. That's more than anyone knew twenty years ago."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't meant to be."

She stood in the doorway of the bookshop with her hands jammed in her pockets, and for a moment she looked very young, the way she must have looked at twenty-three, studying his case files in some training room, believing they were true.

"Give me until morning," she said. "I can get a warrant. I can have uniforms there by eight."

"The annotations say tonight." He tapped the last of the anonymous orders, still sitting on the counter: *Wuthering Heights*, first edition facsimile, with a line pencilled inside the cover in his uncle's hand — *The house keeps what the family buries. Come before it thaws.* "Whoever wrote this knows the marsh freezes after dark and softens by dawn. They want me walking out there while the ground is hard enough to hold a path."

"Or they want you walking out there alone because alone is easier to kill."

Theo looked at her. "If they wanted me dead, Sorrel, I'd have been the second body. Or the third. I've been wearing my own name on my back since this started, standing in an unlocked shop every night. They've had twenty years to rehearse. They don't want me dead." He picked up the torch from under the till. "They want me *reading*."

* * *

The marsh road was silvered with frost, and Theo walked it the way he had walked it as a young man, before he knew that roads could be evidence. The moon was up, thin as a paring. To his left the reeds stood rigid, each one furred white, and the sound of his boots was the only sound in the world.

Dravenmoor House rose out of the flat land like something the tide had failed to take. Two storeys of grey stone, shuttered, roofline sagging on the east side where a beam had gone rotten years ago and nobody had paid to fix it. The police tape from the original investigation had long since rotted away; a newer strand of it, bright yellow, hung across the gate like a ribbon on a gift.

He stopped at the gate. His breath came out in small ghosts.

He had not stood here since the funeral. That was the fact he kept circling and refusing to land on: twenty years, and he had never once come back, though the house was forty minutes' walk from his shop, though he passed the turning for the marsh road every day of his working life. He had told himself it was grief. Standing here now, with the frost biting through his trousers and his heart going like a trapped bird, he understood it had never been grief at all.

Grief would have brought him back.

He pushed through the gate. The path to the front door was overgrown, but someone had walked it recently — the frost was broken in a single line of prints, going up, not coming back.

*Come before it thaw,* the annotation said. And inside the shop's copy of *Great Expectations*, ordered two weeks ago, another line in his uncle's hand: *Start where the boy started.*

The boy. Him. Nine years old, arriving with his mother after the divorce, hating the house, hating the smell of it, learning the marshes the way other children learned streets.

The front door was not locked. It was not even latched. It swung inward at his touch with a groan that seemed to come up through the foundations, and cold air rolled out to meet him, air that smelled of dust and plaster and, beneath both, faintly, impossibly, of beeswax polish — as if someone had been keeping the place ready.

His torch found the entrance hall. The checkerboard tiles were filmed with grime except for a swept channel leading to the stairs. On the newel post, a sheet of paper had been pinned with a drawing pin.

Not typed. Handwritten, in a careful, upright script he did not recognise — and yet the words were in a voice he did.

*Room One. Your uncle's study. You were eleven when you first saw him burn papers in the grate and were told it was tax season. Tax season lasted all year, every year. Go and look in the grate.*

Theo stood in the hall a long time, listening to the house breathe around him. Then he climbed the stairs, because there was nowhere else in the world to go.

* * *

The study door was open. Of course it was.

The room was exactly as memory had filed it and nothing like it at all — smaller, meaner, the window smaller than he remembered, the desk a shabby thing he could have lifted one-handed. Memory had furnished this room with authority; the actual room had only ever contained a man. Edmund Dravenmoor, his uncle, solicitor to half the county, keeper of the estate, dead six years now and still, apparently, writing notes.

The grate was full of paper. Not ash — paper, curled and browned but never burned through, as if the fires had always been lit carelessly, or put out early. Theo crouched and drew out a fragment with two fingers.

It was letterhead. Ambrose Pell & Co., Solicitors. The date was twenty-two years old. Most of the text was eaten by damp, but one line survived, in Pell's cramped hand:

*—the Voss girl's declaration will need countersigning before the foreign office copy is lodged. E.D. to witness. No others.*

The Voss girl. Ada.

Theo sat back on his heels. The cold of the floor came up through his knees and he barely felt it. A death declaration. Countersigned by his uncle. Witnessed by no one else. Filed abroad, where no one from Hollowmere would ever look too closely.

He had spent weeks telling himself the killer was staging the case file — the murders, the trial, the roles. But this wasn't the trial. This was earlier. This was the foundation under the foundation.

There was a second note pinned to the desk blotter.

*Room Two. The nursery landing. You heard your uncle and Pell arguing there the night of the murders. You told the police you heard nothing. Ask yourself who taught you that nothing was worth hearing.*

He didn't remember the argument. That was the thing — he searched for it the way you probe a socket where a tooth used to be, and found smoothness, found scar tissue, found nothing. But the note described the landing precisely: the third stair from the top that creaked, the linen cupboard whose door never closed flush, the narrow window where, as a boy, he had watched the marsh lights and made up stories about smugglers.

He went and stood on the landing. The third stair creaked under his weight, faithful after all these years. The linen cupboard gaped. And the window —

The window looked down on the drive, and on the bend of the marsh road beyond it, and Theo's stomach turned over, slowly, like something rolling in its sleep.

Because he remembered the window. He remembered watching it. And underneath the story about smugglers — under twenty years of concrete poured over it — there was a shape. A woman, standing at the gate in the rain, not coming in, not going away. Just standing. Watching the house the way you watch a thing you are deciding whether to burn.

He had seen her. The night of the murders, nine years old, he had seen a woman at the gate, and he had told someone — he must have told someone — and then he had never thought of it again, not once, not in twenty years, until Nell Croft laid a photograph on a café table and showed him the same woman, the same stance, the same terrible patience.

*Ask yourself who taught you that nothing was worth hearing.*

"I'm asking," Theo said aloud. His voice fell into the empty house and died without an echo.

The next note was on the nursery door.

*Room Three. Your bedroom. You slept through four deaths in this house. Either you were the deepest sleeper in England, or someone made sure you woke up knowing nothing. Look under the floorboard your uncle called loose.*

* * *

His childhood room had been stripped bare — bedframe gone, wallpaper hanging in pale ribbons, a constellation of drawing-pin holes where posters had been. The floorboards wore a skin of dust, all except one, near the window, which was darker, cleaner-edged, recently pried and replaced.

Theo knelt. The board came up with a squeal that set his teeth on edge.

Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, was a child's tin cashbox, the kind with a combination lock every boy in England once owned. He knew the number before he touched the dial. 7-14-21. His birthday. His uncle had helped him set it, laughing, saying *no one will ever guess a clever lad like you*.

Inside was a notebook. His own handwriting — round, unformed, eleven years old. A diary, of the kind he'd kept for one obsessive summer.

Most of it was rubbish: scores of imaginary cricket matches, complaints about school dinners, a running feud with a boy named Peter Loxley. He turned the pages fast, embarrassed, and then slowed, and then stopped.

*12th October.* The night of the murders. The entry read:

*Saw the lady at the gate again. Uncle E says she is nobody and I must not look. But she waved at me. She had a green scarf. Uncle E gave me a whole pound to forget her and I said I wouldnt but I did buy the soldiers so maybe I did forget her a bit.*

A whole pound. In 1970s money, a fortune to a nine-year-old. Enough for a regiment of lead soldiers.

*Maybe I did forget her a bit.*

He sat on the dusty floor of his dead childhood, holding the diary, and understood — not suspected, not theorised, *understood* — that the forgetting had not been his. It had been purchased. Cultivated. Watered like a plant, year after year, with pocket money and praise and the slow, warm assurance that he was a good boy who remembered nothing inconvenient. His silence hadn't been taken from him. It had been *farmed*. And the harvest had been a career — the famous cases, the celebrated testimony, the detective everyone trusted — all of it built on a pound note and a regiment of toy soldiers.

The bookshop. God help him, the bookshop. Who had funded the lease? Who had co-signed the loan? Uncle Edmund, generous to a fault, proud of his nephew's new trade.

Every room of his life had a false wall in it, and someone had just walked him through the house of himself, opening doors.

The last note was tucked inside the diary's back cover, and it was different. Shorter. The handwriting pressed harder, the pen dragged, as if written by someone whose hand was shaking.

*Cellar. You know the way. You always knew the way. Bring the diary. Bring everything you've worked out. You owe the dead one honest hour, and so do I.*

No signature. But at the bottom, half-smudged, a small drawn mark: a circle with a line through it, like a coin seen edge-on.

Theo had seen that mark before. It was stamped, faintly, in gold leaf, on the flyleaf of every first edition in the locked cabinet at the back of his shop — the collection his uncle had left him, the collection that had paid for the shop's refurbishment, the collection he had never once questioned.

He put the diary in his coat, inside pocket, over his heart. He took up the torch. And he went down through the sleeping house toward the cellar stairs, following a path his feet seemed to know better than he did, while behind him, somewhere upstairs, a door he had closed swung slowly open on its hinge.
