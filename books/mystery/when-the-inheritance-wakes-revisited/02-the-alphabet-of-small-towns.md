## 2. The Alphabet of Small Towns

There is a particular kind of silence in a small town, and it is not the absence of noise. It is the presence of agreement. Everyone has decided, together, not to say certain things, and the collective restraint hums like power lines. Harrow's Bend had been humming for five years, and I had been one of its wires.

I reopened the case on a Tuesday, which felt appropriately bureaucratic. Not officially—officially, Edith Amberdale was dead of natural causes, her estate settled, her will probated by me, Jonah Amberdale, attorney at law, a man who trusted paper more than people and had never once been betrayed by it until three days ago.

The letter sat in my desk drawer like a loaded thing. *You read the wrong will. You know it now. Look again.*

So I looked again.

The signature page was the tell. I'd missed it because I'd wanted to miss it—because the document had arrived through channels I trusted, bearing seals I recognized, in an envelope handled by people whose families I'd known my whole life. But forgery is rarely about skill. It's about access. Someone had gotten to the signature page between the signing and the filing, and the ink on Edith's name sat a half-shade darker than the rest, the way ink does when it's been coaxed onto old paper with a damp brush and patience.

Patience. That was the word that kept me up that night. Whoever did this hadn't been clever so much as *unhurried*. They had known exactly how long a small town's memory takes to set.

I pulled the coroner's report from the sealed file—the real sealed file, the one in the county archive rather than the sanitized copy in my own cabinet—and requested it under the pretense of an estate audit. The clerk who processed the request worked two doors down from Mara Quill.

By Thursday, she was standing in my office doorway with a manila folder hugged to her chest like a shield, wearing the expression of someone who has rehearsed a speech in the car and intends to deliver all of it before losing her nerve.

"You requested the Amberdale autopsy," she said.

"I did."

"Under an estate audit."

"Estates get audited."

"Not five years after they close, and not by the lawyer who closed them." She stepped inside without being invited, which I noted, and shut the door behind her, which I noted harder. "Jonah. What did the letter say?"

I should have lied. A good lawyer would have lied. Instead I heard myself say, "How do you know about a letter?"

"Because you've alphabetized this town's secrets for fifteen years, and the week you start pulling sealed files is the week after Edith's funeral, and you look like a man who hasn't slept." She set the folder on my desk but kept her hand flat on top of it. "And because my father wrote that coroner's report, and before you build anything on it, you need to know he was told to."

The room went very quiet. Outside, someone's dog was losing an argument with a squirrel.

"Told to by whom?"

"That's the problem." Her hand lifted off the folder, and I understood she was giving me something she couldn't take back. "He never said. He died before he could say it, or before he was willing to. But I found his working notes after the funeral—his real ones, the ones he kept at home. Cause of death: cardiac event, consistent with the official finding. Time of death: revised twice. And in the margin, in pencil, the only honest sentence he ever wrote about Edith Amberdale: *Not what I saw.*"

*Not what I saw.* Three words, and my whole profession's architecture shifted under them. I had spent my career believing documents were the truth's skeleton. Here was proof they could be fitted with a false spine and taught to stand up straight.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "Your father—"

"Is dead, and can't be shamed, and left me holding a lie I didn't write." Her chin came up. "Also, whoever killed Edith—if someone killed Edith—has had five years to feel safe. People who feel safe get careless. I want to be there when it happens."

It was the least romantic declaration of alliance ever delivered in my office, and I found myself more moved by it than by most wedding vows I'd witnessed. Two archivists, recognizing each other across a desk full of other people's secrets.

"We work carefully," I said.

"We work quietly," she corrected. "Careful gets noticed. Quiet doesn't."

Mara's POV

Here is what nobody tells you about being the coroner's clerk's daughter in a town of four thousand people: you learn early that every file has two versions, the one in the drawer and the one in the town's mouth, and your job is to keep them from touching.

I had loved Jonah Amberdale from a distance measured in filing cabinets. He came into the records office maybe twice a month, always for something dull, always precise, always thanking me by name as though my name were a document worth handling correctly. I used to invent reasons to reshelve things near wherever he was standing. I told myself it was professional interest. I am a liar of long standing, but only on paper.

When he started pulling the Amberdale files, I knew within a day, because I know the sound our archive makes when someone opens the 2019 drawer—it sticks, and then gives, like a held breath released. And I knew why within three days, because grief makes people reckless and Jonah was grieving something no one else could see: the death of his own certainty.

So I brought him my father's notes, and I watched him read the pencil line—*Not what we saw*—and I watched a man decide, right there at his desk, to burn down the version of Harrow's Bend he'd spent his life maintaining. It was, I confess, a little beautiful.

Then we made the map.

It was Jonah's idea, naturally—he thinks in exhibits—but I supplied the method, because I am the one who knows where everything sleeps. We took a sheet of butcher paper and listed everyone with access to the sealed files in the five years since Edith's death. The county clerk. The deputy registrar. The sheriff, past and present. The estate's executor, Wendell Marsh, beloved donor of the library wing, coach of the Little League team for twenty years, a man so thoroughly decent that suspicion bounced off him like rain off waxed canvas. My father, posthumously. And me.

"And you," Jonah said, writing my name without hesitation, which I chose to find flattering rather than alarming.

"Me," I agreed. "Every suspect pool needs its inside woman."

"It's not a suspect pool. It's a list of people the killer trusted us not to look at." He tapped Wendell's name. "Look at the pattern. Every alteration—the will, the report, the filing dates—required someone the system waves through. Not a stranger. A fixture."

That was the night I understood the shape of what we were doing. Not investigating, exactly. Exhuming. Digging up the town itself to see what it had been buried over.

We worked late. He ordered food we barely ate. At some point our hands met over the same page—the margin note, the pencil ghost of my father's honesty—and neither of us moved away, and the silence changed key.

"This is a bad idea," I said, not moving my hand.

"Which part?"

"The part where the person who could destroy my family's name is also the person I—" I stopped. Filed the sentence unfinished. Some things don't go in the record.

Jonah looked at me the way he looks at a clause that doesn't parse. "Mara. If this goes anywhere, whoever did this will use you against me, or me against you. That's not a reason to stop. That's just the price list."

"Love is a liability," I said. "It doesn't file away."

"No," he said. "It doesn't."

We left it there, hands still adjacent, the butcher paper between us covered in the names of the town's saints. And that might have been the whole story—two lonely archivists, cautiously orbiting—if the phone hadn't rung at seven the next morning.

It was the county archive. There had been a break-in overnight. One file missing.

The Amberdale file. The original will—gone.
