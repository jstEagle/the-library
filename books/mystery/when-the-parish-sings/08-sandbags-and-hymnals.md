## 8. Sandbags and Hymnals

The crowd at the graveside made the sound a congregation makes when the sermon goes wrong — not a gasp, exactly, but a collective inhale, thirty people forgetting to breathe at once and then remembering in ragged stages, like a round sung badly.

I had my recorder up before the lid finished lifting. Old habits. When the world detonates, the podcaster's instinct is not to run toward safety but toward the sound, microphone first, like a moth with a press badge.

What lay in the coffin of Cornelius Ashgrove, founder of Hollowmere, benefactor of St. Brannoc's, the man whose granite likeness had watched over the square for a hundred and forty years, was this: four sandbags, a hymnal, and nothing else. No bones. No founder. No dust of the great man himself. Just sand and song, arranged with a care that was somehow worse than carelessness would have been.

Someone had *made the bed*. That was the detail that lodged in me like a splinter. The sandbags were stacked neatly at the head end, pillow-fashion, the way a mortician positions a body. The hymnal lay centered on them, closed, its spine aligned with the coffin's long axis. Whoever had done this hadn't just hidden an absence. They had *furnished* it.

"Stand back, please. Everyone stand back." The coroner, a small brisk woman named Dr. Ferrell whom I'd met exactly once at the chippy and who had already forgotten my name twice, was waving her arms with the weary authority of someone herding cats in a wind tunnel. "This is now a matter for the police. The *heritage* survey is concluded."

"Concluded," repeated Mr. Pengelly, the gravedigger, in a voice like a wheelbarrow going over gravel. He was seventy if he was a day, built like a fence post that had decided to become a barrel, and he had not moved from the foot of the grave. He was staring down into the coffin with an expression I couldn't read — grief, or guilt, or the particular blankness of a man doing arithmetic he'd hoped never to finish. "Thirty years I've mowed round that stone. Thirty years. And there was never anybody in it."

"Mr. Pengelly—"

"I dug that grave," he said, to no one, to everyone, to the hole itself. "I dug it in '89. Rain all week. Clay like butter, though. Clay's never hard in Hollowmere. My father always said—" He stopped. His face did something complicated. "I dug it," he said again, quieter, and I understood that the sentence had changed meaning entirely.

The vicar was white. Not pale — white, candle-wax white, the white of something that has been near a flame and decided against it. Nathaniel Pryce stood at the edge of the small crowd with his hands folded in front of him, and I watched his thumb press hard against the back of his left hand, over and over, a small metronome of distress that he probably thought nobody could see.

I saw it. I see everything. It's the whole job.

* * *

They let the crowd disperse and then, because I am constitutionally incapable of dispersing, I did not disperse. I drifted instead toward the churchyard wall and stood there with the particular studied idleness of a man who is not idle at all, while Dr. Ferrell and two constables from the nearest town — it took them forty minutes to arrive, which told me something about how seriously Hollowmere had been taken by the wider world — photographed the coffin, the sandbags, and the book.

The book.

I'd seen it the moment the lid came off, of course. Everyone had. A hymnal, dark green cloth, the gilt on its edges worn to ghost-silver by decades of thumbs. The kind of book that gets handed down through pews until it belongs to no one and everyone. I'd assumed it was a church copy, tucked in with the sandbags as a gesture, a joke, a prayer — *here, founder, you'll want something to read*.

It was only when the constable lifted it out, gloved and gingerly, that I saw the front cover properly.

There was a name stamped in gold on the lower right corner, the way churches mark their property. But this wasn't a church stamp. It was a presentation plate, the old-fashioned kind, and beneath the gold — much of it rubbed away — I could make out the engraved letters through my camera's zoom:

*To Ada, who makes the psalms behave. With love, the Choir, Easter 1978.*

Ada.

I took eleven photographs of that book in the space of four seconds and I am not ashamed of a single one.

"Excuse me," I said, materializing beside the constable with the smile I'd practiced in mirrors until it stopped looking like a threat. "Emrys Whitlock. I'm doing some local history work — would it be all right if—"

"No," said the constable.

"Absolutely not," said Dr. Ferrell, arriving at speed. "Mr. — Whitlock, was it? The podcaster." She said the word *podcaster* the way you'd say *rat-catcher*, with a flicker of grudging respect. "I know who you are. Half my nurses listen to you. The other half want you struck off something."

"That's the usual split," I said.

"The book stays with us. The scene stays sealed." She peered at me over her glasses. "And you'll be wanting a formal interview, I expect."

"I'd settle for a cup of tea and five minutes."

"You'll settle for an appointment. Tuesday. My office. And Mr. Whitlock—" She glanced back at the open grave, at the sandbags lying in their neat, terrible order, and something moved behind her professional face. Something that looked, for a moment, like fear. "In thirty years of coroner's work I have seen coffins with the wrong people in them. I have seen coffins with too many people in them. I have never once seen a coffin with *furniture* in it. Whoever did this wanted it found. Not today, maybe. But by someone. Someday."

She turned back to her constables. I turned back to my recorder, which had caught every word, and my hands were not entirely steady, which was new, and not entirely unwelcome, which was newer.

Because she was right. And because I had already worked out the part she hadn't said out loud.

The exhumation wasn't an accident of bureaucracy. Oh, the heritage survey was real — I'd read the paperwork, DNA archiving, the whole modern ritual of proving the dead are who we say they are. But the order had moved through the system with unusual speed, and the survey had specified Ashgrove's plot *by name*, when a dozen other founders' graves in the county had been quietly skipped. Someone, somewhere, had nudged. Someone had wanted the lid lifted.

And whoever that someone was had left a message inside, addressed — I was suddenly, coldly certain — to exactly one kind of reader.

To whoever finally dug.

* * *

Mrs. Trelawny had the kettle on before I got my coat off, which I was learning was less hospitality than reflex, the way other people breathe. The B&B kitchen smelled of soda bread and woodsmoke, and the rain had started again, drumming on the slate roof with the patience of something that intended to go on for geological ages.

"You heard, then," she said. It wasn't a question. In Hollowmere, by half past three, everyone had heard, and by four everyone would have an opinion, and by six the opinions would have hardened into the town's new geology.

"I heard."

"Sandbags." She set a mug in front of me. The mug said WORLD'S OKAYEST GARDENER and had a chip shaped like Wales. "Forty years I've lived in this parish, Emrys, and I have never been so embarrassed by furniture."

Despite everything — despite the cold in my stomach and the book-shaped thought lodged behind my eyes — I laughed. She allowed herself the ghost of a smile, then took the chair opposite me and folded her hands, and the smile went wherever smiles go when they're not wanted.

"You'll write about it," she said. "Your podcast. Tonight, probably."

"Thursday," I said. "I have standards."

"You have a schedule." She said it without heat. "I've heard your little programs, you know. My niece set them up on my telephone. You have a very confident voice for someone who eats bread like it's a competitive sport."

"Thank you. I think."

"It wasn't entirely a compliment." She looked out the window at the rain. "I want to ask you something, and I want you to answer me honest, because I'll know if you don't. When you saw that book come out of the ground — what did you think?"

Here's the thing about Mrs. Trelawny: she asked questions the way a surgeon asks them, already knowing where the knife will go. I could have lied. I've lied to better interrogators. But there was something in the set of her shoulders — a tiredness, a *readiness* — that made lying feel like kicking a dog.

"I thought it was Ada Voss's," I said. "And I thought it was left there on purpose. For whoever dug."

The silence went on long enough that the kettle had time to think better of things and click off. Mrs. Trelawny's hands stayed folded. Her thumbnail, I noticed, was pressing into her knuckle — the same small repeated pressure I'd seen on the vicar's hand an hour before. I filed that away in the drawer where I keep things that frighten me.

"Ada Voss," she said at last, "was the finest musician this parish ever produced, and the kindest, and the most stubborn, and if that book was hers then it was hers to the last page, because she never let go of anything she loved." She stood, abruptly, and took her mug to the counter, and spoke with her back to me, which I understood was the Hollowmere way of saying something true. "There's a loaf in the tin. Don't eat it all standing up like a gull. And Emrys—"

"Yes?"

"Whatever you think you've found down in that hole — it isn't a mystery yet. It's only a hole. Mind the difference."

She left the kitchen. I sat with my tea and the rain and the loaf I was absolutely going to eat standing up like a gull, and I turned her last sentence over, because it was a warning wearing a proverb's clothes, and Mrs. Trelawny did not strike me as a woman who wasted words on proverbs.

*It isn't a mystery yet. It's only a hole.*

Which meant she knew what it would take to make it a mystery. Which meant she'd thought about it before. Which meant—

I put the thought down carefully, the way you put down something that might be live.

* * *

That night, in my attic room with the rain doing its patient work on the roof, I laid out everything I had on the bed like a surgeon laying out instruments, except my instruments were a laptop, a notebook, two audio recorders, a printout of the 1994 missing-persons file, and a photograph I'd taken through a telephoto lens of a hymnal on a police evidence table, which I had no business having and which I had already enhanced past the point of decency.

The front plate. *To Ada, who makes the psalms behave. With love, the Choir, Easter 1978.*

I pulled up the missing-persons file. Ada Voss, choir mistress of St. Brannoc's, last seen the evening of the ninth of October, 1994, walking from the church toward home. Never found. No body, no belongings, no bank activity, no sightings. A woman who had walked out of the world and left no fingerprints on the doorframe.

Except, perhaps, one.

Because here is what I kept coming back to, the thought that would not sit down: a hymnal is a *personal* object. Church pews are full of anonymous ones. But a presentation copy, inscribed, loved, carried — that's a diary that only sings. You don't bury your own hymnal with a stranger. You don't bury it with sandbags and a joke. You leave it where it will be found *only if someone goes looking*, and you leave it because the dead man's grave is the one place in a parish that nobody — *nobody* — will disturb until the right question is asked.

The grave wasn't a burial. It was a *letter*.

And letters have authors. And authors have reasons. And reasons have a shape, and the shape of this one said: *I could not tell you while I lived, and I could not tell you plainly, but if you ever dig here — if you ever, ever dig here — know that what this town buried was never Cornelius Ashgrove. What this town buried was the truth, and here is the only piece of it I could put my hands on.*

I sat back on the bed. The rain kept on. Somewhere below me, Mrs. Trelawny's floorboards creaked their slow midnight rounds, and the house settled around its secrets the way old houses do, with small sighs.

There was one more thing. I'd almost missed it, in the flurry of the graveside and the drive home and the tea. But the photograph didn't miss things. That's what photographs are *for*.

I zoomed in on the hymnal's spine, where the constable's gloved fingers had held it open for the camera, and there, on the flyleaf visible for half a second between glove and page — I saw ink. Not print. Handwriting. A single line, faded, in a careful old-fashioned hand, written beneath the presentation inscription as if in answer to it:

*Sing me home when you find me.*

I must have looked at it for twenty minutes. Long enough for the rain to change its mind twice. Long enough for the candle-shaped lamp to buzz and the house to go fully quiet, that particular country quiet that isn't silence but listening.

*Sing me home when you find me.*

Not *find my body*. Not *find my killer*. *Find me* — as though the finding were still possible, as though somewhere in the world a woman who vanished in 1994 was, in some sense that made my skin prickle, still findable. And *sing me home* — addressed, I'd have bet my microphone on it, to the choir. To whoever came after her. To the voices she'd trained, the psalms she'd made behave.

A message left in a grave, in a stolen coffin, under a founder's stone, by someone who knew that someday the paperwork of the living would come for the dead — and who wanted the right person to be reading when it did.

I opened my laptop. The cursor blinked in the empty episode-fifty document, patient as a gravedigger.

I typed the only title that seemed honest:

*Episode Fifty: The Letter in the Coffin.*

And then, because I am who I am, I picked up my phone and dialed the county records office — yes, at eleven at night; no, I don't sleep like other people — and asked, in my most disarming voice, for one small thing.

I wanted to know who had filed the heritage survey. Who, exactly, had named Cornelius Ashgrove's plot.

The clerk on duty, bless her insomniac heart, pulled it up while I held. And when she read me the name of the survey's sponsoring party, I wrote it down in my notebook, and I looked at it for a long time, and outside the rain finally stopped, the way rain does in Hollowmere — all at once, like a held breath let go.

The name on the form was the parish itself.

The signature authorizing it belonged to the Vicar of St. Brannoc's.

N. Pryce.

The man whose face had gone candle-white at the graveside. The man whose thumb had beaten its little frightened rhythm against his hand. The man who had, three weeks before any survey was public knowledge, welcomed me to Hollowmere with warmth I'd privately filed under *suspicious*.

He hadn't been shocked by the empty coffin.

He'd been *dreading* it. For years, maybe. The way you dread a letter you sent yourself, finally arriving.

I closed the notebook. I did not sleep. And in the morning, I was going to church.
