## 11. The Man in 214 Speaks

Edmund Voss had spent eleven years learning how to be a ghost, and the first thing a ghost learns is patience.

He had been patient through the exhumation — he'd heard about it before Sheriff Colter finished telling him, because the Marlowe Grand's walls were thin and Mrs. Abernathy in 216 narrated everything to her sister on the phone at a volume that could be heard from space. He had been patient through the sheriff's warnings, through the keyed car downstairs (which he regretted; that was the mayor's boy's work, crude and frightened), through six weeks of eating soup he didn't want because Dr. Okafor's replacement had put him on a low-sodium diet and death, apparently, was no excuse.

But the concierge girl was going to ruin everything, and patience could not save him.

She had found Room 214. He knew it the way you know weather is coming — not by seeing it but by feeling it in your bones. The keycard log. Of course the keycard log. In eleven years of hiding in plain sight, in a town where half the population would have walked into traffic for him, it was not a face or a fingerprint or a careless word that undid him. It was a ledger. He should have been flattered.

So on the evening after she stood in his room touching his Bible with one finger, like it might bite her, Edmund Voss did the thing he had sworn to Colter he would never do.

He invited her up.

* * *

He dressed for the occasion. This took twenty minutes and cost him more sweat than he cared to admit, but there were standards. A man who has faked his own death owes the world at least a pressed shirt when he finally admits to being alive. He chose the gray cardigan Amara had once said made him look like a professor instead of a pirate, then hated himself for choosing it, then wore it anyway, because at ninety-one years old you stop pretending your ghosts don't dress you.

At seven o'clock exactly, someone knocked. Three soft raps. Polite. Professional. She even knocked like a concierge.

"Mr. Voss," Petra Okonkwo said when he opened the door, and he watched her decide, in real time, whether to scream, run, or check him in.

To her enormous credit, she checked him in.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said. "You're also standing in a room I'm fairly sure doesn't exist on my books. One of these is a bigger problem than the other, and I haven't decided which."

"The room," Edmund said, stepping back to let her in, "has always existed. It's the books that are wrong. That's been the trouble with this parish from the beginning — the books are wrong and everyone agrees to love them anyway."

She came in the way she did everything, he was learning: carefully, completely, missing nothing. Her eyes went to the Bible on the nightstand. To the photograph face-down on the dresser, which he had turned over after her visit and now, foolishly, turned face-up again as if honesty were a thing you could do in increments. To the pill organizer on the table, Monday through Sunday, each compartment a little monument to his failing heart.

"Sit down, Miss Okonkwo," he said. "Or stand. You strike me as a woman who stands when she's working."

"I'm not working," she said. "I'm off duty."

"Liar," he said pleasantly. "Sit anyway."

* * *

She sat in the chair by the window with her spine straight and her hands folded, and Edmund lowered himself onto the edge of the bed because the chair would have swallowed him and getting out of it again would have required a crane and a prayer.

For a while neither of them spoke. Down in the street, someone was practicing organ at St. Brigid's — badly, the way they always did on Thursdays, the same four bars of "Be Thou My Vision" climbing and falling like a man learning to swim.

"You sent for me," Petra said at last. "Colter said — well, Colter didn't say much of anything, which is its own kind of saying. But the tip. The guestbook. You signed *E. Voss* three days before they dug up your grave. That wasn't carelessness."

"No," Edmund agreed. "It was an invitation."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only person in this parish who notices things and might still keep a secret." He held up a hand before she could object. "Don't argue. I've watched you for six weeks. You notice the soap is wrong in the lobby bathroom. You noticed Mrs. Pruitt's son was stealing from the collection plate two years before anyone else did, and you said nothing, because his mother was sick and the money bought her medicine. Don't look surprised. Colter tells me things. It's mostly gossip. Old men in hiding run on gossip the way cars run on gasoline."

"That's not —" She stopped. Started again. "I reported him eventually."

"You did. After she died." Edmund nodded slowly. "You let mercy run its course first. That's the whole job, Miss Okonkwo. Most people either notice and tell, or keep quiet and go blind. You do the harder thing. You see everything and choose what to carry."

"And what makes you think I'll choose to carry *you*?"

"Nothing," he said. "That's rather the point. I've spent eleven years surrounded by people who carry me because they owe me, or love me, or are afraid of what happens if they set me down. Theo digs my empty grave and calls it gratitude. Dane guards my door and calls it loyalty. Father Mike absolves himself every Sunday and calls it faith. None of them ever once asked me if I deserved any of it." His voice caught on something — phlegm, probably, though he suspected it was older than phlegm. "You're the first person to walk into this room who doesn't owe me a thing. So whatever you decide, it will actually count."

Petra looked at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed — a short, disbelieving sound.

"You're charming," she said. "That's the worst part. I had built up this — this monster, this founding-father criminal, and you're sitting here in a cardigan making conversation like we're at a hotel wine tasting."

"I was never a monster," Edmund said. "Monsters are simple. I'm only a man who did one unforgivable thing and then had the poor judgment to live a very long time afterward."

"One thing," she repeated. "Amara Diallo."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"Yes," he said quietly. "Amara Diallo."

* * *

He told it the way he had rehearsed telling it for forty-five years, and then, somewhere around the middle, abandoned the rehearsal entirely, because the girl's eyes were steady on him and lies need an audience willing to blink.

"We started the mill together. 1974. Her money, my name — nobody lent to a woman then, especially not to her, especially not in this county, and she was smart enough to know it and furious enough to agree. We ran it five years. Good years. The best of my life, if I'm honest, and I'm trying tonight, God help me, to be honest." He pressed his palms flat on his knees. "Then the audit came. Not a government audit — a private one. Investors. And there was money missing. A great deal of money."

"You took it," Petra said. Not a question.

"I took some of it," Edmund said, and winced at how the sentence sounded, how it had always sounded, how it would sound carved on stone. "I took some of it, and when the investors demanded a name, I gave them hers. She signed documents she shouldn't have — I asked her to, back when we trusted each other, back when signing for each other was just what partners did. I handed those papers over and I watched them take her, and I told myself she'd understand. I told myself she'd fight it and win, because she was smarter than all of them." His hands had begun to tremble; he pressed them harder. "She didn't fight. She ran. Winter of '79, gone, declared dead eventually, and I let the world bury her because a buried partner can't testify."

"And the money?"

"Gone with her. Or so I told myself for thirty years." He looked out the window at the darkening street, at the church spire against the last orange light. "You have to understand what she was to me. She wasn't a partner I betrayed. She was the person I built everything *with*, and I burned the blueprint and called the fire progress. Every dollar this town ate during the recession — the food pantry, the school roof, Theo Okafor's legal fees when he was nineteen and stupid — all of it came out of a theft I committed against a woman whose name I forbade anyone in this parish to speak. I made her a ghost twice over. Once by law. Once by silence."

Petra hadn't moved. But her hands had come unfolded, and he saw that she was gripping the arms of the chair.

"She betrayed me first," Edmund said, and heard immediately how small it sounded, how exactly like every guilty man in every courtroom since the invention of courtrooms. He held up a hand. "No. Let me finish, because I'm going to say it and then I'm going to take it back, and you deserve to hear both. That's what I told myself, all these years. That she skimmed first. That the missing money started before I touched anything. And maybe it's even true — I genuinely don't know anymore. Forty-five years of retelling a story wears grooves in it. You slide into the groove and call it memory." He exhaled. "There. Taken back. Whether she betrayed me first is a question I lost the right to ask in 1979, when I let them hunt her for my crime. Whatever she did, I did worse, because mine had a witness and hers had only me."

The organ downstairs stumbled through the same four bars again.

"What do you want from me?" Petra asked. "Really. Not the speech. The actual thing."

Edmund studied her. Ninety-one years old, and here was the first honest negotiation of his life, arriving at the end like a bill collector.

"I want to say her name in front of the town," he said. "Before I die. Which is soon — don't make that face, I've had the cardiologist's opinion and the cardiologist does not flatter. I want to stand in St. Brigid's, or on the courthouse steps, or in the middle of Main Street if it comes to that, and say *Amara Diallo* out loud where everyone who kept her silence can hear it. And I can't do it alone, because the moment I try, Dane will have me sedated and shipped to a clinic in Reno before lunch. I need someone the town trusts. Someone clean." He smiled, and knew it was a crooked smile. "Someone who works the front desk and knows where every body in this parish is buried. Figuratively. Mostly figuratively."

"You're asking me to arrange your confession."

"I'm asking you to arrange my *funeral*," Edmund said. "The real one. The one I skipped eleven years ago. Everyone deserves one funeral where the coffin matches the contents. Mine's been running a deficit since 2013."

* * *

She stood, then, and walked to the window, and he let her think, because thinking was clearly her native language and interrupting it would be rude.

"You said she betrayed you first," Petra said, without turning around. "You took it back. But you said it. Why?"

"Because it's the last wall I have," Edmund said simply. "A man can give up his money, his name, his grave, his god. But if he gives up the story where he's the wounded party, he has nothing left to stand on. And I am very tired, Miss Okonkwo, and standing is difficult."

She turned. In the lamplight her face was unreadable, which he suspected meant it was extremely readable and she was choosing mercy.

"There's a woman," she said slowly, "two counties over. Ruth Diallo. Goes by another name now. She came back to this parish recently, asking questions about you. About the mill. About 1979." She paused. "Ruth Diallo is Amara's daughter, Mr. Voss. And I don't think your wife — your *partner* — is dead either. I think she's been alive this whole time, and I think she may be dying now, and I think her daughter wants something from you that I don't think you've guessed yet."

The room tilted, gently, the way rooms tilt when a heart misbehaves. Edmund gripped the bedpost.

"Alive," he said. The word came out cracked down the middle. "She's —"

"If I'm right," Petra said, "then your funeral is going to have a guest of honor. And Mr. Voss?" She picked up her keys from the table, and her voice was kind in the way that weather is kind right before it changes. "Whatever you're planning to confess on Sunday — I'd start practicing the part where you say her name. Because I have a feeling you're going to have to say it to her face."

The door closed softly behind her, the way doors close at good hotels.

Edmund Voss sat alone in Room 214, in his dead wife's favorite cardigan, and discovered that a man can survive fraud charges, a fake funeral, and eleven years of exile — but not the sentence *she's alive*, delivered by a concierge in a pressed uniform, in a voice that left no room whatsoever for doubt.

Downstairs, faintly, the organ finally got the hymn right.
