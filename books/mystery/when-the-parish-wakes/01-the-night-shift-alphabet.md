## 1. The Night Shift Alphabet

The clock above the dispatch desk said 3:07 a.m., which was the hour Coral Yarrow thought of as the bottom of the alphabet — the point in the night where the letters ran out and the town had nothing left to say to itself.

Marrow's End Taxi & Dispatch occupied the ground floor of what had once been a chandler's shop on Salt Lane, back when the harbor still worked for a living. Now it was a taxi office, a night dispatch point for the parish's emergency after-hours line, and — though the sign out front didn't mention it — the closest thing the town had to a memory. Coral ran all three functions from a single desk with a green-shaded lamp, a radio console that hummed in B-flat, and a filing system he had spent eleven years perfecting and would defend, if pressed, with something close to violence.

The system went like this. Every call, every fare, every after-hours request for an ambulance, a locksmith, a priest, came into the office and got logged in a ledger — paper first, always paper, because paper didn't crash — then transcribed into the digital log, then cross-indexed by street, by caller, by time of night, and by what Coral privately called *weather*: the mood of a call, marked in the margin with a small code of his own invention. A flat calm was **C** for clear. A drunk argument was **S** for static. A call that made the hair on his arms stand up was **Q** — for quarry, though he'd never told anyone that, and never examined why.

Eleven years of ledgers, forty-one volumes, each one indexed in his own hand. He knew the town the way other men knew their mothers' voices. He knew that Mrs. Petraglia on Cordwainer Row called at 2:40 every morning to say her late husband was knocking in the walls, and that the correct response was to send a taxi past her house with the interior light on, which settled her until dawn. He knew that the payphone outside the Fisherman's Rest only worked when the humidity dropped below sixty percent, and that when it did, it was usually Danny Fitch calling his ex-wife, and that the correct response was to let it ring through and not log the name. He knew which streets flooded, which houses had dogs, which had guns, which had grief.

He knew, in other words, everything. And knowing everything was how he had arranged his life so that he needed no one, and no one needed him — a bargain so clean he'd stopped noticing it was a bargain at all.

The radio crackled. Unit Twelve, out on the coast road, checking in. Nothing. The clock said 3:11.

Coral pulled the night's ledger toward him and began the filing.

This was the part of the shift he loved, though he'd have denied the word *love* under oath. The transcribing. The alphabetizing. The small, holy act of putting a night in order. He wrote in a hand so even his old schoolmaster had accused him of tracing. Street names first, then caller or no caller, then time in and time out, then the weather code. Tonight had been a quiet night — three fares, one lost dog on Bellwether Hill (returned by the butcher, logged under Animals, Subsection: Recovered), and a 1:15 call from the chapel asking whether anyone could check on old Mrs. Vane, who hadn't been seen since Tuesday. Someone had. She was fine. She'd been at her sister's. Coral had logged it, cross-indexed it, and closed the volume on it.

That was the whole art, as he practiced it: everything gets a place, and everything stays in its place.

He was on the *M*s — Marsh Road, Mill Lane, the Mole — when the phone rang.

Not the radio. The landline. The direct line, the old copper number that the parish had published in 1987 and never bothered to update, the one that rang through to his desk with a bell instead of a chirp. At 3:22 a.m.

He let it ring twice. Habit. The first ring was almost always a wrong number or a drunk; the second ring was where the truth started.

"Marrow's End dispatch, Coral Yarrow."

Silence. Not dead air — *listening* silence. He'd logged enough hang-ups to know the difference. Dead air was a pocket dial, wind, a phone in a bag. Listening silence had a person in it, holding still, and this silence had a person in it the way a locked room has a person in it.

He waited. He was good at waiting. Eleven years of night shift taught you that most people fill silence eventually, and what they fill it with is always the true thing.

"Is this the night line?" the voice said at last.

It was a man's voice, or a woman's pitched low — smoothed out, deliberate, like someone reading from a script they'd rehearsed until the rehearsal stopped showing. Middle-aged. Local. Coral could place an accent to the street in this parish, and this one was Marrow's End born and bred, the flat vowels of the harbor end, softened by years of speaking carefully to people who weren't from here.

"It's dispatch," Coral said. "Who's calling?"

"I need to report something."

"Go ahead. What's the location?"

Another silence. Then, and Coral would remember this for the rest of his life, the voice said:

"Quill House."

The pen stopped.

Quill House stood on the headland past the quarry road — the big grey place with the iron gates, empty going on a decade, the last of the Quills gone or scattered, the gardens gone to bramble. Everyone in Marrow's End knew Quill House the way everyone knows a grave. But it wasn't the emptiness that stopped Coral's pen. It was the fact that Quill House had no address. Not in the postal registry, not in the parish rolls, not in his own index — and Coral's index had everything. The house predated street numbering on the headland. The lane it sat on had never been named. In forty-one volumes of meticulous records, there was no entry under Quill House because Quill House, officially, did not exist.

"Say again your location," Coral said, and heard his own voice do the thing it did when he was unsettled — get quieter, get flatter, get *precise*.

"Quill House," the voice said. "Headland road. Past the quarry turn. You'll know it."

"Sir, there's no registered address at that location. If you're reporting an emergency, I need—"

"There's no emergency." A pause. "Not yet."

Coral wrote the word *not yet* in the margin before he could stop himself, and then looked at it, and did not cross it out.

"What are you reporting?" he asked.

"A drowning."

The lamp hummed. The radio hissed its B-flat. Somewhere out on the harbor the tide was doing what the tide does, which is the same thing it has always done, indifferent and on schedule.

"When?" Coral asked. His pen was poised. This was the discipline: get the facts, log the facts, the facts will hold you up when nothing else will.

"Fifteen years ago," the voice said. "Give or take. I'm reporting a drowning that happened fifteen years ago, and no one ever logged the call."

Coral set the pen down. He did it carefully, aligned with the ledger's edge, because his hands wanted to do something else with them.

"You're telling me someone drowned at Quill House fifteen years ago."

"At the quarry below it. The old swimming hole, before they fenced it. A girl." The voice paused, and for the first time the smoothness cracked — just a hairline, just enough to show the thing underneath was breathing. "Ada Quill. June of 2009. It was ruled accidental. It wasn't."

The name went through Coral like a current through water. Ada Quill. He'd been twenty-two that summer, three months into the night desk, still learning the streets, still writing in a hand his schoolmaster wouldn't have recognized. He remembered the summer the girl drowned — the whole parish remembered it; it was the kind of thing Marrow's End kept the way the sea keeps things, on the floor, under grey water, apparently forgotten and never once gone. He remembered the funeral, the iron gates, the brother in the black coat. He did not remember a call about it. He would have sworn, sitting there at 3:26 in the morning with a stranger's voice in his ear, that no call had ever come through this line about Ada Quill.

And then a colder thought arrived, quiet as the tide: *would you remember?*

Eleven years of ledgers behind him. But 2009 was before the ledgers. 2009 was the loose-leaf years, the years before the system, the years he'd boxed up and stored and never indexed. There were boxes in the back room he hadn't opened in a decade.

"Who is this?" Coral asked.

"You're the one who keeps the records," the voice said. "Check yours."

And the line went dead. Not slammed, not hung up with any violence — just set down, gently, the way you'd set down something you were finished with.

Coral sat with the receiver in his hand for a long time. The dial tone came and went. Outside the window, Salt Lane lay empty under the streetlight, wet from an evening rain, the whole town dark except for the chapel lamp and, far up the hill, one window in the almshouse where old Mr. Tredwell never slept. Coral knew that window. Coral knew every lit window in Marrow's End at 3 a.m., the way a lighthouse keeper knows the boats.

He put the receiver down. He opened the ledger to the current night and, in his even hand, logged the call: *3:22, direct line, male voice (or female, low), no caller ID. Reported drowning, Ada Quill, quarry below Quill House, June 2009, ruled accidental, disputed. Weather code: Q.*

Then he sat back and looked at what he'd written, and understood that the filing system had just failed him for the first time in eleven years, because there was no subsection for this. There was no place to put a confession that arrived fifteen years late, from a house that had no address, reporting a death that had already been explained.

*Not yet*, he'd written in the margin. He looked at those two words again. The voice had said them like a promise. Or a schedule.

Coral Yarrow was not a brave man, and he would have been the first to say so — not with false modesty, but with the flat accuracy he applied to everything. He was a careful man. A thorough man. A man who had built an entire architecture out of the town's small emergencies so that he never had to stand inside one himself. He dispatched; he did not attend. He logged the fire; he did not feel the heat. That was the whole design of his life, and it had worked, and he had called the working of it *competence*, and the calling of it competence had been the kindest lie he knew.

But he could not file this. That was the thing that kept him in the chair past four o'clock, past the first grey suggestion of dawn over the harbor wall. The call had no place. And a thing with no place, in Coral Yarrow's world, was not a nuisance — it was a hole. A hole in the alphabet. And Coral had spent eleven years learning what came through holes.

At 4:40 he did something he almost never did. He got up from the desk, walked back through the taxi office with its sleeping fleet of keys on hooks, and unlocked the door to the back room — the archive, he called it, though it was really just a storeroom with shelving he'd built himself, and the boxes from the loose-leaf years stacked at the far end under a tarp, unindexed, unalphabetized, unlooked-at since the Obama administration.

He pulled the tarp off. The cardboard had gone soft at the corners. The top box said, in his own younger, less even handwriting: **2009 — JAN–JUN.**

June of 2009.

He stood there with his hand on the box, and behind him the office phone began to ring — not the direct line, the business line, at 4:44 in the morning, which no one had ever once called at that hour in eleven years — and Coral Yarrow, who knew every sound this town made in the dark, stood very still in the back room and understood that the alphabet had just changed on him.

He let it ring. He reached into the box.

* * *

The first thing his fingers found was not paper.

It was an envelope, placed on top of the 2009 files — placed, not dropped, squared to the box's edge with a precision that made the skin of his neck tighten, because it was *his* kind of precision. Nobody squared things to edges in Marrow's End except him.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, unmarked. No stamp. No return address. His name on the front in a hand so even it might have been traced — a hand that looked, Coral thought with a coldness spreading through his chest like the dawn coming up over the water, almost exactly like his own.

Behind him, the business line rang on, patient as the tide.

He opened the envelope.
