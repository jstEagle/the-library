## 9. The Second Voice

The software did the work in forty minutes. Mara spent four hours watching it, because she didn't trust anything that came that easy.

She had copied the archived 911 call onto a drive that had never touched the company network — a cheap gray stick she'd bought with cash at a pharmacy on Delancey, along with a pair of earbuds and a sandwich she didn't eat. Then she'd gone home to her apartment above the laundromat on Corbin Street, where the radiator knocked like a prisoner asking to be let out, and she'd run the file through a spectral analysis program she'd downloaded under a name that wasn't hers.

The call was ninety-one seconds long. The caller — the neighbor, Mrs. Okafor, dead now three years of a heart that simply quit — was hysterical in the way people are when they've found something they were never meant to see. Her voice climbed and broke and climbed again. *He's on the patio, oh God, he's on the patio, there's blood—*

And underneath her, low and level as a dial tone, there was the second voice.

Mara had isolated it on the third listen. It lived in the room's ambience, in the space between Mrs. Okafor's words, so quiet that five years ago no dispatcher — including her, because she hadn't been the one who took this call; she'd checked, twice, and hated how much relief she'd felt — had ever flagged it. But the program stripped away everything else, and what remained was a man speaking in a register just above a whisper, unhurried, almost bored:

*Fourteen hundred block, Wexler Terrace. Balcony fall. Male, forties. Ambulance required.*

Given before Mrs. Okafor said any of it. Given while she was still screaming about blood.

Mara sat back from the screen and let the apartment's silence press against her ears. The radiator knocked. Somewhere below, the laundromat's dryer thumped its slow arrhythmic heartbeat, a sound she'd slept beside for six years until it became indistinguishable from silence itself.

Someone had scripted the emergency. Someone had stood in that apartment — or close enough for their voice to carry into the phone's microphone — and fed the address to the caller like a prompter feeding lines to an actress who didn't know she was in a play.

She ran the comparison next. The program built a voiceprint from the second speaker: pitch contour, formant structure, the particular way he clipped his consonants, the faint upward slide at the end of declarative sentences that most people only used when asking questions. Then she fed it the reference library she'd been building for years without quite admitting what it was — dispatch audio, hundreds of hours of it, calls logged and timestamped and archived by a company that kept everything because keeping everything was cheaper than deciding what to throw away.

The match came back at 3:47 in the morning.

Eighty-two percent confidence. A taxi pickup request, logged at 11:52 p.m. on the night of Elias Voss's fall, placed from the payphone outside the Wexler Terrace building — a payphone, in this century, which meant someone who didn't want the call traced to a handset. The caller gave his location, requested a car to the airport, and then, while the automated system processed the fare, said one sentence to someone off-microphone:

*It's done. Same as we talked.*

The voiceprint overlapped. The cadence overlapped. The little upward slide at the end of *same as we talked* — turning a statement into something that wanted permission — overlapped.

Mara knew the name before she let herself look at it. She'd known it since the garage footage, since the man with the stretcher-shaped bundle turned his head toward the camera and gave her a face she'd seen a thousand times in a context that had nothing to do with Voss at all.

Tomas Reyes. Dispatcher, shift 4, six years ago. Fired for cause — attendance, they'd said, but everyone knew it was the drinking, and everyone knew why it was the drinking, though nobody had ever said that part out loud either. He'd trained half the current night shift, back when he still bothered to show up. He'd taught Deshawn the grid codes. He'd once bought Mara a coffee at 4 a.m. because she looked, he said, like a woman carrying something heavy, and heavy things got lighter if you put them down for ten minutes.

She played the two clips side by side until dawn. The rehearsed voice on the 911 call. The tired voice on the taxi line. The same throat. The same man.

*It's done. Same as we talked.*

Reyes hadn't witnessed the fall. Reyes had produced it.

---

She should have gone to him immediately. That was the discipline — move while the trail is warm, ask questions while the answers are still embarrassed to be found out. Instead she slept four hours on top of her blankets with her clothes on, woke at noon with the taste of copper in her mouth, and made herself do the thing she hated more than any other part of the work: nothing.

Because there was a variable she couldn't account for, and variables unaccounted for were how invisible people stopped being invisible.

At 6:14 p.m., twenty minutes into her shift, the notification came through on her phone. Not the dispatch system — her personal account, the one attached to the name she banked under, the one exactly two people in the world knew. The retainer account. Deposit received: $10,000. Reference line blank, as always. Sender obscured behind a holding company whose registered agent was a law firm whose managing partner had died in 2019, leaving a signature stamp that apparently still worked.

Punctual as a heartbeat. Every Friday, 6:14 p.m., for five weeks now. She'd set a timer against it once, out of curiosity, and the deposit had landed within ninety seconds of the chime.

Mara stared at the screen in the blue glow of the dispatch floor, surrounded by the soft electronic weather of the night shift — the murmur of units checking in, the hum of the map wall, Deshawn two desks over working a fender-bender on the viaduct with the patient competence of a man who had never once doubted that the world could be organized into grids and codes.

Five weeks. Five deposits. And in those five weeks, every single lead she'd pulled had been findable. Not easy — never easy — but findable. The archived tape had been misfiled in a way that put it exactly where a person looking for anomalies would look. The garage camera across the street belonged to a company whose retention policy had quietly changed eleven months ago, extending storage just long enough. Renata Voss had agreed to the insurance pretext with a smoothness that suggested she'd been briefed on the possibility of smooth-talking strangers. Even Brandt — even the detective's reluctant, off-the-record honesty — had come after Mara left a message at a number she'd found in a file that had been left open in a shared archive.

She had thought she was following a scent. Now, sitting very still in the amber light of the dispatch floor, she considered the possibility that she had been walking a corridor. That the doors had opened ahead of her not because she was good — though she was good, she knew precisely how good she was — but because someone had unlocked them. In order. On schedule. With the patience of a person laying track and watching the train run down it.

The money wasn't payment. The money was a leash made of increments. Ten thousand dollars, every Friday, arriving punctual as a pulse — not to buy her work, but to tell her, week after week, *you are on course.* A reward schedule. She'd read enough behaviorist literature during the nurse case to know the shape of it. Pigeons didn't need to understand the lever. They just needed the grain to keep coming.

And the cruelest part — the part that made her set the phone face-down on the desk and press her palms flat against her thighs — was that it was working. She had followed every door. She had walked every corridor. She had told herself at each step that she was choosing, and maybe some part of each step had been choice, but the architecture — the architecture was not hers.

Someone wanted Elias Voss investigated. Someone wanted the fall unpicked, thread by thread, by hands too careful to tear anything and too anonymous to be blamed. Someone had chosen her not despite her invisibility but because of it — because an invisible woman finding terrible things was a rumor, not a record. Because if it all went wrong, there would be no Mara Nakamura anywhere in the story. Just a series of coincidences that ended wherever her patron decided to stop paying.

Or worse. Just a series of coincidences that ended wherever her patron decided she'd become inconvenient.

"Mara."

Deshawn's voice, low, from beside her desk. She hadn't heard him cross the floor, which was unlike her, which was another data point in a night full of them.

"You've been staring at that phone like it owes you money." He nodded at it. "Everything okay?"

"Fine." The word came out automatic and she watched him hear it, watched him file it away with the small hurt of a man who'd covered her shifts and eaten her terrible birthday cake and never asked for anything except the truth about whether the chili in the break room fridge was hers.

"It's the Voss thing," he said. Not a question. "You're still pulling archives. I saw the request log."

"The request log is supposed to be confidential."

"So am I," Deshawn said. "That's kind of my whole job description lately." He lowered himself onto the edge of her desk, which he never did, which meant his knees were bothering him, which meant he'd been standing too long worrying about her instead of sitting down. "I'm not going to lecture you. I did that already and you looked at me like I'd kicked a dog. But I'm going to say one thing and then I'm going to go back to my fender-bender and pretend I didn't say it."

Mara waited.

"Whoever's paying you," he said quietly, "they're not paying you to find something. They're paying you to find it *for them*. There's a difference, and the difference is who ends up holding it."

He pushed off the desk and walked back to his station, and Mara sat with the sentence the way you sit with a diagnosis — knowing it was true, knowing it changed nothing, knowing that knowing and stopping were different verbs entirely.

---

At 2 a.m., with the city gone quiet in the way cities only go quiet between the bars closing and the bakers waking, she pulled up the file on Tomas Reyes one more time.

Address on Kessler Avenue, a walk-up above a nail salon. Last known employment: a private security firm that had dissolved amid a lawsuit she couldn't afford the database fees to read. Phone disconnected eighteen months ago. No utilities in his name. A man being erased in slow motion, or a man erasing himself — and after tonight she no longer trusted herself to know the difference between those two things, because both could be arranged, and both could be arranged by the same hand.

She thought about the coffee he'd bought her, six years ago. *Heavy things get lighter if you put them down for ten minutes.* She'd never told anyone that. It had seemed like a small kindness from a man on his way out, the kind of thing people say at 4 a.m. and mean completely and remember never.

Now it sounded like a man testing a lock. Checking whether the woman beside him noticed weight. Whether she carried things. Whether she might, someday, be useful.

Had he known even then? Had the plan been old enough, deep enough, that a fired drunk dispatcher buying a rookie a coffee was already a piece on a board she couldn't see? Or was she doing the thing she despised in bad novelists and conspiracy theorists alike — connecting dots that were only adjacent, mistaking pattern for plot?

She didn't know. That was the disorientation, and it was total: for three years she had been the person who knew, the quiet certainty moving through other people's chaos, and now she was the chaos, and somewhere out there was another quiet certainty watching her move through it with satisfaction.

The question had shifted under her and she felt it turn like a stair giving way. Not *who killed Elias Voss.* Not even *was Elias Voss killed.* But: *who wants the truth found, and why does finding it require me?*

Truth didn't need a dispatcher. Truth needed a lawyer, a journalist, a cop with a conscience. Truth needed someone with standing. What it didn't need was a woman with no institutional existence, no testimony worth taking, no protection worth offering — unless what the truth needed, specifically, was to be found by someone who could then be disowned.

A finder who could be discarded. An instrument with no handle that pointed back to anyone's hand.

Her phone chimed. 6:14 a.m. — no. She looked. It wasn't the retainer. It was a text from an unknown number, sent from a burner that would be dead by morning, and it contained no greeting and no signature, only a street address on Kessler Avenue and a single line:

*Apartment 3B. Come alone. Before they realize I stopped being useful.*

Mara read it three times. The phrasing was wrong somehow — too composed for a frightened man, too literary for a drunk — and she caught herself parsing it the way she parsed dispatch calls, listening for the second voice underneath, the prompter in the room.

But beneath the phrasing, beneath everything, one fact stood clean and cold: Tomas Reyes knew she was coming. Knew her name, her number, her habits. Which meant either the man in the garage footage had finally broken cover —

— or the hand on the leash had decided she was ready for the next door, and had opened it.

Mara pocketed the phone, clocked out, and stepped into the gray beginning of the morning, telling herself she was choosing to go.
