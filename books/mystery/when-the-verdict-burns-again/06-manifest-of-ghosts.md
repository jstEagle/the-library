## 6. Manifest of Ghosts

The harbormaster's office had been closed for eleven years, but the man who had kept its ledgers still lived four streets from the water, in a narrow yellow house with a garden gone to seed and a mailbox that leaned like a tired sailor.

Oscar knew his name the way she knew most names in Greywater — from listening. Halvard Nilsen. No relation to her father, though the town had always found it funny that two Halvards worked within fifty meters of each other for twenty years. The clerk and the fisherman. One kept records of what came into the harbor; the other kept records of what came out of the sea. Her father used to joke about it over Sunday coffee: *Between us, Oscar, we know everything that moves on this coast. Your mother just knows everything that moves in this house.*

She'd looked up Nilsen's address at the library, in the old municipal register, because asking around directly was no longer an option. Not after Eirik Lund's nurse had come back with that one sentence — *ask about the cargo manifest* — and not after Oscar had spent three nights turning the sentence over like a stone, looking for what lived underneath.

A manifest. A list. Paper.

Her father's death had been filed under weather. Weather needed no paperwork. But if a ship had been there that night — if something had come into Greywater harbor that wasn't supposed to be there until later — then somewhere, someone had written down a lie. And lies on paper have a smell. They go stale differently than truth does. She'd learned that twice already: once with the fall from the quarry crane that wasn't a slip, once with the kitchen fire in the Bergström house that wasn't grease. In both cases, the paper had told her what the people wouldn't.

So she stood at Nilsen's gate on a Tuesday morning, holding a bag of cinnamon buns from the bakery on Kransegate, rehearsing an opening line that didn't sound like an interrogation.

He answered the door before she knocked twice. A small man, seventy-something, with white hair combed with visible care and cardigan buttons done up one hole off. His eyes went to the buns first, then to her face, and something moved behind them — recognition, or its cousin.

"You're Iversen's girl," he said.

"Oscar."

"I know." He didn't open the door wider. He didn't close it either. He stood in the gap the way a man stands in a doorway when he has spent years deciding who to let in and has mostly decided on no one. "You have his chin. And his habit of standing like you're waiting for an answer to a question you haven't asked yet."

"That's a dispatcher thing," she said. "We wait a lot."

Something almost like a smile crossed his face and was gone. He looked past her shoulder, up the street, then down it. Empty. Gulls. A delivery van idling two houses down, which she noted without noting, the way she noted everything now.

"The bakery still does the ones with cardamom?" he asked.

"Wednesdays only."

"Then those are yesterday's."

"They were yesterday's best," Oscar said. "I'm told age improves them."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh in a younger throat, and stepped back from the door. "Come in before the gulls organize."

* * *

The house smelled of coffee, wool, and old ink — a smell that hit her somewhere below thought, because it was the smell of her father's boat cabin, of logbooks and thermoses and the particular dust of things kept carefully. Nilsen's living room was small and fiercely ordered. Shelves of binders. A wall clock that ticked too loudly, as clocks do in quiet houses.

He poured coffee without asking and sat across from her, hands wrapped around his cup like it was warm company.

"Eirik sent you," he said.

It wasn't a question, and she didn't pretend otherwise. "His nurse brought me a message. Ask about the cargo manifest."

Nilsen nodded slowly, as if confirming a diagnosis he'd suspected for years. "Eirik. Dying men get honest. It's the one advantage of the condition." He drank. Set the cup down with a small precise click. "You should understand something about me, Oscar Iversen. I was a clerk for thirty-one years. I wrote down what came in and what went out, and I wrote it true, because a harbor ledger is a promise the port makes to itself. Then one week in October, fifteen years ago, I was asked to stop making promises."

"By whom?"

"By the harbormaster, who was asked by someone above him, who was asked by someone above *him*. I never met the top of the ladder. That's how ladders work." His eyes came up to hers, steady and tired. "I did what I was told. I've regretted it in the way you regret a scar — not every day, but completely."

Oscar set down her cup. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside, something was running very fast. "Tell me about the ship."

"There was no ship," Nilsen said. "That's the point. Officially, the night your father drowned, nothing larger than a fishing trawler entered or left Greywater harbor. Quiet night. Fog coming in. Everyone home." He paused. "Except that I was on duty until midnight, and at nine-forty in the evening, a vessel came in dark — no lights, no radio call, no pilot request logged through normal channels. She came in on Eirik Lund's guidance alone. Eirik walked into my office at ten-fifteen, soaked to the knees, and told me to write down that the *Meridian Star* would arrive Thursday. Two days later. And that Wednesday night's page should show nothing at all."

The room got very small and very loud with ticking.

"*Meridian Star*," Oscar said. The name landed in her chest like a dropped anchor. "A cargo ship."

"A coastal freighter. Voss Shipping flag — though the registry was layered through two companies, which even then I thought was a coat worn backwards." Nilsen's voice had gone flat, recitation-flat, the voice of a man reading from a page he'd memorized against his will. "Thursday morning she docked in daylight, proper as church, took on pallets of processed timber, and left. Perfectly ordinary. Except she'd already been here. Except whatever she unloaded Wednesday night never appeared on any manifest, because Wednesday night officially never happened."

Oscar sat very still. This was the feeling she knew from her other cases — the moment the flat surface of the story tilted and showed its underside. The thrill of it was shameful and enormous, a heat in the ribs, and underneath the heat, grief, cold and patient, saying: *this is real, this is really real.*

"My father," she said. "October ninth. The *Solveig* went down between the outer markers. The report said squall, swamped cockpit, no distress call received."

"No distress call," Nilsen repeated softly. "On a night when the harbor was busy enough that I had to sit with the lights off so nobody would ask why the office was open."

The clock ticked. Somewhere outside, the delivery van's engine cut off.

"Why?" she asked. "Why would they need the ledger changed? If the ship just came in late—"

"Because ships don't come in dark, girl. Ships come in dark when what they carry can't be seen." He rubbed his thumb along the rim of his cup. "And because your father saw it. That's the part I pieced together after, too late, when it was safe to piece nothing together. Halvard fished the outer grounds. He was out there that evening — the fog came early, he said he'd run in slow. He must have come around the breakwater right as she was unloading. Lights off, cranes going quiet whenever anyone passed. He *saw*, and then two days later he was dead, and the sea was blamed, and I signed a clean page."

His hands had begun, very slightly, to shake. He steadied them against his knees.

"I told myself for years that changing a date was a small sin. Ink is cheap. But dates are load-bearing, Oscar. Move one date and the whole building leans. Everything that happened Wednesday night became homeless. No record, no witnesses, no crime. Just weather."

Oscar looked down at her coffee. When she spoke, her voice came out lower than she intended. "Did my father say anything? To you, to anyone?"

"He called the office the next morning. October tenth, eight-twenty. I remember because I'd just gotten in and the phone startled me." Nilsen's eyes had gone somewhere else entirely, fifteen years away. "He asked me, casual as anything, what ship had been working the outer anchorage the night before. I told him — stupidly, honestly — that nothing had been in. And he laughed. That laugh. You know it. He said, 'Well, then I dreamed a hundred-and-forty-foot freighter with her running lights taped over.' And he said he'd be by to look at the logbook when he got back from the banks."

He never got back from the banks.

Oscar heard the sentence finish itself in the silence Nilsen left, and her eyes stung, and she let them, because this man had earned the sight of it. Her father hadn't stumbled into something. He'd *asked*. He'd noticed, and he'd asked, and he'd planned to walk into an office and read a book — and someone had decided the book must stay clean, and the reader must go into the water.

"He was careful," she said. "He was the most careful man on that coast."

"Careful men die of carelessness all the time," Nilsen said. "Someone else provides the careless part."

* * *

She stayed another hour. He showed her the old ledger — the official one, the clean one, Wednesday's page blank as fresh snow, Thursday's entry neat and false — and told her where the archives kept duplicate manifests, customs copies, tide tables. He gave her names: a fuel dock worker, retired to Spain; a crane operator who'd taken sudden disability leave that winter and never explained it.

At the door, he stopped her with a hand light on her sleeve.

"One more thing. Free advice from an old clerk." His face was grave. "Paper doesn't forget, but people are paid to. The day after I signed that clean page, an envelope came to my house. Cash. Enough to make a winter easy. No note. Every year after, same week, same envelope. It stopped five years ago when the harbormaster retired — I think whoever runs the payments decided I was safely old." He looked at her steadily. "But the year it started, I understood exactly what I was: not a witness anymore. An expense. And expenses, Oscar, get audited. Be careful who sees you looking at the books."

"I'm a taxi dispatcher," she said. "Nobody looks at me. That's sort of my whole personality."

He didn't smile. "Your father probably said something similar about being a fisherman."

* * *

She walked back along the harbor road with the wind off the water pushing at her shoulders, and the anomaly hummed in her blood like a live wire. A ship that arrived two days late. A page wiped clean. A phone call her father made and never followed up. For fifteen years the case had been a shapeless grief; now it had edges, coordinates, a name painted on a hull. *Meridian Star.* She said it silently, testing it, the way you test ice with your weight.

She was so full of it — the thrill, the terrible bright thrill of the first real thread — that she almost missed the other thing.

Almost.

But noticing was her trade. Fifteen years of dispatch work teaches you to hear the note that doesn't belong in the chord. So when she reached the corner of Kransegate and glanced back out of pure habit — the dispatcher's tic, checking the board — she saw the gray sedan.

It had been parked near Nilsen's street when she arrived. She remembered it idly: a gray sedan, engine off, a man inside doing nothing, which is itself a kind of activity. Now it was half a block behind her, moving at walking pace, unhurried, keeping her in its mirror the way she kept drivers in her board.

She didn't turn her head again. She turned the next corner, took the shortcut through the fish market alley — narrow, cobbled, always crowded at noon — and came out on the waterfront road, where she stepped into the doorway of the chandlery and waited, pretending to study crab pots in the window.

Thirty seconds. Forty. The sedan rolled past the mouth of the alley, slowed — and kept going, smooth as water finding its level, disappearing toward the marina.

Not following. *Confirming.* Checking where she'd been, maybe. Or simply letting her know.

Oscar stood among the crab pots with her heart going hard and even, and felt the ground shift beneath the thrill. Last night she'd been a woman reading an old letter. This morning she'd been a clerk's visitor. Now she was a car on someone's board, a blip being tracked, and the invisible life she'd built so carefully — the night shifts, the quiet competence, the art of being overlooked — had developed a crack in it, and through the crack she could see a simple fact:

Somewhere in Greywater, someone kept a ledger too.

And she had just been written into it.
