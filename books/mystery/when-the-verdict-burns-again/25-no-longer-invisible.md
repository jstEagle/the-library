## 25. No Longer Invisible

The ashes were still warm when Oscar came back to Kransegate.

She hadn't meant to come back so soon. She had told herself she'd wait a day, maybe two — let the fire investigators do their careful work, let the smell of wet char and burnt insulation settle into the walls where it would live for years in whatever apartment they eventually rented to some other unlucky soul. But grief has its own dispatch schedule, and hers kept pinging her back to the curb across from her building at six in the morning, coffee going cold in her hand, staring up at the black rectangle where her kitchen window used to be.

The evidence wall was gone. Eleven days of work — tide logs, photocopies of photocopies, Signe's misdated logbook page, the timeline she'd built out of string and pins like something from a detective film — all of it reduced to a smear of ash on brick. Rune hadn't even needed to search for it. He'd known exactly which wall it was on. That was the part that sat in her stomach like swallowed ballast: not that they'd burned it, but that they'd known precisely where to burn.

Except.

She turned her coffee cup slowly in her hands, watching the steam curl off it in the cold morning air.

Except she'd been a dispatcher for nine years. Nine years of knowing that anything worth saying gets said twice, that any signal worth sending needs a backup frequency, that the first rule of the night shift is *assume the line will go dead*. She had photographed every inch of that wall two weeks ago, when the photographs still felt paranoid. And three nights ago — after Rune's visit, after the offer of money and reinstatement and silence delivered with that terrible reasonable smile — she had stood in Per's hotel room in Oslo with an envelope of duplicate prints and a roll of stamps, and mailed them to herself at the Greywater post office, general delivery, care of nobody.

They could burn her wall. They couldn't burn the mail.

Which meant the question wasn't whether she still had proof. The question was what proof was for, now.

* * *

She spent the morning doing something she was very bad at: sitting still.

Per's hotel room smelled of industrial carpet cleaner and someone else's cigarettes, and Per himself sat by the window with his ledger — *her* ledger now, though he still touched it like a man touching a stove he'd been burned on — and watched her pace.

"You're thinking about doing something loud," he said. It wasn't a question. Fifteen years of accounting had given him a nose for liabilities, and apparently she'd become one.

"I'm thinking about the newspaper," Oscar said.

Per went very still, the way men do when the number in front of them is worse than the one they'd braced for. "Maja Brandt."

"You know her?"

"Everyone knows Maja Brandt." He said it the way you'd say *everyone knows the tide comes in*. "She wrote a piece in ninety-eight about fish quota fraud. Voss Shipping pulled its advertising for eight months. The paper nearly died. She didn't retract. She just... waited. Ran smaller stories. Kept the big one folded in her desk drawer like a knife." He looked at his hands. "I met her once, at a municipal thing. She asked me three questions about harbor financing that I'm fairly sure were traps. I liked her enormously and lied to her twice."

Oscar stopped pacing. "You're saying she can't be bought."

"I'm saying buying her is more expensive than most people can afford, and Voss already tried, and what he got for his money was eight months of silence and a permanent enemy who files things." Per's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Also, she drinks her coffee like it owes her money and she will eat you alive if you come to her unprepared. So prepare."

He slid the ledger across the table toward her — the actual ledger, the paper original, the thing two people had already been hurt over. "Take it. Photographs are copies; copies get dismissed as forgeries. This is the thing itself. If you're going to stand in the light—" he paused, and something old and tired moved through his face, "—stand there holding something heavy."

* * *

The *Greywater Tidende* occupied a narrow brick building between a shuttered chandlery and a bakery that had changed hands four times since Oscar was a girl but had always smelled the same. The newsroom was on the second floor, up a staircase that announced every footstep like a witness taking the stand, and Oscar climbed it slowly, the ledger in a canvas bag against her hip, feeling each stair report her arrival to the whole building.

Good, she thought. Let it announce me. I'm done arriving quietly.

Maja Brandt was fifty-something and built like the coastline itself — weathered, unmoved, shaped by decades of things crashing against her. Her hair was iron-grey and cut like she'd done it herself with kitchen scissors and no regrets. She was standing at a composing table with a red pen in her hand, murdering someone's draft, and she looked up without surprise, which unsettled Oscar more than any amount of surprise would have.

"You're Halvard Iversen's daughter," Maja said.

It wasn't a guess. It was a filing system being consulted.

"Yes."

"And your flat burned down last night. Kransegate. The fire brigade log came through on the wire at 2:40 this morning." She set down the pen. "I've been waiting since 2:41 for you to walk up those stairs. Sit down before you fall down. You look like you slept in a hotel, which means you slept badly."

Oscar sat. There was a chair by the window with a view of the harbor, grey and glittering under a sky deciding whether to rain, and for a moment neither woman spoke. Somewhere below, the bakery's exhaust fan rattled on, and the smell of cardamom buns drifted up through the floorboards, absurdly domestic, as if the world were insisting on continuing.

"My father's drowning wasn't an accident," Oscar said. "Fifteen years ago Anders Voss had him killed because my father photographed an unmanifested cargo transfer off the *Meridian Star*, and the police investigation was steered, and last night someone burned my apartment to destroy the evidence I'd assembled proving it. I have the original ledger of the payments. I have a confession tape from the harbor pilot who helped stage it. I have a coast guard officer who signed the false arrival date. And I have spent five weeks learning that being invisible doesn't protect you — it just means you burn alone."

She said it all in one breath, and then made herself breathe again, and put the canvas bag on the desk between them.

Maja didn't reach for it immediately. That was the first thing Oscar noticed about how she worked: nothing was immediate. She poured coffee from a pot that had clearly been on the burner since the previous administration, handed Oscar a cup, took one herself, drank, grimaced, and drank again.

"Eirik Lund," Maja said. "The pilot. He's at the hospice in Brekke."

"You know."

"I know Eirik sent me a letter in March that said, quote, 'There's a story in Greywater older than your press and heavier than your presses.' No signature, no details. I assumed it was the usual — half the town thinks they've got a story, and usually the story is their neighbor's fence." She set her cup down. "Then a dispatcher starts pulling fifteen-year-old port records through a cousin of Signe Aas's clerk, and then a taxi follows another taxi home from the hospice road, and then a flat burns. I don't cover fires, Miss Iversen. But I do cover patterns."

"And the pattern?"

"The pattern," Maja said, "is that everything quiet in this town for fifteen years has suddenly started making noise, and noise always has a source." She finally reached for the bag and drew out the ledger, and Oscar watched her hands — steady, unhurried, a printer's hands — open it to the first page.

For a long time there was only the sound of pages turning, and the bakery fan, and the gulls outside arguing about the harbor.

"Per Iversen's handwriting," Maja said at last, quietly. "Your uncle. The accountant who left town." She looked up. "You know what this line item is? 'I.H. — resolution.' Do you understand what 'resolution' means in accounting language?"

"It means a debt settled."

"It means a problem solved," Maja said. "Your father was a line item. Initials and a figure." She closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin. "I have wanted this book for twenty years without knowing it existed. And I want you to understand what happens next, because I don't think you do yet."

"Tell me."

"Voss owns or advertises with or banks with nearly everyone who keeps this paper alive. When I print this, the advertisers pull. The bank calls the loan. Half my staff starts updating their CVs. My last issue might be the issue with your father's name on the front page." Maja said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd read a tide table. "And I'll tell you the other thing that happens. Half this town has known something was wrong for fifteen years. They've felt it the way you feel weather coming — in the joints, at night, no proof. Your father's funeral had four hundred people at it and not one of them believed the word 'accident,' and not one of them said so, because saying so cost something and nobody knew what. The moment I print this, the cost changes sides. Suddenly it costs something to stay silent instead."

Oscar felt something loosen in her chest — not relief, nothing so kind, but the specific release of putting down a weight you'd convinced yourself you could carry alone.

"That's why I'm here," she said. "I spent five weeks trying to do this quietly. Quiet got my job suspended and my home burned. I'm not asking you to be safe. I'm asking you to be loud enough that they can't burn us both without the whole town smelling the smoke."

Maja studied her for a long moment. Outside, the sky finally committed to rain, soft and grey against the window.

"You know what I keep in that drawer?" Maja said, nodding at the bottom drawer of her desk. "The quota fraud story from '98. Twenty-two pages. Sourced. Verified. Killed by economics, not by law." She pulled it out — a fat folder gone soft at the corners — and set it on top of the ledger. "I told myself I was keeping it for the right moment. The truth is I was keeping it because throwing it away felt like agreeing. Twenty-six years of agreeing, one folder at a time." She tapped the ledger. "This goes to press Thursday. Front page. Full spread. The ledger reproduced page by page, Eirik Lund named, the coast guard signature named, the fire on Kransegate named — *your* fire, in *your* paper, on the record, dated, so the next person who burns something knows the whole town is watching the smoke."

"And Voss?"

Maja smiled, and it was not a warm smile, and Oscar found she liked it better for that.

"Anders Voss has spent thirty years teaching this town that looking away is the polite thing to do," she said. "Thursday morning, every doorstep in Greywater is going to be impolite."

* * *

Oscar walked back along the harbor in the rain without hurrying.

That was new. For five weeks she had walked everywhere quickly, hood up, eyes down, a woman practicing disappearance. Now she walked the way her father used to walk the pier — slow, visible, letting the water see her face. A fisherman she half-recognized nodded to her from under the crane shed. Old Mrs. Tangen waved from the fish market awning. Small things. Ordinary things. But each one landed differently now, like signals received on a frequency she'd forgotten she was broadcasting on.

Her phone buzzed. Tomas.

*"Heard you visited the Tidende,"* he said, no greeting, dispatcher-style. *"Half the drivers know by lunch. You want company tonight?"*

She considered lying, out of habit. Then she considered the habit itself, and set it down.

"No," she said. "Tonight I want every driver in the fleet listening to the scanner. Tomorrow the whole town reads the paper. After that—" she watched the rain stitch silver lines across the grey water, over the spot where the *Meridian Star*'s secret had gone down with her father, "—after that we see what surfaces."

A pause on the line. Then Tomas, quietly: *"About time, sjef."*

She hung up and stood a while longer at the rail, rain running off her chin, no longer hiding from anyone.

Behind her, up the hill, the lights of the Voss estate burned steady above the harbor — and if she'd looked closely, she might have seen one light come on in a room that had been dark, and a silhouette at the window, watching the woman at the rail watch the water.

But Oscar wasn't looking at the hill anymore.

She was looking at the town.
