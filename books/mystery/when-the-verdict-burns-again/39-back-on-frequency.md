## 39. Back on Frequency

The dispatch office smelled the same as it always had — burnt coffee, cold radiator dust, and the faint electrical warmth of a radio that had been talking to Greywater since before Oscar was born. But when she pushed through the door on her first morning back, everything looked different anyway.

There was a plaque on her desk.

*Oscar Iversen — Senior Dispatcher, Night Operations.*

Someone had screwed it into the wood while she'd been gone. She stood there holding her thermos, staring at it, feeling like a woman who'd come home to find the furniture rearranged by a well-meaning ghost.

"They did it Tuesday," said Tomas from the doorway behind her, grinning around his unlit pipe. He never lit it anymore; he just liked having something to chew on during long fares. "Henriksen's idea. He tried to make it a surprise but he can't keep a secret to save his life. He told me, then told me not to tell you, then told the cleaning lady."

"Henriksen," Oscar said, "is a good man with the operational security of a colander."

"He also gave you the corner desk. The one with two windows." Tomas nodded toward it, and there it was — her old desk moved six feet east, where the morning light came in low over the harbor cranes and lay across the console like something offered rather than taken.

She set her thermos down next to the plaque and didn't say anything for a moment, because if she said anything she was going to have to admit that her eyes were doing something embarrassing, and Tomas would be kind about it, and somehow that would be worse.

"First shift back," Tomas said gently. "You want me to take it? Give you a day?"

"No." She sat down. The chair knew her. Fifteen years of night shifts had worn it into the exact shape of her spine, and when she settled into it, something in her chest unlocked that had been locked since the fire on Kransegate. "No, I want the shift. I've been off frequency too long."

Tomas chewed on his pipe a moment, studying her with the particular patience of a man who had driven her past surveillance vans and photographed a boathouse out his passenger window without asking why. "You look different," he said finally.

"I'm older."

"You were older last month too. It's something else." He tapped his temple. "You're listening different. I can see it already and you haven't even touched the radio."

He was right, though she wouldn't have known how to explain it if he'd asked. Before, the radio had been her instrument — a tool she played with quiet competence, the way a locksmith plays a lock. Now it was something else. Every voice that crackled through it was a whole life compressed into static and shorthand, and every one of those lives contained at least one thing somebody, somewhere, would very much prefer stayed forgotten. She had learned that the hard way, in a boathouse, with a recording device running and half the town's scanners tuned to a dead frequency.

The radio sounded the same. She was the one who'd changed channels.

* * *

Her first call came at 9:14 p.m., forty minutes into the shift, and it was Mrs. Halvorsen, who called every single night to report that the young men outside the fish-and-chip shop were "loitering with intent."

"Intent to do what, Mrs. Halvorsen?"

"Something. You mark my words. They've got that look."

"Which look is that tonight?"

A pause, the sound of curtains being drawn back with theatrical care. "Tonight they're laughing. Nobody laughs at eleven o'clock unless they're up to something."

"Mrs. Halvorsen, I'm going to send Unit Four past there in about ten minutes, and I'll tell him to drive slowly with his window down so you can hear whether the laughter has any criminal qualities."

"You're mocking me, Miss Iversen."

"A little," Oscar admitted. "But I am also sending the car."

Another pause, longer, and then, in a smaller voice: "It's good to have you back, dear. The other dispatcher doesn't listen properly. He says 'noted' like I'm a form to be filed."

Oscar smiled at the console. "I'll tell him you said so."

She logged the call, flagged it for a slow pass-by, and leaned back. Outside the window, the harbor lights burned steady across black water, and the Meridian Star's old berth sat empty — Voss Shipping's lease terminated three weeks ago, the town council voting nine to nothing after the newspaper ran Maja Brandt's final editorial under the headline *WE KNEW*. The paper had survived, barely, kept alive by subscriptions that tripled in a month and a printing fund that Signe organized with the ruthless efficiency of a widow who had spent fifteen years being polite about being bought.

Voss himself was awaiting retrial. The first trial had collapsed — bought lawyers, technicalities, a judge who recused himself only after the story made recusal cheaper than scandal. But the second one had a different prosecutor, a different climate, and Dahl testifying with a folder of altered autopsy notes that he had personally carried to the state attorney general's office, off the books, in defiance of everyone who'd ever signed his paycheck. Whatever happened in that courtroom, Greywater had already rendered its verdict. Voss's ships couldn't get insurance. His name got spat into gutters. And the house on the hill stood dark, its boathouse padlocked by court order, waiting like everything else in this case for the law to catch up to what the town already knew.

The radio crackled. Unit Seven, a fare dispute at the train station. Unit Two, requesting the all-clear on Fjordveien because of ice. Routine. Beautifully, blessedly routine.

At 11:40, Henriksen appeared in the doorway with two coffees and the sheepish expression of a man approaching a bear he'd personally decorated.

"Before you say anything," he began, "the promotion wasn't charity. The board wanted someone senior on nights who could handle the licensing review, and frankly your name carries weight now that—"

"Henriksen."

"—and also possibly a little charity," he conceded, setting the coffee down. He hovered. He was a big man, Henriksen, built like a breakwater, and hovering made him look like a breakwater attempting ballet. "How are you? Actually. People ask me and I don't know what to tell them."

Oscar considered the question honestly, which was more than she usually managed. How was she? Her apartment was new — third floor, different street, nothing in it that remembered smoke. Her father had a grave now with a proper stone, and a memorial service that half the town attended, and a final logbook line read aloud at the rebuilt pier while gulls screamed approval overhead. Eirik Lund had died in March, lucid at the end just long enough to hear that the tape had mattered, and to say, "Good. Now I can stop carrying it." Per had stayed in Greywater through the winter — her uncle, the accountant, the small gear who'd turned out to be the one gear that ground the machine to a halt — and they had coffee on Sundays now, awkwardly, carefully, two people learning to be family again out of shared wreckage.

"I'm fine," she said, and found it was mostly true. "I sleep badly. I check my locks twice. But I'm fine."

"That's the most honest answer anyone's given me all year." Henriksen nodded at the console. "Anything interesting tonight?"

"Mrs. Halvorsen reports loitering with intent. The intent appears to be laughter."

"God help us all." He started for the door, then stopped. "One thing. There's a box in the archive room with your name on it. Came in while you were... away. I didn't open it. Figured you'd want first look."

"What kind of box?"

"Records request fulfillment, officially. From the port authority." He scratched his jaw. "Unofficially, it's been sitting there three weeks because nobody wanted to be the one to hand it to you. It's tide logs, Oscar. Fifteen years' worth. Somebody at the port pulled them together and mailed them here instead of to your apartment."

After the fire, she thought. After they understood what she'd do with paper. Someone in that building had decided the records should survive, and had chosen the one address in Greywater that had proven itself fireproof by stubbornness alone.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me. I just work here." He paused at the door. "Although — for what it's worth — the whole office listened that night. The frequency. We all heard it. Bjørn still keeps the scanner on channel four sometimes, just in case anyone ever needs half the town to hear something again."

He left. Oscar looked at the coffee, at the plaque, at the harbor lights, and let herself feel it — the strange, unglamorous grace of coming back to a place that had decided, while she was gone, to make room for exactly who she'd turned out to be.

* * *

The box was waiting for her at 2 a.m., when the shift went quiet the way it always did — that dead hour between the last late fares and the first bleary airport runs, when the radio murmured and the town breathed.

She cut the tape and lifted the lid.

Fifteen years of tide logs, bound in gray cardboard folders, each one labeled in a clerk's careful hand. And tucked inside the top folder, an envelope with no return address, containing a single typed note:

*These are complete. The originals were not. Check 2009, October, pages 41–44 against the microfilm. Some entries were rewritten before binding. Whoever did it missed the film. — A friend of the pier.*

Oscar sat very still for a long moment.

Then she laughed — a short, disbelieving sound in the empty office — because of course. Of course there was another layer. This case was like the tide itself; every time you thought you'd reached the bottom, the water pulled back and showed you one more thing it had been keeping.

She found the microfilm reader in the archive room, dusty and temperamental, and threaded the October 2009 reel with hands that remembered how, from some other lifetime of late-night curiosity. The screen glowed green. Pages ticked past.

Pages 41 through 44.

The bound logbook — the official record, the one the box contained — showed a routine night. Moderate swell, normal tidal exchange, no anomalies. She'd seen these numbers before; they matched what the police had used to close her father's drowning as an accident. *Nothing unusual in the water that night,* the file said. *Weather consistent with a single-boat mishap.*

The microfilm disagreed.

On the film, page 42 recorded a swell reading nearly double the bound version, and a note in the margin — initialed, she was almost certain, by the same harbormaster's clerk who had once misdated a logbook for money and kept a photocopy out of spite — that read simply: *unusual traffic, outer channel, no manifest, reported to H.M.* Harbormaster. Reported to the harbormaster, who had been Signe's husband, who had died conveniently before he could ever be asked about it.

And on page 44, in the hour her father's boat went down, the film recorded what the bound book had erased entirely: *water disturbance reported off Kvalvik point, 23:50, possible vessel activity. No follow-up conducted.*

No follow-up conducted. Five flat words where a rescue should have been. Someone had logged the disturbance and then done nothing — or someone had ordered the log to forget that anything had been logged at all, and gotten sloppy between the ink and the film.

Oscar wrote the readings down in her notebook with a steady hand, though her heart was going like a taxi meter in rush hour. It changed things. Not the verdict — the town's verdict was already in, carved deep — but the legal one, the one Dahl was fighting for, the one that might actually put Anders Voss in a cell instead of merely in disgrace. Altered official records. A suppressed distress report. Proof that the harbor itself had registered her father's death as something other than weather, and had been made to un-register it.

She copied the pages, photographed the film, locked the notebook in the drawer where Eirik's letter lived — her drawer of things that refused to stay buried — and sat back down at the console just as the radio woke up.

"Dispatch, Unit Six. Got a pickup at the hospital, elderly gentleman, heading out to Kvalvik. Says he wants to see the point at night. You believe that? Ninety years old and wanting to stand on a headland at three in the morning."

"Unit Six, dispatch. Take him. Drive slow. Some people are keeping appointments fifteen years late."

A chuckle on the line. "Copy that, dispatch. Good to have you back, by the way. Sounds right again, the frequency. Like it grew its ears back."

Oscar keyed the mic. "Dispatch copies. Drive safe."

She settled into her chair, into the light of the two windows, into the shape the years had worn. Down in the harbor the tide was turning, sliding out clean over the sand where her father's boat had finally come home. Somewhere across town, Dahl was probably awake too, building his retrial brick by brick. Somewhere a courtroom waited. Somewhere Maja Brandt was already sniffing around the port authority, because editors could smell a document the way sharks smelled blood.

And somewhere in the green glow of a microfilm reel, five words sat waiting to be spoken aloud in front of the whole country: *no follow-up conducted.*

Oscar poured the last of Henriksen's coffee, opened the log for the 3 a.m. entries, and began to write.

The radio murmured. The town slept. And the watcher was back on watch — listening differently now, hearing in every voice the story underneath the story, patient as water, certain as the tide, that nothing buried in Greywater was going to stay buried on her frequency.

Not ever again.
