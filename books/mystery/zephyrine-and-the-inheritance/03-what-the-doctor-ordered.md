## 3. What the Doctor Ordered

The death certificate arrived at the depot by accident, the way most truths do.

It came folded inside a manila envelope of estate paperwork that should have gone to the solicitor's office on Marrow Street—misdelivered, Casper assumed, until he saw his own name penciled in the corner in a hand he almost recognized. Almost. That was the trouble with Dr. Edmund Hale's handwriting. It was the kind doctors cultivated for a lifetime: elegant enough to flatter, illegible enough to hide in.

Casper stood under the depot's buzzing fluorescent tube at two in the morning and unfolded the photocopy with hands that had stopped pretending to be steady days ago. Zephyrine Ashcombe. Cause of death: cardiac arrhythmia secondary to congestive failure. Certified by Edmund Hale, M.D., attending physician of twenty-two years.

He set it beside the confession letter, which he now kept in a fireproof box beneath the dispatch counter like a relic, and compared dates. Then he pulled the taxi logs—the real ones, the duplicates he'd made after finding his originals disturbed—and turned to October, seven years back.

There it was again. The fare from St. Brendan's Hospital to the Ashcombe estate, logged at 11:40 p.m., the night Odile Ashcombe officially died. Passenger listed as *O.A.* And there was the hospital record Vera had slipped him without meeting his eyes: *Odile Ashcombe, admitted, discharged to family care*—signed by the same elegant, evasive hand.

Hale hadn't just certified Zephyrine's death. He'd certified her sister's too—a woman who had been breathing the whole time, riding Casper's cab once a month in a gray coat and a scarf drawn high, paying cash, never speaking above a whisper.

Casper sat down slowly on the rolling stool and let it settle over him. The doctor had killed her. Or helped kill her. Either way, he had signed his name to a lie twice, and lies that old require maintenance—they require money, silence, and the removal of loose ends. The letter writer, poor drowned Mr. Tibbet, had been a loose end. Casper understood, with the clarity of a man watching water rise around his ankles, that he was one now too.

He spent what remained of the night doing the thing he did best. Not driving. Not running. Reading.

The estate's finances were not exactly public, but Hale's signature was everywhere once you knew to look: invoices copied into Zephyrine's own files, which she had apparently kept in triplicate because she trusted no one, least of all her doctor. Roof repairs billed four times. A "security retainer" for a house with no locks that worked. Charitable disbursements to a foundation whose registered address, Casper discovered with a cold thrill, was a post office box forty feet from Hale's clinic.

Seven years of bleeding. Quiet, methodical, patient. The kind of theft only an executor could commit, because only an executor is trusted to guard the wound.

*Friday,* Casper thought. The will reading. Two days away. If Odile Ashcombe surfaced before then, everything Hale had built would collapse—but if Hale found her first, she would die properly this time, and the paper trail would die with her. And Casper Whitlock, night dispatcher, keeper of ten years of logs, would follow Mr. Tibbet into the reservoir.

He looked at the phone on the wall. He thought about calling Vera. He thought about how she had warned him—*stop digging, Casper*—and how her voice had cracked on *digging*, and how he still didn't know whether the crack was fear for him or fear of being dug up herself.

He didn't call anyone.

* * *

In the carriage house behind the Ashcombe mansion, Odile Ashcombe counted her heartbeats the way other people counted money.

She had learned the trick in the first year—when the silence of the place pressed so hard against her ears that she thought she might go mad, when she'd caught herself talking to the moths and naming them. Count your heartbeats. Sixty to the minute if you're calm. Ninety if something is wrong.

Something was wrong. She was at one hundred and ten.

Headlights had swept the tree-lined drive twenty minutes ago and stopped—not at the mansion, where the solicitor's clerks came and went preparing Friday's reading, but farther down, near the carriage house itself, idling. Then dark. Then footsteps on gravel, unhurried, a man walking who had every right to walk.

Through the gap in the boarded window she watched him pass under the moon: tall, silver at the temples, carrying a doctor's bag. Edmund Hale. Her sister's physician. The man who had held her hand in the hospital seven years ago and said, gently, *Your sister has arranged everything, Odile. You'll be looked after.*

She had believed him then. She had been half-dead and grieving their mother, and Zephyrine's plan had seemed like mercy—disappear, let the town bury you, let the inheritance pass untainted through the years until it could be given wholly to the shelter Zephie dreamed of. Zephyrine had wept over it. *They'll take less of the fortune if they think you're dead, darling. Grief makes people honest about money.*

But grief, Odile had learned, makes people honest about everything else too.

Hale tried the carriage house door. It was locked—it always was; she kept it locked with a bolt Zephyrine had installed herself, iron, older than either of them. The handle turned, stopped, turned again. A long pause. Then, softly, in a voice meant to be heard through wood:

"Odile. It's time to come home."

Her heartbeat went to one hundred and thirty.

"I know Zephie wrote to someone," Hale continued, conversational, terrible. "A letter. Postmarked the day after the funeral. Letters are such dangerous things, aren't they? Paper outlives us all." A pause. "The man who mailed it is dead, Odile. Did you know that? Drowned. These reservoirs are treacherous in autumn."

She pressed her back against the far wall, among Zephyrine's covered furniture, her sister's ghost in every dust-sheeted shape. Seven years of hiding had taught her body things her mind refused: breathe shallow, stand off the floorboards' singing spots, wait.

"Friday," Hale said. "After the reading, none of this matters anymore. But before it—" the door shuddered under a shoulder, once, testing— "I do need to tidy up."

Then headlights again, sweeping the drive, and a second engine, and Hale's footsteps retreated with the unhurried grace of a man who had all the time in the world and intended to use it.

Odile slid down the wall onto the floor and counted her heartbeats until they slowed. Then she began, for the first time in seven years, to plan her exit.

* * *

Casper knew the depot had been broken into before he got the door open.

The lock was intact—that was the professional touch—but the frame was splintered low, where a pry bar does its quiet work, and the interior smelled wrong: cold air, wet gravel, and beneath it, faintly, antiseptic. The fluorescent tube over the dispatch counter flickered its dying stutter, and in that strobing light Casper saw his life laid open.

Every drawer pulled. Every file fanned across the floor like shed feathers. The fireproof box stood open and empty, and the confession letter was gone.

But the logs—his duplicates, hidden in the false bottom of the fare cabinet where he'd moved them three nights ago—were gone too. All ten years. A decade of nights, of names, of *O.A.* entries in his careful dispatcher's hand. Hale had read him like a ledger. Had probably been reading him for days.

Casper stood very still in the wreckage and waited for the fear to come, the animal fear that says *hide, disappear, become invisible again*. It came. He let it. He examined it the way he examined a strange fare—where from, where to, what was really being carried.

And what he found underneath the fear was not cowardice. It was fury.

They had taken the letter. They had taken the logs. They had taken every scrap of paper that proved anything. But paper had never been the proof—had it? He was the proof. Ten years of nights lived wide awake while the town slept. He knew things no document recorded: the smell of the passenger in seat two, the way Dr. Hale's sedan idled outside St. Brendan's at odd hours, the route nobody rode and everybody forgot.

Vera had told him to stop digging. Well. The digging had come to him now, kicked in his door and emptied his drawers. There was no more staying invisible. There was only forward.

Casper righted his stool, swept the glass from the dispatch counter, and sat down in the flickering light with a fresh sheet of paper. From memory—his memory, the one vault they couldn't ransack—he began to write: dates, plate numbers, fares, times. Everything. His whole decade poured out in ink.

When he finished, dawn was graying the windows. He folded the pages into his coat, next to his chest, where they rose and fell with his breathing.

Then he picked up the depot phone and dialed the number he'd promised himself he wouldn't dial. Vera answered on the third ring, her voice sleep-roughened and wary.

"It's me," Casper said. "He took the logs, Vera. Which means Friday isn't about the will anymore." He looked out at the pale morning, at the road leading east toward the Ashcombe estate. "It's about who's still standing when it's read. I'm going to the house tonight—and I think I can bring him the one witness he can't afford."

On the line, Vera went very quiet. When she spoke, her voice trembled with something that wasn't warning anymore.

"Casper," she said, "you need to know what my aunt made me promise. Before you walk into that house—you need to hear it from me first."
