## 5. Lease Terms and Other Expirations

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which Marisol Vega would later decide was the universe's idea of a joke, because Tuesdays were her worst baking day—her sourdough always sulked after the weekend rush, the starter going moody and slow like a teenager asked to do chores—and she was elbow-deep in flour when Mr. Abernathy's knuckles rapped against the shared wall between their two kitchens.

Not the door. The wall.

That was how you could tell it was serious. In three years of renting the two halves of the old Whitlock building—the narrow brick storefront on Cordelia Street that housed Ivo's bakery on the east side and hers on the west, divided by a plaster wall that had never once been soundproof—they had developed an entire language of knocks. Three quick taps meant *I've got extra egg yolks, want them?* Two slow ones meant *your oven is making that noise again.* A single tap meant *good morning,* delivered at 4:15 sharp every day without fail, so reliable that Marisol had once set her alarm by it during the week Ivo went to visit a cousin in another town and overslept for the first time in years.

But knuckles dragged down the plaster, long and deliberate, was new. That was the knock of a man who wanted you to know he'd rather be anywhere else.

Marisol wiped her hands on her apron—a gesture that accomplished nothing, since her apron was already more flour than cloth—and pushed through the connecting door into Ivo's kitchen, where she found Mr. Abernathy standing between the proofing racks with his hat literally in his hands, turning it by the brim like a man winding up to apologize for something he hadn't done yet.

He was seventy-some years old, their landlord, with a mustache that predated the building and opinions about gutters that he shared freely and often. He had never once come inside before eight o'clock. It was not yet six.

"Oh no," Marisol said. "Who died?"

"Nobody." He cleared his throat. "Well. Nobody yet."

"That's not comforting, Mr. A."

Ivo stood by the worktable with a bowl cradled against one hip, wooden spoon paused mid-stir. In the lamplight they looked, as always, composed and slightly elsewhere—a tall, quiet presence in rolled sleeves, dark hair escaping its knot, watching the world from behind eyes that catalogued everything and surrendered nothing. But Marisol had spent three years reading this person across a shared wall, and she saw the small things: the way their shoulders had already drawn up half an inch, the way the spoon stopped turning.

"What is it?" Ivo asked.

Abernathy sighed the sigh of a man who had rehearsed this in his truck for twenty minutes and was now discovering the rehearsal had been useless. "Building's sold," he said. "Closed last week. New owners are—" he turned the hat again, "—they're a development group out of the city. They're not evicting anybody. They want to be clear about that. They're very proud of not evicting anybody."

"But," Ivo said.

"But," Abernathy agreed, "they're restructuring the leases. Both units reapply as of next month. And here's the part I hated writing down—" he produced a folded paper from his jacket, smoothed it on the worktable with great ceremony, as though presenting a treaty, "—they're only offering *one* commercial lease for the building. One tenant. They figure a bakery's a bakery."

Marisol stared at him. Then at Ivo. Then back at him.

"They think we're competitors," she said. "We share a wall. Ivo's sourdough has made my croissants better out of pure spite. We're not competitors, we're—"

"A single business entity, if you want the lease," Abernathy said. "Joint application or nothing. Their words, not mine, God help me." He put his hat back on, which seemed to signal the formal portion of the morning had concluded. "You've got until the end of the festival week. That's when the current arrangement expires. I argued for longer. They don't listen to me anymore; apparently I'm 'legacy management.'" He said the phrase the way other men said *tax audit.*

At the door he paused, and his voice softened into something almost paternal. "For what it's worth, I told them they'd be idiots to lose either of you. Told them this building's been two bakeries for forty years and nobody ever complained about the smell." He tipped the hat. "Figure it out. You're both smarter than the paperwork."

Then he was gone, and the door clicked shut, and Marisol became aware of two things simultaneously: the tick of Ivo's cooling oven, and the fact that Ivo had gone very still—not the stillness of thinking, but the stillness of someone listening to a sound only they could hear.

* * *

Here was what Marisol knew about Ivo Okonkwo, compiled over three years of flour trades and shared walls and Sunday mornings when the ovens ran hot and there was nothing to do but wait:

She knew they took their coffee black and their bread seriously and their conversations carefully, always circling the personal, never landing. She knew their handwriting slanted left, that they hummed—no, didn't hum, never hummed—that they moved through the world like someone walking on a floor they suspected might give way. She knew they kept their past sealed up tighter than the good vanilla, and that whatever was in there, it had teeth. She knew the flinch. The customer who'd hummed a cello line, four months back, some half-remembered concerto drifting from the radio in his truck—and the way Ivo's whole body had gone rigid for exactly two seconds before resuming, smooth as poured cream, as if nothing happened.

Marisol noticed things. It was her professional condition. You couldn't laminate croissants without noticing everything—temperature, humidity, the mood of the butter, the patience of the dough. She noticed that Ivo measured joy out in teaspoons, that kindness came off them in waves they seemed unaware of and slightly embarrassed by. She noticed that when she laughed too loud, Ivo's mouth did this thing—not quite a smile, more like a smile remembered from instructions.

And she had noticed, over the past several weeks, that Ivo had become *distracted*. Distracted in a specific way: checking the mail twice a day instead of once, going quiet mid-sentence, standing in the alley behind the building some evenings just looking north, toward the orchard road, with an expression Marisol couldn't name. Something had arrived—or was arriving—in Ivo's life, and it was big, and they weren't sharing it.

She'd told herself it wasn't her business. She'd told herself this approximately nine hundred times, with decreasing conviction.

Now she watched Ivo set down the bowl with exaggerated care, the way people handle objects when they don't trust their hands.

"Well," Marisol said, aiming for breezy and landing somewhere near breathless. "Partnership, huh. That's one option."

"It's one option," Ivo said carefully.

"There are others."

"Are there?"

"I could move. There's that little space by the hardware store—no, wait, that's a vape shop now. The old diner on Route 9?" She was talking too fast and knew it. "It smells like fifty years of fryer grease, but I could—"

"You'd hate Route 9," Ivo said quietly. "No foot traffic. No river. No—" They stopped.

"No what?"

Ivo looked at the floor, which was answer enough, and Marisol felt something turn over in her chest like dough being folded—resistance giving way to structure.

"Okay," she said, and pulled out a stool, and sat down at the worktable like a woman settling in for negotiations, because she was one. "Let's talk numbers."

* * *

They talked numbers for two hours while the morning proofs rose and fell and rose again, and it was, Marisol thought, the strangest and best conversation they'd ever had, because it let them say true things sideways.

"I do the books for my unit," Ivo said. "Yours are a disaster. I've seen them. They're written on receipt paper."

"My books have *personality.*"

"Your books have a coffee ring obscuring Q3."

"And your books," Marisol shot back, leaning across the table, "have no soul whatsoever, and if we're doing this, we're getting a real ledger, a nice one, with a ribbon bookmark, because I refuse to run a business that looks like an insurance office."

Ivo blinked. "If we're doing this."

"If." She let it sit there between them, honest as salt.

Because that was the thing—she wasn't sure. Not about the business. About Ivo. She watched them across the table, this person who had become the fixed point of her mornings, and she thought: *a partnership means knowing someone all the way down.* Shared accounts, shared failures, shared three-a.m.-the-freezer-died emergencies. It meant Ivo Okonkwo's name next to hers on a document, which meant Ivo Okonkwo actually existing in her life in a documented, permanent, auditable way, instead of the lovely adjacent way they existed now.

She wanted it so badly it embarrassed her.

"I have conditions," she announced.

"Of course you do."

"One: the bakery gets renamed eventually. Something with both our names, or neither. Two: decision-making is split fifty-fifty, even when you're right, which will be often, and I'll resent it gracefully. Three—" and here she leaned forward and held their gaze, because this one mattered, "—no secrets that affect the business. If something's happening in your life, something big, something with a deadline, I get to know. That's not nosiness, Ivo. That's just what partnership *is.*"

Something flickered across Ivo's face—there and gone, fast, like a fish under ice.

"Reasonable," they said. And then, softer, almost to themselves: "More reasonable than I deserve."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you agree with your own indictment before anyone else can file it." She reached across and tapped the table between them. "I'm asking you to sign your name next to mine. That's not a punishment."

Ivo was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the first delivery trucks grumbled past on Cordelia Street; the light through the front window had gone from blue to gold. When they finally spoke, their voice was careful, each word placed like a stone in a stream.

"When I was young, someone told me who I was supposed to be. Very precisely. Down to the repertoire, the posture, the—" they stopped, recalibrated. "I spent a long time being that person. Then I stopped. And the cottage, the bakery, this town—it's the first place I've ever chosen for myself. Small. Quiet. Known only to me." Their eyes came up. "A partnership means being known. Fully. If I sign that paper and you learn all of me, and any part of it changes how you look at me—"

"Hey." Marisol's voice came out lower than she intended. "I've been smelling your failures for three years, Ivo. Every flat loaf, every burnt batch. I've eaten them. On purpose. And I still cross the wall every morning to see what you're making next." She shrugged, suddenly shy. "That's the whole deal. That's all partnership is. You keep showing up and letting somebody see the loaves."

Ivo laughed—a short, startled, genuine laugh that Marisol counted among her private treasures—and for one shining moment she thought they might say yes. Might reach across the table. Might tell her whatever it was, the thing with the deadline, the thing they kept looking north toward.

Instead Ivo looked at their left hand, resting on the table, and Marisol saw the faintest tremor move through it, quickly stilled, and heard them say:

"I need time. A few days. There's something I have to—to sort out first. Something I should have told you about already." A breath. "I'll tell you. Soon. I promise."

The word *soon* hung in the warm air between the proofing racks, unweighted by any date, and Marisol, who could read dough by smell and people by silence, understood that *soon* was a promise made of fog.

"Okay," she said. "Soon. But Ivo—the lease deadline isn't soft. Festival week. Same week as whatever it is you're not telling me about, judging by how you flinched just now." She tried to make it light. It came out truer than light. "Our clocks are running down together. Whatever you're sorting, sort it fast."

* * *

After Ivo retreated to the east kitchen—after the connecting door clicked shut with a gentleness that somehow sounded like an apology—Marisol stood alone in her own kitchen, surrounded by the ordinary miracles of her morning: the starter breathing under its cloth, the butter blocks chilling, the day assembling itself the way it always did.

She picked up a bench scraper and began portioning dough, and her mind refused to cooperate, circling back and back.

*A few days.* What took a few days? What arrived in envelopes? She thought of Ivo checking the mail twice daily. Of the evenings staring up the orchard road. Of the flinch at the cello line, and—newly remembered, newly lit—the way Ivo had once, drunk on exactly one glass of cider at the harvest fair, hummed four notes of something before catching themself like a thief in the act, and gone home early.

Four notes. Low strings. Marisol wasn't musical—she'd washed out of piano at age nine, defeated by middle C—but she knew a cello's voice when she heard it, the way you knew a neighbor's dog.

She looked down and realized she'd portioned the same piece of dough twice.

"Get it together, Vega," she muttered.

But her heart was doing something inconvenient—pounding not with worry but with a fierce, unreasonable hope, because underneath the confusion, one thing had crystallized with total clarity during those two hours at the worktable: she wanted this. Not just the building. The whole improbable architecture of it—Ivo's precision beside her chaos, the shared ledger with the ribbon bookmark, the two names on the line. She wanted to be the person Ivo trusted with the whole story, whatever it was. And she was tired, bone-tired, of loving someone in teaspoons.

The festival was five weeks away. The lease expired that same week. Two clocks, ticking in the same tower, and somewhere in Ivo's sealed rooms, a third clock she couldn't see yet.

She tied her apron tighter and got back to work, and if her croissants came out that morning with a little more fury than usual—well, fury laminates beautifully.

Across the wall, faintly, she heard the sound of water running, then stopping. Then nothing. Then, very softly, a sound she'd never heard in three years of shared mornings:

Ivo Okonkwo, humming. Four notes, low and trembling, cut off almost before they began—as if the person humming had frightened themselves.

Marisol set down her scraper, pressed her palm flat against the cool plaster of the dividing wall, and whispered to no one at all:

"Soon, huh. Okay. But I'm counting."
