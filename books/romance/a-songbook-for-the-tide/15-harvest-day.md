## 15. Harvest Day

Edmund woke before the gulls did, which on Saltmere Island was saying something, because the gulls here had no respect for anyone's schedule and began their arguments at first light.

He lay in the narrow bed of the orchard cottage — *his* cottage now, or half his, or a third his if you counted the way Mrs. Okonkwo had annexed the kitchen for festival prep — and listened to the wind check the window latches like an anxious parent. No rain. The storm two weeks past had taken a bite out of the sea wall and most of everyone's sleep, but today the sky over the channel was the pale, rinsed blue of a bowl wiped clean.

Today was Harvest Day.

He got up, dressed in the dark, and went down to the kitchen he'd spent three weeks learning. It was not his kitchen at Iversen's on the mainland — that one had marble counters and a deck oven that cost more than a car and a precise, humming order to it that Edmund had built brick by brick over eleven years. This kitchen had a flagstone floor that held the cold like a grudge, an Aga with moods, and a door that swelled shut every time the humidity turned unless you lifted the handle and pushed with your hip in exactly the right way.

He loved it. That was the alarming part. He loved it the way you love a person who is difficult on purpose, who makes you earn every small kindness.

The dough had been proofing overnight under cloth: brioche for the morning trays, a slow cold ferment that smelled faintly of butter and patience. He uncovered it, pressed a knuckle into the surface, and watched the dimple spring back slowly. Ready. He'd learned the island's rhythm too — everything here ran a quarter-beat behind the mainland, tides and tempers both, and if you fought it you lost, and if you waited you were rewarded.

*The heart is trainable muscle.* His grandmother's creed, drilled into him across a childhood of 5 a.m. lessons: fold, rest, fold again. Discipline as devotion. He had built a whole life on it, and it had kept him upright through a failed engagement, a failing café, and a bank letter he still hadn't opened, though he knew its contents the way you know weather coming through a door left ajar.

But lately the creed had developed exceptions. Wren Calloway, for instance, laughing at him with flour on her nose. The ferryman's kids calling him *Chef* with their mouths full. The whole ridiculous, stubborn island that had adopted him suspiciously and then completely, the way a cat adopts a household — on its own terms, without apology.

He worked. Brioche first, then the apple galettes made with Bramble Orchard fruit — the trees they'd cleared together, Wren hauling branches while narrating the orchard's history like a documentary presenter who swore when she stubbed her toe. Then the cardamom knots, because Mrs. Okonkwo had tried one at the storm-work party, gone silent for a full ten seconds, and then said, "Boy, you put your whole grief in this," which Edmund had decided was the finest review of his career.

By seven the barn smelled like forgiveness felt.

* * *

They'd set the tables out on the orchard's west slope, where the ground flattened and the view opened onto the channel. Borrowed tables — every table on Saltmere, apparently, including one that was clearly a door laid across two sawhorses, and another that Mrs. Okonkwo introduced as "the church hall's good table, don't tell Father Byrne." Mismatched chairs. Jam jars of late asters and rosehips. Bunting that the ferryman's kids had sewn from old sails, so that the whole orchard fluttered faintly of salt and tar.

Wren had been up since five too, running the logistics with a clipboard and the focused ferocity she usually reserved for intertidal survey data. She wore a dress — an actual dress, navy, with tiny crabs printed on it that Edmund pretended not to find charming — and wellingtons, because she was still Wren.

"Tables are set," she reported, arriving at the barn as he carried out the first tray. "Okonkwo's jam stall is positioned for maximum foot traffic and minimum competition. Dev's doing cider. The kids have organized a crab race, which I have agreed to sanction despite it being chaos."

"Sanctioned chaos."

"The only kind worth having." She took the tray from him, and their hands overlapped on the edge of it for a moment longer than the task required. Neither of them mentioned the night of the storm, or the almost that lived between them now like a splinter neither would dig out. "Brioche smells illegal."

"It's legal in forty countries."

"Not this one. This one it should require a permit." She set the tray down on the trestle he'd built from orchard apple wood, straightened, and looked at the slope — the tables, the bunting, the gray-green sea beyond — and something crossed her face that she smoothed away quickly. "My grandfather used to talk about harvest days here. I thought he was romanticizing. Old men do that."

"And?"

"And he undersold it." She said it briskly, the way she said all emotional things, and went off to yell at someone about cider placement.

* * *

The ferry brought people at ten.

Not the last ferry — that was still weeks off, a fact Edmund found himself checking against like a man counting down to a verdict. But the weekend boat came heavy, deck crowded with mainlanders drawn by the posters Wren had papered the harbor with: HARVEST FESTIVAL, BRAMBLE ORCHARD, ALL WELCOME, BRING AN APPETITE AND YOUR OWN FORK IF YOU'RE FUSSY.

They came up the hill in a loose stream, and Edmund baked and sold and baked and sold until his hands moved on their own training and his mouth ran on its own goodwill. The cardamom knots went first. Then the galettes. A woman from the mainland bought four brioche and ate one standing up, eyes closed, and pronounced it "a religious experience," and Edmund said "the yeast does most of the work," which was a lie, and everyone knew it was a lie, and it was fine.

Mrs. Okonkwo's jam stall did roaring trade, and she cross-sold his pastries with the shameless fluency of a woman who had run market stalls since before he was born. "Jam on the galette, love, trust me. Chef, give the woman the one with the extra glaze, she's had a week." How she knew everyone's weeks was a mystery Edmund had stopped trying to solve.

Dev's cider went fast. The crab races descended into scandal when one entrant, a large crustacean named Admiral, was discovered to be missing a claw and thus racing with what the ferryman's son called "a handicap, which means he tries harder," and Admiral won anyway, to enormous cheering.

And through all of it, Wren.

She moved through the afternoon like the tide itself — everywhere, necessary, unglamorous about it. She untangled the bunting from a child's hair. She talked a nervous mainland couple through the orchard's history, gesturing at the rows with a pride she didn't seem to notice she was carrying. She stood beside Edmund at the trestle during the rush, working the till badly and charmingly, making change wrong in his favor twice and insisting on correcting it while a queue of people smiled at them both with expressions Edmund chose not to interpret.

"You're staring," she said, mid-transaction.

"I'm assessing queue flow."

"You're staring at me and doing math about it."

"Queue math," he said, and handed a galette across the table, and she rolled her eyes, and something in his chest did a thing he declined to name.

* * *

By three o'clock, the trestle was bare.

Not nearly-bare. Bare. Crumbs and parchment and the smell of butter, and Edmund standing behind it with flour to his elbows and nothing left to sell, staring at the empty trays with the mild shock of a man whose forecast has been demolished by reality.

"That's it?" said the ferryman's daughter, peering into a tray as if more pastry might be hiding in the corners. "All gone?"

"All gone."

"But I saved my pocket money *specifically*—"

"There's jam," offered Mrs. Okonkwo, without conviction.

The girl looked at the jam the way children look at vegetables, and Edmund crouched to her level. "Tell you what. Next bake day, you come help. You can be my apprentice. First rule of apprenticeship: the chef eats last, but the chef's apprentice eats second-to-last."

Her whole face reorganized itself around this information. "Second-to-last?"

"Very prestigious."

"Okay," she said gravely, and shook his hand with a fisherman's grip she'd clearly learned from her father, and ran off to report her promotion.

Wren appeared at his elbow holding two cups of Dev's cider. "Sold out."

"Sold out."

"Do you understand what that means? Do you want me to do the numbers? Because I've been doing the numbers all afternoon, quietly, in my head, like a normal person—"

"You did *survey data* in your head at a festival."

"I multitask." She pushed a cup into his hand. "It means, Edmund, that if you did this every weekend — even just weekends, just the season — the margin isn't trivial. It's not café-saving money alone, but it's not nothing. It's a floor. Something to stand on while we figure out the rest."

*We*. She said it easily, the way she said all the words that undid him.

"We," he repeated, testing it.

"Don't make it weird."

"I would never."

"You're making it weird with your face right now."

"This is my face."

"I know," she said, softer than the joke deserved, and drank her cider, and looked out at the orchard, and let the silence hold what it held.

* * *

The hat appeared at dusk.

Edmund didn't see it start. That was the thing about Saltmere — things happened sideways here, by consensus, without minutes being taken. One moment the light was going gold over the water and people were drifting toward the bonfire pit with lanterns, and the next moment Mrs. Okonkwo was moving through the crowd with Dev's flat cap in her hand, and people were putting things in it.

Edmund noticed because the ferryman's daughter — his new apprentice, second-to-last eater, keeper of the fisherman's grip — tugged his sleeve and pointed, solemn as a court witness.

"What's she doing?"

"Shh," said the girl. "You'll ruin it."

So he shushed, and watched.

Mrs. Okonkwo went from group to group, unhurried, unembarrassed, saying something low to each. He couldn't hear the words, but he saw the effect: the sheepish duck of heads, the digging into pockets, the folded notes passed with the particular care people use when giving money they've decided is a matter of principle. The mainlanders contributed too, hesitantly, following the locals' lead. Dev put in a note that made Edmund's stomach clench with its size. The ferryman, a man who argued about the price of everything, fed bills into the cap without breaking eye contact with the horizon.

And Wren — Wren stood apart, arms crossed, watching the cap move through the crowd with an expression Edmund had never seen on her: completely undefended. Like someone watching a tide come in over ground she'd thought was permanently dry.

The cap reached the bonfire pit, where Edmund stood holding a cider he'd forgotten to drink. Mrs. Okonkwo stopped in front of him. Around them, the crowd went quiet in that island way — not silent, never silent, but listening-quiet, the hush of people pretending to examine the sky.

"Now," she said, "before you get dignified about it, which would be a waste of a perfectly good evening, you'll take this."

"I—" He looked into the cap. Notes. Folded, creased, some coins, a great many small offerings that were clearly everything the givers could spare. "Mrs. Okonkwo. I can't take—"

"You can and you will." Her voice was gentle and absolutely immovable, the voice of a woman who had buried a husband and raised three children on this rock and did not negotiate with modesty. "That's for the café. For your ovens, your rent, whatever the bank's been sniffing after. Don't argue. I counted heads. There's two hundred and six of us on this island in winter, and every single one of us has eaten something free off you since you washed ashore, and none of us forgot it, and none of us intend to let some mainland bank take the only man on this coast who bakes like grief and grace had a baby."

A ripple of laughter, quickly hushed. Someone near the front said, "Hear, hear," and it spread.

Edmund looked down at the cap. His throat had closed in a way that no amount of professional composure could disguise, and he was distantly aware that his eyes were doing something embarrassing, and that the entire island was watching him do it, and that nobody was looking away, because that wasn't how things were done here either.

"My grandmother," he managed, "used to say the heart is trainable muscle. That you train it by showing up. By doing the work whether anyone notices or not." He swallowed. "I thought she meant discipline. I think she might have meant this."

"Well," said Mrs. Okonkwo, "she sounds like she was right about most things," and there was a small silence in which Edmund heard, underneath it, everything she wasn't saying about grandmothers and orchards and old letters, and he wondered — briefly, guiltily — how much of the story this island already knew.

Then the bonfire caught, and the moment broke open into music and noise, and the cap was pressed into his hands, and the crowd closed around him like warm water.

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and the mainlanders had staggered back to the ferry and the lanterns were going out one by one along the path — Edmund sat on the church-hall's-good-table with the cap of money beside him and counted it because counting was what he did instead of feeling.

Four hundred and twelve pounds. Some of it in coins. Some of it in notes soft with handling. One envelope, unsigned, containing fifty pounds and a scrap of paper that read *for the oven element, we heard it's dying.*

It wouldn't save the café. It wasn't meant to. It was a floor. Something to stand on.

Wren dropped onto the table beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, both of them watching the embers. She smelled of woodsmoke and salt and, faintly, of his kitchen, of flour and cardamom, and Edmund thought: *I could spend thirty years learning this exact smell.*

"Good day," she said.

"Good day."

"The best kind of day. The kind where nothing dramatic happens and everything changes anyway."

He laughed — quietly, so as not to break it. Down the slope, Mrs. Okonkwo was herding the last stragglers homeward with the efficiency of a sheepdog. The orchard rows stood dark and patient around them, full of next year.

"Wren." He turned the unsigned envelope over in his hands. "The developer's offer. The thirty days. They're nearly up."

Her shoulder went very slightly still against his.

"I know," she said.

"And the cooperative's counter-offer comes on the last ferry."

"I know that too."

He looked at the fire, at the money in his lap that proved people could surprise you, at the woman beside him who had become, without his permission, the person whose opinion of him mattered most. "Whatever the number is," he said carefully, "I need you to know that today — this —" he gestured at the orchard, the embers, the whole improbable golden afternoon — "changed the arithmetic. Not entirely. But enough that I'm frightened of it."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had a carefulness in it he'd learned to recognize as the sound of her deciding how much truth the moment could carry.

"Then hold onto that feeling," she said, "until Friday. And Edmund — whatever happens with the number — thank you. For staying for the thirty days. For the knots. For teaching a nine-year-old to be second-to-last." She slid off the table, picked up the cap, and pressed it back into his hands. "Get some sleep, Chef. Tomorrow we count the jam money, and you're helping, and yes, there will be math."

She walked up the path toward the cottage, a silhouette against the last of the firelight, and Edmund sat alone in the cooling orchard with four hundred and twelve pounds and a dread he couldn't name.

Because he'd seen her face just now, when the number came up. And beneath the warmth of it he'd caught something else — something quick and guilty, there and gone, like a fish turning under clear water.

As if she knew something about Friday's number that he didn't.

As if she'd had a hand in it.
