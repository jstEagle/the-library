## 19. The Last Ferry

There is a particular sound a contract makes when you tear it in half. It is not dramatic. It is not the thunderclap of destiny or the crack of a breaking chain. It sounds like wet paper, because my hands were sweating so badly that the signature page had gone soft at the edges, and it tore with a small apologetic sigh, like a thing embarrassed to be destroyed.

I stood at the end of the dock with the pieces in my fist and thought: *I have just thrown away Poppy's tuition.*

Then I thought: *No. I have just thrown away the version of her future where she learns that love is something you sign for, in triplicate, with an exit clause.*

The ferry wouldn't leave for another forty minutes. The last crossing of the season — the harbor authority runs it through Labor Day weekend and then mothballs it until spring, which has always struck me as the town's way of admitting, once a year, that we are an island in every way that matters. Half of Saltmere was on the dock. The Okafor twins were arguing about whether gulls could taste anything. Mrs. Halloran from the library had brought a thermos of coffee she guarded like state secrets. Somebody's radio played something tinny and old.

And in my jacket pocket, folded into quarters, was a receipt.

I had found it Thursday night, going through the shop's mail like a man doing triage. An accounting statement from the library's community fund — they administer a hardship relief program nobody uses because nobody wants to be seen using it. The statement listed disbursements. Three months of back rent on 14 Harbor Lane, paid in full, beginning in June. No donor name. Just a routing code and a note in the ledger's margin, written in a hand I would have known anywhere, because I had spent a summer learning it letter by letter across a hundred pages:

*For the bookshop. Please don't make this strange.*

Marguerite Voss had been paying my rent since June. Since before the letters got tender. Since while she was sitting beside her father at council meetings while he talked about "revitalizing underperforming parcels" — parcels that included mine — she had been quietly, anonymously keeping my door open.

I had spent two weeks hating her for being my pen pal and not telling me. I had spent those same two weeks not answering her letters, composing cruel little masterpieces of silence, telling myself that everything tender between us had been a lie told by a woman who knew exactly whose heart she was handling.

She hadn't known. That was the part that broke me open. She'd been writing to a stranger named "Alder" and falling for him honestly, while by day she fought me openly, and somewhere in the middle of all that contradiction she had done the one thing neither her father nor I had thought to do: she had simply kept the shop alive. Not to win. Not to bargain. Just kept it alive, the way you keep a fire going in a house you're afraid will be condemned.

I had signed the chain's acceptance Wednesday. I had torn it up Saturday morning, standing in my kitchen while Poppy ate toast, and she had watched me do it without a word, then gone and gotten her map off the wall — the one with every business on our block drawn in colored pencil, each one labeled in her careful ten-year-old hand — and folded it into her pocket like a passport.

"Are we going to the dock?" she'd asked.

"We're going to the dock."

"Are you going to say the thing?"

"What thing?"

"The thing you've been practicing in the shower. I can hear you through the wall. You're very bad at sounding casual."

* * *

Here is what I had practiced in the shower, badly: *Marguerite Voss, I know it's you. I've known since June. I stopped writing because I was a coward who would rather lose you than find out I'd been a fool twice. I'm sorry. I choose you over the safe thing. I choose you out loud, in front of everyone, because you deserve a man who isn't anonymous in any room you're standing in.*

It sounded better in the shower. Most things do. In the shower there's no audience, no gulls, no daughter taking notes.

I looked down at the torn contract in my hand. The chain's logo smiled up at me from the letterhead — a little green sprout, absurdly, as if a corporation that had eaten four hundred independent bookstores were a seedling. I walked to the end of the dock where the harbor authority keeps a rusted bin for fishing line, and I dropped the pieces in.

Mrs. Halloran raised her thermos to me from twenty feet away. She didn't ask. Librarians never ask; they just make sure you know they saw.

That was when I heard running.

You hear a lot of things on a dock — rigging, water, the arguments of gulls — but running is different. Running means someone is trying to arrive before something else does. I turned, and there she was, coming down the ramp road at a pace no Voss had ever moved in public, Marguerite Voss in a linen blazer that had clearly been put on in a moving vehicle, hair half-escaped from whatever architecture usually contained it, clutching a folder against her chest like a life vest.

She was barefoot.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left: Marguerite Voss, who wore heels to a potluck, who dressed for war even when the war was a zoning hearing, came down that dock barefoot, shoes in one hand, folder in the other, breathing like she'd run the whole length of Front Street, which she had, because I could see the bakery behind her, dark, CLOSED sign hanging crooked in the window for the first time in its history.

"You signed it," she said, stopping three feet away. Not a question. Her eyes went to my empty hands, then to the bin, then back to me. "You signed the chain's offer. I heard it from Delia at the bank, and I—" She stopped. Recalibrated. Looked at the bin again. "What did you just throw away?"

"A contract," I said. "It wasn't working out."

"Lior." My name in her mouth did what it always did, which was rearrange the furniture of my chest. "The withdrawal notice goes to the council Monday morning. I filed it Friday. Me, not my father — I have power of attorney on the acquisition account, he never changed the paperwork after Mother died, and I used it. The deal is dead. And these—" she lifted the folder, and her hand shook, and I saw that it wasn't fear shaking it, or not only fear, "—these are copies. Ledger pages. Lease clause. Everything. They go to the *Gazette* and the ethics board tonight, whether or not—"

"Whether or not what?"

"Whether or not you ever speak to me again." She said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "Because I owe you an apology that doesn't fit in a letter, and I've written eleven drafts, and they were all garbage, because the truth is I don't know how to say sorry to you in writing anymore. Writing is how we hid. So." She gestured at the dock, the town, the gulls, the whole assembled jury of Saltmere. "No paper."

Behind her, I saw heads turning. The Okafors had abandoned the gull debate entirely. Mrs. Halloran had lowered her thermos, which in Halloran terms was a standing ovation.

"Poppy," I said, because my daughter was standing right there, and some things you say in front of your child precisely so you can't take them back.

"I know," Marguerite said, and her voice cracked clean through. "Hi, Poppy."

"You're the pen pal," Poppy said. Not a question either. Ten-year-olds don't ask questions when they already have the answer; they just issue verdicts. "Dad reads your letters out loud when he thinks I'm asleep. He does voices. Yours is the posh one."

"*Poppy.*"

"It's true! You do a whole—" she began, and I gave her the look, the parental look that says *we will discuss your betrayal later*, and she grinned and retreated exactly zero inches.

Marguerite laughed — a wet, broken laugh, the kind that's mostly relief — and then sobered, and stepped closer, and the dock seemed to narrow around us until it was just the two of us and the sound of the water.

"I need to say the true names of things," she said. "My mother used to say that. That most cruelty is just misnaming — you call greed 'duty,' you call fear 'prudence,' you call a person an obstacle, and then you can do anything to them. So. True names." She breathed. "I am Marguerite Voss. I wrote to you as Wren because I wanted, for one hour a week, to be someone my father hadn't built. I fell in love with Alder before I knew he was you, and I fell in love with you after, and I have no idea where one ends and the other begins, and I've decided that not knowing is the honest answer. I paid your rent because watching you count change in that register made me complicit in something I couldn't name yet, and paying rent was the only rebellion I could manage in June. I am sorry. I am terrified. My father is going to destroy me professionally and possibly personally, and I did it anyway, and the reason I did it anyway is standing in front of me holding nothing, having thrown away his daughter's security, and I need him to tell me that wasn't a mistake."

The ferry horn sounded. Boarding call. Nobody moved. Half of Saltmere was pretending very hard to look elsewhere, and failing completely.

"My turn," I said.

I took the map out of my pocket — Poppy's map, which I had borrowed without asking, because some props you steal from your child. I unfolded it. Every shop on our block, drawn in colored pencil: the bookshop, the hardware store, Delia's bank, the chandlery, the bakery. At the bottom, in Poppy's handwriting from months ago: *WHICH ONE WILL BE MINE?*

"This is what I was protecting her from losing," I said. "This is why I almost signed. Because a salary and tuition felt like a wall I could build around her, and I have spent six years building walls and calling them parenting." I looked at Marguerite. "But walls are just misnamed fear. Your mother was right about naming things. Here are mine: I am Lior Fairweather. I knew your handwriting from the first letter — you filed a complaint about my sidewalk display in April, and you dot your i's like you're stabbing them. I should have reported the clerical error. Instead I wrote back, because it was the first interesting thing to happen to me in years, and because I am a coward who would rather flirt with an enemy than admit I was lonely. I stopped answering because I found out who you were and chose pride over you. That was the mistake. Not the letters. The silence. And this—" I held up the map, "—is the answer to Poppy's question. All of them. All of them will be hers, if half this town has anything to say about it, and apparently half this town is standing within earshot right now."

"We can hear you fine," called one of the Okafors.

"Thank you, Marcus."

"It's Milo."

"Thank you, whichever."

Laughter ran down the dock, and under its cover Marguerite closed the distance, and I will not pretend the kiss was private, because it wasn't, because nothing in Saltmere is ever private, and because I think we both understood by then that privacy had been the whole problem. We had loved each other in envelopes, in aliases, in the dark of assumed names, and here we were doing it in daylight, salt wind and gull noise and Poppy making a theatrical gagging sound that fooled absolutely no one including herself.

When we broke apart, Mrs. Halloran was holding out her thermos like a chalice. "Coffee," she said. "You both look terrible. In a romantic way."

* * *

We boarded together. That was the thing people talked about afterward — not the kiss, though the kiss made the *Gazette* under a headline Delia at the bank may or may not have suggested (*VOSS HEIR SINKS FAMILY DEAL AT DOCKSIDE*, which Marguerite framed, which tells you everything). What they talked about was that we boarded together, the Fairweather girl between us holding both our hands like a towline, and that when the ferry pulled out, the whole town stood along the rail facing backward, toward the town, the way you do on the last crossing, taking the inventory of what you're leaving and what's waiting when you come back.

The block looked small from the water. Fourteen Harbor Lane with its crooked awning. The bakery with its lights still off, waiting for whoever would flip them Monday morning. Poppy's map, made flesh, every roofline accounted for.

"Monday's going to be a disaster," Marguerite said quietly, beside me. "The ethics board. The press. My father. There will be lawyers, Lior. There will be weeks of it. I have a folder full of evidence against my own name and no idea what my life looks like on the other side."

"I have a bookshop with no lease guarantee, a register that won't survive August, and a daughter who does voices," I said. "Between us we have one functional adult, and I believe it's the ten-year-old."

"She does do excellent voices."

"She gets it from her mother." I said it easily, and then felt the old ache move through and past me, the way grief does when you've finally given it room — not gone, but no longer in charge. Marguerite's hand found mine on the rail. She didn't say anything about it. She just laced her fingers through, and that was its own kind of letter, the kind you hand over in person.

The town shrank behind us. The gulls followed anyway, because gulls have no loyalty, only optimism.

"Dad," Poppy said, looking up from her map, which she'd smoothed out on the rail. "Can I add the ferry?"

"To the map?"

"Somebody should keep track of the things that carried us."

I looked at Marguerite. She was watching my daughter with an expression I recognized from her letters — the one about proofing times, about how the best things rise slowly and can't be rushed, about how her mother said you fail bread by demanding it be done before it's ready. People, too, maybe. Towns. Summers.

"Yes," I said. "Add the ferry. Add all of it."

And because I am who I am — a man who spent a whole summer teaching his heart to stay closed and failed at every lesson — I took the receipt out of my pocket one last time. The rent payments. *Please don't make this strange.* I unfolded it, read it once more, and handed it to her.

"You should keep this," I said. "First page of the next chapter. I hear the author's very good."

She took it. She was crying and laughing at once, which is the only honest way to arrive anywhere.

"Monday," she said. "Ethics board. Will you be there?"

"Front row," I said. "I'll bring the posh voice."

The horn sounded across the water, long and low, closing out the season — and somewhere behind us, in a folder on a dock bench, the evidence waited, and ahead of us a courtroom full of reckoning, and none of it, not one word of it, frightened me the way silence used to.
