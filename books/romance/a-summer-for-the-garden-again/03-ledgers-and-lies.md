## 3. Ledgers and Lies

The letter came on a Tuesday, in the same gray envelope as all the others, but this one was heavier. Lior felt the difference in her hand before she saw the bank's crest embossed in the corner — Meridian Agricultural Trust, second notice, *final*.

She was alone in the kitchen when she opened it. Juniper had left before dawn for the east rows, some note about soil samples propped against the coffee pot in handwriting Lior still couldn't look at too long without something in her chest pulling sideways. Twelve years of silence, and now there were notes on the counter again, casual as anything, as if the intervening years had been nothing but a long weekend.

*Dear Ms. Vane,* the letter began.

*This letter serves as formal notification that the outstanding balance of $214,600 against the Vane Hollow property must be paid in full no later than October 14th — the conclusion of the current harvest season. Absent full payment, Meridian Agricultural Trust will initiate foreclosure proceedings pursuant to clause nine of your loan agreement.*

Lior read it three times. The numbers didn't change. The date didn't change. Outside the window, the vineyard went on being beautiful in the way that had always felt like a lie — gold light through the trellises, the hills soft with morning haze, everything green and thriving and worth nothing to anyone holding paper.

October 14th. Harvest ended around then, if the weather held. Which meant the entire crop — every grape, every bottle they might press and sell, every desperate hope she'd been rationing out to herself like water in a drought — would have to become cash in six weeks. Not revenue eventually. Cash by a date certain, or the deed with two names on it would belong to a bank.

She sat down at the kitchen table and put her head in her hands.

There was a version of this where she told Juniper immediately. She could picture it: sliding the letter across the table, saying *here, this is what we're actually up against*, watching Juniper do what Juniper did — go still, go sharp, start solving. That was the version where they were real partners. That was probably the version where they had any chance at all.

But Juniper had come back intending to sign away her share and leave on a train. Lior knew that. Whatever the frost night had changed — whatever had kept Juniper past Friday, and then past the next Friday — the offer was still sitting there between them like an unexploded shell. If Lior showed her a number like $214,600 due in six weeks, Juniper would do the math faster than Lior could. She'd see exactly what kind of sinking ship she'd been roped onto, and she'd sign, and she'd be gone, and this time it would be final because this time Lior would have handed her the reason herself.

No. This was Lior's mess. Her father's mess, technically, but inheritance didn't distinguish. She would carry it.

She burned the letter in the wood stove and watched the bank's crest curl black.

* * *

The trouble with lying to Juniper Ashe was that Juniper Ashe noticed things.

It started small. On Wednesday, Lior came down to find the kitchen table covered in the vineyard's financial records — the shoebox of receipts, the dog-eared ledger her father had kept in handwriting that deteriorated year by year like his health had, the laptop Lior used mostly as a very expensive lamp. Juniper stood over it all with her sleeves rolled up and her hair pinned back with what appeared to be a pencil, looking like a general surveying a battlefield she intended to win.

"We're paying Hollis Brothers forty cents a gallon more than anyone else for sulfur," she said, without preamble. "And we're buying it retail."

"Everyone buys from Hollis."

"Everyone buys from Hollis because everyone's lazy." She tapped a column of figures. "I called a co-op in Walla Walla. Same compound, thirty-one percent less, delivered. I set up an account."

"You called—" Lior looked at the clock. It was seven in the morning. "You called Washington state?"

"I called at six. They open early during crush season." Juniper flipped the ledger shut. "Also, your irrigation pump is running four hours a night it doesn't need to. Your father set the timer during the drought and nobody ever changed it back. That's real money on the electric bill, Lior. Real money every single month, for years."

Something hot crawled up the back of Lior's throat. It wasn't quite anger. It was closer to shame wearing anger's coat.

"I know how to run my own vineyard," she said.

Juniper looked at her for a moment — that level, unhurried look she'd had even at fourteen, the one that made you feel like a specimen under glass. "Do you?" she said, not cruelly. "Because I've been here eleven days, and I've found four thousand dollars a year leaking out of this place through holes a first-year viticulture student would have plugged."

She turned and went out to the rows, leaving Lior standing among the scattered records with her ears burning.

That was the shape the week took. Juniper moved through the vineyard's finances the way she moved through everything — quietly, thoroughly, without asking permission — and everywhere she touched, money stopped bleeding. She renegotiated the fuel contract. She found that the local restaurant, Marisol's, would pay a premium for contract grapes if someone simply asked, and asked. She reorganized the cellar so the good barrels weren't sitting in the warm corner near the compressor, which apparently degraded them, which apparently mattered, which apparently was the kind of thing everyone else in the valley knew except the Vanes.

Each saving was a small mercy and a small knife. Because every dollar Juniper found was a dollar Lior hadn't known was there, in twelve years of drowning. And every dollar brought the hidden number in Lior's head into sharper relief — $214,600, October 14th — and made the gap between what Juniper could save and what the bank demanded look less like a gap and more like a canyon.

Friday evening, Juniper came in with dirt on her knees and a spreadsheet printed off the office computer, and laid it on the table with something almost like excitement in her face.

"If we do the Marisol contract, cut the sulfur costs, and get a decent price per ton at harvest — and I mean decent, not the scrap rate your last buyer offered — we clear maybe sixty thousand above operating costs. Maybe seventy." She sat down across from Lior, and for a moment she looked young, the way she had at thirteen, presenting a science fair project. "Sixty or seventy thousand, Lior. That's not nothing. That's breathing room. We could—"

"We'll see," Lior said. "Harvest prices move around."

Juniper's excitement dimmed by degrees, like a lamp turned down. "Right," she said. "We'll see."

She gathered her papers and went upstairs, and Lior sat alone with the ghost of the burned letter and did the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing: sixty thousand, against two hundred fourteen. Even if the weather held, even if the crop came in perfect, even if Juniper squeezed blood from every stone in the county — it wasn't enough. It wasn't close to enough. And every brilliant, practical thing Juniper did only made the eventual reckoning crueler, because it meant Juniper believed. She was building something she thought had a future. She was staying.

Lior pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw stars.

* * *

Sunday night, she drove into town to meet Tomás Reyes at the feed store after hours, because Tomás was the one man in the valley who'd known her father and didn't hate her for the debts, and because she needed to hear someone say out loud what the letter had said.

Tomás listened while she talked, chewing an unlit cigar, and when she finished he was quiet for a long time.

"Meridian," he said finally. "They bought your paper off First Cascadia two years ago?"

"Last spring."

"They're vultures. They buy distressed ag loans cheap and strip the land for whatever the parcels fetch." He spat into a nail keg. "You know what your north parcel would go for, subdivided? That creek frontage? There are people in Seattle who'd pay for that view."

"I know what it would go for."

"And the girl?" Tomás said, and Lior's stomach dropped. "Ashe. She know?"

"She doesn't need to know."

Tomás gave her a look over the dead cigar. "Your father hid things from people too. Said it was protecting them." He let that sit. "How'd that work out for everybody?"

Lior drove home with the windows down and the cold air in her teeth, and didn't answer him, because there wasn't an answer, because there never had been.

The house was dark when she got back except for one light — the kitchen, where Juniper sat at the table with a mug of tea going cold beside her and a piece of paper in front of her, folded once. She looked up when Lior came in, and her face in the lamplight was unreadable in a way that made the cold air find Lior's spine.

"There was mail today," Juniper said. "From Meridian. Addressed to both of us, this time — they pulled the updated deed." She turned the envelope over with one finger. "I called the number on their website this afternoon. Asked about the status of the loan." A pause, precise as a blade. "They were very happy to tell me everything, Lior. Apparently co-owners are entitled to full disclosure."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, an owl called across the vines, indifferent.

"So," Juniper said, and her voice didn't rise at all, which was somehow worse than shouting. "Six weeks. Two hundred fourteen thousand dollars. When exactly were you planning to tell me — or were you going to let me spend the whole harvest building something you already knew was a coffin?"

Lior opened her mouth, and found she had nothing — no defense, no deflection, nothing her father hadn't tried first.

"The train," Juniper said softly, standing, folding the letter closed. "The last train before harvest. I want to know if you hid this because you were protecting me, or because you wanted me gone before I found out."

And she stood there waiting for the answer, and Lior — who had spent twelve years learning how to survive silence — discovered she didn't have one.
