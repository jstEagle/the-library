## 8. Rootstock

The letter had been in her coat pocket for six days, folded into a square no bigger than a matchbook, and Juniper had carried it the way one carries a stone in a shoe — aware of it with every step, unwilling to stop and shake it out.

Now she stood on the platform at Halloway Station with a single suitcase and a ticket for the 7:42, and there was nothing left to do but stop.

The morning was cold enough to see her breath. Frost had silvered the rails all the way down the valley, and beyond the station fence the vineyard rows climbed the hill in their long dark stitches, half of them broken, half of them hers now, legally, if she wanted them. The appointment letter sat on top of her folded shirts in the suitcase — *Dr. Juniper Ashe, Senior Fellowship, Institute of Viticulture, Montpellier* — beginning the week before harvest, beginning the day after this train. She had told no one. She had told Lior least of all, and that was the part that had kept her awake, staring at the ceiling of the east bedroom, listening to the house settle around a woman who was already halfway to leaving her.

The 7:42 was late. Of course it was. Halloway Station had never once been on time in Juniper's entire life, and she had a sudden, irrational faith that it would not begin today.

She took out the letter.

She had read it once already, in the orchard, standing over the tin box they'd buried when they were eleven — two girls with a shovel and a pact. She had read it standing up, in shock, with Lior twenty feet away pretending to prune a tree that had been dead for a decade, and she had only gotten as far as *I did it to protect you* before Lior had seen her face and deflected, and the moment had closed like water over a dropped coin.

Now she sat down on the bench beneath the station clock, and she read it properly. Slowly. All of it.

---

*Juniper —*

*I'm writing this because I can't say it. If I say it out loud my father will hear it, and if my father hears it he'll find a way to make it worse, and he's already made it worse enough.*

*You know what happened at the auction. You think I sold the Ashe parcel out from under your family because I wanted it. Everyone thinks that. Let them.*

*Here is what actually happened. Your father came to my father in the spring and asked to borrow against your farm to cover the co-op fees. My father said yes, and then he started drinking again, and I heard him on the phone with the bank telling them he'd found a way to take the Ashe land whole if the loan went bad. Not help you. Take you. He said your father was a fool and fools' land comes cheap eventually, and he laughed.*

*I was fourteen. I couldn't stop a loan. But I could stop him from ever getting your land, so I bid on the parcel myself — with money I didn't have, with my mother's ring as collateral, with anything — so that it would pass out of his reach and sit in a stranger's hands until your family could buy it back. I let you hate me because if you didn't hate me, you'd have defended me, and he'd have known I was the one who'd done it, and he'd have gone after your family twice as hard to prove a point. He told me once, when he was drunk, that the only thing he loved more than winning was being forgiven by people who'd wronged him. I made sure you'd never forgive me. It was the cruelest thing I've ever done and I did it on purpose and I'd do it again.*

*The parcel sold to the Hollis estate. Your father got the co-op fees from the sale price — I arranged that part through Mr. Brandt, who is decent, whatever else he is. Your farm survived. That was the whole point. You surviving was the whole point.*

*I'm sorry I let you carry it. I'm sorry I let you carry it for twelve years. I keep telling myself you're better off hating me than owing me, but I don't believe it anymore. I just don't know how to unpick it.*

*If you're reading this, it means you found the tin, which means you came back, which means maybe I get one more chance to be brave in the ordinary way — the way where I just tell the truth and let it land where it lands.*

*— Lior*

*P.S. The north field was always your favorite. I never let anyone replant it. I told the crew the soil was wrong. The soil was fine. I just wanted one thing in this valley that stayed yours.*

---

Juniper read the last line twice, and then a third time, and the platform tilted gently around her.

Twelve years. Twelve years she had built her whole self on the foundation of that betrayal — the scholarships, the departure, the career built at a distance from anything that smelled of Halloway soil, all of it a fortress against a girl who had sold her family's land out from under her. And the whole time, the girl had been standing in the wreckage of her own house, holding the door so Juniper could get out.

*He said your father was a fool and fools' land comes cheap.*

She thought of Marcus Vane, the big laugh, the generous pour, the man who'd taught them both to graft on the porch steps one summer and had seemed, to a child, like the warmest thing in the valley. She thought of her own father, who had died three years ago still saying, gently, *I never understood what happened with Lior, June. She was the best friend you ever had.* He had never understood. He had died not knowing that Lior Vane had saved his farm.

And Lior had let her believe the worst of it. Had stood in the orchard six days ago and deflected — *it was a long time ago, Juniper, it doesn't matter now* — because apparently twelve years of being hated was a price she'd already paid and saw no reason to stop paying.

The train came around the bend, a small yellow engine dragging its three cars through the frost, right on time to be late.

Juniper stood up.

She did not board it.

It was, she would think later, the least dramatic decision of her life. There was no running, no tears, no scene on the platform. The train hissed, the doors opened, a man got off with fishing rods, and the doors closed, and the 7:42 pulled away without her the way it had pulled away from a hundred of her old imagined escapes, and Juniper Ashe stood on the platform of Halloway Station holding a fourteen-year-old's letter and felt the strangest thing: not grief, and not relief, but a kind of furious clarity, like frost burning off a field at sunrise.

She picked up her suitcase. She did not go home to pack for Montpellier. She walked up the hill road to Vane Hollow, and she went to find the woman who had never once, in twelve years, asked to be forgiven — because she'd decided she didn't deserve it.

* * *

Lior was in the barn, and she was not glad to see her.

Juniper knew it before she'd crossed the threshold. Lior stood at the workbench with her back half-turned, sorting grafting knives, and she didn't look up, and the set of her shoulders said everything the silence didn't. Since the hailstorm, since the shouting, since *everyone leaves, it's what Vanes do and what Ashes do better* — since Juniper had packed a bag in the dark and said things she'd regretted before they finished leaving her mouth — the house had been a museum of two people not speaking. Lior had slept, apparently, in the barn loft. Juniper had heard her come down at dawn.

"I'm not going," Juniper said.

"I saw the train go past." Lior's voice was flat. She lined up a grafting knife with the others, precisely, unnecessarily. "You'll want to catch the afternoon one. The road's faster if you take the ridge."

"I'm not going to Montpellier either."

That landed. Juniper watched it land — watched the knife stop mid-slide, watched Lior's hand close around the handle and stay there.

"Don't do this," Lior said quietly.

"Do what?"

"Whatever this is." Now she turned, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified all at once, and it broke Juniper's heart in a new place, a place she hadn't known was still whole. "You got the letter. I saw your face in the orchard, and then you packed a bag, and now you're standing here telling me you're staying, and I know what that means. It means you read it and you feel sorry for me, and you're going to stay out of pity, and I will not be a charity, Juniper. I have been the town's charity my whole life. I would rather lose the vineyard."

"Would you shut up and listen to me?"

Lior's mouth closed.

Juniper crossed the barn floor and put the letter down on the workbench between them, unfolded, smoothed flat with both hands like she was pressing the years out of it.

"I read it four times," she said. "I read it on the platform. I read it while the train was sitting there with its doors open. And I need you to understand something, because you have clearly spent twelve years misunderstanding it, so I'm going to say it very plainly." She took a breath. "I am not staying because I pity you. I am staying because I am *furious* with you. There's a difference, and it matters."

Lior blinked. "Furious."

"Enraged. Livid. You let me hate you. You let me build my entire life on a lie — a lie you told *for* me, which is somehow worse, because now I have to grieve twelve years I can't get back and I can't even be angry at the right person for the right reason, because the right reason is that you were fourteen and brave and stupid and alone, and you decided your whole self wasn't worth as much as my father's farm." Her voice cracked and she let it. "You decided you were disposable, Lior. You decided it at fourteen and you've decided it every day since, and I watched you do it again last week — you found out about Montpellier and you didn't even fight, you just started building the wall, because God forbid anyone choose you and you have to live with it."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it? Say the letter isn't exactly that. Say I'm wrong."

Lior looked at the letter. Her jaw worked. When she spoke, her voice was barely there. "You were supposed to be angry at me. That was the design. It worked for twelve years."

"Well, the design's failed."

The barn was very quiet. Through the open door, the frost was burning off the vineyard rows, the whole hillside steaming gently in the early sun like something breathing. Somewhere up the valley a tractor started, and Juniper thought: three days. Three days to harvest, half the crop wrecked, pickers short, the bank's inspector coming at dawn, and two women standing in a barn with twelve years of wreckage between them and no time at all.

Lior sat down slowly on the workbench stool, like her legs had made the decision without consulting her. "I don't know how to be forgiven," she said at last. "I genuinely don't. I've had twelve years of practice being hated and I'm — I'm good at it. It's the one thing I'm good at."

"You're not," Juniper said. "Good at being hated. You're terrible at it. You flinch every time someone in town says your father's name." She came closer, close enough to see the exhaustion carved under Lior's eyes, the hailstorm still living there. "Here's what's going to happen. I'm staying. Not for the vineyard, not out of pity, not for the deed. I'm staying because I read a letter from a fourteen-year-old girl who gave up everything for my family and never told anyone, and I have spent six days being angry at her and I have about fifty more years of being angry at her and I intend to spend all of them *here*, where I can do it properly."

"That's the least romantic thing anyone's ever said to me."

"I'm not done. I'm also staying because of the north field." She watched Lior's face go still. "You kept it mine. All these years. You let the whole valley think the soil was bad." Juniper's throat tightened. "Lior. Nobody does that for someone they've written off. Nobody keeps a field fallow for twelve years out of hate."

Lior looked down at her hands. They were shaking, very slightly, and she pressed them flat against her knees to stop it, the way she did everything — privately, stubbornly, as if needing things were a debt she refused to incur.

"I don't know how to let someone stay," she said finally. "I'm telling you that up front. I'm going to be bad at it. I'm going to push. I'm going to say things like I said in the storm, and I'm going to mean them in the worst moment and not mean them an hour later, and I don't know how to stop."

"I know," Juniper said. "I hid Montpellier from you for two weeks. We're both liars. We'll work on it."

That got the ghost of a laugh out of her — wet, unwilling, real. Lior scrubbed a hand across her face and looked up, and for the first time since the hailstorm she looked at Juniper directly, and what was in her eyes wasn't forgiveness or gratitude or even love, not yet, not sayable yet. It was something more fragile than all of those. It was the look of a woman checking whether the ground would hold.

"Three days," Lior said. "Half the crop is hail-damage. We're short eleven pickers. The inspector comes at dawn on harvest day, and if the tonnage is under the restructuring floor, the bank takes it — takes everything, including the north field." Her voice steadied as it turned to numbers, the way it always did, the way Juniper was beginning to understand was its own kind of armor. "It's probably not enough. Even if we work the whole night. Even if—"

"Then we'd better find some pickers," Juniper said, already reaching for her coat. "Because I spent last night making a list of everyone in this valley who owes you a kindness, Lior, and I have to tell you — it's longer than you think. You've been so busy being hated that you never noticed how many people were quietly on your side."

She was at the barn door when Lior spoke behind her, quiet enough that the words almost didn't survive the distance.

"Juniper. Why did you really get off the train?"

Juniper turned. The morning light stood full in the doorway behind her, and the vineyard steamed up the hill, and she thought of a fourteen-year-old girl with a shovel and a lie, keeping a field fallow for twelve years.

"Because somebody once chose me," she said, "when it cost her everything. I figured it was my turn — and this time, she gets to know about it."

Then she stepped out into the cold bright morning to raise an army, and behind her, in the barn, Lior Vane sat very still with an old letter open in her hands — and began, for the first time in her life, to believe she might be worth staying for.
