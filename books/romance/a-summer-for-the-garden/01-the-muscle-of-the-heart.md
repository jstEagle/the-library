## 1. The Muscle of the Heart

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a house when a book is finished. It isn't peace. Peace implies you wanted quiet. This silence is more like the ringing in your ears after a door slams — the house itself seems surprised that you're done with it.

I saved the file at 6:14 in the morning, because I'd been awake since three and had run out of reasons not to be finished. The word count sat at the bottom of the screen like a small, smug animal: 94,312. Ten years of my life, give or take the years I spent pretending I wasn't writing it. *The Salt Almanac.* A woman inherits a garden she's never seen from a mother she barely knew, and spends a summer learning that grief is less like weather and more like a muscle — it only works if you use it, and it aches most on the days you thought you were resting.

I got the muscle metaphor from W, actually. Though he'd hate being called "he," since in ten years of letters he has never once confirmed so much as a pronoun. W is a letter of the alphabet and nothing else, which is exactly how I like my people: legible, contained, incapable of disappointing me at a dinner party.

I did not celebrate. Celebration implies an audience, and my audience was a cold cup of tea and a cat named Bishop who has never once read my work but sits on every draft with the confidence of a hostile critic. Instead I did what I always do when something enormous happens inside this house, which is nothing at all, out loud.

This is the discipline. People think containment is repression, that it's a failure of nerve. Containment is a practice. It's pruning. You cut back the wild growth so the fruit comes in true. My grandfather kept a lighthouse for forty-one years and told me once, when I was small enough to sit on his knee and ask why the light had to turn instead of just shining everywhere, that a beam scattered in all directions lights nothing. You narrow it. You aim it. That's how you keep ships off the rocks.

I have narrowed myself accordingly. It has worked beautifully for thirty-four years.

* * *

The rules came later, though they feel older than that, like something I was born knowing and only wrote down eventually to make them official. I keep them in the front of my writing notebook, which means they travel with me everywhere, which means they are less rules than a spine.

One: Never tell anyone the whole truth about yourself. Ration it. Truth given all at once becomes a weapon in someone else's hand by Tuesday.

Two: Love people at a distance you can survive. Correspondence is ideal. Letters arrive when they're ready; they cannot stand in your kitchen dripping rain and asking questions.

Three: Want things quietly. Wanting loudly invites witnesses, and witnesses remember.

Four: When in doubt, write it down and send it away. Paper holds what people can't be trusted with.

Five — and this one is written smaller than the others, the way you write the rule you break most often — Five: Do not mistake being unseen for being safe.

I recite them the way other people say grace. Not because I believe anyone's listening, but because the recitation itself is the shelter. Every morning since I was twenty-three, when the first letter arrived postmarked nowhere I could find on a map, signed with nothing but that single initial, I have said the rules to the bathroom mirror like a woman doing calisthenics for the heart. The heart, I have come to understand, is a muscle like any other, and left unattended it will atrophy into something soft and useless, or worse, it will grow wild and unpruned and drag the whole trellis down.

W keeps my heart in excellent condition precisely because W asks for none of it. Once a year — sometimes twice, if the year has been generous — we exchange letters. Long ones. The kind that require two stamps and an apology to the postal service. In ten years I know this much: W reads more than writes, notices everything, has a temper that surfaces only in the margins (once, entire paragraphs of furious annotation about a newspaper article I'd mentioned), and once went silent for seven months, returning with no explanation except a single line: *Forgive the gap. Some winters take longer than others.*

I know nothing else. No name, no face, no city I could point to. The return address is a post office box in a town whose name I've looked up exactly four times and then made myself stop, because looking up towns is the first step down a staircase that ends with me standing outside someone's actual window, and I have rules.

People hear this and assume loneliness. They picture me as a woman pressing her palm to glass. But here is what they don't understand: W is the only relationship I've ever had that couldn't hurt me. You cannot be abandoned by someone who was never obligated to stay. You cannot be lied to by someone who never claimed a face. Every human arrangement I've watched up close — my parents' marriage, which detonated when I was nine; my grandfather's long widowhood; the marriages in this town, which seem to run on a fuel of grievance and garden tours — every one of them taught me the same lesson, which is that proximity is where love goes to get complicated.

Letters don't get complicated. Letters get better. Ten years in, W and I have said things to each other I doubt either of us has said to a living soul. I told him about the night my grandfather died, how I sat with him in the lighthouse while the lamp turned above us and neither of us spoke for an hour, and how it was the best conversation of my life. He told me — once, carefully, the way you hand someone something breakable — that he had done something he couldn't undo, and that he thought about it every day, and that he wasn't asking forgiveness, only witness. I gave him witness. It cost me nothing and seemed to save him something. That is the whole architecture of our friendship: load-bearing walls, no windows.

So yes. I am the woman who loves her correspondent. I'm aware of how it sounds. I've read the novels; I practically write them. But there is a difference between loving someone and needing them within arm's reach, and I have built an entire life on that difference, and the life holds.

Mostly.

* * *

By ten o'clock I had showered, dressed, and performed the small theater of normalcy: kettle on, curtains open, Bishop fed and informed that he was a parasite. On the kitchen table lay the manuscript, printed overnight on paper I probably shouldn't have spent money on, boxed, addressed to my agent in London with a note that read, in its entirety: *It's done. Don't call before noon. — C.*

And beside it, the letter.

I had written it over four nights, which is fast for me; some annual letters take weeks. This one came easily because the year had been strange in ways I needed to narrate to exactly one person. I told W about the book. I told him about the festival committee's letters arriving already, Harlan Pike's handwriting getting more imperious each spring, the lighthouse trust wanting the old keeper's logs catalogued for the centennial. I told him the tide had been behaving strangely in March, the way my grandfather used to predict weather from the smell of the wrack line, and that I'd caught myself doing it too, standing on the dunes sniffing seaweed like a lunatic, and that inheritance is a strange thing — it skips your consent entirely and lands in your body.

Then, near the end, I wrote the thing I almost didn't write. I have learned to recognize these sentences. They arrive fully formed, refusing revision, and they are always the reason for the letter.

*I finished the book today,* I wrote. *Ninety-four thousand words about a woman learning to use her grief instead of storing it. And I realized somewhere around chapter twelve that I was writing about myself, which is embarrassing, and that you were in it, which is worse. There's a character who exists only in letters. He's the wisest person in the book and he never appears on the page. My agent will want to know if he's real. I'll tell her he's structural. But between us, W — and this stays between us the way everything does — I think you may be the only person who has ever known me completely, and I have never once minded. I don't know what to do with that information, so I'm sending it to you, which is what I do with everything.*

I read it back twice. Rule Four says write it down and send it away. Rule One says never tell anyone the whole truth. The sentence violated Rule One flagrantly. I sealed the envelope anyway. There is a version of containment that isn't armor but ritual — you put the dangerous thing in the mail precisely so it can't live in the house with you. W would read it, hold it gently or not, and answer in eleven months or whenever, and the danger would remain safely theoretical, folded in tissue paper, three hundred miles from my actual chest.

That's the trick of it. That's the whole trick.

* * *

The village post office is not really a post office anymore; it's the corner of Fenwick's shop where Mrs. Adeyemi presides over a counter, a scale, and an intelligence network that would embarrass most governments. I go at odd hours specifically to avoid the intelligence network, but it was nearly eleven by the time I'd wrapped the manuscript, and the shop was full.

"Casper Dunmore." Mrs. Adeyemi weighed the manuscript box with the gravity of a customs official. "This is a heavy one. Another book?"

"Another book."

"Well. Your grandfather would—" She stopped herself, the way everyone in this town stops themselves at his name, mid-sentence, a little cliff edge they all walk to and retreat from. "He'd be pleased about the lighthouse logs, anyway. Festival committee's very grateful you're doing them."

"I haven't started yet."

"Harlan Pike asked after you Saturday." Her tone did something complicated with the words *asked after*, suggesting that in Harlan's mouth the phrase meant something closer to *summoned*. "Said the Dunmore name should be represented properly at the centennial. Said it twice."

"Once per century seems sufficient."

She laughed, then lowered her voice over the stamps. "You hear about the cottage?"

"What cottage?"

"The keeper's cottage. Next to yours." She said it the way you'd announce a comet. "Let finally. Young man moved in yesterday. Well — man. Hard to say. Keeps to himself. Came in for bread and batteries and nothing else, didn't say two words. Mr. Fenwick thinks he's hiding from something. Mr. Fenwick thinks everyone's hiding from something, mind, but this one—" she tapped her temple— "this one has the look. You'll see him. Smoke coming out of the chimney last night, first time in what, fifteen years? That place has been falling down since your grandfather's day. Somebody's thrown money at it fast. Money and hurry. That's a combination I don't care for."

I cared for it even less than I let on, which was not at all outwardly and considerably inwardly. The keeper's cottage had been part of the landscape of my childhood the way the rocks were — ruined, familiar, mine in the way that anything you grew up looking at is yours. My grandfather had kept it swept for years after the last keeper left, out of some professional courtesy between men of lamps, and then stopped, and then it became a ruin with a good view, and ruins are comfortable neighbors. Ruins don't smoke.

"Probably a writer," I said. "They rent places like that in summer. They're harmless."

"Mm," said Mrs. Adeyemi, in the tone of a woman filing my naivety under a specific date for future reference.

I paid for my stamps, mailed the manuscript, mailed the letter — W's address written in my careful hand, the post office box number I have never once investigated further, Rule Two holding firm — and walked out into a morning so bright it felt like a personal remark.

* * *

The dunes between my house and the cottage belong to no one, which around here means they belong to everyone's opinions. I took the long way home along their spine, as I do most days, partly for the walk and partly because from the crest you can see both houses at once: mine, low and grey-shingled behind its windbreak of sea buckthorn, and beyond it, past the collapsed fence line, the keeper's cottage with its new slate patches glinting like coins on an old coat.

Smoke stood up from the chimney in a thin, deliberate column. Someone had lit a fire in June, which meant either the man was fragile or the house was damp, and judging by the state of that house, both.

I told myself I wasn't going to look. I looked. This is another thing about rules — they're excellent until they intersect with curiosity, at which point they become less laws than suggestions with good posture.

He was in the garden. Or what passed for one: a rectangle of ground the cottage had surrendered to nettles sometime during the previous administration. He was pulling the nettles. Bare-handed, which told me something, though I couldn't have said what — that he was tough, or careless, or the kind of man who learns things the hard way and keeps choosing the hard way out of loyalty to it. He was tall in a stooped sort of way, dark-haired, wearing a shirt that had been white in some former life, and he worked without music, without phone, without any of the apparatus people now carry like organs. Just him and the nettles and the wind off the water.

I should have walked on. Any sensible practitioner of containment walks on. Instead I stood on the dune crest for what I judged to be a defensible interval — a passerby might pause, might admire the view, might be composing a paragraph — and watched him work.

And then, without warning, without turning around, without any earthly way of knowing I was there, the man straightened up, looked directly at the spot where I stood, and raised one dirt-darkened hand. Not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. The gesture of someone saying: *I see you seeing me. We can leave it there.*

I walked home faster than dignity allows, went inside, and stood in my kitchen with my heart doing something unscheduled against my ribs.

Ridiculous, I told it. He's a neighbor. Neighbors acknowledge neighbors. It's practically contractual.

But the heart, as I have said, is a muscle, and muscles have memory, and mine was remembering something I hadn't felt in ten years — the specific jolt of the first letter from W, that envelope with no return address, the sense of being *seen* by someone who had no business seeing me. I had spent a decade building a life where that feeling arrived safely, annually, in paper form, on my terms.

Now it was standing in a ruined garden next door, pulling nettles with its bare hands.

I made tea I didn't want and sat with the notebook open to the rules, reading them like scripture, like a spell, like a woman checking the locks twice. Rule Two: love at a distance you can survive. Fine. He was forty yards away. Distances don't come more survivable.

Outside, the smoke rose straight up in the windless air, thin and patient, like a question being asked very quietly, over and over, of no one in particular.
