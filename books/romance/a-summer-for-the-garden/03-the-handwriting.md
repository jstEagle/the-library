## 3. The Handwriting

There are three kinds of mail that come to a woman like me, which is to say a woman who has arranged her life the way one arranges a museum: nothing may be touched, but everything must be dusted.

The first kind is bills, which I pay on the day they arrive, because a debt left overnight is a small anarchy. The second kind is the publishing correspondence, which arrives in fat manila envelopes that smell faintly of other people's ambition. And the third kind — one letter a year, every year, for ten years — comes from W.

I want to be precise about W, because precision is the only devotion I've ever been good at. W's letters arrive in the first week of June, always. They are written on unlined paper, in an ink that leans blue-black, in a hand that slants left as though the words are trying to escape the page and have to be gently restrained. The letters are long, funny in a dry way that never announces itself as humor, and they never, in ten years, contain a name, an address, a photograph, a phone number, or a single verifiable fact about a life. What they contain is the other thing. The true thing. The thing I cannot get from any face.

I have built an entire self out of that handwriting. This is not an exaggeration. When my editor asked me, years ago, why my prose had changed — gotten braver, she said, gotten stranger — I almost told her the truth: because a stranger on paper taught me that a sentence could be a room with the door left open.

So understand, then, what it means that on a gray Tuesday in the middle of June, I found a returned parcel in my own mailbox, addressed in that handwriting, and it was not addressed to me.

* * *

It started, as most catastrophes in my life have started, with the postman's mistake.

My mailbox sits at the end of the gravel lane that serves both my grandfather's old house — mine now, all lighthouse and draft and memory — and the keeper's cottage next door, the one that has stood empty for eleven years, since the last of the coastal trust's caretakers gave up on it and moved inland to somewhere with functioning plumbing. The lane ends at the road. One box, two properties. For a decade this was no problem at all. Now there was a stranger in the cottage, and the stranger, apparently, received mail.

I had been watching him, I'll admit it. From the dunes, with the binoculars my grandfather used for counting seals, which sounds sinister and was, in my defense, only moderately sinister. He had arrived three weeks ago in a car with out-of-county plates and a trunk full of boxes, and he had done nothing since but repair things. The cottage had been a ruin — sagging porch, windows like cataracts — and this man, tall and dark and moving with the careful economy of someone who has learned not to trust his own body, had begun to mend it. I watched him replace a window sash. I watched him sit on the porch at dusk with a notebook, writing, and I felt something I refused to name, because the last time I named feelings promptly they got out and reproduced.

He kept to himself. He did not come to the village shop. He did not attend the festival committee's first meeting of the season, which everyone attends, including people who hate the festival, because hating the festival together is half the point of it. He bought his supplies, I learned from Mrs. Adeyemi at the shop, in one large fortnightly sweep, paying cash, and when she asked his name he said, "Vale," and nothing else, in the tone of a man handing over a counterfeit bill.

Vale. I turned the name over for two weeks like a stone looking for what lived under it. It told me nothing. That itself was a kind of information. People who give you one name are people whose other names have cost them something.

But none of that — not the plates, not the cash, not the notebook, not even the way he watched the sea the way you watch a person who owes you money — none of it prepared me for the parcel.

* * *

It was raining, the fine sideways rain that the coast specializes in, the kind that isn't weather so much as the sea trying to get a word in. I walked down the lane to the mailbox under my grandfather's old oilskin, and there, wedged behind the box's little door at an angle that suggested the postman had given up on it, was a brown paper parcel, roughly the size of a hardcover book, with a red-and-white striped sticker across one corner.

RETURN TO SENDER. ADDRESSEE UNKNOWN.

I nearly left it. Let me be clear about that. My first instinct — the instinct I have spent ten years and one published novel cultivating — was to leave it, to walk back up the lane, and to let the post office sort out its own errors, because other people's mail is other people's lives and I do not open doors I haven't been invited through. Containment. That's the word I use. Rule three, if you're counting, and I am always counting: *Don't go looking for what has agreed to find you.*

I even turned to go.

But the rain was coming in at an angle that found the back of my neck, and the parcel was going to soak, and soaked parcels get returned to the depot and mislabeled and lost, and — this is what I told myself, standing in the rain, lying to myself with the fluency of long practice — it would be *responsible* to at least check the address. To make sure it wasn't mine. Postmen make mistakes. The handwriting was slanted, after all. Anyone could misread a slant.

I looked at the address.

The handwriting slanted left. The letters were narrow and restrained, each one leaning back from the one before it as if under gentle arrest, the *y*s descending in a long unbroken stroke, the cross of the *t* drawn once, decisively, like a man signing something he has decided not to take back.

I know this handwriting the way you know your own heartbeat: not by studying it, but by the fact that it has been going on inside you, unnoticed and essential, for ten years.

I have forty-one letters from W in a locked drawer in my desk, and I have read them so many times that I could reproduce the hand from memory — the particular way the capital *W* is formed, like two valleys and a mountain between them; the *f* that crosses both its own lines; the small, stubborn dot over every *i*, placed exactly centered, as though even the punctuation refuses to be careless. I once spent an entire insomniac winter, in the bad year, comparing letterforms the way other people compare faces of celebrities they think they've met. I know this writing. It has been the steadiest voice in my life.

And it was on a parcel addressed to *I. Vale, The Old Keeper's Cottage, [the lane], [the village]*.

I stood in the rain for a long time. Long enough that the oilskin gave up at the shoulders. Long enough that the parcel's brown paper went dark and soft at the corners under my fingers.

Here is what my mind did, in that time, in order:

First, it said: *coincidence.* Two people in the world with that handwriting; one of them happens to have moved into the ruined cottage next door. The universe is large. Handwriting is not fingerprints. I am a novelist; I know how coincidence is supposed to be handled — sparingly, and never at the load-bearing points.

Second, it said: *the postman misread the return address.* W's letters come from somewhere different each year — a postmark in the mountains one June, a city the next, once, memorably, from a port town I looked up and found was four hundred miles from anywhere I've ever been. Perhaps W had sent something to this address years ago, to the trust, to a previous caretaker, and it had finally come back. Perhaps. The sticker said ADDRESSEE UNKNOWN, though, and the address on the front was current, was *now*, was the cottage with the new window sash and the man on the porch with the notebook.

Third — and this is the one I have not been able to put down since — my mind said: *he knows where you live.*

Because here is the thing I have never written down anywhere, the thing that has lived in my chest for ten years like a bird I refused to name so it couldn't be caged: W has always known more than W has said. The letters never ask questions I haven't already answered in mine, never reference a place I haven't mentioned. But once, in the seventh year, W wrote — in passing, in a postscript, the way the important things always arrive — *"I hope the light on the headland is still lit for the festival. Some things should not be allowed to go dark."* I had mentioned the lighthouse. I had never mentioned the festival, or that the light was ceremonially relit each year, or that my grandfather had been the last keeper. W knew. W had always known slightly more than I had given, the way you know more about a person than you've been told, if you have been paying attention from close enough and long enough.

I had decided it was tenderness. Attention as a form of love. I had decided this because the alternative was unbearable, and I am, above all things, a woman who has organized her life around the avoidance of the unbearable.

Now there was a man three hundred meters from my house who received mail in W's hand, and the alternative was standing on the porch of the keeper's cottage, and I could see its chimney from where I stood, and there was no smoke coming out of it, and somehow that was worse. A cold chimney. A man who mends windows and writes in notebooks and does not exist in any register of the village, receiving parcels in the handwriting that raised me.

* * *

I took the parcel inside. I want to be honest: I told myself I was keeping it safe for him. I set it on the kitchen table, on the oilcloth, and I dried it with a tea towel the way you'd dry a found animal, and I made tea I did not drink, and I sat across from it for the better part of two hours while the rain worked through its repertoire.

I did not open it. Rule one, the first rule, the rule underneath all the others: *What is given freely may be kept; what is taken is never yours.* Ten years of letters have been given freely. Whatever was in that parcel had been sent to a man whose name I didn't have until three weeks ago, and it had been sent by a hand I trusted more than any face I've ever known, and the two facts were sitting on my kitchen table together refusing to be reconciled, and I could not open it, because opening it would make me a person who opens things.

But I could look at the return address. The return address is not a violation. The return address is public information, practically a greeting.

There was no return address.

Of course there was no return address. There has never been a return address, in ten years, not once. W writes from postmarks and vanishes behind them. The absence was so familiar it was almost a signature in itself, and that familiarity was the worst part, because it meant this was not a stranger's parcel. This was a parcel from *my* correspondent, from the one relationship in my life that has never demanded a face, and it was addressed to the stranger, and I was not the stranger, and for the first time in ten years I looked at W's handwriting and felt something other than safety.

I felt, specifically, the way you feel when you come home and the light is on and you know you turned it off.

* * *

Here is what I did next, and I record it without pride, because pride is for people whose curiosity hasn't cost them anything yet.

I got the forty-one letters out of the locked drawer. I put them on the table next to the parcel, in their bundle, tied with the ribbon from the first one, because I am sentimental in exactly one compartment of my life and I keep it locked for a reason. And I compared. Properly. The way I compare nothing else in my life, with a patience I have never once extended to a human being standing in front of me.

The *W* — two valleys, a mountain. Identical. The *f* crossing both lines. Identical. The centered dots, the decisive *t*, the leftward lean like words being restrained. Identical, identical, identical, down to the pressure of the pen, which I could read in the parcel's address the way you can read mood in a knock on a door: firm, unhurried, a hand that knows what it's writing and has decided to write it anyway.

And then I found the one difference, because there is always one difference, and the difference is where the truth hides.

In my letters, the address — my name and this lane, this village — is written in the same hand as everything else, but *slower*. I don't know how else to describe it. The letters are the same shapes but the pressure is different, lighter, as though writing my name required a different kind of care than writing anything else. Ten years I thought it was tenderness. Tonight, with the parcel on the table, I considered, for the first time, that it might have been something else. Guilt, maybe. The particular pressure of a hand writing an address it has reasons to be careful about.

On the parcel, my lane and my village — his lane, now, apparently, his village — were written at the same speed as everything else. No care. No weight. Just an address.

*I. Vale* had been written the way you write a name that isn't really yours.

I put the letters away. All of them. I locked the drawer, and I stood in my kitchen with my hands flat on the oilcloth, breathing the way my grandfather taught me to breathe in a squall — slow, at the bottom of the breath, where the panic can't reach — and I made myself say the possibilities out loud, because saying things out loud is how I keep them from breeding.

"One," I said. "W and this man are the same person, and he has moved three hundred meters from my house without telling me, and has now received a parcel in his own hand addressed to a false name."

The kitchen said nothing. The rain said nothing. The sea, far off, said the thing the sea always says, which is *I know, I know, I know.*

"Two," I said. "W and this man know each other. Correspondents, colleagues, family. The handwriting is a coincidence of intimacy, which is somehow worse."

The rain intensified, as though taking notes.

"Three," I said, and this one came out smaller, because it was the one I was afraid of, the one I'd been circling for two hours like a bird around a lit window: "W has always known where I live. W has always known slightly more than I gave. And now W's handwriting has arrived at my nearest neighbor's door, and either I have spent ten years writing my heart to a man who was already watching me —"

I stopped. Because the fourth possibility was the one underneath all the others, the one I couldn't say out loud even alone, even to a kitchen, even in the rain: that the letters had never been what I thought they were. That the room with the open door had been a room with a mirror in it. That the woman I became through his words had been built by someone who could see her the whole time, and had chosen, year after year, to stay behind the wall.

I am not a woman who is loved carelessly. I know that. I built it that way on purpose. But I am apparently a woman who can be *known* carelessly, and there is a difference, and the difference was standing in the rain on my table in brown paper with a striped sticker on it.

* * *

I slept, or something adjacent to sleep, and in the morning the rain had gone and the light was that washed, rinsed coastal light that makes everything look forgiven, which is a lie the coast tells daily.

I took the parcel back down the lane. This was, I told myself, the responsible act. Return it. Say nothing. Watch his face when he sees the handwriting — because I would see his face, that was the point, that was the whole point, and I am a novelist, and faces are supposed to be my trade.

He was on the porch when I came up the cottage path, doing something patient to the hinge of the storm door, and up close — I had only ever seen him through binoculars, through glass, through distance, all my preferred media — he was worse than the binoculars had prepared me for. Tall, dark-haired, with the kind of face that looks like it's been edited for length, and eyes that did something I can only describe as *bracing* when he saw me, the way a man braces when a wave he's been watching finally arrives.

"Parcel," I said, and held it out, and my voice was admirably level, given that my entire interior architecture was on fire. "It was left in my box by mistake. The rain was getting at it."

He took it. And I watched — I gave myself full permission to watch, this once, openly, the way I had watched for three weeks through glass — and I saw it happen. I saw his eyes go to the handwriting. I saw the exact moment he recognized it, which was not the moment of a man seeing his own hand; it was the moment of a man seeing something he thought was buried come up out of the ground. His thumb moved over the striped sticker. The hinge tool in his other hand went very still.

"Thank you," he said. His voice was low and careful, a voice that had been managed. "That was kind."

"It was practical," I said. "It's what the box is for."

And then, because I am apparently not the woman I have spent ten years constructing, because the rules were already smoking somewhere behind me, I said:

"It's striking handwriting. Very distinctive. The left slant. You don't see that often."

Something crossed his face. I will spend the rest of this book trying to describe it and I will never quite succeed. It was not guilt exactly, and it was not fear exactly; it was the expression of a man watching a door he had nailed shut discover, with some interest, that it had never been a door at all but a window, and that someone had been standing outside it for some time.

"Do you write?" I asked him. Sweetly. The sweetest thing I have said to another human being in a decade, and it was a knife.

He looked at me for a long moment, and the sea filled the silence the way it always does, and I understood that he was deciding something, and I understood — with a dread that arrived all at once, whole, like a wave that has been assembling itself for ten years offshore — that whatever he decided, I had already lost something in standing on this path asking.

"Everyone writes," he said finally. "Some of us just get mail about it."

And he went inside, with the parcel, and closed the door, gently, the way you close a door on a room where something is sleeping.

I walked back up the lane in the rinsed light, and my hands were steady, and my mind was not, and the thought that carried me home, that sat down inside me and refused to leave, was this:

He hadn't asked my name. Three weeks, one path, one mailbox between us — and he had never once asked my name. As though he already had it. As though he had been reading it, once a year, every June, for ten years, in his own careful, restrained, leftward-leaning hand.

The festival was six weeks away. The light on the headland would be relit, the way it always was, the way W had once written it should never be allowed to go dark. And I was going to find out what was in the parcel, and who I. Vale was, and what a man does with a decade of a woman's letters — but first, I was going to stand in my kitchen and admit the thing I had been refusing to admit since the rain:

I was not afraid he was a stranger.

I was afraid he wasn't.
