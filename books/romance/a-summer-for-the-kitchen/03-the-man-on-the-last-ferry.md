## 3. The Man on the Last Ferry

Elias Marchetti had eaten his way through forty-one countries, cooked for presidents and pop stars, and once talked a Milanese food critic out of a zero-star review with nothing but a plate of braised short ribs and twenty minutes of charm. He was not, by any reasonable measure, a man who frightened easily.

And yet here he was, thirty-eight years old and famous enough that strangers recognized him in airports, standing at the rail of the *Marrow Belle* like a man being ferried to his own execution, watching a gray-green coastline assemble itself out of June fog.

"You look green," said the ferry captain, who had introduced herself an hour ago as Captain Ruiz and had not stopped chewing toothpick since. "Seasick?"

"No."

"Scared of small towns?"

"No." He adjusted the strap of his knife roll — the one bag he'd insisted on carrying himself, because you didn't check your knives, and you especially didn't check the knives your grandmother gave you when you were nineteen and still called risotto "rice soup." "I've just never been to Marrow Bay before."

That was a lie, and it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Captain Ruiz looked at him the way experienced women look at men who are lying badly. "Everybody says that," she said. "First time in Marrow Bay. And then they get off the boat and somebody hollers their name from the dock, and it turns out their cousin married into the Pell family or their mother summered here in '89. Nobody's really a stranger here. They just haven't been introduced yet."

"I'm judging a competition," Elias said. "Three days. Then I'm gone."

"Sure," said Captain Ruiz, and spat her toothpick over the rail with the serenity of a woman who had heard every promise a visitor ever made and believed approximately none of them.

* * *

He should have said no to this trip. That was the honest accounting, and he'd been doing a lot of honest accounting lately, mostly at three in the morning in hotel rooms that all smelled the same.

The burnout had come first, though he hadn't named it that. You couldn't name burnout when you were Elias Marchetti, when your face was on the side of buses, when your cookbook was in its ninth printing, when restaurants in three cities carried your name like a blessing. Burnout was for line cooks, for people who hadn't made it. What he had was — he'd told his manager — *fatigue*. A creative fatigue. A need to reconnect with why he started cooking in the first place. The kind of language that sounded good in press releases.

Then the scandal, which did not have good language.

It wasn't even a real scandal, which was the humiliating part. A former sous chef — Marcus, talented, ambitious, and lately very chatty with a food writer — had given an interview claiming that Elias's flagship restaurant ran on fear, that Elias had thrown a sheet pan at a wall during service, that the celebrated warmth of Marchetti cuisine was a performance for cameras while behind the pass he was "a different man entirely."

The sheet pan was true. The wall was true. The rest was true too, if you squinted, and Elias had spent two weeks doing damage control and one very bad night doing something worse: looking in the mirror above a hotel bathroom sink and asking whether Marcus was wrong.

His publicist had suggested the competition. *Small town, charming, great optics. You go, you judge some lobster rolls, you remind America that you're the nice one.* And Elias, who could not remember the last time he'd been the nice one anywhere, including his own kitchen, had said yes before he finished thinking about it.

Which is how he came to be reading the assignment email again on his phone, standing in the ferry's cabin because the wind off the water had teeth, when he saw the name of the host venue for the first time in full.

**Competition headquarters and welcome dinner hosted by Winterbourne Books, 14 Harbor Street, Marrow Bay.**

He read it twice. Three times. His thumb hovered over the screen as if he could swipe the words away.

Winterbourne.

No. It was a common enough name. There were Winterbournes everywhere, probably. It was practically a generic brand of English surname, like Smith, like Walker. The odds that the only bookshop in a fishing town of four thousand people was owned by —

The ferry rounded the headland, and Marrow Bay opened up below him like a hand uncurling: a crescent harbor full of boats, a main street climbing away from the water in crooked increments, church steeple, water tower, gulls wheeling and complaining overhead. And there, on Harbor Street, close enough to the dock that he could read the painted letters on the window even from the water, a storefront with a deep green awning and gold lettering:

**WINTERBOURNE BOOKS**

His stomach dropped so fast and so completely that he genuinely wondered, for one surreal moment, whether the ferry had hit something.

"Rough crossing after all?" Captain Ruiz asked, appearing beside him with fresh toothpick.

"I know the owner," Elias said. His voice came out strange. Flat. "Or — I knew her. Twenty years ago."

Ruiz chewed. Considered. "Beatrix?"

He turned to look at her.

"She owns the bookshop," Ruiz said, with the mild satisfaction of a woman confirming a bet with herself. "Quiet one. Keeps to herself, mostly. Got a little girl, Wren — hell of a kid, does the story hour on Saturdays, has the whole dock memorizing sea shanties." She squinted at him. "How'd you know Beatrix?"

"We went to culinary school together," Elias said.

Something flickered across the captain's face — quick, there and gone, like a fish under a boat. "Huh," she said. "She never mentioned culinary school."

There it was. Twenty years later, and the wound was still right where he'd left it, still bleeding through other people's casual sentences. *She never mentioned culinary school.* As if those two years at Aldercrest Academy had been cut out of her life with scissors, as if he had been cut out with them, and maybe he had been, maybe that was exactly what he deserved.

"Well," said Captain Ruiz, clapping him on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him. "Welcome to Marrow Bay."

* * *

The dock was chaos in the gentlest possible way. The competition committee had turned out in force — a woman named Dot Pell who shook his hand like she was testing his grip strength, a man from the chamber of commerce who kept calling him "the celebrity" as though Elias might forget, two teenagers holding a banner that read WELCOME CHEF MARCHETTI in glitter letters, one S already peeling loose and drifting toward the harbor.

Elias smiled the smile. The camera smile, the bus-side smile, the one that lived separately from his actual face. He posed for photos. He said the things: honored to be here, what a beautiful town, can't wait to taste what your cooks bring. Dot Pell informed him that the welcome dinner would be held tonight at seven, at the bookshop, catered by half the town, and that he'd be staying at the Harborside Inn once the ferry traffic cleared.

"The bookshop," Elias said carefully. "The dinner's at the bookshop."

"Winterbourne's got the biggest room in town," Dot said. "She's closing early for us. First time in I don't know how long she's closed for anything but weather." She patted his arm. "You'll love her. Salt of the earth."

He doubted, sincerely, that Beatrix Winterbourne would describe him as salt of anything.

He killed two hours the way he always killed time in new towns: walking. Marrow Bay rewarded walking. He passed a diner called Ruthie's with pie in the window and a handwritten sign arguing with itself about whether the blueberry or the rhubarb was superior (current score: blueberry 1, rhubarb 0, *this means war*). He passed a hardware store with a dog asleep in a wheelbarrow. He passed children selling something from a folding table — he didn't stop to find out what; in coastal towns it was best not to ask — and an old man mending a net who nodded at him without recognition, which was, Elias realized with a start, the most relaxed he'd felt in two years.

Nobody knew him here. For one afternoon, he was just a tall man in a good coat who walked like his feet hurt.

He bought coffee at a place called the Percolator, where the barista — a college-age kid with flour on his apron — took his order without a flicker of recognition and gave him change without a flicker of flattery, and Elias stood at the window drinking it and let himself imagine, briefly, dangerously, that this trip could be simple. Judge the competition. Eat some lobster. Go home. Reconnect with why he started cooking.

Except the why he'd started cooking had a name, and the name was on a green awning fifty yards up the street, and the why had hated him for twenty years, and he had earned every day of it.

Because here was the thing Elias had never told anyone, the thing he kept locked in the same internal room where he kept his grandmother's knives and his father's voicemail and every other inheritance he didn't know how to put down:

He knew what people believed about the fire. He knew that the official story — the story Aldercrest told its donors, the story the newspapers printed, the story that followed Beatrix Winterbourne out of the academy and into the dark — was that Beatrix set it. Jealousy, they said. Rivalry curdled. The quiet girl with the brilliant palate, pushed too far by the golden boy's rise.

And Elias had let them believe it. Worse: he had *confirmed* it, in his grief and his shock and his youth, nodding along when Chef Odile took his arm in the smoking aftermath and said, softly, so only he could hear, *"I saw her, Elias. I'm so sorry. I saw her near the storeroom before it started. Some people can't bear to watch someone else shine."*

He had been twenty years old and hollowed out and Odile Vasseur had built him, trained him, believed in him since he was seventeen, and when she handed him a story he took it, because the alternative was unthinkable. Because the alternative meant the woman who loved him best in the world had done a monstrous thing, and he couldn't survive that, so he survived the smaller lie instead.

By the time he was old enough to understand what Odile had actually been — what she'd confessed to him, years later, drunk on anniversary wine, laughing about how she'd *saved* him, how she'd pruned the competition before it could choke him — it was too late. Beatrix was gone. Vanished. No forwarding address, no trail, nothing but a reputation in ashes. And his own career was rising on the thermals of that fire like it had been launched from it, which, in every way that mattered, it had.

He had told himself for twenty years that he'd look for her. That he'd make it right. He had told himself this the way you tell yourself you'll learn Italian, or call your brother, or start running: sincerely, often, and without ever once doing it.

And now the universe, with its terrible sense of humor, had delivered him to her doorstep with a glitter banner and a TV crew arriving Thursday.

Elias drained his coffee, crushed the cup, and stood in the middle of Harbor Street like a man deciding whether to walk into the sea.

* * *

He told himself he wouldn't go early. He had three hours before the dinner. He would check into the inn, shower, change, compose himself, arrive at seven with his armor on and his smile loaded.

Instead he found himself, at ten past five, standing in front of Winterbourne Books with his hands in his pockets, reading the window display like it was a menu.

It was a good display. That was the first thing that hit him — not dread, not longing, but professional admiration. Someone had built a table around the theme of *food in fiction*, and built it well: cookbooks fanned like playing cards, novels propped at angles, a chalkboard sign in handwriting so precise it looked typeset — **EVERY GREAT LOVE STORY HAS A KITCHEN IN IT SOMEWHERE.**

He knew that handwriting.

Twenty years, and he knew it instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall. The sharp ascenders. The way she crossed her T's like she was signing a treaty. He had watched that handwriting for two years across the demo bench at Aldercrest, taking notes faster than anyone, annotating recipes in margins, once — he'd never forgotten it, God help him — writing *you're showing off, Marchetti* on a scrap of paper and sliding it across the stainless steel mid-lecture, and he'd kept that scrap of paper in his wallet for six years until the ink faded to a ghost.

Through the window, he could see the shop's interior: warm lamplight, tall shelves, a rolling ladder. People moving inside — setting up folding tables, carrying chairs, stringing lights. Preparing for his welcome dinner. And moving among them, directing them with small economical gestures, a woman with dark hair streaked prematurely silver at the temples, wearing an apron over a sweater, laughing at something an older man said.

Laughing.

Elias's chest did something complicated and painful, like a knot tied tighter by a careful hand.

She was beautiful. That was the second thing that hit him, right behind the handwriting. Time had been — not kind, exactly, but *honest* with her. It had carved lines around her mouth and silvered her hair and put a gravity in her posture that the girl he'd known had never had, and the sum total of it was a woman who looked like she had survived something and built a life in the wreckage, and Elias stood on the sidewalk of Harbor Street wanting, with a want that scared him more than any scandal ever had, to be allowed inside it.

The bell over the door chimed when he pushed it open. He hadn't decided to do it. His body had simply mutinied, the way it used to back when he was brave.

Warmth rolled over him — old paper and lemon polish and, underneath it, faintly, unmistakably, bread. Someone in this building had baked bread today, and the smell went through Elias like a blade through dough, straight and clean, all the way back to a summer kitchen with copper pots and a fan turning slow.

Beatrix was on a stepladder, pinning a paper lantern to a beam. She didn't turn around. "If you're here for the romance display, it's not up yet," she called down, in a voice pitched for retail, easy and bright. "Dot keeps threatening to alphabetize it by emotional damage, but — "

She turned. She saw him.

The lantern slipped from her fingers. He caught it — reflexes, twenty years of catching falling things in kitchens — and for a moment they were both frozen, him holding a paper lantern in a bookshop doorway, her on the ladder staring down at him with an expression he'd seen exactly once before, across a smoke-filled parking lot, the night everything ended.

"Elias," she said. Not a greeting. An autopsy.

"Hello, Bea," he said, and heard his own voice crack on the nickname like he was twenty again, like no time had passed at all, like the last two decades were a dish he'd forgotten to take off the heat.

From somewhere deeper in the shop, a child's voice — bright, curious, already approaching — called out:

"Mom? Who's that?"
