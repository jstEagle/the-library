## 2. A Stranger's Handwriting

The contract was on the table between them like a card someone had already played.

Wren Calloway didn't touch it. She'd learned that much in six years of land surveying — the person who reaches first wants it more, and the person who wants it more gets paid less. So she sat in the leather chair across from Marcus Vane's desk, in the forty-first-floor office with a view of the harbor that cost more per month than her flat did per year, and she let him look at her instead.

Vane was good at looking. He had the kind of face that had been arranged rather than grown — silver at the temples, a jaw you could set a level against, eyes the pale grey of a valuation report. He wore his charm the way some men wore cologne: generously, and always slightly too much of it.

"Merrow's Reach," he said, letting the name sit in the air. "You know it?"

"I know of it." She crossed her legs. "Island off the north coast. Ferry twice a day in summer, once in winter if the weather allows. Fishing, farming, one pub, one church, and a population that's been halving every decade since the nineties."

"Which is precisely the problem." Vane leaned back, lacing his fingers over his waistcoat. "And precisely the opportunity. Halloway has secured options on three parcels along the eastern shore. What we need — what *I* need — is a full topographical and soil survey of the coastal holdings, plus valuations on the remaining private land. The orchard especially."

"The vineyard," Wren said. "Brightwater."

Something flickered behind Vane's eyes — surprise, quickly filed away. "You've done your homework."

"It's my job to do my homework." It was also, though she didn't say this, that she'd spent two nights reading everything she could find about Merrow's Reach after the preliminary brief landed in her inbox. Old newspaper clippings about the Brightwater family. A grainy photograph from 1987 of an old man and a boy standing between rows of apple trees, both squinting into the sun as if it owed them money. The boy would be Emrys Brightwater now. Twenty-nine, maybe thirty. Sole owner, sole employee, sole inheritor of a hundred and forty acres of debt.

"Then you'll know it's not a straightforward job." Vane slid a folder across the table. This time she took it, because refusing twice would be theater, and Wren didn't do theater. Inside were maps, aerial photographs, a spreadsheet with figures already filled in — figures that made her eyebrows rise despite herself. "The islanders are sentimental about their dirt. They'll resist. Some of them will resist *stupidly*. Our timeline doesn't accommodate stupid resistance."

"Timelines rarely do."

"So we arrive early. We arrive quietly. We learn the ground before anyone knows we're standing on it." He smiled, and it was a good smile, calibrated to the millimeter. "Which brings me to the second part of the assignment. The part I suspect you'll find quaint."

He told her about the pen-pal program then. Wren listened with her face carefully neutral while something inside her tilted, very slightly, like a picture frame knocked by a passing truck.

It was called the Merrow's Reach Letter Circle, and it had been running for eleven years, founded by the postmistress after the ferry route was cut back and the island began to feel itself thinning out. The idea was simple: mainlanders paid a small fee, were assigned an island correspondent, and wrote letters through the winter and summer months. Half the participants, Vane explained, were retirees and romantics and lonely people who liked the idea of a stranger on an island. The other half were former islanders keeping a thread tied to home.

"And the third half," Wren said, "is developers using it as reconnaissance."

Vane laughed — a real laugh, which was worse than a fake one. "I knew I hired well. Yes. The program is administered through the post office, assignments are anonymous, and no one checks credentials. You sign up under your own name — that's required, for the postmistress's records — but your correspondent never learns it unless you tell them. You'll be on the island weeks before any official announcement. A surveyor who arrives early draws attention. A woman who comes because she's writing letters to a fisherman draws none."

"You want me to extract intelligence through correspondence."

"I want you to *build rapport*." He said the word like it was a tool he kept in a drawer. "These people will tell a letter-friend things they'd never tell a man with a clipboard. Who's struggling. Who's desperate. Who'd sell tomorrow if the price was kind. Who'll fight to the last fence post. That last category matters most — I want to know who we're fighting before we fight them."

Wren looked down at the folder. The aerial photograph on top showed the orchard in high summer, the rows running green and orderly toward a coastline that looked, from that height, like something drawn by a child with a blue crayon. Beautiful. Legible. Valuable.

"And if I decline the letter-writing portion?"

"Then I give the whole contract to Devlin & Marsh, who'll subcontract it to three junior associates and produce exactly the kind of report that gets us sued." Vane spread his hands. "Wren. You're the best coastal surveyor on the mainland. Your numbers hold up in court, your reports don't leak, and you have the rare ability to walk a field and see both its past and its price. I'm offering you a fee that clears your student loans twice over and a credit line on a project that will make your name. All I'm asking in return is that you write some letters."

*Some letters.* As if it were nothing. As if words were weightless.

She thought about her overdraft. She thought about the promotion she'd been passed over for in March because the director's nephew needed a title more than she did. She thought about the flat with the mold in the corner of the bedroom ceiling that her landlord swore was condensation.

"Fee plus expenses," she said. "And my valuations are mine. If the data says a parcel is worth more than your spreadsheet hopes, I report the data."

"Of course." Vane's smile didn't move but something behind it recalibrated. "I'd expect nothing less from a professional."

Later, walking to the tube with the folder under her arm, Wren tried to locate the unease she could feel sitting somewhere beneath her sternum and couldn't quite name it. It wasn't the spying — she'd done competitor research before, boundary disputes where both sides hired her firm at different times, and she'd learned to keep her conscience and her invoices in separate drawers. It wasn't even the deception, exactly.

It was the letters themselves. She hadn't written a letter in nine years. Her mother used to say she had her grandmother's hand — a slanted, impatient script that leaned forward like it was trying to get somewhere faster than the sentence allowed. Somewhere in a box in storage there were letters her grandmother had written her, back when Wren was small enough to read them aloud with a finger under each word. She couldn't remember the last time she'd thought of that box.

She got on the train. The folder sat on her knees. Outside the window, London scrolled past in brick and glass, and somewhere beyond all of it an island waited with its crayon-blue coast, and a man she'd never met was standing in his grandfather's orchard not knowing yet that she was coming.

* * *

The ferry left from a concrete slipway at the end of a road that stopped pretending to be a road about ten miles before the water.

Wren arrived ninety minutes early, out of habit and out of something she refused to call nerves. The morning was grey and mild, the sea the color of unpolished pewter, and the vessel itself — the *Kestrel*, according to the peeling paint on its hull — was smaller than she'd expected: a workhorse ferry with a car deck, a passenger cabin that smelled of diesel and old upholstery, and a queue of maybe fifteen people loading crates and bicycles and one extremely indifferent sheep.

She bought a ticket from a kiosk run by a woman who looked her up and down with the frank appraisal of someone who catalogued strangers for a living.

"Visiting or coming home?" the woman asked.

"Working," Wren said.

"Mm." The woman handed over the ticket. "Everyone says that. The island decides what you are eventually." And she turned to the next customer as though she hadn't just said something that would lodge in Wren's chest like a splinter.

The crossing took fifty minutes. Wren claimed a seat by the window in the nearly empty cabin, opened the folder, and pretended to study contour lines until the mainland shrank to a brown smudge and then to nothing at all. Then she closed the folder.

In her bag, wrapped in a paper sleeve from the stationery shop where she'd stopped on the way to the ferry — a real shop, wooden counters, a bell over the door, the proprietor so astonished to sell writing paper to anyone under sixty that he'd given her a discount — was a packet of cream-laid notepaper and a fountain pen she'd owned for six years and never used.

Her assignment letter from the postmistress — forwarded through Vane's office, which meant Vane had read it, which meant she should assume he'd read everything — gave her a box number and a name. Not a full name. Just a designation, the way the program worked: *Your correspondent for the season is Correspondent No. 14. Letters may be addressed care of Merrow's Reach Post Office.*

No. 14. She'd been No. 9. The symmetry of small numbers pleased her in a way she distrusted.

There was no information about who No. 14 was. That was the point, apparently. Strangers, honestly met. She'd asked Vane's assistant whether they could at least confirm the correspondent lived near the eastern parcels, and the assistant had checked, and come back looking faintly embarrassed: the postmistress didn't release that, and the postmistress, everyone agreed, was not a person you pushed.

So Wren knew nothing except that somewhere on this island, a person had signed up to write to a stranger, expecting a retiree or a romantic or a homesick expat, and was going to receive her instead.

The ferry rose and fell. Through the salt-hazed window, Merrow's Reach assembled itself out of the sea — first a dark line, then hills, then the grey stone bulk of the village clustered around its harbour like people huddled against weather. Gulls tracked the boat, screaming their opinions. The sheep on the car deck bleated once, philosophically.

Wren took out the notepaper. Uncapped the pen. And discovered that her hands, which could hold a theodolite steady in a thirty-knot wind, had gone slightly uncertain around a nib.

*Dear Correspondent No. 14,* she wrote, and stopped.

What was she supposed to be? Vane wanted a friendly ear, a soft interrogation, a slow map of the island's weak points drawn in ink that looked like affection. She could do that. She was good at being whatever a room required — she'd been doing it since she was nineteen and realized that competence alone didn't get you invited into meetings. But the pen hovered over the page and she found she couldn't start with the performance. Something in her — professional pride, or the ghost of her grandmother's handwriting, or just the strange nakedness of writing to someone who couldn't see her face — insisted on beginning true.

*Dear Correspondent No. 14,

I'm told we're to be pen-pals for the season, which means you're about to receive letters from a complete stranger, and I've decided the only fair thing is to be honest about the kind of stranger I am.*

The ferry engine changed pitch. They were coming into the lee of the island now; the water smoothed, and the gulls' cries came clearer through the hull.

*I'm not retired. I'm not homesick. I'm not writing because I believe in the romance of the postal service, although I'm beginning to suspect I might be about to. I'm a surveyor — I measure land for a living — and I've taken work that brings me to your island this summer. I won't pretend that's not why I signed up for this program. But I'll tell you something I haven't told my employer: I grew up in a house that got sold out from under us when I was fourteen. A developer bought the street, doubled the rents, and my mother spent the last year of that house packing boxes and pretending it was an adventure. So I know both sides of a For Sale sign better than most people, and I've never entirely made peace with which side I ended up on.*

She paused. Read it back. It was too much — far too much for a first letter, too raw, too revealing, the kind of thing a smart operative kept locked away. Vane would have hated it.

She kept it anyway. There was no one to show it to. That was the strange liberty of anonymity: you could put down the true thing and no one could trace it back to the polished, careful, ambitious Wren Calloway who had shaken Marcus Vane's hand an hour ago and named her price.

*Ask me anything,* she wrote. *That seems to be the custom here, from what I gather. Ask me anything and I'll answer honestly, which is more than I can say for most of my correspondence these days. In exchange I'll want to know about the island — not the brochure version. The version the people who live there would recognize.*

*Fair warning: I'm told I argue. I'm told I ask too many questions. I'm told my handwriting leans like it's in a hurry. All three accusations are accurate.*

*— No. 9*

She signed it with the number because signing her name felt suddenly impossible — not because of operational security, though that was the excuse she gave herself, but because the letter belonged to No. 9, whoever that was turning out to be, and Wren Calloway hadn't written it.

The ferry swung toward the slipway. Engines reversed. On the quay, a handful of islanders waited with the unhurried patience of people whose arrivals and departures were governed by tides rather than calendars. An old woman with a cart. Two men in oilskins. A boy throwing stones at the water and missing on purpose.

Wren sealed the letter, wrote *Correspondent No. 14, c/o Merrow's Reach Post Office* on the envelope in her leaning, hurrying hand, and held it a moment longer than necessary.

Somewhere past that harbour wall, past the village and the fields, a hundred and forty acres of orchard stood in the June light, and a man was living inside the number she'd been hired to reduce. She pushed the thought down where she kept the other inconvenient ones — the moldy ceiling, the sold house, her mother's boxes — and stood, and joined the queue waiting to step onto the island.

The postmistress, when Wren found the post office an hour later — a narrow stone building smelling of paper and paraffin, run by a small fierce woman named Mrs. Penhale who examined Wren's letter like a customs officer examining luggage — accepted it without comment.

"First one?" Mrs. Penhale said.

"Yes."

"They're always the hardest." She dropped it into a wooden pigeonhole marked with a brass 14. "After that, you'll wonder how you ever managed without them."

Wren walked out into the island light, blinking. The air smelled of salt and cut grass and, faintly, unmistakably, of blossom — late apple blossom from some sheltered orchard row, hanging on past its season the way certain stubborn things do.

She had a room above the pub, a contract in her bag, and a lie distributed neatly across a summer.

And somewhere in a wooden pigeonhole, her first letter lay waiting for a stranger who didn't know yet that the vulture was already circling — or that the vulture, against all instruction, had told him the truth.
