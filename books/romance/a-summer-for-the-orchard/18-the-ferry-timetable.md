## 18. The Ferry Timetable

The booking confirmation arrived at 4:17 in the afternoon, and Wren Calloway stared at it the way you stare at a diagnosis.

*One passenger. Wren Calloway. Merrow's Reach to Penharrow Junction, connecting service to the mainland. Departure: Saturday, the twenty-third of August, 6:40 a.m.*

She had typed her own name into the booking form and felt like a forger.

The ticket was refundable until midnight Friday. She noticed that detail the way she noticed everything now — compulsively, hungrily, cataloguing exits the way she used to catalogue soil samples. Refundable until midnight Friday. As if some part of her had already known she'd want the door left open a crack.

Her laptop sat closed on the desk of her rented room above the chandlery, and she looked at it for a long time without touching it. Through the window, the harbor did its evening business: fishing boats coming in with the tide, gulls arguing over scraps, the ferry — the *Kestrel*, small and white and reliable as a heartbeat — tied up at the pier with its running lights just beginning to glow in the blue hour. Tomorrow morning that boat would carry her away from all of it. Away from the orchard, away from the village, away from Emrys Brightwater's face when he'd said *get off my land*, away from the letters she could no longer answer and the ones she could no longer stop rereading.

It should have been simple. It had been simple once. You took the contract, you did the survey, you filed the valuation, you collected the fee, and you moved on to the next coastline that needed measuring. That was the job. That had always been the job. Land was data. Boundaries were lines on a map. People were stakeholders, and stakeholders were obstacles or assets, and neither category required you to know their grandfather's name or the way they touched a tree trunk like a shoulder.

Except she did know his grandfather's name now. Aldous Brightwater, planted 1961 through 1978, forty-one rows, and the man's grandson walked them every evening like a rosary. Except boundaries weren't lines anymore; they were arguments, and griefs, and Marta Tull's empty windows. Except people were not categories, and one of them wrote letters that had rearranged the furniture of her entire interior life, and she had lied to him in every single one.

Not lied, exactly. Omitted. Curated. Committed the slow fraud of a person showing someone only the version of herself she could bear to be.

Same thing, her conscience said, in Marcus Vane's voice, which was the cruelest part.

She got up and made tea she didn't drink, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

* * *

The station was not really a station. It was a platform and a shelter and a single track that ran along the coast road before curving inland toward the junction where the mainland line began. In summer it saw four trains a day. In August, at the end of the season, the timetable thinned to almost nothing, and the final departure — the last train that would connect with the last ferry of the operating season — was circled in red marker on the notice board by someone with a sense of drama or a sense of mercy. Wren didn't know which.

She hadn't meant to come here at night.

After the booking, after the tea, after an hour of lying on her bed staring at the ceiling beams while the chandlery's dog barked at something below, she had put on her coat and walked out into a night so clear the stars looked freshly washed. Her feet had taken her along the harbor road without consulting her, and then up the lane, and then here — to the platform, where a single lamp buzzed overhead and moths threw themselves against the light with soft, idiotic persistence.

She sat down on the bench under the timetable and looked up at it.

The board was old-fashioned, painted metal, updated by hand each season by a man named Colm who also drove the school bus and had once bought her a pasty when she looked peaky on the 7:15. The summer schedule would come down soon and be replaced by the winter one: two trains a day, then one, then none on Sundays. But for now the board still held the full season, every departure listed in neat white rows, and there at the bottom, the last entry:

**23 AUG — 06:40 — PENHARROW JCT (LAST SERVICE OF SEASON)**

Someone had drawn a small star beside it. A star. As if it were an event worth attending rather than an ending.

Wren sat beneath it and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for three days.

Saturday morning she would leave. Monday morning she would be at her desk in the Halloway Development offices, in the glass-and-steel building that smelled of carpet cleaner and ambition, and Marcus Vane would stop by her workstation with his cufflinks catching the light, and he would say something like *Glad to have you back, Calloway. Now let's talk about those numbers,* and she would have a choice to make. The choice she'd been making since June, in smaller and smaller increments, each one easier than the last because each one was only slightly worse than the one before.

Or Saturday morning she would leave, and she would not go back to the office at all, and everything she had built — eleven years, two professional accreditations, a reputation for clean work and tighter discretion, the flat in Bristol she still paid rent on out of habit, the career that was the only thing she'd ever finished — all of it would become a thing she used to have.

There was a third option. There was always a third option, and it was the worst one: go back, fix the valuations, lower them the way Vane wanted, bury whatever flickering thing had started in her chest beside a storm-soaked trellis, and live. People did it constantly. People buried things all the time and called it maturity.

She thought about Emrys reading her letter by lamplight — no. She thought about him reading *her* letter, the one she'd written as his stranger, the one where she'd told him about her father's hands, about the houses they'd lost, about how she'd learned early that security was a story you built out of other people's displacements and tried not to look too closely at the foundations. He had written back within two days. He always wrote back within two days. His handwriting slanted forward like it was in a hurry to reach her.

*I don't think you're a fraud,* he'd written. *I think you're someone who was taught the wrong map and is trying to find her way home anyway. Those are different things. Believe me. I've spent two years confusing them myself.*

She had read that sentence maybe sixty times. She had read it standing in the post office, sitting on the sea wall, once in the middle of a site survey with her theodolite forgotten and her assistant asking if she was quite all right. And every time, the same thought arrived with the same quiet devastation: *he would not say this if he knew.* He would take those words back. He would burn them. Because the wrong map she'd been following had led her directly onto his land with a clipboard and a mandate to price it for the wrecking ball.

A moth hit the lamp glass again. Wren flinched.

"You're talking to yourself on a train platform at midnight," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the empty air, and no one heard it, and that was the whole problem, wasn't it. She had spent her whole adult life being unheard on purpose. Careful in meetings. Careful in reports. Careful in letters that couldn't be traced to her. Careful, careful, careful, until the carefulness had eaten everything except a voice that only existed on paper, addressed to a man who hated her.

The wind came off the water and moved the timetable's corner, and the star beside the last service seemed to wink.

"Right," she said. "Right."

She opened her bag and took out her laptop.

* * *

The screen's glow turned her hands blue-white in the dark. The platform was deserted; the last bus had gone at ten; somewhere down the coast road a car passed and faded, and then there was nothing but the moths and the sea doing its endless patient work against the rocks.

She logged in. The Halloway secure portal loaded with its familiar logo — a stylized wave, blue and green, meant to suggest harmony between development and nature, designed by an agency in London for a sum that could have kept Marta Tull's farm running for a decade. Wren had never once looked at that logo without something in her stomach turning, and she had always, always ignored it.

Her project folder opened. *MERROW'S REACH — PHASE ONE.* Subfolders nested inside subfolders, months of her own meticulous work, and she understood suddenly that she had built the case against herself with perfect professionalism. Every document was dated. Every document bore her digital signature. Every valuation she had quietly inflated — the shoreline parcels marked up thirty percent, the orchard's development potential flagged *constrained* with engineering jargon thick enough to hide behind, Marta Tull's acreage assessed at figures Vane would call fantasy — every act of sabotage was documented, timestamped, attributable.

If she copied these files, she wasn't hesitating anymore. She was evidencing.

Her cursor hovered over the first folder.

Here was what she knew, laid out with the same clarity she brought to a boundary dispute. If she got on the train Saturday and went back and fixed the numbers, she kept her career and lost herself — and Emrys Brightwater lost his orchard, and Marta Tull lost her vindication, and the syndicate's quiet purchase of the vineyard's debt would proceed exactly as planned, and in eighteen months there would be a resort where the rows were, and a marina where the boathouse was, and a plaque in the lobby saying something tasteful about honoring the site's agricultural heritage.

If she copied the files and took them to someone — a regulator, a journalist, a lawyer who handled whistleblower cases — she lost everything she had spent eleven years building, and she might lose it publicly, messily, with Vane's lawyers painting her as a disgruntled employee with a grudge and a fabrication problem. Her name would be in filings. Her photograph might be in articles. No firm would touch her afterward; surveying was a small profession and reputations traveled faster than ferries.

And if she did nothing — if she simply left, quietly, refundable ticket redeemed, contract abandoned mid-survey — then Vane would hire someone else, someone hungrier or more tired, and the valuations would come in low and clean, and the machine would run over the island without her fingerprints on it. She would be innocent. She would be useless. She would spend the rest of her life knowing the difference between those two words.

The moths battered the lamp. The sea worked the rocks. Wren Calloway sat alone on a platform at one in the morning and discovered that the choice had already been made — had been made weeks ago, in increments, in inflated numbers and argued-down deadlines and one furious meeting where she'd called Vane's manufactured code violation against Marta Tull *garbage* to his face and watched him smile and file it away.

All that remained was the paperwork.

She clicked the first folder. Valuations, April through August. She copied them to a drive she'd bought in Penharrow two weeks ago and told herself was for holiday photos. Then the email archive — Vane's instructions, always verbal or oblique in writing, but not always; the man was arrogant enough to put things in messages when he thought the recipient was loyal, phrases like *let's see if we can encourage the Brightwater number downward before the bank call* and *the Tull situation resolves itself if the inspection finds what it needs to find.* She forwarded copies to a personal account she'd created under a name that wasn't hers. Then the survey drafts, the internal memos, the spreadsheet where the projected resort revenue assumed acquisition costs that only worked if the owners sold desperate and cheap.

File by file. Folder by folder. The progress bar crawled. Outside, the tide turned, and the gulls went quiet, and the stars wheeled a degree or two westward, and Wren sat in the cold copying the evidence of her own complicity into a small black drive that weighed less than an apple.

Somewhere in the middle of it — between the memo about the council members on retainer and the draft press release titled *Halloway Brings Prosperity to Merrow's Reach* — she stopped, and put her head back against the bench, and laughed. It came out cracked and wet, closer to the other thing.

He had asked her, in the last letter she'd answered, *what do you want? Not what are you supposed to want. What do you actually want?* And she had written back something evasive and clever, because the true answer had frightened her too much to type: *I want to be the person you think I am. I want to deserve the letters. I want to stay.*

She picked up her phone instead of the laptop. Opened the message thread with the anonymous program's forwarding address — the channel now poisoned, silent since the boathouse, since *you don't know what I am.* She typed: *I'm going to try to make it right. Whatever happens, I need you to know that.* She looked at it for a long moment. Then she deleted it, word by word, because it wasn't enough and it was too much and it was cowardice dressed as courage, and she was done mailing him pieces of herself with the sharpest edges sanded off.

If she was going to do this, she would do it whole. Face to face, eventually, if he ever let her near enough. Documents first. Confession second. Absolution — no. She didn't get to want absolution. She got to want the orchard to survive, and that was different, and she could build a life on different.

The copy finished at 2:51 a.m. She ejected the drive, wrapped it in a wool sock, and pushed it deep into the inner pocket of her coat, where it sat against her ribs like a second heart.

Then she sat back and looked up at the timetable one more time.

**23 AUG — 06:40 — LAST SERVICE OF SEASON.**

Four hours from now. The train would come, and she would be on it, but not to Penharrow Junction and the connection home. Two weeks ago she'd booked a follow-up appointment — routine, forgettable — with the regulatory authority's regional office on the mainland, the one that handled land-fraud complaints. She'd told herself it was reconnaissance. She'd told herself a lot of things.

The appointment was Monday at nine. Between now and then there was a ferry, a junction, a night in a cheap room, and a version of her life that ended the moment she walked through that office door and handed over a wool sock full of evidence.

She stood, stiff with cold, and folded the ticket confirmation into her pocket — the return leg, the refundable one, the door left open a crack. She wouldn't refund it. Let the money go. Let it be tuition.

At the end of the platform, the lane dropped toward the harbor, where the *Kestrel* slept at her moorings with her lights off, waiting for dawn the way everything on this island seemed to wait for dawn — patiently, without illusion, certain the light would come because it always had.

Wren pulled her coat tight around the drive against her ribs and started walking.

Behind her, the red-circled date glowed faintly under the lamp, and the little hand-drawn star beside it kept its vigil over the last train of the season — which, though the timetable did not yet know it, was about to carry a passenger to a very different destination than the one printed on her ticket.
