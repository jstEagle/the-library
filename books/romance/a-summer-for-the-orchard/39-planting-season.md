## 39. Planting Season

**Emrys**

The frost came off the ground in the last week of February, and the orchard smelled like waking up — wet earth and cut wood and the faint green ghost of things not yet grown. Emrys stood at the top of the south field with a spool of twine in one hand and a bundle of stakes in the other, looking at forty empty rows he'd measured twice and argued about three times.

"You're doing it wrong," Wren said behind him.

He didn't turn around. "I'm doing it *straight*."

"Straight isn't the same as right." She came up beside him, boots muddy to the ankle, hair escaping from under a wool cap she'd stolen from his peg by the door months ago and never returned. She carried a clipboard — of course she did; some habits survived even betrayal and redemption — and on it was a hand-drawn map of the field with her own annotations crowding the margins. "The sun tracks lower here than you think in March. If you run your rows north-south you'll shade the rootstock half the day."

"I've planted rows on this island my whole life."

"And your grandfather planted them east-west," she said, tapping the map, "because he knew what he was doing, and because he wasn't too proud to take advice from a woman with a clipboard."

Emrys looked at her. She looked back, unrepentant, that particular tilt to her chin that had infuriated him across a pub table two summers ago and now did something entirely different to him.

"East-west," he conceded.

"That's all I ask." She kissed his cheek, tasting faintly of tea and cold air, and went down the row to start marking holes.

They'd ordered the rootstock in January — six hundred vines, cold-hardy hybrids, a blend Wren had spent three weeks researching with the ferocity she brought to everything, calling nurseries on the mainland, cross-referencing soil reports she'd written herself back when the land was an asset to be valued instead of a thing to be kept. Pinot Meunier for the sheltered hollow. Solaris for the exposed slope, because it could shrug off the salt wind. A row of Rondo along the boundary wall, where Marta Tull could see it from her kitchen window.

"For luck," Wren had said when she added it to the order.

"For spite," Emrys had said. "Marta will want to know if we're doing it better than she ever did."

"Both," Wren had said. "It can be both."

Now they worked down the first row together, laying out the line, driving stakes, the rhythm of it settling into something wordless after twenty minutes. This was the part he hadn't expected — not the forgiveness, which had come hard and slow and incomplete, but this: the ease. Two people who had learned each other's worst truths and found the shape of their hands still fit.

Halfway down the row, the farmhouse door banged open and Devlin Okafor came across the yard at a jog, coat flapping, waving a piece of paper like a man signaling a rescue plane.

"Emergency," he announced. "Board emergency."

"We're planting," Emrys said.

"Exactly. Catastrophe." Devlin was sixty, a retired fisherman with hands like knotted rope, and he had appointed himself the trust board's self-declared chairman through the simple expedient of talking louder than everyone else. He thrust the paper at Emrys. "The Halloran boy's applied for a plot lease. The old dairy field."

"So? That's what the trust is for."

"He wants to plant *daffodils*, Emrys. Commercial daffodils. On trust land."

Wren took the paper, scanned it. "That's... actually a reasonable diversification. Bulbs do well in coastal soil, and the spring market—"

"Don't you start with your spreadsheets." Devlin pointed a ropey finger at her. "His grandfather would turn in his grave."

"His grandfather grew *turnips nobody bought*," Emrys said. "Give him the lease. Trial season. If the daffodils fail, we've lost nothing but a field we couldn't afford to plant anyway."

Devlin grumbled, pocketed the paper, and stood there a moment longer, watching them work. Then, softer: "Your grandfather would have liked this, you know. Rows going in again."

Emrys looked down the line — the string taut between stakes, the holes waiting, Wren kneeling in the dirt with her cap pulled low. "Yeah," he said. "I know."

Devlin left. Wren looked up. "Daffodils?"

"Diversification," Emrys said gravely, and she laughed, and the sound went down the empty rows ahead of them like something planted.

* * *

**Wren**

The board meeting happened on Thursday nights in the farmhouse kitchen, because the church hall wanted fifteen pounds an evening and Marta Tull declared that extortion and refused to allow it. So eleven people crowded into a kitchen built for four, steam on the windows, the kettle going constantly, and governance happened in the gaps between tea runs.

Wren had a seat at the table now — elected in October, over Devlin's objection that she was "a mainlander with a laptop," and carried by every other vote including Marta's, who said only, "She fought for us when it cost her. That's more than most born here can say." Wren had gone out to the yard afterward and stood in the dark until she could trust her voice again.

Tonight's agenda, scrawled on the back of a seed catalogue: the daffodil lease, the cider press repair quote (outrageous), the spring festival budget, and Item Five, which Emrys had written as simply *the letters*.

"The pen-pal program," he said, when they got there. "Mrs. Pendergast ran it for thirty years, and she's told me plainly she wants to hand it off before next winter. She's eighty-one and she says her handwriting's going, which — having seen her last circular — is generous."

"It's a shambles," Mrs. Pendergast herself said fondly, from the corner where she presided over the biscuit tin. "Half the pairings never write, a quarter fall in love, and one couple ended up in a lawsuit over a disputed hedge. It's the best institution on this island."

There was laughter. Wren watched Emrys take it, watch him let it land on him without flinching. He'd been different since autumn — not lighter exactly, but *held*, the way a well-staked vine is held. He still counted every pound twice. But the ledgers didn't look like verdicts anymore. They looked like plans.

"So I'm proposing," he went on, "that the trust takes it over formally. Budget for stamps and paper. And that someone volunteers to run it." He paused, and Wren saw it coming the way you see weather coming off the sea. "I'll do it."

"You?" Devlin snorted. "You spent two years writing to a stranger and didn't know it was the woman sitting across the pub from you."

"Which makes me uniquely qualified," Emrys said dryly, "to spot the ones who are lying to themselves about why they're writing." A ripple of knowing laughter — the whole village knew the story now; Wren had made peace with being a parable. "Someone has to read the letters going out. Not the contents — just make sure they're going. Pair the shy ones with the patient ones. Keep Mrs. Pendergast's rules." He glanced at the corner. "All seven of them."

"All nine," Mrs. Pendergast corrected. "Rule eight: no money changes hands. Rule nine: if you fall in love, you say so out loud within the year. Look how well that one works."

Wren felt her face go warm and hid it in her tea. Across the table, Emrys caught her eye, and there was the whole history of it in one look — the letters, the lies, the lamplight, the rain on the platform road — compressed into something small and steady that passed between them like a coin.

Motion carried, eight votes to one, Devlin dissenting on principle. The program was theirs. First assignments to go out with the spring post.

Afterward, when the kitchen had emptied and the last of the board — Marta, moving slowly now, refusing help on principle — had been walked to her gate, Wren dried dishes while Emrys wrote the first assignment cards at the kitchen table. Twelve names. Twelve strangers.

"Who gets who?" she asked.

"Instinct." He wrote a name, considered, matched it to another. "Mrs. Pendergast always said pairing is half reading the person and half prayer."

"And the other half?"

"Postage." He sealed an envelope, set it in the out-tray, and looked up at her. "You could write one, you know. Sign up yourself. Anonymous."

"To whom?"

"Whoever I assign you. Could be anyone. Could be the Halloran boy. Could be Devlin."

Wren dried her hands slowly. "You'd let me? After everything?"

"Wren." He set down the pen. "You wrote me the truest letters I've ever received while lying about everything else. You're the best correspondent on this island. It'd be a waste of postage not to."

She came around the table and sat on the arm of his chair, and outside the window the new rows ran away into the dusk, stakes and string, the geometry of a second chance laid out straight across the field.

"East-west," she said.

"East-west," he agreed.

* * *

**Emrys**

March came in like a rumor and left like a promise. They planted the six hundred vines over eleven days, in weather that swung from bright to brutal and back, and half the village turned up unasked with spades and thermoses — Devlin directing operations nobody had asked him to direct, the Halloran boy laying out his daffodil field next door and shouting cheerful insults across the wall, Marta Tull sitting on a folding chair at the end of the Rondo row like visiting royalty, offering judgments on hole depth that were, annoyingly, always correct.

On the last day, Wren pressed the final rootstock into the earth and Emrys packed the soil around it, and they stood together at the bottom of the slope looking up at what they'd made. Bare sticks in brown dirt. Nothing to see, unless you knew how to wait.

"My grandfather used to say," Emrys said, "that planting a vine is a letter to someone you'll never meet."

Wren leaned into his shoulder. "Then we'd better teach whoever reads it how to prune."

Inside, on the kitchen table, twelve sealed envelopes waited for the morning post. New strangers. New first words. Somewhere among them, maybe, another guarded heart and another fierce handwriting, another summer waiting to be written down.

Emrys picked up the top envelope, turned it over once in his hands, and set it by the door — addressed, stamped, and ready to begin.
