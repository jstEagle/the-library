## 2. A Letter in a Lawyer's Envelope

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was the day the bank had chosen to ruin her.

Ines Zhou saw it first as a shape — a pale rectangle wedged between a flyer for gutter cleaning and an envelope from the utility company that she already knew was bad news. It sat there on the doormat of Salt & Rise at 6:47 in the morning, while she stood in the doorway with two sacks of flour and the particular exhaustion that comes from having slept four hours in the apartment above your own failing business.

She almost stepped over it.

That was the thing she would remember later, when everything had changed: she almost stepped over the letter that saved her life. She had flour under her fingernails and a payment notice burning a hole in her apron pocket and no room left in her body for one more piece of paper. The world had spent three months teaching her that paper only ever meant one thing — *you owe, you owe, you owe* — and she had learned the lesson so thoroughly that a lawyer's cream-colored envelope with actual handwriting on it registered, for one full second, as just another bill.

Then she saw the handwriting. Real ink, real penmanship, the kind of careful cursive nobody under seventy used anymore. And a return address that said *Halloway & Finch, Attorneys at Law, Port Merrow*.

Port Merrow. The island town she'd grown up in, fled from, and somehow never managed to leave behind entirely, because here she still was, running a café on its mainland edge, close enough to smell the ferry diesel on windy days.

Ines set down the flour, crouched, and picked up the letter like it might be hot.

* * *

She didn't open it right away. That was another thing she'd remember. She carried it inside, propped it against the espresso machine where she could see it, and went about the morning ritual — ovens on, dough out of the cold proof, the day's first batch of morning buns going in at seven sharp because whatever else was falling apart, the buns were never late. The regulars came. Marta with her double cortado and her crossword. The two nurses off the night shift who split a cardamom knot and argued amiably about television. Old Dev, who paid in exact change counted out from a tin he kept in his truck, and who always sat by the window facing the water even though the view was mostly a parking lot and a sliver of gray sea.

The letter sat against the machine all morning, patient as a cat.

By ten-thirty she had invented six explanations for it. A class-action settlement she didn't remember joining. An error — someone else's inheritance, someone else's lawyer, someone else's life delivered to the wrong address. A summons. Her landlord finally moving to formalize what they both knew was coming. Her mind, trained by ninety days of bank letters, could only produce catastrophes dressed in stationery.

"You're going to burn the next batch if you keep staring at that," Marta said, not looking up from her crossword. "Seven across. *Legal document.* Five letters."

"Deed," said one of the nurses.

"Will," said the other.

Marta filled in *deed*, and Ines felt something cold walk up her spine, and she laughed — too loud, too bright — and said, "It's probably nothing," and both women looked at her with the flat, unconvinced expressions of people who had been watching a woman drown slowly for months and were tired of pretending not to notice.

She opened it in the back room, standing up, because sitting down felt like surrender.

*Dear Ms. Zhou,* it began, in that same careful hand, though this part was clearly typed — she could see the correction ribbon's ghost, or maybe she imagined it, some lawyer somewhere still using a machine older than she was.

*We write regarding the estate of Elias Marchetti, deceased March 3rd of this year.*

Elias Marchetti. The name landed strangely, half-remembered, like a word from a language she'd spoken as a child. Great-Uncle Elias. Her grandmother's uncle, or maybe her grandfather's — the family tree had always been a tangle on her mother's side, Zhou married into layers of island families, Marchetti and Okafor and others whose names had thinned out over the generations until only Sunday mentions remained. She had met him exactly twice that she could recall. Once at a funeral, when she was small enough to be lifted onto a chair to see over the crowd. Once at the ferry dock, when she was seventeen and leaving, and a broad old man with soil-dark hands had gripped her shoulder and said, *Come back and see the trees sometime, girl. They'll be here.* She hadn't come back. She'd gone to culinary school instead, then to a city, then to a man named Theo Marchetti, and then away from him, and eventually back to the mainland across the water, where she opened a café with a loan she could no longer service and told herself the island was a closed chapter.

*They'll be here.* Well. Apparently the trees had been here, and now so was their owner's paperwork.

*Mr. Marchetti has named you as co-beneficiary of his estate. Specifically, you are willed an undivided one-half interest in the property known as Marchetti Orchard, comprising forty-one acres on the eastern shore of Port Merrow Island, together with all improvements, equipment, and standing crop.*

She read the paragraph three times. The words refused to organize themselves into meaning. *One-half interest.* Forty-one acres. Standing crop. She thought of the orchard the way everyone in Port Merrow thought of it — as a fact of geography, like the lighthouse or the tide charts. Rows of apple and cherry trees climbing the eastern slope, visible from the ferry on clear days, a green checkered blanket somebody's great-grandparents had laid over the hill a century ago. Nobody thought about who owned it. It was simply *the Marchetti place*, the way the church was simply the church.

And now half of it was hers. Half of a working orchard, willed to a grand-niece who couldn't have picked its owner out of a lineup with any confidence, who had sent him a Christmas card exactly once — three years ago, out of a sudden guilty impulse — and received nothing back, and had told herself that proved he wanted to be left alone.

*Please contact our office at your earliest convenience to schedule the formal reading of the will, at which time the identity of the co-beneficiary will be confirmed and the terms of the estate discussed. We note that the property carries certain encumbrances and obligations which will be addressed at that meeting.*

*Certain encumbrances and obligations.* Of course. Even miracles, apparently, came with footnotes. Ines laughed again, alone in the back room, and it came out cracked.

But underneath the laugh, underneath the bewilderment, something else was happening in her chest — something she distrusted immediately and completely, because hope, in her recent experience, was just the setup for a worse fall. Still. It was there. A small warm coal of it, refusing to be stamped out. Forty-one acres. Standing crop. It was June; the cherries would be coming in weeks. She knew fruit prices the way she knew flour prices, from sourcing for the café, from the Saturday market stalls. She knew what a good cherry season could gross. Not enough to matter, usually, not enough to fix anything — but she wasn't one person anymore, was she? If she owned half an orchard, she wasn't a woman drowning in a leaky boat. She was a woman with land. Land was the one thing the bank couldn't repossess out from under her, the one asset that grew instead of decayed, that you couldn't burn through in a bad quarter.

Her mind, traitorous and practical, was already doing arithmetic. Lease the rows to a local grower. Sell the standing crop wholesale. Or — and here the coal flared — bring the fruit across the water herself, into her own kitchen, into her own cases. Orchard pastries. Cider donuts. A menu that tasted like the island, sold to the tourists who already came for the island. Salt & Rise reborn as something rooted.

She caught herself smiling at a stack of dirty sheet pans and made herself stop.

There would be a catch. There was always a catch. *Encumbrances and obligations.* And a co-beneficiary — some cousin, presumably, some Marchetti descendant she'd also never met, some stranger she'd now have to share a hillside with, negotiate with, trust. Her experience with partners was limited to banks and landlords, and neither had inspired confidence lately.

Still. She read the letter one more time, folded it along its crisp creases, and put it in her apron pocket, where it lay against her hip all afternoon like a second heartbeat.

* * *

That night she climbed the stairs to the apartment with a bottle of cheap Prosecco she'd been saving for good news that never came, and drank one glass of it standing at the window, looking east across the black water toward where the island would be, invisible tonight, somewhere beyond the dark.

Her phone buzzed. Her mother.

*Mama called the lawyer back already?* No — her mother didn't know yet. The message was the usual: a photo of dumplings, a voice note about an aunt's blood pressure, and then, because mothers are barometers: *You sound tired on the phone last week. Did the bank thing get worse?*

Ines stared at the message for a long time. She could tell her. She should tell her. Instead she typed: *Bank thing same. But I got strange mail today. Might be good. Will call tomorrow.*

Three dots appeared, vanished, appeared. Then: *Strange good or strange strange?*

Ines looked at the letter on the counter. At the words *one-half interest*. At the name *Marchetti*, which she had spent ten years training herself to say without any expression at all, and which now sat in a legal document with her own name beside it, joined by the word *and*, joined by law, joined by a dead man's decision.

*Strange good,* she typed. *I think.*

She put the phone face-down and finished the Prosecco.

Sleep wouldn't come, so she did what she always did when sleep wouldn't come: she went downstairs and baked. Not for the case — for herself, for the thinking, for the way dough gave her hands something honest to do while her head ran its circuits. She made a simple frangipane tart base, blind, without deciding to, and then stood in front of the walk-in with the door open, cold air spilling over her ankles, looking at the flat of frozen cherries she'd bought in February at painful expense for a Valentine's special that had flopped.

Island cherries. Marchetti cherries, if she let herself think it. Dark, small, intense things that grew best on that eastern slope where the fog burned off early and the stone held the day's heat into the night. Every pastry chef on the mainland bought them when they could get them and mourned them when they couldn't. She had spent years buying them from a distributor, third-hand, marked up twice, and never once — not once in ten years — letting herself think about the hillside they came from, or who walked those rows, or whether the old man with the soil-dark hands was still alive down there among the trees.

He wasn't. That was the new fact of her life. He wasn't, and he had thought of her anyway, at the end, with lawyers and ink and forty-one acres, and she did not know why, and the not-knowing itched like a wool sweater.

She pitted cherries at midnight and laid them into the frangipane in concentric rings, and the kitchen filled with the smell of almond and dark fruit, and she let herself imagine — carefully, the way you test ice before putting weight on it — a version of September in which the café was still hers. Not thriving. Just hers. Doors open, ovens on, the debt carved down to something survivable. She let herself imagine walking into the bank with proof of income, proof of assets, proof that the woman they'd been sending letters to was not, in fact, finished.

Cautious. She made herself add the word like a warning label. *Cautious hope.* Hope with a fire extinguisher next to it. Because she had been here before — the loan approval, the lease signing, the first glowing review — and every one of those bright mornings had turned, eventually, into a Tuesday with a bank letter in her pocket.

But none of those mornings had involved inheriting an orchard.

At two in the morning she ate a slice of the tart standing at the steel counter, and it was good — genuinely good, the kind of good that made her angry, because it reminded her she could still do this, that the failure was circumstance and not her hands — and she wrapped the rest for the morning case and decided, with the decisiveness of the deeply tired, that she would call the lawyer first thing.

* * *

First thing turned out to be 9:04 a.m., after the rush, after she'd changed her shirt twice because her hands shook on the buttons both times.

"Halloway and Finch." A young-sounding receptionist, bored, gum in her voice.

"I'm calling about — I received a letter. About the Marchetti estate. I'm Ines Zhou."

The boredom vanished so fast it was almost audible, replaced by a briskness that felt rehearsed. "Oh! Yes, Ms. Zhou. Mr. Halloway was hoping to hear from you. We have you down for the reading Thursday at eleven, here at the office. The other beneficiary has confirmed as well."

Something in the way she said *the other beneficiary* — a slight hitch, a tiny intake before the words, as if the name required care — snagged in Ines like a fishhook.

"The other beneficiary," Ines repeated. "Who—"

"We'll go over everything Thursday," the receptionist said smoothly. Too smoothly. "Eleven o'clock. Do you need directions? Everyone says the parking is impossible but honestly if you come around the side of the building—"

"Thursday," Ines said. "Fine. Thank you."

She hung up and stood in the empty café, the lunch prep untouched, the letter's twin creases pressed into her memory, and tried to make herself feel only the good thing. Half an orchard. A chance. A rope thrown to a drowning woman.

But the hitch in the receptionist's voice stayed with her, the way a wrong note stays in your ear. *The other beneficiary has confirmed.* Confirmed from where? The office was on the island. Beneficiaries of island estates tended to be island people, or near-island people, people within reach of a Thursday-morning reading at eleven.

People who took ferries.

Ines wiped down the already-clean counter, once, twice, and told herself the tightness in her chest was just caffeine. It was June. The cherries were coming. Whatever — whoever — waited in that lawyer's office on Thursday, she would walk in, sign what needed signing, and walk out with ground under her feet for the first time in a year.

She was not prepared. There was no way she could have been prepared. But she put the letter in her best bag, ironed the one blazer she owned, and spent Wednesday night practicing a neutral, professional smile in the reflection of the pastry case, so that whoever sat across the table from her on Thursday would see a businesswoman, a stranger, a person with no history at all.

The smile lasted four seconds past the moment Mr. Halloway slid the deed across his desk and said, "Ms. Zhou, Ms. — well. Before we begin, perhaps I should introduce your co-beneficiary," and the office door opened behind her.
