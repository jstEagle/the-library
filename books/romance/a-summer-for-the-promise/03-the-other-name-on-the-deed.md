## 3. The Other Name on the Deed

The lawyer's office smelled like lemon polish and old paper, the way every office in Port Alder smelled when it had been around longer than the people who used it. Ines sat on a chair too small for her knees and tried not to look at the clock. It was 9:58. The reading was at ten. She'd arrived at nine-fifteen because she hadn't been able to stand being inside her own café for one more minute — because standing behind the counter counting the day's first till felt too much like standing at the edge of something.

Marguerite Delacroix, attorney-at-law, came out with a folder under one arm and reading glasses pushed up into gray curls that refused to be tamed.

"Ms. Zhou. Good morning." She shook Ines's hand with a grip that meant business. "Coffee?"

"I run a café," Ines said. "If I drink anyone else's coffee, it feels like cheating."

Something flickered across the lawyer's face — sympathy, maybe, or the professional patience of a woman who had watched this town's fortunes rise and fall through three decades of probate files. "Then let's get started."

Ines followed her into a room with a long table and windows that looked out on Harbor Street. From here she could see the corner of her own awning, faded red, and beyond it the water, flat and silver this morning, the ferry dock empty until the eleven o'clock boat. Somewhere out there was an island she'd visited exactly twice as a child, to attend a funeral and then, years later, a wedding that wasn't hers. Marchetti Orchard. Great-Uncle Ambrose. A man who had sent her a card every birthday for thirty years, signed *Uncle A.*, in handwriting like fence posts, and whom she had never once called back.

She should have called him back. That thought had been sitting on her chest since the letter came, heavy as a wet coat.

"Before we begin," Marguerite said, settling behind the table, "I want you to understand that Mr. Marchetti — Ambrose — was very specific about his wishes. He revised his will four times in the last two years. He knew what he wanted."

"Which was?" Ines asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt.

"To take care of you. And to make sure the orchard stayed an orchard." Marguerite opened the folder. "There are conditions. I'll walk you through them. But first —"

The door opened.

Ines didn't turn around right away. She heard footsteps, unhurried, and the soft click of the door closing, and some animal part of her brain registered them as familiar before the rest of her caught up. Then she turned, and the past slammed into the present so hard she actually gripped the edge of the table.

Theo Marchetti stood in the doorway of Marguerite Delacroix's office, ten years older than the last time she'd seen him and wearing the years badly in all the ways that made it worse — broader through the shoulders, a line between his brows that hadn't been there at twenty-four, the same dark hair pushed back from the same stubborn forehead. He was holding a coffee cup from the kiosk on the ferry dock, and he stopped dead when he saw her, and the cup tilted, and coffee sloshed over his knuckles, and he didn't even notice.

"Ines," he said.

Her name in his mouth. Ten years, and he still said it like that — like the second syllable mattered, like it was a question he'd never gotten answered.

"No," she said, which was not a word she'd planned. It just came out. "No, no — you're not —"

"The co-heir," Marguerite said mildly, from behind her glasses. "Mr. Marchetti, do come in. You're dripping."

* * *

They ended up seated at opposite ends of the long table, which was not large enough. Ines kept her hands folded in her lap where they couldn't betray her. Theo wrapped his burned hand in a napkin and did not look at her, which was its own kind of conversation. Through the window, the eleven o'clock ferry slid into the dock, right on time, indifferent.

"You knew," Ines said finally. Not a question. "When you wrote me. When you sent the letter about the inheritance. You knew he'd left half of it to both of us."

Marguerite looked up over her folder. "Ms. Zhou —"

"He's your client too, isn't he? He's known for weeks. I found out four days ago." Her voice was climbing and she made herself stop, made herself breathe, because she would not do this. Not here. Not in front of a lawyer and a window full of daylight and ten years of carefully constructed distance. "You let me find out like this."

"I found out three weeks ago," Theo said quietly. "And I've spent those three weeks trying to figure out how to tell you, and every version of the conversation ended with you hanging up on me. So I let the letter do it." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were the same brown, warm and dark and impossible to lie to, which was why she'd never been able to lie to him, which was the whole problem. "I'm sorry. That was cowardly."

"Yes," she agreed. "It was."

"And yet here we are," Marguerite said, with the crispness of a woman who had mediated worse, "so perhaps we should discuss what Ambrose actually wanted, before the two of you re-litigate whatever this is." She tapped the folder. "Shall we?"

Ines nodded once. Theo nodded once. Neither of them looked at the other.

* * *

Here is what Ambrose Marchetti had done, according to Marguerite Delacroix, who read it aloud in the level tone of someone reciting coordinates:

He had left everything — the orchard, forty acres of cherry and apple trees on the island's north shore; the press house; the cottage; the equipment, much of it older than either heir; the ledger books; and the debts, which were considerable — to his great-niece, Ines Zhou of Port Alder, and his great-nephew, Theo Marchetti, currently of Seattle. Half each. Not negotiable.

With conditions.

"The orchard is to be operated jointly for one full season," Marguerite read. "One harvest, beginning now, in June, ending with the final pressing in October. Both heirs must participate. Both must reside on the island for the duration of the season — Mr. Marchetti, I see you flinching, but it says reside, and I drafted it myself so I can assure you that word means what it says. At the end of the season, if both heirs agree to continue, the property passes to you jointly, free and clear, subject to the debts, which the estate will settle from the operating reserve."

"And if we don't agree?" Theo asked.

"If either heir declines, or if you cannot reach agreement by the end of the season, the property goes to auction. Proceeds split evenly after creditors." Marguerite removed her glasses. "I'll be blunt, because Ambrose paid me to be blunt. There is a developer who has made inquiries. Corliss Vane, Vane Holdings. They want that north shore parcel, and they have the money to get it, and if this goes to auction they will win it and bulldoze it by spring. Ambrose knew that. It's why the will reads the way it does. He wasn't just dividing an inheritance. He was setting a trap — or a test, depending on how romantic you're feeling about dead men."

Silence. Out the window, the ferry finished unloading. A gull screamed once and gave up.

"Why us?" Ines asked. It was the question that had been circling her since the letter. "Why me? I met him twice. I sent flowers when — " she stopped, because she hadn't even sent flowers, she'd sent a card, pre-printed sympathy, the kind you buy at a drugstore, and the shame of it rose up her throat. "I barely knew him."

"You knew him better than you think," Marguerite said. "He kept a file on you, Ms. Zhou. Every clipping about your café. The review in the *Sentinel*, three years ago. The fundraiser you catered for the library roof. He was proud of you." She paused. "He was proud of both of you. And he was sorry about more than either of you knows. But that's not in the will, and I'm not authorized to elaborate. Yet."

"*Yet,*" Theo repeated.

"There are documents to be opened later. Conditions of timing." Marguerite closed the folder with a snap. "Ambrose liked a schedule. Now. Do either of you wish to decline?"

Ines stared at the table. Decline. Walk away. Let the auction happen, take her half of whatever remained, throw it at the bank, save the café, and never think about Marchetti Orchard or the man sitting six feet away from her ever again. It was the sensible path. It was the path she'd have chosen yesterday, before she walked into this room.

But she could hear her grandmother's voice: *We don't leave family's things to rot, Ines-yah. Even family we didn't know well. Especially them.*

And underneath that, quieter, more dangerous: *the fruit tastes like memory.* She hadn't eaten a Marchetti cherry in fifteen years and she still dreamed about them.

"No," she said.

Beside her, after a moment: "No," Theo said.

"Then congratulations," Marguerite said dryly. "You co-own an orchard."

* * *

The meeting broke up at noon. Marguerite pressed keys into their hands — two iron keys on separate rings, cut from the same mold — and a date: June fifteenth, the press house, first official meeting of the co-managers. She shook each of their hands and held Ines's a half-second longer.

"For what it's worth," she said, low, "he told me once that the two of you were the only thing he got right. Arranging this, I mean. He said, 'Those two kids were good together before the world got at them. Maybe the trees can remind them.'" She released Ines's hand. "I told him he was a meddling old man. He said, 'Yes. It's my best quality.'"

Then she was gone, and Ines was alone in the hallway with Theo Marchetti, and the building was quiet except for a clock somewhere and the sound of her own pulse.

"So," he said.

"Don't," she said.

He shut his mouth. They stood there. The clock ticked. Downstairs, someone's radio played something with steel guitar, faint and tinny through the floorboards.

"You look good," he said anyway, because apparently ten years had taught him nothing.

"You look like someone who drinks gas-station coffee," Ines said, and immediately hated herself for it, because it was true and petty and because his mouth twitched — *twitched* — like he was fighting a smile, like he had any right to find her funny.

"That was dock kiosk coffee, actually. It's worse."

"Theo." His name came out sharper than she intended, and she watched it land, watched him go still the way he used to go still when she raised her voice — not afraid, never afraid, just *attentive*, like she was weather worth tracking. She made herself say the rest plainly. "I need you to understand something. Whatever this is — the will, the orchard, the season — I'm doing it for the land. For him. Not for you. You and I ended a decade ago, and I'd like to keep it ended. We can be business partners. We can be polite. We cannot be anything else. Are we clear?"

He took it the way he took everything, straight on, no flinch. "Clear."

"Good."

"But Ines —" and there it was, the old stubbornness, surfacing like a rock in shallow water — "for the record? You're wrong about how it ended. About whose fault. And someday, when you can stand to hear it, I'm going to tell you. Not today." He lifted his coffee cup, ruined and cold, in something like a toast. "Today I'll settle for polite."

He walked down the stairs ahead of her, and she stood at the top and watched him go and hated how easily her eyes followed him, how her body remembered the exact width of his shoulders against doorways, against kitchen counters, against her. Ten years. She had built a whole life on the far side of those ten years — a café, a reputation, a self — and one afternoon in a lawyer's office had put a crack straight through all of it.

* * *

She didn't go back to the café. She couldn't; she'd have to pass the dock, and the dock was where the ferry was, and the ferry was where he'd come from, and she wasn't ready to watch him leave again. Instead she walked up Harbor Street to the bluffs, to the bench above the water, and sat with her keys in her fist until the metal warmed.

An orchard. Forty acres. A season.

She turned the iron key over. It was old, pitted at the edges, the teeth worn smooth by decades of a dead man's hand. Somewhere on an island she could see from here, a green smudge on the north horizon, that key opened a press house full of debts and ledgers and secrets, and the other key — the identical one — was riding north across the water in the pocket of the last person on earth she wanted bound to her.

*Polite,* he'd said. Like politeness was a truce they could hold for five months. Like they hadn't already proven, once, catastrophically, that the two of them couldn't manage *casual.*

Her phone buzzed. The bank. Automated reminder: payment due in nine days. She looked at the screen without answering, and the number on it — the number that had been chasing her for ninety days — suddenly looked different. Smaller, almost, next to the shape of what Ambrose had left her. An orchard could pay debts. A harvest could be money. If the season went well. If the co-managers didn't kill each other by July.

Below her, the ferry horn sounded — the noon departure heading back to the mainland. She watched it pull away from the dock, white wake unfurling, and thought: *he's staying.* The realization landed strangely, somewhere between dread and something she refused to name.

He was staying. All summer. Forty acres and one press house and one small island between them.

Ines closed her hand around the key until the teeth bit her palm, stood up, and started walking back down toward her café, her failing, stubborn, surviving café — rehearsing, already, the first polite sentence she would say to Theo Marchetti, and hating herself for rehearsing anything at all.
