## 16. Cherries at Midnight

The heat came in like a debt collector.

It had been building for three days — a thick, yellow haze pressing down on the island from the mainland, the kind of heat that made the ferry's diesel smell sweet and the tar on the dock road go soft underfoot. By Thursday afternoon the orchard thermometer nailed to the press house door read ninety-eight in the shade, and there was no shade to speak of. The cherries were ripening all at once, a week ahead of schedule, and ripe cherries in that kind of heat don't wait. They split. They drop. They ferment where they fall and draw the wasps up from the hollow by the creek in black, furious clouds.

I stood in Row Twelve at four in the afternoon with sweat running into my eyes and understood, finally, why my uncle had kept that ledger the way he did — every entry in his small, patient hand, every debt tracked like weather. An orchard is a race you run against things that never stop coming: heat, rot, rain, time. He'd been running it alone for forty years. I'd been gone for ten of them.

Ines found me at the end of the row, both hands full of split fruit I hadn't had the heart to drop in the bucket.

"We can't pick in this," she said. "They'll cook before we get them to the press."

"So we lose them."

"No." She shaded her eyes and looked down the rows, all forty of them, the trees heavy and dark against the bleached sky. "We pick at night. It drops twenty degrees after midnight. My grandmother used to do it with the figs back home — said the fruit comes off sweeter when it's cool." She looked at me. "Lanterns. We start at nine, go until we can't stand."

It wasn't a request. It was never a request with Ines Zhou; it was a forecast of what was about to happen whether or not I agreed. Ten years ago that quality had driven me half out of my mind. Standing there dripping in Row Twelve, I found it steadying, the way you find a lighthouse steadying even while it's telling you the rocks are right there.

"Fine," I said. "But if I fall off a ladder in the dark, I'm haunting you specifically."

"You'd be an annoying ghost," she said, already walking. "You'd haunt the ledger."

* * *

By nine the heat had broken open into something almost tender. The sky went from bruised orange to a deep, clean violet, and the first stars came out over the water the way they only do on islands — close, insistent, like someone had strung lights just offshore. The air smelled of warm leaves and dust and, underneath it, the green metallic scent of the cherries themselves, thousands of pounds of them breathing in the dark.

We hung six lanterns along the row wires, old hurricane lamps from the press house with soot-freckled glass, and the light they threw was amber and unsteady and made everything look like a memory of itself. Ines had brought two ladders, four buckets, and a thermos of coffee strong enough to strip varnish. She'd also brought music — a little battery speaker tucked in the crook of a tree, playing something low and Spanish-adjacent that she claimed helped the picking rhythm.

"It helps your picking rhythm," I said.

"Everything helps your picking rhythm. You pick like a man doing taxes."

"I pick precisely."

"You pick slowly," she said, climbing her ladder, "and then you write it down."

Which was fair. I'd been keeping my own column in the ledger since June — pounds picked, rows cleared, losses to splitting and birds — because the numbers were the one part of this summer that made sense to me. The rest of it, the part where I spent my days ten feet from a woman who had once gotten on a ferry and not looked back, the part where I dreamed about the press house and woke up with my uncle's name in my mouth — none of that lent itself to columns. But pounds per row did. Pounds per row was a thing I could hold.

We worked. And somewhere in the second hour, without either of us deciding it, we fell into rhythm.

It happened the way tide happens — gradually, then all at once. She'd reach high into a tree and I'd take the low branches behind her; when her bucket filled I'd trade it for an empty one without either of us breaking stride; when a ladder needed moving she'd steady mine while I carried it, her hand flat against the rail, close enough that I could feel the warmth of her palm through the wood. The music changed. The lanterns swung. The cherries came off their stems with a soft double-click sound, *tick-tick*, filling the dark, and our hands moved through the same green shadow over and over, sometimes nearly touching, never quite.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the one currency I've been short on my whole adult life: it was the best I'd felt in ten years. Maybe ever. There was no past in that row, no deed, no auction clause, no bank letters with red borders. There was a tree, a lantern, a woman humming three feet above my head, and fruit coming off the branch faster than the night could take it.

"This one's perfect," Ines said at some point, holding a cherry up to the lantern light. It glowed like a garnet. "Look at that. Your uncle knew what he was doing with these late rows."

"He knew what he was doing with all of them." I took it from her fingers — carefully, by the stem, telling myself the care was for the fruit. "He just didn't write down half of what he knew. Half of that ledger is numbers and the other half is riddles."

"Riddles can be solved." She ate the cherry herself, which felt like a verdict of some kind. "Numbers are just numbers until somebody decides what they mean."

"That's either very wise or very Ines."

"Both things are true," she said, and laughed — actually laughed, head back, the sound going up into the dark trees — and I stood there on my ladder with an empty bucket in my hand and let it hit me like weather.

Here is what neither of us would say, standing in that lantern light with the whole summer hanging around us like fruit too high to reach: that this was easy. That it had always been easy, that was the whole tragedy of it, that the hard parts were everything we did *around* each other — the leaving, the silence, the decade of rehearsed sentences — and the being-together part fit like a hand in a glove worn soft. That if we kept picking like this, row after row, night after night, one of us was eventually going to say something true, and neither of us had survived the last true thing intact.

So instead she said, "Your side of the tree's slacking," and I said, "My side of the tree has structural disadvantages," and we kept working, and the warmth between us stayed exactly as unnamed as the ledger's missing payments.

* * *

At midnight we quit Row Twelve and sat on the tailgate of the orchard truck with the thermos between us, our backs against the wheel wells, looking down the long dark aisle of trees toward the water. The island lights across the channel were scattered and low. Somewhere a night bird was making its one complaint over and over.

"Twelve hundred pounds," I said, checking my tally. "From one row. In one night. If we can hold this pace for four nights, the early crop is saved."

"If we can hold this pace," Ines said, "I'm going to need new hands. These ones are stained permanently. Look." She held them up to the starlight — dark to the wrist, the creases of her palms dyed purple-black. "I have a café to run. Customers are going to think I strangle people."

"They'll think you make good pie."

"They'll think whatever Theo Marchetti tells them to think, apparently, since you've become the town's favorite grandson overnight. Mrs. Okafor's neighbor asked me last week if you were seeing anyone." She said it lightly, tossing it off, but I heard the little hitch under it, the place where the sentence caught on something.

"And what did you tell her?"

"I told her I wouldn't know," she said, "since we're business partners, and business partners don't discuss such things." A pause. The night bird complained again. "That's the terms of co-existence, right? Article one."

"Article one," I agreed.

The trouble with agreements is that they only govern what people say. What I was thinking was not governed by anything. I was thinking that her hands were stained the color of the orchard and that she'd come back to it anyway, year after year, in her way — the pies, the tarts, the fruit she bought at the mainland market and never once admitted reminded her of this place. I was thinking that my uncle had watched us be young and stupid together at these very harvest suppers and had maybe, in his quiet cipher of a heart, decided that if he couldn't fix what we broke, he could at least force us into the same field until we did. I was thinking that I had flown nine thousand miles to sell my half of this land and had now spent a full season finding reasons not to call the lawyer.

"Ines." Her name came out before I'd authorized it.

She turned. In the lantern light her face was half gold and half shadow, and there was cherry juice at the corner of her mouth, and she waited — she was always so *patient* with me at the exact moments patience undid me — she waited like the answer mattered.

"Don't," she said softly, before I could speak. Not cruel. Almost gentle. "Whatever sentence you're loading up right now — don't fire it at midnight in a cherry field. Neither of us will mean it right."

"How do you know I'd mean it wrong?"

"Because I know what my sentences sound like at midnight," she said, and looked away, down the rows, and her voice did the thing it did when she was keeping herself level by main force. "They sound like escape hatches. We're both full of sentences, Theo. That's our problem. We've been rehearsing them for ten years and none of them have made anybody brave yet."

I should have argued. Instead I drank the terrible coffee and watched the lanterns sway on their wires and thought: *she's right, and I hate it, and God help me I love her,* which is a sentence I managed not to load, much less fire.

"Four more nights," I said instead.

"Four more nights," she said. Then, quieter, almost to the trees: "Maybe the heat will break."

It didn't break. But we kept the pace — four nights became five, five became six, and the town started noticing the glow of lanterns in the orchard after dark and inventing stories about it, most of which reached us by morning through the café gossip pipeline, all of which Ines denied with a straight face and ears that gave her away completely. On the sixth night we finished the last of the early rows at 2 a.m., and she stood in the empty aisle with her stained hands on her hips and pronounced judgment on the whole crop like a small satisfied emperor, and I wrote the final number in the ledger with hands that shook slightly from exhaustion, or possibly from something else I declined to examine.

It was on the drive back — her truck, because my rental couldn't be trusted on the orchard track — that she said it. Casual. Eyes on the road. The headlights sweeping the rows like slow searchlights.

"I found something today. In the ledger. Or rather — I found where something *isn't*." She glanced at me. "There's a gap, Theo. Forty years of gaps. Payments going out to somebody who isn't named. Same amount, every quarter, like clockwork. Your uncle was paying someone, all this time, and he hid it inside the cipher."

The truck bumped over the cattle guard. My chest did something complicated.

"Paying who?" I said.

"I don't know yet," Ines said, and the look she gave me then was the look she used to give me across exam desks and across kitchen tables and across the whole width of the silence we built between us — bright, relentless, already halfway to the answer. "But I think I know how to find out. And Theo — I think whoever it is might be the reason we still have this orchard at all."
