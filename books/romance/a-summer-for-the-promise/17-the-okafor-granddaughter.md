## 17. The Okafor Granddaughter

The name had been sitting in Ines's apron pocket for three days.

Not the name itself — that lived in her head now, carved there by the harvest supper and Mrs. Ellery's wine-loosened tongue and old Tom Bexley's careful silence. *Okafor.* The word had weight. It had sat down at her table uninvited and refused to leave, and every time she rolled out pastry dough or counted the till or looked across the orchard rows at the press house with its peeling white paint, it said itself again in her grandmother's voice, though her grandmother had never once spoken it aloud while she was alive.

*Okafor.*

Amara Okafor, according to the school directory Ines had borrowed from the counter stack at the library. Fifth grade, Bellhaven Elementary. Room 12.

Ines had written the name on a receipt slip and carried it around like a stone.

She told herself she wasn't going to do anything about it yet. There was a harvest to run, a café to keep breathing, a co-heir she could barely look at without hearing the storm-night argument replaying behind her ribs. There were invoices from the trellis supplier and a walk-in cooler that made a sound like a dying animal and forty acres of cherries coming ripe three weeks ahead of schedule because the summer had decided, apparently, to be cruel in every direction at once.

But on Thursday morning, after the breakfast rush emptied out and Marisol took the counter for the mid-morning lull, Ines untied her apron, left a note that said *errands*, and drove the coast road toward the elementary school with the receipt slip trembling on her thigh.

She didn't let herself think about what she'd say. If she thought about it, she wouldn't go.

* * *

Bellhaven Elementary smelled like floor wax and pencil shavings and the ghost of a thousand packed lunches. Ines signed in at the front office as a "community visitor," which felt like a lie even though technically it wasn't — she was a member of the community, and she was visiting — and waited on a plastic bench under a wall of construction-paper suns until the lunch bell rang and the hallway exploded with children.

She watched them stream past and thought about what it meant to inherit something you never asked for. These kids inherited their names, their faces, their teachers' expectations. Nobody chose any of it. And still they carried it into every room.

Room 12's door was open. A woman stood at the window adjusting a paper mobile of the solar system, and Ines knew her before she turned around — not from memory, but from the photograph in the town historical society's display case that Theo had shown her two nights ago, the one of the Okafor family on the orchard steps in 1979, everyone squinting into the sun. The girl in the front row with the gap-toothed grin had grown into this woman's jawline, this woman's steady dark eyes.

"Can I help you?" Amara Okafor asked. She had a teacher's voice — warm, but calibrated to detect nonsense at forty paces.

"I'm Ines Zhou." Her own name came out smaller than she intended. "My great-uncle was Emil Marchetti. He owned Marchetti Orchard."

Something moved through Amara's face — quick, controlled, gone before Ines could name it. Not surprise. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone who has been waiting for this conversation for years and is furious at herself for how much she wants it.

"The orchard," Amara said. "Yes."

"I know that sounds — I know what that name means. Or I'm starting to." Ines's rehearsed opening — she had, despite herself, rehearsed something in the car, three versions, all of them terrible — collapsed entirely. "Mrs. Ellery mentioned your family at the harvest supper. And then I found things in my uncle's ledger that don't make sense unless—"

"Unless the land was never really his," Amara finished quietly.

A kid poked his head out the classroom door. "Ms. Okafor, Marcus took my—"

"Lunch recess, Devon. Go solve it with words." She waited until he'd gone, then stepped into the hallway and pulled the door mostly shut behind her. "I have twenty minutes. There's a bench by the playground that nobody uses. We can talk there if you want."

It wasn't an invitation so much as a sentence being handed down, and Ines accepted it the way you accept sentences: by standing up and following.

* * *

The bench sat under a big locust tree at the edge of the blacktop, its paint worn to bare wood in the shape of generations of waiting parents. Amara sat with her knees angled toward the playground, hands folded, a woman who spent her days making children feel safe and was now, visibly, making herself do the same.

"My grandfather was Daniel Okafor," she said, without preamble. "He came to this valley in 1961 with nothing but a degree in agricultural science from a university that wouldn't hire him afterward, and he built the best cherry operation on the peninsula. Better than the Marchettis. Better than anyone. He did it in eleven years." She said it flatly, factually, the way she probably taught long division. "In 1980 he sold it. That's what the county records say. Sold it, fair and square, to Emil Marchetti, signature and all, and moved his family into a rented house in town and went to work managing someone else's orchard until he retired."

"And it wasn't true," Ines said.

"It wasn't true." Amara turned to look at her directly, and Ines felt the full force of those classroom eyes. "My grandfather never sold anything in his life. He was a man who kept receipts for shoelaces. When he died in 1994, we found a shoebox of papers — letters he wrote to the county clerk, to a lawyer in Port Angeles, to the state attorney general. All of them asking someone, anyone, to look at the deed. None of them answered. By then the statute of limitations had closed like a door."

"What happened? How does a man lose an orchard he built?"

Amara's laugh had no humor in it, only exhaustion worn smooth by decades. "That's the question, isn't it. My mother says there was a crop failure in '79 and a loan called in early — a loan from a bank whose board, wouldn't you know, included two men who'd been trying to buy our bottom field for years. My grandfather was drowning. And then a man came to the house with papers and a pen and a story about restructuring the debt, and my grandfather — who trusted people, who was raised to believe a handshake meant something — signed what he was told was a refinancing agreement." She paused. "What he actually signed, according to the recording on file, was a sale. His signature is on it. But my mother swears the handwriting is wrong — too stiff, the descenders clipped. She thinks he traced something or was guided, or that the whole page was swapped after he signed a different one. We've never been able to prove anything. We're teachers and nurses and one very tired electrician. We don't have lawyers on retainer."

The playground noise washed over them — shrieking, bouncing balls, the metal groan of a swing set. Ines sat with her hands pressed flat against the bench's rough wood and felt the guilt arrive exactly as she'd known it would, settling into her chest like water finding low ground.

"He paid us," Amara said suddenly.

Ines looked up. "What?"

"For forty years. Small amounts. Cash, mostly, or money orders, no return address, always postmarked Bellhaven. My grandmother thought it was a scam at first. Then my grandfather figured it out — or guessed — and he never cashed another one. Kept them in the shoebox with everything else. Forty years of restitution from a man who couldn't say sorry out loud." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and she smoothed it over with visible effort. "Emil Marchetti knew. Whatever else is true, whatever the courts would ever decide — he knew what had been done, and he spent his whole life paying it back in secret because he was too much of a coward to give the land back and too decent to keep all of it."

"That's why the ledger doesn't balance," Ines whispered. It wasn't a question. The gaps Theo had found, the payments to someone unnamed going back four decades — she'd read them herself just last week, running her finger down columns of ciphered shorthand. "There are entries. Every year. Same date, roughly. Payments to—" She stopped. "We haven't decoded them yet. We didn't know who."

"You do now." Amara said it without triumph. That was somehow worse. If she'd been angry, Ines could have braced against it. Instead there was only this level, weary grief, the grief of a woman who had done this math in her head ten thousand times. "I want to be clear about something, Ms. Zhou. I don't blame you. You weren't born. Neither was I, when it happened. You inherited a title; I inherited a shoebox. Neither of us asked for any of it."

"But it's mine anyway," Ines said. "The benefit. I grew up on money that — the café, my whole life, my parents' stability, some of it traces back to—"

"To a crime," Amara said simply. "Yes. Welcome to inheritance. Everybody's got one; most people just never open the box." She glanced at her watch, and something in Ines sank — but then Amara surprised her by turning back fully, giving her the whole of her attention again. "You came here. That matters. Ellery told me you might. She also told me Vane's people have been sniffing around, offering money, asking questions about the Okafor parcel. Asking *me*, actually. A man in a nice coat came by the school in May. Said he represented interests interested in 'consolidating historic parcels.' I told him the only thing I'd consolidate was my foot in his—"

She stopped. Smiled, briefly, dangerously.

"So you know Corliss Vane," Ines said.

"I know what he is. Do you?" Amara studied her. "Because here's what I can't figure out, and I've been figuring it since the supper, where half the town saw you and Marchetti's boy sitting together looking like two people reading the same terrible book. Are you here because you want to make this right? Or are you here because Vane told you the Okafor claim is the fastest way to break the estate apart, and whoever gets to me first gets to use me?"

The question landed like a slap, clean and cold, and Ines understood — with a strange, dizzy respect — that this woman had survived being used before, by banks and lawyers and forty years of polite institutional silence, and had developed antibodies.

"I'm here because I found the ledger," Ines said. "And because my uncle wrote numbers to your family for forty years and never once put his apology in writing, and somebody should have made him, and he's dead, so it has to be me. And because—" Her throat tightened. She thought of the café, ninety days from gone, saved now only if the harvest held; she thought of Theo's face when he'd found the gaps in the accounts; she thought of the forged-deed rumor that had followed them both to the supper like smoke. "Because I don't want the orchard if it isn't ours to have. I don't know what that means legally. I don't know if it means anything. But I know I can't build a life on top of your family's grave and call it home."

Amara was quiet for a long moment. On the playground, the bell rang — not the end of recess, just the five-minute warning — and children began reluctantly gathering balls and driftwood and each other.

"You sound like him," Amara said finally. "My grandfather. He used to say a thing about land. *The ground doesn't care whose name is on the paper. It cares whose hands are in it.*" She stood, brushing off her skirt, and looked down at Ines with an expression that had thawed perhaps two degrees, which from this woman, Ines suspected, was practically an embrace. "Come back Sunday. Come to dinner. Bring the ledger — the real one, not copies. And bring Marchetti's grandson, if he's got the stomach for it. If you're serious about proving what happened to my grandfather's signature, you'll need three things: the original deed, the ledger, and a living witness who saw it done. We have none of those. Yet."

"Yet," Ines repeated.

"Yet." Amara almost smiled. "Forty years is a long time to wait. What's a few more weeks, if it means doing it right?" She paused at the edge of the blacktop, then turned back, and the teacher-voice dropped into something older and harder. "One more thing, Ms. Zhou. The man who brought the papers to my grandfather's house in 1980 — the one with the pen and the story. My grandfather described him once, near the end, when his memory was going soft and he mixed up decades. Young man, expensive shoes, worked for a land company out of Seattle. The company's name changed twice since then. But companies are like rivers — new name, same water."

Ice slid down Ines's spine. "Vane's firm."

"I never said the name. You did." Amara picked up her bag. "Sunday. Six o'clock. Don't be late — my mother cooks like she's feeding the whole county, and she takes lateness personally."

* * *

Ines sat on the bench for a long time after the bell rang and the hallway swallowed the children and the locust tree dropped its small green shadows across her hands.

She should go back to the café. There were orders to place, a cooler to beg forgiveness from, a hundred small fires that were hers alone to put out. Instead she pulled out her phone and stared at the screen, at Theo's name, at the message thread that had gone quiet since the supper — polite logistics, picking schedules, nothing with blood in it.

*Found the granddaughter,* she typed. Deleted it. Typed it again. Deleted it.

Some things you couldn't say through glass. Some things you had to drive across town and knock on a door for, the way she'd just done, the way she was apparently going to keep doing now, whether she wanted to or not, because the stone in her pocket had turned out not to be a stone at all but a seed, and seeds did what they liked regardless of your plans.

She thought about Amara's shoebox. Forty years of uncashed checks, kept as evidence, kept as accusation, kept — maybe — as hope. Somebody in that family had believed, all along, that someday someone would come asking. And now someone had.

Her phone buzzed. Theo: *Ledger's open on the kitchen table. Page 3, 1981. I think I finally see the pattern. Call me?*

Ines looked at the message for a long moment, thumb hovering, heart doing something complicated and unwelcome in her chest.

Then she started the truck and pointed it toward the orchard, and the name *Okafor* rode beside her the whole way — lighter now, somehow, and heavier both at once, the way truth always is.
