## 5. What the Songbook Keeps

The Vane house announced itself before the road did.

Beatrix had driven past it a hundred times in her life — the long gray shingle pile at the end of Harbor Lane, all widow's walks and weathered gables, the kind of house that looked like it had been arguing with the sea for a century and winning. But she had never driven *up* to it, never turned through the iron gate with the seahorse scrollwork, never parked her dented hatchback on crushed shell beside a Range Rover that cost more than three years of café revenue.

She sat in the car a moment longer than she needed to, hands on the wheel, engine ticking down.

*You need this,* she told herself. *That's all this is. A job. Flour and butter and a contract with your name on it.*

The trunk of her car was full of proof that she intended to keep it professional: sheet pans, cooling racks, a stand mixer she'd carried out of the café like a wounded animal, and four crates of ingredients measured to the gram. She'd spent two nights recalculating quantities for a hundred guests instead of forty regulars. She'd slept four hours. Her hands were steady anyway. They always were when they had work to do.

The kitchen, when the housekeeper let her in, was a betrayal of everything she'd expected. She'd imagined something cold and showroom-perfect, a kitchen no one cooked in. Instead it was warm-lit and slightly worn, copper pots hanging from a rail gone dull with use, a scarred pine table under the window. Someone had cooked here. Someone had cooked here for years.

*His mother,* Beatrix thought, and then refused to think about his mother at all.

She unpacked with the efficiency of ritual — flour first, then sugar, then butter unwrapped onto marble to soften. The memorial weekend was Saturday; today was Thursday, and Elias Vane had been precise about it: *three days of prep, full menu by Friday noon, my assistant will handle anything you need.* He had not been present when she arrived. She had checked. She would deny checking.

By nine she had the first cardamom buns proofing under cloth, the kitchen filling with the smell of yeast and warm spice, and by ten she was piping almond paste into tart shells while gulls argued over the harbor beyond the window. It was good work. It was the only kind of work that had ever made her feel safe — the alchemy of it, the way a recipe could not lie to you, could not leave without saying goodbye, could not buy the ground beneath your feet and call it opportunity.

The songbook came out of her bag at eleven.

She hadn't planned to bring it. But the memorial menu was built entirely from her grandmother's recipes — Ingrid Nystrom's cardamom buns, her sea-salt honey cakes, her plum torte with the lattice top that Beatrix had spent one whole failed winter learning to weave — and it felt wrong, somehow, to bake them here without her. Wronger still, if she was honest, to leave the book alone in the café overnight, in a building that belonged to someone else now. To Elias Vane. Everything felt like it belonged to someone else now.

So the songbook sat on the pine table in the borrowed kitchen, open to the honey cake page, propped against the flour canister like an old woman watching her granddaughter work.

It was a fat, ruined thing, the songbook. Seventy years of grease spots and thumbprints, its spine held together with surgical tape and stubbornness. Ingrid had written songs in it first — actual songs, verses and chords in a slanted hand — and then, somewhere along the way, the songs had stopped and the recipes had started, crowding into the margins, taking over pages, until the book became what it was: a record of everything the woman had loved enough to write down.

Beatrix knew every page of it. That was the thing people didn't understand about heirlooms — they weren't objects, they were conversations. She had been reading this book since she was six years old, standing on a chair at her grandmother's elbow, flour to her wrists. She knew which recipes were written fast and which were written slow. She knew the ones annotated in pencil — *less salt, Marta doesn't like it* — and the ones guarded in ink. She knew that page 61, the honey cake, had a small water stain shaped like Norway, and that Ingrid had drawn a tiny sailboat next to the cardamom bun recipe because Beatrix, age seven, had demanded one.

Which is why, when she flipped to page 88 for the plum torte and saw writing she didn't recognize, her hands went still.

Not unfamiliar handwriting — she knew Ingrid's hand the way she knew her own pulse. But unfamiliar *words*. A line of ink along the outer margin, faded to the color of weak tea, in a script more formal than her grandmother's, more schooled:

*For I., who makes even August taste of nothing else. — H.V.*

Beatrix stared at it.

H.V.

She read it again. Then again, as though repetition might dissolve it. The initials sat there in their faded confidence, patient as a tide line. *Who makes even August taste of nothing else.*

Her grandmother had died four years ago. In all those years of reading, of baking, of tracing these pages with floury fingers — she had never seen that line. But then, she had never needed to look at page 88's margin. The recipe filled the page. Margins were where you didn't look unless you were looking for something.

She was looking now.

She washed her hands. Dried them on her apron. Pulled the lamp closer, though the morning was bright enough, and began going through the book page by page, the way you'd search a lover's coat pockets.

There were others.

Page 12, the rye bread: a single word in that same formal hand — *again?* — with a small inked smile beneath it. Page 34, the fish soup: *Sunday, then. You know which door.* Page 57, the almond tarts Beatrix was, at that very moment, piping into shells across the room: *These undid me. Come before the light does.*

And on page 70, in Ingrid's own hand, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper, a reply that stopped Beatrix's breath in her chest:

*Last summer of this. After September we are only sensible women. Burn this if you love me.*

Below it, in the other hand, unhurried, almost tender:

*I have never once done what is sensible. — H.V.*

The kitchen sounds fell away — the hum of the refrigerator, the gulls, the tick of the oven — until there was only the paper and her own blood in her ears.

H.V. Henrik Vane. Elias's father. The man whose memorial portrait she'd seen in the local paper's archive when she'd researched the family after the sale — silver-haired, handsome in the way of men who had never been refused anything, dead two years now, survived by a widow who had just died herself, and one son.

One son who had left this town at nineteen without saying goodbye, and come back at thirty owning half of it.

Beatrix sat down slowly on the pine stool, the songbook open in her lap like something molting.

*After September we are only sensible women.*

September. She thought of her grandmother in Septembers — the way Ingrid had always gone quiet at the end of August, standing at the café window watching the ferries thin out, her hands folded like a woman waiting for a verdict. Beatrix had assumed it was the season ending. The tourists leaving. The money drying up.

It had been a door closing. Every year. For decades, apparently.

She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Ingrid had married Grandpa Nils in 1968. Nils had drowned in '79, when Mama was eleven — the official story, the stone in the churchyard, the story Beatrix had grown up feeding her own grief on. And these notes — some of the ink was newer than others, layers of it, strata of summers — some of these were written in ballpoint. Ballpoint that hadn't existed in 1968.

The affair hadn't been a season. It had been a *life*, running underneath her grandmother's sensible surface like an underground river, surfacing in margins, in Augusts, in a handwriting Beatrix had never once been shown.

*Burn this if you love me.*

Ingrid hadn't burned it. Ingrid, who had taught her that a recipe kept was a promise kept, who had locked her feelings down so tight that Beatrix had inherited the lock rather than the key — Ingrid had kept the one thing she'd sworn to destroy, hidden in plain sight, in the book she'd handed down like scripture.

Betrayal arrived strangely. Not as anger, not at first, but as vertigo — the floor of her childhood tilting, every memory quietly reassessing itself. The Septembers. The silences. The way her grandmother had flinched, actually *flinched*, the one time twelve-year-old Beatrix had asked about the Vane house on Harbor Lane. *We don't have business with those people,* Ingrid had said, in a voice like a slammed drawer.

They hadn't had business. They'd had something else entirely.

And now — now the son of H.V. owned her café, and she was standing in his mother's kitchen baking her grandmother's almond tarts, the same tarts that had *undone him*, page 57, and the universe was not subtle, the universe had never once been subtle, it was a gossip and a show-off and it had arranged all of this with its sleeves rolled up.

Across the room, the oven timer chimed. Beatrix didn't move.

She thought about the letter. Her letter — the one she'd written at nineteen and slipped into the Vanes' mailbox and never spoken of again, the confession she'd sealed shut like a wound. She'd written it in this same spirit, she realized now. This same inheritance. *Say it once, hide it forever, be sensible after September.* Her grandmother hadn't given her the lock. She'd given her the whole method.

*Come before the light does.*

Had Ingrid walked that path? Down Harbor Lane in the dark, apron still on, flour in her hair, letting herself in a door that wasn't hers while her daughter slept? Had she baked here, in this kitchen, in another woman's house, wearing the same expression Beatrix was wearing right now?

The front of the house sounded — a door, footsteps, the low register of a voice giving instructions. Elias. Home early. Of course he was; the universe was showing off.

Beatrix closed the songbook gently, the way you close a casket, and stood. She smoothed her apron. She checked the tarts, pulled them golden, set them to cool with steady hands. When he appeared in the kitchen doorway — sleeves rolled, that careful unreadable face, the face of a man who had learned composure from someone — she was elbow-deep in plum filling and absolutely, perfectly calm.

"Smells like my childhood," he said.

"I'm sure it smells like someone's," she said, and watched something flicker behind his eyes — quick, startled, gone.

He crossed to the counter, glanced at the tray of almond tarts, and smiled the professional smile she was beginning to hate. "My mother's favorite. How did you—"

"Lucky guess," Beatrix said.

Because she could not say: *your father wrote about these tarts in my grandmother's margins for thirty years.* Because she could not say: *we were entangled before either of us was born, and one of us has known since Tuesday and one of us has known since eleven o'clock this morning.* Because the songbook sat closed on the table between them, heavy as testimony, and she had not yet decided whether it was a weapon or a wound.

Elias looked at her a moment too long. "You're angry," he said. Not a question.

"I'm baking," Beatrix said. "Angry is extra."

He almost laughed — she saw it land and get swallowed — and then his phone buzzed and he stepped toward the hall to take it, and Beatrix stood alone in his mother's kitchen with plum filling on her knuckles and her whole inheritance rewritten in fading ink.

On the table, the songbook's cover had curled back an inch, just enough to show the edge of page 57.

*These undid me.*

She understood, suddenly, with terrible clarity, exactly what she was holding. Not recipes. Leverage. The kind that could stop a development, unmake a family's shining name, burn down everything the Vanes had built on being sensible.

The question — the one that would follow her down every corridor of the coming days — was whether she was her grandmother's granddaughter or her grandmother's ghost.

Behind her, in the hall, Elias lowered his voice and said into the phone, "No. She doesn't know yet. And she won't — not before Saturday."

Beatrix's hands went still in the bowl.
