## 9. The Last Ferry of the Season

**Dominic**

The ferry dock smelled like diesel and cold salt, and Dominic had been standing on it for forty minutes pretending to read a notice about winter mooring rates.

He'd read the same sentence eleven times. *All vessels must be removed from the water by November 15.* He could have recited it in his sleep. He could probably recite it in Ada's voice, which was the real problem, because everything in his life lately came back around to her voice eventually — the way she said his name like she was testing whether it still fit, the way she'd laughed two nights ago when the tractor wouldn't start and he'd threatened to negotiate with it personally.

The hearing had ended an hour ago. He'd been there. Half the town had been there, wedged into the community hall that usually hosted the harvest potluck, sitting under paper streamers nobody had taken down since July. And he'd watched Marcus Hale stand up with his lawyers and his maps and his confident smile, and watched all of it come apart like wet cardboard.

Because of her. Because Ada Reyes had stood up in front of everyone — her former boss included — and told them about a survey error from 1987, a boundary line that had never been legally corrected, a technicality so dry it should have come with its own packet of crackers. She'd found it in three days of digging through county records after she'd already lost her job for testifying against the project at all. She'd handed the whole thing to the town attorney like she was passing over a recipe.

Hale's claim hadn't just collapsed. It had evaporated. His lead lawyer had packed up mid-session, and Hale himself had sat very still for a long moment before leaving without looking at anyone, which was somehow worse than if he'd shouted.

And then Ada had walked out of the hall, past Dominic, close enough that their shoulders almost touched, and said only: "The last ferry is at four."

That was it. That was the whole conversation.

Now it was 3:47 and he was reading mooring regulations and trying not to do math about how long it took to walk from the community hall to the dock, or how long it took to pack a life, or whether a person who had once left him for eleven years might find it easier to leave again than to stay.

"You're going to wear a hole in those planks," said Mrs. Okonkwo-adjacent — no, Mrs. Delgado, who ran the bait shop and had known him since he was nine and had apparently appointed herself witness to this entire ordeal. She was sitting on a cooler with a thermos, knitting something aggressively orange.

"I'm just waiting for the ferry," he said.

"Mm." She turned a row. "I've been waiting for ferries since before you were born. Nobody waits for a ferry. You either catch one or you don't." A pause, needles clicking. "She testified against her own company, you know. My niece works at the county office. Says that girl pulled records until midnight every night this week. Fell asleep at the microfiche machine Tuesday. Woke up with a stripe of ink across her face."

Dominic's chest did something complicated. "I didn't know that."

"Course you didn't. You were here being stubborn, and she was there being stubborn, and the two of you are so busy being stubborn separately that—" The ferry horn sounded, long and low, rolling across the gray water. Mrs. Delgado looked up. "Well. Here's your chance to be stubborn together."

Down the road, a figure appeared. One suitcase, rolling behind her. Coat collar up against the wind. Hair doing whatever it wanted, as always.

Dominic forgot how to breathe in a completely ordinary, inconvenient way.

* * *

**Ada**

The suitcase weighed almost nothing, and that was the strange part. Eleven years of building a career, and it all fit in one bag: some clothes, a laptop she wasn't sure she'd ever open again, a framed photo of her parents she'd rescued from her desk on her last day — carried out through an office where people looked at her the way you look at someone who has jumped off something high.

Her resignation letter was already sent. Her severance was already forfeited. Hale had made sure of that the moment she stood up at the town meeting, and then again, more thoroughly, when she'd handed the boundary documents to opposing counsel. There was a word for what she'd done to her career, and the word was *ended*, and the astonishing thing was how little it hurt compared to what she'd expected.

What hurt was the walk to the dock. Every step closer, the fear got louder: *he won't be there. Why would he be there? You asked him to trust you twice and burned him both times. You told him to leave you alone in a vineyard in June and he did, and men who learn that lesson don't unlearn it—*

And then she saw him.

Standing at the end of the dock in his ridiculous canvas jacket, hands jammed in pockets, next to an old woman with a thermos and knitting needles who was watching them both with the frank delight of a woman at a play. Ada stopped walking.

He didn't wave. He didn't call out. He just stood there, and she understood that this was deliberate — that he was giving her the last few yards to cross on her own terms, the way you hold a gate open without pushing anyone through it.

She walked. The suitcase wheels rattled on the planks. When she reached him, she set it down and said, "The ferry's late sometimes. In autumn. I checked."

"I know," he said. "I've been reading about it." He nodded at the notice board. "Winter mooring rates. Riveting stuff."

A laugh escaped her — small, cracked, real. "Dominic."

"Yeah?"

"I don't have a job."

"I heard."

"And one suitcase."

"It's a very efficient suitcase."

"And I torched my career in front of the entire town, and I'd do it again, and I need you to know that I'm not asking you to fix any of it. I'm not even asking for—" Her voice wobbled and she hated it and kept going anyway. "I'm asking if the seat next to you is taken. On the ferry. That's all. Just the ferry."

Mrs. Delgado made a sound that was suspiciously like a suppressed sob and pretended to knit faster.

Dominic looked at her for a long moment. Wind off the water. Gulls arguing overhead. Then he picked up her suitcase — lifted it easily, frowned at it. "This weighs nothing."

"I know."

"Eleven years, Ada. And it fits in one bag?"

"The important things don't pack," she said. "They stay put. They wait." She looked at him. "Like orchards."

Something moved through his face — the whole history of it, the anger and the grief and the summer they'd just survived, all of it settling into something quieter. He held out his hand. Not to shake. Palm up, open, the way you offer your hand to someone crossing a gap.

She took it.

* * *

**Dominic**

The ferry came in slow, pushing gray water ahead of it, and they boarded together with one suitcase between them and no plan beyond the crossing itself.

They sat on the outside bench at the stern because Ada liked to watch where she'd been, and Dominic liked to watch where they were going, and for eleven years neither of them had compromised on anything, so they sat angled toward each other instead, knees touching, solving nothing and everything.

The island fell away behind them — the vineyard rows going gold and rust, the orchard a dark green block on the hillside that he could pick out instantly, would always be able to pick out instantly, the way you can find your own house in any photograph. Somewhere in that orchard was a tin box full of letters from two kids who hadn't known what they were losing. Somewhere on that hillside was a deed with two names on it, and as of yesterday, thanks to a survey error older than both of them, it was untouchable.

"You know what's funny?" Ada said, watching the island shrink.

"Tell me."

"Hale spent two years and half a million dollars trying to buy that land. And it turns out the thing that saved it was a filing clerk in 1987 who couldn't draw a straight line."

"Not funny," Dominic said. "Perfect. There's a difference." He paused. "My uncle knew, you know. About us. Before we did, maybe. He engineered the whole deed — I found the letters. He bet on us."

"He bet on two idiots," Ada said fondly.

"He bet on two idiots *with land*," Dominic corrected. "There's a difference there too."

The ferry horn sounded as they cleared the point. Ahead, the mainland lights were coming on one by one in the early dark, and Ada leaned into his shoulder, and he let his cheek rest against her hair, and neither of them said anything for a long while, because some crossings don't need narration.

Then she stirred. "Dominic."

"Mm."

"When we get back — and we're coming back, I've decided, I hope that's clear — there's something in my suitcase I want to show you. I pulled it from the county archive the night before the hearing. My uncle's signature. On the original sale papers, 1994. Your uncle bought out my family's share." She tilted her head up to look at him, eyes bright with something between mischief and tears. "But there's a second page. A right of first refusal, never exercised. My family's claim on the orchard never actually expired."

Dominic stared at her. "Ada. Are you telling me the orchard was legally ours — *both* of ours — the entire time?"

"I'm telling you," she said, smiling now, really smiling, "that my family never stopped meaning to come home. And neither did I."

The ferry cut through the dark water, carrying them toward the mainland lights, and Dominic Okonkwo — who had inherited debts, and deeds, and a woman with legal standing — started to laugh, and couldn't stop, and didn't want to.
