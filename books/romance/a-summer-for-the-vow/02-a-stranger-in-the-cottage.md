## 2. A Stranger in the Cottage

The boat had been my father's, which meant the boat was a liar. Every plank of her said *patience*, said *take your time*, said *there is no grief that forty grit sandpaper can't eventually smooth* — and I'd been believing her for three weeks now, standing in Theo Amberdale's side yard with the hull of the *Maren Lee* propped on sawhorses like a patient who refuses to die and refuses to recover.

I was fairing the port chine when I heard the porch door open for the fourth time that morning. No footsteps followed it. Just the door, opening and closing like a mouth deciding whether to say something.

I straightened up, pressed the back of my wrist to my forehead, and looked over at the cottage.

Theo stood on the porch with a mug of coffee she wasn't drinking, staring at a section of railing as if it had insulted her grandmother. She'd been doing this all week — coming out, looking at something, going back in. The porch rail had a patch of weathered gray wood where the storm had chewed through the paint, and every day she looked at it, and every day she did not touch it, and every day the gray patch got a little more confident.

"Morning," I called.

She startled badly enough that coffee sloshed over the rim. "You're here early."

"I'm always here early. You just usually pretend not to notice until nine."

"That's not pretending. That's scheduling." She came down the steps, mug clutched like a defensive weapon. "How's the boat?"

"Dishonest."

"Boats usually are."

I set down the sander and wiped my hands on a rag that had given up being a rag sometime last Tuesday. Up close she looked worse than from across the yard — not bad, exactly, but *elsewhere*. There's a particular look people get when they're carrying something they haven't decided to put down yet. My mother used to have it. I know it the way you know a weather pattern: by the shape of the sky before anything falls.

"You've got a spot there," I said, nodding at the railing. "Been watching it grow all week."

"It's on the list."

"The list."

"The list of things to fix." She said it with the crisp authority of a woman who has many lists and trusts them all implicitly. "It's number six."

"What are numbers one through five?"

"None of your business."

"Fair enough." I picked up my rag again, folded it, unfolded it. "Want some help? With number six, I mean. Sanding's mostly waiting anyway, and I'm between coats."

She looked at me the way people look at an offer they can't find the catch in. "Why?"

"Because if I watch you stare at that railing one more day, I'm going to have to say something about it, and then we'll both be uncomfortable, and I live in your cottage, so statistically most of the discomfort lands on me."

A corner of her mouth moved. Not quite a smile — more like a smile checking whether the coast was clear. "You're very direct for someone renting my guest cottage."

"I'm very direct for someone whose father built boats and never once told him what he actually thought about anything. Directness is the family rebellion."

That landed somewhere — I saw it land, saw her glance flick toward the *Maren Lee* and away again. Everyone in Saltmere Bay knew whose boat I was restoring; the harbormaster had told me the second day, with the gentle pity people reserve for orphans and men fixing their dead fathers' mistakes. But Theo didn't ask. That was the thing I liked about her, insofar as I'd let myself like anything about anyone in this town: she didn't ask questions she wasn't prepared to hear answers to.

Which made it strange, then, that she was so obviously sitting on one herself.

"All right," she said finally. "Number six. But I'm supervising."

"Naturally."

"And we use my sandpaper. Yours has sand in it."

"That's — it's sandpaper. It's supposed to—"

"*My* sandpaper, Callum."

So we worked. She held the light — an extension cord run out the kitchen window, because the porch socket had died with the storm and was apparently number eleven on the list — and I worked the orbital sander along the rail while she watched the dust curl off the wood like smoke. The morning was one of those coastal mornings that can't decide what it wants to be, sun and fog trading custody of the yard every few minutes.

"You're good at that," she said, after a while.

"At sanding?"

"At making it look easy. My grandfather could do that too. Make hard work look like a conversation he was having with the material."

"Your grandfather built this place back?"

"His father built it. He kept it. Thirty-one years as keeper before they automated the lamp." She said it evenly, but her thumb moved against the side of the mug, a small repetitive motion, like worry learning to be quiet. "He died in March."

"I heard. I'm sorry."

"Everyone heard. Small towns are very efficient at grief distribution." She paused. "That came out wrong."

"It didn't."

She glanced at me sideways, reassessing. I went back to the rail. There's a rhythm to sanding that lets conversations happen sideways — nobody has to hold eye contact, nobody has to commit. I'd learned that in the three weeks since I'd arrived, which was also roughly the length of time I'd been having conversations with anyone in Saltmere Bay longer than *fill it up, please* or *how much for the bait*.

"So how does a man from—" she squinted at me, "—wherever you're from end up restoring a fishing boat in my yard?"

"Essex. Originally. And I told you. My father's boat."

"That's the *what*, not the *why*. Why here? You didn't know him here. Did you?" Something moved behind her eyes when she said it — quick, involuntary, gone. "Know him here."

"No," I said. Which was true, as far as it went. "I found the registration papers after he died. Last home port: Saltmere Bay. Eleven years ago he sold the *Maren Lee* and we moved inland and he never said why, and then in February he was gone, and I figured the least I owed him was to come see the place where the story changed."

It was more than I'd meant to say. It was maybe half of what there was to say. I watched her do the arithmetic on it — watched her decide, visibly, not to push.

"Eleven years ago," she repeated, quietly, like she was filing it somewhere.

"Why'd you come out here four times this morning without touching anything?" I asked, because fair was fair, and because watching her flinch was easier than feeling myself flinch.

"I didn't—"

"Four times. I count things. Occupational hazard of restoration — you learn to notice what's worn and what's not."

For a long moment she looked at the gray patch of railing instead of at me. The fog rolled back in over the yard, softening everything, making the lighthouse above us look like something remembered rather than something standing.

"There's a letter," she said at last. Then, quickly, as if closing a drawer: "Never mind. It's nothing. It's probably nothing."

"Things that are nothing don't get looked at four times a morning."

"You don't know that."

"I know that about myself, at least." I killed the sander. The sudden quiet sat between us. "Look — you don't have to tell me. I'm the guy renting your cottage. I'm aware of the terms. But I'll say this: whatever it is, it's been sitting with you for days, and things that sit that long stop being problems and start being tenants. I would know. I'm currently both."

That got the almost-smile again, fuller this time, and then she caught it and put it away, the way you'd put away something breakable before company arrives.

"You're strange," she informed me.

"I've had complaints."

We worked another hour. She stopped supervising around the twenty-minute mark and started sanding the section nearest the steps herself, sleeves pushed up, a smudge of gray dust appearing on her cheekbone like a beauty mark applied by accident. I noticed it. I noticed that I noticed it, and filed that under *not useful*, where I filed most things.

Because here's the truth I hadn't said to anyone, not even to the boat: I hadn't come to Saltmere Bay just for the story's setting. In my jacket pocket, folded into eighths until it was soft as cloth, was half a photograph — the left half, showing the base of a lighthouse and the edge of a woman's shoulder, torn down the middle with a violence that said *this was done in anger or in fear, not in tidiness*. My mother's handwriting on the back, faded: *the summer we were happy.* No year. No explanation. Just that, and the fact that she'd kept it hidden in her jewelry box for thirty years, and the fact that three weeks after I found it, she packed a bag in the night and vanished, and the fact that the only landmark in the photograph was a lighthouse, and the only lighthouse I could find matching its profile was the one standing at the top of Theo Amberdale's hill, watching us work right now with its blind white eye.

I wasn't ready to say any of that. I barely had it arranged in my own head. Some stories you tell; some stories you carry until they're heavy enough to speak for themselves.

But I watched Theo sanding her railing with that careful, controlled motion of hers — like even her arms were keeping lists — and I thought: *she's carrying something too. Something with a date on it.*

"Callum." She'd stopped working. She was looking past me, up the hill, at the lighthouse. "Can I ask you something strange?"

"I'd be disappointed otherwise."

"When you were looking for a place to stay — why this cottage? Out of all the rentals in the bay?"

I turned the question over and found, to my surprise, that I didn't have to lie. "Honestly? The listing photo. There was a shot taken from the garden path, and the lighthouse was in the background, and I don't know. It felt like a signpost." I shrugged. "Stupid, probably."

"No," she said slowly. Her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely — eleven years elsewhere, maybe, or further. "No, I don't think it's stupid."

The fog chose that moment to lift all at once, the way it does, and the whole yard came into focus: the boat, the porch, the two of us standing in sawdust like people who'd known each other longer than a month. Theo brushed the dust from her hands and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read — part apology, part decision.

"There's something I need to check," she said. "In town. At the archive."

"Okay."

"It might be nothing."

"Things that are nothing," I said, "don't get checked."

She almost laughed. Then she was gone up the path toward the lane, walking fast, and I stood alone on her porch holding her sandpaper, looking at the lighthouse, and looking at the *Maren Lee*, and feeling — for the first time since I'd arrived — like the two things were somehow pointing at each other.

Like the tide had started turning, and neither of us had asked it to.
