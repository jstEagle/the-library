## 2. The Reunion Clause

The ferry from the mainland took forty minutes, and Marisol Reyes spent thirty-nine of them rehearsing excuses.

*I can't stay the whole weekend. Work emergency. Client crisis. Something with a deadline.* All of it true in spirit, none of it usable, because her mother had already texted twice this morning—*the reading is Saturday at ten, don't be late*—and Elena Reyes did not accept excuses. She accepted attendance. She accepted posture. She accepted daughters who came home when summoned and sat where they were told.

Marisol stood at the rail as Harbor's Rest assembled itself out of the fog: the crooked lighthouse, the cannery roofline gone rust-red, the inn her grandmother had run for fifty years sitting at the harbor's edge like a ship that had decided, at some point, to stop sailing and simply be loved instead. The Reyes House. Even from the water she could feel its gravity, that old pull of salt bread and Sunday dinners and being known by everyone whether you wanted to be or not.

She hadn't been back in six years. Six years, two months, eleven days—not that she was counting, except that she was, the way you count anything that's become a scar you press on to make sure it still hurts.

Her phone buzzed. Her sister-in-law, Priya: *Everyone's asking when you arrive. Theo's here already.*

Theo.

Marisol closed her eyes. Of course he was. Theo Calloway had never once missed an opportunity to be present, polished, and devastatingly casual about both. He'd be standing in the inn's front parlor right now in some jacket that cost more than her car, saying things like *funny running into you* to relatives who would then spend the weekend reporting every interaction back to her mother like court stenographers.

And the family. God, the family. Aunt Rosa, who asked about her love life the way other people asked about weather systems—tracking fronts, predicting storms. Cousin Benjie, who'd bring up the fraud at dinner because Benjie brought up everything at dinner. And her mother, whose disapproval didn't need words anymore; it had achieved a kind of efficiency, communicated entirely through the angle of her chin.

*You could still turn around,* she told herself, watching the dock grow closer. *You could stay on this ferry until it turns around and ride it back and never get off.*

But Abuela's will was being read Saturday, and whatever strange final instructions Ines Reyes had left behind—and there would be instructions; the woman had once left Marisol a letter containing nothing but a recipe and the sentence *you'll understand when you're older*, and Marisol was thirty-four and still didn't understand—whatever she'd left, Marisol owed it to her to be in that room. Even if the room contained everyone else.

The gangway dropped. Marisol walked down it with her one suitcase and her heart going like a trapped bird, and made herself a promise: she would survive this weekend the way she survived everything. Alone, over-prepared, and with no one allowed close enough to see her flinch.

It lasted four hours.

* * *

Sugarline Café sat on Cordova Street like a held breath—small, warm-windowed, smelling so aggressively of butter and cardamom that Marisol stopped dead on the sidewalk outside it, ambushed by memory. This place hadn't existed when she lived here. It was new, which meant it belonged to the version of Harbor's Rest that had gone on without her, which meant she should hate it, which meant instead she pushed open the door because her hands were shaking and she needed coffee and possibly an entire cake eaten alone in a corner.

The man behind the counter looked up, and something in her chest performed a small unauthorized maneuver.

Lena Okafor. She knew him distantly—the way you know people in a small town, through accumulated rumor and a handful of shared rooms. He'd come to Harbor's Rest maybe four years ago and opened this café against everyone's prediction that it would fail within a season. It hadn't failed. His pastries had won things. He was quiet in a way people mistook for coldness until they watched him hand a free morning bun to the fisherman's kid who hung around every afternoon, and then they understood it was something else: a man who kept his warmth under glass, like the display case, visible but not for sale.

"Rough crossing?" he said, because she was still standing in the doorway looking like a woman fleeing a crime scene.

"The crossing was fine." She approached the counter. "Everything after the crossing is the problem."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. A smile considering an application. "What can I get you?"

"Whatever has the most butter. And a coffee large enough to swim in."

He turned to the case without comment and came back with a slice of something layered and golden and a mug the size of a soup bowl. When she paid, he said, "You're one of the Reyes sisters," not as a question.

"Marisol." She offered her hand across the counter, absurdly formal, and he shook it. His grip was warm and brief. "You're Lena. My cousin Priya says your kouign-amann changed her religion."

"Priya is generous." He wiped his hands on his apron. "Back for the reading?"

The question landed exactly where all the week's questions had been landing, in the soft bruised part. "Saturday," Marisol said. "I'm staying at the inn tonight. Family's gathering tomorrow." She picked up her plate and mug and retreated to the window table before she could say anything else stupid, and for twenty minutes she ate the pastry—it was, infuriatingly, transcendent—and watched the harbor and tried to build a plan.

Here was the plan she had: walk into the Reyes House Friday evening. Endure approximately forty hours of scrutiny. Attend the reading. Leave.

Here was the flaw in the plan: she would do all of it alone, and alone was how she'd done everything since she'd left, and alone was precisely what made her mother's chin tilt and Aunt Rosa's questions sharpen and Theo's charm land like a verdict. Alone, she was *poor Marisol, who couldn't hold on to anything*. Accompanied—even platonically, even obviously strategically—she became someone who had chosen, someone who had options, someone the town had to look at slightly differently.

She needed a shield. Not a boyfriend. A buffer. Someone who could stand next to her at dinner and absorb the ambient fire.

She looked up. Lena was refilling the pastry case, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with flour, moving through his kitchen with the economy of a man who trusted his own hands completely. He was, she noted with detached professional interest, objectively attractive in a way that had nothing to do with the plan—dark eyes, careful mouth, the kind of stillness that made other people fill silence for him. He was also, crucially, a stranger to the Reyes family drama. Neutral territory. Switzerland with excellent laminated dough.

It was a terrible idea. It was such a terrible idea that she was already carrying her plate back to the counter.

"I have a proposition," she said.

He glanced up. "We close at four."

"It's not a pastry proposition."

"I assumed." He set down the tongs and gave her his full attention, and she discovered that being looked at by Lena Okafor was a physical event, like stepping into sun. "Go on."

And here is what Marisol had planned to say: something measured, something with an exit ramp, something like *this is unusual but hear me out*. What actually came out, propelled by six years of loneliness and thirty-nine minutes of ferry rehearsal collapsing all at once, was:

"Marry me."

Silence. A espresso machine hissed somewhere. Lena's eyebrows achieved altitude.

"That came out wrong," she said quickly. "Not marry. Fake. Fake-date me. This weekend. At my family reunion." The words kept coming, unstoppable now, a burst pipe. "My grandmother's will is being read and my entire family will be there including my ex-boyfriend who is beautiful and successful and makes me look like the family disappointment, and I cannot—I am not doing it alone. So. You. Be my boyfriend for two days. And I'll pay you."

She heard herself finish and wanted to lie down on the floor of the café and be absorbed into the floorboards.

Lena was quiet long enough that she counted three full breaths. Then he said, "Pay me."

"Yes."

"To pretend to date you."

"For one weekend. Two nights maximum. You'd have to be convincing—my family notices everything—but you seem—" *calm, solid, like a wall that bakes* "—capable."

"You want to hire me," he said slowly, "to be your boyfriend."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"How else would I say it?"

"Like it's a business arrangement! Which it is. People hire—people rent—there are professionals for this in Japan, probably. I'm offering you a weekend gig. Room and board at the inn, plus a fee. Name a fee." She was babbling. She could hear herself babbling and could not stop, the way you can't stop a cart rolling downhill. "I have savings. Not enormous savings, but real ones. I've been saving for—never mind why. Consider it hazard pay. My aunt Rosa will ask you about your intentions within nine minutes of meeting you."

Something happened to his face then—a tightening, quick and involuntary, gone almost before it arrived, like a cloud crossing water. She'd learn later what it meant. In the moment she read it as hesitation about the money, or the weirdness, or the fact that a stranger had proposed fake marriage to him between the pastry case and the espresso machine.

"One weekend," he said finally.

"One weekend."

"No real feelings."

"God, no." She laughed, relieved, too loud. "Absolutely none. Feelings are off the table. We'll put it in writing if you want. I'm serious—name your number."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she had the odd sensation of being weighed, assessed, sorted into some internal ledger she couldn't see. Whatever he found seemed to decide him.

"We can talk terms," he said. "Tomorrow. After the lunch rush."

"Terms," Marisol repeated, dizzy with relief and terror in equal measure. "Yes. Terms. I'll come back at—"

"Three."

"Three." She stuck out her hand again. He took it, and this time the shake lasted half a second longer than business required, though she'd spend the rest of the day telling herself she imagined it.

She gathered her plate and mug, walked to the door, and paused with her hand on the handle.

"Lena? Why are you even considering this?"

He was already turning back to his case, arranging croissants with unnecessary precision. "Good rate of pay," he said, without looking up. "For low-risk work."

It was a lie. She knew it was a lie the way you know rain is coming—not from any single sign but from the whole pressure of the air. But she was desperate, and desperation is a poor reader of faces, so she smiled, said "See you at three," and stepped out into the gray-gold afternoon feeling, for the first time in six years, like she might survive the weekend.

Behind her, through the warm glass, she saw him stop arranging the croissants and stand very still, one flour-dusted hand pressed flat against the counter, like a man who had just signed something.

She didn't wonder what. That was the trouble with transactions—you only ever check your own ledger.

At three o'clock the next day, she'd have her answer about the fee. What she wouldn't have, for a long time yet, was his.
