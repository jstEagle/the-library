## 6. Rain on the Glasshouse

The storm came in off the water like it had a grudge to settle.

Beatrix felt it first as a pressure behind her eyes, the same barometric ache she'd known since childhood — the orchard's own weather-sense, her grandmother used to say, bred into anyone who stayed long enough. By late afternoon the sky over Vane Orchard had gone the color of a bruise, and by five o'clock the first fat drops were hammering the slate roof of the great house like an impatient visitor.

She had escaped to the glasshouse on purpose.

It stood at the far end of the walled garden, a skeleton of white-painted iron and fogged panes that had once been her great-grandfather's pride. Now half the roof was missing, ferns grew up through the cracked flagstones, and a family of wrens had claimed the broken cold frame as their personal estate. It was, in Beatrix's opinion, the only honest structure on the property: everything else at Vane was maintained for show, but the glasshouse had simply been allowed to become what time made of it.

She'd brought her notebook, half-hoping the storm would shake something loose in the stalled second novel. What she got instead was Elias Thorne, ducking through the door twenty minutes later with rain streaming off his jaw and his shirt plastered to his shoulders.

"The inn lost power," he said, wringing out a sleeve. "Marcus cornered me in the dark and started talking about zoning variances. I judged the glasshouse the lesser threat."

"Statistically, you may be right. Marcus has never physically injured anyone. He just makes them wish they'd been somewhere else for forty-five minutes."

Elias laughed — really laughed, not the polished hotelier version — and shook the rain from his hair like a dog, which was so unguarded and so unlike the man who'd charmed Aunt Rosalind at dinner that Beatrix had to look away and pretend great interest in a dead fig tree.

They settled on an old iron bench beneath the one intact section of roof. The storm built around them, thunder rolling down from the ridge, rain drumming on the surviving panes until the whole glasshouse seemed to be playing some vast percussion piece. Lightning turned everything silver for a heartbeat — the ferns, the wrens' nest, the sharp line of Elias's profile as he watched the sky.

"My brother hated storms," Elias said, apropos of nothing.

Beatrix kept her eyes on the rain. They had a rule about this — rule one, no real names of exes, no excavating each other's wreckage. But rules, she was learning, were like the glasshouse roof: full of gaps.

"You don't have to—"

"He loved them, actually. That's what I mean." Elias's voice was quiet under the drumming. "He'd stand at windows like this one and watch the whole thing like it was a play put on for him. He wasn't afraid of anything." A pause. "That's what makes it worse, I suppose. You can't even tell yourself fear would have stopped him."

Beatrix didn't ask what *it* was. She understood instinctively that the shape of the wound mattered less than the fact of it. Instead she said, "My grandmother used to bring me here during storms. She said the glasshouse was the best seat in the house for weather. 'Everything else in this family pretends,' she told me. 'The glass doesn't pretend.'"

"That sounds like a woman I'd have liked."

"She's difficult to like. She's magnificent and terrible, like the sea." Beatrix hugged her knees. "I keep trying to write her into my novel and she keeps coming out wrong. Too soft or too cruel. Never whatever she actually is."

"Maybe you're too close. Maybe you need someone who's never met her to tell you what they see."

"And what do you see? You've met her exactly once, through a closed door while she interrogated Rosalind about the catering."

"She looked at you across that room," Elias said slowly, "like she was checking your teeth before a sale."

Beatrix barked a laugh that surprised her. "God. Yes. Exactly that."

The thunder crashed closer. The wrens scolded from their nest. And in the strange blue light between lightning strikes, Beatrix became aware of several things at once: how near Elias's shoulder was to hers; that she could smell rain on him and something underneath it, clean soap and cedar; that she had spent four days memorizing the weight of his hand when it touched hers for the cousins' benefit, and had told herself it was research.

"I should be writing," she said, without moving.

"You should," he agreed, without moving either.

"This is a professional disaster. My editor would weep."

"Then we'll blame the storm. Force majeure. Every good contract has a clause for it."

Something in the word *clause* made them both laugh, giddy and slightly hysterical, two people laughing at a joke only they fully understood. And then the laughter faded and neither of them filled the silence, because filling it would have required admitting what was actually happening, which was that the space between them had been shrinking all afternoon, all week, all four days of a fiction that had stopped feeling fictional somewhere around the second shared joke.

Lightning flashed. In its afterimage Beatrix saw that Elias was looking at her — not the practiced, charming look he deployed at dinners, but something rawer, almost frightened.

"We should probably—" she began.

"Yes," he said, and did not move away.

She kissed him. Or he kissed her. Later she could never reconstruct the physics of it, only the fact: rain hammering the glass, thunder swallowing her heartbeat, and Elias Thorne's hand coming up to cradle her jaw like she was something breakable and precious, which was precisely how she felt. He tasted like the cider they'd stolen from the cellar. His other hand found her waist and pulled her closer and she went willingly, gloriously, forgetting every rule she'd written, forgetting the cousins and the will and the year-long trial, forgetting everything except the astonishing discovery that kissing Elias Thorne felt less like pretending than anything she had done in years.

When they finally broke apart, gasping, foreheads touching, the storm had softened to a steady wash.

"Oh no," Beatrix said.

* * *

"Oh no," she said again, more firmly, pulling back to arm's length as though distance could retroactively undo it. "No, no, no. That was — okay. That was fine. That was strategic."

Elias blinked. Water dripped somewhere in the ferns. "Strategic."

"For the cousins. Obviously." She was on her feet now, pacing the flagstones, hands gesturing wildly. "Marcus has been watching us like a hawk with a spreadsheet. If we're going to sell this, we need — authenticity. Method acting. Total commitment to the bit. So that kiss was essentially — it was craft, Elias. It was *craftsmanship*."

He rose slowly. She watched the warmth drain out of his face by degrees, replaced by the careful hotelier mask, and hated herself for every degree.

"Craftsmanship," he repeated.

"Very convincing craftsmanship. Rosalind won't suspect a thing."

"No," he said quietly. "I don't imagine she will."

The silence stretched. Outside, the rain gentled further, as if the storm itself were embarrassed on her behalf. Beatrix heard her own words replaying and winced. *For the cousins.* She had kissed him like he was oxygen and then handed him a receipt.

"Elias—"

"I think I'll walk back," he said. "Before it gets dark. The path's still passable."

"You'll be soaked."

"I'm already soaked." He picked up his jacket, paused at the door with his hand on the wet iron frame. For a moment she thought he might say something else — the set of his shoulders suggested words queued up and waiting. Then the moment passed. "Goodnight, Beatrix."

The door banged shut behind him. The wrens complained. Beatrix sat back down on the bench and put her face in her hands.

*Idiot,* she told herself. *Absolute, prize-winning idiot.*

Because here was the truth she'd fled from: the kiss hadn't been for the cousins. It hadn't been craft or strategy or method anything. It had been the most honest thing she'd done since arriving at Vane, and honesty terrified her more than any inheritance trial, because honesty meant wanting something she could lose. Her whole life she'd protected herself with irony and small ambitions and a lighthouse cottage three hours from everyone who might disappoint her. Elias had walked through a gatehouse and straight past every defense she owned, and instead of saying so, she'd handed him a cover story and watched him fold it into his pocket like a bill he was owed.

She sat in the empty glasshouse a long time, listening to the drip of rain off the broken roof, doing sums she couldn't balance.

* * *

Elias took the long way back, deliberately, letting the thinning rain soak him a second time. It gave him something to feel besides the memory of her mouth and the sound of his own name in her voice when she said *oh no.*

He had been here before. Not the glasshouse, not the kiss — but the moment after, the moment where a warm thing turned transactional in his hands. His father had taught him that move without ever meaning to: take something real, slap a price tag on it, call it business. Elias had spent thirty-eight years swearing he was nothing like Harold Thorne, and tonight he'd discovered the resemblance ran deeper than blood. Because he'd wanted it to be real — God, he'd wanted it — and instead of saying so, he'd let her retreat, let her file the kiss under *work*, because pushing back meant risking the answer.

Cowardice, he decided, trudging through the wet grass, wore many costumes. Tonight it wore hers.

He was cutting along the hedge row behind the stables when a figure detached itself from the shadow of the tack room. Marcus Vane, holding a large black umbrella he clearly considered beneath him to use properly, stepped into Elias's path with the smoothness of a man who had rehearsed ambushes in mirrors.

"Mr. Thorne. Caught in the weather?"

"Just heading in."

"Mm." Marcus smiled his boardroom smile. "You know, I've been watching you this week. You're better at this than you let on. The devoted boyfriend act — very convincing. Almost like there's something real under it." He let that hang. "Which is why I'm puzzled that Bea's been spending her evenings in the archives. Grandmother's study. The old ledgers. Very odd hobby for a woman supposedly focused on impressing the family."

Elias kept his face neutral. "People have hobbies."

"People with something to hide do." Marcus reached into his coat and produced a card, holding it out between two fingers like a cigarette. "I represent certain parties — developers, investors, people with vision — who'd very much like to know what Bea thinks she's found in those ledgers. And whether Grandmother's little trial can be... managed. You seem like a practical man, Thorne. Practical men understand that loyalty is lovely, but it doesn't pay off lawsuits."

The word *lawsuits* landed like a hook set deep. Elias thought of Daniel's lawyer's letter sitting unanswered in his email, the number at the bottom of it, the ruin his brother was building toward. He thought of Beatrix's face in the lightning, and her voice afterward, wrapping the truest thing he'd felt in years and labeling it *craft.*

He took the card.

"I'm not promising anything," Elias said.

"Of course not." Marcus's smile widened. "Nobody ever does. That's rather the point."

He folded his umbrella and strolled toward the house, leaving Elias standing in the drizzle with a stranger's name in his hand and rain running down his neck like a verdict.

Above the walled garden, the last of the storm slid out to sea. In the glasshouse, forgotten, Beatrix's notebook lay open on the bench, and on its page, in handwriting she didn't remember producing, were six words she'd have denied under oath:

*Not for the cousins. Not at all.*
