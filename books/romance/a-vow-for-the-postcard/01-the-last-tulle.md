## 1. The Last Tulle

The orchids had arrived at dawn, four hundred of them, blushing white as a bride's first lie, and Paloma Ashgrove had personally turned each vase a quarter-inch so that the light through the ballroom's east windows would catch them exactly right. No one else would have noticed. No one else ever noticed. That was the point.

"Three minutes," said Odette, his assistant, materializing at his elbow the way she always did—silent as a stagehand, sharp as a pin. She held out his earpiece and, in her other hand, a espresso so small it was practically an apology. "String quartet's tuned. Caterer swears the sea bass is holding at temperature. The flower girl has been located; she was hiding under the gift table eating a macaron with the wrapper still on."

"Wrapper and all?"

"Foil and all. I've confiscated the evidence." Odette sipped her own coffee, watching him over the rim with those dark, unimpressed eyes that had seen him through six years and two hundred weddings and had never once let him get away with anything. "You've got your crisis face on."

"This is my face."

"This is your *wedding* face. Your crisis face is different. Your crisis face is when the ice sculpture melts or the groom's mother starts drinking before noon. Right now you look like a man waiting for something to go wrong so he'll have an excuse not to feel whatever it is he's not feeling."

Paloma took the espresso because it gave his hands something to do. "I'm feeling perfectly fine."

"Mm." Odette looked out over the ballroom of the Wrenfield Estate—the chandeliers dripping crystal like frozen rain, the aisle runner rolled out white as a fresh page, the string quartet sawing gently through their warm-up scales—and then back at him. "Two hundred and eleven."

"What?"

"Weddings. This makes two hundred and eleven. I keep count because somebody in this partnership should be capable of sentiment." She patted his arm once, briskly, the way one pats a horse before a race. "Go stand where they can't see you. You know how you get when they see you."

He did know. Brides cried harder when they caught sight of him, for reasons no one had ever adequately explained—something about his face, people said, something about the way he watched them walk down the aisle, as though he were memorizing it, as though he were grieving it. He wasn't grieving. He was *quality-controlling*. There was a difference, and he'd built an entire career on the difference, and if he repeated it often enough in his own head perhaps one day it would stop sounding like a defense entered into evidence.

* * *

The ceremony began at four o'clock precisely, because Paloma did not permit things to begin imprecisely, and the sunlight came through the windows the way he had promised the bride it would, gilding everything, forgiving everything. Two hundred guests rose as one. The string quartet lifted into the processional—a piece Paloma had chosen himself, something old and aching, all suspended chords, music that sounded like remembering.

And then the doors opened, and the bride appeared on her father's arm, and Paloma stood in the shadows by the east wall doing what he always did: watching everything, cataloguing everything, making sure the candle flames weren't guttering, that the hem of the train wasn't catching, that the officiant had his reading glasses—

That was when the father began to weep.

It started small. A tremor in his jaw. A blink held too long. And then, somewhere around the third row of chairs, it broke open entirely, this silver-haired man in his beautiful rented suit, tears sliding down his face without shame or attempt at concealment, his mouth pressed tight against whatever was rising in him, his hand trembling on his daughter's arm as he gave her away step by step down an aisle that was, Paloma knew to the centimeter, forty-two feet long.

*Forty-two feet,* Paloma thought. *Four minutes at processional speed. He gets four minutes left of being the man who walks her everywhere.*

Something moved in Paloma's chest. He identified it immediately, the way one identifies a familiar intruder, and escorted it firmly off the premises.

Because here was the thing about grief: it had excellent timing. It waited, patient as a wedding planner, for the exact moment when a man was standing alone in the shadows of someone else's perfect day, and then it arrived wearing the face of everyone who had ever left him. His mother, going out for cigarettes in 1998 and becoming a postcard instead of a person. His grandmother Elena, whose voice on the phone had gone quieter every year until it went silent altogether. And Daniel.

Daniel, who had stood in a kitchen very much like the Wrenfield kitchen, eight years ago now, with a ring box still in his pocket and a speech half-rehearsed, and had looked at Paloma across the counter where Paloma was color-coding seating charts for a stranger's wedding, and had said, quietly, in the tone of a man stepping off a ledge:

"I don't think you know how to be loved, Paloma. I really don't. And I can't spend my life auditioning for a part you won't cast."

And then—this was the detail that lived in Paloma like a splinter, this was the detail he returned to at three in the morning the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth—Daniel hadn't even argued. Hadn't slammed a door. He had simply taken his coat off the hook, and kissed Paloma on the forehead like a brother, and walked out into the rain, and never called again. Silence. Not even the courtesy of anger. Eight years of silence, long enough that Paloma sometimes wondered whether he'd invented the whole engagement, whether there was any proof it had happened at all except a ring in a drawer he couldn't bring himself to throw away and couldn't bear to wear.

The bride reached the altar. Her father folded her hands into the groom's hands and stepped back, and his shoulders shook once, twice, and then he sat down in the front pew and bowed his head, and Paloma looked away because looking at it felt like pressing on a bruise to check if it still hurt.

It still hurt.

*You're fine,* he told himself, in the crisp internal voice he used for vendors running late. *You have a timeline to protect. Candle check at minute twelve. The cake table needs re-draping before the reception. Focus.*

So he focused. He moved through the ceremony like water through cracks—invisible, essential, everywhere and nowhere—straightening a boutonniere here, cueing the quartet there, catching the flower girl's eye during the vows and pointing gravely at the macaron crumbs on her dress until she giggled and wiped them. This was his genius, if he was honest, and he was honest only in the privacy of his own skull: not romance, not love, but *logistics*. The architecture of other people's happiness. He could build it flawlessly, floor to rafters, and he had never once needed to live in it himself.

A cathedral of joy, and he was the maintenance man.

* * *

By cocktail hour the father had recovered enough to laugh, and by dinner he was dancing with his daughter to a song Paloma had vetted personally, and the whole golden machine of the evening was turning exactly as designed. Paloma stood near the bar with a glass of champagne he was using as a prop—he rarely drank on duty, control being the only intoxicant he trusted—and watched the father lift his daughter's hand and spin her, clumsy and laughing, while the guests applauded.

"He's going to come find you later," Odette said, appearing beside him with a clipboard she didn't need. She kept it purely for intimidation purposes. "Crying fathers always come find you. They want to shake your hand and tell you it was the best day of their lives, and then they want to cry again, and then you're trapped for twenty minutes."

"I'll be gracious."

"You're always gracious. It's exhausting." She studied him sideways. "You didn't eat the vendor meal."

"I wasn't hungry."

"You're never hungry. You know what I think? I think you ran out of hunger years ago and never filed the paperwork." She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to, then nodded toward the dance floor. "Two hundred and eleven weddings, boss. You've thrown more bouquets than anyone alive. When's it your turn?"

"I'm married to the job."

"You say that like it's a joke. It stopped being funny around wedding ninety." Odette clicked her pen shut. "For the record—and I'm saying this as your employee, so you can't fire me—I watched that father cry today, and I watched you watch him, and you had the exact same expression you get when you find a wilted peony in a delivery. Like something beautiful got damaged and you're the only one who noticed."

"The peony was replaced within four minutes."

"That's what I'm saying." She drained her champagne and set the glass down with a click. "Go home tonight. Actually home, not the office. Sleep in a bed like a person."

"I have the Delacroix proposal due Monday."

"It's Saturday."

"And yet Monday arrives regardless. Tragic system we live under." He touched her shoulder—one of the few gestures of affection he permitted himself on duty, brief and dry as a signature. "Thank you, Odette."

"Don't mention it. Seriously. Don't. I have a reputation." But she was smiling faintly as she drifted off toward the cake table, and Paloma stood alone at the bar with his prop champagne, watching the father and daughter finish their dance.

The band swung into something faster. Guests flooded the floor. And in the sudden shuffle and glitter, the father found Paloma across the room—found him the way crying fathers always did, some homing instinct for the man who had built their happiness—and raised his glass to him from across the ballroom, mouthing *thank you*, eyes still red-rimmed and shining.

Paloma raised his own glass in return and smiled the smile he kept for such moments, flawless and load-bearing, and inside the smile he thought: *four minutes at processional speed, forty-two feet, and then you hand them off and sit down and that's the whole story.*

His phone buzzed in his jacket pocket.

He almost ignored it. Duty nights, personal phones stayed silent—that was doctrine. But the buzz came again, insistent, and something in its rhythm snagged on him, and he stepped out through the terrace doors into the cool blue dark of the garden, away from the music, and answered without checking the caller ID.

"Paloma Ashgrove."

"Well." The voice on the other end was female, clipped, and achingly familiar—the particular blend of smugness and affection that only one person on earth had ever achieved. "Still answering calls like a funeral director, I see. Some things never change."

Paloma closed his eyes. The music swelled faintly behind the glass behind him, two hundred strangers dancing inside a happiness he had built with his own hands.

"Marisol," he said. "To what do I owe the pleasure?"

There was a pause on the line, and when she spoke again, her voice had changed—something underneath the smugness now, something careful. "It's about Grandmother Elena's will, Paloma. The lawyer sent letters. We need to talk."
