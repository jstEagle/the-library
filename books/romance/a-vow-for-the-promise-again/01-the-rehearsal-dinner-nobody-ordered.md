## 1. The Rehearsal Dinner Nobody Ordered

The candles were wrong, and Astrid Joubert was the only person in the Château de Rocheval who knew it.

They were, technically, perfect—hand-dipped beeswax in ivory and pale gold, arranged at precise intervals down the length of the banquet table, each flame steady in the still June air. Any of the forty guests filtering into the orangery would have called them beautiful. But they were supposed to be *blush*, not gold. The florist had substituted them at the last minute because the blush shipment had been delayed in Lyon, and she had made the swap without telling him, which was forgivable, and had then arranged them so that the gold ones clustered near the head table like a small, smug sun, which was not.

Astrid adjusted one candlestick two centimeters to the left, stepped back, and considered.

Better. Not right. But better.

"You're doing the thing," said Marguerite, his floor manager, appearing at his elbow with a clipboard she didn't need because everything on it lived in her head anyway. "The candle thing."

"There is no candle thing."

"You're doing it right now. Your jaw does this little—" She mimed a twitch. "It's your tell. You should get that seen to."

"My jaw is fine. The candles are gold."

"They're lovely."

"They're *supposed* to be blush." He said it quietly, because the mother of the bride was passing behind them in a cloud of Chanel and anxiety, and the last thing anyone needed was to overhear the wedding planner admitting that anything, anywhere, was less than intended. "Blush says *tenderness*. Gold says *we have money and we want you to know it*. This is a second marriage for both of them. They wanted tenderness."

Marguerite looked at the candles, then at him, with the expression of a woman who had worked for him for six years and had long ago stopped being impressed by any of this. "Astrid. It's a rehearsal dinner. In four hours these people will be drunk on a very good Sancerre and nobody will remember what color the candles were."

"That," said Astrid, straightening another candlestick, "is exactly why they pay me. So they never have to remember."

He meant it as a joke. Marguerite didn't laugh, which meant she'd heard the other thing underneath it, the thing he hadn't quite meant to say: that he built days so seamless, so frictionless, so *forgettable in their perfection*, that no one ever had to hold on to them at all. That was the product, really. Not weddings. Anesthesia.

The orangery at Rocheval was one of his favorite rooms in France—glass and old stone, wisteria pressing against the panes from outside, the light going honey-colored as evening came on. Tonight it would hold sixty people for dinner before tomorrow's ceremony under the cedar tree. The couple, Hélène and Bruno, were fifty-something, kind-faced, and so visibly delighted with each other that Astrid's staff kept making faces at each other behind their backs—not mocking faces. Wistful ones. Bruno had cried during the menu tasting. Over the *menu*. Specifically over the memory of the first meal Hélène had ever cooked him, which the chef had agreed to recreate, and which was, by all accounts, a slightly overcooked chicken.

Astrid had watched him cry and felt nothing he could name, which was its own kind of professional skill.

"Timeline," Marguerite said, tapping her clipboard. "Guests seated at seven-forty. Toasts at eight-fifteen. Hélène's brother is speaking and he thinks he's funny."

"How unfunny?"

"Wedding-crash-unfunny. Twenty minutes unfunny."

"Cut him to six. Tell him there's a surprise dessert course and if his toast runs long, the kitchen can't hold it. People will forgive a boring toast; they'll never forgive a ruined soufflé."

"You're evil."

"I'm *efficient*. There's a difference and the difference is the invoice."

She grinned and went off to lie beautifully to Hélène's brother, and Astrid stood alone in the middle of the room he had built, surrounded by candles that were the wrong color, and let himself do the thing he never did in front of anyone:

He checked his phone.

Not for work. Work was handled; work was always handled; work was the one country where he held citizenship and spoke the language fluently. He checked it the way you check a wound—with a certain grim curiosity about whether it had gotten worse overnight.

There it was. A message thread with his mother, last updated eleven hours ago, ending with her most recent text, which read, in full:

*Camille tells me you haven't answered about Belle-Île. I am not nagging. I am simply noting, as a historian notes facts, that the ferry bookings open Monday and that your grandmother has asked specifically whether you are bringing someone this year. Also I have made the fig tart you like. Also answer your mother.*

Astrid put the phone face-down on the table, next to a gold candle that was supposed to be blush.

* * *

Here is what Astrid Joubert was very good at: problems with solutions.

A late caterer? He had three backups within an hour's drive and a fourth who owed him a favor dating back to 2019. Rain on an outdoor ceremony? He could move two hundred guests under glass in forty minutes and make it feel like an upgrade. A groom with cold feet, a bride with a migraine, a best man who'd discovered God or gin an hour before the vows—Astrid had scripts for all of it, calm little sequences of words that de-escalated, redirected, resolved. His clients described him as *unflappable*, which appeared in enough testimonials that Marguerite had once suggested printing it on the letterhead.

What he was not good at was problems without solutions, and his family reunion was a problem without a solution, and it was now nineteen days away.

Belle-Île. Every summer since he was a child—the salt air, the white houses with their blue shutters, the whole sprawling loud impossible Joubert clan decamping to the island for the festival week like a benign invasion. And this year, this particular year, the invasion had teeth, because this year his cousin Margaux was announcing her engagement, and Margaux's engagement meant the whole family apparatus would swing around to the question it had been orbiting for three years with increasing, loving, unbearable pressure:

*Astrid, when is it your turn?*

And underneath that question, unspoken but audible to everyone, the older one, the one with rust on it:

*Astrid, after what happened, are you all right?*

He was fine. That was the official answer, the one he'd been issuing for three years with the same polished competence he brought to candle placement. He was fine, he was thriving, his company had gone from a struggling one-man operation to the most sought-after wedding planning studio on the Atlantic coast, and yes, he was seeing someone, it was new, it was good, he'd bring her to the island when it was serious enough.

That last part was the lie. It had started as a small lie—a deflection to his mother on the phone, eighteen months ago, when she'd caught him on a Sunday evening sounding too quiet. *I'm seeing someone,* he'd said, mostly to end the call, and had then spent the following week constructing the person: a woman, obviously, because his mother would worry otherwise; a professional, because his mother would meddle otherwise; someone outside the industry, because his mother would interrogate otherwise. He had given her a profession (something with plants—he wasn't sure why, but it had felt right), a temperament (warm, opinionated, allergic to nonsense), and a slow-burning timeline (*it's early days, Maman, I don't want to rush it*) that bought him reunion after reunion.

The lie had a maintenance cost. All lies did. It required him to remember details, to plant evidence, to occasionally let his sister Camille "accidentally" see a message from "her" on his phone. Camille, who knew him better than anyone alive, had looked at him for a long moment the last time and said nothing, which was worse than if she'd called him a liar outright.

And now the bill was coming due. Because *new* had a shelf life, and *serious* was the only currency his family traded in, and Grand-mère Solange—who was ninety-one and missed nothing—had asked Camille point-blank last Christmas whether Astrid's young woman existed or whether Astrid had invented her the way he used to invent excuses for missing church.

So: nineteen days. One weekend on an island with forty relatives and one fictional girlfriend who needed to become real, or needed to be confessed, or—

Or.

Astrid picked up his phone again and opened the message thread and typed: *Yes, I'm coming to Belle-Île. And yes. I'm bringing her.*

He stared at it for a long time. Then he deleted *her* and wrote *someone*, which somehow felt more honest and more damning at once, and sent it before he could think better of it, and immediately his phone buzzed with his mother's reply, which was a row of heart emojis followed by *FIG TART*, as though the pastry itself had been waiting years for this news.

"Right," Astrid said to the empty orangery, to the gold candles, to the version of himself that had just committed to a crime with a deadline. "Right."

* * *

The rehearsal dinner went flawlessly, because of course it did.

Hélène's brother told stories for six minutes and ten seconds and got his biggest laugh at the four-minute mark, and Astrid, standing at the back with his hands folded, gave the signal that brought the soufflés out at exactly the right moment, and the room turned toward dessert like a field of flowers turning toward sun. Bruno toasted Hélène and cried again, briefly, dignified, and Hélène pressed her thumb to the corner of his eye and said something too quiet for anyone else to hear, and Astrid watched them the way he watched all of them—from just outside the glass, taking notes.

*Hold the pause after the third line of the toast,* he noted mentally. *The wisteria outside the east windows photographs better at dusk—tell the photographer. Hélène touches Bruno's wrist when she laughs; seat them accordingly tomorrow.*

It was a good living. It was, he reminded himself, a genuinely good living, built from nothing, rebuilt from worse than nothing. Three years ago the name Astrid Joubert had been a punchline in certain circles—a cautionary tale passed between vendors at trade shows, the planner whose own wedding collapsed mid-ceremony while half the industry watched. People had taken bets. People had taken *photographs*. And instead of drowning, he had surfaced, and worked, and built, until the story became: *whatever happened to him, look what he made of himself.* Clients loved a redemption arc. Redemption arcs were excellent for business.

Nobody asked what the redemption had cost. That was the beauty of a well-run event: nobody ever asked what anything cost. They just admired the result.

At half past eleven, the last guests drifted out into the warm dark, and the staff began the quiet dismantling, and Astrid walked the orangery one final time with his checklist even though he didn't need the checklist, because walking the room was how he thought.

The candles had burned down to soft pools of wax. Gold, all of them. Hélène had remarked on them twice—*how golden everything feels, Astrid, like the beginning of something*—and had squeezed his arm, and he had smiled his client smile and said, *that's exactly the feeling we were after,* and the lie had slid out smooth as silk ribbon, and she had believed him, because everyone believed him, because believing him was the service he provided.

His phone buzzed. Camille.

He almost didn't answer. He answered.

"Don't say anything," Camille said, by way of greeting. Her voice had the particular brightness she deployed when she was about to be devastating. "First: the dinner tonight, Marguerite posted a photo, the room looked like heaven, bravo, etcetera. Second: Maman forwarded me your text. *Someone.* Astrid. *Someone.*"

"It's a pronoun, Camille."

"It's a hostage situation, is what it is. You've written yourself a role with no actress. The show opens in nineteen days. What's your plan, mon frère? Because knowing you, the plan is already insane, and I'd like to hear it now rather than discover it mid-scandal."

"I don't have a plan."

The silence on the line was eloquent. Camille had been present for every disaster of his life, from the broken arm at nine to the broken engagement at thirty-two, and she had learned that *I don't have a plan*, in Astrid's mouth, was either a lie or a cry for help, and she had gotten very good at telling which.

"That," she said finally, "is the scariest thing you've said to me since the wedding."

"Low blow."

"Honest blow. Listen—" and here her voice softened, which was dangerous, because Camille's softness had always been able to reach places no one else's could—"come to the island. Come alone, if you have to. Solange doesn't care. I don't care. Maman cares but Maman would also forgive you by the second fig tart. You don't have to bring a person. You could just—you could come and be my brother and eat too much cheese and let us love you without a plus-one attached."

For one unguarded second, Astrid wanted it. Wanted it the way you want to set down something heavy.

"I'm bringing someone," he said.

"Astrid—"

"I'm bringing someone, Camille. It's handled."

"*Handled.*" She exhaled slowly through the phone. "You know that's the word you use for things that are about to explode. You said 'it's handled' about the Delacroix tent collapse and I still have nightmares about the chandelier."

"The chandelier was fine."

"The chandelier was in the *pool*, Astrid."

He laughed despite himself, and heard her laughing too, reluctantly, and for a moment it was like being twenty-five again, conspiring with her across a kitchen table while their mother pretended not to listen.

"Nineteen days," Camille said. "Find a human being, Astrid. A real one. With opinions and rough edges and a pulse. Because if you show up on that island with some polished mannequin you've briefed within an inch of her life, Solange will know inside a minute, and then Maman will know, and then you'll wish the chandelier had fallen on you."

She hung up before he could reply, which was her way of winning.

Astrid stood in the darkened orangery among the burned-down candles and the folding of chairs, and admitted, in the privacy of his own skull, that Camille was right. The mannequin plan—the dossier plan, the briefing plan, whatever shape it eventually took—was insane. It was the act of a man who solved problems with logistics because logistics couldn't leave him at the altar.

Which raised the practical question, the one he'd been circling for weeks without letting himself land on it: where did one find a woman with opinions and rough edges and a pulse, willing to spend a weekend lying to forty strangers, on nineteen days' notice?

He was still turning the question over when Marguerite found him, keys jingling, ready to lock up.

"One more thing," she said. "The florist for tomorrow—the one who subbed the candles, Marchand, out of Vannes? She's asking whether you want the arch arrangements moved for the ceremony or rebuilt. She has, quote, 'opinions.'"

"Of course she has opinions. Everyone has opinions. What are they?"

"She wouldn't tell me. She said—" Marguerite consulted her phone, squinting. "She said, and I am quoting directly: 'Tell Joubert the arch is wrong and I'll explain it to his face in the morning.' End quote."

Astrid felt something flicker under his ribs that was not quite annoyance and not quite interest, and filed it away the way he filed everything: neatly, temporarily, dangerously.

"Tell her," he said, "seven o'clock. And tell her to bring her opinions. I'll bring mine."

He did not yet know that he had just booked the most consequential meeting of his life. He only knew that somewhere out there was a florist who thought his arch was wrong, and that tomorrow morning, for one hour, he would get to argue with someone about something that actually mattered.

It was, he reflected, locking up the château behind him under a sky full of indifferent stars, the best he'd felt in nineteen days.
