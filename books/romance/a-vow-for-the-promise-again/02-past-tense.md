## 2. Past Tense

The memory box lived on the top shelf of Astrid's office closet, behind a box of obsolete chargers and a stack of branded tote bags he kept meaning to donate. It was not, he would have insisted if anyone had ever asked—and no one ever asked—a shrine. It was inventory. Archived materials from a defunct project. Every event planner worth his fee knew that you didn't throw things away; you catalogued them, sealed them, and put them somewhere they couldn't hurt the client.

The client, in this case, had been himself.

He thought about the box the way he thought about weather in a coastal town: acknowledged, monitored, never engaged with directly. Some mornings it was simply there in the back of his mind, gray and distant. Other mornings—like this one, three years almost to the day—it pressed against the windows of him like a storm front.

Astrid Joubert did not open storm fronts. He drew the curtains and made coffee.

* * *

Three years earlier, the morning of his own wedding, Astrid had woken before dawn in a rented villa above the harbor at Sauzon, lain perfectly still for four minutes, and then gotten up to check on the flowers.

This was, Camille later told him, the most Astrid thing he had ever done. The man wakes up on the most important day of his life and audits the peonies.

But the flowers had been his one non-negotiable. He'd planned two hundred weddings by then—he was thirty-one, already known in certain circles as the man who could make rain feel intentional—and he had watched, over and over, what happened when couples surrendered every detail to other hands. They arrived at their own ceremony feeling like guests at someone else's party. Astrid had refused. He would plan everything else; the flowers would be Théo's gift to him.

Théo had chosen peonies. Coral Charm peonies, the ones that opened blush-pink and deepened as they aged, so that by the end of the night the arrangements would be a different color than they'd been at the start. *Like us,* Théo had said, laughing into Astrid's neck. *By midnight we won't even recognize ourselves.*

Astrid had believed, with his whole meticulous heart, that this was romantic.

At six-forty that morning he stood in the villa kitchen among buckets of them, still tightly furled, and adjusted the water level in the third vase because the stems were sitting too shallow, and felt the specific happiness of a man whose life has been built to code. Everything inspected. Everything signed off. Nothing left to chance except the vows themselves, which were folded in his breast pocket, written and rewritten eleven times, the final draft only forty seconds long because he'd learned that brevity read as confidence.

Théo was staying in town with his sister the night before, per tradition. He'd texted at eleven: *Last night as a free man. Come collect me tomorrow.* A joke. Astrid had smiled at it for a full minute.

At seven-fifteen, his phone rang. Not Théo's number. Théo's sister's.

He would spend years afterward reconstructing that phone call, turning it over the way he turned over vendor invoices, looking for the line item he'd missed. What she said, exactly. How long she spoke before he understood. Whether her voice had sounded rehearsed. He could summon fragments—the word *gone*, said twice; the phrase *he left his phone*; something about a ferry—but the whole of it refused to assemble. Memory, he'd learned, was the one event he could not stage-manage. It cut footage without permission. It lost the master file.

What he remembered cleanly, with terrible clarity, was the next hour: standing in the kitchen among the peonies, calling Théo's number and hearing it ring out in an empty room somewhere, once, twice, seven times. Watching Camille arrive with her hair half-done and take one look at his face and sit down hard on a kitchen chair. Making the decision—not feeling it, *making* it, the way he made all decisions—to walk out to the garden where eighty guests were beginning to gather under a sky doing its best impression of mercy, and tell them.

Because that was the alternative, wasn't it? Let them sit through a ceremony with an empty altar, let them wait, let them whisper. Astrid Joubert did not leave clients waiting. Even now. Especially now.

He walked down the aisle he had designed himself—white chairs, no runner, the sea doing its usual show-off routine behind the arbor—and he stood where the officiant should have stood, and he told eighty people that the wedding was off.

He did not cry. He noted, distantly, that he should probably cry, that everyone was watching for it, that tears would have been the correct emotional deliverable. But his body had gone into what he would later privately call *event mode*: calm, competent, catastrophically organized. Within twenty minutes he had arranged transportation back to the hotel. Within forty, he had personally spoken to the caterer about converting the reception dinner into a farewell meal, because throwing away food offended him more, in that moment, than anything happening inside his chest.

The accusations started that evening.

They came gently at first, wrapped in sympathy the way pills come wrapped in sugar. *You must have known something was wrong. A man doesn't just run. What happened between you two?* And then less gently, from Théo's family, who arrived at the ruined reception like creditors at a bankruptcy: whispers that Théo had been unhappy for months, that Astrid had controlled everything, *everything*, that some men mistake planning for love. That Théo's own mother had warned him. That the wedding had been more Astrid's than theirs anyway—look at it, look how perfect, whose day was it supposed to be?

And Astrid—who knew, in the private locked room of himself, that Théo had seemed happy, that Théo had chosen the peonies, that Théo had texted *come collect me*—said nothing.

That was the part he could never explain, even to himself, though he'd spent three years conducting internal depositions about it. Why hadn't he defended himself? He'd had evidence. Text messages. A hundred witnesses to Théo's laughter. He could have fought. Instead he stood in the wreckage of his own reception holding a glass of champagne he had no memory of picking up, and let the story settle around him like dust after demolition, and said, finally, to Théo's mother, who was watching him with an expression he couldn't read:

"I'm sorry your son is hurting."

Which, he understood much later, was the sentence that convicted him. An innocent man would have raged. An innocent man would have said *your son left me at the altar, ask yourself why.* Astrid had apologized—to *her*—because apologizing was what he did, professionally and pathologically, whenever anything went wrong within a hundred meters of him. The apology was reflex. Everyone heard guilt.

The peonies, when he finally noticed them, had opened fully. Deep coral, nearly red. By midnight they wouldn't even recognize themselves.

He'd paid the vendors himself, every last invoice, including the ones technically covered by contracts and deposits. It took him fourteen months. People called it class. Camille called it penance for a crime he hadn't committed, and said so, loudly, until he asked her to stop.

* * *

Present-day Astrid came back to himself standing in front of the open closet, holding the memory box, with no memory of taking it down.

This was the thing about grief managed like inventory: occasionally the inventory audited *you*. He looked at the box—a plain archival container, acid-free, the kind he used for clients' keepsakes—and felt the familiar professional disgust at his own sentimentality. Labeling it had been too much. Not labeling it had been worse. In the end he'd labeled it with a date range, like a fiscal year. *2019–2021.* As if his engagement had been a product line, discontinued.

He should put it back. He always put it back.

Instead—he told himself it was because the shelf needed reorganizing, which was even true—he carried it to his desk and set it down in the afternoon light.

Inside: the vow draft, folded eleven times' worth of soft. A swatch of the linen napkins. His boutonnière, dried to a brittle brown comma. A place card with his name in his own handwriting, because he'd done those too, of course he had. And at the bottom, wrapped in tissue paper he'd never unwrapped since the day he'd wrapped it, the small velvet box that had cost him four months of savings and contained a ring Théo had helped choose and then never worn.

Astrid touched none of it. He looked, the way a project manager looks at a site visit. Assessed condition. Confirmed contents against manifest.

There was a letter in the box too—one item not on any manifest, because he'd added it eight months after the wedding, when Théo's family's lawyer had sent the settlement agreement. Nondisclosure, mutual silence, a modest payment from the Delacroix side toward Astrid's unrecovered costs, framed as generosity. *In the interest of both families moving forward.* He'd signed it in his kitchen at two in the morning, alone, and then printed a copy for himself and put it here, in the dark, with the peony petals that had shed onto everything like confetti at a funeral.

He had signed it because the business was eighteen months old and bleeding, because two corporate clients had quietly withdrawn after the scandal, because his mother had cried on the phone and asked him to make it stop. He had signed it because silence, he'd told himself, was just another form of professionalism. Discretion. Client confidentiality, applied to his own life.

He had signed it, and the story had hardened into official record: the controlling planner, the groom who fled, the apology that sounded like confession. Astrid had rebuilt on top of the rubble. He was very good at building on rubble. It was, if he was honest, his core competency.

The intercom buzzed. He flinched hard enough to knock the vow draft off the desk.

"Mr. Joubert?" Nadia, his studio manager, voice tinny through the speaker. "Sorry to interrupt. There's a delivery issue at the Marchand account again—they're saying the invoice from the Vasseur wedding is past due, but I have the confirmation right here, signed and dated, and honestly the florist on the phone sounds like she's about to come down here personally, and I thought maybe—"

"Give me five minutes," Astrid said, "and then send her up," and he heard Nadia's pause, the tiny hesitation of an employee deciding whether to warn him, before she said, "She's already in the lobby."

He looked down at the open box. At the letter with its neat legalese, its mutual silences, its careful burial of everything true. At the dried peony petal resting on the signature line like a wax seal.

Later, he told himself. Later he would reshelve it, seal it, return it to the dark where archived things belonged. He closed the lid, slid it back behind the obsolete chargers, and straightened his collar in the reflection of the dark window—a man smoothing the surface of himself before company arrived.

He was halfway to the door when it occurred to him, with a small cold drop of dread, that the florist on the phone—the one with opinions, the one coming up in the elevator right now—had worked the Vasseur wedding.

And that the Vasseur wedding had been held at the same venue, the same month, three years ago, as another wedding. One that had ended badly. One where a young florist with sharp eyes and sharper hands had watched him stand in front of eighty guests and apologize to the wrong people.

The elevator chimed.

Astrid put on his smile—the good one, the load-bearing one—and went to meet her.
