## 4. A Florist With Opinions

The invoice had been unpaid for forty-one days, and Elise Marchand had a system for forty-one days.

Days one through ten: polite email, cheerful subject line, exclamation point deployed like a white flag. Days eleven through twenty: phone calls to an office that always seemed to be "just stepping into a meeting." Days twenty-one through thirty: the emails lost their exclamation points and gained cc's. Day thirty-one onward: she began composing, in her head, during the quiet hours when she was conditioning stems in the back of Marchand & Fille, increasingly vivid scenarios of what she would say when she finally stood in front of whoever was responsible.

By day forty-one, the scenario had a running time of roughly four minutes and ended with her walking out with a check, an apology, and — in the most satisfying versions — a small audience of witnesses who applauded.

What she had not planned for was the studio itself.

She'd expected something corporate. Glass, steel, a receptionist with a headset who would ask if she had an appointment. Instead, Astrid Joubert's event design studio occupied the ground floor of a narrow Marais building that had once been a bookbinder's, and it looked like what would happen if a wedding and a library had a very tasteful child. Long wooden tables stacked with fabric swatches. Candles in jars, unlit but ready. A wall of seating charts pinned like butterfly specimens. And everywhere, everywhere, evidence of competence — the kind of meticulous, expensive-looking competence that made her invoice feel like a pebble thrown at a cathedral.

"Madame Marchand."

He came out from behind a partition carrying two espresso cups on a tray, as though he'd been expecting her, which was impossible, or as though he greeted angry vendors with coffee every day, which she supposed he might.

"Monsieur Joubert." She did not take the coffee. "Do you know how long it's been?"

"Forty-one days," he said, setting the tray down. "I'm sorry. It's inexcusable. The Delacroix account has been — complicated. Sit, please. Or don't, if you'd rather stand and loom. Some people loom better standing."

"I am not looming."

"You're holding your bag like a shield and you haven't blinked since I walked in. Looming is a spectrum."

Elise blinked, furious at herself for blinking. "Your office manager told me last Tuesday that payment had been processed."

"It hadn't. I found out Wednesday. I've been trying to fix it without bothering you, because I assumed you'd rather have money than updates."

"I would rather have both," she snapped, "which is what a normal person wants. Money *and* being treated like a human whose rent exists."

Something flickered across his face — not guilt exactly, more like recognition, the look of a man hearing a bell he'd heard before. "You're right," he said simply. "That was badly handled. The full amount plus late fees will be transferred today. I'll do it while you watch, if you want. There's a laptop right there."

The wind went out of her sails so abruptly she nearly staggered. She had prepared for denial, deflection, bureaucracy. She had not prepared for a man who folded immediately and offered receipts.

"That's—" She adjusted her grip on the bag. "Fine. Yes. Do it now."

He did it now. He actually sat down at the long table, opened a slim silver laptop, typed with the brisk economy of someone who paid invoices the way other people breathed, and turned the screen toward her. *Transfer initiated.* Forty-one days of righteous fury, resolved in ninety seconds.

"There," he said. "And the flowers were extraordinary, by the way. The Delacroix ceremony looked like the inside of a good mood. My client's mother cried before the vows even started, which is the highest compliment our industry offers."

"The arch collapsed," Elise said. "Halfway through the processional. Your rigging crew built it on a slope."

"And your floristry held it together long enough for everyone to pretend it hadn't." He closed the laptop. "I know. I watched the video eleven times. If your mechanics hadn't been over-engineered — and they were, thank God, I could tell from the photos that you brace everything like you expect a hurricane — the whole entrance would have gone down in front of two hundred guests instead of just embarrassing my crew chief."

She stared at him. This was not how these conversations went. These conversations were supposed to end with her winning, loudly, and going home to tell Colette at the flower market about it over cheap wine.

"You're being reasonable," she accused.

"I'm being guilty. It looks similar from the outside."

* * *

The trouble was that she couldn't leave. Not yet. Because now that the invoice was settled — now that the actual business between them was finished — there was room for the other thing, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since she'd seen his name on the studio door downstairs and felt her stomach drop through three floors of Parisian limestone.

Astrid Joubert. She knew that name from somewhere other than vendor lists. She knew it the way everyone in this industry knew it, the way you know the name of a famous shipwreck.

Three years ago, she'd been a second-year freelancer doing table arrangements for a wedding at a château outside Fontainebleau. A big one. The kind of wedding where the florist budget alone could have bought a car. She remembered it in fragments: the heat, the roses she'd conditioned overnight, the groomsmen drinking champagne at ten in the morning. And she remembered the moment it all stopped — the murmur moving through the crowd like weather, the bride's chair empty, the officiant standing at the altar with the particular frozen smile of a man realizing his afternoon has just become a story people will tell for years.

The groom had stood there. That was the part she'd never forgotten, the part that had lodged under her skin like a thorn. The groom had stood at that altar for eleven minutes — she'd counted, because counting was what you did when you didn't know what else to do with your hands full of peonies — while two hundred people watched him understand, in real time, publicly, that his life was coming apart.

His name had been Astrid Joubert. And afterward, when the story made its rounds — and it had made its rounds, it had made its rounds for months, it was still making its rounds — the story had a shape, and the shape was not kind to him. Money problems, people said. Debts hidden. A coldness in him the bride had finally seen clearly, just in time. He never denied any of it. That was the detail that had always bothered Elise most, the splinter inside the splinter: he never denied *any of it*. Not once, not a word, not a statement, not a lawsuit. He let the whole ugly version stand and walked away and rebuilt himself into the man whose name was now on tasteful lettering above a tasteful studio, the wedding planner other planners cited respectfully, the survivor.

And here he was, offering her espresso and apologizing about late fees like a decent person, and she wanted — unreasonably, unprofessionally — to shake him.

"You were at the Fontainebleau wedding," she said.

It came out blunter than she'd intended. His hands, which had been tidying the espresso cups with small precise movements, went still.

"Which one?" he asked. Lightly. Too lightly. "I've done hundreds."

"The one where the bride left." She set her bag down on the table — not a shield anymore; a declaration. "June, three years ago. Château de Vaux-Rousselin. I did the table flowers. White garden roses, blue delphinium, because the mother of the bride insisted on blue even though it fought with everything. You were wearing a gray suit and you stood at the altar for eleven minutes."

The studio was very quiet. Somewhere behind the partition, a clock ticked with the smugness of clocks.

"You have a good memory," he said.

"For flowers, yes. For cruelty dressed up as romance, also yes." She crossed her arms. "Everyone in this business knows that story, Monsieur Joubert. It gets retold at every industry dinner like a ghost story. 'The Joubert Wedding.' And in every version, you're the villain — the cold, secretive fiancé with the hidden debts. And in every version, nobody mentions that you never said a single word in your own defense. Not one. I've waited three years for somebody to explain that to me, and now I'm standing in your studio, so I'm asking. Why?"

He looked at her then — really looked, the professional warmth draining out of his face until what remained was younger and wearier and much harder to look at. For a moment she thought he might answer. She could see the answer assembling somewhere behind his eyes, feel the room tilt toward it.

Then he smiled, and it was like watching a shopkeeper pull down the shutters.

"That's a long story," he said, "and it's not on the invoice."

"No," she agreed. "But I carried your arch pieces down two flights of château stairs in the rain after everyone left, while you sat in a car in the courtyard talking to lawyers. So consider us even for a story."

Something moved through his expression too fast to name. "You stayed after? To strike the install?"

"Somebody had to. The family was in pieces. The coordinator had fainted — genuinely fainted, into the dessert table, which was the only dramatic thing that happened on schedule that day." She heard her own voice sharpening and couldn't stop it. "I stayed because that's the job. You show up for the beautiful part and you stay for the wreckage. It's the same wedding. You'd know that, if you ever talked about yours."

For a long moment he said nothing. He picked up the espresso he'd poured for her, still untouched, and drank it himself, and she understood it was a way of putting something in his mouth that wasn't words.

"Why does it matter to you?" he asked quietly. "You got paid. Eventually. The roses were lovely. The story isn't yours."

"Because I hate bad craftsmanship," Elise said. "And that story is badly made. Villains don't stand at altars for eleven minutes. Villains check their phones. I was thirty meters away, Monsieur Joubert. I watched your face. Whatever happened at that wedding, whatever the version is that everyone tells — it doesn't match what I saw. And it clearly doesn't match what you know. You flinched just now when I said *eleven minutes*, like the number was a bruise."

"You count too precisely for your own good."

"I'm a florist. Precision is the entire personality." She uncrossed her arms and planted both hands on his beautiful long table, among his beautiful fabric swatches, claiming territory. "Here's what's going to happen. You're going to pay me — done, fine, thank you. And someday, not today, maybe not this year, you're going to tell me the real version. Because I can be patient. I condition ranunculus for three days before I use them. Patience is also the entire personality."

"That's two things that are the entire personality."

"I contain multitudes. They're all opinions about flowers."

And there — briefly, involuntarily — he laughed. It transformed him. For half a second the polished event designer vanished and what was left was a tired, funny, cornered man who laughed like he'd gotten out of practice and was surprised the machinery still worked. Then it was gone, shuttered again, but she'd seen it. She filed it away next to the eleven minutes.

"You should go," he said, but gently. "Before I start telling you true things. I'm told it's contagious."

"I'll come back for my cure," Elise said, retrieving her bag. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and looked back at him standing alone among his perfect swatches and his unlit candles, a man who had arranged hundreds of the happiest days of other people's lives and appeared to have no furniture in the rooms of his own.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the delphinium was the wrong call. Blue against those roses. The mother was wrong and everyone was too scared to tell her. Somebody should have defended the palette."

His head came up sharply, and for a heartbeat she saw it again — the altar, the eleven minutes, the man who never defended anything, including himself.

"Noted," he said.

She let herself out into the street, where the afternoon smelled of rain and roasted chestnuts, and only when she was half a block away did she realize she was smiling, and only a block after that did she realize why it bothered her: because she had come for forty-one days of owed money, and she was leaving having promised herself a truth he owed her too — and worse, she already knew she'd wait for it. Patiently. Like a stem in water, waiting to open.

Behind her, in the studio window, Astrid Joubert watched her go, and reached — she caught it in the reflection — for a drawer she kept locked.
