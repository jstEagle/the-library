## 5. Terms of Engagement

The invoice sat between them on the drafting table like a grenade with the pin already pulled.

Astrid had faced down screaming mothers-of-the-bride, a groom who fainted twice during vows, and one memorable disaster involving a doves-and-confetti cue that fired four minutes early. He had survived all of it by doing what he did best: staying calm, staying pleasant, and staying three moves ahead.

Elise Marchand, standing in his studio with rain in her hair and righteous fury in her posture, was not a move he'd planned for.

"Fourteen hundred euros," she said again, tapping the paper. "For two hundred stems of garden roses that I drove to the port myself at five in the morning because your courier quit mid-wedding-season. The Delacroix wedding would have had *empty vases*, Astrid. Empty. Vases."

"And I told you the client disputed the floral line item," he said, keeping his voice at the register he used for difficult vendors — warm, reasonable, faintly apologetic, entirely immovable. "I'm not refusing to pay you. I'm asking for patience while I—"

"Patience." She said the word like she was tasting something spoiled. "You know what I have patience for? Peonies. They take years to establish and then they sulk if you look at them wrong, and even they don't make me wait four months for money I earned."

He should have simply written the check. That was the sensible move, the cheap move — fourteen hundred euros to make this woman leave his studio and never think about her again. Instead, some traitorous part of his brain, the part that had spent the last week staring at his mother's voicemail like it was a summons, looked at Elise Marchand — sharp-tongued, unimpressed, immune to his professional charm — and thought:

*She's perfect.*

"Would you like coffee?" he heard himself say.

"I would like my money."

"Coffee *and* your money." He was already moving toward the machine in the corner, because his hands needed something to do that wasn't visibly scheming. "I'll transfer the full amount today. Right now, if you'd like to watch me do it."

That stopped her. He felt the pause behind him like a change in air pressure.

"You're being suspiciously agreeable," she said.

"I'm always agreeable. It's a professional hazard."

"No. Agreeable is smiling at me and offering a ten percent discount on my next event. This is *guilty*. You've done something wrong and you want me docile." A beat. "What did you break?"

He laughed before he could stop himself — a real laugh, short and startled, the kind he hadn't produced in front of a stranger in years. He turned around with two cups of coffee and found her watching him with narrowed eyes, arms crossed, one hip against his worktable as though she owned it. There was a smear of green sap on her wrist and a thorn scratch along her forearm, and she stood in his immaculate studio like a weather system that hadn't gotten the memo about interior design.

"Nothing's broken," he said. "Yet. Sit down. I have a proposition, and I suspect you're going to hate it, and I'd rather you hated it sitting down."

* * *

She didn't sit. She perched on the edge of the chair like a bird deciding whether the branch was trustworthy, took the coffee without thanks, and said, "Proposition."

Astrid set down his own cup, aligned it precisely with the table edge — an old habit, straightening the world when he couldn't straighten anything else — and laid out the situation the way he'd rehearsed it in the shower that morning, minus the parts that made him sound pathetic.

"My family reunion is in three weeks. On Belle-Île. It coincides with the summer festival, which means the entire Joubert clan will be in one place, plus half the town, plus — as of last Sunday — my cousin Margaux's engagement announcement, which means the weekend has officially become a competition."

"A competition of what?"

"Partners," he said. "Careers. Children, eventually. Who brought whom. My mother has been asking about my 'someone' for two years, and I have been answering with increasing creativity, and last week she informed me that if I arrive alone she will spend the entire festival introducing me to the daughters of her bridge club, and Camille — my sister — has threatened to stage an intervention involving a slideshow."

Elise's mouth twitched. It was the first non-hostile expression he'd seen on her, and he was annoyed at how much it pleased him.

"So hire an escort," she said. "There are agencies."

"There are agencies full of strangers who can't tell a second cousin from a first and who flinch when my grandmother grabs their hand. I need someone who can hold a conversation with my aunt Odile about hydrangeas for forty-five minutes and survive my uncle's opinions about oysters." He leaned forward. "I need someone who argues with me in public and looks like they mean it. Nobody believes a partner who agrees with everything. Everyone believes a partner who thinks I'm occasionally an idiot."

"Everyone would be right."

"*Thank you.* That. Exactly that."

She stared at him over the rim of her cup. "You're serious."

"I'm completely serious. One weekend. Belle-Île, Friday to Monday. You pose as my partner — we've been together eight months, we met through work, keep it simple. Room and board covered, obviously separate rooms, and a fee of—" he named a figure that made even him wince slightly, "—for three days of eating good food and letting my family adore you."

Silence. Outside the studio window, a delivery van rattled past, and somewhere below, the street market was packing up its afternoon.

"That number is insane," Elise said finally.

"It's a fair rate for discretion and—"

"That number is insane because you clearly haven't priced what you're actually asking for." She set down the cup. "You're not asking for a date. You're asking me to lie to your entire family, in their own homes, for three days, while they watch me for cracks. Do you know what happens when a lie meets a grandmother? The grandmother wins. Every time. Grandmothers are the KGB of family life."

"My grandmother is ninety-one and mostly interested in whether people eat enough."

"Then she's the dangerous kind." Elise stood and walked to the window, arms folded, looking out at the street with the expression of a woman running calculations she didn't like. "And there's the other problem. Which is you."

"Me?"

"You." She turned. "I know who you are, Astrid. Not the wedding-planner-with-the-glossy-portfolio version. I was *there*, three years ago. Saint-Malo. The wedding that ended at the altar."

The room went very quiet. Astrid kept his face still, which after three years of practice cost him almost nothing, which was perhaps the saddest thing about it.

"Half the industry talked about nothing else for a month," Elise went on, softer now, but no less direct. "The groom left standing in front of two hundred guests. And then the story changed shape — by the end of the summer, somehow everyone was saying things about *you*. That you were cold. Controlling. That the bride's family wouldn't speak to you. And you never said a word. Not once. Not to defend yourself, not to correct anyone. You just... let it settle over you like weather." Her eyes were steady on his. "So here's my problem. I don't buy the public version. I've never bought it. And now you want me to walk into your family's house wearing a smile and a fictional relationship, and pretend I don't have questions."

"Then don't come," he said quietly. "I'll pay the invoice and we'll both—"

"That's not what I said." She came back from the window and sat down properly this time, facing him, elbows on her knees. "I said I have questions. I didn't say I wasn't listening. Go on. Finish the offer."

* * *

They negotiated for two hours.

It was, Astrid reflected later, the most honest conversation he'd had in years, which was ironic given that every word of it was about constructing a lie. Elise bargained like she arranged flowers — fast, instinctive, ruthless about anything that didn't serve the whole.

Separate rooms: agreed, non-negotiable, she said it before he could. Full payment up front, half refundable if she walked early — she insisted on the refund clause, "so you remember I can leave." No physical contact beyond what the audience required, and the audience got to define very little. She would learn the family tree, but she refused to memorize a script. "Scripts crack," she said. "Give me true things instead. Your sister's name, your uncle's terrible opinions, what your mother actually worries about. I can improvise around truth. I can't improvise around fiction — nobody can, that's why weddings need planners."

He wrote it all down in his neat planner's hand, feeling the strange vertigo of building a contract for something that had no business being contractual.

"One more term," Elise said, when the page was nearly full.

He looked up. She had gone still in a new way — not combative anymore, but careful, like someone stepping onto ice and testing it.

"One question," she said. "Sometime before the weekend ends, I ask you something, and you answer it truthfully. Fully. No planner-smooth deflection, no changing the subject, no charming half-answer. One true answer, whenever I choose to collect it."

Astrid's pen stopped moving.

He knew, with the certainty of a man who had spent three years avoiding exactly this, what question she was holding. It was right there in her face, patient and unblinking. *Why did you never defend yourself?*

He could refuse. He could counter-offer — one question, but he approved it in advance, vetted it, sanded off its edges until it was safe. That was what the old Astrid would have done, the Astrid from before, and God knew it was what the current one wanted to do. His whole career was built on controlling what people saw and when. One weekend of pretending was manageable. One unguarded answer was not.

But she was watching him, and beneath the sharpness there was something else — not pity, thank God, he couldn't have borne pity — but a kind of attention. She was the first person in three years who had looked at the scandal-shaped hole in his life and said *I don't believe the story* instead of politely looking away from it.

"All right," he said. "One question. Answered truthfully."

Her eyebrows rose slightly, as if she'd expected more of a fight. "Just like that?"

"Just like that. But I get a term too." He turned the notebook toward her and wrote as he spoke. "No research. You don't go digging into the Saint-Malo wedding, you don't interview old vendors, you don't read whatever the internet still says. If you have a question, you ask *me*. Deal?"

Something flickered across her face — quick, unreadable, gone before he could name it. Later, much later, he would understand that flicker for what it was. In the moment, he read it only as surprise.

"Deal," she said. "No digging. I ask you directly, or I don't ask at all."

They shook on it. Her hand was small and calloused from stem cutters, and her grip was brief and businesslike, and Astrid noted with detached professional alarm that he noticed the callouses at all.

"I'll send the contract tonight," he said. "Plain language. I promise it won't be forty pages."

"See that it isn't. I read fine print for sport." She gathered her bag, drained the last of the coffee, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. For a moment she seemed to be reconsidering something — the whole day, possibly the whole arrangement.

"For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "your family sounds like they'll see through this inside of an hour. Mothers always do. Sisters worse."

"Then I suppose we'd better be convincing."

"Mm." She glanced back at him, and there it was again — that twitch at the corner of her mouth, the ghost of a smile she hadn't authorized. "Eight months together, we met through work. Fine. But get the details right, Astrid. When did you decide I was the one? What did you tell your sister first? What do I call you when you're being impossible?"

"...You just called me Astrid."

"Yes, and notice how I made it sound like an insult. Practice." She pushed open the door, and the sounds of the late-afternoon street spilled in — gulls, a bicycle bell, someone arguing cheerfully about cheese. "Contract tonight. And Astrid?"

He looked up.

"The invoice better be paid before I finish my coffee tomorrow morning. Some promises aren't negotiable either."

The door closed behind her. Astrid sat alone in his immaculate studio, surrounded by color swatches and seating charts and other people's happily-ever-afters, and realized he was smiling at nothing, like an idiot, which — according to the woman who had just agreed to pretend to love him — everyone would find entirely believable.

He opened his laptop to draft the contract, and it was only when his fingers hit the keys that the weight of it landed: in three weeks he would stand in front of his entire family with a stranger on his arm and a debt of one truthful question hanging over his head, and he had just shaken hands with the only person in the world who seemed determined to collect it.

He typed the first clause anyway. *This agreement shall commence upon arrival at the port of Le Palais...*

Outside, the gulls wheeled over the rooftops, and somewhere across the city, Elise Marchand was walking home through the evening with fourteen hundred euros on the way to her account and a question banked like a lit match in her pocket.
