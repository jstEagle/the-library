## 6. Packing the Performance

The dossier was eleven pages long before Astrid admitted to himself that he had a problem.

Not a problem with the dossier itself — the dossier was excellent. He'd built it the way he built everything: at his drafting table by the studio's north window, with good coffee and better lighting, in the two hours before Elise Marchand was due to arrive for their first "rehearsal." He'd printed it on heavy stock. He'd used tabs.

That was the problem. The tabs.

He stared down at the little colored flags — *Family Tree* (blue), *Key Relationships* (green), *How We Met* (yellow), *Physical Affection Protocol* (pink, and God help him) — and felt something in his chest fold inward like a badly packed tablecloth.

Three years ago, he told himself, you couldn't lie your way out of a paper bag. Now look at you. You've color-coded it.

He picked up his coffee, found it cold, drank it anyway.

* * *

The family tree took up the first page because the Joubert family took up most of any page they were given. He'd drawn it out properly, boxes and connecting lines, the way he'd once drawn seating charts for four hundred guests. His parents at the top: Henri, retired harbormaster of Sauzon, a man who communicated primarily through weather observations and disapproving silences; and Colette, who communicated through everything else, loudly, lovingly, and usually all at once.

Then the siblings. Camille first, always Camille first — older by eighteen months, sharper by a decade, the only person on earth who could read Astrid's professional smile and know exactly which tooth was clenched. She taught literature in Vannes, was married to Yann (kind, bearded, constitutionally incapable of gossip, which in that family made him either a saint or a spy), and had two children: Léo, nine, an interrogator in training; and Manon, six, who had once asked Astrid why his eyes looked sad when his mouth was happy, and had never been forgiven for it.

Then Aurélien, younger, easygoing, running the family boatyard and married to Inès, who ran Aurélien, efficiently and with great affection.

And then Margaux. Cousin Margaux, twenty-nine, radiant, recently engaged to Baptiste Rousseau — a man whose surname Astrid had written down and then underlined twice, because Baptiste Rousseau was the kind of person who introduced himself with both names even to people he'd met four times. Margaux was not cruel. That was the thing people got wrong about her. She was warm, generous, genuinely delighted by other people's happiness — she simply also kept meticulous score, the way some people kept diaries, and her engagement announcement at the reunion would be the opening move of a game everyone else had forgotten they were playing.

*Astrid will be expected to match her announcement,* his mother had said on the phone, in the tone of a woman stating the tide schedule. *Or at least arrive with someone who makes it clear you're not hiding.*

*I'm not hiding,* he'd said.

*Astrid.* Just his name. His mother could fit an entire cross-examination into six letters.

So: the tree. He annotated it now in his small, precise handwriting, the same hand he used for client contracts. *Grand-mère Solange — matriarch, widow of Édouard, lives year-round in Le Palais. Sharp. Do not perform for her; she can smell varnish.* He paused over that line, wondering if it was advice for Elise or a warning he'd failed to take himself.

*Camille — protective of me. Will test you. If she offers you wine, accept; if she offers you silence, take that too.*

*Margaux — engaged to Baptiste. Kind but competitive about happiness. Compliment the ring early and often.*

*Baptiste — finance. Will ask what you do. Have a number ready.*

Elise did have a number ready, actually. Her flower shop on the rue des Orfèvres had survived three years of a bad economy, one flood, and a landlord she described — in the single meeting they'd had since she'd stormed into his studio over that unpaid invoice — as "a man who charges rent in the currency of his own personality." Her business was real. Her opinions were real. That was, Astrid reflected, the strange genius of this arrangement: he wasn't hiring an actress. He was hiring a woman who already existed so vividly that no one would think to check whether the rest of it was true.

He just had to make sure the rest of it held.

* * *

The talking points went faster than they should have.

That was the sentence that kept snagging in him, like a thread pulled loose from a cuff. *Faster than they should have.* Because there was no *should* here, was there? There was no moral speed limit on fabricating a relationship. And yet some internal auditor in his chest kept flagging the line items.

*Topic: work.* Easy. They actually talked about work constantly, mostly in the form of disagreement. Elise believed flowers were architecture; Astrid believed flowers were weather. She had called his signature centerpiece style — those low, dense, architectural arrangements he'd become known for — "beautiful mausoleums." He had called her wild garden-scatter approach "a florist having a panic attack in a meadow." They had both, privately, meant these as compliments. He wrote: *You may insult my aesthetic freely. It will read as intimacy.*

*Topic: family.* Medium difficulty. He gave her the pronunciation guide — Joubert with a soft *zh*, Solange with the full three syllables, never rush Grand-mère — and the landmines. Don't ask Henri about the boatyard dispute of 2019. Don't mention Camille's first novel unless Camille mentions it first, and then be effusive. Do not, under any circumstances, ask Margaux when the wedding is; she wants to be asked, but she wants to be asked *wistfully*, with envy, and Elise would need to calibrate.

*Topic: us.* Here his pen slowed.

He had drafted the how-we-met story three times already, on napkins, in his head at two in the morning, once in the shower where all lies are born clean and rinsed off looking respectable. The truth was unusable: they had met at a wedding, three years ago, the wedding, hers one of the vendor contracts on a day that had ended with an empty altar and a bride's mother screaming in a car park, and Elise had watched him stand in the wreckage of his own life and say nothing in his own defense. He could hardly put that in the briefing.

The version he'd settled on was adjacent enough to feel stable — the best lies, he knew from experience, were load-bearing walls built next to the truth. *We met through work. A wedding, two years ago. You thought my flowers were too rigid. I thought your chaos was a liability. We argued about peonies. We kept running into each other after that — the trade is small. Something started slowly.*

It was almost true. It was true the way a photograph is true: accurate, cropped, and missing everything that mattered.

He wrote it out anyway. Then he added a detail, because details were what sold a story — he'd learned that from watching grooms rehearse vows, the ones who added one specific memory always landed it. *Our first proper date: the crêperie near the Marché des Lices on a Sunday in October. You ordered cidre brut. I remember because you argued with the waiter about whether it was properly dry.*

He stopped.

He had invented that. All of it. There had been no crêperie, no October, no argument about cider — though he now realized with a small, sinking amusement that he could hear exactly how Elise would argue about cider, could see the tilt of her chin, and that the invention had come to him fully furnished, like a room he'd been decorating without noticing.

That was the sickness, right there. Not the lying. The ease.

Astrid Joubert had built a career on making other people's love look effortless. Somewhere along the way — he could mark the date, almost; somewhere in the months after the scandal, when the cancellations came in and then, slowly, the calls came back, from clients who wanted the man with the interesting past, the survivor, the one whose weddings *held* — he had learned to manufacture intimacy the way he manufactured centerpieces: to spec, on deadline, indistinguishable from the real thing at ten paces.

He looked at the yellow tab. *HOW WE MET.* He looked at the fabricated Sunday in October, sitting there in his own handwriting, warm and plausible and completely false.

"Logistics," he said aloud to the empty studio, and the word sounded like what it was: an alibi.

* * *

He broke for lunch and didn't eat it. He stood at the window instead, eating an apple he didn't taste, watching the street below where a couple was arguing cheerfully about directions, the woman gesturing left while the man walked right, both of them laughing, neither of them lost.

There had been a time — he let himself have the memory for exactly as long as it took to finish the apple, no longer, because grief managed well was grief contained — when he hadn't needed dossiers. When Théo had met his mother and Colette had cried, actually cried, and grabbed Théo's face and said *finally, finally*, and Astrid had felt so stupidly, publicly happy that he'd planned his own wedding down to the gram weight of the candles. He'd been good at it. That was the joke nobody laughed at anymore. He had planned the perfect wedding and it had detonated at minute nineteen, mid-vows, and he had stood at the altar learning in real time what every planner secretly knows: that you cannot control the one variable that matters, which is whether people mean it.

And afterward — afterward there had been questions. Why did Théo leave? What did you do, Astrid? What aren't you telling us? And Astrid, who had spent his whole life being the competent one, the reliable one, had discovered the terrible arithmetic of survival: every answer cost more than silence. So he'd paid in silence. He'd paid until silence became a business model, and the business thrived, and here he was, three years later, color-coding a fiction.

The apple core went in the bin with more force than necessary.

* * *

By late afternoon the dossier was done — twelve pages now, the extra page being the section he'd dreaded and therefore saved for last, labeled with the pink tab and, after thirty seconds of staring at it, retitled from *Physical Affection Protocol* to simply *Notes on Closeness*, which fooled absolutely no one including himself.

This was the part he could not fake-logistics his way through, and he knew it, and it made him irrationally angry. Hand-holding was fine — he'd written *initiate sparingly; she'll likely initiate more, and follow her lead*. An arm around shoulders at family photos, fine. But the reunion weekend contained, structurally, inevitably, the shared bedroom at his mother's house — Colette had already assigned rooms with the ruthless efficiency of a woman who'd been waiting years for Astrid to bring someone home, and had put them in the sea-facing room with the double bed and the quilt Grand-mère Solange had made, and Astrid had not had the courage to correct the assumption because correcting it would require explaining why he was bringing a fake girlfriend home at all, which was a conversation he was scheduling for approximately never.

So: *Notes on Closeness.* He wrote practical things. *Sleep back-to-back. I run warm. The floor is an option if you want it; the mattress is old and betrays movement.* He wrote honest things, which surprised him: *I don't expect anything from you beyond the performance. Whatever happens or doesn't happen in that room stays in the category of logistics.* And then he sat with his pen hovering, because there was a third category of things he wanted to write — things like *your hands are always cold and I noticed that at the studio and I don't know why I noticed* — and those did not belong in a dossier, and the fact that he'd drafted them even mentally was a data point he chose, professionally, to ignore.

He closed the folder. Tied it with string, because Elise would appreciate the irony of a briefing bound like a florist's order form, and because Astrid Joubert did not hand over twelve pages of fabrication in a plastic sleeve like some amateur.

Then he checked the clock, swore softly, and saw that she was due in forty minutes and he still hadn't decided the biggest open question, the one he'd been circling all day like a moth around a lamp:

What was he going to tell her about the wedding?

Not the public version — she'd already told him to his face, invoice in hand, that she didn't buy the public version. *Everyone says the groom got cold feet,* she'd said, arms crossed in his studio, surrounded by his careful white orchids. *But I was there, Monsieur Joubert. I was arranging the boutonnières when the screaming started, and cold feet doesn't sound like that.* She'd watched him too closely after that, and he'd changed the subject with the smoothness of a man changing subjects for a living, and she'd let him, and taken the job anyway, which frightened him more than if she'd refused.

She would have questions this weekend. Camille alone generated questions the way the Atlantic generated weather. And Astrid had to decide, before Elise walked through that door with her sharp tongue and her cold hands and her inconvenient perceptiveness, how much truth he could afford to pack alongside the performance.

He looked at the dossier. Twelve pages of beautiful, load-bearing lies.

He took out a thirteenth sheet, blank, and held his pen over it for a long moment.

Then he heard footsteps on the stairs — early, of course she was early, punctuality was clearly another weapon in her arsenal — and he slid the blank page back into the drawer, untied nothing, changed nothing, and went to answer the door wearing the smile Camille called his *client face*.

"You're early," he said.

"I'm thorough," said Elise Marchand, stepping past him into the studio, where her eyes went immediately — inevitably, fatally — to the folder on the drafting table, with its neat string and its color-coded tabs. She raised one eyebrow. "Is that what I think it is?"

"That," said Astrid, "is your homework."

"Oh, wonderful." She picked it up, weighing it in her hand like something that might be armed. "Twelve pages. Astrid. I agreed to one weekend of pretending to like you. This looks like a syllabus."

"There will be a quiz."

"I'm sure there will." But she was already flipping it open, scanning the family tree, and he watched her face do something complicated around the blue tab — around the name *Solange*, maybe, or the annotation *do not perform for her* — and then she looked up at him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere, and said:

"Before I memorize a single word of this — the how-we-met story. Page four." She tapped the yellow tab. "The crêperie. The cider argument." A pause, delicate as a blade. "Which Sunday in October was it, Astrid? Because I have a feeling one of us was there, and it wasn't me."

The studio went very quiet. Outside, the harbor light was going gold, and Astrid Joubert — the man who had lied his way back to respectability one flawless wedding at a time — opened his mouth and found, for the first time in three years, that the script had run out.
