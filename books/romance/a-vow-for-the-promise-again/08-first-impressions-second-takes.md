## 8. First Impressions, Second Takes

The dossier had a section on arrivals. Elise had read it twice on the ferry and once more in the taxi that smelled of salt and hot vinyl: *Arrive together but not too together. Let me introduce you; don't perform affection before an audience has formed. My mother will hug you whether you want it or not—brace your core.*

Brace your core. As if she were about to be tackled by professional athletes instead of embraced by a retired schoolteacher.

The house at the top of the hill was not what the word *family home* had prepared her for. She'd imagined something restrained, tasteful, the kind of place Astrid Joubert would design down to the napkin rings. Instead it was a sprawling white farmhouse with blue shutters in three different shades of blue, as if each generation had repainted according to whim and none of them had consulted the others. A bicycle lay in the gravel like it had been flung there mid-escape. Someone's laundry waved from a line strung between two fig trees. A dog of indeterminate breed was asleep in the shade of the gate, occupying it entirely, and did not move when they arrived, merely opened one eye with the expression of a customs official who had already decided they were smuggling something.

"That's Biscotte," Astrid said. "She'll pretend to guard the house. She won't."

"Good to know the security briefing is accurate."

He gave her a look—the look, the one he'd been giving her since the ferry, the one that said *we agreed to be convincing, not charming*—and then the front door burst open and all strategy died on the gravel.

"Astrid!" The woman coming down the steps was small and round and moving at a speed that suggested she'd been waiting behind that door for some time. "You're late, the ferry was early, this is typical—" And then she saw Elise and stopped so abruptly that her sandals skidded. "Oh," she said, in a voice that carried across the entire garden and possibly to the mainland. "Oh, she's *real*."

"Maman." Astrid's voice had gone carefully pleasant, which Elise was learning was his tell for panic. "This is Elise Marchand. Elise, my mother, Hélène."

Hélène Joubert did not shake hands. Hélène Joubert took both of Elise's hands in hers, held them out at arm's length, and examined her the way Elise herself examined a delivery of peonies—checking for damage, assessing potential, forming opinions that would take years to revise.

"You have good hands," Hélène announced. "A worker's hands. Astrid, you didn't tell me she was beautiful, you only said her name like you were reading it off a form."

"I sent you a photo."

"A photo is paper. This is a person." She pulled Elise into a hug that smelled of lavender and coffee and something baking, and whispered against her ear, with alarming warmth, "Thank God. Three years of 'I'm focusing on work.' I was starting to think I'd raised a monk."

Elise, over Hélène's shoulder, caught Astrid's eye. He looked like a man watching his own blueprint collapse in real time. She smiled sweetly and mouthed: *brace your core.*

* * *

Inside, the house was chaos of the most organized kind—or rather, chaos that had clearly been organized by someone and then immediately undone by everyone else. Shoes by the door in no discernible pattern. A table already set for fourteen, though Elise counted coats suggesting more. From somewhere upstairs came the sound of children running, followed by an adult voice saying *not down the stairs, we talked about this,* followed by the unmistakable percussion of someone going down the stairs anyway.

And then there were the Jouberts themselves, materializing from doorways as if the house produced them.

Camille first—tall, dark-haired, with Astrid's exact eyes and none of his caution. She looked at Elise for precisely one second too long, and Elise felt it like a pin being placed in a map. "So you're the florist," Camille said, shaking her hand firmly. "Astrid told us you run your own shop in Nantes."

"He does his research," Elise said lightly.

"He does everyone's research. It's exhausting." But Camille smiled when she said it, and the smile was genuine even if the eyes weren't finished yet.

Then a man introduced himself as Uncle Rémi, kissed Elise on both cheeks and told her she was too thin, which was apparently the Joubert equivalent of a passport stamp. Then two cousins whose names went in one ear and out the other, then a brother-in-law holding a baby, then the children from the stairs, flushed and victorious, trailed by their defeated parent.

It was, Elise realized within ten minutes, like walking into one of her own arrangements after someone had shaken it. Everything alive, everything tangled, nothing where a planner would have put it—and somehow better for it. She watched Astrid move through it, kissing cheeks, fielding questions, deflecting a cousin's inquiry about his business with a smoothness so practiced it barely registered as deflection, and she thought: *he built himself out of this.* All that polish, all those flawless events—it was this house, sanded down and lacquered until nothing stuck.

The thought arrived uninvited and stayed.

* * *

Grand-mère Solange held court from an armchair near the window, positioned—Elise noted with professional respect—where she could see both the front door and the kitchen without turning her head. She was tiny and upright, with white hair pinned in a way that had probably been fashionable under a different republic, and eyes the color of strong tea that missed absolutely nothing.

Astrid made the introduction with the same careful pleasantness. "Grand-mère, this is Elise."

Solange looked at her. Not the sweeping assessment Hélène had performed, not Camille's forensic once-over. Just looked, steadily, the way you look at weather coming in.

"Sit," Solange said, indicating the chair beside her, and it was not a suggestion.

Elise sat. Beside her, Astrid hovered with the expression of a man whose event had developed an unscheduled program item.

"Grand-mère, perhaps later, we should let Elise settle—"

"Astrid." Solange didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. "Go see if your mother needs help with the tarte. Your mother does not need help with the tarte. Go anyway."

He went. It was, Elise thought, remarkable how quickly the most competent man she knew could be managed by a woman under five feet tall.

For a moment Solange said nothing at all. She watched the room—Rémi refilling wine at four in the afternoon, the baby being passed hand to hand like a precious and slightly alarming parcel, Hélène in the kitchen doorway pretending not to watch them—and then she spoke, quietly, in a voice meant only for Elise.

"My grandson tells you he doesn't like fuss?"

It wasn't quite a question. Elise hesitated. "He tells me he likes things *organized*. Which I've learned is different."

Something moved at the corner of Solange's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment of a correct answer. "Organized," she repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. He was an organized child. Labeled his toy boxes. Made schedules for his holidays." Her tea-colored eyes came back to Elise's face and settled there. "You are not what he described."

"No?" Elise kept her voice easy, though her pulse had picked up. "What did he describe?"

"A serious partner." Solange turned her teacup on its saucer, a quarter turn, precise. "He described a job title and a length of relationship. Eleven months, I believe? He described you the way he describes a venue. Square meters, capacity, access times." She lifted her eyes again. "He did not mention that you look at him like that."

"Like what?" Elise asked, before she could stop herself, and immediately regretted the question because it was an admission of something.

But Solange only patted her hand—once, briefly, her grip surprisingly strong, dry papery skin over iron—and said, "Never mind. Drink your coffee before Rémi waters it."

* * *

The welcome dinner was everything Astrid's briefing had promised and nothing it could have prepared her for. The table groaned. The wine flowed with a generosity that suggested the cellar had been warned. Children migrated between laps. A debate about the correct way to salt tomatoes escalated to genuine volume and was resolved, as far as Elise could tell, by everyone simply continuing to salt tomatoes their own way while insisting the others were wrong.

Elise found herself seated between Camille and a teenage cousin who wanted to know if she'd ever done flowers for anyone famous, and she discovered—to her mild horror—that she was having a genuinely good time. Not performing enjoyment. Having it. The Jouberts were warm the way a kiln is warm: overwhelming, slightly dangerous, and capable of permanently changing whatever was placed inside them.

Across the table, Astrid caught her eye during a lull. He raised his glass a fraction—an inch of acknowledgment, *you're doing well*—and she raised hers back, and the whole exchange lasted two seconds and cost nothing, and she was annoyed at how much she liked it.

"So." Camille leaned toward her, conversational, cutting her chicken with surgical attention. "Eleven months."

"Give or take."

"And you met—remind me? At one of Astrid's weddings?"

"At a wedding he planned, yes. I supplied the flowers." True. Every word true. That was the trick of the dossier, Elise was realizing: it was built almost entirely of true things arranged dishonestly, like a bouquet wired to hide its stems.

"Romantic," Camille said. "All those weddings. Do you believe in them? Weddings, I mean."

"Careful," Elise said, "that's a trap question at this table."

"It's always a trap question at this table." Camille's smile was warm and her eyes were not. "I only ask because Astrid used to believe in them. Enormously. When we were young he planned our neighbor's daughter's wedding for free, age nineteen, in our garden. He cried at the vows. He'll deny it."

"He cried at the vows," Elise repeated.

"He'll deny it," Camille confirmed, and returned to her chicken, and left the sentence sitting there between them like a gift with a blade inside.

Down the table, Hélène was telling the story of Astrid's toy boxes to the new son-in-law, complete with sound effects. Astrid was laughing at something, head tipped back, and for a moment—unscripted, unrehearsed—he looked twenty-three years old and unlabeled, and Elise had to look away and concentrate very hard on her wine.

* * *

Later, when the dishes were stacked and the children had been extracted from various hiding places and deposited into beds, the family dispersed onto the terrace for the last of the wine. The sea was a black presence beyond the wall, audible more than visible. Elise stood near the railing with a cup of something herbal that Hélène had pressed on her "for digestion," and she was alone for perhaps forty seconds before Solange materialized beside her, the way old women do, without any intervening movement.

They stood in silence. Below them, Astrid was helping clear glasses, moving through the lamplight, and Solange watched him with an expression Elise couldn't read—fondness worn down to something harder and older underneath.

"He sleeps badly here," Solange said at last. "Did you know that?"

"I—no."

"The first night, every time. Even now. Thirty-eight years I've known that boy and he still lies awake the first night in this house, listening to it, making sure it's all still here." She sipped her wine. "You'll hear him, maybe. The floor above the kitchen creaks. If you're sharing a room." Her tone made it a statement about architecture, nothing more.

Elise said nothing, which was its own answer, and Solange accepted it as such.

Then the old woman set down her cup and took Elise's hand—not the ceremonial pat from earlier, but a real clasp, both hands around Elise's one, warm and deliberate—and squeezed. Once. Firmly. Holding it a moment longer than the gesture required, looking up at her with those tea-colored eyes that had counted every lie in this family for decades and filed them all under *love, mostly.*

Whatever she saw in Elise's face, she nodded slowly, as if confirming a figure in a ledger.

"Good," Solange said. Just that. "Good."

And she released her hand and went inside, small and straight-backed, leaving Elise alone at the railing with her heart beating oddly and the certain, disorienting knowledge that of everyone in that warm chaotic house, the person who had seen through her first—the fake girlfriend, the hired florist, the performance—was the one person whose seeing felt less like exposure and more like being *counted.*

From the terrace doorway, Camille appeared with a fresh bottle, glanced at Elise, glanced at the empty chair where Solange had been, and smiled a smile that was entirely too knowing.

"So," Camille said, settling into it. "Tell me more about these weddings you and my brother share."
