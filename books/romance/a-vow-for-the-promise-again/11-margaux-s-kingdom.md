## 11. Margaux's Kingdom

Elise had survived wedding-planning committees before. She had once spent four hours mediating a dispute between a bride and her mother over whether peonies were "too common." She had talked a groom down from ordering two hundred white doves for an indoor ceremony in February. She considered herself, at this point, functionally unshockable.

Margaux Joubert tested that theory within ninety seconds of cornering her on the terrace.

"You're a florist," Margaux said, materializing beside her with the efficiency of someone who had been waiting for exactly this moment of solitude. She was holding two cups of coffee and offering one with the practiced grace of a woman who understood that hostages were easier to manage when caffeinated. "And you have *taste*. Actual taste. Do you know how rare that is in this family? My aunt Odile thinks carnations are sophisticated."

"I—thank you?" Elise accepted the coffee because refusing seemed more dangerous than taking it.

"Here's the thing." Margaux lowered her voice as though imparting state secrets, though the terrace was empty except for a cat washing itself on the far wall with the indifference of a creature who had heard every family conversation worth hearing. "My engagement party is in nine days. Nine. And my mother has opinions, my grandmother has *stronger* opinions, and my fiancé's mother has flown in from Lyon with what I can only describe as a vision board."

"A vision board."

"Laminated," Margaux said grimly. "There are lilies on it. Lilies, Elise. At a summer party. On an island. It's like she wants the place to smell like a funeral home having an identity crisis."

Despite herself, Elise felt the corners of her mouth twitch. "Lilies aren't a crime."

"They're a *mood*. And not the mood I'm going for." Margaux turned to face her fully, and Elise recognized the expression—the one clients got when they'd decided you were the solution to a problem they hadn't yet finished articulating. "You did Astrid's flowers. For events. Professionally. So you understand how these things work, the logistics, the—"

"Margaux." Elise set down her cup. "I came here as Astrid's girlfriend, not his vendor."

"Of course. Obviously. Completely different capacity." Margaux waved a hand as if brushing away the suggestion. "This would just be... a favor. Between almost-family. A little creative consultation. Nothing formal."

Elise had been in this business long enough to know that nothing formal was how everything formal began. She thought about saying no. She thought about it seriously, the way one thinks about jumping into cold water—aware that it was probably fine, aware that people did it all the time, aware also that she would rather do literally anything else.

The problem was that Margaux was watching her with those Joubert eyes—warm, dark, unnervingly direct—and Elise was beginning to suspect that the entire family had been issued the same pair and trained to use them.

"One consultation," Elise said. "I'll tell you what flowers won't embarrass you. That's it."

Margaux beamed like she'd just closed a merger. "Wonderful. We'll start tomorrow morning. Nine o'clock. I'll send a car."

"A car? It's an island. The island is forty minutes wide."

"The car is symbolic," Margaux said, already walking away, coffee in hand, victory secured. "It says: this matters."

*

The car, it turned out, was a battered Renault van that smelled of salt and old pastries, driven by a teenager named Baptiste who appeared to be related to half the island through some genealogical mechanism no outsider could hope to map. He dropped Elise at a house above the port—a tall, shuttered thing with blue shutters faded to the color of a morning sky and a garden gone gloriously, defiantly wild.

Inside, chaos reigned with tremendous confidence.

Margaux had colonized the dining room. Fabric swatches covered one end of the table like a battlefield map. A laptop sat open to a spreadsheet with eleven tabs. Her mother—Astrid's Aunt Sylvie, Elise gathered, though the family tree in Astrid's dossier had been suspiciously vague on which branches produced the most noise—was arguing on the phone in the doorway. And in the corner, presiding over a plate of madeleines like a small empress, sat Grand-mère Solange.

"Ah," Solange said, spotting Elise. "The florist."

Not *Astrid's friend*. Not *the girlfriend*. The florist. Elise couldn't decide whether the old woman had seen through the performance or simply found it irrelevant. With Solange, she suspected, both things could be true at once, and neither would be mentioned.

"Madame Joubert."

"Solange." The old woman pushed the plate of madeleines an inch toward her, a gesture that was somehow both generous and a test. "Eat. You're too thin for all this. This family will eat you alive and then ask why you didn't put up more of a fight."

"Mémé," Margaux said, without looking up from her spreadsheet, "we talked about this."

"We talk about many things. It doesn't mean we agree." Solange's eyes stayed on Elise, mild and bottomless. "Sit, child. Tell me. What do you think of lilies?"

"Overused," Elise said, taking a madeleine because declining Solange anything now seemed physically impossible. "At a summer party, they'd overwhelm everything else. If Margaux wants something that smells like the season, she should think about fig leaves, rosemary, maybe some of the wild fennel that grows along the cliffs. Structure from greenery, color kept restrained. Let the sea do the decorating."

There was a silence.

Then Solange smiled—not widely, but with the deep satisfaction of a woman whose suspicions had just been confirmed in her favor. "You see, Margaux? This is what taste sounds like. Listen to her. Your father's mother used to say a house tells you what flowers it wants, if you shut up long enough to hear it."

"That's actually—" Margaux looked up from the spreadsheet, genuinely startled. "That's better than anything on the vision board. Can you write that down?"

And so it began.

By noon, Elise had a notebook full of sketches, a list of local growers Margaux insisted only she could evaluate ("you have the eye; I have a budget and a terror of my future mother-in-law"), and a growing, uneasy awareness that she had been conscripted into something considerably larger than a consultation. By two o'clock, she was on the phone with a grower in Sauzon about dahlia availability while Margaux held up fabric swatches against the window light and asked, casually, terribly casually:

"So. How long have you and Astrid actually been together?"

The question landed with the soft precision of a dart finding its mark.

"Eight months," Elise said, without hesitation, because hesitation was death and she'd rehearsed this. "We met through work."

"Through work." Margaux repeated it pleasantly, holding a swatch of sage-green linen against her wrist. "He doesn't usually date people from work. Or at all, really. There was someone, years ago, but—" She stopped. Adjusted the swatch. Started again in a lighter register. "Anyway. Eight months is lovely. Where did he take you first? Astrid plans everything, you know. Dates, weekends, conversations. If your first date wasn't scheduled with a contingency plan for rain, I'll eat my hat."

"It rained," Elise said. "He had a plan for it."

Which was true, in its way. The dossier had a section on their fabricated first date, complete with weather contingencies. Elise had memorized it on the ferry, annoyed at how thorough it was, annoyed at how touched she'd been by the thoroughness despite knowing it was fiction.

"Mm," said Margaux.

It was a small sound. A nothing sound. But Elise had spent ten years reading brides and mothers-of-brides and grooms who lied about loving peonies, and she knew a filed-away observation when she heard one. Margaux wasn't suspicious, exactly. Margaux was *collecting*. The whole family seemed to collect—small facts, small inconsistencies, stored away in the warm chaotic archive of themselves, to be produced later at precisely the wrong moment.

She needed to be more careful. She also, irritatingly, needed to stay, because Margaux had just slid a folder across the table toward her, and the folder had the power to change the shape of the afternoon entirely.

"What's this?"

"Old vendor files." Margaux shrugged. "Maman keeps everything. When I told her I wanted to see how Astrid structured his contracts—for reference, since apparently my fiancé's family thinks contracts are optional—she gave me a box. It's mostly boring. Invoices, insurance certificates, that kind of thing." She paused, and something flickered across her face—quick, quickly smoothed over. "Some of it isn't boring."

Elise opened the folder.

The top sheet was a catering invoice dated three years ago, June. A venue contract beneath it. A florist's order form—her own shop's predecessor, a wholesaler she still used—and then, near the bottom, a payment schedule from an event planning company she recognized instantly, because everyone in the industry recognized it: *Joubert Événements*, Astrid's company, back when it was young and hungry and took every job it could get.

Dated three years ago. June.

The month of the wedding. *The* wedding.

Elise kept her face pleasant and her hands steady and turned the page as though she were reviewing paper stock options. Inside, she could feel her pulse doing something unprofessional.

Because here was the thing nobody said out loud on Belle-Île, the thing that lived in the pauses after Astrid's name at family dinners: three years ago, Astrid Joubert had planned the society wedding of the season—his own—and it had collapsed mid-ceremony when his fiancé walked out, and in the wreckage afterward there had been whispers. About money. About a scene. About what Astrid had or hadn't done, said, known. The whispers had never resolved into anything. They had just settled over him like dust, and Astrid had never once swept them away. He'd let them sit. He'd built his reputation back anyway, plank by polished plank, and never defended himself, and everyone had drawn their own conclusions, and the conclusions had mostly been unkind.

Elise knew, because she had been there. Not at the ceremony—at the periphery of it, delivering flowers for another vendor when the whole glittering machine seized up and died. She had watched Astrid Joubert stand in a church doorway in a perfect suit with a face like a man listening to a building fall, and she had never forgotten it. She had told him, in his studio, during their first argument, that she didn't buy his public version of events. She had never told him how much space the memory took up in her head.

And now his cousin was handing her his contracts.

"These are Astrid's?" she asked, keeping her voice at the register of professional interest.

"Some of them. Maman saved things from that year—it was a big deal for the family, his company landing that wedding, and then..." Margaux trailed a finger along the table edge. "It ended badly. You know that much, presumably."

"He's never talked about it."

"No," Margaux said. "He wouldn't." She said it without judgment, which somehow made it worse. Then she brightened with the deliberate brightness of someone changing lanes. "But look—here's the useful part. See how he itemized the deposits? Maman says no one in the region structures payment schedules like he does. Copy this format for our party and my future mother-in-law might actually stop sending me laminated lilies."

Elise copied the format. She was good at her job; her hands knew what to do even when her mind was elsewhere, turning over the pages she'd glimpsed deeper in the folder—the ones that weren't invoices at all. A letter, maybe. A settlement of some kind. She'd seen the edge of a second signature block beneath Astrid's company name, a name she didn't recognize, and she'd seen Margaux watch her see it.

"There's more in the box," Margaux said lightly, too lightly, closing the folder. "But honestly, the rest is ancient history. Family archaeology. Maman keeps everything; it doesn't mean any of it matters anymore."

"Right," Elise said.

"Although." Margaux tilted her head, and for a moment she looked startlingly like Astrid—the same assessing tilt, the same careful face arranged over quicker thoughts. "You'd tell me, wouldn't you? If something in all this seemed off. You seem like someone who notices things."

"I notice flowers," Elise said. "Mostly."

"Mm," said Margaux again.

*

Baptiste drove her back along the coast road in the late gold light, the sea flashing between the pines like a coin being flipped over and over. Elise sat in the passenger seat with her notebook on her knees and the madeleines Solange had pressed on her at the door—*take them, child, you'll need the strength*—in a paper bag against her feet.

She should have said no to the whole day. She knew that. She had come to this island to play a role for one weekend, collect her fee, and leave with her professionalism intact. Instead she was now, by Margaux's delighted declaration, "basically the party's creative director," with a site visit scheduled for Thursday and a flower order forming in her head whether she wanted it to or not. She had been recruited, flattered, fed, and folded into the family machinery in under six hours, and the truly humiliating part was how natural it had felt. How *good*. Solange's dry approval. Margaux's ferocious competence. Even the chaos—especially the chaos—felt like something Elise hadn't known she'd been missing: a family loud enough that no one noticed you didn't quite belong, warm enough that you stopped minding.

And underneath all of it, folded into the folder like a trap dressed as a favor: the past. Astrid's past, in paperwork, in his own meticulous handwriting, sitting in a box in his aunt's house where anyone with a reason could open it.

*You'd tell me, wouldn't you? If something seemed off.*

What Elise kept returning to, as the van rattled toward the port, was the second signature block. The name she hadn't recognized, printed neatly beneath Joubert Événements on a document dated weeks *after* the wedding collapsed. Why would a dead wedding generate paperwork after it died? Settlements did. Cancellations did. But settlements had a smell to them, and this one—if it was one—had smelled less like grief and more like money changing hands for reasons no one wanted written down.

She thought of Astrid at dinner last night, taking her hand under the table without thinking, the contact lasting one beat too long to be performance. She thought of the way his voice went flat and clinical whenever anyone drifted near the old story, a man describing a room he refused to enter.

She thought: *he would hate me looking at that folder.*

She thought: *he never asked me not to.*

The van pulled up outside the house where the family had installed them both—separate rooms, propriety observed, a detail Astrid had negotiated with his mother over the phone in a tone Elise had privately found adorable and had told him so, to his visible discomfort. Through the open window she could hear Camille's laugh, the clatter of someone making coffee, the ordinary music of an evening that didn't know what Elise now carried in her notebook bag.

She climbed out, thanked Baptiste, and stood a moment on the lane, the sea behind her, the lit windows ahead.

Tomorrow she would order dahlias. Thursday she would walk the party site. Somewhere in between, she decided, watching Astrid's silhouette pass behind the kitchen curtain—head bent over whatever he was always planning, always managing, always keeping tidy—she was going to find a way back into that box in Sylvie's house.

Not to betray him. She told herself that, and mostly believed it.

But a woman could only stand so close to a locked room before she started wondering what, exactly, everyone was so determined not to remember.
