## 17. Solange's Kitchen

The kitchen at the Joubert house smelled like a decision had been made about butter and there was no going back.

Elise had come down for water—just water, a simple, uncomplicated errand—and found the door already open, spilling warm light onto the terrace stones, and Grand-mère Solange standing at the counter in a cardigan the color of dried lavender, rolling dough with the unhurried authority of a woman who had been doing this since before ovens had opinions.

"You," Solange said without turning around. "You walk like someone who is trying not to be noticed. Sit down. It never works."

"I was just getting water."

"The tap is right there. The chair is also right there." Solange nodded at the kitchen table, which was set for two with mismatched cups, as though she had known exactly who would wander in and when. "One of them gives you water. The other one gives you company. Choose wisely."

Elise chose the chair. She was learning that in this house, you did not get offered things so much as you got maneuvered into them, gently, by people who loved you and had decades of practice.

Solange poured something amber into both cups—not tea, unless tea had learned to burn pleasantly on the way down—and sat across from her with the small satisfied sigh of a woman settling into her favorite part of the day.

"My son-in-law thinks I shouldn't drink this after eight," she said. "My son-in-law also thinks margarine is butter. We all have our delusions."

Elise laughed before she could stop herself. "Astrid mentioned you don't take much nonsense."

"Astrid mentions very little, which is how I know he takes too much of it." Solange wrapped her hands around her cup. Outside, the last blue of evening was sliding off the harbor, and somewhere down the hill a dog was making its case to the moon. "So. You are the florist."

"I'm the florist."

"Who is dating my grandson."

"Who is—" Elise paused, recalibrating. "Yes. That."

"Mm." Solange took a sip, watching her over the rim with eyes that were pale gray and entirely unfooled. "You hesitated. Astrid would have liked you to hesitate less. But I like it. Hesitation means you're still deciding things. The ones who don't hesitate anymore have usually decided to stop looking."

"That's very specific advice for a first conversation."

"This isn't our first conversation." Solange smiled, and there was something in it—warmth folded around an edge. "This is our first conversation where we're both pretending it's casual. Those are my favorite kind. Shall I tell you what I know?"

"I feel like you're going to regardless."

"I am going to regardless. It's one of the few privileges of being old—that people assume you'll say whatever you like, so they stop being surprised when you do." She set down her cup. Her hands were spotted and strong, baker's hands, gardener's hands, hands that had clearly held a great many things together that other people had let fall.

* * *

"What do you know about weddings?" Solange asked.

"I make them beautiful for a living. Whether or not they deserve it."

"And do they? Deserve it?"

"Some of them." Elise turned her cup slowly. "Most couples deserve the flowers. A few deserve each other. It's not always the same list."

Solange made a sound low in her chest, almost a laugh, almost grief. "Three years ago, my grandson was going to be married. You know this."

"I've heard things."

"You've heard *things*. In this family, 'things' is a full-time occupation. Camille deals in them professionally. Margaux trades them like currency. And Astrid—" she paused, and for a moment the humor went out of her face entirely, leaving something older and more tired underneath, "—Astrid built a wall out of them and lives inside it."

Elise said nothing. The dog outside had won its argument with the moon and gone quiet.

"The wedding was here. Not on Belle-Île—the mainland, a château near Nantes, all ivy and pretension. His father was still alive then; he wore the blue suit he hated because Astrid asked him to. Solange's voice had gone soft and even, the cadence of a story told many times only to herself. "Two hundred guests. Peonies—I remember the peonies, enormous, ridiculous, pink as a scandal. Camille cried during the rehearsal dinner and claimed it was the wine. Margaux was furious the bride's dress was more expensive than hers would be. Everything was normal. Everything was *fine*."

"And then?"

"And then, halfway through the ceremony, the bride was gone."

Elise kept her face still. She had heard versions of this—the florist version, passed around vendors' tables like contraband: *the Joubert wedding, the one where the groom got left at the altar, the one where they say he—* but hearing it from this kitchen, in this voice, was a different thing altogether. This wasn't gossip. This was a wound described by someone who had watched it happen.

"Gone," Elise said quietly.

"Gone. No note, no scene, no dramatic running in heels down the aisle, though God knows the novelists would have preferred it. One moment she was standing beside him, and the next moment her place was empty and her mother was crying in a way that didn't look like surprise." Solange's thumb traced the rim of her cup. "Do you know what two hundred people look like when they don't know what to look at? They look at the floor. All of them, at once, like a field of flowers bowing in wind. Except they weren't looking at the floor. They were looking at *him*."

"At Astrid."

"At Astrid. Because that is what crowds do. Someone must be at fault, and the person standing alone at the altar is the easiest answer." She lifted her chin slightly. "By morning, the story had grown teeth. That he had driven her to it. That there had been fights, cruelty, something hidden. By the end of the week, the story no longer needed facts. It had momentum."

"Did anyone ask him what happened?"

"Everyone asked him what happened. That was the problem. He answered every question except the true one." Solange rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking out at the dark water with her back very straight, the posture of a woman who had spent three years refusing to stoop. "His mother wanted to hire lawyers. His father wanted to drive to the château and have words with the bride's family. Camille wanted to fight the entire internet, which in those days was smaller but no less stupid. And Astrid said: *let it go.*"

"He said that?"

"He said, 'It doesn't matter.' Three words. I have thought about those three words more than I have thought about most sermons." She turned back. "Because it mattered. Of course it mattered. But he had decided that the fastest way to survive was to agree with the lie—to fold it up neatly, put it in a drawer, and build his business on top of the drawer. And he is *very good* at building things on top of drawers. Have you seen his filing system? Terrifying. Immaculate. You could hide a whole marriage under there and no one would ever find it."

Elise's throat felt tight. "And you believed the story? About him?"

Solange came back to the table and looked at her for a long moment, and whatever was in her face made Elise glad she hadn't lied about anything yet tonight, because she suspected nothing would survive that gaze.

"I was in the third row," Solange said. "I saw her face in the last minute before she vanished. I saw her look at someone in the crowd—not at Astrid, *past* him—at someone sitting near the front on her family's side. And I saw that person look away first." She sat down again, slowly. "I have never told anyone that. Not Camille, not my daughter, not the priest who came to console us and mostly consoled the canapés. Because Astrid had decided to carry it, and a grandmother learns that sometimes love means letting someone carry a thing badly rather than wrestling it from their arms and dropping it yourself."

"But you're telling me."

"I'm telling you because you looked at him today at lunch the way I looked at my husband for fifty-one years, and because you flinched—only a little, only I saw it—when Margaux mentioned the château wedding. Flinching means you know something. And I am an old woman with excellent butter and no patience left for watching two people circle a truth like cats around a saucer." She reached across the table and covered Elise's hand with her own. Her grip was surprisingly firm. "Whatever you know, child—you may keep it. That's your right. But know this: whatever the world decided about my grandson that week, the world was wrong. I don't need documents to tell me. I raised him. He has been apologizing for a crime since he was twenty-nine years old, and the only question I have never been able to answer is—who taught him he deserved the sentence?"

The fire ticked in the stove. Somewhere upstairs a door closed, footsteps crossing toward the bathroom—Camille, by the heavy tread—and Elise became aware of how loud her own heartbeat had gotten.

"There's something else," Solange said, softer now. "That night, after everyone had gone, after the château had turned off its beautiful lights and pretended none of it happened—one person stayed. Sat with him in the empty reception hall until two in the morning while he took down the seating chart card by card, because he couldn't bear to leave it for strangers to dismantle. Do you know who stayed?"

"Camille?"

"No." Solange's mouth curved, sad and fond. "Me. And I will tell you what he said, because I have carried it three years and it is heavy and you look like you can hold things. He said, '*Grand-mère, if I tell the truth, I lose everything I've built. If I stay silent, I only lose myself.*' And I said, 'Astrid, you can rebuild everything. You cannot rebuild yourself.' And he said—" her voice caught, just barely, a hairline crack in old porcelain, "—he said, 'Watch me.'"

* * *

They sat in silence for a while after that, the good kind, the kind that doesn't demand filling. Solange refilled their cups. Outside, the lighthouse at the point swept its slow arm across the water, patient as ever, finding the same rocks every time.

"Why are you telling me this?" Elise finally asked. "Really."

"Really?" Solange considered it honestly, which seemed to be her default setting. "Because this weekend, things are coming to this island that should have stayed buried, and I am too old to dig, and you have young hands." She said it lightly, but the words landed like stones dropped into still water. "Also because you haven't once asked me for anything, and in this family that makes you either a saint or a spy, and saints are rarer."

"I'm neither."

"Nobody is. That's what makes us interesting." She patted Elise's hand once more and released it. "One more thing, and then I'll let you escape. The peonies at that wedding—everyone remembers the peonies. Do you know why?"

Elise went cold. "Because they were mine."

"Because they were yours." Solange nodded, unsurprised, unsurprising. "The only vendor who called afterward—not to complain about payment, mind you, though I hear you were never paid, which is a sin I intend to discuss with my grandson at length—but to ask whether *he* was alright. A florist. Everyone else sent invoices. You sent a question." She smiled, and for a moment she looked mischievous, almost young. "I remembered the name. Marchand. I forget where I put my glasses and I remember *Marchand*. So when Astrid announced he was bringing a serious partner named Elise Marchand, I made these rolls, and I waited."

"How long did you wait?"

"Since Tuesday. I'm very patient. It's my best quality after the butter."

Elise laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and felt some knot behind her ribs loosen by a single degree. Then tighten again, because the truth was a crowded room tonight and she was holding more of it than she'd walked in with: the contracts in her bag upstairs, the signature she'd matched, the payment that had bought silence. *The only question I have never been able to answer,* Solange had said. Elise could answer it. She had the paperwork to answer it. And saying it here, in this warm kitchen, would detonate everything before Astrid was ready—or worse, before he chose it himself.

"You look like a woman doing arithmetic," Solange observed.

"Just deciding what to have for breakfast."

"Liar. Good. You'll fit in." Solange began stacking cups, then paused. "He'll tell you himself, eventually. Or he won't, and he'll spend the rest of his life arranging flowers over the hole. Either way—" she shrugged, flour drifting off her cardigan like snow, "—that's his vow to make. I only wanted you to know that somebody witnessed the original one."

Elise stood, thanked her for the drink she couldn't name, and made it as far as the doorway before Solange spoke again, casually, terribly:

"Oh—and Elise? The tall man with the white linen trousers who arrived on the afternoon boat with Margaux's fiancé. The one who watched Astrid across the terrace today like a cat watches a bird it has already eaten once." She didn't turn from the sink. "He was at the wedding too. Third row. Her family's side."

The lighthouse swept the water. Somewhere upstairs, Astrid's footsteps crossed the ceiling, moving toward the room where Elise's bag sat open, the contracts inside it, the truth folded in triplicate.

She climbed the stairs slowly, doing the arithmetic all the way up.
