## 19. Public Hand-Holding

Astrid had planned beach gatherings before. He knew the choreography of them the way some men knew the liturgy of a church: where the coolers went, how many umbrellas the wind could be trusted to hold, the precise ratio of chairs to people that made a gathering feel casual rather than abandoned. He had drawn the seating map for this one himself, on a napkin, at six in the morning, because the Joubert family did not do casual so much as they did *unstructured with intention*, and someone had to be the intention.

The beach below the family house was a crescent of pale sand that the tide had been sculpting for centuries into something almost architectural. Camille had spread blankets. Margaux had arrived with a portable speaker and a playlist that could only be described as aggressive joy. The children were already in the water, shrieking in the particular register of children who have discovered that cold water is a personality trait. Grand-mère Solange sat in the one chair that had been carried down for her, wearing a wide-brimmed hat and the expression of a woman who had seen every family drama this beach could produce and intended to outlive them all.

And then the car came down the coastal road, and Astrid's stomach did something he had not authorized.

He had known, of course. Margaux had announced it at breakfast with the breezy cruelty of someone who did not know she was holding a knife: *Julien's business partner is on the island this week, he's joining us today, you'll adore him, Théo Vasseur.* She had said the name the way one says a wine label. Astrid had put down his coffee very carefully, the way one does when the alternative is throwing the cup at the wall, and had gone to find Elise.

She had been on the terrace, reading his dossier — his own fabricated how-we-met story — with the critical eye of a florist judging a rival's arrangement.

"Théo Vasseur," he had said. "He'll be at the beach today."

She had looked up. She hadn't asked who Théo was. She had read the dossier; the dossier said *partner, event planner, no further details*, because Astrid had written it himself and had discovered that his pen simply refused to form the sentence. But she had looked at his face, and whatever she saw there had made her close the folder and say, "Okay. What's the plan?"

There was no plan. That was the thing. Astrid Joubert had a contingency for weather, for vendor no-shows, for drunk uncles and collapsing tiered cakes and the moment when a bride's mother announces, mid-reception, that she never liked the groom. He did not have a contingency for the man who had left him at the altar walking down a beach in linen trousers, tanned and easy and *greeting people*, as though the last three years had been a vacation he'd taken from Astrid's life rather than the demolition of it.

"Théo!" Margaux's voice carried across the sand, bright as a struck bell. "You made it!"

Astrid stood very still near the umbrella he had personally anchored, and watched the past approach at a casual jog.

Théo Vasseur had not aged so much as he had been *maintained*. The same gold-brown hair pushed back from his forehead, the same smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his face, as though the smile were an advance team sent to secure the territory. He embraced Margaux, embraced Julien, shook hands with Camille's husband, laughed at something one of the children said. He was good at this. He had always been good at this. It was one of the things Astrid had loved about him, once, back when he mistook fluency for sincerity.

Then Théo turned, and saw him, and the smile widened by exactly one degree.

"Astrid Joubert." Théo crossed the sand with his hand already extended. "Look at you. Three years and you haven't changed a bit. Still the most organized man on any beach in France, I'd wager."

There it was. The charm laid over the blade like a napkin over a table setting. *Still organized.* As if the wedding had been a scheduling conflict. As if Astrid had stood in front of two hundred guests holding a ring and a speech and a future, and the only thing that had failed was his calendar.

"Théo." Astrid shook the hand. It was warm, firm, dry. He hated that he noticed. "I didn't realize you knew Julien."

"Business partners now. Small world, the wedding industry — well." Théo laughed, and the laugh had an edge in it, fine as wire. "I suppose you'd know that better than anyone. How *is* the business? I hear you've become quite the name. Very resilient of you."

*Resilient.* Astrid felt the word land the way a stone lands in still water, rings going out in every direction. Resilient, as in: you survived what I did to you. Resilient, as in: I'm still watching to see how much of it you remember.

"I keep busy," Astrid said, and his voice came out level, and he was privately astonished by this.

"And this must be the famous Elise." Théo turned to her, and Astrid realized with a lurch that Elise had appeared at his side at some point in the last thirty seconds — had crossed the sand without him registering it, the way weather arrives, the way rescue arrives. She was holding a glass of something cold. She was wearing a linen dress the color of sea glass and an expression of pleasant, total attention.

"Famous," Elise said. "I should hope so. I did the flowers for the Duval-Lemaire wedding in June. Two hundred guests, a heat wave, and a florist in Lyon who sent peonies that had already given up on life. I rebuilt the entire ceremony arch in four hours. You may have read about it."

"You're a florist?" Théo's smile did its advance-team routine.

"I'm the reason that wedding didn't end up in the gossip columns," Elise said pleasantly. "Which, given your history with weddings, Théo, you'll appreciate is a specialized skill."

The beach went very slightly quiet. Somewhere behind them, a child shrieked about a crab. Margaux's smile froze in the particular way of a hostess who has heard a glass chip and is deciding whether it matters.

Théo's eyebrows rose a fraction. "My history?"

"Well." Elise sipped her drink. "Everyone has a history. Mine is peonies. Yours is — well, I'm sure it's fascinating. Astrid never talks about it, which I always think says more than talking would." She turned her head and looked at Astrid then, and her eyes were doing something complicated: fierce and amused and, underneath it, a question — *is this the line, should I stop, tell me and I'll stop* — and Astrid, who had spent three years being the most composed man in every room, found that he could not produce a single signal.

So she didn't stop.

"Anyway," Elise went on, breezy as a forecast, "Astrid's told me all about his work, obviously, but hardly anything about the people in it. He's very private. I find it one of his most annoying qualities." She said it with such easy affection that it landed as intimacy, and Astrid watched it land on Théo like that: *she knows him, she's inside the walls you never got through.* "You'll have to tell me stories sometime. I collect them."

"I'm sure Astrid tells his own stories," Théo said, and the wire was back in his voice.

"He's terrible at it," Elise said. "That's why I'm here."

She took Astrid's hand.

It was, he understood later, a tactical decision — a clean, visible, unambiguous piece of stagecraft, exactly the kind of move the dossier would have recommended, hand-holding as set dressing, the fake couple's most reliable prop. But she took it *now*, in front of Théo, with her fingers lacing through his like they'd done it a thousand times, her thumb moving once across his knuckles in a small, absent, devastatingly practiced gesture, and every piece of stagecraft in the world fell away from it. It was just her hand. Warm, a little sandy, holding on.

"Astrid," she said, "Solange is signaling that you're neglecting the umbrella situation. I believe it's code."

"It is absolutely code," said Grand-mère Solange, from her chair, without opening her eyes.

Astrid went. He let Elise tow him across the sand toward the umbrella, toward his grandmother's imperious little wave, and it was only when they were safely out of Théo's sightline — behind the windbreak, in the smell of sunscreen and salt — that he realized he was still holding her hand, and that she was letting him.

"You okay?" she said, low.

"I don't know," Astrid said, which was the most honest sentence he'd produced in three years.

"Good answer." She squeezed once and let go, and the absence of her hand felt like a removed prop in a room he'd already started living in. "He's going to keep doing that, you know. The charm-with-a-knife-in-it. All weekend, if we let him."

"I know."

"So here's what I think." She turned to face him, blocking his view of the water, which was a kindness; he hadn't realized he was staring at the beach where Théo was laughing with Julien until she took the view away. "We don't run. We don't perform either. We stand there, we hold hands, we let him look at the life he thinks he ruined, and we have a *nice afternoon*, Astrid. That's the whole strategy. Spite, but well-dressed."

A laugh came out of him — short, startled, real. "Spite, but well-dressed."

"It's my family motto." She smiled, and for a moment the fierceness softened into something else, something that looked almost like worry. "Hey. You did fine back there. You didn't flinch."

"I flinched internally."

"Internally doesn't count. That's the whole point of a good exterior." She glanced over her shoulder at the beach, at the linen figure now sprawled in a folding chair like he'd never left it. "Although — Astrid. He's not here by accident. Margaux's fiancé's business partner, on this island, this week, at this beach? That's a lot of coincidence for a man who once had your entire future in his hands."

Astrid had thought the same thing, at six in the morning, staring at his ceiling. He had decided not to think it again, because thinking it led to a question he didn't want to ask: *what does Théo want?* And under that question, quieter and worse: *what is Théo afraid I'll say?*

"Small world," he said. "The wedding industry."

"Don't quote Théo at me," Elise said, "it's unbecoming," and she took his hand again, and this time it wasn't tactics, or it wasn't only tactics, and neither of them said anything about it at all.

* * *

The afternoon arranged itself around them the afternoons of Astrid's childhood used to: swimming, and the cold picnic, and Camille's husband losing a volleyball point to an eight-year-old and taking it with the dignity of a fallen king. Astrid found, to his surprise, that he was having a good time — that under the vigilance, under the part of his brain that kept tracking the linen figure across the beach like a lighthouse tracking a ship, there was a warm running current of ordinary happiness, and that most of it had a source, and the source was currently arguing with Margaux about whether an engagement party could legally feature both a seafood tower and a cheese wheel.

"It's not a question of *legality*," Margaux was saying, "it's a question of *taste*—"

"It's a question of both, and the answer is yes to both," Elise said. "A seafood tower *on* the cheese wheel. Trust me. I've seen it done. People wept."

"People wept?"

"People wept, Margaux. Isn't that what you want?"

Margaux looked at her for a long moment with the narrowed eyes of a woman recognizing a superior force, and then said, "You're helping me with this party. I've decided. Don't argue, I've already decided," and Astrid, watching from the umbrella, felt the strange double sensation of pride and alarm — pride because Elise was magnificent, alarm because Margaux's engagement party would put Elise deep inside the family's paperwork, its vendors, its history, its *contracts*, and there were contracts in this family's history that Astrid had hoped were sleeping.

He put the thought away. He was getting very good at putting thoughts away. It was, he was beginning to suspect, the only skill the last three years had actually taught him.

Théo, for his part, was playing a longer game. Astrid could see the shape of it by mid-afternoon: Théo did not attack again. He was charming to Camille, complimentary to Solange, delightful to the children, who had no idea and adored him. He made a point of telling Astrid, twice, how impressive his career had become — *I follow your work, you know, everyone in the industry talks about Joubert Events* — and each time he said it, Astrid heard the sentence underneath, the one Théo had carried for three years and had finally come to check on: *I know what you never said. Do you?*

At one point Théo wandered over to the umbrella while Elise was in the water with Camille, and stood beside Astrid in a silence that had weight.

"You've done well for yourself," Théo said, looking out at the sea. "I mean that. I always worried, after — well. I'm glad it worked out."

*After.* The word did so much work, standing there. After what, Théo? Say the whole sentence. Say *after I walked out of a church with two hundred people watching and let them decide it was your fault.*

"It did," Astrid said.

"And you're — happy?" Théo glanced at him, and there it was, under the tan and the linen: not guilt exactly. Something adjacent to guilt. Something that had learned to live in the same house as guilt without paying rent. "You seem happy. That's all I ever wanted, you know. For everyone to land somewhere soft."

Astrid looked at him. Three years of rehearsed composure rose in him like a tide, and for one dangerous second he considered spending all of it at once — right here, on this beach, in front of his family — saying everything, the whole architecture of that day, the letter, the phone call, the family in the back row, the silence he'd been paid to keep. It would take ninety seconds. He had planned speeches in less.

Then he saw Elise coming up out of the water, hair slicked back, laughing at something Camille said, and he thought: *not here. Not like this. Not with her holding my hand for a reason that isn't real yet.*

"Everyone landed somewhere," Astrid said.

Théo studied him for a moment, and then smiled the advance-team smile. "Good," he said. "That's good. Because I'd hate to think there was anything unresolved between us. Old wounds, you know. They fester. Better closed." He put a hand briefly on Astrid's shoulder — a touch that was meant to look warm and landed like a seal on a document — and strolled back down the sand toward Julien's cooler.

Astrid stood very still and understood, with the cold clarity he usually reserved for weather calls, that Théo had just told him the terms. *Stay closed. Stay quiet. Or we find out what festering looks like.*

"You look like a man doing math he hates," said Solange, without opening her eyes.

"Grand-mère."

"Mm." She adjusted her hat. "The tall one with the smile like a hotel lobby. He was at your wedding, wasn't he? The one who — " she made a small, dismissive gesture, the gesture of a woman for whom even catastrophe was a scheduling matter. "I never liked his shoes. A man who wears shoes like that to a beach has something to prove."

Astrid almost laughed. "You noticed his shoes."

"I notice everything, that's my tragedy." She opened one eye and looked at him, and her gaze was sharp and old and kind. "You didn't eat the last of the ham. Camille will be wounded. Go. And Astrid — " he paused, and she said, quietly, "whatever that man thinks he's holding over you, remember that I have been holding this family together since before he was born, and I have never once lost to a hotel lobby."

He went to eat the ham. His hands were not entirely steady, and he was grateful, for once, for the family's chaos, because nobody was watching closely enough to see.

* * *

The sun went down the way it does on that coast, all at once, like a lamp being switched off by someone in a hurry. Blankets came up. Children were towed, dripping and protesting, toward the house. And Astrid stood at the water's edge, shaking sand out of the umbrellas, when Elise came to stand beside him with two glasses of wine from the bottle Camille had abandoned.

"For the crew," she said, handing him one. "We earned it. I told a man his history was fascinating to his face and nobody disowned us. That's a win."

"You were extraordinary today."

"I was well-dressed and spiteful. As planned." She clinked her glass against his. Then, quieter: "He got to you, though. At the end. The shoulder thing."

"The shoulder thing," Astrid agreed.

"What did he say? Really. Under the charm."

Astrid looked out at the dark water. He could tell her. That was the terrible, simple fact of the evening: he could tell her, and she would listen, and she had already proven today that she would stand between him and a loaded smile without being asked. He could tell her about the letter, the money, the silence, the whole rotten structure he'd agreed to live inside.

Instead he heard himself say: "He said he's glad I landed somewhere soft."

Elise looked at him for a long moment. The last light was going; he couldn't read her face, only the outline of her, and the fact that she didn't look away.

"That's not what he said," she said finally. Not accusing. Just accurate, the way she was accurate about stems and seasons and people. "But all right. Not yet, then." She finished her wine. "Come on, planner. There's a cheese wheel to design, and your cousin has already texted me twice, and I have the distinct feeling that helping Margaux Joubert plan a party is going to be more educational than either of us expects."

She walked back up the sand toward the lights of the house, and Astrid followed, and it was only halfway up the beach that he registered what she'd said — *more educational than either of us expects* — and felt, somewhere under the wine and the salt air and the ghost-warmth of her hand in his, the first cold thread of a question he would spend the next two days failing to answer:

What, exactly, was in the family's files — and what would Elise find there first?
