## 20. What Elise Found

The box arrived at Margaux's villa on a Tuesday morning, and it was, in Elise's professional opinion, the most beautiful thing she'd seen all week.

Not because of what was inside—though what was inside turned out to be dynamite wrapped in tissue paper—but because of how it was packed. Whoever had assembled Margaux's mother's archive of party ephemera had done it with the reverence of a museum curator and the neurosis of someone who alphabetized their spice rack. Every ribbon coiled separately. Every invitation sleeved in acid-free plastic. A handwritten index taped to the inside lid in looping, immaculate script.

"Your mother," Elise said, lifting out a folder labeled *Catering — 2011 — DO NOT REUSE THE FISH GUY*, "is my new hero."

Margaux looked up from her phone, where she had been conducting what appeared to be a low-grade war with her fiancé's business partner over seating arrangements for the engagement party. "Maman doesn't throw anything away. She says a family that loses its paperwork loses its memory." She waved a manicured hand at the box. "Everything you need for the party should be in there. Old vendor lists, contacts, the whole history of Joubert entertaining. Consider it your treasure chest."

*The whole history of Joubert entertaining.*

Elise's hands went still on the folder.

She had been on this island nine days now. Nine days of fake smiles and real laughter, of learning which cousin told which stories too loudly, of memorizing the way Astrid's shoulders dropped half an inch when his mother wasn't watching. Nine days of sleeping—chastely, absurdly—in the guest room next to his, both of them performing the role of a settled couple so convincingly that sometimes Elise forgot which parts were acting.

And nine days of the question sitting in her chest like an unswallowed stone: *what actually happened at that wedding?*

She knew the public version. Everyone in their world knew the public version. Three years ago, Astrid Joubert—the rising star of event planning, engaged to a beautiful woman from a wealthy family—stood at the altar while his bride never came. And then, in the weeks after, the whispers: that there had been trouble before the wedding, that money had gone missing from somewhere, that Astrid had been seen arguing with vendors, that maybe, just maybe, the runaway bride had run for a reason. The story had no shape and no source, which was exactly what made it stick. Rumors without edges are like water; they get into everything.

Astrid had never refuted any of it. He'd simply gone quiet, rebuilt his business brick by careful brick, and become the man who planned other people's perfect days with a precision that bordered on penance.

Elise had been there, that day. Junior florist, twenty-three years old, holding a bouquet worth more than her rent while the church filled with murmuring guests and the organist played the same prelude four times. She remembered Astrid at the front, remember the exact moment his face did something complicated—not devastation, not yet, but something *before* devastation, something that looked almost like recognition—and she had thought, even then: *he knows something we don't.*

She had never bought the whispers. But she hadn't known what to buy instead.

Now Margaux's mother's beautiful, obsessive archive sat open in front of her, and somewhere inside it, presumably, were the contracts from the most famous non-wedding in recent memory.

"Don't go digging through anything embarrassing," Margaux said lightly, already turning back to her phone. "There are things in there Maman would die over."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Elise said, and heard how easily the lie came, and decided not to examine that too closely either.

* * *

She waited until afternoon, when Margaux departed for a fitting and the villa emptied into that particular golden silence of shuttered rooms and distant sea. She made herself a coffee first. She told herself it was due diligence—a party planner reviewing past vendors to avoid repeat disasters—which was true, in the way that a key is technically a piece of metal.

The folder was thin. That was the first strange thing.

For a wedding of that scale—three hundred guests, imported peonies, a string quartet flown in from Lyon—the paper trail should have been a flood. Instead she found a short stack: a venue agreement, a catering summary, a floral contract with her own old employer's letterhead that made her stomach flip sideways, and then—

Then a settlement document.

It wasn't labeled as such. It was labeled *Amicable Resolution of Outstanding Matters*, which was the kind of language lawyers used when they wanted a thing to sound like a handshake instead of a muzzle. Elise read it twice, three times, her coffee going cold beside her.

The gist was simple. In exchange for a payment—substantial, dated six weeks after the wedding—the families agreed to "refrain from public discussion of circumstances surrounding the event." No statements. No interviews. No litigation. Everyone walks away. Everyone stays quiet.

The payment hadn't come from Astrid's side.

Elise stared at the signature block at the bottom of the page. The bride's family name she recognized—everyone did, it was on buildings in Paris. But the signatory wasn't the father. It was a corporate entity, some family holding company, signed by its director. She read the name aloud to the empty room, because saying things out loud sometimes shook loose what reading alone couldn't.

"Delacroix-Vasseur."

Théo Vasseur.

She sat back. The sea made its patient sound against the rocks below the terrace, and somewhere down the lane a donkey complained about something, and Elise Marchand held a piece of paper that rearranged three years of story in her hands.

Because here was the thing about Théo Vasseur: he wasn't supposed to be in this story at all. He was Margaux's fiancé's business partner, the charming man in linen who'd been orbiting the family all week, all teeth and compliments. He was also, according to a conversation Elise had overheard between Camille and a friend at the beach two days ago, *the one who introduced Astrid to his bride in the first place.* Best man material. Family-adjacent. Trusted.

And his family's company had paid—six weeks after the disaster—to make sure nobody ever talked about what happened.

Elise got up and paced. She went to the window. She came back and read it again, as if the words might have reorganized themselves into innocence while she'd been standing up.

They hadn't.

Her mind, which had spent nine days carefully not thinking about the ruined wedding, now refused to think about anything else. If Théo's family had paid for silence, they'd paid for a reason. You didn't spend that kind of money to bury nothing. And if there was something to bury—if the scandal had been manufactured, or managed, or steered—then every whisper that had followed Astrid out of that church had been not just cruel but *planted*. He hadn't failed to defend himself. He'd been paid off into silence, or frightened into it, or—

Or he'd taken the deal himself. That possibility sat there too, ugly and plausible. Maybe he'd been offered a choice: quiet ruin or loud ruin, and he'd chosen quiet, because quiet let him keep the business. Elise thought of the confidentiality clause buried in her own contract—the one covering "all matters pertaining to prior engagements"—and felt something cold move through her. Habit, he'd said. Just habit.

Whose habit? His—or theirs?

She gathered the pages with hands that weren't quite steady and photographed each one, hating herself a little for how naturally her thumb found the shutter, hating herself less when she finished anyway. Then she put everything back exactly as she'd found it, aligned to the millimeter, because Margaux's mother deserved that much respect even if her archive had just detonated under Elise's fingers.

She closed the lid. She stood in the golden quiet with her cold coffee and her hot head, and she understood, with terrible clarity, that she was now carrying something heavy.

Knowledge is a burden. Nobody warns you about that when you're young; you think ignorance is the burden, that truth will set you down gently. But truth is a crate you carry, and it doesn't have handles, and you can't put it down without making noise.

She could give it to Astrid. That was the obvious move. Walk across the garden, find him wherever he was being flawless about festival logistics, and say: *I found the contracts. Your almost-brother-in-law's family bought the silence. Why didn't you tell me? Why didn't you tell anyone?*

But even as she formed the thought, she saw how it would land. He would go white. He would do the thing with his jaw that he did when he was managing panic into professionalism. He might thank her. He might shut her out. And underneath whatever he said, she'd see the same question she'd been circling since the night walk, since the rain on the terrace, since dessert: *how much of his silence was imposed, and how much was chosen?*

Because that distinction mattered. It mattered enormously. If Astrid had been silenced, he was a victim wearing a survivor's suit, and everything Elise had felt building between them—the almost-kiss on the harbor wall, the lantern night she could feel approaching like weather—was built on solid ground. But if he had *sold* his silence, if he'd signed the amicable resolution with clear eyes and kept taking clients off the corpse of his own reputation because the math worked—

Then she'd kissed a man who chose the story over the truth, and she needed to know which man she'd kissed before she kissed him again.

* * *

She found him at four o'clock, at the harbor, up a ladder.

This was, in fairness, very Astrid. The festival committee had given him a consultative role that everyone understood to be absolute authority, and he was currently directing the hanging of lantern frames along the seawall, calling adjustments in a voice pitched perfectly between authority and warmth. From below, Elise watched him reach to test a hook, sleeves rolled, forearms smudged with rust, looking less like a wedding planner than like a man building something that mattered.

Her chest did the inconvenient thing it had started doing around day five.

He saw her and his whole face changed—that specific softening he probably didn't know he did, the one that made her want to climb the ladder and ruin his professionalism personally.

"You're back early," he called down. "Did Margaux eat you alive?"

"She delegated me to the archives. Your mother-in-law-to-be's mother keeps records like the Vatican."

"Madame Joubert-Sorel," he said, descending, "could tell you what was served at every family christening since 1974, including one incident involving a flambéed pineapple that we do not discuss." He reached the ground, brushed off his hands, and smiled at her, easy and real, and Elise felt the photographs burning a hole in her pocket like coals.

Tell him now. Say it here, with the gulls screaming and the lanterns swaying, before tonight, before the lantern release, before whatever was growing between them grew another inch on top of a foundation she no longer trusted.

"I found something," she began.

His smile flickered—not away, but inward, like a candle reacting to a draft only he could feel. "In the archives?"

"In the—" She stopped. Down the quay, Théo Vasseur had appeared, linen shirt, effortless charm, laughing at something with one of the festival volunteers, and Elise watched Astrid watch him. Watched the exact moment the warmth drained out of him and the performance poured in to fill the space, seamless as stage lighting.

"Yeah?" Astrid said, when she didn't finish. His voice was still light. His eyes weren't.

"The old vendor files," she said slowly. "For the engagement party. There's a lot of useful history in there." It wasn't a lie. It was a door left ajar, and she hated how good she was getting at this—at the managed reveal, the strategic half-truth. Nine days of playing a woman in a fabricated relationship, and apparently the fabrication leaked.

"Good," he said. "That's—good. We can go through it together tomorrow, if you want. I know those files better than anyone." Something moved behind his eyes when he said it, some old sediment stirred up, and Elise realized he thought she meant party logistics. He thought she'd found fish guys and florists. He had no idea she was standing three feet away from him holding the end of his old life in her phone.

"Tomorrow," she agreed.

He touched her elbow—brief, warm, unconscious, the touch of a man who had stopped remembering he was acting—and said he had to meet the electrician, and went, and Elise stood among the half-hung lanterns feeling like the worst kind of thief: the kind who steals the truth from someone and then can't figure out how to give it back.

* * *

That evening she sat on the guest room floor with her back against the bed and laid it all out in her head like a centerpiece she couldn't get right.

Option one: tell him everything tonight. Risk: he clams up, manages her, possibly invokes the clause, definitely retreats into that terrifying competence of his. Also possible: he tells her the rest, the part the contracts couldn't show—what he knew, when he knew it, what he traded. Possible in the way that lottery tickets are possible.

Option two: say nothing, keep digging. Find out who else signed what, whether the bride left a statement, whether Camille's mentioned letters existed. Build the full picture before showing him a single card. Risk: he finds out she knew and didn't say, and the thing between them dies of exactly the disease that killed his last relationship—secrets, withheld, weaponized by silence.

Option three—and this was the one her stomach kept returning to, the one that felt less like strategy and more like fear—wait. Wait for the lantern night. He'd promised her a true thing, hadn't he? Not in words, but in the way he'd been circling toward honesty all week, the way a man circles a cold pool before committing. Give him tomorrow night. Give him the lanterns and the dark and whatever courage he was assembling. And if he gave her something real—something that matched the paper in her phone—then she'd lay the contracts on the table between them and they could carry the weight together.

And if he didn't. If tomorrow night he handed her another polished half-truth, another beautifully arranged version of events, another centerpiece designed to be admired rather than believed—

Then she'd know. And she'd decide what to do with what she knew, and he could hate her for it, but at least she'd hate herself accurately.

Outside her window, the town was preparing for the festival. Strings of bulbs went up along the crooked streets; somewhere a band rehearsed the same eight bars over and over, stubbornly, hopefully. Tomorrow night the whole island would carry its lanterns down to the water and let them go, hundreds of small lights released over the harbor, wishes and griefs and beginnings all rising together.

Elise looked at her phone, where the photographs of the settlement sat in a folder she'd labeled, with grim humor, *Party Research.*

One night, she thought. One true thing first. Then the paper.

Down the hall, faintly, she heard Astrid's door close, and the house settle around its secrets, and the sea keep up its patient work against the rocks, wearing everything down eventually, the way truth does, the way lantern-light does, the way love does, if you let it.
