## 23. The Contract She Signed

Elise had always read contracts the way other people read menus—thoroughly, with a running commentary, and a certain appetite for finding the thing nobody wanted her to find.

It was a professional deformity. Ten years of supplying flowers to weddings had taught her that the most dangerous clauses never announced themselves. They hid in Schedule B. They lurked in the definitions section, where "floral installations" somehow came to include "all decorative elements within a three-meter radius of said installations," which had once been used to bill her for a rented arch she'd never touched. She had learned, the hard and expensive way, that love's biggest days were governed by documents drafted by people who expected everything to go wrong.

So it should not have surprised her that Astrid Joubert—a man who color-coded his grief—had given her a contract.

She found it on the second morning after the lanterns, in the canvas tote she'd brought to Belle-Île, tucked beneath her spare apron and a paperback she hadn't opened. He had slipped it there sometime before dawn; she'd heard him moving through the house at six, careful as a burglar in his own home, and by seven he'd been gone—to the festival committee, he'd said, pressing a kiss to her hair at the door like a man rehearsing the gesture until it looked natural.

The contract was four pages. Clean type. His letterhead—the good one, the heavy stock, the one reserved for clients who paid five figures for centerpieces.

*Independent Engagement Services — Addendum.*

She almost laughed when she saw the title. *Engagement.* Someone had chosen that word on purpose, or worse, no one had, and it had simply arrived, fully armed, out of the vocabulary of his profession.

She made coffee first. This was important. She had learned from her mother that serious reading required serious coffee, and what she suspected she was about to read required the entire French press.

Then she sat at Astrid's kitchen table—the long oak one where they'd eaten dinner the night before, where he'd refilled her wine without being asked, where he'd told her about the lanterns and the boy he used to be—and she read.

Pages one and two were harmless. Payment terms, generous. Travel expenses, covered. A schedule of appearances through the weekend: the welcome dinner (completed), the beach gathering (completed), Margaux's engagement party (upcoming), the festival's final night. It read like a call sheet. It read like the fiction it was, dressed in the grammar of business.

Page three began with a section header in bold:

**Section 7 — Confidentiality.**

Her coffee went cold in the cup, and she didn't notice.

*The Service Provider agrees to maintain strict confidentiality regarding all matters pertaining to prior engagements of the Principal, including but not limited to: the engagement of the Principal and Théo Vasseur (dissolved June 2021), any circumstances surrounding its dissolution, any communications, agreements, settlements, or payments related thereto, and any information acquired in the course of providing services hereunder that bears upon the foregoing.*

She read it twice. Then a third time, because the third time is when you stop looking for loopholes and start looking at yourself.

*All matters pertaining to prior engagements.*

Not "the wedding." Not even "what happened." *All matters.* The clause was wide enough to drive a hearse through, and it had been built that way deliberately—he was a planner, precision was his religion, and this document bore every fingerprint of his care. He had not dashed this off. He had drafted it, revised it, printed it on good paper, and slid it into her bag while she slept.

While she slept. After the lanterns. After he told her he'd stopped believing he deserved a wedding, and she had kissed him like an answer, and he had kissed her back like a man coming up for air after years underwater.

And somewhere in the hours between that kiss and dawn, he had sat down at his desk and added fine print.

Elise set the page down very carefully, the way you set down something that might still be dangerous.

There was a signature line. Of course there was. And beside it, in his tidy hand, a note—an actual handwritten note, warm as a fresh croissant:

*For form's sake only—you've already earned every cent. But my lawyer insists, and I sleep better knowing you're protected too. —A.*

*You're protected too.*

She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes and laughed, one short, ugly syllable that startled a gull off the windowsill outside.

Protected. That was the word for it. Protected the way witnesses were protected. Protected the way evidence was protected. She thought of the contracts she'd found in Margaux's planning files—the vendor list from 2021, the payment from the Vasseur family firm, the money that had moved like a curtain being drawn across a stage. She had matched the signatures last night, sitting cross-legged on her bed, her heart going like a trapped bird. Théo's family had paid to bury the truth. Astrid had absorbed the blame in silence. She knew now. She held the whole rotten architecture of it in her head.

And he had written a clause to make sure she could never say it out loud.

No—that wasn't quite fair, and fairness mattered, because she was furious and fury always overreached. The clause didn't mention Théo's payments. It mentioned *prior engagements*, plural, general, a net thrown wide enough to catch whatever she might drag up from the wreck. Maybe he didn't know what she'd found. Maybe the timing was coincidence, the paranoid habit of a man whose career had been built on the ruins of one bad weekend.

But that was exactly the problem, wasn't it. That was the whole cathedral of the problem.

He hadn't asked her what she knew. He hadn't come to her in the morning light and said, *I'm frightened, I've spent three years managing this story, help me put it down.* He had managed her instead. He had kissed her honest on the harbor wall and then, in the dark, quietly, competently, he had contracted her silence.

Habit, he would say, if she confronted him. Habit, not malice. She could already hear it, delivered in that low reasonable voice, with that small helpless shrug that made strangers want to forgive him things. And maybe it was true. Maybe some part of him genuinely believed the note, believed he was protecting her, believed that paper could hold a door shut against a flood.

But Elise Marchand had spent her life around arrangements, and she knew what they meant. You could tell everything about a person from what they reached for when they were afraid. Some people reached for each other. Astrid Joubert reached for a pen.

* * *

She did not cry. She wanted to note this for the record, later, whenever the record of this weekend got written up: she did not cry. She got angry instead, which was cleaner, and anger kept your hands steady, and her hands needed to be steady because she had a job to do.

Because here was the thing—the thing that made her laugh again, bitterly, into her cooling coffee. She was still under contract. Not his addendum, which she had never signed, thank God, thank every skeptical bone in her body—but the real one, the one with Margaux. The engagement party was tomorrow night. Two hundred guests, a marquee on the clifftop meadow, peonies ordered from a grower in Nantes who would sooner die than deliver late. Elise had promised Margaux a celebration that would make the whole island forget its own name. She had promised it with flowers, which was the only language she trusted completely, because flowers never pretended to be anything but what they were: beautiful, temporary, and honest about wilting.

So she packed her kit. She braided her hair back. She looked at herself in the bathroom mirror—Astrid's bathroom mirror, with his absurdly folded towels—and she practiced a face that revealed nothing, and she was disturbed by how quickly she found it.

On her way out, she passed the little desk in the hall. His desk. And there, squared to the edge with geometric precision, sat a leather box she'd noticed before and never touched: dark green, worn soft at the corners, with a brass clasp gone dull with handling.

The memory box. Camille had mentioned it once, half-joking, half-warning: *He files everything, our Astrid. Even the things that broke him. Especially those.*

She hadn't planned to open it. She told herself that afterward, and it was even mostly true. But the clasp was unlatched—it was always unlatched, Camille had said, a detail that had seemed sentimental at the time and now seemed like a wound left deliberately unbandaged—and the lid lifted under her fingers like it had been waiting three years for someone brave enough or stupid enough.

Photographs. The ceremony, mid-collapse. Guests standing in rows turned toward the aisle like sunflowers tracking a disaster. A chair with a ribbon on it, empty. Programs scattered on flagstones, the ink smudged by feet that had trampled them leaving. And underneath the photographs, in a folder labeled in his handwriting—*Correspondence, 2021*—a thin stack of letters.

She read the top one. It was from the Vasseur family's lawyer. It was polite the way knives are polished. It offered, in essence, a choice: silence and a settlement, or ruin and a lawsuit, and it made clear—with the gentle menace of expensive stationery—which option a sensible man would choose.

The date on the letter was eleven days after the wedding.

Eleven days. He had been offered the deal before the gossip even finished setting. He had taken it before anyone could blame him for hesitating. And he had lived inside that bargain ever since, so long, so carefully, that when a woman finally kissed him under floating lanterns and meant it, his first instinct in the dark was to draft her a confidentiality agreement.

Elise closed the box. Latched it—no, she thought, and unlatched it again, because it had been left open for three years and she wasn't going to be the one to seal it.

Then she walked down to the harbor to build Margaux's kingdom, carrying the contracts in her bag and the letters in her memory, and feeling—for the first time since the lanterns—not like a woman falling in love, but like a woman holding something fragile in one hand and a receipt in the other, trying to decide which one she'd been handed.

* * *

The clifftop meadow was chaos by noon, glorious chaos, the kind Elise usually loved. Margaux stood in the middle of it in white linen, directing movers like a weather system, and when she spotted Elise she lit up with genuine, uncomplicated delight, and Elise felt the twist of it—because Margaux was not the enemy. Margaux was just a girl in love who happened to share blood with a family that ran on appearances like an engine runs on fuel.

"You're a miracle," Margaux declared, kissing both her cheeks. "The arch. Tell me about the arch."

"The arch is happening. The peonies are happening. Everything is happening." Elise handed her the sample ribbon. "Tell me about your fiancé's business partner. Is he coming tomorrow?"

Margaux's smile flickered—just a hairline crack, there and gone. "Théo? He confirmed this morning. Julien says he's essential to the hotel project." She busied herself with the ribbon, not meeting Elise's eyes. "Astrid knows. Camille told him yesterday."

"Did he," Elise said, in a voice with nothing in it.

"He's being strange about it. Calm-strange. You know how he gets—" Margaux caught herself, glanced up, and something moved behind her eyes, quick and searching. "Elise. Are you all right? You've got your working face on."

"This is my face."

"No." Margaux tilted her head, and for a moment she looked startlingly like Solange. "Your working face is bossy. This is something else. This is the face women wear when a man has done something and hasn't figured out yet that we know."

Elise held her gaze for a long moment. The wind came up off the water and bent the grass flat in silver waves.

"Margaux," she said, "when you plan a party—who do you plan it for?"

"What kind of question—"

"Indulge me. I'm a professional."

Margaux considered, twisting the ribbon around her finger until the fingertip went pink. "For the couple," she said slowly. "Obviously. But—" and here she frowned, honest despite herself, "—half the time I think you're really planning it for whoever's watching. For the version of the day that everyone keeps afterward. The story."

"The story," Elise repeated. "Yes. And what happens if the story and the truth don't match?"

Margaux laughed, uncertain, and let the ribbon spring free. "Then someone eventually tells the truth at the worst possible moment, and it's a disaster, and ten years later everyone admits it was the best thing that ever happened to that family." She paused. "Why do you ask?"

"No reason," said Elise, and went to yell at a mover about the arch.

* * *

She worked until the light went long and gold. She wired stems and argued with the tent company and repositioned the same bucket of sweet peas four times, and all the while the clause sat in her mind like a stone in a shoe.

By dusk the meadow was transformed—she had to admit it, grudgingly, professionally: it was beautiful. The arch stood against the sea like a door cut into the sky. Lanterns waited in crates for tomorrow night. It was, by any measure, the best work she'd done in a year, built for people she'd come to like, funded by a lie she'd helped perform.

Astrid found her there at sunset. She watched him come across the grass with two paper cones of roasted chestnuts from the festival stalls, and she watched her own heart do the unforgivable thing, the hopeful thing, lifting at the sight of him before slamming back down against the facts.

"There you are." He handed her a cone. His smile was tired and real and it made her want to shake him. "I've been looking everywhere. The committee meeting ran long—they want me to speak at the final night. An actual speech. Can you imagine."

"I can imagine," Elise said.

"They want the redemption story, of course. The local boy who rebuilt himself." He shook his head, laughing softly, and broke a chestnut from its shell. "I keep thinking I should write something true for once, and then I remember I don't entirely remember how."

The words hung there between them, an opening, a door left ajar. Say it, she thought. Ask me what I know. Tell me about the letter dated eleven days after. Tear up the clause and stand in the wreckage with me like a man instead of a manager.

Instead he smiled and touched her elbow, gentle, proprietary, and said, "Come on. Camille's opening a bottle she's been saving, and my mother wants to interrogate you about your childhood. One more performance before the big show."

And Elise Marchand took the chestnuts, and smiled her new practiced smile, and walked beside him across the meadow he had built on top of his own grave—while in her tote bag, unsigned and unread by him, four pages of good heavy paper waited like a verdict, and in her head, a deadline quietly began to run.
