## 25. Théo's Offer

Astrid had a system for difficult conversations. He'd developed it over years of managing brides whose mothers had opinions about peonies and grooms who discovered, three days before the ceremony, that they were terrified. The system was simple: control the room, control the timing, control the exits.

Théo Vasseur took all three away from him in under a minute.

It happened on the cliff path above Port Donnant, the morning after Margaux's engagement party, when Astrid had escaped the house with the flimsy excuse of checking on the festival's floral installations. He'd been crouched at the base of an archway he'd designed—white stock and sea holly, meant to look as though the wind itself had arranged it—when a shadow fell across his work.

"You always did fuss," Théo said. "Even at school. You'd re-straighten a crooked picture frame until someone made you stop."

Astrid rose slowly, brushing sand from his knees, and turned around with the expression he used for difficult mothers-in-law: pleasant, immovable, faintly bored.

"Théo." He let the name sit there like a stone dropped into still water. "I thought you'd have caught the early ferry back to Nantes."

"I extended my stay. Business opportunities keep presenting themselves on this island." Théo smiled, and it was the same smile Astrid remembered from a decade of dinners and holidays and one catastrophic wedding day—the smile of a man who had never once needed anything from anyone and wanted everyone to know it. He was tanned, silvering elegantly at the temples, dressed in linen that probably cost more than Astrid's first car. "Walk with me."

"I'm working."

"It's flowers, Astrid. They'll survive five minutes without your supervision."

*They almost didn't survive yours*, Astrid thought, and was surprised by how much venom the thought carried after all these years. He should have walked away. Every instinct honed by three years of reputation management told him to walk away, to be seen nowhere alone with this man, to leave no photograph, no witness, no ambiguity.

Instead he heard himself say, "Five minutes," and fell into step beside him on the path.

The sea below was doing its morning business—glittering, indifferent. Gulls argued over something on the rocks. It was, Astrid reflected bitterly, exactly the kind of beautiful day that made people believe promises.

***

For the first two hundred meters, Théo said nothing, which was its own kind of tactic. Astrid knew the technique; he'd used it himself. Silence made people fill the space, and whatever they filled it with was usually worse than what they'd planned to say.

He refused to fill it. He looked at the ocean and counted waves.

Finally Théo laughed—a short, genuine-sounding laugh that was somehow more insulting than any of his smiles. "You've gotten good at this. The waiting game. You used to crack in thirty seconds."

"People change."

"Some do." Théo stopped walking. Below them, the beach curved white and empty, too early in the season for tourists. He turned to face Astrid fully, and for a moment—just a moment—the charm dropped and Astrid saw underneath it: something tired, something calculating, something that might once have been afraid. "Here's my situation. My firm is bidding on the renovation of the Hôtel du Phare. Big project. The investors are old-fashioned; they like their vendors vetted, their partners stable. And there's a name that keeps coming up in those meetings. Yours."

"Flattery," Astrid said. "You'll have to try harder than that."

"It's not flattery, it's leverage, and we both know the difference." Théo's voice stayed light, conversational, the voice of a man discussing the weather. "Here's *my* offer. A joint statement. Short, dignified. Something along the lines of: recent years have brought clarity, both parties regret the pain of that summer, certain misunderstandings have since been resolved amicably. We release it together, quietly, before the festival. And in exchange, I make one phone call, and Joubert Events becomes the preferred planner for every event the Hôtel du Phare hosts for the next decade. Weddings, conferences, the works. That's seven figures over the contract's life, minimum. Maybe eight."

The wind came up the cliff and pushed against Astrid's chest like a hand.

"A joint statement," he repeated.

"'Misunderstandings resolved amicably.'" Théo spread his hands, presenting the phrase like a sommelier presenting a vintage. "Nobody has to admit anything. Nobody has to retract anything. The story simply... settles. Like sediment. In a few years nobody will remember there was ever a question at all."

"And if I don't sign?"

Théo's smile returned, softer now, almost kind, which was the cruelest thing he'd done yet. "Then nothing happens. Of course nothing happens. I'm not a villain in a novel, Astrid—I don't threaten people on clifftops." He resumed walking, hands clasped behind his back, strolling like a tourist. "But contracts go to people whose stories are simple. Investors are simple creatures, despite their money. And your story, currently, has a loose thread in it. A very old loose thread. Loose threads have a way of unraveling at inconvenient moments. I'd hate to see that happen during your festival week, with half the town watching and your mother beaming in the front row."

There it was. Not a threat—a weather forecast. *Rain expected. Bring an umbrella or drown.*

Astrid stopped walking.

For three years he had built a fortress out of competence. Every flawless wedding, every grateful client, every five-star review had been another stone in the wall, and inside the wall he had kept the truth of that June—the truth that Théo had walked out mid-ceremony not because of cold feet but because of a plan, a deliberate, premeditated humiliation engineered by two families who had decided Astrid was the convenient villain, and that Astrid had *let them*, because fighting would have cost him everything and silence cost only himself.

He had signed nothing then. That was the bitter joke of it. There had been no paper, no clause, no formal agreement—just a meeting in a lawyer's office where Théo's father had slid a settlement across the desk and said, gently, *the industry is small, Astrid, and memories are long,* and twenty-six-year-old Astrid, bankrupt in spirit if not yet in fact, had nodded.

Now here was paper. Here was a pen, metaphorically speaking, being held out on a clifftop with the sea glittering below like a witness.

"No," Astrid said.

Théo turned. For the first time, he looked genuinely surprised. "No?"

"No. I'm not signing your statement. I'm not taking your contract. And I'm certainly not going to stand here while you dress up blackmail in linen and call it generosity." His voice was steady. His hands, he noticed distantly, were not. "Was that the whole pitch? Because I have flowers to fuss over."

Théo studied him for a long moment, and something shifted in his face—not anger, worse than anger: recalculation. The look of a man who had budgeted for a predictable opponent and received an unpredictable one.

"She's put ideas in your head," he said. "The florist. I hear she's got quite the temper. Quite the memory, too. She was there, you know. At the wedding. Working the flowers." He smiled again. "Small industry."

Every hair on Astrid's arms stood up. "Leave her out of this."

"There it is." Théo pointed at him, delighted, as though Astrid were a symptom he'd finally diagnosed. "That's the tell. Three years of marble composure and it takes one florist to crack it." He stepped closer, lowered his voice, and now the kindness was gone entirely, replaced by something flat and patient. "Think about it properly, Astrid. Not with your heart—with your ledger. If the old story stands, you lose a hotel contract. Annoying. Survivable. But if the old story *changes*—if someone starts asking why you never defended yourself, what you knew, when you knew it, what you agreed to in that lawyer's office—then it isn't just the contract. It's the festival speech they've asked you to give. It's your mother's face. It's your sister. It's every client who ever booked you because you were the man who survived a scandal with grace. Grace, Astrid, is only graceful as long as nobody looks underneath it."

The words landed exactly where he knew they would land, because they were Astrid's own words, the ones he said to himself at three in the morning, wearing Théo's voice now like a borrowed coat.

"I'll give you until the end of the festival," Théo went on, stepping back, restoring the charm like a mask sliding home. "After that, the investors' due diligence gets thorough. These things leak, you understand. Paperwork has a way of surfacing." He began to walk back down the path toward town, then paused, glancing over his shoulder. "Oh—and tell Elise her stock flowers were always better than her attitude. She'd remember me saying that."

And he was gone, down the switchback path, linen catching the wind, leaving Astrid alone on the cliff with the archway of white stock and sea holly and the enormous, ringing silence of a future that had just changed shape.

***

Astrid did not go back to the house.

He walked instead to the far side of the point, past the lighthouse, to a bench he'd found years ago on childhood summers—a bench facing open water, no view of the port, no view of anything except the Atlantic doing its eternal, unbothered work. He sat down and did the arithmetic.

Seven figures. A decade of security. The company, safe. His employees—there were eleven of them now, eleven people whose livelihoods he'd built out of the wreckage—all safe. His mother, spared. The story, settled like sediment.

All it would cost was a signature and the last scrap of a soul he'd been pretending was intact.

*Grace is only graceful as long as nobody looks underneath it.*

"That's not true," he said aloud, to the ocean, and the ocean declined to argue.

Because here was the thing Théo didn't know—or maybe did know, and was counting on anyway: three days ago, Astrid had kissed Elise Marchand under festival lanterns and meant it. Two nights ago he had woken beside her and felt terror instead of joy, and instead of telling her the truth he had reached for a confidentiality clause like a man reaching for a life raft made of the ship's own planks. She had found it. She had confronted him. She had walked out, and he had deserved it, and he had spent yesterday drafting two versions of a festival speech—one safe, one true—unable to burn either.

If he signed Théo's statement now, he wouldn't merely be protecting the past. He'd be choosing it. Choosing the sediment, the settled story, the version of himself that was survivable and false. And Elise—who had stormed into his studio over an unpaid invoice and stayed to dismantle him piece by piece, who knew more than she'd said and had given him a deadline made of mercy—Elise would be standing in the crowd at the festival watching him shake hands with the man who ruined him, smiling, complicit, forever.

*Habit is character.* Her voice, from the low-tide confrontation, still ringing.

His phone buzzed. He looked at it with the dull expectation of a man expecting bad news, and it was—of a sort. A message from Camille:

*Saw Théo's boat still in the harbor. If he talked to you, I want to know what he said. Tonight. And Astrid—before you decide anything, come see me. There are things I've been keeping for you. Three years is long enough.*

Astrid stared at the screen. *Things I've been keeping for you.* Camille, who had covered for him since they were children, who had lied to their mother about his love life, his finances, his health, his happiness—Camille had been keeping something, all this time, and the message read less like a warning than like a door being unlocked.

Below the lighthouse, the tide was turning. He could feel it in the air, that faint shift in the smell of the water, the sea beginning its slow pull toward withdrawal, exposing everything the water had hidden.

He typed back: *Tonight. After dinner.*

Then he put the phone away, stood up, and walked back toward the house where his family was waiting, carrying in his pocket the weight of an unsigned offer and the growing, terrifying suspicion that the choice Théo thought he was offering had already been made—somewhere between a lantern and a broken centerpiece, somewhere between a lie and a woman who refused to accept one.

He just had to survive the telling.
