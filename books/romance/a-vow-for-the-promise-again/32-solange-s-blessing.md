## 32. Solange's Blessing

The morning after Margaux's party, Astrid woke to a house that smelled of coffee and judgment.

His mother had left a note on the kitchen table in her looping hand — *Your grandmother wants to see you. The garden house. Before lunch. Wear something that isn't black; you look like a funeral director.* — and beneath it, as if to soften the summons, a plate of madeleines wrapped in a cloth. He ate two standing up, which was how he'd eaten everything since he was nine years old and learned that sitting down meant someone might ask you a question.

The garden house sat at the end of his aunt's property, past the fig trees, a glass-and-weatherboard structure Solange had claimed as her own territory decades ago and defended with the quiet ferocity of a small nation. Inside it smelled of rosemary and old paper. She was at the potting bench when he arrived, repotting something with more confidence than tenderness, and she did not look up when he came in.

"You broke your own centerpiece," she said.

"Good morning to you too, Grand-mère."

"Half of it. You snapped the arch of white ranunculus like a man killing a snake." She pressed soil down around a root ball with both thumbs. "Margaux is telling everyone a guest did it. Camille told her the truth. Now Margaux isn't speaking to either of you, which means she'll be speaking again by Thursday, because Margaux cannot sustain silence the way other people sustain breathing."

Astrid lowered himself onto the wicker chair by the window, the one with the cushion embroidered with faded poppies that had been there since before he could read. "I assume Mother told you why you wanted to see me."

"I want to see you." She finally turned, wiping her hands on her apron, and looked at him the way she looked at everything — directly, without apology, as though the world were a slightly disappointing arrangement she intended to fix. "Sit properly. You're folding yourself up like a letter someone's about to throw away."

He straightened. It was absurd, at thirty-four, how completely she still owned his spine.

For a while she let the silence do its work. She moved along the bench, watering, deadheading, murmuring to plants in the tone she used for people she found tiresome but forgivable. Outside, the light came through the glass roof in long pale bars, and somewhere beyond the fig trees the sea kept up its patient argument with the rocks.

"Do you know," she said at last, without turning around, "the night of Théo's rehearsal dinner — not yours, theirs, the family dinner his parents threw — I watched you count the place settings three times. Not because you doubted the caterer. Because counting calms you. Your grandfather did the same thing with his tools." She set down the watering can. "You were twenty-nine years old and already arranging other people's happiness like it was a load-bearing wall."

"Grand-mère—"

"And then a year later my grandson stood at an altar in front of two hundred people and was left there like a coat on a hook." She said it plainly, no cruelty in it, only the flat accuracy she reserved for weather and truth. "And I have spent three years watching what you did next."

He looked at his hands. They were steady. He'd trained them to be.

"I built the business," he said. "I built it out of nothing, out of the worst day of my life, and I built it honestly. Every wedding since—"

"Yes, yes, every wedding immaculate, every client weeping with gratitude, every invoice paid on time." She waved this away like smoke. "I'm not talking about the weddings, Astrid. I'm talking about you."

* * *

She crossed the room then, slowly, one hand trailing along the shelf of terracotta pots, and sat in the chair opposite him. Up close she smelled of rosemary and the lavender soap she had used since 1974, and her eyes — the same grey-blue as his mother's, as Camille's, as his own in certain lights — held him without effort.

"I have known for years," she said.

The words landed in the room and just stayed there, ringing faintly, like a fork dropped on tile.

"Known—" He heard his own voice come out carefully level, the event-planner's voice, the voice for handling drunk uncles and collapsing tents. "Known what, exactly?"

"That you knew." She folded her hands in her lap. "That after the wedding, when Théo's family came to you with their lawyers and their settlement and their story — the story where poor Théo simply panicked, where the accusations against you were regrettable misunderstandings, where everyone would be spared if only you signed — you didn't sign because you believed it. You signed because you were drowning and they were the only boat."

The garden house was very quiet. A bee bumped once against the glass roof and moved on.

"How long have you known?" he asked.

"Since Camille was twenty-six and got drunk on Pernod at your cousin's christening and cried in my kitchen." Solange's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "Camille has never once managed to keep a secret from me. She doesn't even try anymore. She just arrives, pours, and confesses. It's practically liturgical."

"So you've known for six years." He did the arithmetic numbly. "You've known I took their money. That I let them bury whatever really happened. That I let everyone believe—" He stopped, because finishing the sentence aloud, to her face, was a different act than writing it in a speech he might burn.

"That you let everyone believe the girl who vanished had good reason to run," Solange finished for him, merciless and gentle at once. "That you let them believe the rumors about you were at least half true, because a half-true scandal dies faster than an investigated one. Yes. I've known all of it."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

"Because you weren't ready to hear it." She leaned forward. "Because a man who has just been gutted in public does not need his grandmother telling him he chose wrong. He needs to survive first. So I waited. That's most of love, Astrid — waiting longer than you want to, saying less than you know, and keeping the door unlocked anyway."

He found that his eyes were burning and hated it, and blinked it back, and she pretended not to notice, which was its own mercy.

"The family image," she went on, "has been our religion for three generations. Your great-grandfather built the vineyard name into something people whispered respectfully. Your grandfather maintained it. Your mother polishes it every morning like silver. And when your wedding collapsed, do you know what the family cared about? Not you. Not whether you were all right, alone in that rented house with two hundred unanswered messages. Whether the *name* would recover." Her jaw tightened, a small movement, quickly mastered. "I watched them do it. I watched them tell you, in a hundred small ways, that the important thing now was discretion. And I watched you obey, because you were twenty-nine and bleeding and obedience felt like shelter."

"It wasn't only that," Astrid said quietly. "They offered me the contract. The hotel group. Théo's family steered it to me. It was the account that made everything else possible. If I'd fought them, if I'd told the truth about what Théo did, I'd have lost it, and I'd have lost the referrals, and I'd probably have lost—" He gestured vaguely at the garden, the island, the whole careful architecture of his life. "All of this."

"All of this," Solange repeated. She looked around the glass house with its orderly pots and its disciplined green things. "Yes. Look what you bought. A beautiful cage with excellent lighting."

Despite himself — despite the ache in his chest — Astrid laughed. It came out cracked, but it came.

"There it is," she said, satisfied. "There's the boy who used to laugh before he learned to schedule it."

* * *

She rose then, and went to the old walnut cabinet in the corner — the one he had never once seen open, that he had privately assumed held fertilizer and shame — and took from it a flat box wrapped in soft leather gone dark with age. She carried it back and set it on his knee.

"Open it."

Inside, on a bed of yellowed cotton, lay his grandfather's watch. Henri Joubert's watch — the heavy silver one Astrid remembered from childhood, the one that had lived on the wrist that lifted him onto tractors and pointed at storms coming in off the water. The face was scratched. The band had been repaired twice, badly, with obvious love.

"He stopped wearing it the last winter," Solange said. "Said it ticked too loudly. I think what he meant was that he could hear time running out, and he preferred not to be reminded." She sat back down beside him, closer now, her shoulder almost touching his. "When he died, I put it away. Everyone assumed I'd give it to your mother, or to Julien as the eldest. But your grandfather left instructions. He said: give it to whichever grandchild needs to be told that time is not a savings account."

Astrid stared down at it. "What does that mean?"

"It means you cannot hoard it, child. You've been hoarding yourself for six years. Saving up the truth for some future moment when it will be safe, when it will cost nothing, when everyone will applaud." She shook her head. "That moment does not exist. It has never existed for anyone. Truth is always expensive. The only question is whether you pay it while you can still choose the terms — or wait until someone else sets the price."

He thought of Elise. Of course he thought of Elise; he thought of her the way sailors think of lighthouses, constantly and against their better judgment. He thought of her walking out at low tide with her chin up and her eyes wet and furious. He thought of the contracts she must have seen by now, the clause he'd slipped in like a coward's signature, the way she had looked at him across the wreckage of his own centerpiece last night and said nothing, and how her silence had been worse than any accusation.

"She knows," he said. His voice came out rough. "Or she's close to knowing. About the settlement. About what I agreed to keep quiet."

"Mm." Solange did not sound surprised. "The Marchand girl. She has hands like someone who works for a living and eyes like someone who misses nothing. What did you do to her?"

"What I do to everything. I managed her." He laughed again, without humor. "I put her under contract, Grand-mère. I put a confidentiality clause on a woman who was starting to kiss me back."

Solange considered this with the gravity she gave to failing crops. "Then you have two problems," she said. "One of them is fixable today. The other will take as long as it takes, and groveling, and probably flowers — though not ones you grew yourself, that would be cheating."

"Thank you for the strategic counsel."

"I'm full of it. Ask anyone." But her hand came over his then, dry and strong and warm, and covered the watch box, and pressed it down against his knee. "Take it. Wear it. And when you stand up in front of this town tomorrow night, I want you to feel it ticking. Not as a threat. As permission."

"Grand-mère, if I say what actually happened — if I name Théo, if I admit I took their money and stayed silent — the hotel group is finished. Half my clients will walk. Margaux will—" He stopped. "Mother will be humiliated in front of everyone she's spent her life impressing."

"Your mother," Solange said, "is a woman who irons her pillowcases. She will survive a scandal. What she would not survive — what none of us would survive — is watching you spend another decade shrinking yourself to fit inside the family crest." She fixed him with those grey-blue eyes. "The image was never worth you, Astrid. Do you hear me? Not the name, not the vineyard, not the reputation, not one single polished dinner party. We would trade all of it for one honest grandson and consider ourselves robbers getting away with something."

Something in his chest that had been clenched for six years — some muscle he hadn't known he was holding — let go all at once, and it hurt, the way blood hurts returning to a limb grown numb.

"I don't know if I can do it," he said. It was the truest thing he'd said in years, maybe ever. "I've written the speech. Both versions. I've been carrying them around like—" He huffed. "Like inventory."

"Of course you can't do it." Solange patted his knee briskly and stood, brushing soil from her apron. "Nobody can do it. You do it anyway, shaking, with your voice cracking, and afterward you'll vomit behind the bandstand, and then you'll be free. That's how courage works. It's not a feeling, it's a decision with terrible customer service."

He laughed — really laughed this time, helpless, wiping his eyes with the heel of his hand — and she allowed herself one brief, triumphant smile before resuming her sternness.

"Now get up. It's nearly lunch, and if you're late your mother will interpret it as a sign of moral collapse." She paused at the potting bench, her back to him, and added, quieter: "And Astrid. The Marchand girl. When you find her — and you will find her, men like you always eventually stop running toward the wrong thing and trip over the right one — don't manage her. Don't brief her. Don't hand her a folder." She glanced back over her shoulder. "Just tell her the truth and then be quiet and let her decide. It's terrifying. It's the only thing that's ever worked."

* * *

He walked back through the fig trees with the watch in his pocket, its weight swinging against his thigh with every step like a small persistent heartbeat. At the edge of the garden he stopped, took it out, and fastened it around his wrist. Too big — his grandfather had wrists like mooring posts — but it sat there, ticking, indifferent and faithful.

His phone buzzed. Camille.

*Where are you? Also don't panic. Also panic a little. A journalist named Perrin just called the house asking for comment on 'documents relating to the Marchant-Vasseur wedding.' She says she's publishing tomorrow.*

Astrid stood very still among the fig trees, the watch ticking against his wrist bone, and watched the sea glitter past the hedge like a lie told beautifully.

Tomorrow. Not next week. Not someday safe.

Tomorrow.

He typed back: *Tell Mother I'm coming. And Camille — find Elise.*
