## 38. The Answer She Waited For

Three days after the festival, Astrid Joubert woke at five in the morning out of habit, lay in bed listening to the gulls argue over the harbor, and discovered that for the first time in three years he had nothing to manage.

No dossier to revise. No speech to draft—both versions had been delivered, in a sense: the safe one to no one, the true one to everyone. No Théo to brace against; the man had left the island on the morning ferry with his jaw set like a man chewing glass, and the hotel contract had died so quietly that Astrid only learned of it from a two-line email that read, in its entirety, *Per our conversation, we are withdrawing interest. We wish you well.* The *we* was doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Clients had cancelled. Four of them, then a fifth. Margaux still wasn't speaking to him, though Camille reported—over a phone call during which she ate an entire baguette audibly, as punctuation—that Margaux had cried at the wedding announcement and therefore could not maintain righteous fury indefinitely. Solange had sent him a single text: *Proud of you. Come eat something.*

And Elise Marchand had not come to see him.

He had counted the days the way he used to count RSVPs. Three days. He knew where her shop was; he'd walked past it twice, seen her through the window arranging ranunculus with the focused violence of a woman strangling an old enemy, and had kept walking, because she deserved the right to come to him or not, and because his legs had gone strange and unreliable both times.

So when the knock came on the fourth morning—at seven-forty, which meant she'd taken the first ferry over from the mainland, which meant she'd decided this before dawn, which meant something—he opened the door with his heart already halfway up his throat.

She was holding a paper bag that smelled like croissants and a small pot with something green and defiant growing in it.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been told honesty is my new brand."

"It suits you badly. You were prettier when you lied." She pushed past him into the kitchen, set down the bag, and put the pot on his windowsill. "Basil. It needs sun and neglect. I thought you could use a plant whose care instructions you can actually follow."

"Elise."

"Don't," she said, holding up a hand. "Not yet. I came here with a structure, Astrid, and I'm going to follow it, because one of us in this relationship should know how to run an event."

The word *relationship* landed between them like a dropped glass. Neither of them bent to pick it up.

She took the croissants out of the bag, arranged them on a plate with unnecessary precision, poured two coffees from the pot he'd made, and sat down at his kitchen table as though she'd been sitting there for years. He sat across from her. His hands were not steady. He wrapped them around the cup and pretended they were.

"Three days ago," she said, "you stood up in front of your entire family and half the town and told the truth about the worst thing that ever happened to you. And it cost you. I watched the cancellations come in—I have the vendor group chat, Astrid, do you understand what those people say about you now? Half of them are horrified. A quarter of them are gleeful. And the rest—" she paused, and something moved across her face that looked suspiciously like pride—"the rest are quietly rethinking what kind of man they want planning their weddings."

"I didn't do it for the vendors."

"No. You did it because Camille gave you an ultimatum and Solange gave you a watch and I gave you nothing, which was the point." She sipped her coffee. "I want to be very clear about why I'm here, because I am not here to absolve you. Absolution is not mine to give. What you did—taking the money, taking the silence, letting everyone believe you were the villain rather than fight—you did that to yourself, and you'll be carrying it for a while, and I'm not going to pretend a nice speech fixed it."

"Okay," Astrid said quietly.

"But." She set down the cup. "I spent three days deciding whether the man who did that is someone I can love, and I kept coming back to the same problem, which is that I was asking the wrong question. Because the man who did that isn't standing in front of me. He's three years and one catastrophic speech behind you. So I'm not going to ask about him."

Astrid's chest felt tight in a way that had nothing to do with dread, or maybe everything to do with it; he'd forgotten they were neighbors, dread and hope.

"Then what are you going to ask?"

She reached into her jacket pocket and pulled out a folded piece of paper, smoothed it flat on the table, and turned it toward him. On it, in her blunt handwriting, was a single line:

*Who do you want to be now?*

"My second question," she said. "I banked it in week one. I've been saving it for the moment you could actually answer it, and I almost wasted it on the terrace during the rain, and I'm glad I didn't, because back then you'd have given me a beautiful answer and it would have been furniture." She slid the paper across the table until it touched his fingers. "So. Take your time. But I need the plain version. Not the version you'd put in a brochure."

Astrid looked at the question for a long time.

Outside, the harbor did its morning business—the fish boat coming in, the gulls conducting their endless litigation, someone's radio playing old French pop through an open window. The basil sat on the windowsill, needing sun and neglect.

"The honest answer," he said finally, "is that I don't entirely know yet. And I think that has to count as an answer, because for three years I knew exactly who I was supposed to be. I was the man who never failed. I was the planner whose weddings went off without a hitch, whose reputation was spotless, whose life was a series of flawless events attended by people who paid him well. That man had a spreadsheet for everything, including his own feelings." He turned the paper over, then back. "That man is the one who wrote a confidentiality clause into your contract while he was falling in love with you. I keep trying to figure out how he did that—how he could kiss you under lanterns and then, the next morning, protect himself from you like you were a liability. And I think the answer is that protecting himself was all he knew how to be. Survival was the whole architecture. Take that away and there wasn't much floor underneath."

"And now?"

"Now the floor's gone too. I burned it publicly. Which means I get to find out what's underneath, and I genuinely don't know." He looked up at her. "But I know some things. I know I want to plan weddings again—not as redemption, not as performance, but because somewhere under all of it there's still the idiot who was twenty-four and believed a wedding was the most hopeful thing a community could build together, and I'd like to meet him again. I know I want to be someone my sister doesn't have to cover for. I know I want to be someone Grand-mère can be proud of without having to pretend. And I know—" his voice caught, and he let it, because hiding the catch would just be more management, wouldn't it, and he was so tired of managing—"I know that when you walked through that door this morning, the first feeling I had in three days that wasn't dread was this. Whatever this is. You, holding croissants, insulting my face."

"Your face is fine," Elise said gruffly. "It's the rest of you that needed work."

"That's the other thing I want to be. Someone you can insult honestly. Someone who hears it." He pushed the paper back toward her. "That's the plain version. It's not finished. I don't think it will be finished for a long time. But it's true, every word, and I checked."

Elise looked at him for a moment longer than was comfortable, which he suspected was deliberate. Then she picked up the paper, folded it, and put it back in her pocket.

"Good," she said. "That'll do for a first draft."

* * *

They ate the croissants. Somewhere in the middle of the second one, Astrid realized his shoulders had come down from around his ears for the first time in four days, and that Elise had noticed, and that she was saying nothing about it, which was its own kind of tenderness.

"There's a second item on my agenda," she said, brushing flakes from her hands. "Item two of the structure."

"I'm listening."

"I've been thinking about contracts." She said it lightly, but her eyes weren't light at all. "Specifically about the fact that I signed one. With a confidentiality clause covering—and I quote—'all matters pertaining to prior engagements.' Which, it turns out, included the truth about Théo's family paying to bury evidence, which I found before you confessed, which I sat on, which—" she exhaled—"which I also managed, in my way. We're not so different, you and I. You file things in dossiers. I file things in flower coolers. Either way, we both sat on the truth because we thought we were protecting someone."

"You found the contracts before I confessed?" Astrid stared at her. "Elise, you carried that for—"

"A week. Don't make it noble, it was awful, I got cramps from clenching my jaw." She waved him off. "The point is: I'm not here to litigate the clause. You tore it up, I saw you burn it, it was very dramatic, the fire pit appreciated it. The point is that if we do this—if there's an *us*, and I'm fairly sure there's an us, because I took a ferry at six in the morning and I hate mornings—we are not doing it on paper. No contract. No terms. No clause. If you ever feel the need to legally protect yourself from me again, Astrid, don't lawyer up. Just tell me you're scared. I respond reasonably well to scared. I've seen yours. It looks like a man alphabetizing napkins."

"Noted," Astrid said, and meant it with his whole chest.

"However." She held up one finger. "I did bring terms."

"Of course you did."

"I'm a florist. We negotiate with seasons. Everything has conditions—it's called horticulture." She counted on her fingers. "One: no more briefings. If I meet your family again, I meet them as myself, opinions intact, including the opinion that your cousin Margaux's taste runs to 'hotel lobby in a sad decade.'"

"She'll hear about that within the hour."

"Good. Keeps her engaged. Two: when you're afraid, you say so within twenty-four hours. Not after you've built a structure around it. Twenty-four hours, maximum, and then it's mine to help carry or ignore, my choice. Three—" and here her voice changed, softened into something almost shy, which on Elise was so rare it stopped his breath—"three: you let me see the drafts. The unfinished answers. The version of 'who do you want to be' from six months from now, when it's changed. I don't want the final copy, Astrid. I never wanted the final copy. I want the man with the pencil marks."

Astrid sat very still.

"In exchange," she went on, "I'll do the same. No more sitting on truths in coolers. No more deciding alone what you can handle. We tell each other the ugly middle parts, not just the polished endings." She shrugged, suddenly awkward, suddenly young. "Those are my terms. There's no penalty clause. There's no exit provision. There's just—if you break them, we talk about it, and if we can't fix it, we fix ourselves, and try again. That's the whole document. It fits on a napkin. Do you want to see it?"

"You wrote it on a napkin?"

"I own a flower shop. Napkins are the only paper I respect." She produced it from her pocket—a real napkin, slightly creased, with three lines in her handwriting and, he noticed, a small pressed violet taped to the corner. "The violet is non-negotiable. It means something in the language of flowers."

"What does it mean?"

"'Return to me,'" she said. "'With faithfulness.' Or possibly 'I'm watching you, don't mess this up.' Florists disagree."

Astrid laughed—an actual laugh, unmanaged, surprising them both—and then, because there was nothing left in him that wanted to perform anything, he reached across the table and took her hand.

"Then let's start again," he said. "Properly. No contract, no weekend, no role. Elise Marchand, would you have dinner with me tonight? As a first date. An actual first date, three weeks after we fell in love, because our timeline has always been ridiculous and I refuse to fix it now."

"Tonight's no good," she said, and his heart dropped through the floor before she continued, deadpan, "I'm busy until six. Six works."

"Six works."

"And Astrid—one more thing." She turned her hand over so their palms met. "Camille called me yesterday."

"Of course she did."

"She wants to talk to you about a wedding. Her wedding. Apparently she and Marc set a date last night, drunk on festival wine, and apparently she told him, quote, 'if anyone on earth can make me cry at my own wedding without making it tacky, it's my idiot brother, and he owes me.'" Elise's mouth curved. "She's giving you your first job since the fall. Your sister is handing you her actual wedding, Astrid. The woman who gave you ultimatums is now trusting you with the biggest day of her life."

Astrid felt something open in his chest that he didn't have a name for—something older than relief, closer to vocation, the twenty-four-year-old idiot stirring awake and blinking at the light.

"When does she want to meet?"

"She said, and I quote again, 'tomorrow, and tell Elise she's doing the flowers, this is not a request, I've seen her peonies.'" Elise drained her coffee and stood, collecting the plate and the napkin and, after a moment's visible debate, leaving the basil on the windowsill. "So. You have a wedding to plan, Joubert. A real one. For someone who knows every worst thing about you and asked anyway."

At the door, she paused, and kissed him—briefly, thoroughly, like signing something.

"Six o'clock," she said. "Don't be early. Early is a form of managing."

The door closed behind her. Astrid stood in his kitchen, in the sudden quiet, with a basil plant to neglect and a napkin contract to frame and a sister's wedding blooming on the horizon like the first brave flower of a season he'd thought would never come again.

Then he picked up his phone and dialed Camille, and when she answered, he said the words he'd been waiting three years to say:

"Tell me everything. Dates, venue, guest count—all of it. I'm going to need the full dossier."

On the other end of the line, his sister started to laugh.
