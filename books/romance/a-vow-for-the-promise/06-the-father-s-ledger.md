## 6. The Father's Ledger

The cellar under the Ashgrove house smelled of dust and cold stone and, beneath it all, the faint vinegar ghost of wines that had failed decades ago and never been poured out. Emrys had hated the smell as a boy. He hated it now with a more complicated grief.

He'd come for a box of his mother's letters. That was the story he'd told himself on the drive over from Marguerite's, past the hedgerow that divided the two properties like a suture, past the rows of Vale chardonnay gone gold in the late light. His mother had died three years after his father; her letters were up here somewhere, in the attic or the cellar, and he wanted them before Callum boxed everything off into the past tense. It was even true. He was learning that the best lies were true things arranged in the wrong order.

The ledger was where it had always been—bottom shelf of the oak cabinet his grandfather had built, wedged behind a row of empty demijohns, its spine cracked, its cover furred with the grey bloom of cellar mold. *Ashgrove Vineyards — Accounts*, in his father's square, unhurried hand. Emrys lifted it out expecting invoices. Expecting nothing.

The pages smelled of pencil shavings and time. The early years were ordinary: tonnage, barrels, the small brutal arithmetic of weather. Then 1998.

His father's handwriting changed that year. It tightened. The numbers stopped breathing.

Emrys read the entry twice, then a third time, standing very still in the cold, because the body understands certain truths before the mind consents to them. The harvest of '98—the frost year, the year the Vales lost everything, the year Henri Vale stood in the county office and was called a cheat to his face while his daughter watched from the doorway—was recorded here in his father's hand as a fiction. Tons of fruit logged that had never been picked. Barrels filled with juice bought quietly, cash, no paper trail, from a grower two counties south whose own crop had been mediocre but sufficient. Enough to keep Ashgrove's contracts alive through the frost while the Vale orchard—*orchard*, his father had written it himself, in the margin, with a contempt Emrys could feel through sixty years of distance—stood bare.

And beside it, one line, dated the week the records were filed:

*H.V. will take the blame. He always was too proud to check the books.*

Not an accident. Not a desperate improvisation that spiraled. A plan, entered calmly between a delivery note and a barrel count, the way a man might note that rain was expected Thursday.

Emrys sat down on an upturned crate. The stone bit through his trousers. Somewhere above him, water moved in the pipes, the house going about its evening without him.

He thought: *I know this handwriting.* Not his father's. His own. Five years ago, when the scandal broke—a senator's memoir, ghostwritten, polished until the man's corruption read as conviction—Emrys had been offered a choice by the publisher's lawyers. Disavow the manuscript, testify to what he knew, lose the name he'd built. Or sign a statement saying he'd only ever transcribed what he was given. He had chosen the statement. He had chosen it in eleven minutes, sitting in a conference room with a view of the harbor, and he had told himself ever since that he'd had no real choice at all.

*He always was too proud to check the books.* His father had known Henri Vale would be too proud—or too ruined—to audit his own disaster. And Emrys had known the senator was too arrogant to read what he signed. Both of them had counted on another man's flaw to bury their own.

The mirror didn't shatter. That was the terrible part. It simply hung there in the dark of the cellar, patient, showing him a face he recognized.

Footsteps on the stairs. Callum came down sideways, ducking under the low beam, a flashlight in one hand and a bottle of beer in the other, and stopped when he saw what was open on Emrys's knees.

For a long moment neither of them spoke. The flashlight beam trembled slightly against the far wall.

"You found it," Callum said.

Not *what is that*. Not *where did you get that*. *You found it.* Which meant Callum had known. Had known for how long? Since their father's death, when the estate papers passed to him? Since '98 itself, a boy of nineteen watching his father burn the southern fruit receipts in the yard?

"How long have you known?" Emrys asked. His voice came out level, which surprised him. Ghostwriters learned to keep their voices level. It was the first thing the work taught you: your voice is not the point.

Callum set the beer down on a barrel stave. "Dad told me the year before he died. Made me swear." He rubbed his jaw. "Said the family couldn't survive it coming out. Said the vineyard would go under, the loans, everything. Said—" He stopped. Started again. "Said Marguerite Vale was better off hating us than knowing her father was too soft to fight back."

"Too soft," Emrys repeated.

"That's what Dad said."

"And you believed him."

"I *wanted* to believe him." Callum's voice cracked along some old fault line. "There's a difference, Emrys. You'd know about differences like that, wouldn't you? Living under a fake name, writing other people's lives." It wasn't cruel the way it sounded. It was desperate. "You ran. I stayed. We both found our ways not to look."

Emrys closed the ledger. The sound was very small in the big cold room.

"The lawyers are coming tomorrow," he said. "Marguerite's filed. The covenant—it can't be dissolved without both signatures, and now it won't dissolve at all without a judge. And if this goes to discovery, Callum, this ledger comes out anyway. Subpoenaed, catalogued, read aloud in a courtroom with her sitting there. Would you rather she heard it from a court clerk?"

"I'd rather it never existed." Callum crossed the space between them and put his hand flat on the ledger's cover, over the title, as if he could press the years shut. "Burn it. Tonight. There's a stove in the workshop, nobody's used it since—"

"No."

"Emrys—"

"*No.*" Louder than he meant. The word rang off the stone and came back to him sounding like someone else's voice, someone braver, and he decided to let it stand. "I spent five years being the man who burns things. Do you understand? Not literally. Worse. I take the truth and I make it *serviceable*. I make it palatable. I make it belong to whoever's paying. And every time I tell myself it's craft, it's survival, it's just the job." He looked down at his hands. They were steady. He almost wished they weren't; steadiness felt like his father's steadiness, the calm of a man entering a lie into a ledger between two honest entries. "Henri Vale died believing he was a cheat. His daughter grew up carrying that. And we—" his voice caught, and he let it, "—we drank good wine at Christmas and said nothing."

Callum took his hand off the ledger slowly, like a man removing his palm from a wound.

"If you give it to her," he said quietly, "she'll use it to take the whole valley. The litigation, the covenant, everything. Dad wasn't wrong about that part."

"Then that's the price. We pay it."

"You don't even live here anymore. What do you care what it costs?"

And there it was—the question underneath all the others, the one Emrys had driven up the valley road to avoid. Because he did care. He cared about a woman who quoted Neruda to her pickers and salted her tomatoes too heavily and laughed like the sound cost her something. He cared about a brother who still kept their mother's geraniums alive on the kitchen sill. He cared about belonging to something he had no right to, built on a silence he had inherited and then, God help him, *maintained*.

"I care," he said, "because I've been her ghostwriter for four months. I've been writing her life, Callum. Her father's shame is in every chapter, and I knew—I suspected—and I wrote around it. Smoothed it. Made it literary." He stood, and the ledger was heavy in his arms, heavier than paper and board had any right to be. "I did to her exactly what Dad did to Henri. I decided what she got to know."

* * *

They stood in the yard afterward, in the blue hour, the vines going silver-dark down the slope. Callum had followed him out, and for a while they just breathed the cold air that smelled of ripening fruit and coming rain.

"Burn it," Callum said again, but softly now, and it wasn't a demand anymore. It was a plea from a drowning man. "Please. I'll do anything. I'll co-sign whatever she wants, the covenant, the land, I don't care. Just not this. Not in a courtroom. Not with her looking at us."

Emrys held the ledger against his chest. Above the hedgerow, the lights of the Vale farmhouse came on, one window, then another, warm as struck matches.

"Tomorrow," he said, "the lawyers arrive. And tomorrow night, whatever happens, this stops being ours to hide."

Callum looked at him for a long time. Something moved across his face—not forgiveness, not yet, maybe not for years—but recognition, the awful fraternal kind, seeing yourself in someone at your worst.

"You're going to tell her yourself," Callum said. "Before the lawyers do."

Emrys turned toward the lights across the hedge. Tomorrow, Marguerite would learn that her ghostwriter was the son of the man who had written her father's ruin into a ledger as casually as a weather note. Tomorrow she would learn that the voice she'd trusted with her life's story had been lying to her in the second person all along.

But tonight, the ledger sat in his arms, and the truth weighed what it weighed.

"Yes," he said. "Tonight."
