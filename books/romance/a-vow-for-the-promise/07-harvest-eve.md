## 7. Harvest Eve

The lawyers arrived with the weather.

Both came down from the hills together, a gray front sliding over the valley, and by nine in the morning the Vale farmhouse smelled of wet dust and printer toner instead of the bread Marguerite had promised to bake. Two men in charcoal suits sat at her kitchen table like they'd been carved from the same block of slate, and between them they spread the covenant documents the way surgeons spread their instruments—each page laid flat, squared, annotated in a red so precise it looked arterial.

Emrys stood at the counter with a coffee he wasn't drinking and watched Marguerite hold her ground. She'd dressed for battle in her own way: the good boots she wore to funerals and harvests, her hair pinned back so severely it changed the architecture of her face. She was not the woman who had wept over her father's photograph last week, or the woman who had laughed at his terrible joke about the tractor, her flour-dusted hands pressed to her mouth. She was Marguerite Vale, proprietor, and she was going to make these men say every ugly thing out loud.

"Mr. Halloran's firm has reviewed the covenant language," the taller one said. He had a voice like a filing cabinet. "It's airtight. Neither parcel can be conveyed, encumbered, or subdivided without the co-signature of the adjoining holder. Your father knew that when he signed in '99. He signed it anyway."

"He signed it because the alternative was losing everything," Marguerite said. "That's not agreement. That's a man with his back against a wall, and your client built the wall."

Emrys wrote none of this down. He was supposed to be writing it down—the memoir had chapters to fill, and litigation was material, and he had spent five years teaching himself that everything, even grief, was material. But his pen stayed capped in his pocket, and the coffee went cold in his hand, and he found himself watching the rain begin against the kitchen window, fat drops sliding down the glass like the valley itself was trying to wash something away.

The second lawyer—Ashgrove's man, younger, with the same heavy jaw as Callum and the same careful way of not looking at Emrys—slid a folder across the table. "There's a settlement offer. Your client releases all claims regarding the '98 records in exchange for a boundary adjustment along the south fence line and a cash consideration."

Marguerite didn't touch the folder. "A cash consideration." She said the words the way you'd spit out a bad grape. "My father died believing he'd failed. Believing the whole valley believed it. You're offering me a fence line and a check to keep it that way."

"I'm offering you what the court is likely to give you anyway, minus three years of discovery."

"Then we'll spend the three years."

The filing-cabinet voice sighed. Emrys understood the sigh. He'd heard it from editors when a manuscript refused to behave, when the truth of a story was more inconvenient than the version everyone had agreed to want. *Discovery.* He turned the word over in his mind and felt the floor of the last five years tilt beneath it, because that was exactly what was coming—not just the lawyers' kind, with their subpoenas and their depositions, but the other kind, the kind that didn't respect privilege or confidentiality, the kind that dug down through the strata of a family until it found the thing buried deepest.

His father's ledger was in the trunk of his car. It had been there for six days.

* * *

The rain settled in by noon, soft and steady, the kind that made the vineyard look like a memory of itself. The lawyers left for the Ashgrove estate to deliver the same offer from the other direction, and Marguerite stood in the doorway watching their car descend the drive, her arms folded against the damp.

"They'll take the fence line," she said. "Callum will want it settled before harvest. He's not his father, but he's not brave either."

"You don't know that."

"I've known Ashgroves my whole life, Emrys." She turned, and something in her face had softened, or perhaps just tired. "I know exactly what they are."

He should have told her then. He knew that later, replaying it, cataloguing the moment the way he catalogued everything—*here, this was the door, and it was open*—he should have set down the coffee and said *Marguerite, there's something I have to tell you before this goes any further, before the depositions, before anyone else says my name out loud.* The ledger was in his car. The truth was six days old and gaining weight by the hour. And she was standing in the doorway with rain coming down behind her, offering him, without knowing it, one more chance to be the man she thought she'd hired.

Instead he said, "You should eat something. You didn't touch the bread."

She laughed—a short, surprised sound—and something in his chest cracked along a fault line he hadn't known was there. "The bread's a lost cause. I'm a vintner, not a baker." She came back inside, shaking rain from her sleeves, and for a moment the kitchen was warm again, the two of them in the lamplight with the storm pressed against the windows, and he thought: *I could stay here. I could be this person. The one who notices when she doesn't eat.*

That was the trouble with ghosts. They got greedy for warmth.

* * *

It happened at four o'clock, and it happened because of a courier.

The settlement counter-offer Marguerite had drafted needed to go to Halloran's firm before five, and her lawyer had insisted on certified delivery, and the courier needed a legal name for the signature block. Marguerite had the paperwork out on the table, filling in what she knew—*Marguerite Vale, Vale Orchard and Vineyards, parcel 114*—and then she came to the line for the co-signing witness, the party of the second part, and she looked up at him with the pen already extended.

"You never gave me your full name. Isn't that ridiculous? Five months, and I've been introducing you to my pickers as just Emrys, like a pope or a cat."

"It's on the contract," he said. His voice came out strange. Flat.

"The contract says *E. Wren, Ghostwrite Services, care of* a PO box in Ashford." She was smiling, still smiling, turning the pen in her fingers. "I assumed Wren was real. I assumed—" and he watched it happen, watched the smile slow and stall as her eyes went to the counter-offer, to the line she'd already drafted, *Vale v. Ashgrove Vineyards, LLC*, watched her read the name she'd written and then read it again, and then look up at him with the pen motionless in her hand.

The rain filled the silence. It was the loudest thing in the valley.

"Say it," she said quietly. "Say your name."

He could have lied. God knew he was good at it; he'd built a whole life out of the craft, a whole career, a whole self. He could have said *Wren, Emrys Wren,* and she might have believed him, might have wanted to believe him, and he could have gone on being the man who noticed when she didn't eat.

"Emrys Ashgrove," he said. "My father was Aldous Ashgrove. I'm his son."

She didn't step back. That was the worst of it, later—that she didn't step back, didn't raise her voice, didn't do any of the things that would have let him tell himself she'd overreacted. She just stood there in her kitchen with the pen in her hand and the rain on the windows, and she looked at him the way you look at a manuscript you'd loved right up until the moment you found the forgery in chapter three.

"The whole time." Her voice was very even. "The interviews. My father's photograph. The night I told you what they did to him—what your family did—you sat at this table and took notes."

"I was going to tell you."

"When?" Not cruel. Worse than cruel: genuinely curious, the question of a woman who wanted to understand the mechanics of her own ruin. "After the book? After the settlement? After I'd told you everything and you'd made a beautiful manuscript out of my grief and sold it under someone else's name?"

"The ledger—" He stopped. He hadn't meant to say it. It came out of him like blood from a reopened cut. "There's a ledger. My father's. It proves the '98 records were falsified. It's been in my car for six days because I've been trying to find a way to give it to you that wouldn't—" He pressed his hand over his mouth, a child's gesture, and forced the rest out. "Wouldn't cost me this. Wouldn't cost me you. That's the truth. That's all of it. I found the proof of what my father did and I hid in my car with it because I'm a coward, and I've been a coward since I was twenty-six years old."

Marguerite set the pen down. She did it carefully, aligned it with the table's edge, and he understood that the care was the only thing keeping her hands steady.

"Do you know what I told you in this kitchen?" she said. "About my father, the last harvest of his life, standing in the rows because he couldn't bear to be in the house? I gave you that. I gave you my mother's letters. I gave you the worst year of my family's life because I believed you were a stranger who'd chosen to listen." She picked up the counter-offer, squared it the way the lawyers had squared their documents, and held it out to him. "You'll take this to the courier. And then you'll take your ledger, and your name, and whatever else you've been carrying around in that car, and you'll go. I don't want you in this house. I don't want you in this book."

"Marguerite—"

"You want to know the cruelest part?" For the first time her voice broke, just at the seam. "I was starting to trust you. Not the writer. *You.* I was starting to think—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and thrust the papers at him. "Go. Please. Before I say something I'll mean even more."

He took the papers. His hand brushed hers and she flinched, actually flinched, and that—more than the dismissal, more than the name spoken aloud in the rain-gray kitchen—was the thing that followed him out the door and down the drive to his car.

* * *

The courier office in town closed at five. He made it with four minutes to spare, handed over the counter-offer, signed the receipt *E. Wren* in a hand that didn't look like his, and then sat in the parking lot with the engine off while the rain drummed the roof and the valley went blue with dusk.

The ledger was in the trunk. He could feel it the way you feel a wound, a low constant pressure under everything. Six days he'd driven around with his father's crime riding behind him, telling himself he was being careful, being strategic, waiting for the right moment—and the right moment had been every moment, and he had missed all of them, and now the woman whose trust he'd wanted more than he'd wanted anything in five years knew his name and wanted him gone.

*Staying invisible cost him everything visible.* He thought it clearly, almost with a writer's detachment, and then the detachment failed and he put his forehead against the steering wheel and let the shaking come.

Through the windshield, down the valley, he could see the lights of the Ashgrove house coming on one by one—Callum's kitchen, the old man's study, the windows of the room where Emrys had grown up believing that silence was the same thing as safety. And beyond it, farther, the Vale farmhouse with its single lit window, where Marguerite was alone with the wreckage of a trust he'd spent five months building and five minutes destroying.

The rain was letting up. Tomorrow the pickers would come, harvest eve turning to harvest, the grapes hanging heavy and ready whether anyone deserved them or not.

Emrys lifted his head. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand, the way his father used to after funerals, and he looked at the ledger through the rearview mirror as if it were a person.

He knew what he had to write. He'd known for six days.

He started the car and drove toward the light.
