## 7. Collision at the Dock

The notice went up on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day bad news prefers; Mondays are too raw with self-pity and Wednesdays already belong to hope.

I found it pinned to the conservatory's corkboard between a flyer for a bake sale and Priya's hand-lettered manifesto demanding better rosin for the viola section. The notice was typed, laminated—laminated, as if to protect it from our tears—and it announced that the mainland arts board had "restructured" its winter disbursement. Effective immediately. The winter concert, the one Ines had been building toward all season like a woman stacking sandbags against a flood, would receive forty percent of its promised funding.

Forty percent. I did the arithmetic standing there with my coffee going cold in my hand, because that is what trained musicians do instead of screaming: we count.

Forty percent meant no guest musicians. It meant no transport for instruments. It meant, if you followed the numbers down their grim little staircase, no heating oil past January, and a hall full of children breathing visible breath while they played Vivaldi.

"You've seen it, then."

Ines had come up behind me without sound, which at her age and girth should have been impossible, but she moved through that building the way weather moves through a valley—announced by effect rather than presence.

"I've seen it," I said.

"They'll fight me on it," she said, in the tone of a woman who intended to be fought *with*, not *for*. "There's a public meeting Friday. At the dock, of all places. The board sends someone over on the ferry, they hold court in the waiting room like it's still 1970, everyone shouts, nothing changes." She adjusted her glasses. "You should come."

"I don't shout."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "But you glare beautifully, and half those board members are terrified of cellists. They think we're all temperament and broken marriages." She patted my arm and swept off toward her office, leaving me alone with the laminated cruelty and my cold coffee.

I should explain something about myself, since apparently this narrative requires honesty: I am not brave. I was never brave. What people mistook for bravery onstage—the stillness before the downbeat, the willingness to walk out alone under lights—was actually just a very elaborate form of hiding. You cannot see the audience if you are inside the music. For thirty years I hid inside the music, and when the music stopped being available as shelter, I discovered there was nowhere left to stand.

So when Ines asked me to come to a public meeting about budget cuts, every instinct I owned said *no*. Stay in your room. Practice your scales. Keep your heart a muscle trained into silence.

I went anyway. Not from courage—from curiosity, which is courage's cheaper cousin and often shows up uninvited.

* * *

Friday arrived gray and hard, the kind of island morning where the sea looks less like water than like hammered tin. The ferry came in at ten, and with it, the board's man.

I should have known. Of course I should have known. The universe has a sense of humor, and like most conductors, it cannot resist a dramatic entrance.

He came down the gangway in a charcoal coat that cost more than Amos earned in a season, carrying a leather portfolio and an expression of profound inconvenience, as though the crossing itself were a personal insult he intended to bill someone for. The wind took his hair and he let it, because men like him have decided that looking ruffled is beneath them even when they are.

Elias Marchetti.

I had seen him twice since arriving on the island—once arguing with staff about a broken radiator, once standing at the window of the library reading room with the particular stillness of a man watching weather he resents. We had exchanged the nods of two people who recognize each other as members of the same doomed profession and want no part of the acquaintance. He had a reputation that preceded him across three bodies of water: brilliant, ruthless, finished. A conductor who had burned bright enough to scorch the people standing nearest him, and then—depending on which version of the story you heard—either imploded or was imploded upon. The details varied. The wreckage didn't.

And now here he was, stepping off the boat with a portfolio full of cuts, and I understood with a lurch that *he* was the board's man. He was the one they'd sent to do the arithmetic aloud.

Ines was already at the dock, positioned in front of the waiting-room doors like a fortress with a cardigan. Priya stood beside her with her arms crossed and her chin set at the angle she reserved for injustice. Amos hovered near the benches holding a thermos he kept offering to people who didn't want tea. And a scattering of parents and townsfolk had gathered, drawn by the ancient human magnetism of a public argument.

Marchetti reached the head of the gangway and stopped. His eyes found mine across twenty feet of wet planking, and something flickered through his face—not recognition exactly, but the wariness of a man who has spotted a familiar shape in unfamiliar terrain.

"Cyrus—" Ines began, all warmth and ambush, "you know Mr. Marchetti?"

"We've met," he said, before I could decide whether to lie. His voice was exactly as I remembered it from somewhere I couldn't place: low, precise, with the faintest edge of impatience, as if the world consistently spoke too slowly for him. "Though I'm surprised to find you here, Mr.—"

"Sandoval," I said. "Odile Sandoval."

Something moved behind his eyes then. Something quick and unreadable, gone before I could name it. If he recognized the name—if he knew what I'd been, what had happened—he gave no sign beyond a fractional stiffening of the shoulders.

"The cellist," he said. Neutral as a metronome.

"Retired," I said, just as neutrally, and watched his jaw tighten fractionally at the word, which interested me more than it should have.

* * *

The meeting happened in the ferry waiting room, which seats forty humans comfortably and fifty-five uncomfortably, and we packed in sixty-one. Marchetti stood at the front near the ticket window with his portfolio open, and he began, as these men always begin, with context.

"The mainland arts endowment has lost eleven percent of its value in eighteen months," he said. "Every institution is taking reductions. Every one. The conservatory's allocation reflects a proportional share, not a targeted cut."

"It reflects a share calculated by people who've never taken the ferry," Ines said, "and who think 'proportional' is a synonym for 'fair.'"

"Fairness isn't the metric. Sustainability is."

"Sustainability," Priya repeated, from the back, with the devastating flatness only a fifteen-year-old can achieve. "That's a word adults use when they're about to take something."

A ripple of uncomfortable laughter ran through the room. Marchetti didn't smile. He turned a page in his portfolio with a crisp economy that made me, absurdly, think of a bow hand. Clean attack. No wasted motion.

"The reduction means the winter concert proceeds at reduced scale," he continued. "Local ensemble only. No guest artists, no instrument transport subsidy. Heating allocation through February."

"Heating allocation through February," Ines said, "at current prices, means the hall goes cold in mid-January. Children playing in coats. Is that the image the board wants in the papers? Because I can arrange the photograph myself, Mr. Marchetti. I'm told I photograph terribly, which will help your case."

"Threatening the press won't restore funds."

"No. But it might embarrass the men who cut them, and embarrassment is the only currency they've ever honored."

It went on like that for twenty minutes—a duel conducted in committee language, both of them fluent, neither yielding an inch. I stood near the wall with my arms folded and watched, and I confess I watched him more than her. There was something hypnotic about Elias Marchetti in combat. He never raised his voice. He never gestured wider than the page in front of him. He simply absorbed each blow, rephrased it, and returned it with interest, and the whole performance had the terrible polish of a man who had spent years conducting rooms full of egos larger than his own and had learned exactly how much force a human argument could bear before it snapped.

Then he made his mistake.

"To be clear," he said, closing the portfolio, "the board's position is that a winter concert of this scale—in this location—is not a defensible use of restricted funds. Frankly, the program itself is aspirational. A youth ensemble performing repertoire well above its level, in an unheated hall, for an audience of—"

"For an audience of the community that built this place," Ines said.

"For an audience of forty people," Marchetti said, "most of whom are related to the performers. The board's responsibility is to institutions that can demonstrate viability."

And I heard myself speak before I had authorized it.

"What does viability look like from the mainland?"

The room turned. He turned. For a moment we simply looked at each other across the waiting room, over the heads of the town, and I felt the strange double awareness I'd been carrying since the gangway sharpen into focus.

"Mr. Sandoval," he said.

"You've never seen the ensemble play," I said. "None of them have. They've read a spreadsheet. They've made a judgment about children they've never heard, based on numbers produced by an institution currently selling its own building to cover shortfalls—which I understand, because I read the annual report on the ferry over, since apparently some of us do homework." A murmur went through the room. "So I'm asking. What does viability sound like? Describe it. Since you're the expert."

His eyes narrowed—not in anger, I realized, but in the way a musician narrows his eyes at a phrase that's been played wrong in a way he can't immediately diagnose.

"Viability sounds like a program that doesn't depend on charity and sentiment," he said quietly.

"Everything depends on charity and sentiment. Music especially. Ask any orchestra board in the world. Ask the great halls. They run on donors who fell in love with a sound when they were nine years old. You cut the moment of falling in love because the accounting doesn't capture it, and in twenty years you wonder why nobody donates." I shrugged. "But you know this. You must know this. Which means the question isn't whether the concert is viable. The question is whether anyone on that board remembers why music matters, and I suspect the answer is no, and I suspect you know it's no, and I suspect—" I paused, and the next sentence arrived fully formed and cruel, "—that's why they sent you. People send the man who doesn't flinch."

The silence afterward had texture. Weight. Somewhere behind me Amos set down his thermos with a small ceramic click, loud as a gunshot.

Marchetti looked at me for a long moment. The waiting room clock ticked. The tide slapped the pilings under the floor.

"You argue like a man giving a deposition," he finally said, "for a case he's already lost."

"And you defend like a man who knows the verdict's bought and paid for, and hates himself for taking the fee."

"That," he said softly, "is none of your business."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Nothing here is my business. I'm retired." I used the word like a blade this time, and saw it land—saw the tiny flinch around his eyes, the way his hand flattened on the portfolio—and I felt a flash of satisfaction so hot and immediate it frightened me, because it wasn't righteous. It was personal. It was the pleasure of finding a soft place in a stranger and pressing it, and knowing, somehow, that it would hurt.

"Meeting's concluded," Ines announced into the silence, with the brisk mercy of a good chairwoman. "Thank you all for coming. Mr. Marchetti, the four o'clock ferry will take you back—or you're welcome to stay and hear the ensemble rehearse this afternoon. See what your spreadsheet left out."

He gathered his portfolio. "I have a return crossing to make."

"Of course you do," said Priya, loudly enough for everyone.

* * *

He almost made the four o'clock. I know because I was on the dock—I'd taken to walking the shore road in the late afternoons, a habit I told myself was exercise and knew was avoidance—and I watched him come down the path from the conservatory at ten past four, moving fast, coat collar up, and stop dead at the top of the gangway.

The ferry was already pulling out. The harbor master, a woman named Gudrun who missed nothing and forgave less, called something cheerful and merciless after him. He stood there a moment, watching the wake widen, and then he turned, and I was the only person on the dock, and retreat was no longer geometrically available to either of us.

We regarded each other across fifteen feet of planking.

"Missed your boat," I said.

"Observant."

"There's another tomorrow. Weather permitting. Sometimes not even then."

"So I've been told. Repeatedly. By everyone." He came down the dock anyway, hands in his pockets, and stopped a few feet away, looking out at the water rather than at me. Up close, off duty, he looked tired in a way the waiting room hadn't shown—the fatigue sitting deep, structural, like a crack in a foundation that the paint had been covering for years. "Your argument this afternoon," he said. "It was theatrical."

"It was true. Those aren't mutually exclusive."

"No. But they're rarely comfortable together." He was quiet a moment. The tin-colored sea knocked against the pilings. "For what it's worth, the recommendation wasn't mine. The allocation model wasn't mine. I was sent because—" he exhaled through his nose, almost a laugh, entirely without humor, "—well. You diagnosed it accurately. I don't flinch."

"Then don't flinch now. Tell me if you believe it. The cut. Do you believe the conservatory should lose its winter concert?"

He turned and looked at me then, directly, and I braced for the committee voice, the polished deflection.

"I believe," he said, "that the piece your young violist is fighting for—the one she wrote the manifesto about—it's the Sinfonia Concertante, yes? And she plays the second movement like she's apologizing for existing, and someone should have told her months ago that the movement is a demand, not a request. I believe the janitor writes string quartets in a notebook he hides in the supply closet, and they're better than half the commissions the board funded last year. And I believe that if the hall goes cold in January, none of that survives to spring, because talent at that age doesn't wait. It dies of neglect or it leaves." He paused. "So no. I don't believe the cut is right. I believe it's inevitable, which is a different thing, and the difference between those two words is most of what's wrong with the world."

I stared at him. The wind pushed between us and he didn't move.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked.

"Because you asked me not to flinch." He glanced back toward the town, where the lights were coming on one by one in the blue dusk. "Is there lodging on this island that doesn't smell of fish?"

"The Anchor Inn smells of fish. The rooms above the bakery smell of cinnamon, which is worse by the third night."

"Noted." He picked up his portfolio. Then, without quite turning back: "Your playing. I've heard recordings. Years ago."

My whole body went still, the way water goes still before ice.

"There was a Brahms recording," he went on, eyes on the darkening water, voice carefully level. "The Double Concerto. It was—unusual. Most cellists play it like a monument. You played it like a conversation." A beat. "Whatever happened to end the career, the recordings suggest the career was worth having."

And he walked away up the dock, toward the cinnamon-smelling rooms, leaving me standing in the wind with my heart doing something it had no business doing.

Because here is the thing I could not explain, standing there while the harbor lights came on and the cold worked through my coat: the argument had felt electric. Not pleasant—God, no, nothing about it had been pleasant—but alive, in the way a nerve is alive, in the way a struck string is alive. I had spent three years feeling nothing stronger than dread, and in one afternoon this arrogant, exhausted, honest stranger had made me furious, made me sharp, made me *speak*—made me forget, for entire minutes at a time, to be ashamed.

I told myself it was adrenaline. Combat does that. Any enemy will do.

I told myself that all the way home, and almost believed it, and then I unpacked my mail from the day and found, at the bottom of the stack, a letter in unfamiliar handwriting, addressed to me care of the conservatory, postmarked from the mainland.

From Wren.

I stood in my doorway in the dark, holding it, thinking about a man on a dock who had described Priya's playing and Amos's hidden quartets with the precision of someone who had been listening far longer and far closer than any visiting auditor should have been.

Thinking: *what a coincidence.*

Thinking: *is it?*

I carried the letter inside, and did not open it yet, and sat with it unopened on the table like a held breath, because some part of me—the part that had pressed on his soft place and felt the electricity jump back—already suspected that the two men in my life who made me feel most seen might be standing much closer together than the geography allowed.

The letter waited. The wind rose. Tomorrow's ferry, weather permitting, might not come at all.
