## 12. Rehearsal Sabotage

I have conducted orchestras through blackouts, bee invasions, and one memorable evening when the second oboe's dentures flew into the timpani. I thought I understood catastrophe. Then on a gray Tuesday in November, three mothers and a father arrived at rehearsal with the particular expression of people who have read something on the internet and believe themselves informed.

"Is it true?" the tallest mother asked, holding up her phone like a warrant. "That you're closing?"

The ensemble room went quiet in that specific way rooms do when children are pretending not to listen. Priya's bow stopped mid-stroke. Amos, who had been stacking chairs with the serenity of a man who has outlived several institutions, set down a chair very carefully, as if it might be the last one.

"We're not closing," Ines said from the doorway, arriving with the timing of a woman who had been listening from the hall for exactly long enough to hear the question. "We're having a difficult autumn."

"The website says—" the father began.

"The website is a rumor wearing a coat," Ines said. "Rehearsal continues."

Two of the mothers left anyway, collecting their children like umbrellas forgotten at a restaurant. The third hesitated, looked at Priya—looked at her the way people look at other people's children when calculating risk—and stayed. Small mercies. By Thursday we were four students short.

I want to be precise about what this did to me, because precision is the only honesty I've ever been good at. It did not make me angry. Anger is a luxury of men whose careers are intact. What it made me was *recognized*. Someone out there had done to this conservatory what I once did to Odile Almeida: stood at a distance, said a few well-placed words, and watched the structure come down. I knew the weight of those words. I knew how light they felt leaving your mouth.

So when I found myself standing outside the practice rooms that afternoon, watching Almeida through the window as he corrected a student's bow hold—hands steady, face closed, a man built entirely of locked doors—I did not think: *he did this.* I thought: *someone with money and a grudge did this, and there are only so many of those, and most of them have his name in their little black books.*

It was not a fair suspicion. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept. It was the suspicion of a man who had spent twenty years reading rooms and had learned to assume the worst motive in every silence. But then again—

Then again, Almeida had arrived on this island in September with a patron's letter of introduction and the haunted look of a man mid-negotiation with his own past. Patrons do not simply release their cellists. They lend them, the way banks lend money, and they collect.

* * *

I cornered him after rehearsal, in the corridor by the archive, because I am a coward and corridors feel less like ambushes than offices do.

"A word," I said.

"You just used several." He didn't stop walking. His coat was already over his arm; he had the escape routes of a man who had spent a career avoiding post-concert conversations.

"Your friends," I said, "are pulling my students out of my orchestra."

He stopped. Turned. And here is the thing about Odile Almeida that no photograph captures: he does not look guilty when accused. He looks *insulted*, which is either innocence or excellent training, and I genuinely could not tell you which, and that uncertainty has been eating me alive since October.

"My friends," he said, "are dead, retired, or embarrassed to know me. Pick whichever flatters your narrative."

"Somebody leaked it. The endowment gap. The board vote. That's not public information—it wasn't even *board* information until last week."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because you're the newest variable." I heard my own voice go cold, the podium voice, the one I'd sworn off. "Because you have patrons with deep pockets and longer memories. Because you walked onto this island and within two months the institution starts bleeding, and I'm supposed to believe in coincidence?"

The corridor was very quiet. Somewhere below us, Amos was mopping, and the smell of soap rose through the floorboards like an apology neither of us deserved.

"You think I called someone," Almeida said slowly, "to destroy the only place that would take me."

"I think wounded animals bite the hand that feeds them if they think the food's running out."

For a moment I thought he would hit me. I want to say I braced for it; really I stood there like a man waiting for weather, half-hoping. Instead he laughed—one short, terrible note, the laugh of a man finding a joke at his own funeral.

"You know what the difference between us is, Marchetti?" He stepped closer. He smelled of rosin and cold air and something herbal, tea maybe, the discipline of a man managing his own nerves chemically. "When you destroyed me, you did it in front of two thousand people and got a standing ovation for your honesty. If I were going to sabotage this place, I'd at least have the decency to do it anonymously. Like a pen pal."

Something went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. He couldn't have known. He couldn't—he was talking about the program, the letters, the whole wretched institutional exercise. But my hands, traitors both, wanted to check my coat pocket where Wren's latest letter sat folded against my ribs like contraband.

"You should be careful," I said, and my voice came out wrong, too soft. "Accusations travel."

"So do apologies," he said. "I've never received one. Do they actually exist, or are they like the yeti?"

And he walked out into the rain without a coat, because of course he did, because everything about the man is a small performance of self-destruction that he'd call pragmatism if anyone dared ask.

I stood in the corridor and hated him with a thoroughness that frightened me, because underneath the hatred, in the basement of it, was the letter, and the letter said things like *you make me want to try*, and the letter was addressed to a man I had just verbally gutted in a hallway, and the arithmetic of that did not balance.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of sleeping: I made calls.

This is its own confession. I had sworn off the phone the way reformed drinkers swear off the bottle—not because the thing itself is evil but because of what I become holding it. But I still knew people. A fixer in Vienna who owed me from a festival gone wrong. A manager in London who owed me nothing and told me everything anyway because gossip is her love language. Three calls, forty minutes, and I had the shape of it.

The leak hadn't come from Almeida's circle. It had come from a man named Corvus—yes, really; some people are born to their villainy—who ran a cultural investment fund and had been circling the island's waterfront property for two years. He'd gotten wind of the endowment gap through a board member's estranged brother, and he'd done what such men do: whispered to parents, planted a paragraph on a local news aggregator, and waited for the building to panic-sell itself.

Corvus had also, my London source mentioned with relish, once tried to sign Odile Almeida for a "prestige residency" and been refused so publicly that it made the trade papers. Which meant Corvus had a grudge against the conservatory *and* against its famous broken cellist, and leaking the closure rumor hurt them both with a single knife.

So. Not Almeida. Never Almeida. I sat in my cottage with the wood stove ticking and understood that I had taken the worst man I know and dressed him in my own sins, because my own sins fit him so conveniently.

There is a version of this story where I keep this information. Where I let Almeida sit under suspicion I no longer believed, because suspicion was the only currency I held that he couldn't match. I had spent my whole career learning that information is power and power is safety, and safety was the thing I had crossed an ocean to find and hadn't yet located.

Instead I did something worse, or better—I've stopped trying to sort the categories. I wrote it all down. Names, dates, the chain from board member to brother to Corvus. And in the morning I put it in an envelope, and I did not mail it to anyone, because there was no one to mail it to, because the person who needed it was fifty feet away across a courtyard, and delivering it meant knocking on his door.

I am, as established, a coward. But I am a coward with a deadline, because the board had moved its vote up, and rumors move faster than corrections.

* * *

The board met on Friday in the library, around the long table under the portrait of some founder whose eyes follow you with the weary patience of a man who has watched a century of meetings change nothing. I was not supposed to be there. Conductors are not board members; we are weather, noted and endured. But Ines had asked me to present the winter concert proposal, and I had said yes before remembering that saying yes to things is how my last life ended.

Almeida was there too—or rather, he was there the way a storm front is there, standing at the back near the scores, arms folded, having invited himself on the grounds that the concert concerned him. We had not spoken since the corridor. When our eyes met, he looked at me the way you look at a bill you intend to dispute.

Halfway through my presentation—the program, the fundraising target, the modest miracle of Priya's ensemble playing in public—a board member named Halloway cleared his throat.

"There's another matter," Halloway said. "We've received a communication suggesting that Mr. Almeida's presence here may represent a reputational liability. That his—history—could complicate donor relations for the concert."

The room did the thing rooms do: everyone became fascinated by their own hands. Almeida didn't move. I watched him not move, and it was the most effortful stillness I have ever witnessed, a man holding himself together with furniture.

"From whom?" Ines asked.

"An anonymous communication. A letter."

Of course. A letter. The universe has a sense of humor and it is entirely epistolary.

"With respect," I heard myself say, "that's garbage."

Every head turned. Including his. Especially his.

"Mr. Almeida's history," I continued, standing, because sitting felt suddenly dishonest, "is that he collapsed onstage because he was human, and then had the poor taste to survive it. If donors are frightened of human beings, I suggest we recalibrate our donor base rather than our faculty. Furthermore—" and here I committed the act, the whole act, laid it on the table like a card I'd been hiding in my sleeve for days— "furthermore, I know where the leak came from. Not Mr. Almeida. A property speculator named Corvus, working through a board relative, and I have documentation. He doesn't want to close us. He wants us panicked enough to sell cheap. Every parent we lose, every rumor we repeat, we're doing his work for him, unpaid."

Silence. The founder's portrait gazed down with what I chose to interpret as approval.

Halloway recovered first. "You investigated this?"

"I made phone calls," I said. "It's a skill one picks up, running organizations. People tell you things. Usually things you don't want to hear about yourself, but occasionally useful."

Ines was looking at me with an expression I couldn't parse—gratitude braided with suspicion, or perhaps just surprise that the island's resident storm cloud had produced lightning in a helpful direction. And Almeida—

Almeida was looking at me the way you look at a locked door that has just opened on its own. Not trust. Nothing so generous. More like the recalculation of a man who had filed me under *enemy* and now found the file wouldn't close.

* * *

Afterward, in the courtyard, the rain had stopped and everything smelled of wet stone and salt. He was waiting by the wall, and I considered walking past him, and then considered that I had spent my entire adult life walking past things, and stopped.

"Why?" he said.

"Because it was true."

"That's not an answer. You could have let them hang me with it. One anonymous letter, one nervous board—you'd have been rid of me. No more corridor ambushes. No more—" he gestured vaguely at the space between us, which contained, unbeknownst to him, several hundred pages of correspondence, "—whatever this is."

I looked at the harbor. There was a boat out past the breakwater, small and stubborn, going somewhere against the wind.

"Because whoever wrote that letter," I said, "wanted you destroyed by reputation. Quietly. Without having to stand in a room and watch it happen." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "I know that method. I invented it. I'm not going to stand in a courtyard and benefit from it a second time."

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain dripped off the eaves, counting seconds.

"You're a strange man, Marchetti."

"I'm frequently told. Usually by critics."

"And the documentation. You'd burn yourself giving it to the board. Your name attached to more wreckage."

"It's already attached. I'm just choosing the wreckage now." I pulled my collar up. "Consider us temporarily aligned. Against Corvus, against the vote, against whatever anonymous correspondent fancies himself a critic. Temporarily."

"Temporarily," he agreed, and there was—God help me—almost humor in it, dry as driftwood. Then, as I turned to go: "Marchetti."

I stopped. Didn't turn. The word *Wren* sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the apology exists. I've never gotten one either. But I'm told they're real."

I walked back to my cottage in the dark and took out the unsent letter I'd been drafting for a week—the one that began *Dear Wren, I have to tell you something about a night in March*—and I added a line at the bottom, in the margin, where confessions hide:

*Today I defended a man I've spent months despising, and it was the easiest honest thing I've done all year. I'm starting to suspect the letters aren't the only place I've changed.*

I sealed it. I addressed it. I put it in the outgoing tray at the office, where Ines collects the program mail every morning at eight.

And then I stood in my kitchen with my heart going like a timpani line, realizing what I had just done: mailed the truth to a man who hates Elias Marchetti, signed by Wren, and there is no version of that envelope that survives contact with the morning.

The ferry comes Thursday. The letter will arrive Wednesday.

I have three days to decide whether to warn him, confess in person, or stand on the dock like an idiot and watch him read it in the wind.

I have never once, in my life, chosen the brave option early. But the tide, as they say, is turning, and I am beginning to suspect it intends to turn whether I'm ready or not.
