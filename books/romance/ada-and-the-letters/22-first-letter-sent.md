## 22. First Letter Sent

### Casper

The post office on Halloway Island opened at nine, and at 8:47 I was standing outside it like a teenager waiting for a concert, a letter burning a hole in my coat pocket.

Ten years I'd carried an unsent letter in some form or another—first the one about the fire, folded and refolded until the creases went soft as cloth, then the apology I never found the courage to say aloud, then all the ordinary sentences that piled up behind it like snow against a fence. Ada had heard the big confession on the dock in October. She'd kissed me back with the last ferry idling twenty feet away, its horn blowing like it disapproved of romance conducted on county property. But there was still one letter left in me, and I had learned something this year: unsent letters are just debts you keep paying interest on.

So I wrote a new one.

It wasn't much to look at. One page, my handwriting slanting downhill the way it always did when I hurried, which was always. It said:

*Ada —*

*I've been thinking about what my grandmother would do with two names on a deed and no one to explain them, and I think she'd laugh at us for taking four seasons to get here. So here is my invitation, formal as I know how to make it: stay. Not because the deed says so. Because I want you to. Plant your name in the ground next to mine. Teach me how you prune. Argue with me about the cider press. Be here in March when the mud comes, and in July when the wasps get ambitious, and in every season after that until we're two old women arguing about whose turn it is to fix the fence.*

*The orchard is yours. So am I, if you'll have me.*

*Casper*

I'd rewritten it eleven times. The first draft sounded like a legal brief. The fourth sounded like a eulogy. The ninth had a joke about goats that didn't land even in my own head. This one—the eleventh—was the truest thing I could manage, which is the only standard that matters.

The postmistress, Mrs. Okonkwo, took the envelope from me and weighed it on her little brass scale as though it might contain contraband.

"Local delivery?" she asked.

"Next room over," I said. "Roughly."

She looked at me over her glasses with the particular patience of a woman who has watched an island's entire romantic history pass across her counter. "You know," she said, stamping it with a thump, "in forty years of running this office, I have mailed exactly three letters addressed to people living under the same roof. All three were love letters. All three writers were terrified."

"Were they worth it?"

"They married each other," she said. "Two of them, anyway. The third couple runs the bait shop." She slid the letter into the outgoing bin, where it landed face-up, my handwriting exposed to the fluorescent light like a suspect in a lineup. "There. Committed."

It was 9:04 in the morning. The mail truck wouldn't run until afternoon. Which meant I had approximately six hours to walk home, act natural, and wait for my own letter to arrive—a delay I had not thought through and now had to live with.

I bought a cinnamon roll from the bakery next door to steady my nerves. It did not help. Nothing helps when you've put your whole heart in a mailbox and the mailbox operates on island time.

* * *

### Ada

Casper came home smelling of cinnamon and guilt, which on this island is the smell of someone who has done something brave and regrets it immediately.

I knew something was up the way you know weather is coming—not from any single sign but from the whole pressure of the morning. She'd been up before dawn. I'd heard her at the kitchen table, the scratch of her pen, the small groan she makes when she crosses out a whole line. She writes the way she prunes: too much at first, then ruthless, then a long anxious hovering over what remains.

"Cinnamon roll?" she offered, holding out the bag like a peace treaty.

"You went to town at dawn for pastry."

"I went to town at dawn for *reasons*. The pastry was incidental."

I let it go. On this island, letting things go is a survival skill; everything arrives eventually if you don't chase it—the ferry, the rain, the truth.

We spent the morning in the south block, winter pruning. The trees stood bare and patient in the cold, their cuts from last month already healed to dark knots. Casper has gotten good at this—her saw strokes clean, her angles right—and watching her work still does something to me I haven't found the right word for. It isn't just pride, though it's that too. It's the sight of a person becoming permanent somewhere. I spent ten years being temporary everywhere. Watching Casper belong to this ground, cut by cut, taught me what belonging looks like from the outside. Now I'm learning what it feels like from the inside, and it turns out it feels like cold hands and a warm chest and someone calling across the rows, "Ada, does this limb go or does it *go*?"

"It goes," I called back. "Everything on that tree goes. You're supposed to be thinning, not negotiating."

"I'm building consensus."

"With a tree."

"Trees respect process."

I laughed hard enough to startle a crow out of the hedge. This is the other thing about belonging: it makes you ridiculous, and you stop minding.

At noon the mail truck rattled past the gate and stopped. We both watched it. Only afterward did I notice that Casper watched it the way you watch a doctor walk toward you with results.

* * *

### Casper

Mrs. Okonkwo, traitor and saint, had apparently decided my six hours of suffering were excessive. The letter arrived at 12:15, hand-delivered by the mail carrier, Rune, who read the address aloud in a delighted voice—"Ada Lindqvist, c/o the orchard, *next door to sender*"—before handing it over with a grin I could have fried an egg on.

Ada took it on the porch steps. I sat down beside her, close enough that our shoulders touched, and pretended to be fascinated by the bare apple rows while she opened it.

I will not pretend I wasn't terrified. Ten years ago I failed to send a letter and lost a decade. The stakes felt exactly that high again, except this time the fear had joy underneath it instead of grief, and joy is somehow harder to sit still inside.

She read it once fast. Then again slow. Her thumb moved along the edge of the page the way it does when she's keeping herself from crying, which she'd deny under oath.

"The cider press argument," she said finally, "is not hypothetical. You load the rack wrong every single time."

"That's what you're responding to?"

"I'm getting there." She folded the letter with great care, tucked it into her coat pocket—over her heart, I noticed, though I had the grace not to say so—and looked out at the orchard, all those bare trees standing in the cold light like an audience waiting. Then she stood up, went inside, and shut the door.

For one horrible minute I thought I'd misjudged everything. Then I heard the scratch of a pen.

* * *

### Ada

He asked for an answer in writing. Very well. Some things deserve ink.

I sat at the kitchen table—the same table where we'd nearly broken each other open last autumn, where the truth about the letters and the fire and the money had all come out in pieces, sharp and necessary—and I wrote:

*Casper —*

*Your grandmother used to sign her letters "Bramble." I asked her once why, and she said a bramble is a plant that survives anything and gives fruit anyway, and that she intended to be one. I have thought about that a great deal this year.*

*Here is my answer: yes.*

*Yes to March mud and July wasps. Yes to the fence, which will be your turn more often than mine because I will cheat at assigning chores. Yes to the cider press, where I will continue to supervise and you will continue to pretend you don't need supervising. Yes to planting new trees over the old foundation come spring—six of them, one for each letter I kept, and one extra for the letter you never sent, which I forgive completely and intend to tease you about for fifty years.*

*Fifty years is my counteroffer. Take it or leave it.*

*Yours —*
*Ada*

*P.S. The deed amendment goes through Thursday. Two names. I checked twice. Mrs. Okonkwo showed me the filing with what I can only describe as ceremony.*

I sealed it, walked twelve feet down the hall, and knocked on the door of the room Casper used as a study, mostly for storing seed catalogs and avoiding things.

"It's for you," I said through the wood. "Urgent delivery."

The door opened. She looked at the envelope, then at me, then at the envelope again, doing the arithmetic of the hallway.

"You could have just said it."

"Some things deserve ink," I said. "You taught me that."

She opened it right there in the doorway, leaning against the frame, and I watched her read her future. When she got to *fifty years*, her breath caught—that small audible hitch I first heard on the dock in October, the sound of a woman letting herself hope out loud.

"Fifty," she repeated. "That's a long contract."

"I'm told you honor contracts. Even the ones with two names."

She laughed, and then she wasn't laughing anymore, she was kissing me in the doorway with the letter crushed gently between us, and somewhere outside a crow complained and the wind moved through the bare rows, and the house around us did what houses do when they are finally full: it held everything.

* * *

### Casper

Later—much later, when the light had gone gold and low—we planted the letter.

This was Ada's idea, naturally. She keeps rituals the way I keep receipts. She took my invitation and her answer, folded them together into a jar with a handful of orchard soil, and buried it at the base of the youngest tree in the south block, the sapling we'd set over the burned foundation last spring, already knee-high and stubborn.

"So in fifty years," she said, patting the earth flat, "somebody digs it up and finds out we were ridiculous."

"We're not telling anybody where it is."

"We're absolutely telling everybody where it is. That's half the fun. Mystery requires witnesses."

We walked back to the house as the light failed, our boots printing side by side down the row, and I thought about my grandmother signing her letters *Bramble*, surviving everything, giving fruit anyway. She'd known, I think. Sitting alone in this kitchen with her pen and her conscience, writing checks disguised as correspondence, she'd known exactly what she was planting. Two names on a deed. A decade of patience. An inheritance measured not in acres but in the specific miracle of two guarded hearts finally, foolishly, saying yes.

Inside, the kettle went on. Ada argued with the wood stove and won. I got out two cups, the chipped ones, the ones we always reach for.

On the table between them, I left a blank sheet of paper and a pen.

"What's this?" she asked.

"First line of next year's letter," I said. "Might as well get a head start. I hear the writer takes forever."

She picked up the pen. She wrote one word, turned it toward me, and smiled.

*Stay,* it said.

Which was easy. I was already home.
