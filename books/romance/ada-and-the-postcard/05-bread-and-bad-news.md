## 5. Bread and Bad News

The last loaves came out at four, and by six I was scraping the trays and pretending the ache in my shoulders was satisfaction instead of dread. Closing time had its own liturgy: chairs up, cases wiped, till counted twice because Mama taught me that a woman who counts once is hoping and a woman who counts twice is knowing. The register said what it always said this season—not enough.

I was elbow-deep in the sink when the bell over the door gave its half-hearted jangle, the one that meant someone had pushed it but not come in. I dried my hands on my apron out of habit, though everything in this shop ended up floury anyway, and looked toward the doorway.

Emrys Almeida stood there like a man who'd forgotten why doors exist.

They were still in their field clothes—khakis salt-stiff at the cuffs, that faded green shirt with the torn pocket they always wore like a uniform—and they were soaked through, though it hadn't rained since morning. Sea spray, maybe. Or they'd stood at the tideline too long. Their face had the particular blankness of someone who has been crying or is about to be and has decided neither is acceptable.

"Shop's closing," I said, which was a lie, because the OPEN sign was still lit and we both knew it.

"I know." Their voice came out scraped thin. "I just—" They stopped. Looked at the floorboards like they might apologize to them personally. "Sorry."

Now here's a thing about me: I don't ask questions I'm not ready to hear answers to, and I could see from across the room that whatever answer lived behind Emrys's face was not a small one. It sat on them the way grief sits on people who've carried it so long they've stopped noticing the weight until they set it down somewhere and realize how heavy it actually was.

So I didn't ask. I pointed the wooden spoon at the corner table, the one with the wobbly leg I'd shimmed with a folded menu three years ago.

"Sit," I said. "You look like a woman who hasn't eaten since Tuesday."

"It's Thursday."

"Then you're overdue."

* * *

I didn't ask questions while I cooked, either. That's the covenant of my kitchen, learned from watching Mama feed half the fishermen on this coast without ever once asking why Roderick Pike drank or why the Okafor boy's mother left or why old Sam Trench ate his breakfast standing up like he expected to be called away mid-meal. You feed first. Questions keep. Some questions, if you let them sit long enough in good bread and hot soup, answer themselves.

I had day-old sourdough, which is nearly as good as fresh for toasting, and a pot of the fish stew I make on Thursdays because the boats come in Wednesday and Marcus Hale saves me the heads and trimmings nobody else wants. There were greens from the box Mrs. Adeyemi leaves on my back step, wilting but proud. I put all of it in front of Emrys with more food than one person should eat and less ceremony than one person needs.

"Eat," I said. "All of it. I'll be insulted otherwise, and you don't want that on your conscience along with whatever else you're carrying."

Something flickered across their face—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if the roads were open. They picked up the spoon.

I went back to my wiping-down, keeping my back to them mostly, giving them the privacy of not being watched while they ate. That's another thing about feeding people: sometimes the kindest thing you can do is pretend you're busy.

"You didn't have to do this," they said finally, when the bowl was half empty and their voice had found some of its floor again.

"Mama used to say hunger makes liars of us all. Can't have you lying to me, Almeida. Bad for the neighborhood."

"What did she say made truth, then?"

"Bread," I said. "Bread and time. Everything else is negotiable."

Behind me, quiet as tide coming in, Emrys laughed. Just once, short, surprised out of them. I kept my eyes on the counter and let them have it without making it a whole event.

* * *

We'd been circling each other for months, Emrys and me. I want to be honest about that, because everyone in this town will tell you I'm friendly, and I am, but friendly isn't the same as known. Emrys rented my cottage—the little one above the bakery, the one Mama used to call the birdhouse—and paid late more often than not, and I teased them about it, and they took the teasing the way a person takes warmth when they're not sure they deserve it: carefully, with both hands, checking for traps.

I'd watch them from my kitchen window some mornings, down at the seawall before dawn with their clipboard and their counting. Counting turtles. Counting nests. Counting anything that couldn't count them back. There's a way some people have of measuring the world so they never have to be measured by it, and I recognized it because I'd invented half its techniques myself. My version was flour. Theirs was data. Same wall, different bricks.

And I'd catch them watching the shop, too. Standing at the seawall at odd hours, looking at my windows like the light inside was something happening to other people. I used to wave. They'd raise a hand, that minimal acknowledgment, and then be gone.

So when they showed up soaked and hollow-eyed at my door, I understood something without needing it explained: whatever had broken open today had been waiting behind that wall a long time.

* * *

They finished the stew. They ate the bread I toasted and buttered too heavily on purpose, because heavy butter is how I say things I can't say out loud. And then they sat there with their hands wrapped around the empty mug of tea I'd pressed on them, and I saw them deciding something—weighing whether to speak, testing the weight of it.

Whatever they decided, they kept. But their shoulders came down an inch, and their breathing changed, and when they looked up at me there was something in their face that wasn't gratitude exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of one drowning person noting where another one's head breaks the water.

"Thank you," they said. "For not asking."

"I'll ask eventually," I warned them. "I'm patient, not polite."

"I know." And then, quieter, almost to the mug: "That's what scares me."

I laughed at that, properly, and something in the room loosened. They helped me stack the chairs without being asked, which told me more about them than any confession would have. People show you who they are in how they treat a stack of chairs.

It was full dark when they left, and I watched them cross the lot to the outside stairs of the cottage, moving slow, pausing at the bottom step to look back at the shop. At me, through the glass. I raised my hand this time, and they held it a beat longer than necessary before climbing into the dark.

* * *

I found the letter the next morning, pinned to the door with a brass tack driven clean through the paper, deliberate as a threat.

Halloran Development Group, the letterhead said, all sharp serif fonts and money-green accents. A rendering of my building—my mother's building—redrawn in glass and pale wood, with a terrace where the ovens were and a wine bar where the counter was. RE: NOTICE OF INTENT TO PURCHASE AND DEVELOP. The word "legacy" appeared twice, both times about their own company.

I read it standing in the doorway with the dawn coming up gray behind me and the smell of yesterday's bread still in the walls, and I felt something in my chest go very quiet and very hard, the way it does right before a storm decides.

Ninety days. They were offering ninety days' courtesy, as if courtesy were something you could measure in days against fifteen years of my mother's hands in this dough.

I pinned the letter to the corkboard behind the register, next to Mama's photograph, where she could look at it. Then I tied on my apron, fired the ovens, and opened the door, because the fishermen would be in at seven and the world doesn't stop for bad news—it just gets fed anyway.

But I'll tell you what I thought about, all that morning, while my hands worked and my mind ran: I thought about Emrys Almeida sitting in my corner chair, soaked through, hollowed out, eating my bread like it was the first warm thing they'd touched in years. Two of us now, carrying letters we didn't ask for. Two of us, guarded as fortresses, recognizing the architecture.

And I wondered—kneading, shaping, watching the seawall through the window for a figure with a clipboard—what kind of letter had done that to them. What news, delivered eleven years late or eleven minutes early, could leave a person standing in a doorway with no reason left to come inside.

Because I'd seen the postcard-shaped hole in them. I just hadn't seen the postcard yet.

And I had no idea that by week's end, the two fights—mine and theirs—would turn out to be the same fight, wearing different clothes.
