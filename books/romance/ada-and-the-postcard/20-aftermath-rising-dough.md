## 20. Aftermath, Rising Dough

The news came in the way news came to Salt Hollow: sideways, on foot, through the door of the bakery before the bread had even cooled.

Priya from the hardware store arrived first, still in her slippers, holding her phone out in front of her like a lantern. "It's everywhere," she said. "Rask. Suspended. Pending inquiry. The university pulled his funding page down overnight—someone screenshotted it before they did, look, it's already gone."

Ada wiped her hands on her apron and looked at the screen without touching it. There he was, Dr. Peter Rask, in a photograph she now understood had been taken at the height of his glory—tanned, silver at the temples, standing in front of a research vessel with the easy smile of a man who had never once had to pay for anything. Below the photograph, the headline did what headlines do, which was arrive a decade late and act like they'd been on time all along.

*Marine biologist suspended pending investigation into falsified data; whistleblower testimony prompts formal review.*

Whistleblower testimony. Ada's chest did something complicated. She thought of Mrs. Okafor in her small neat kitchen two towns over, her hands folded on the table, her voice steady as she told the room—first Ada, then Emrys, then, finally, the hearing itself—what she had been paid to forget. *I signed my name to numbers I knew were wrong, and I let a young person carry the blame because they told me my pension would vanish if I didn't.* She had wept only once, at the end, and she had wept for Emrys, not for herself.

"They're saying it might go to a full ethics board," Priya said. "Criminal referral, even. And Halloran—" she scrolled, triumphant, "—Halloran Development has formally withdrawn its purchase offer on the harborfront parcel. Withdrawn! There's a statement. Something about 'reassessing community sentiment.' Which is corporate for *we got caught standing next to the wrong man in a photograph.*"

Ada laughed. It came out of her like something uncorked, surprising them both, and then Priya was laughing too, and then the bell over the door rang and Deacon the fisherman came in with his cap in his hands like a man arriving at church, and by seven-thirty the bakery was fuller than it had been on any morning since her mother was alive, and nobody was really buying anything so much as they were gathering, the way people gather where the good news is, the way gulls gather where the fish are.

Ada moved through it all in a kind of daze, pulling trays, pouring coffee, letting the tide of voices wash over her. Someone had brought a radio. Someone else had brought a bottle of something that was absolutely not appropriate for seven-thirty in the morning and was absolutely being poured into coffee anyway. The word *co-op* was already in the air, passed around like a recipe—*the town should buy it, we should buy the building ourselves, there's a fund, the fishing association has money set aside, my cousin does this kind of thing in Maine*—and every time Ada heard it she had to stop what she was doing and press the heel of her hand briefly against her sternum, the way you do when your heart is trying to leave without you.

Her mother had built this place out of nothing. Out of a secondhand oven and a recipe box and a stubbornness that the whole town had learned to rely on the way you rely on a lighthouse. Ada had spent three years watching it slip toward the water's edge of ruin—rising rent, failing equipment, a developer's offer letter pinned to her own door like a threat—and now, in one morning, the water had gone out and the ground was firm and everyone she loved was standing on it.

She looked up once, mid-morning, and found Emrys in the doorway.

They hadn't come in. They were standing just outside, in the light, in their field jacket with the salt-stained cuffs, holding a paper bag of what Ada knew would be the first of the morning's rolls, purchased quietly at some point when she hadn't been looking, and they were watching the room with an expression Ada had never seen on them before. It took her a moment to name it.

Relief. Not their own—*for* someone else's. Emrys Almeida, who had spent fifteen years perfecting the art of not being in rooms, was standing in a doorway looking at a room full of people celebrating their vindication, and the look on their face was pure, unguarded gladness.

Ada pointed a floury finger at them. "You. Inside. Now."

Emrys shook their head, smiling. "I'm on my way to the survey."

"It's the day after the hearing. The turtles can wait one morning."

"The turtles," Emrys said, "do not know about the hearing." But they came in anyway. They came in, and Priya made room at the counter without being asked, and Deacon clapped them on the shoulder hard enough to stagger a smaller person, and someone poured something into Emrys's coffee that made them cough, and Emrys stayed. For two hours, in the middle of a crowd, Emrys stayed.

Ada watched them from behind the counter and thought: *this is what it looks like when someone comes all the way back from somewhere far away.*

* * *

By afternoon the celebration had burned down to embers and the bakery smelled of cinnamon and exhaustion. Ada sent the last of the stragglers home, flipped the sign, and started on the ledger out of pure habit—her mother's ledger, the same green cloth-bound book her mother had kept, because some inheritances you don't get to put down even when you want to.

That was when she found the envelope.

It was tucked into the ledger's back pocket, where her mother used to keep receipts, and it had no name on it, just a single line in Emrys's small, precise, tide-chart handwriting: *For the building. Before anyone else asks you to sign anything.*

Ada's hands went still.

Inside the envelope was a bank draft. She looked at the number twice, three times, because the first two times her eyes had refused to arrange the digits into anything she recognized. It was not a contribution. It was not a generous gesture. It was, as far as she could tell from the figure and from the fact that Emrys Almeida lived in a rented cottage above her shop and drove a fifteen-year-old truck with a rusted bed, *everything*.

She sat down on the flour sack by the window, which was not designed for sitting and did not care.

She thought about the hearing. She thought about Emrys standing up in front of the packed council chamber with Marguerite Voss's notebook in their hands, their voice shaking only at the very beginning and then not at all, telling fifteen years of truth in eleven minutes while the whole town leaned forward like a single animal. She thought about the journalist Okafor had called, and the way Rask had come apart—not all at once, but the way bad structures come apart, one load-bearing lie at a time, each question from the crowd pulling out another brick until the man who remained was small and damp and finished. She thought about the settlement that must be coming—lawyers had already called, twice, using words like *restitution* and *wrongful termination* and *we can make them pay for the decade*—and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the number in the envelope was the number the lawyers had told Emrys to expect. Given away in advance. On faith. Before the check had even cleared.

*Before anyone else asks you to sign anything.*

Ada pressed the envelope flat against her chest and sat in the empty bakery and cried, briefly and without ceremony, the way you cry when you've been holding a heavy thing for so long that setting it down hurts more than holding it.

Then she got up, washed her face, and went to find Emrys.

* * *

She found them at the seawall, of course. Where else. The evening tide was coming in and Emrys sat on the low stone wall with a clipboard on their knees, not writing anything, just watching the water with the particular stillness that Ada had once mistaken for distance and now understood was the opposite—a person so full of attention that they had to aim it at something that couldn't be overwhelmed by it.

Ada sat down beside them without asking. She put the envelope on the stone between them, face down, like a card she was refusing to play.

"You went through my ledger," Emrys said. Not accusing. Almost pleased.

"You put money in my ledger."

"I put money in an envelope. You did the going-through."

Ada looked at them. The light was doing that thing it did in the last hour of the day, turning the harbor to hammered copper, and Emrys's face was half gold and half shadow and entirely, infuriatingly calm.

"That's your settlement," Ada said. "That's not even the settlement. That's the *estimate*. Nell told me the lawyers said the full restitution could take years to fight through. And you've signed it away to a building. To a *building*, Emrys. You could have a life on that money. You could go anywhere. You could—"

"I had a life on that money," Emrys said. "It was the life I had before. It's the price of the one I had before. I don't want it." They turned the clipboard over, then back. "Ada. Fifteen years ago, a man took my name off my own work and the whole world believed him, because the whole world had never met me and had met him at a conference. And the reason it worked—the reason it worked *so well*—is that I had made myself easy to erase. No partner. No community. No one whose day would be ruined by my disappearance. I was a name on a paper and nothing under it." They looked at her then, and Ada felt it go through her like current. "I am not doing that again. I am not building my life somewhere I can be erased from. If the bakery goes, I go. If the town goes, I go. So the money isn't a gift. It's an anchor."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. The tide filled the silence, patient, professional.

"You could have told me," she said finally. "You could have asked. That's—" and here her voice did something she hadn't authorized, went rough, "—that's the whole point of what you said at the hearing, Emrys. You stood up in front of everyone and said the truth out loud, and it was the bravest thing I've ever seen, and then you snuck around behind my back with an envelope because you still think you have to buy your way into being allowed to stay."

Emrys flinched. Actually flinched, visibly, like a person touched on an old burn.

"I know," they said.

"You *know*."

"I know." They set the clipboard down on the stones. "I've spent fifteen years learning that help is a transaction and love is a liability and the only safe way to care about anything is to do it from far enough away that nobody notices. You don't unlearn that in one hearing. But I'm—" they exhaled, and Ada watched them choose the next words with the care of someone defusing something, "—I'm trying to unlearn it *out loud*, where you can see me getting it wrong. That's the best I have today. Getting it wrong where you can see."

Ada looked at the envelope between them. Then she picked it up, and Emrys's whole body went tense, and she understood in one bright flash that they thought she was going to hand it back.

She didn't. She tucked it into her apron pocket.

"We're going to do this properly," she said. "Tomorrow. There's a meeting—Deacon's cousin from Maine, the co-op lawyer, the fishing association, the whole ridiculous committee that formed itself in my bakery this morning like it had been waiting years for permission. And you're going to be there. Not as a donor. As a *signatory*. Your name on the deed next to mine, next to Deacon's, next to Priya's, next to Mrs. Okafor's, who called me an hour ago to ask, and I quote, 'how much for a window with my name on it.' We are going to own this building together, all of us, in broad daylight, on paper, where nobody can erase anybody." She stood up, brushed off her apron, and looked down at Emrys, who was looking up at her with an expression she was going to be thinking about for a long time. "And Emrys? Next time you decide to give away everything you have—ask me first. Not because I get a vote. Because I want to be there when you do it."

Emrys nodded. Then, quietly, with the ghost of a smile: "For the record, I was going to tell you. I just wanted the draft to clear first. I didn't want you to feel obligated by a number that might not exist."

"That," said Ada, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me, and I hate it."

Emrys laughed—a real laugh, unpracticed, startled out of them—and Ada walked home along the seawall with the envelope warm in her pocket and the tide coming in behind her, and above the bakery the light was on in the cottage window, and for the first time in three years the number she was afraid of wasn't the one on a rent notice.

It was the number of days until the co-op papers were signed. She intended to make it very small.

* * *

The committee met the next morning at the big corner table, which had never once in its life held anything but flour sacks and was now buried under printouts, and Deacon's cousin from Maine—a brisk, sunburned woman named Marta who talked like a manila folder—laid it all out: shares, bylaws, a lease structure that would keep the bakery a bakery in perpetuity, no developer, no eviction, no single person able to sell it out from under the rest.

"And the anchor investment," Marta said, "the one that makes the bank take us seriously—"

"Is already pledged," Ada said, before anyone else could. She caught Emrys's eye across the table. "In broad daylight. On the record. Ask them."

Emrys, to their credit, didn't look away. "Everything I have," they said simply. "It's not charity. It's where I live."

Marta wrote it down. Priya whooped. Mrs. Okafor, who had driven up for the occasion in a hat that meant business, patted Emrys's hand and said, "Good. Now the rest of you match me," and put her own check on the table with a click like a rifle bolt.

Ada was still basking in it—still standing in the middle of the warm, loud, impossible room she had spent three years trying not to lose—when the bell over the door rang, and Nell Voss came in out of the bright morning with a cardboard box in her arms and an expression on her face that stopped the room cold.

She set the box on the corner table, right on top of the printouts, and looked at Emrys.

"There's more," Nell said. "Marguerite kept more. And Emrys—some of it's addressed to you."
