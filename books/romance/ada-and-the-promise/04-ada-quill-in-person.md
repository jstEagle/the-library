## 4. Ada Quill, In Person

The bread was the whole plan, and the plan had taken me three days.

Not the baking—baking was easy, was muscle memory from a life I'd mostly used up in other cities, other kitchens, other years I no longer counted as mine. No, what took three days was the standing at my own door with the loaf wrapped in a tea towel, listening to the sounds of the flat next door: the particular creak of floorboards that meant he'd crossed to the window, the kettle's rising note, and once—briefly, unbearably—the low hum of a cello string being tested and abandoned, like a man clearing his throat and then deciding against speech.

Three days of listening through a wall to a man who didn't know I could hear him. There was a word for that. Several words, actually, and most of them unflattering.

But here is what you have to understand about knowing someone for ten years through an envelope: you learn their silences before their sentences. Ines Hartwell wrote to me the way some people pray—not asking for things so much as arranging his interior life on paper where someone might witness it. He told me about his hands. He told me about his father's funeral, which he'd attended in a grey suit and felt nothing, and then wept in the car park afterward at the sight of a stranger eating an apple. He told me, in letter forty-something or maybe fifty, that if I ever stood in front of him he would probably not recognize me, because he had built his entire self around the assumption that I was a fiction he'd invented to survive being alone—and that this was fine, he said, this was even good, because fictions never leave.

I had underlined that sentence. I had written *we'll see* in the margin, in pencil, in 2019, in a rented room in Lisbon with rain coming down like a verdict.

And now here I was, three floors up from the street, one thin wall from the man himself, holding bread like a peace offering to a war he didn't know we were fighting.

My cane clicked against the doorframe when I knocked. I'd practiced that too—practiced not flinching at the sound of it, practiced making it part of me instead of an announcement. The leg had other opinions. The leg had been staging its slow mutiny for two years now, and it chose that exact moment to send a spark of white fire up from ankle to hip, so that when the door opened I was already half-smiling, the way you do when pain arrives and you've decided not to give it the room.

He opened the door the way a man opens a door when he's been dreading exactly this for seventy-two hours.

I know, because I'd watched him dread it. Through the peephole of my own imagination, anyway—I'd seen him hesitate in the stairwell two days ago, seen him take in the boxes and the cane and the laugh (my laugh; I'd heard myself laugh at the delivery man's joke about the piano, and I'd felt the sound travel up the stairwell like smoke), and then I'd watched him retreat up the steps with the particular speed of a man fleeing a crime scene. His crime. Or mine. We hadn't sorted out whose yet.

"Ines Hartwell," I said.

Not a question. That was the first mistake, or the first confession, depending on how you wanted to read it, and God knows I'd read it both ways by the time the kettle boiled. A stranger would have said *hi, I'm your new neighbour*. A stranger would not have said his name like that—like the opening bar of something they'd rehearsed.

He went very still. It was extraordinary to watch, actually, this stillness arriving on him like weather. I had imagined his face for ten years—had built him out of adjectives, out of the way he described light on water and the specific shame of dropped bows—and now the real one stood in front of me, younger than I'd decided and more tired, with flour on his sleeve (he baked too; of course he did; we were going to be insufferable) and eyes that did something complicated: widened, narrowed, and then performed a kind of deliberate blankness that fooled absolutely no one, least of all me, because I had read four hundred pages of that blankness translated into ink.

"Hello," he said. "You must be—the new neighbour."

"I'm Ada." I held out the bread. "Ada Quill. I moved in next door. I brought bread, because my mother raised me to believe that moving into a building without bringing bread is a form of trespass."

He took the loaf. Our fingers didn't touch—he made sure of that, with a precision that would have been funny if it hadn't hurt quite so much—and he looked down at the tea towel as if it might contain instructions.

"That's—" He stopped. Started again. "Thank you. That's very kind."

*Very kind.* Ten years of letters and the first thing he ever said to my face was *very kind*, in the voice of a man reading a script written by someone who had never met either of us. I almost laughed. I almost cried. I did neither, because I'd promised myself on the train north that I would be patient, that I would let him arrive at me at his own speed, that the worst thing I could do—the cruelest thing, the thing that would break this before it began—was to say *it's me* too soon, and watch him bolt.

So instead I said: "Your stairwell has excellent acoustics. I noticed that about the building. You could hold concerts on the landing."

Something flickered across his face. Something with teeth.

"I don't play," he said.

"No?" I tilted my head. "The moving men said the neighbour plays cello. They said they heard it through the walls last time they worked this building."

"The last tenant played." His voice had gone smooth and closed, a door pulled shut. "I teach. And repair. It's different."

"It's always different," I agreed solemnly, "from the inside."

And there it was again—that flicker, that almost-recognition, the sense that he was hearing an echo and couldn't place the room it came from. Because he knew this. He knew this exact rhythm, this way I had of agreeing in order to disagree, of circling a subject like a cat deciding whether to sit on the newspaper. He had told me once, in a letter I could recite from memory, that talking to me felt like *being read aloud to by someone who occasionally writes in the margins.*

"You'll have to tell me if I'm too loud," I said, nodding at my flat. "I unpack at strange hours. I talk to myself. I've been told I sing while I cook, badly, and with conviction."

"I'll keep it in mind."

"And if you hear thumping—" I tapped my cane against my calf, lightly, making a joke of the thing that wasn't a joke, watching him look at the cane and then away from it with a courtesy so careful it was nearly tender, "—that's just me losing arguments with furniture. I win eventually. It's a long campaign."

He smiled. It was small and it cost him something—you could see the cost, see the muscles around his mouth working against their own training—but it arrived, and it was aimed at me, and I thought: *ten years, and there it is.*

"I should let you get back to unpacking," he said.

"Probably." I didn't move. Neither did he. We stood in his doorway in a silence that had a shape, a familiar shape, the shape of every pause in every letter where one of us had almost said the true thing and chosen, instead, to describe the weather.

"Well," I said finally. "Neighbour."

"Neighbour," he said.

I turned for the three steps to my own door, and I let the cane announce me, click, click, click, and behind me I heard him start to close his door and then stop, and I heard him stand there—I would have bet money on it—with the bread in his hands, looking at the tea towel, thinking.

Thinking: *where do I know her from.*

That was the gift I'd come to give him, actually. Not the bread. The question.

* * *

Here is what I had not planned for: how much it would cost, standing in that doorway, to not say it.

I'd spent a decade being brave on paper. Paper is forgiving. Paper lets you revise. On paper, I had told Ines Hartwell things I had never told a living soul—about the diagnosis that didn't exist yet but whose shadow I'd grown up inside, my mother's hands going wrong in her forties like instruments detuning, the family curse we called *the weather* because saying its name felt like inviting it in. On paper I had confessed that I was afraid of stairs now, of ice, of kerbs, of the small treacheries of a body renegotiating its contract. On paper I had written, once, at three in the morning in a hospital waiting room in another city: *if I ever meet you, I will probably lie to you for the first hour, out of habit, and then tell you everything, out of terror that there won't be a second hour.*

I had meant it as a joke. Most of my truest sentences wear joke costumes.

Now the second hour was ticking, and I had lied through the entire first one, and the lie sat in my chest like swallowed stone. *I don't play,* he'd said, and I had let him. *The new neighbour,* I'd said, and he had let me. Two liars in a stairwell, each holding the other's secret like a hot dish passed between gloved hands, each pretending the gloves were for politeness.

I unpacked the kitchen first. I always do—Marisol says it's because I'm a control freak, and Marisol is right, and I love her for saying it to my face, which is why she got the bookshop recommendation and I got the flat above the bakery, which smells, at five every morning, like the inside of a promise.

The kitchen took an hour. My hands took longer than they used to; the left one especially, the left one with its new habit of dropping things without warning, keys, pens, once a full mug of coffee that shattered and made me sit on the floor of a rented flat in Bristol and laugh until I cried, alone, at the sheer theatrical timing of it. I put the mugs away carefully. I put the pills away more carefully—in the tea tin, the blue one, the one that looked like it held Earl Grey and nothing else, because a tin is a kind of privacy and I had learned to hoard privacy the way other people hoard money.

Then I sat by the window with tea I couldn't taste properly anymore—one of the smaller betrayals, taste going patchy at the edges like old wallpaper—and I listened to the wall.

He was moving around over there. Restless. I could track him by the floorboards: window, table, window, the closet where the cello lived (I refused to believe it lived anywhere else; a man does not say *the last tenant played* in that voice unless the cello is six feet away and he can feel it breathing). Once, a long pause directly on the other side of the wall from my chair, and I had the mad, wonderful, terrible certainty that he was standing there listening back.

I raised my mug to the wall.

Somewhere in the flat next door, a floorboard creaked, very quietly, like a held breath released.

* * *

He knocked on my door at nine the next morning. I know because I was awake—I was always awake by six, the leg saw to that, mornings were its favourite hour for presenting its itemized complaints—and because I heard him hesitate outside my door for a full ninety seconds first. Ninety seconds. I counted. I lay in bed with my tea going cold and listened to a man work up the courage to return a tea towel, and I fell a little bit in love with the hesitation itself, with the care of it, with the fact that after ten years of writing to me at midnight with his whole heart spilled out like a broken jewellery box, he still approached my actual door like it might be wired.

When I opened it, he was holding the towel folded into a square so precise it could have passed inspection at a military academy, and the bread, sliced, arranged on a plate, with butter beside it in a small dish.

"You returned it with interest," I said.

"It seemed—" He looked at the plate as if surprised to find it in his own hands. "It seemed rude otherwise. To just—keep the towel."

"Ines." I took the plate. "It's a tea towel. If you'd kept it I'd have assumed you liked it and bought six more."

He almost laughed. I saw it happen and fail—saw the laugh rise in him and get intercepted somewhere around the collarbone, processed, denied exit. Three years, I thought. Whatever happened to him, whatever he'd told me in fragments across a hundred letters and withheld in a hundred more, it had taught him to intercept his own laughter at the border. I wanted to build him a customs office where laughter got through free.

"Did you sleep?" I asked, because it was the smallest true question available.

"Enough." Which from him meant no. "Did you?"

"Like a stone," I lied brightly. "A stone with strong opinions about mattresses."

He looked at me then—a real look, direct, searching, the look of a man comparing a face against a memory that shouldn't exist—and for one suspended second I thought he was going to say it. I thought: *here it comes, the stairwell confession, the moment the letters become flesh and we both have to live in the same country at last.* My heart did something undignified. My hands, traitors to the end, tightened on the plate.

"You'll come to Sunday lunch," he said.

I blinked. "Will I?"

"Marisol—the bookshop, on the corner, she does a lunch. Sundays. Tomás comes, and Priya, my student. It's—" He paused, visibly editing himself, choosing between versions of the truth. "It's loud. And there are too many opinions about composers. But there's food, and nobody will ask you questions about your leg, and—" he stopped, hearing himself, appalled at himself, "—I mean. If you want. Neighbours come. Sometimes."

"Sometimes," I repeated, savouring it.

"If they're invited," he amended, with dignity.

"Then yes." I handed back the empty plate and kept the smile steady even though something in my chest had cracked open along a seam I'd thought was sealed. Sunday lunch. An audience of strangers who would become, if I had anything to say about it—and I did, I had spent a year of planning and a lifetime of stubbornness ensuring that I did—his family. Mine too. Time was a currency I'd stopped pretending I had much of, and I intended to spend every coin loudly. "Sunday lunch. I'll bring bread. Apparently that's my whole personality now."

"It's a good personality," he said, and then went scarlet, visibly horrified at having said a whole sentence with warmth in it, and retreated down the stairs with the plate held out in front of him like a shield.

I closed my door and leaned against it and laughed—really laughed, the laugh the delivery man had heard, the laugh that travelled—and I pressed my palm flat against the wall, the shared wall, the thin plaster membrane between his life and mine.

*I know it's you,* I said silently to the wall, to him, to the decade. *I know you know. Take your time. I brought time. It's the one thing I came with plenty of.*

Which was, of course, the second lie I'd told him since arriving.

The tea tin sat on the shelf where I'd placed it, blue and innocent, full of pills and countdown. I looked at it for a long moment. Then I put the kettle on, and sang while I waited—badly, and with conviction—and through the wall, faintly, unmistakably, I heard a single cello string sound and die, like a man testing whether his voice still worked.

It did. It does. Sunday, I thought. Hold that thought until Sunday.
