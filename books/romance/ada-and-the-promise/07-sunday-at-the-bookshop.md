## 7. Sunday at the Bookshop

The knock came at nine on Sunday morning, which was, by Ines's reckoning, a violation of at least three of his rules.

Rule one: no visitors before noon. Rule two: no plans made for him by other people. Rule three—unwritten but load-bearing—no Ada Quill before he had finished his coffee and rehearsed the particular flatness of voice that kept conversations from becoming anything else.

He opened the door anyway. He would spend years afterward wondering why.

She stood in the stairwell with her cane hooked over one arm and a scarf wound twice around her neck, though it was only October and the building's radiators ran hot enough to bake bread. Her cheeks were pink from the climb. She looked, he thought, like someone who had already won an argument he hadn't heard yet.

"Wear the grey coat," she said. "Not the brown one. The brown one makes you look like you're attending your own funeral."

"I'm not going anywhere."

"You are. Marisol's bookshop. There's a thing on Sundays."

"There is no 'thing.'"

"There is absolutely a thing," Ada said. "Tomás brings biscuits. Priya argues about composers. Marisol pretends to referee and secretly keeps score. I've been three times and they've asked about you all three times, which is more interest than anyone has shown in you since—" she paused, delicately, "—well. Since ever, probably."

"I don't do groups."

"I know." She said it without mockery, which was somehow worse. "That's why we're going. You can stand near the door. You can leave whenever you want. I'll even walk you home if it gets bad. But you're coming, because I promised them, and because—" here she did something dangerous, which was to look directly at him, her eyes the colour of strong tea, unblinking, "—because you've spent long enough being nobody's, Ines. One Sunday. That's the toll."

He should have said no. He had a whole architecture of no built over ten years, buttressed and inspected, load-tested against exactly this kind of weather. Instead he heard himself say, "The grey coat is in the closet behind the winter boots," and watched her smile like a woman who had just been handed keys.

* * *

Marisol's bookshop occupied the ground floor of a corner building on Vell Street, wedged between a shuttered tailor's and a café that changed names every eight months. The window was crowded with paperbacks arranged less by genre than by mood—Ada explained this on the walk over, narrating like a tour guide who had fallen in love with the country. *There's the grief shelf. There's the shelf for people who've just quit their jobs. That one's for heartbreak, though Marisol rotates it seasonally so regulars don't get embarrassed.*

A brass bell announced them, and the smell hit him first: old paper and wood polish and, underneath it, coffee and something baked. The shop was deeper than its front suggested, aisles narrowing toward a back room whose doorway hung with a curtain of mismatched beads. Chairs were visible through the beads—maybe eight of them, arranged in a lopsided circle.

"Ines!" A woman emerged from behind the counter with the speed of someone who had been waiting all morning. She was perhaps fifty-five, silver-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils, reading glasses pushed up onto her head like a second pair of eyes. She took his hand before he could offer it. "Marisol Vega. Finally. Ada talks about you the way other people talk about weather in countries they miss."

"Marisol," Ada said warningly.

"What? It's true." Marisol didn't let go of his hand; she turned it over briefly, glancing at the calluses on his fingertips with the frank appraisal of someone cataloguing. "Cellist hands. Good. We need someone to settle arguments. Come through, come through, everyone's early except Tomás, and Tomás is never late, he's just ferry-time."

"Ferry-time?" Ines managed.

"Forty years on the water," Marisol said, as if that explained everything, and perhaps it did. "You'll see."

The back room was smaller than it had looked from the front—low ceiling, a radiator ticking under the window, shelves of overflow books leaning against every wall like guests who'd stayed too long. Two people were already seated. One was unmistakably Priya: fifteen, spine straight, a canvas bag of sheet music at her feet, wearing the expression of a person prepared to be disappointed by adults. The other was an enormous man folded into a small chair with the ease of long practice—seventy if he was a day, white beard trimmed short, hands like mooring rope resting on his knees.

"Ah," said the enormous man, rising with surprising grace. "The neighbour." He extended a hand that swallowed Ines's whole. "Tomás Ferreira. You're taller than she said. Not better-looking than she said, mind. Just taller."

"Tomás," Ada said.

"Accurate reporting is a public service."

Ines found himself steered into a chair between Priya and the biscuit tin. The circle closed around him with the gentle, terrifying efficiency of water finding its level. He recognized the geometry instantly—he had spent three years avoiding exactly this shape, this arrangement of faces angled inward, this implicit contract that everyone present had agreed to be seen.

"Before we start," Marisol said, settling into her chair with a battered paperback, "Priya has a complaint."

"I always have a complaint." Priya crossed her arms. "Last week Tomás said Brahms was 'a heavy man who wrote heavy music,' which is not criticism, it's a horoscope."

"It *is* heavy music," Tomás said placidly, opening the biscuit tin. "Like a ship in ballast. Beautiful, but you feel the weight."

"That's not—you can't just—" Priya appealed to the room. "This is why we need a musician. An actual musician. Not a ferryman with opinions."

"He played accordion on the night boats," Marisol offered.

"That makes it worse!"

Ines became aware, with a sensation like vertigo, that the argument had opened a space for him—that four faces had turned, casually, without pressure, toward the gap where his opinion belonged. Nobody demanded it. They simply left the door open, the way you leave a door open for a cat.

"Brahms is heavy," he heard himself say, "but the weight is the point. He doesn't apologize for it."

Silence. Then Priya sat forward, eyes narrowing with the particular intensity she reserved for his lessons, and Tomás pointed a biscuit at him and said, "There. Ballast," and Marisol wrote something in the margin of her paperback, and Ada caught his eye across the circle and mouthed, *told you*, and Ines understood that he had just lost a war he hadn't known was being fought.

* * *

The Sunday circle, he learned over the next hour, had no agenda, no name, and no rules except attendance. Marisol chose a book each month—not always a good one, she admitted freely; last month's had been "an act of vandalism against the English language"—and they read aloud, taking turns, arguing in the margins. Today's book was a slim novel about a lighthouse keeper's daughter, and when Marisol handed it to Ines and said, "Your turn, page forty," his stomach dropped with a familiarity so old it felt architectural.

Reading aloud. Being heard. His voice leaving his body and entering other people's ears, where it could be judged, misjudged, remembered.

"Page forty," Marisol repeated, kindly, holding the book out.

He took it. His hands did not shake—they had stopped shaking for small audiences years ago; it was large ones that broke him—but his throat tightened around the first sentence like a fist closing. He read. He kept his eyes on the page, on the lighthouse keeper's daughter and her inventory of storms, and somewhere in the third paragraph he noticed that no one was watching him critically. Tomás had closed his eyes. Priya was following along in her own copy, lips moving slightly. Ada was looking out the window at the rain starting against the glass, listening the way she listened to everything—completely, without performance of her own.

His voice steadied. By the end of the page it was almost warm.

"Well," said Tomás, when Marisol tapped the table for the handover. "Ferry-voice. My father had it. The men who read to the night crossings—there was a rule, you know, someone always read on the late boat. Kept the nervous ones calm." He nodded at Ines. "You've got the register for it."

"I haven't read aloud since school," Ines said.

"Then you're overdue," said Marisol, and moved on as though the matter were settled, and the terrible thing—the thing he would turn over later, walking home—was how little it had cost him. How the room had simply absorbed his voice and given it back unchanged.

* * *

They did not release him at the end. This was the trap he saw too late: the reading ended, chairs scraped, coats were gathered, and then Marisol said, "Stay for soup, I've got too much again," and Tomás said, "I'll carry the chairs," and Priya said, "Can we ask him about the Elgar now?" with the directness of a teenager who has identified the room's one locked door.

"The Elgar," Ines said carefully.

"Priya's obsessed," Marisol said, ladling soup into mismatched bowls at a side table. "She's been after me to get a recording for the shop."

"It's not for the shop, it's for *me*, there's a difference—" Priya turned to him. "You teach it, don't you? Or you used to. Ada said—"

"Ada says a lot of things," Ines said, too quickly.

The room did something subtle. Nothing stopped, exactly—the soup continued, Tomás continued stacking chairs—but a kind of attention shifted, the way wind changes direction before you can name the weather. Ada met his eyes across the table with an expression he couldn't read: apology, or warning, or both.

"I played it once," Ines said. "Publicly. A long time ago." He picked up his spoon. "It didn't go well."

"Define didn't go well," said Priya, who had not yet learned, at fifteen, that some doors stay locked for reasons.

"Priya," Ada said softly.

"No, but—musically. Was it the tempo? Because everyone rushes the adagio, my last teacher said the adagio is where cellists go to lie about their feelings—"

"*Priya.*"

"It's fine," Ines heard himself say, and was surprised to find it was nearly true. "It wasn't the tempo." He looked down at the soup—lentil, too much cumin, good—and said the sentence he had not said aloud in three years, in a voice so level it surprised him: "I stopped in the middle of the first movement. In front of eleven hundred people. And I walked off, and I never went back."

The silence held for exactly as long as it needed to. Then Tomás, without ceremony, passed him the bread.

"Eat," Tomás said. "Then, if you want, tell us about the boat. Every man who's walked off a boat has a story about the water, and none of them are as simple as they look. But eat first. Nothing true gets said hungry."

And that was the moment, Ines thought later—the bread, the absurdity of it, the enormous retired ferryman treating his catastrophe like a common ailment with a common remedy. Not pity. Not the careful hush he had dreaded for three years, the lowered voices, the *of course you never went back*. Just bread, and the assumption that he would still be here in five minutes, and ten, and next Sunday.

He ate. He told them a little—not the whole architecture, not the mentor, not the nineteen-year-old's letter burning in memory, but the shape of it. The hall. The freeze. The corridor afterward, and the specific quality of silence that follows a man who has failed publicly. Priya listened with her chin on her fists. Marisol refilled his bowl without asking. Ada watched him with an expression he refused, for his own safety, to interpret.

When they finally spilled out into the street at four o'clock—the rain cleared, the light going amber—Priya fell into step beside him and said, "Next week you're reading page sixty-one. I checked. It's the storm chapter."

"Is that a request?"

"It's a schedule." She adjusted her bag on her shoulder. "Also, the adagio isn't where cellists lie about their feelings. That's the cadenza. The adagio is where they tell the truth and hope nobody notices." She said it matter-of-factly, the way she said everything, and then turned off toward her street without waiting for a response.

Ada walked beside him the rest of the way, hands in her pockets, cane tapping a slow rhythm on the pavement. She didn't gloat. He noticed that. She had won, completely and utterly, and she didn't say a word about it until they reached their building, and then only:

"Same time next Sunday?"

He should have hedged. He should have invoked a rule, cited a prior obligation, performed the small bureaucratic evasions that had protected him for three years. Instead he stood on the bottom stair with the taste of Marisol's soup still in his mouth and Tomás's bread in his coat pocket—someone had pressed it on him at the door, wrapped in a napkin, no discussion—and said:

"Page sixty-one."

"Yes."

"And Priya will argue about the adagio."

"Guaranteed."

"Fine," Ines said, and climbed the stairs, and was halfway to his door before he understood what had happened, which was that he had made a plan for next Sunday, and that the plan involved people, and that some part of him—some part he had believed amputated years ago—had already begun counting the days.

Behind him, Ada hummed something under her breath as she unlocked her own door. It took him a moment, standing in his darkening flat with the grey coat still on, to place it: the opening bars of the Elgar, slow and patient, like a promise being kept in another room.

He did not sleep well that night. But it was not the old insomnia, the grinding vigilance. It was something closer to anticipation, which frightened him more.

On Monday morning he found a note slid under his door, in handwriting he knew better than his own:

*Tomás wants to know if you'll bring the cello sometime. No pressure. He just thinks a room full of books deserves music. — A*

Ines held the note for a long time. Then he put it in the drawer with the others—the growing drawer, the drawer that violated every rule—and told himself, firmly, that the answer was no.

He spent the rest of the day composing the letter explaining why.
