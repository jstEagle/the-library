## 13. A Decade of Tuesdays

It happened on a Tuesday, because everything between us had always happened on Tuesdays, though I did not know that yet when I climbed her stairs with two cups of tea and the particular dread of a man about to open a door he has spent ten years leaning against.

The rain had come in off the river around four o'clock and settled in for the evening, that fine grey drizzle that makes the whole street sound like paper being turned. Ada's window was lit amber against it. I could see her silhouette through the curtain—she was doing something with her hands raised, slow and deliberate, and I stood on the landing like an idiot for a full minute trying to work out what it was before I understood: she was conducting. No music playing. Just her, keeping time with something only she could hear.

I knocked.

"Come in, it's open," she called, and then, as I pushed the door with my elbow because both hands were occupied by tea, "Ines Hartwell. You knock like a man serving a subpoena."

"You said come in."

"I said come in. I didn't say you'd look so guilty about it." She was wrapped in a blanket the colour of storm clouds, her cane hooked over the arm of the chair, and she'd cleared the table—actually cleared it, wiped it down, set out two chairs facing each other instead of the usual arrangement where her chair sat at an angle to mine like we were both waiting for someone else to arrive. Two chairs, face to face. An interrogation setup. Or a confession booth.

I put the teas down. Chamomile for her—she'd told me once, in a letter dated the eleventh of March some seven years ago, that chamomile tasted like "a lullaby that gave up halfway through," which meant she loved it—and the black tea I drank myself, strong enough to stand a spoon in.

"So," she said.

"So."

"We should probably talk about it."

"I've been drafting something in my head for three weeks," I admitted. "It has footnotes."

Ada laughed—that laugh, the one I had known for a decade in ink before I ever heard it aloud, the laugh that arrived in my life first as a description of itself, *I am laughing now, Ines, the ugly honking kind, you'd disown me*—and she pulled the blanket tighter. "Footnotes. God. Of course you have footnotes. Sit down. Read them to me."

And that is how it began—not with the grand accounting I had rehearsed in the shower, not with the letter I had burned in chapter after chapter of my own imagination, but with two people who had written each other ten years of truth sitting face to face and discovering they were shy.

* * *

The parcel had been misdirected. That was the beginning of it, though neither of us had ever laid out the whole sequence in one place; our history existed the way a coastline exists, visible only in sections.

"It was a Tuesday," Ada said, "in October. I was twenty-two and living above a laundrette in—" she waved a hand vaguely northward, "—that city whose name I refuse to say because the flat had mould and the landlord had a ferret. I'd ordered sheet music. A Rota transcription, if you can believe it. And what arrived instead was a cello bow."

My cup stopped halfway to my mouth. "That was yours?"

"That was *yours*. Well—the sender's address was yours. Yours was the return address, smudged, half legible. Some shop had swapped the labels or the courier had a stroke of genius, I don't know. But there was this bow, this beautiful stupid bow, and a note in your handwriting—I still have the note—saying, *For the student, tell him the frog is weighted for a heavier hand than his, he'll grow into it.*"

"I remember that bow." I said it quietly, because remembering it hurt in an old familiar way. "I was teaching then. My second year. The boy's name was Daniel. He snapped two bows before I found one heavy enough to survive him."

"And I wrote to the return address to say, *You have sent me a bow and no cello, and I feel this is a metaphor for something.*" She smiled at the table. "And you wrote back."

"I wrote back." Ten days later. I remembered that too—the delay, the terror of writing to a stranger, the strange safety of it. A person with no face could not be disappointed in you. A person across a postal distance could not sit in a hall and watch your hands fail.

"For ten years," Ada said, and her voice did something complicated, went light in a way I recognized as effortful. "Tuesdays. It wasn't even deliberate at first. You wrote on Sundays, I noticed, and I answered on Tuesdays, and then it became law. Unwritten law. The best kind."

"You moved eleven times."

"Twelve. You never knew about Hull."

"Hull," I repeated, tasting the word.

"Six months. Terrible. There was a flood. Anyway—twelve moves, and the letters always found me, because I always left a forwarding address with the post office and nowhere else. Do you understand what that means, Ines? I let the postal service know where I was and no one else. Not my sister. Not my doctors." She said *doctors* the way you might say *weather*, lightly, already moving past it, and I felt the old cold touch the back of my neck and chose—as I had chosen now a dozen times—not to follow it. "The letters were the fixed point. Everything else was weather."

I looked at her. Really looked, the way I was learning to do, which was its own discipline, harder than scales. Her face in the lamplight, the fine map of lines around her eyes that no letter had prepared me for because letters don't carry faces. Ten years of her handwriting—her handwriting I could have identified blind, the way the *f* descended like a hook, the way she crossed her *t*s with a small violent flourish—and I had never once seen her cross a *t* until six weeks ago.

"The silence," I said. "The eighteen months."

Her expression flickered. "You want to talk about the silence."

"I've never wanted to talk about anything less."

"Then why bring it up?"

"Because it's the part I'm ashamed of." The words came out flat and true, the way things do when you stop polishing them. "You disappeared for eighteen months and I told myself you'd died, and then I told myself you'd married, and then I told yourself—told *myself*—that you'd finally realized I was not worth the postage. And I wrote to you anyway. Every Sunday. Into the silence. For eighteen months I mailed letters to a void, and I need you to tell me why you were gone, because I have built seventeen theories and all of them are load-bearing."

Ada was quiet for a long moment. The rain filled the room. Somewhere below us, Tomás's television murmured its evening murmur, the ferryman's eternal soundtrack.

"I was sick," she said at last. Simply. The way you'd report a delayed train. "Not—don't do the face. Not the way you're thinking. I was sick in the way that required hospitals, plural, and a great deal of boring time in corridors, and I decided that if I told you, you would become a hospital correspondent instead of a friend. You'd write me get-well letters. And I couldn't bear—" her hand made a small fierce gesture, cutting the air, "—I could not *bear* for you to become careful with me. You were the one person in the world who wasn't careful with me. Do you know how rare that is? Everyone else tiptoes. You wrote me furious letters about Shostakovich. You insulted my taste in poets. You were never once gentle, and gentleness is what everyone does to you when you're ill—they start handling you like a message they're afraid to drop."

"So you vanished."

"So I vanished. And when I came back I wrote you a letter full of lies about a cottage and bad weather, and you believed me, because you wanted to believe me, because the alternative was asking questions, and asking questions means risking the answer." She looked up at me, and her eyes were bright and dry and terrible. "We are both very good at not asking questions, Ines. It's practically our national sport."

I thought of a tea tin. I thought of bruises explained by ice. I thought of a cane laughed away. I sat with all of it in my chest like swallowed stones and said nothing, and hated the nothing, and kept it anyway.

"Tell me about your silences too," she said, gentler now, tilting her head. "There were gaps on your side. 2019. Four months, wasn't it? I counted."

"You counted."

"I count everything. Four months, no letters. I nearly sent a search party. I nearly came here, actually, that time. I had the address. I stood outside a travel agency for twenty minutes."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because the promise was the same room *someday*, and someday shouldn't be rushed." She paused. "Why the four months?"

"My father died." I hadn't said it aloud in years. It came out rusty. "In April. I found out the letters had become—everything I said to him I was saying to you instead. All the things sons don't say to fathers, I'd been mailing to a stranger every week. And when he died I couldn't write to you anymore, because you were the place where I kept him, and the place was wrecked."

Ada reached across the table. She didn't take my hand—she took my teacup, refilled it from the pot, and set it back in front of me, and somehow that was the same thing, better than the same thing. "You grieved at me for four months by not writing," she said. "That's the most Ines sentence I've ever heard."

* * *

We talked until the tea went cold twice and the rain stopped and started again. We excavated the decade like archaeologists, laying artifacts on the table between us: the letter where she'd convinced me, briefly, that she was a man named Arthur (a joke that had run eight weeks and cost me genuine anguish); the letter where I'd told her about the stage and she had answered with such fury on my behalf that I'd read it forty times and then hidden it under a floorboard, which is where it remained; the letter where she described the sea in winter and I had gone, alone, in February, just to check whether the sea was as she said, and it was, and I had stood there like a pilgrim and told no one.

But somewhere past midnight, the talking ran out. Not the truth—there was plenty of truth left—but the speaking of it. Our mouths were clumsy. Ten years of practice had gone into ink, and ink was where we lived.

It was Ada who solved it. She got up, rummaged in the drawer by her bed, and came back with a pad of paper and a pen, which she set on the table between us with the ceremony of a duelist laying down gloves.

"Write it," she said.

"Write what?"

"Whatever you just failed to say with your mouth. The thing you were circling for the last hour. Write it. I'll answer in kind. We'll pass it back and forth." She was already smiling, tired and triumphant. "Some truths are easier in the letter-voice. We both know that. So let's use it. Right here. Across twelve inches of table instead of however many miles."

I picked up the pen. My hand, which had shaken over a broken E string in front of thirty parents, shook now over a blank page in front of one woman, and I understood these were the same tremor, the same fear wearing different clothes.

I wrote: *I spent ten years being brave on paper and a coward everywhere else. I don't know how to be the person from the letters in a room.*

I slid it across. She read it without touching it, lips moving slightly, and then she took the pen and wrote beneath, unhurried, her hook-tailed *f*s marching across the page:

*Good. Because I didn't fall in love with the room-version either. I fell in love with the pen. The room-version can catch up.*

I read it three times. The word *love* sat there in her handwriting, unhidden, unqualified, and she watched me read it and did not flinch and did not explain it away, and I felt something in my chest crack along a line that had been forming for ten years.

*I'm terrified,* I wrote next. Honest, at least.

*Naturally,* she wrote back. *Do it anyway. That's the whole religion, Ines. That's the entire liturgy.*

* * *

The notes across the stairwell started three days later, and they spread through the building like a benign infection.

It began innocently—a scrap of paper tucked into my door: *Your floorboards squeak in E-flat. Fix it or transpose.* I answered on the back: *The building is in tune. Your hearing is sentimental.* By Friday there was a note taped to the stairwell rail at exactly hand height, and by the following week Marisol had seen them and begun leaving contributions—book recommendations folded into origami cranes—and Tomás, who claimed not to understand what was happening, nevertheless started carrying a pencil in his cap specifically for the purpose.

We developed conventions. Notes left on the door meant ordinary business. Notes on the stairwell rail were for anyone to read—a public channel, absurdly, our private language posted in a communal space. And notes slid under the door after midnight were the third category, the deep water, the ones we didn't discuss in daylight.

Tuesday nights became theirs. I would hear her cane on the stairs at nine—slow, measured, the rhythm I had memorized without meaning to—and a note would appear, and we would write back and forth until eleven, sometimes about music, sometimes about nothing, once for forty minutes about whether a specific cloud had been shaped like a cello or a loaf of bread (she maintained bread; I maintain, to this day, cello).

Priya caught us at it once, coming up the stairs with her case, and read the rail note upside down with the speed of the young. "*'Debussy is just impressionism for people afraid of feelings'*—who wrote this?" She looked between us. "This is you two. You're doing a correspondence. In the same building. Face to face. This is the most ridiculous thing I've ever seen and also I'm going to cry."

"It's efficient," Ada said.

"It's insane," said Priya, taking a photograph of it.

But that was the shape of it. Two people who had learned each other entirely in ink, now learning each other's faces—learning that Ada's silences had a texture (she pressed her tongue to her back teeth when she was deciding whether to lie); learning that my own face, apparently, broadcast everything (*"You're composing an objection," she'd say, "your left eyebrow is drafting it"*). The letters had given us each other's souls and skipped the bodies entirely, and now the bodies had catching up to do, and the bodies were slower, clumsier, more frightening, and infinitely more worth it.

One night in late November I found a note on my door that was different from the others. Longer. Written in her careful hand, the formal hand, the letter-voice:

*Ines. I have been thinking about the day we met—you, nineteen, mailing your first reply to a stranger, hands probably shaking. I have been thinking about the boy who wrote me that he wanted "to be someone who stays in rooms." I want to know everything about that boy. Not the version you've edited. The original manuscript. Someday soon. —A*

I stood in my doorway holding it for a long time. Below me, her light went out. The stairwell smelled of rain and Tomás's pipe smoke and, faintly, of the bread Ada baked on Sundays.

*Someday soon,* she had written. And I understood, standing there in the dark with ten years of Tuesdays in my hands, that she was asking for the story I had never told anyone entire—the story of the final performance, of the hall full of critics, of the mentor's voice and the walk offstage and the long burial of a life. The original manuscript. The wound, unedited.

She had given me her secret's shadow already; I had seen the tin, said nothing, chosen blindness. Fair was fair. Fair was terrifying.

I got out my good pen—the one I used for letters, the one that deserved them—and a clean sheet of paper, and I sat down at my kitchen table, and I began to write: *Dear A. Before I can tell you about the performance, I have to tell you about my father, and about Aldous Vane, and about the difference between failing and being failed...*

Outside, the rain started again, patient as ever, turning the street to paper.
