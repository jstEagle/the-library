## 17. The Argument About Snow

The snow came in the night, the first real snow of the year, and by morning the street had gone soft and white and treacherous, the kind of snow that lies about itself—looks like a blanket, behaves like glass. Ines stood at his kitchen window with his coffee going cold and watched Ada come out of the building below, cane in one hand, a baguette tucked under her arm like she was carrying it home from some small victory, and he watched her foot find the ice under the fresh fall.

He watched her go down.

It happened fast—the way these things always happen fast, no time for the body to negotiate with gravity—and then she was on her back in the middle of the pavement, baguette pointing at the sky like an accusation, and for three full seconds she did not move. Three seconds is nothing. Three seconds is also a very long time when you are watching through glass you cannot break quickly enough.

By the time he got down the stairs and out the door, she was already up. She had righted herself with the cane planted like a flagpole, and she was brushing snow off her coat with brisk little swats, and she was laughing.

"Ice," she said, before he'd even reached her. "The city should be sued. Are there laws about this? There should be laws."

"Are you hurt?"

"My dignity is concussed." She held up the baguette, miraculously intact, like evidence for the defence. "The bread survived. That's what matters."

Her wrist was cradled against her ribs. He saw it—he was a cellist, he had spent thirty years reading bodies the way other people read weather, and he saw the way she held the left hand slightly apart from herself, protective, the way you hold a hand that has been slammed in something. And he saw, above the collar of her scarf, the bruise. Not new. Yellowing at the edges, greenish at the centre, the colour of a bruise that was days old and had been hidden under scarves all week.

"You're holding your wrist," he said.

"I'm holding my bread." She smiled at him, and it was a good smile, it had always been a good smile, and for ten years he had trusted that smile across distance and paper where trust cost nothing. "Ines. I fell on ice. It's January. This is not a mystery novel."

* * *

He thought about it all day. That was the trouble with him, he knew—thinking was the thing he did instead of speaking, thinking was where everything went to ferment. He taught his afternoon lessons with half his mind, watched Priya's bow hand drift flat and corrected it without hearing himself correct it, and all the while the morning replayed: the three seconds of stillness, the wrist, the bruise the colour of old leaves.

There were other things too, now that he let himself line them up. The way she took the stairs slowly and blamed the moving boxes she no longer had. The way she sometimes sat down mid-sentence, casually, as though chairs were simply interesting to her. The pills he had glimpsed once, weeks ago, in her bathroom when he'd returned a casserole dish—amber bottles, plural, labels turned away, and how she had appeared in the doorway a moment later saying something about the plumbing, and how he had not asked, because they had rules, because asking was a door he had agreed not to open.

Rules. He had made them himself, in those first weeks, terrified of the size of what was arriving next door. *Neighbours,* he had said. *Nothing more.* And she had agreed too easily, and he had spent months since then breaking his own rules in increments so small he could pretend each one was nothing—a dinner, a Sunday, a note slid across the stairwell in the letter-voice he had sworn off, her laugh coming through the wall at night like a radio station broadcasting from a country he used to live in.

But the rules had never covered this. The rules had never covered watching someone fall and being told it was nothing and knowing—it was knowledge, not suspicion, it sat in him with the cold weight of certainty—that it was not nothing.

At six o'clock he knocked on her door with a bag of salt for her steps, which was true, and which was also the flimsiest pretext he had ever carried anywhere.

She opened the door in socks, hair down, looking soft and unguarded in the lamplight, and for one stupid second he almost turned around and went home and salted his own steps and said nothing forever.

"Salt," he said. "For your stairs. Since the city refuses to be sued."

"You're an angel of municipal grievance." She stepped back to let him in, and he noticed she did it with her weight entirely on her right side, and that she moved her left hand to the wall as she turned, and that none of it looked like ice.

* * *

He put the salt on the shelf inside her door, and then he stood there in her hallway, in the smell of her—bergamot and pencil shavings and something warm underneath, bread or wool or just her—and he heard himself say, in a voice quieter than he intended:

"Show me your wrist."

The apartment went very still. Somewhere a radiator ticked. Ada's face did the thing he had learned it could do, the swift rearrangement, the curtain drawn and replaced with a brighter one—but he had read a thousand letters written in exactly that voice, the voice that jokes its way past the truth, and he was not nineteen anymore and he was not letting go of this.

"It's fine," she said. "It's a little sore. It's—"

"Ada." Her name came out of him like something he'd been carrying in his mouth for miles. "You didn't slip. I watched you. Your foot went because your foot doesn't answer you anymore. You stood still for three seconds before you moved. People who slip on ice get up angry. People whose legs stop working lie still and count."

She laughed. It was the wrong laugh, light and bright, thrown up like a flare to blind him. "You've been reading too many medical dramas. Next you'll tell me my pupils are dilated and I have six months to—"

"The bruise on your neck," he said. "It's four days old. You've been wearing scarves all week. In a heated building."

The laugh died. He watched it die, watched the brightness drain out of her face and leave something underneath that was older and tireder and frightened, and he understood, with a sick lurch, that he had never actually seen her frightened before—not in ten years of letters, not once, not even the winter she wrote about the bad year in second person as if it had happened to a stranger. She had shown him everything except this.

"Ines," she said, and her voice had changed registers entirely, dropped into something low and careful, the voice of a person standing at the edge of a thing. "Sit down."

"No." His heart was going hard. "No, you don't get to manage me. Sit down, drink tea, be calm—I know how this works, I've done it myself, I've spent three years managing everyone in a ten-mile radius so nobody would look too closely—" He stopped. Breathed. The words had come out faster than he meant, sharper, and he watched them land on her.

"So this is about you," she said quietly.

And that was unfair, and it landed anyway, because some part of it was true and they both knew it.

"No," he said. "Yes. Both. I don't—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "Ada, I have known you for ten years. Ten years. You are the only person who has ever known me without me having to stand up straight for it. And you move in next door, and you're dying—I don't know that, I don't know anything, but I know *something*, I've known something since the tea tin, since the pills, since the stairs, and every single day you hand me a smaller, kinder version of the truth and expect me to eat it and say thank you." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated the crack, hated that his body betrayed him the way it always had, in front of people, at the worst moments. "I am not an audience. I am not someone you perform for. That was the whole point of us. That was the entire point."

The silence after that was enormous. The radiator ticked. Snow ticked against the window in tiny dry grains. Ada stood with her hand on the back of a kitchen chair, and he watched her knuckles whiten on it, and he realized she was holding the chair because she needed to hold something, and that even now, even caught, even cornered, she was doing it gracefully, turning weakness into posture.

"You want the truth," she said.

"I've wanted the truth since October."

"The truth will change everything."

"The truth changed everything the day you moved in with a cane and a laugh I recognized through a stairwell," he said. "I've just been pretending otherwise. We've both been pretending otherwise. You're better at it than I am. You've had practice."

Something moved behind her eyes—hurt, he thought, real hurt, and beneath the hurt a kind of exhausted respect, the look of a chess player conceding that the board has finally been seen.

"You don't understand what you're asking," she said. "You think the truth is a door and we open it and walk through and it's sad but honest and we hold hands in the sad honesty. It isn't. It's a load-bearing wall. You take it out and the whole house comes down, and I built this house, Ines. I built it carefully. One year. I gave myself one year to be a person instead of a patient, and I chose you, do you understand? Out of everyone alive, I chose the person who knew me best, because I wanted to spend what I have being *known*—and instead I have spent three months being *watched*, and it turns out those are not the same thing, and I don't know how to be both."

"They're not different," he said. "That's what you don't—knowing someone means seeing them. All of it. You can't hand-select. You don't get to give me the letters and keep the diagnosis. They came from the same person. They're the same person."

"And if knowing me breaks you?" Her voice rose, finally, finally, the control splintering. "If I tell you, and you do what you do—you pack a bag, you find a train, you disappear into that beautiful disciplined silence of yours, and I spend my last year writing letters to a man who lives twelve feet away and won't answer? I have lost people to illness my whole life, Ines. Everyone leaves eventually; illness just makes the leaving punctual. But I cannot survive you leaving *early*. I would rather lie to you gently for a year than watch you flinch at me for six months. Do you hear me? I would rather—"

"Don't." He said it very quietly, and it stopped her, because he had never used that voice with her, the voice from the stage, the voice that carries. "Don't decide for me what I can survive. Everyone does that. My father did it. Vane did it. They all decided what I could carry and handed me the lighter version, and it nearly killed me anyway, because the lighter version is heavier when you have to carry it alone." He was shaking now, visibly, and he let himself shake. "I missed a train for you. I sat in your kitchen and made you eggs. I am not going anywhere. But I can't stay if staying means being managed. I won't be protected from you. I would rather be broken by the truth than kept comfortable by a lie, and if that makes me difficult, then I'm difficult, but Ada—*Ada*—I love you, and love that isn't allowed to see is just surveillance with better manners."

The word landed between them like a dropped glass.

He hadn't planned it. He had never said it aloud, not to her, not in any of the ten years of letters where they had said everything else. It hung in her kitchen among the bergamot and the ticking radiator, enormous and irreversible, and he watched her face do several things at once—break, and steady, and break differently.

"That's not fair," she whispered. "You can't say that in the middle of a fight. There are rules about that."

"We never had rules," he said. "We had a truce neither of us believed in. You told me so yourself, the day I set them. You agreed too easily."

She laughed then—wet, wrecked, nothing like the stairwell laugh—and pressed both hands over her face, and her shoulders shook, and he crossed the kitchen and put his arms around her, carefully, the way you hold an instrument you are afraid of damaging, and she let him, and for a long time neither of them said anything. The snow ticked at the window. Her heartbeat was fast against his chest, faster than it should have been, and he catalogued that too, helplessly, the way he catalogued everything now.

"If I tell you," she said into his shoulder, muffled, "you can't un-know it. And it changes the air in every room we're ever in together. Every breakfast. Every Sunday. You'll hear it under everything I say."

"Good," he said. "Then I'll finally know what we're talking about."

She pulled back far enough to look at him. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and fond, all at once, the way only hers could be. "You are the most infuriating man I have ever loved in print or in person."

"Ten years of letters," he said. "You knew that when you signed the lease."

"I did." She breathed out, long and unsteady, and looked toward the kitchen counter—to the tea tin sitting there beside the kettle, ordinary as salt—and he watched her arrive at a decision the way you watch weather change, the whole sky shifting at once. "Not tonight," she said. "Tomorrow. Tomorrow I'll tell you all of it, properly, beginning to end, no scarves, no smaller kinder versions. Tonight I need—" her voice wobbled, steadied— "tonight I need to make soup and pretend we fought about snow."

"We fought about snow," he agreed.

"We fought about snow," she said, "and you said a thing you can't take back, and I'm keeping it."

He stayed. He chopped onions badly while she directed operations from a chair, her left wrist resting in her lap, and they talked about nothing—Priya's recital, Tomás's ferry stories, whether Marisol's cat was plotting something—which was to say they talked about everything, around it, the way water talks around a stone. When he left, near midnight, she stood in her doorway and gripped his sleeve.

"Tomorrow," she said. "Come at ten. Bring nothing. Don't bring salt, don't bring bread, don't bring pretexts. Just come."

He walked up the one flight to his own flat and stood in the dark by the window, looking down at the street where she had fallen, at the snow already filling in the shape of it, erasing the evidence, keeping the secret a few hours longer. His hands, he noticed, were still trembling—not with fear, he realized, and the realization arrived like a key turning. With the specific tremor of a string tuned too long to a pitch it was never allowed to sound.

Tomorrow, she had said. And somewhere beneath the dread, beneath the grief already gathering in him like weather on a horizon, there was a terrible, quiet relief—the relief of a man who has carried a suspicion for months and is finally, tomorrow, going to be allowed to set it down and learn its true weight.

He did not sleep. At 3 a.m., through the wall, faintly, he heard her playing something—not cello, she had no cello—humming, low and unhurried, a tune he almost knew. He lay in the dark and listened until it stopped, and then he lay in the dark and waited for morning, the way you wait at a platform, watching the rails, knowing exactly which train is coming and unable to step back from the edge.
