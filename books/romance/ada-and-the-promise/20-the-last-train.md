## 20. The Last Train

The suitcase had lived on top of the wardrobe for three years, gathering the grey felt of disuse, and when I pulled it down at half past nine that night it shed a small snowfall of dust onto the bedspread, onto the folded shirts, onto my hands. I stood there with my arms full of my own escape and thought: this is the shape of me. This is what I do. I leave rooms before anyone can ask me to stay in them.

I had been packing for an hour without admitting it. First the practical things, which is how the lie always begins: the charger, the shaving kit, the spare strings in their paper envelopes. Then the books, because a man who is only going away for a few days does not need Rilke and does not need the score of the Elgar, and I had put both in. Then the letters. I stood over the tin box for a long time—longer than I stood over anything else—and in the end I did not take them. I told myself it was because they were hers as much as mine, that a decade of correspondence does not belong to the man who kept it any more than it belongs to the woman who wrote it. The truth was smaller and worse. I could not carry all that knowing onto a train. It would have been like travelling with a stone in my chest, and I needed, for eleven forty, to travel light.

The flat looked wrong with the wardrobe open. It looked like a mouth.

I worked in the kitchen after that, because my hands wanted occupation and my mind wanted silence, and the two are only ever reconciled at the sink. I washed cups that were already clean. I dried them. I put them away in the order I always put them away, handles to the left, and I listened to the building breathe around me—the radiator's tick, the pipes' long swallowed gurgle, and above it all, through the wall, the small sounds of Ada's flat, which I had learned the way you learn a piece of music: not the notes themselves but the phrasing. The tap. The slow drag of her cane across the floorboards, a rhythm I could set a metronome by, three beats and a rest, three beats and a rest. She was awake. She was almost always awake at this hour, and I had come to think of those overlapping insomnias as a kind of duet, two people keeping each other company through plaster and brick without a single note exchanged.

Tonight the duet was unbearable.

Because I knew now. I had opened the tea tin that afternoon—sugar, I had told myself, I am returning sugar, and the sugar had taken four minutes and the tin had taken four seconds—and I had seen what was inside it, and then I had sat in her kitchen chair with the pills in my hand while she stood at her counter with her back to me and told me everything in a voice that never once broke, which was somehow more devastating than breaking would have been. Degenerative. Progressive. The words doctors use when they mean *there is no version of this that ends well*. She had a year, she had said. She had promised herself a year. She had less than that now, probably, though she said it the way you mention weather, the way you say *it may rain later* about a sky that is already black.

And she had come here. To this street, this building, this flat with the bad radiator and the neighbour who did not play his cello. She had spent what she had of her strength on moving boxes and a new city and a stranger's name spoken aloud for the first time, and the stranger was me, and I had stood in her kitchen with her dying in my hands like something warm and weightless, and I had done the thing I always do.

I had said, *I need some air.*

* * *

Here is what fear does to me. It does not make me run, not at first. It makes me tidy.

I folded shirts with the corners aligned. I rolled the socks the way my father taught me, tight as knots, because loose socks in a suitcase were, in his cosmology, a moral failing. I wrapped the cello—no. I did not take the cello. I looked at the closet where it lived and I closed the door on it, and the sound the door made was very soft and very final, and I thought: see, this is who you are, you are a man who packs a bag and leaves his cello in a closet, and at least you are honest about it now. At least you have stopped pretending the vow of invisibility was humility. It was never humility. It was a door held shut with my whole body, and tonight someone on the other side of it was dying, and I could not hold it anymore, so I was going to go through the other door instead, the one that has always been there, the one marked EXIT in letters only I can read.

The last train to my mother's town—or not to my mother's town, there was nothing there, that was the point, that was the whole architecture of the escape—left at 11:40. I knew this because I had looked it up at 9:15 with my heart going like a beginner's bow-hand, and the knowing had settled over the evening like frost. There was time. There was always time; that was the trick of it. Fear is a patient travelling companion. It lets you wash the cups.

I put on my coat. I took the bag down the stairs, taking care on the third step that creaks, absurdly, considerately, not wanting to wake her even in the act of abandoning her. The stairwell smelled of Ada's bread from two days ago and the cold that comes up from the street at this time of year, and under the stair the light was out again, the bulb that Marisol keeps saying she will replace, and I stood in that small darkness with my bag in my hand and let myself feel, for one full breath, how much of my life had happened in this stairwell. The bread. The notes. The night I carried her shopping up four flights and she laughed at the top and the laugh went through me like a tuning fork. The letter-voice, the paper-voice, the only voice I had ever trusted, and her, A, her, Ada, the same person, the person who had known me at nineteen and knew me now, and who was going to die, and who had chosen, with what remained of her choosing, to die next door to me.

And I was standing in the dark with a packed bag.

* * *

The station was eleven minutes' walk. I know because I have walked it in all weathers, in the years when walking at night was the only prayer I knew. Tonight the streets were rinsed with that thin silver light that comes before frost, and my breath went out of me in flags, and the town was so quiet I could hear the river, which I cannot usually hear, which I did not know I could hear from the high street, and I thought: I have lived here three years and I am only now hearing the river, and I am leaving tonight.

The platform was nearly empty. A woman with a trolley case asleep sitting up. A boy of maybe seventeen with headphones on, mouthing words to a song I couldn't hear, and something about the way he mouthed them—total, unguarded, a boy giving himself entirely to something no one else could hear—caught at me like a fishhook. The clock over the platform said 11:19. The board said the train was on time. On time. The words had a kind of menace. Everything in my life, I thought, has been on time. The failure arrived on time. The silence arrived on time. And now the train.

I sat on the bench furthest from the board and put the bag between my feet like a man guarding something precious, and what I was guarding, of course, was my own smallness. My right to be small. I have thought about this a great deal since—too much, Priya would say, you think too much, Mr. Halloran, just *play*—but that night on the platform I did not yet have the language for it. I only had the feeling, which was this: that leaving would be easy, and the easiness was the tell. Everything I had ever done to protect myself had been easy. Not playing was easy. Not loving was easy. Not opening the tin for three weeks, not asking, not seeing—easy, easy, easy, a staircase of easiness descending all the way down to a life with no one in it. And the hard thing, the single hard thing, the thing with weight and edges, was sitting four hundred metres away behind a wall I could not hear the river from.

The clock said 11:26.

And that was when I felt it—the particular wrongness of a door, the way a building tells you it has been entered. Except I was not in the building. I was on the platform, and the wrongness was in my coat pocket, where there had been nothing when I left, and there was now the small stiff rectangle of an envelope.

I took it out. My name on the front, in her handwriting, the handwriting I had known for ten years before I ever knew the hand that made it: the tall ascenders, the way she crosses her t's like someone signing a treaty. *Ines*, it said, nothing more. She had been in the stairwell. She had heard the third step creak and the wardrobe open and the bag come down—of course she had, she had heard me through a wall for months, she knew my sounds the way I knew hers, three beats and a rest—and she had not come to the door, had not made a scene, had not done any of the things I would have deserved. She had written a letter and slid it under my door and gone back to her kitchen to wait, or not to wait, because Ada did not wait for people, she only made room for them.

I did not open it at first. I held it and the clock said 11:29 and the rails sang their low cold note, the note they sing before a train, the note I have always privately thought of as the sound of the world deciding something.

Then I opened it.

*Ines. You've packed. I know because the third step told me, and because you have been washing cups for two hours, and you only wash cups when you are trying to wash something else.*

*I'm not going to stand in your doorway and argue. I promised myself I would never do that—not because I don't want to. God, how I want to. I want to stand in that doorway and be magnificent about it, I want to be the woman in the film who says the perfect thing at the perfect moment, and I have been drafting that speech since this afternoon and every draft is a lie, because the truth doesn't argue. The truth just sits down and stays.*

*So here is all of it, in the voice we made, the voice that never once lied to you: I am dying. You know this now; I watched you hold the tin. I have maybe a year, and I came here to spend it beside the one person who has known me since before I knew myself, and I knew when I came that this was a risk, that you might be exactly who you are. I took the risk anyway. I have very little left to spend and I spent it on you, and I would spend it again.*

*Here is the other thing, the thing I actually sat down to say. You think I need you to be brave. You think this is one more stage, one more audience, one more impossible standard, and that when you fail at staying I will look at you the way he looked at you, that night, with the terrible arithmetic of disappointment. I need you to hear me: I never asked you to be brave. I have read every word you ever sent me and not once, not in ten years, did I ask you to be brave. I asked you to be honest. I asked you to be curious. I asked you to write back. Bravery was never the assignment.*

*Stay. That's all. Stay, and be present, which is a thing you already know how to do—you do it with Priya's broken string, you do it with Marisol's terrible wine, you did it this afternoon when you held the tin and didn't look away. You are already the man who stays. You just haven't let him have the name yet.*

*The train leaves at 11:40. I know because you looked it up; you always look things up when you're frightened, it's your version of washing cups. And I want you to know that if you get on it, I will understand. I will understand completely, and I will leave the light on, and I will keep writing to you, because that is what we do, and I will not love you one degree less. That is not a bargain, Ines. It's just a fact about me.*

*But the chair in my kitchen is the comfortable one. And I make very good tea. And I am going to be awake for a while yet.*

*A.*

* * *

The clock said 11:36.

I read it twice. I read it a third time with my thumb over the last lines, the way you press on a bruise to be sure it's yours, and the rails were singing louder now, that rising note, and down the line there came a light—not the warm light of a town but the hard white eye of a train, and the woman with the trolley case woke and stood, and the boy with the headphones pulled one ear free, and everything on that platform oriented itself toward the future except me.

Here is what I had thought leaving would feel like. I had thought it would feel like the stage had felt, that night three years ago: the blackness coming up like water, the hall of faces turning to one face, the terrible silence where the music should have been. I had braced for that. I had packed for that. I had put Rilke in the bag to survive that.

But standing there with her letter in my hands, I found that leaving did not feel like drowning. It felt like the opposite. It felt like the silence after the last note of a piece you love—the held breath, the hall waiting, the moment that is not emptiness at all but the shape the music leaves behind. And I understood, with the train's light swelling in my eyes, that if I stepped through those doors I would be walking out of the music. Not the cello. The cello was in the closet and could wait; the cello had waited three years and could wait three more. I mean the other music, the one in a minor key that Ada and I had been composing for ten years in paper and then, impossibly, in bread and stairwells and Sunday afternoons at a bookshop, the one with a dying woman's laugh in it and a teenager's argument about Brahms and a ferryman's stories and a bookshop owner who kept saying she'd fix the bulb. That music was still playing. It was playing right now, four hundred metres behind me, in a kitchen with a comfortable chair, and it had a year left in it, maybe less, and every minute I spent on this platform was a bar rest I could never get back.

The train came in. It came in the way trains do, enormous and indifferent, breathing its brake-steam into the cold, and the doors opened with their pneumatic sigh, and the woman got on, and the boy got on, and the conductor looked at me, and I looked at the doors, and I want to be honest in the voice we made, the voice that never once lied: part of me got on that train. The part that was nineteen. The part that has been rehearsing its escape since before I knew what I was escaping from. I felt it step aboard, I felt it find a seat, and the doors closed on it, and I did not follow.

The train left at 11:40. I stood on the platform and watched its red tail-lights shrink down the line until they were a thought, then a memory, then nothing, and the cold came back into the air where the engine had been, and the rails went quiet, and I was still there.

I had missed it on purpose. That is the sentence I have never managed to say better than that: I missed it on purpose. Not through hesitation, not through some sentimental failure of nerve, but the way you fail to catch a ball you have decided not to catch. I stood in the emptying cold and let the choice settle into me like cold settles into a coat, and I noticed that I was not frightened. That was the strangest thing. Three years of building walls and the night I finally chose, truly chose, there was no black water anywhere. There was just a man on an empty platform at ten to midnight, holding a letter, going home.

* * *

The eleven minutes' walk back took me seven. I did not run, exactly. I walked the way Priya practises scales when she thinks I'm not listening—too fast, all enthusiasm and no discipline, and utterly certain.

The stairwell light was on. Marisol had fixed the bulb; I would learn later that Ada had called her, that afternoon, after I left with my four minutes of sugar and my four seconds of tin, and had said only, *He'll be home late. Make sure he can see his way.* As if she had known. As if she had known the way she knew everything, ten years in advance, in ink.

Her door was ajar. Not open—ajar, a hand's width of warm gold light lying across the stairwell floor like a strip of stage. I stood in front of it with my bag still in my hand, the bag I would have to unpack, the shirts with their aligned corners, the Rilke, the score, all of it, all the apparatus of departure, and I could hear her inside: the tap, the slow three-beat drag of the cane, the small chime of a spoon against a cup. Two cups. I could not have known that from the stairwell. I only knew it later, when I saw them on the table, hers and the other one, the good one, the one without the chip, set out for a man who was supposed to be on a train.

I knocked, or I didn't. I have never been able to remember, and I have decided that the not-remembering is the truest part. What I remember is the door moving under my hand, and the warmth of the flat coming out to meet me, and Ada turning from the counter with her cane in one hand and no surprise at all on her face—only a kind of quiet brightness, the brightness of someone watching a light come on in a window they have been watching all night.

"You missed it," she said.

"I missed it," I said.

And she nodded, once, as if a count had come round correctly, and she said, "The kettle's just boiled," and that was all, that was everything, that was the whole magnificent speech, and I set down the bag in her doorway and stepped over the threshold and did not look back at it, not once, not even when, later that night, I heard the third step creak under Marisol's early feet and thought of everything I had almost carried away.

The tea tin sat on the table between us, lid off, like a treaty signed. I hung up my coat.
