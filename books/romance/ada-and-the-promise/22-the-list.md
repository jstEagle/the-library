## 22. The List

The morning after the last train I did not take, I woke in my own bed for the first time in what felt like years and lay still, listening to the building breathe. Pipes ticking somewhere above. A pigeon arguing with the gutter. And through the wall, faint but unmistakable, the sound of Ada's radio playing something with strings—Vivaldi, maybe, or a piece pretending to be Vivaldi—and beneath it the small percussion of her moving around her kitchen: cup to saucer, kettle to stove, cane to floorboard. The ordinary orchestra of a woman making breakfast.

I had missed the 11:40 by standing on the platform like an idiot with two packed bags, reading her letter four times until the paper went soft at the folds. *Stay. I never asked you to be brave, only present.* Then I had walked home through streets gone silver with frost, let myself into her flat without knocking because she had told me weeks ago that I could and had never once used it, and fallen asleep in her kitchen chair while she pretended not to notice. She'd covered me with the grey blanket from her sofa. I know this because when I woke there was cat hair on it and the smell of her cardigan, and she was at the table drinking tea as though nothing had happened, and neither of us said anything about it, and that silence was the most honest conversation we had ever had.

Now, three days later, she appeared at my door at nine in the morning holding a notebook.

Not just any notebook. A proper one—the kind Marisol sold behind the counter for people who meant it, cloth-bound, unlined, heavy as a brick. Ada held it out to me the way you might hand someone a live coal.

"We're making a list," she said.

"A list."

"Of things I want to do." She pushed past me into the flat, which was her way; she had colonized my rooms the way ivy takes a wall, gently and completely and without apology. "While I can. While the can works and the legs mostly cooperate and the words come out in the right order." She set the notebook on my kitchen table, which was already crowded with bridge blanks and a half-repaired viola da gamba neck, and swept a space clear with her forearm. "Sit down, Hartwell. This is going to take all morning and probably some of the afternoon."

I sat. It is worth noting that I sat. Three months ago I would have found a reason to be elsewhere—a student, a repair, a fictitious headache. Now I sat down across from a dying woman and a blank page and waited, terrified, to find out what she would ask of me.

Because I already knew some of it. That was the thing. I knew her the way you know a piece you've played ten thousand times—not note by note anymore, but in your hands, in the shape of it under your fingers. And I knew that whatever she wrote on that first page was going to require things of me I had sworn off for three years, possibly forever.

Ada uncapped a pen. She always used fountain pens, real ink, and there was something ceremonial in the way she did it now, unscrewing the barrel, checking the nib against the light. Outside the window, February was doing its best impression of a held breath—low grey cloud, air cold enough to taste metallic, the plane tree across the street bare to its last twig.

"Rules first," she said. "Rule one: no editing me. If I say it, it goes on the list. Even if it's stupid. *Especially* if it's stupid."

"All right."

"Rule two: no negotiating on my behalf. You don't get to decide what's too much for me. I've got a neurologist for that and he's very expensive and mostly wrong."

"All right."

"Rule three—" She looked up, and her eyes were doing the thing they did sometimes, the thing where the humor in them went very still and something older looked out. "Rule three is that this list isn't a bucket list. I hate that phrase. Buckets are for carrying water and kicking. This is a list of things I want to *be present* for. Which means—" she tapped the pen against the table, once, twice, "—some of them are going to involve you being present too. That's the whole point of you."

"The whole point of me," I repeated.

"You're catching on."

She wrote the date at the top of the page. Her handwriting slanted forward like someone leaning into wind. Then she sat back and looked at the ceiling and thought, and I watched her think, and it occurred to me—I don't know why it took so long—that I had been watching her think for ten years through paper, through ink, through the long slow correspondence that had made her more legible to me than anyone alive. The difference now was only that I could see her hands do it.

"One," she said, and wrote. "*Hear the sea in winter.*"

The pen scratched. The radiator clanked. Somewhere below us Tomás was arguing cheerfully with his radio about football, a sound that came up through the floors like weather.

"Not summer," she said, looking up to catch my expression before I could arrange it. "Everyone does the sea in summer. The sea in winter is the true one. Grey and enormous and completely indifferent to you. I want to stand in front of something that doesn't care that I'm dying and feel good about it. There's a coast town—Priya will know the trains. Two hours, maybe. We wrap up, we walk the wall, we eat terrible chips. One day. You and me and the North Sea being magnificent at us."

"When?" I asked, and hated how carefully I asked it, how I was already doing the arithmetic she'd forbidden—her strength, the season, the distance between here and a hospital.

She saw me do it. Of course she saw me do it.

"That's rule two, Ines." Gently. The gentleness was worse than anger. "February. Before the light comes back properly. Winter sea or no sea."

"February," I said. "All right. February."

"Two." She wrote. "*Finish Priya's lessons.*"

This one landed soft and stayed soft, a warm stone in the chest. Priya had been coming Tuesdays and Saturdays for two years, a girl who played like weather systems—sudden fronts, dramatic pressure changes, occasional devastating clarity. Ada had appointed herself audience-in-residence sometime in the autumn, sitting in the armchair by the window with her tea, taking notes nobody had asked her to take, and Priya had bloomed under the attention the way plants bloom under lamps.

"She's got the Bach almost where it wants to be," Ada said, "but the phrasing in the second suite is still polite. She plays it like she's asking permission. Someone needs to teach her that music is not permission." She glanced at me. "That someone is you. But I want to be there for the moment it lands. The moment she stops asking. You'll know it when you see it—it'll be in her shoulders."

"And if it doesn't land?"

"It'll land. Have you met her? The girl argued with me about Shostakovich for forty minutes last week and I *won* and I still felt like I'd lost." She wrote the third item before she said it aloud, and I read it upside down across the table, and my stomach dropped through the floorboards into Tomás's kitchen.

"*Hear Ines play the Elgar again.*"

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when a clock is suddenly audible. I could hear my own pulse in my ears. I could hear the Vivaldi-imitating radio next door, absurdly, still going.

"No," I said.

"I haven't even explained—"

"There's nothing to explain. Ada. No."

She put the pen down. She folded her hands on the table, over the notebook, over those three words in wet black ink, and she looked at me with the patience of a person who has done her own arithmetic and arrived at a number she can live with.

"Ines. Tell me what you hear when you say that word. Elgar. What happens in your body?"

I didn't want to answer. I answered anyway, because she had earned every answer I had, and because lying to her had become physically difficult, like trying to sing with a hand over your mouth.

"My hands go cold. My throat closes. I'm twenty-three years old and the hall is full and Aldous Vane is in the fourth row with his arms crossed and I'm standing at the top of a diving board over black water."

"And what do you feel right now, saying that, at this table, with me?"

I checked. Actually checked, the way she'd taught me to check—attention turned inward, patient, clinical, kind. "Cold hands," I admitted. "But the rest of it's... far away. Like hearing a storm through a wall."

"Good." She picked the pen back up. "That's the furthest it's ever been. Notice that. I'm writing it down whether you like it or not."

"Ada—"

"Hear me out. All of it, and then you can refuse, and I'll respect the refusal, and I'll sulk about it privately for exactly one evening." She held up fingers. "One: I am not asking for a concert hall. I am not asking for critics. I am asking to hear you play the adagio of the Elgar Concerto in a room small enough that I can see your face. Two: you have played for me twice now. Badly, beautifully, both times. Your hands shook and the music survived. Music survives shaking, Ines—that's practically its job description. Three—" and here her voice changed, dropped into the register she used maybe once a month, the one that came from underneath everything, "—three: I have spent ten years listening to you describe that piece. The way it opens like a voice speaking after a long silence. The way the adagio is the most private grief ever put into public notes. I have imagined it for a decade. I would like, before I go, to stop imagining."

The radiator clanked. The pigeon had won its argument with the gutter and departed. I sat with my cold hands on my knees and understood, with terrible clarity, that she was going to get this. That I was going to give it to her. Not because she'd argued well—though she had—but because of the sentence underneath all her sentences, the one she'd written on the platform letter and never once taken back: *I never asked you to be brave, only present.* And presence, for her, for this, meant the Elgar. There was no version of staying that didn't eventually arrive here. I had simply hoped the station was further away.

"Where?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded like it belonged to someone younger and much more frightened.

Her face did something complicated and bright. "Marisol's back room," she said immediately, too immediately, and I realized with a lurch that this had been discussed, that there had been a conspiracy, that Marisol and Tomás and possibly Priya were already ahead of me, had perhaps been ahead of me for weeks. "Forty chairs. Fairy lights. Terrible acoustics and excellent company. Spring, maybe April, when the light lasts longer. Nothing announced, nothing reviewed. Just people who love you, which I recognize is somehow more terrifying than critics, and which is exactly the point."

"How many people know about this list?"

"So far? Me." She smiled. "By tonight, everyone. Sunday circle rules. We vote on chips."

*

We worked through the morning. The list grew the way lists do, organically, absurdly, each item spawning commentary and amendment. *Learn to make bread that doesn't depress Marisol.* *Read Middlemarch out loud, all of it, voices included.* *Teach the cat to come when called (unlikely).* *See the cherry trees on the embankment do their one week of glory.* *Tell Priya.* — and she wrote that one without looking up, and I watched the ink dry, and neither of us spoke about it, and it sat on the page between the cherries and the bread like a stone in a stream that the water has to go around.

At some point she tired—she tired earlier these days, the fatigue arriving like a tide rather than a wave, rising by degrees until she simply stopped mid-sentence and rested—and I made lunch without being asked, soup and the good bread from the bakery on the corner, and we ate at the table with the notebook open between us like a third guest.

"Read it back to me," she said, when the bowls were empty.

I read it back. Nine items. The sea in winter. Priya's lessons finished. The Elgar. Bread. Middlemarch. Cherries. The cat. Telling Priya. And at the bottom, added last, in smaller letters, almost as an afterthought except that nothing Ada did was ever an afterthought:

"*Ten. Be known the whole way down.*"

I stopped reading. The kitchen was very quiet.

"That one's not an activity," I said.

"No," she agreed. "It's the umbrella. Everything else fits under it." She reached across and closed the notebook and laid her hand flat on the cover, and her hand was thinner than it had been in autumn, and I noticed, and she saw me notice, and neither of us flinched, which was its own kind of vow. "You agreed to all of it. I want you to know I noticed that too. You didn't negotiate a single item."

"I negotiated the timeline of the sea."

"You asked *when*. That's logistics, not negotiation." She stood, using the table, then the counter, then the cane, the choreography so familiar now that I had stopped seeing it and only saw it in moments like this, deliberately, and had to swallow. At the door she paused, and the afternoon light through my window caught the grey coming in at her temples, and she looked, for a moment, exactly like the person who had written me letters for ten years—wry, luminous, unhurried, a soul leaning into wind.

"Sunday," she said. "Marisol's. Bring the list. Bring your terror. They're both invited."

And she was gone, cane tapping down the stairwell like a slow metronome, leaving me alone with a notebook full of promises and the growing, vertiginous understanding that hope, in her hands, was not a comfort at all.

It was a weapon. Aimed at time. And I had just agreed to carry it.

That night I took my cello out of the closet—not to play, only to look—and opened the score of the Elgar to the first page, and the opening dynamics stared up at me from the paper like a face across a crowded room: *Adagio. Moderato.* A voice speaking after a long silence.

My hands were cold. But the storm was behind the wall.

I left the score open on the stand overnight, which I had not done in three years, and in the morning there was a note slid under my door in slanted, forward-leaning ink: *Item three. First rehearsal Thursday. Wear something you can shake in.*

Below it, smaller: *I've already told Marisol. She's crying happy tears and ordering chairs.*
