## 24. Practicing in Front of One Person

The chair was wrong.

That was the first thing I noticed, standing in Ada's sitting room at seven in the evening with my cello case in one hand and a terror like a swallowed stone in the other: her chair was wrong. She had dragged it from beside the window to the middle of the room and turned it to face me, and there was nothing else in that half of the room at all—no lamp, no book stack, no tea tin, no cat. Just the chair, and Ada in it, and forty years of empty air between us that she had cleared on purpose, the way you clear a stage.

"You moved your furniture," I said, because my mouth needed something stupid to do while the rest of me decided whether to run.

"I made an auditorium," she said. "It's very exclusive. Capacity of one." She patted the armrests as if counting seats. "Sold out, obviously."

The cat—Bramble, who had adopted her before she adopted him, which she said was the correct order of operations for all important relationships—sat on the windowsill behind her, tail curled around his feet, watching me with the flat golden patience of a creature who has never once been asked to perform anything.

I set the case down on her rug. My hands were not shaking yet. That was the strange part. They were perfectly still, almost formal, like hired staff waiting for the disaster to begin.

"We don't have to do this tonight," Ada said, and her voice did the thing it always did—the letter-voice, the voice from ten years of paper, low and unhurried and utterly without pity. Pity would have sent me out the door. "We could play cards. We could argue about whether Brahms was a coward. We could sit here in silence and call it a salon."

"No," I said. "If I don't do it now, I'll negotiate myself out of it by Thursday."

"That's exactly what you'd do," she agreed cheerfully. "You'd draft terms. You'd want a rider. You'd demand the room be soundproofed and the audience be a photograph of someone forgiving."

I laughed despite myself—a short, startled sound—and unclicked the latches.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fear: it has excellent memory. It remembers the hall in Vienna, the third row where Aldous Vane sat with his arms crossed like a verdict already written. It remembers the exact bar number where the Elgar came apart in my hands—bar 84 of the adagio, though I have never told anyone that number, not even A, not even Ada, until last week when I wrote it on a scrap of paper and slid it under her door like a confession small enough to fit through a gap. It remembers the sound of two thousand people discovering together that I was drowning. Fear keeps those files in perfect order. What it does not remember—what it has deliberately misfiled—is that I survived. That I walked off the stage and kept breathing. That three years later a woman with a cane moved in next door and knew my name before I gave it to her.

So my hands were steady right up until they weren't.

I rosined the bow. I tuned, the open strings ringing into her small warm room, and Ada watched me tune the way other people watch weather arrive—attentively, without comment, letting it happen. She had put on the green cardigan, the one with the loose button, the one she wore when she wanted to be comfortable rather than impressive, and I understood suddenly that she had dressed for this the way I used to dress for concerts. Not for me. For the occasion. She considered this an occasion.

"Any requests?" I asked, and hated how thin my voice came out.

"The Sarabande," she said. "Bach. The fifth suite." A pause, and then, gentler: "Unless you'd rather start with something that doesn't mean anything."

But that was the trap, wasn't it. Nothing meant nothing anymore. Every piece I owned had been filed away during the silence, labeled and shelved, and the Bach sarabande was the one I had played alone at three in the morning through the wall to her, the one whose name she had asked for on a note in handwriting I'd have known anywhere, the one that had started the letters again after I'd sworn them off. If I was going to be heard, it was going to be by that piece first.

I sat down. The chair received me. I set the bow on the string.

And stopped.

Not a dramatic stop. Just—my arm declined to move. Some clerk deep in my nervous system stamped the request DENIED and my body obeyed, and I sat there in Ada Quill's sitting room with a cello between my knees like a held breath, unable to make the first note.

"Bar zero," Ada said softly. "A very difficult bar. Most musicians fail there."

I looked up. She wasn't smiling, exactly. She was doing the other thing, the rarer thing: looking at me without any expectation on her face at all, her whole attention arranged around me like the cleared half of the room, like space made on purpose. An audience of thousands, I thought absurdly. She listens like an audience of thousands. Because that was the truth of her attention—it had mass. When Ada listened, you felt the listening land on you, weighty and warm, and most of my life I had experienced attention as a weight too, but the crushing kind, the kind that bent the spine. Hers was the kind that held things up.

I played the first note.

It came out ugly. Thin, pressed, the intonation a quarter-tone sharp with adrenaline, and I flinched at my own sound the way you flinch at your own voice on a recording. But it existed. It was in the room. Ada's eyes closed—not in judgment, I realized, but the way you close your eyes to taste something—and I played the second note, and the third, and the fourth, and then the phrase opened and I was inside the sarabande, inside the slow dark procession of it, and for eleven bars I forgot to be afraid.

Eleven bars. Then the passage came—the shift up the neck, the stretch my left hand had to make across the strings, the position I had fumbled in a hundred private rehearsals and never once fumbled for anyone—and my hand shook. Not metaphorically. A visible tremor ran from my wrist to my fingertips, and the note cracked, split into its ugly overtones, and I stopped.

"Sorry," I said. The word came out automatic, reflexive, the apology of a man stepping on someone's foot. "Sorry, I—"

"Ines." Ada leaned forward. "What are you apologizing for?"

"For stopping."

"Did I ask you to apologize?"

"No."

"Did I ask you to keep going?"

"No."

"Then what," she said, with terrible mildness, "exactly am I allowed to ask you for?"

I stared at her. Bramble yawned on the windowsill.

"Nothing," I said slowly. "You're not allowed to ask me for anything."

"Mm." She settled back. "Interesting arrangement. Continue whenever you like. Or don't. I have nowhere to be for the rest of my life." She said it lightly, the way she said everything about the rest of her life, and we both let it pass the way we let all of it pass—grief folded neatly into humor, filed under jokes, the tea tin of our conversations.

I found the bar again. Played through the crack. The tremor didn't leave; it just became part of the sound, a vibrato too wide, a human noise inside the Bach, and somewhere around the middle of the sarabande I stopped fighting it and let the shaking live in the music the way grief lived in our letters—unhidden, unglamorous, present.

* * *

I stopped the second time near the end.

This one was worse, because it wasn't technical. The sarabande descends at the close, step by step, a line walking down into its own grave, and I have always thought of it as the most honest music Bach ever wrote—no consolation offered, just the descent, and then the final chord like a door closing gently. And I got to the descent and thought, with no warning at all: *she will hear this chord someday soon and know it is about her.* And my bow lifted off the string as if the string had burned it.

The silence afterward was enormous. Her radiator ticked. Somewhere below, Tomás's television murmured its evening dramas. I sat with the bow raised like a man caught mid-oath.

Ada waited. She was good at waiting; illness had trained her in it the way conservatories train the rest of us in scales. When she finally spoke, her voice was careful in a new way.

"What happened there?"

"I thought—" My throat worked. "There's a chord coming. At the end. And I thought about you hearing it."

"And?"

"And I couldn't play it *at* you. It felt like—like playing your funeral while you're sitting in the room."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Play it anyway. Not at me. Play it *for* me. There's a difference, and you know there's a difference, because you've been writing to me for ten years and you've never once written *at* me."

I didn't answer. I brought the bow back down.

The descent came again, and this time I played every step of it looking at her, and she looked back, and neither of us pretended it wasn't what it was. The final chord arrived. I let it ring. In her small room, with its bad radiator and its medicine shelf she thought I hadn't noticed and its cat and its green-cardigan woman, the chord rang longer than it ever had in any hall, because there was no reverb in that room except the kind two people make by refusing to breathe.

Then Ada exhaled, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand—quickly, annoyed at herself, the way she did everything private—and said, "Well. You were terrible."

I barked a laugh. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me, it's true. Your shifts were late, your vibrato was having a medical event, and you stopped twice." She counted on her fingers. "In any professional hall, they'd have thrown fruit."

"Probably."

"But." She held up one finger, silencing me. "But. I have heard the Berlin Philharmonic play that sarabande, in a hall with better acoustics than God's own living room, and I was bored. Comfortably bored. Beautifully bored. And just now I forgot to check the time, forgot my foot hurts, forgot the name of my neurologist—which, frankly, is a miracle, I say it like a mantra. So here is my review." She leaned forward, and her eyes were bright and fierce and wet. "Worst performance I've ever heard. Only one I'll remember."

I looked down at the cello, because looking at her directly had become impossible, the way the sun is impossible—not because it isn't what you want but because it's exactly that.

"It's going to take more than one evening," I said. "Before the—if I ever do the concert. My hands. They remember things."

"Good," said Ada. "Let them remember. Then teach them something new. That's all practice is, Ines. It's not repetition. It's argument. You argue with what your body believes until the body concedes." She smiled crookedly. "I've been arguing with mine for two years. I'm losing, but I've learned a great deal about persistence."

* * *

We did it again. And again. Three times through the sarabande, and by the third time my shifts were only slightly late and my vibrato was merely eccentric, and Ada conducted from her chair with one finger, marking places, demanding things—*more there, less there, why are you rushing, who told you rushing was modesty*—and I discovered that being coached by Ada Quill was like being read by her: total, unsparing, and somehow safe. She had spent ten years telling me the truth on paper. Now she simply did it aloud, in a room, to my face, and the world did not end.

On the fourth pass I stopped thinking entirely. I won't claim transcendence—I've read enough musician memoirs to distrust the word—but something shifted, some internal border checkpoint waved us through without inspection, and for the length of a sarabande I was not a man performing for a witness. I was a man playing in a room where another person happened to be, and the other person happened to be the reason the room existed.

When I finished, Ada didn't applaud. She did something better. She said, "Again tomorrow?" like it was already decided, like it was Tuesday, like it was the letters—our rhythm, resumed, transposed into flesh and rosin dust.

"Tomorrow," I said.

I packed the cello with hands that were tired in the good way, the way hands get after honest work instead of clenched vigil. At the door she stopped me with a touch on my sleeve, light as a page turning.

"Ines. Do you know what you did tonight? You didn't conquer anything. You didn't slay the dragon. You just stayed in the room while it breathed on you." Her thumb pressed once against my wrist, over the pulse. "That's the whole secret. Everyone thinks courage is the leap. It's not. It's the staying."

I walked home the twelve steps to my own flat carrying the cello like something rescued rather than something heavy, and I stood in my kitchen not turning on the lights, and I thought: *eleven bars.* Eleven bars of freedom the first time. Maybe fourteen tomorrow. Maybe, if I argued long enough, a whole page.

I was still standing there, foolish and lit by the streetlamp through the window, when someone knocked on my door—three quick raps, impatient, young, unmistakable.

Priya. Holding her cello case and a sheet of music and an expression I had learned to dread, the expression of a student who has found a hole in her teacher's story.

"Mr. Halloran," she said, before I even got the door fully open. "I need to talk to you about Ada. And don't tell me it's nothing, because I'm sixteen, not stupid."
