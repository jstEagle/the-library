## 26. The Sea in Winter

The list lived on Ada's refrigerator, held by a magnet shaped like a sardine, and item three had been crossed out and rewritten twice: *hear the sea in winter* — *hear the sea in winter, properly, not from a car park* — and finally, in her looping hand, *the real coast, the one with the path, you know the one.*

I did not know the one. But Ada Quill had never once accepted ignorance as an obstacle.

We left on a Tuesday because she said Tuesdays were honest days — no one expected anything of a Tuesday, no one dressed up for it. Marisol packed us a hamper that could have fed a family of six for a week and refused to hear arguments about it. Tomás drove us in his ancient estate car, which smelled of diesel and pipe tobacco and forty years of salt air, and Priya came along ostensibly to "supervise" but actually spent the first hour asleep against the window with her cello case wedged upright between her knees like a sleeping companion.

The road unspooled westward through country that grew progressively less apologetic about itself. Hedges gave way to gorse. The sky widened the way it only does near coasts, as if the land were stepping back to make room. And then, cresting a hill, there it was — a grey-green slab of ocean under a sky the colour of pewter, and the whole car went quiet except for Tomás, who said, "There she is," in the voice other men used for women they'd loved badly.

Ada's hand found mine on the seat between us. Her fingers were cold. They were always cold now; I had taken to keeping my flat overheated so she wouldn't have to admit to being cold when she visited.

"Stop the car," she said. "Tomás. Stop the car, I want to look at it before we get sentimental about it."

"You're already sentimental about it," Priya said, waking, rubbing her eyes.

"Obviously. That's why we have to stop."

So we stood at the top of the lane with the wind pulling our coats sideways, and Ada looked at the sea the way I had once looked at a cello through a shop window at nineteen — with the particular hunger of someone greeting something they'd been separated from. The wind put colour in her cheeks and tears in her eyes and she laughed at herself for both.

"There," she said. "That's the sound. That's what I wanted." The sea was audible even from here, a long exhale against the shore, patient as breathing. "Everything else can be terrible today. I've already had this."

"Nothing else is going to be terrible," I said.

She squeezed my hand. She didn't argue, which should have told me something.

* * *

The path began behind a chapel that had been deconsecrated sometime last century and now sold ice cream in summer and nothing in winter. Tomás elected to stay with the car — "my walking days are behind me, and ahead of me is a flask of coffee" — and Priya came as far as the first stile before admitting she'd promised her mother she'd practise, which was a lie so transparent that Ada kissed her forehead and told her to go and be sixteen somewhere warm.

So it was the two of us, and the path, and the sea below us doing its enormous indifferent work.

For the first mile Ada was magnificent. She walked with her cane swinging like a metronome set to allegro, narrating everything — the sheep ("they know we don't belong here"), the lichen on the drystone walls ("older than both our countries' patience"), a kestrel hanging motionless over the cliff grass ("look at him, he's cheating, he's found the one still spot in all this wind"). She had brought bread and cheese from Marisol's hamper and ate walking, which she said was how food was meant to be eaten, standing up, stolen from your own supplies.

I carried the bag. I carried the thermos. I carried, without letting myself think about it, the growing awareness of how often I checked her face.

Because I had learned her tells by then. Everyone thinks illness announces itself, but it doesn't; it whispers, and you learn its dialect. The way her stride shortened by half a step every quarter mile. The way she stopped gesturing with her right hand and started favouring the cane. The way her laugh stayed exactly the same while everything around it got quieter — that was the tell that undid me most, the constancy of the laugh, like a lighthouse beam sweeping over water that was getting darker.

At the two-mile mark she sat down on a milestone without announcing it, which was itself an announcement.

"Well," she said brightly. "This stone has excellent views."

"We can turn back."

"We absolutely cannot. We are forty minutes from the water and I have been planning this since October." She patted the stone beside her. "Sit. Drink the tea. Let me consult with my legs."

I sat. I poured tea from the thermos into the cup lid and handed it to her and watched her hands around it — steady enough, always steady enough when she decided they would be — and out past the cliff edge the sea kept up its long grey argument with the land.

"Ines." She was looking at the horizon, not at me. "I want you to hear this next part without arguing, because I've rehearsed it and if you argue I'll lose my nerve and we'll end up drinking tea on milestones for the rest of our lives, which, granted, sounds nice, but."

My chest did the thing it did now whenever she began a sentence that way. "Ada—"

"I'm going to need carrying." Still not looking at me. "The last stretch. It's downhill and it's scree and my foot has gone to London without leaving a forwarding address. So you can either carry me or we can sit here and I can listen to the sea from a mile away, which is not nothing, but it isn't the thing either. And I would very much like the thing."

The wind moved through the gorse. Somewhere below us a gull made its opinion known.

"You've been planning this," I said.

"Since October," she agreed. "Priya helped me pick the path. Tomás measured the distance with his car's odometer, which he insists is accurate to within a furlong. Marisol packed extra sandwiches specifically because she guessed I'd burn energy being stubborn." She turned to me then, and her eyes were bright and wet and absolutely level. "Everyone knew, Ines. Everyone conspired. Because everyone loves you and none of them wanted you to have time to talk yourself out of it."

"And you?"

"I wanted you to have no choice," she said. "It's the kindest thing I've ever done to you."

* * *

I lifted her at the milestone, and she weighed almost nothing, and that fact broke something in me that I would spend the rest of the walk learning to carry alongside her.

She weighed almost nothing. Three years ago she had written me a letter describing a cake she'd baked that collapsed in the middle, and she'd signed off saying she ate the whole failure herself, standing up, and I remember thinking even then that she sounded like someone built of substantial things. Now here she was in my arms light as a bundle of letters, her cane hooked over her wrist, her head finding the place against my shoulder where it fit as though the geography had been surveyed in advance.

And she laughed.

That was the part I hadn't been prepared for. Not silence, not apology, not the grim endurance people imagine dignity requires — laughter, low and helpless and continuous, bubbling up out of her with every step as though my walking shook it loose.

"What," I said, "is funny."

"You," she gasped. "Your face. You look like a man defusing a bomb. Relax your shoulders, cellist, you'll do yourself an injury. This is not a recital. There are no critics. It's just a man and a woman and a cliff path and—" another wave of laughter, her hand pressed to her mouth, "—and I weigh nothing, apparently, I'm down to my skeleton and my opinions—"

"Don't joke about that."

"It's either joking or crying and I've done my crying quota until Thursday." She tipped her head back to look at the sky, at the ragged white clouds hauling themselves eastward. "Besides, when am I ever going to be carried again? Never. This is my one historical opportunity and I intend to enjoy it thoroughly. Walk faster. No — slower. Slower. Make it last."

So I made it last. I slowed my steps to half their length and let the path take its time, and the scree crunched under my boots, and the sea came closer with each switchback — audible now as individual waves rather than one long sound, each one arriving, spending itself, withdrawing, arriving again. The air changed too, took on salt and weed and cold iron, the smell of the world's oldest ongoing event.

"Tell me something true," Ada said. It was a game from the letters, from years of Tuesdays — *tell me something true*, meaning not a fact but a confession, small or enormous, whatever the day required.

"The last time I carried anything heavier than a cello was a bookcase, four years ago, and I dropped it."

"Something truer."

"I'm afraid of the moment I put you down," I said. "After. When my arms are empty."

Her laughing stopped. For three steps she was quiet against my shoulder, and I felt her breath change, and then she said, softly, into my collar:

"That's why I picked this path. So the empty arms would come after the sea. So you'd remember them full of something good." A pause. "Tell me something true."

"I love you," I said. Out loud. On a cliff path, to the back of her head, with the wind taking the words and doing what it liked with them. I had thought it for months. I had written versions of it in unsent letters. Saying it was easier than any of those, the way the hardest things usually are once you stop rehearsing.

"I know," she said. "I've known since the sugar incident. Say it again anyway, I'm collecting them."

"I love you."

"One more. For the tin."

"I love you, Ada Quill, who conspired with an entire neighbourhood to get carried down a cliff."

"Perfect," she said. "That's the one for the tin."

* * *

The beach, when we reached it, was the kind that exists only in winter — vast and empty and swept clean, sand the colour of wet gunmetal, the tide far out and dragging its hem across the flats. No families, no dogs, no ice cream kiosk. Just the sea, doing its work, and the wind coming off it with the force of a fact.

I set her down on a driftwood log above the tideline, wrapped her in the blanket from the hamper, and sat beside her, and for a long time neither of us said anything. There didn't need to be anything said. The sea said it — that long exhale, arriving and arriving and arriving, indifferent and enormous and somehow, today, merciful.

"This is it," she said finally. "This is exactly it. The thing I wrote on the list." She was crying a little now, quietly, without drama, tears running sideways in the wind, and she was also smiling, and both things were simply true at once, the way joy and grief had learned to live in her like tenants sharing a room. "Listen to it. It doesn't care about any of us. Isn't that wonderful? Everything else cares so much. Doctors care, and schedules care, and bodies care, relentlessly. And this—" she gestured at all of it, the grey water, the white noise, the horizon holding its line, "—this just keeps going whether we watch or not. I find that so restful."

"It sounds lonely."

"It sounds free." She leaned into me. "You should write it down. Something true."

"I will."

"Not later. Here. While it's happening." She dug in the hamper and produced — of course — a notebook and a pencil stub, because Ada Quill never went anywhere unarmed against forgetting. "Write it while the sea's still in your ears. Otherwise you'll write it later and it'll be a poem about the sea instead of about today."

So I wrote, with numb fingers, on my knee, while she watched:

*The sea does not perform. It arrives anyway. I want to learn this.*

She read it, and read it again, and then folded the page and tore it out and put it in her coat pocket, over her heart, which was where she kept things she intended to keep.

"Good," she said. "Now pour the tea. We have twenty minutes before Tomás sends a search party, and I intend to spend all of them doing absolutely nothing, magnificently."

We did. We drank tea that went cold too fast and ate sandwiches with grit in them and watched the waves come in, and Ada named the colours of the water — slate, bottle, pewter, one impossible stripe of green where the light got through — and I held her against my side and memorized the weight of her, which was almost nothing, which was everything, which I would carry in my arms long after my arms were empty.

When we climbed back up — Tomás driving to the chapel to meet us, Priya running down the last stretch with her scarf flying, shouting questions — Ada rode on my back the whole way, arms around my neck, laughing into the wind, telling everyone within earshot that she had been conveyed to the sea like royalty and that the royalty had complained the entire time.

"She did not complain," I said.

"He complained constantly," she said. "Silently. With his eyebrows. It's his whole language."

Priya looked at the two of us — at Ada on my back, at my hands locked under her knees, at whatever was on my face — and something in the girl's expression shifted, some understanding arriving early, ahead of schedule, the way hard things do. She didn't say anything. She just reached up and tucked the blanket more securely around Ada's shoulders, and walked beside us the rest of the way, close, quiet, seventeen going on something much older.

That night, home, I heard the sea still in my ears as I lay awake. And through the wall, faint but unmistakable, came the scratch of pen on paper — Ada, writing, past midnight, the way she did when a day had given her more than she could hold.

Then the scratch stopped. And a few minutes later, an envelope slid under my door.

I lay there looking at it across the dark room, white as a sail, and did not move to pick it up. Because I knew Ada's rhythms by now, the way I knew her tells, and midnight letters from Ada Quill were never small ones — and this one, I somehow already understood, had been waiting a long time to be written.
