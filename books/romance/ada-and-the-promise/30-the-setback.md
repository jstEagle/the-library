## 30. The Setback

The rehearsal room above the bookshop smells of dust and rosin and, on Thursdays, of Marisol's coffee drifting up through the floorboards like a rumor. I have come to think of it as the warmest room in the city. Forty folding chairs stacked against one wall, a radiator that knocks twice before it settles, a window that looks out onto the street where Tomás sweeps leaves he has no obligation to sweep. Ines stands at the center of it with his cello, and I sit in the third row—third row, always, because that is where he can see me without having to look directly at me, which is its own kind of tenderness—and I take notes in a small green notebook like a manager who is being paid in something better than money.

*Second movement, bar 47 — breathe before the phrase, not after.*
*Your left hand knows this. Let it.*

He plays the Elgar for me the way other men say prayers: sometimes fluently, sometimes with long silences where the words should be, sometimes with a fury that startles us both. Today he is fluent. The adagio rises out of him like heat off summer pavement, and I sit very still in my chair with my cane hooked over the armrest and let the sound move through me the way weather moves through an open window.

This is what I came for, I think. Not the sea in winter, not even the year itself—but this. A man unlearning his fear one bar at a time while I keep score.

I am writing *bar 112 — don't rush the grief* when the numbness arrives.

It has been arriving more often lately, the way rain arrives in autumn—not all at once but as a darkening at the edge of things. It starts in my right hand, the one holding the pen. The nib drags. The letters go soft at their edges, like ink left out in fog. I look down at the notebook and see that I have written the same word three times, *grief grief grief*, each one less certain than the last.

"Ada." Ines has stopped playing. He does that now—hears the change in my breathing before I do. "Ada, what's wrong?"

"Nothing," I say, and it is the oldest lie in my repertoire, worn smooth as a river stone from ten years of use. "Just tired."

I try to stand. That is the mistake. The numbness has moved into my legs without announcing itself, the way it does now, politely, terribly, and my knees simply decline the invitation. The room tilts. The radiator knocks once, twice—

I do not remember falling. I remember Ines's face above mine, white as the dust on the floorboards, and his hands under my shoulders, and his voice saying my name over and over in a register I have never heard him use, low and cracked and utterly without performance. I remember thinking, absurdly, that this was the first time I had ever heard him make a sound without controlling it first.

Then the world went to gray, and the gray went to nothing.

* * *

Hospitals are rooms designed by people who believe time can be managed. Everything in them is measured—pulse, pressure, milliliters, minutes between observations—but none of it adds up to anything you want to know. I lie in a bed with rails on the sides like a boat that has given up on sailing, and I listen to Dr. Okafor explain my own body to me in the gentlest possible terms, as though she is breaking news to a neighbor about a death in the family.

Which, in a way, she is.

"The progression has accelerated," she says. "The last scans—" She pauses, and I watch her choose her words the way Ines chooses notes, discarding several before settling on one. "We are no longer talking about months in the way we were. We're talking about weeks. Perhaps a few more than weeks, if we're fortunate. But I won't insult you by pretending the arithmetic is kind."

Weeks.

The word sits in the air between us, small and enormous, like a stone dropped into still water. I find that my first feeling is not fear. It is irritation—a fierce, ridiculous anger at my own body, at the illness for failing to read the schedule I made for it. One year, I promised myself. One year to be known. I budgeted for it. I planned around it. I bought a winter coat I intended to wear until spring.

"Weeks," I repeat.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry," I say, and hear how sharp my voice comes out, and soften it. "Be honest. Can I still go to rehearsals?"

Dr. Okafor looks at me for a long moment. "With assistance. With rest. Ada—" and here her voice does the thing doctors' voices do, the dipping toward pity that I have spent months learning to deflect, "—you should be thinking about what matters most."

What matters most. As though I haven't been doing nothing else since the day I signed the lease on a flat next to a man whose name I knew before I knew his face.

Ines is in the corridor. I can see him through the glass panel in the door, sitting in one of those chairs built to discourage permanence, elbows on his knees, head bowed, his hands—the beautiful, ruined, recovering hands—hanging loose between them. He has not come in yet. He has been out there for two hours, the nurse tells me, refusing coffee, refusing chairs with cushions, refusing everything except the position of a man keeping vigil.

I could call him in. I could tell him myself, gently, with the words I rehearsed years ago in front of bathroom mirrors: *I'm dying, Ines, I've been dying since before you knew me, and I chose you anyway, because knowing you was worth the arithmetic.*

But I am tired, and the words feel heavy as wet coats, and so I close my eyes instead, and let the machines count what they count.

* * *

It is Priya who tells me later what happened in the corridor. She arrived after school with her cello case and her furious, magnificent refusal to be treated like a child, and she found Ines standing at the nurses' station, and she heard him say the thing he will deny saying for the rest of his life.

Marisol called him, apparently. Or he called Marisol—I get the versions confused, and Priya tells it with the dramatic economy of a sixteen-year-old who has already decided this story belongs to her. Either way: Marisol said, *the concert can wait, love, no one would blame him,* and Ines said, into the phone, in a voice like a door closing:

"Then there's no point. If she won't hear it—if she might not—" A breath. "Cancel it. Cancel everything. What's the point of learning to play if the person it's for isn't in the room?"

Priya says the nurses' station went quiet. Priya says she marched up to him, all five feet two inches of her, and stood there with her jaw set in the exact way I imagine she sets it when a difficult passage refuses to yield, and said:

"You don't get to quit. I've watched you practice for months. I've watched you shake. You don't get to stop three weeks before just because you're scared."

And Ines—my Ines, my letter-writer, my man of careful silences—looked down at her and said, "You don't understand. She may never hear it."

And Priya, who is going to be a force of nature when she grows into her own thunder, said:

"Then she'll hear it from wherever she is. But *you'll* still be the man who played it. Or you'll be the man who didn't. Those are the only two options, and one of them is worse."

She told me all of this sitting beside my hospital bed, peeling an orange in long unbroken spirals because I once told her that was a sign of patience, and she wanted me to know she'd been paying attention. She did not cry. Her eyes were red-rimmed and her voice wobbled exactly once, on the word *worse*, but she did not cry, because she has decided that crying is for after, and after is not yet.

"He didn't cancel," she said. "In the end. Marisol's putting the chairs back up tomorrow."

"I know," I said.

"How do you know?"

"Because he's still here." I nodded toward the corridor, where through the glass I could see him, still in the discouraging chair, still keeping his vigil, a man who had nearly walked away and then discovered that walking away was no longer a direction his body knew how to take. "People who quit leave. He's still here."

Priya followed my gaze. For a moment her face did something young and unguarded, something that looked almost like fear wearing love's clothing, and then she finished the orange spiral and laid it on my blanket like an offering.

"He loves you," she said. Sixteen years old, and she says these things flatly, factually, the way she announces key signatures. "Everyone knows. Even Tomás knows, and Tomás thinks Bach was overrated."

"Tomás is wrong about Bach."

"Tomás is wrong about lots of things." She stood, slung her case over her shoulder, and paused at the door. "Get better, Ada. The front row's waiting."

Front row. Third row, actually—he plays best when I'm slightly off-axis, where he can feel me without watching me—but I don't correct her. Some promises are better kept loosely.

* * *

He comes in at seven, when visiting hours thin out and the corridors go quiet. He carries my green notebook, retrieved from the rehearsal room floor, and he sets it on the bedside table with both hands, carefully, the way you set down something that might be broken.

For a long time neither of us speaks. The machines count. Outside the window, the city puts on its evening lights, window by window, someone turning on lamps in a hundred rooms where people are eating dinner and arguing about small things and not knowing, any of them, how lucky that is.

"I almost quit," he says finally. His voice is rough. He is looking at his hands. "Today. In the corridor. I told Marisol to cancel everything."

"I heard."

His head comes up. "You heard?"

"Priya gives excellent reports." I try for a smile and manage maybe sixty percent of one. My face doesn't always cooperate anymore; another item on the list of things my body is quietly repossessing. "She also says Tomás is wrong about Bach."

"He is wrong about Bach." But there's no lightness in it. He leans forward, elbows on the bed rail, and his eyes when they meet mine are the eyes of a man standing at the edge of something high. "Ada. Weeks. They said weeks."

"They said weeks."

"And the concert—" His voice catches on it. "Three weeks. Maybe four. The math doesn't—" He stops. Starts again, quieter, and this is the voice from the letters, the true voice, the one that only ever lived on paper for ten years: "I started doing this so you could hear it. And now you might not. And I don't know what I'm doing it *for* anymore. I don't know what any of it is for if the whole point isn't in the room."

There it is. The lowest floor of him, the basement he never shows anyone, opened up under the fluorescent lights. I could give him comfort. I could reach for his hand and tell him it doesn't matter, that I'll be there in some form, that love survives its audiences. All true. All beside the point.

Instead I say: "Do you remember what you wrote to me? Nineteen ninety-eight. After your father's funeral. You said—" I close my eyes; I have carried his sentences so long they live behind my eyelids. "*I don't know how to be in a room where he isn't.* Do you remember?"

"Yes."

"And I wrote back and said, *you learn to carry the room with you.*"

"Ada—"

"Ines." I reach out, and my hand shakes, and I let it shake, because hiding the shaking has cost me enough. "Listen to me. I may not be in the room. That's true, and it's terrible, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise, because pretending is what's killing us both faster than the illness is. But you need to hear this, and you need to hear it now, while I'm still here to say it: if you quit—if you pack that cello back into the closet and go back to being invisible—you will not be doing it for me. You'll be doing it to me. You'll be telling me that the year I spent watching you become someone wasn't worth finishing."

The machines count. His breath goes ragged.

"I didn't move next door so you could stay the same," I say. "I moved next door so I could watch you open. And you've opened, Ines. God, you've opened. You laugh now. You have a family that learned your name. You played for me in a kitchen with shaking hands and you didn't die. Three weeks is nothing. Three weeks is *everything*. Don't you dare spend them deciding I was a reason to stop."

He is crying. Quietly, the way men cry who learned early that crying was dangerous—no sound, just the eyes overflowing and the jaw held tight against it. I have seen him cry perhaps four times in ten years, and every time it has undone me more than any music.

"I'm frightened," he says. It comes out like a confession wrung from him. "Not of playing. Of—after. Of the room after the room. Of being the one who stays."

"Oh, my love." The endearment slips out before I can weigh it, and I let it go, because we are past weighing. "Of course you are. Staying is the hardest instrument there is. Nobody teaches it. But you've been practicing longer than you know—every Sunday at the bookshop, every dinner, every note slid under my door. You've been learning to stay this whole time."

He takes my hand. His thumb finds the pulse point at my wrist, and rests there, counting what the machines count, as though his own body needs proof of mine.

"Play the concert," I whisper. "Not for me. For the nineteen-year-old. For Priya. For the man you're becoming. I'll be there if I can. And if I can't—"

"Don't." The word breaks out of him. "Don't finish that sentence."

"All right." I turn my hand over and lace my fingers through his, clumsy and slow. "All right. We won't finish sentences tonight. Tonight we'll just stay in this one."

He stays. Of course he stays. He pulls the discouraging chair as close to the bed as it will go, and he holds my hand through the changing of the shifts and the dimming of the corridor lights, and somewhere past midnight, when my eyes will no longer stay open, I hear him speaking softly—not to me, not quite to himself—to the dark, in the letter-voice, the true voice, the voice that has been carrying us across a decade:

*"Stay. I never asked you to be brave, only present."*

My own words, returned to me. He has been reading them all day, I realize, drifting. He keeps them folded in his breast pocket like a talisman, like sheet music for the heart.

I sleep. When I wake in the gray of morning, he is asleep too, upright in the chair, my green notebook open on his knees. And on the page where I once wrote *bar 112 — don't rush the grief*, there is new handwriting beneath it, careful and unmistakably his:

*Bar 112 — I'll be ready. Teach me the rest.*

Below it, a question mark, and below that, in smaller script, as if he could not help himself:

*Will you be at rehearsal Thursday? Say yes. Lie if you have to.*

I pick up the pen with the hand that still answers to me, and I write, slowly, in letters that lean like a woman leaning on a cane:

*Yes. Front row. No lies required.*

And beneath it, because the lowest point is not the end of the road but the place where the road turns, and because I have weeks, actual weeks, and I intend to spend every one of them:

*Now put the notebook down and go home and sleep. The Elgar isn't going anywhere.*

*Neither, my love, am I. Not yet.*
