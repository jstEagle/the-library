## 31. Ada's Argument

The hospital had a way of flattening time. The ceiling tiles above my bed were arranged in a grid, and I had spent the better part of two days memorizing them—the way one tile sat slightly proud of its neighbours, the water stain in the corner shaped like nothing at all, the hairline crack that ran from the vent toward the window like a river on a map of a country I would never visit. I had named it, in my head. The River Disappointment. It flowed into the Sea of Ceiling.

You learn to make your own weather in places like this.

My body had staged its coup on a Tuesday, mid-rehearsal notes still folded in my bag—*the second movement drags, tell him to breathe before the double stops, he rushes when he's afraid*—and then my legs had simply resigned their post, right there in the corridor outside the bookshop's back room, and the world had gone sideways and kind and terrible all at once. Kind, because Marisol caught me. Terrible, because of what I saw on Ines's face as they loaded me into the ambulance: not fear for me, exactly. Something older than that. Recognition. As if my collapse were proof of something he'd always suspected about joy—that it was a loan, and loans get called in.

Now it was Thursday, or possibly Friday. The doctors had come and gone with their gentle arithmetic. *Weeks, not seasons.* They said it the way you'd comment on the weather, apologetic, professional, already halfway out the door. One of them—a young registrar with kind eyes and a lanyard she kept touching like a rosary—had asked if there was anyone I wanted them to call.

"I have people," I said. "They know where I am."

And I did. That was the strange wealth of it. Marisol had been by twice, bringing books I couldn't concentrate on and gossip I could. Tomás had sent flowers with a note in his ferryman's handwriting, all capitals, like a man shouting gently across water: *GET WELL. THE RIVER MISSES YOU. (I KNOW IT'S NOT A RIVER. MARISOL TOLD ME OFF FOR CALLING THE SEA A RIVER.)* Priya had texted me forty-seven times, each message more furious than the last, which was her love language, and had threatened—in writing—to sneak in past visiting hours with contraband chocolate and "opinions."

But Ines hadn't come.

Two days. Two days of the door opening for nurses and doctors and the clatter of trolleys, and never for him. I knew where he was, though. I could feel him the way you feel weather through a wall—out there in the corridor, or downstairs in the lobby, orbiting the building like a shy moon. Priya confirmed it on her Wednesday visit, arms crossed, chin set at the angle she used when she'd decided the world was being cowardly.

"He's in the waiting room," she said. "He's been there since yesterday morning. He bought a coffee from the machine at four a.m. I watched him do it. He just stood there holding it until it went cold."

"Have you talked to him?"

"I told him you were awake and asking for him." She paused, and her voice did the thing it did when she was younger than seventeen for a moment. "He said—he said he needed to be sure he wouldn't make it worse."

I closed my eyes. Of course he did. My beautiful, catastrophic man, standing in a corridor doing the arithmetic of his own unworthiness, concluding—as he always concluded—that his presence was a weight and his absence a kindness. Three years of teaching himself that rooms were safer empty of him, and now the most important room of my life, and he was out there rehearsing his own exclusion.

"Well," I said, "he's wrong."

"He knows he's wrong," Priya said. "That's the worst part. He *knows*."

* * *

He came at dusk.

I heard him before I saw him—not footsteps but hesitation, that particular quality of pause a person makes in a doorway when they are asking permission of a room. Then he was there, filling the frame of it, tall and rumpled, wearing the shirt he'd worn yesterday because I could see the crease of the chair back pressed into it. He had slept sitting up. His hands hung at his sides like instruments nobody had dared to play.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi yourself." I patted the edge of the bed, the strip of blanket beside my hip, because hugging him required logistics now and I wanted none of them between us. He sat carefully, the way he sat with everything, as though the world were made of eggshell. Up close he smelled of hospital coffee and cold air and, underneath it, faintly, rosin. He'd been near the cello. Maybe carrying it. Maybe just standing next to it in his flat, unable to touch it, the way you stand next to a sleeping animal you're afraid of waking.

"They said weeks," he said. No preamble. Ines never learned small talk; he went straight through the wall.

"They said weeks."

"And the concert is in eleven days."

"Yes."

His jaw worked. I watched him build the sentence I knew was coming, watched him lay each brick of it with that terrible conscientiousness of his. When it came, it came out soft and ruinous:

"Ada. You might not—" His breath caught. He started again, quieter. "You might not hear it."

There it was. The whole argument, pre-grieved, pre-decided, wrapped in tenderness so thick you could barely see the retreat underneath. He had come here not to be talked out of quitting but to inform me, gently, that he was going to quit—for my sake, he'd say. Because it was too much, he'd say. Because playing for a hall while I lay dying was more than any man should carry.

I had two days in this bed to prepare my counterargument. I intended to use every hour of preparation.

"Ines," I said. "Look at me."

He looked. His eyes were the colour of strong tea, and they were frightened, and I loved him with a ferocity that surprised even me, that felt less like an emotion than a geological fact.

"Do you remember what you wrote to me? Nineteen years old, three months after the disaster. Letter fourteen. You wrote—" I closed my eyes; I didn't need to see the page; I had carried those sentences for ten years— "*'I think I will spend my life becoming someone who doesn't need an audience. It seems safer. But some nights I practice anyway, alone, and I can't tell if it's devotion or grief.'*"

His face went white around the mouth. "You remember that."

"I remember everything you ever wrote me. That's rather the point of me." I took his hand. His fingers were cold. Cellist's fingers, long and scarred at the tips, the left hand marked by decades of pressing steel strings into wood. "So let me tell you what I see, and then you can argue with me, and we'll see who wins. I've been bedridden for two days with nothing to do but construct arguments. I'm very well armed."

A ghost of a smile crossed him and died young. "Go on, then."

"You think this concert is a gift for me. A dying woman's last request, a bucket-list item, *hear the Elgar before the lights go out.* And yes—I want to hear it. God, Ines, I want it more than I want almost anything. But that's not why it matters, and if you play only for me, you'll fail, because I might not be in the room, and then the whole thing collapses, and you'll walk away saying *see, it was always impossible, I was right to hide.*"

"That's not—"

"It is exactly what you're doing. Don't interrupt a dying woman; it's rude." His hand twitched in mine, half laugh, half flinch. "Here is the truth, and I need you to hold still for it. The concert was never about my hearing it. The concert is about who you become by playing. Not whether you succeed. Not whether the adagio is clean. Whether you *stay*. Whether you stand up in front of forty people and remain a person in the room instead of a ghost fleeing one. You don't perform for the person in the room, Ines. You perform so you can stay in rooms."

The words landed in the silence like stones dropped down a well. I watched him take them, watched them go down and down without echoing.

"If I play badly," he said finally, barely audible, "in front of everyone. If my hands shake. If I stop again, mid-phrase, the way I—"

"Then you stop, and you start again, and you finish. Badly. Bravely. In front of people who brought biscuits." I squeezed his fingers. "Ines, listen to me. I have spent a year watching you unlearn the lie that you must be flawless to deserve space. Do you know what I'll be doing in that front row, if I'm there? Not judging. Witnessing. There's a difference. Judging says *was it good?* Witnessing says *you existed, I saw it.* Forty people, and every one of them came to witness, not to judge. Marisol strung fairy lights, for heaven's sake. Fairy lights, Ines. No critic alive has ever reviewed a room with fairy lights."

He laughed—a broken, wet sound—and pressed his free hand over his eyes. His shoulders shook once, twice. I let him. I had learned, this year, that the kindest thing you can do for a man like Ines Hartwell is to let him break a little without rushing to fix it, because he had spent his whole life being fixed against his will, reshaped by mentors and halls and his own merciless standards, and what he needed was someone to sit beside the wreckage and say *yes, this happened, and you're still here.*

When he lowered his hand, his eyes were red and steady.

"And if you're not in the room," he said. "If it's the day after, or the week after. If I finish the Elgar and turn to the front row and the chair is empty."

I had known this question was coming. It was the real one, under all the others, and I refused to soften it, because softening it would be lying, and I had promised myself a year of truth.

"Then you play it for the empty chair," I said. "And the empty chair will be full. That's the whole secret of audiences, my darling—they're never really made of people. They're made of everyone who taught us to speak. Your father is in yours. That monster Vane is in yours, though God knows he earned no seat. The nineteen-year-old who walked off the stage is in yours. And I will be in yours, whether I'm breathing or not, because I have been your audience for ten years and death is not a sufficient excuse for poor attendance."

"Ada—"

"I'm not finished." I leaned forward, as far as the bed allowed, and made my voice as level and clear as I could, because this was the sentence I had built the whole argument to carry. "You asked me once, in letter sixty-one, why I never let you quit anything. Here is your answer. Quitting feels like safety, but it isn't—it's just a smaller room, and then a smaller one, and eventually you're living in a space the size of your own fear, and they close the door, and you call it peace. I refuse. I refuse to let you shrink to fit your terror. You are going to play that concerto, Ines. You are going to shake, and rush, and probably swear under your breath at the double stops, and you are going to *finish*, and on Monday morning you are going to wake up as a man who stays in rooms. And whatever happens after—however many weeks I get—you will still be that man. That is what I am leaving you. Not a memory. A *capacity*."

The machines around me ticked and hummed. Outside the window, dusk had gone blue, and somewhere below a car door slammed, ordinary life continuing its indifferent business. Ines held my hand in both of his now, bowing his head over it like a man at an altar, and I felt the tremor run through him—not fear anymore, or not only fear. Something being decided.

"The adagio," he said at last, hoarse. "It's nine minutes. If I get through the first movement and fall apart in the adagio—"

"Then you'll have played nine minutes longer than the nineteen-year-old managed. Every minute you stay is a victory over him. Take it minute by minute. That's how I'm doing the rest of it, too." I smiled. "We're quite alike, you and I. We just chose different instruments. Mine is a body that's leaving early. Yours is a stage you fled. Same lesson, both of us: *stay in the room.*"

He raised his head. And there it was—the thing I had waited two days to see, the thing no doctor could prescribe: the exact moment the argument stopped being mine and became his. I watched it happen, the way you watch weather change, the pressure shifting, the light turning. His spine straightened. His hands, those scarred, beautiful, terrified hands, went quiet in his lap.

"Will you take notes?" he asked. "At rehearsal. Like before. Bossy marginalia."

"From the front row," I said. "Wheelchair and all. I intend to be insufferable."

"Good." He swallowed. "Good. Because I've decided something." He looked at me then with a steadiness I had only glimpsed in him before, in stairwells and letters, in the rare unguarded minutes when the armour slipped. "Eleven days. Whatever the doctors say, whatever happens—I'm playing it. All of it. Even if my hands betray me. Even if I have to crawl back to the beginning of the phrase. I'm finishing it, Ada. For the nineteen-year-old. For the empty chair. For you."

"For all of us," I corrected gently. "Never for just one person. That's how music works. That's how staying works."

He bent and kissed my forehead, and his lips lingered there, and I felt him memorizing me, which I permitted, because I was memorizing him right back—the smell of rosin and cold coffee, the tremor finally stilled, the heartbeat under the ruined shirt going steady as a metronome set to *andante, con courage.*

He stayed until the nurses chased him out. At the door he turned, silhouetted against the corridor light, and said, "Rehearsal tomorrow, seven o'clock. Marisol's bringing the good chairs."

"I'll bring the bossy marginalia," I said.

And when he was gone, I lay back against the pillows and looked up at my ceiling—my grid of tiles, my River Disappointment flowing on toward the Sea of Ceiling—and I let myself feel, for one unguarded minute, the terror underneath all my fine arguments. Eleven days. Weeks, not seasons. The arithmetic was brutal and I had lied to no one about it, least of all him.

But I had seen his face change. I had watched a man decide to stay in a room.

Whatever came next—whatever the days held, however few—we would meet them as people who stayed. I closed my eyes, and slept, and dreamed of fairy lights.
