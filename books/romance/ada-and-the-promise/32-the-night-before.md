## 32. The Night Before

Sleep does not come. It circles the flat like a cat that has not yet decided to trust him, brushing once against the window, once against the door, and then withdrawing into the dark of the stairwell where all the unslept nights of his life seem to live.

Ines lies on top of the covers in his clothes because changing into pyjamas would be an admission that this is a night like other nights, and it is not. Tomorrow at four o'clock he will sit in a chair in Marisol's back room with forty people arranged around him like a jury that has already decided to be kind, and he will play the Elgar Concerto—the whole of it, every movement, including the adagio that broke him open eleven years ago in a hall with better acoustics and worse intentions.

He gets up at half past eleven. The floorboards know him; they keep their opinions to themselves.

The letters are in the bottom drawer of the desk, beneath the spare strings and the rosin he no longer uses, wrapped in a scarf Ada knitted for him two winters ago before either of them knew what the word *diagnosis* would come to mean in this building. He unwraps them the way he always does, slowly, as though they might startle. Ten years of envelopes. A decade of Tuesdays, mostly—she had written on Tuesdays, he had answered whenever the silence got too loud—and the paper has gone soft at the folds, the ink faded from blue-black to the colour of shallow water.

He carries them to the kitchen table and sets them down in order, oldest to newest, a paper spine running the length of the wood. Then he makes tea he will not drink and begins at the beginning.

*Dear whoever is reading someone else's post,* says the first one, dated March of a year when he was nineteen and did not yet know what fear could do to a pair of hands. *I believe this parcel was meant for number 14, not 12, but it has your name on it too—or a name close enough to yours that I suspect the universe is being lazy rather than cruel. I have left it with your landlady. Also there was a book inside that fell out and I read the first page, which I am sorry about, except I am not, because it was very good.*

He had written back. He still cannot fully explain why, except that she had signed it only *A*, and something in him had recognized the shape of that letter—a person who also lived slightly sideways to her own life—and had answered before his discipline could intervene. He was nineteen. He had no discipline yet. He had only hands that worked beautifully and a father who watched them work the way a creditor watches collateral.

The second letter. The third. He reads them all, or enough of each to be pulled under, and by one o'clock he has stopped being a man sitting at a kitchen table and become instead a boy moving through the years at the speed of paper: the winter A wrote from three different cities in five months and made displacement sound like a kind of freedom; the summer he told her about the conservatory, about Vane, about the way praise from that man arrived like weather you were expected to survive gratefully; the autumn he wrote her that he had been selected for the debut, and how her reply came back so fast it must have been written before his letter finished arriving—

*I don't want you to play it perfectly,* she had written then. *Perfect is a room with no windows. Play it like someone who lives there.*

He had not understood her. He had thought she meant it as encouragement, a small kindness to tuck into a pocket. He understands now that she had seen the truth of him through two sheets of paper: that he was building a room with no windows and calling it excellence, and that a person can suffocate in a place like that without ever making a sound the audience can hear.

Until, of course, they heard nothing at all.

Two o'clock. The radiator ticks. Outside, the streetlamp paints its usual amber rectangle on the ceiling, and somewhere below, in the flat whose wall shares his own, Ada is presumably asleep, or lying awake doing whatever she does on the nights before things—which, he suspects, is exactly this: keeping vigil over paper, over time, over the small inventory of a life.

He reaches the last letter. Hers, from Tuesday, short, uncharacteristically plain:

*Tomorrow you will be afraid. Good. Fear means you've finally found something worth standing in front of. I'll be in the front row. That's not a threat, it's a promise. —A*

He reads it four times. On the fourth reading he becomes aware that his hands are shaking—not the old shaking, the tremor of collapse, but something closer to the vibration of a string tuned too tightly, holding pitch, refusing to snap.

And then, without deciding to, he pulls a blank sheet toward himself and picks up the pen.

*Dear Ada.*

His hand hovers. Ten years of letters, and he has never once written her the letter that mattered. He wrote her everything else—the small weather of his days, the students, the repairs, the jokes he allowed himself only with her—but never the thing underneath, the load-bearing wound, the reason a nineteen-year-old with the finest hands in his year walked off a stage and did not return for a decade. She deserved it tonight. Tonight, of all nights, she deserved the whole architecture, not the rooms he kept furnished for company.

He writes:

*You have known me ten years and I have never told you the true story of that night. I have told you versions. I told you I was ill. I told you I lost my nerve. I told you, later, when I trusted you with more, that Vane said something unforgivable in the interval. All true. None of it the truth.*

*Here is the truth.*

*My father used to stand in the doorway of the practice room. Not sit—stand, with his coat still on, as though he might need to leave if I disappointed him quickly enough. He had wanted to be a musician once. I learned this the way you learn most things about fathers: late, and from someone else. What I learned directly was the weight of his attention, which was not love, though it wore love's clothing. When I played well he said nothing, which I understood to mean: good, continue justifying my sacrifice. When I played badly he said less than nothing—he would look at his watch. My entire childhood was conducted in that grammar. Play, and be worth watching. Stop, and be a cost.*

*So I learned to play the way soldiers learn to march: not for the music of it, but because the alternative was unthinkable. And it worked. God help me, it worked. I won things. I played things. Teachers called me serious, which is a word adults use for children who have already begun to disappear.*

*Vane understood all of this instantly. Predators always recognize animals raised in captivity. He never had to threaten me. He only had to withhold. For three years he rationed his approval like bread in a siege, and I starved so efficiently that on the morning of my debut I genuinely believed that if the performance went wrong, I would stop existing. Not fail. Cease. Because I had never built anything underneath the playing—no self that could survive a bad afternoon, no window in any of my rooms. He knew that. In the interval, with the critics settling into their seats and my hands already cold, he leaned close and said, "Try to remember, tonight, that you are replaceable."*

*Not cruel, exactly. True, even—that was the genius of it. Every word he ever said to me was true in the way a knife is true. And I walked out for the second half knowing he was right, and the Elgar began, and somewhere in the adagio I looked down at my hands and saw not my hands but his investment, and the school's reputation, and the critics' evening, and my mother's hope, and none of it—none of it—was mine. There was no me in the hall at all. There was a vessel full of other people's meanings, and it tipped over.*

*They say I froze. I didn't freeze. I drowned. There is a difference. Freezing is fear; drowning is discovering there was never any floor. I stood there in front of nine hundred people while the orchestra waited, and I felt myself go under, and the terrible merciful thing—the thing I have never told anyone—is that going under felt like relief. Like finally being allowed to put it down.*

*I walked off. I did not play publicly again. And for eleven years I told myself this was damage, when actually it was doctrine. I had concluded, from all available evidence, that a self is a liability on a stage. So I dismantled mine. Teaching children whose parents watch from the back row. Repairing instruments I never have to perform on. One rule above all others: never again stand anywhere that requires a whole person.*

*Then you moved in next door with a cane and a laugh I knew from paper, and you began—without once asking—to rebuild the windows.*

*Here is what I understand tonight, at three in the morning, with all ten years of you spread across my table: tomorrow I am not going out there to conquer Vane, or the Elgar, or my own history. I am going out there because you taught me that a room with people in it can be a home instead of an auditorium. You will be in the front row, and for the first time in my life the person in the front row will not be weighing me. You will simply be glad I'm there. I have spent my whole life preparing for audiences who were creditors. I do not actually know what to do with a witness.*

*I think I will find out tomorrow.*

*If my hands shake, let them shake. If I stop, I will start again. This is the promise I owed you at nineteen and am only now solvent enough to make: I will stay in the room. Whatever happens in it. I will stay.*

*Yours—in the way that word finally means something—*

*Ines*

He signs it and sits for a long moment with the pen still warm, listening to the building breathe. The radiator. The wind finding the gap in the stairwell window that Tomás keeps promising to fix. From behind Ada's wall, silence, or the particular quality of silence that might contain a person awake.

He does not seal the envelope. Sealing it would make it a finished thing, and finished things can be regretted. Instead he folds it once, stands, and goes out into the cold corridor in his socks.

Her door is three steps away. Under it, the dark seam where the hallway light dies. He kneels—the stone is bitter through the wool of his socks—and slides the letter through, slowly, the way you feed bread to something wild you are hoping to tame. He hears it land on the other side, a soft paper sound, almost nothing.

For a moment he stays crouched there, forehead nearly touching her door, and he thinks he hears—from very far inside, or perhaps only from inside himself—a breath change its rhythm. Awake, then. Or dreaming of him. Either way, the letter is hers now, and the night is nearly spent, and tomorrow there is nowhere left to hide, which is another way of saying there is finally nowhere he needs to.

He goes back inside. He does not sleep, but he stops trying, and instead he takes out the cello and plays, pianissimo, through the wall—just scales, just the opening phrase of the adagio, once, like a hand laid flat against a door.

When the grey light comes, it finds him dressed, fed, and afraid, and ready.

* * *

At noon, Marisol's fairy lights go up in the back room between the poetry shelves and the biography section, forty chairs unfolding one by one with a sound like slow applause. Tomás arrives early to prop the door. Priya comes straight from school with her hair pinned up and the page-turns already marked in pencil.

And at twenty past three, when Ines is tuning in the corner with his back to the room, he hears the wheelchair's wheels on the threshold, and Marisol's voice, gentle and ceremonial: "Front row, as requested."

He turns. Ada is looking at him across forty empty chairs that are beginning, seat by seat, to fill.

She is holding his letter against her chest like sheet music.
