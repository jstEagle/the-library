## 37. The Good Season

The reprieve arrives the way weather does — without permission, without explanation, and with a lightness that makes you suspicious of your own skin.

It begins on a Tuesday in late February, when I wake and my hands are simply hands. Not traitors. Not strangers wearing my gloves. I lie there for a full minute before I trust it, flexing my fingers against the quilt, watching them obey like well-trained animals. I pick up the glass of water on my nightstand one-handed. It does not slip. It does not shatter. It is such a small thing, a glass of water, and I hold it up to the grey window light like an artifact from a lost civilization.

"Look at that," I say to the empty room, and my voice doesn't slur either.

The doctor will call it a plateau later, when I see him, using his careful word — *a period of stabilization* — as though my body were a country that had agreed to a ceasefire it had no intention of honoring. But that morning, before any medical vocabulary can get its hands on it, it is simply this: a good day. Then another. Then a week of them, strung together like lights.

I have learned not to call these weeks anything hopeful out loud. Hope, in my experience, is a landlord who raises the rent without warning. But I let myself feel it privately, the way you let yourself look at something beautiful in a shop window without going inside. The numbness in my left foot recedes to a faint static, ignorable. The fatigue lifts from lead to iron to ordinary tiredness. I walk to the corner shop without the cane twice, then three times, then I stop counting because counting makes it a performance, and I am so tired of performing.

What I do instead is live loudly in small ways. I cook things that require two hands and attention — a proper ragout, bread that needs kneading, kneading being its own kind of prayer. I reorganize the bookshelves Ines and I built together in November, when my arms could barely hold the brackets. I write letters. Real ones, long ones, the kind I used to write him across a decade and an ocean of misdirected parcels, except now I only have to carry them four feet down the hall.

And every night, through the wall, the cello.

He thinks I don't know he knows I'm listening. That's the joke of it now, the sweet private comedy of our lives: Ines Halloran, a man who spent three years perfecting the art of being unheard, playing *on purpose*, at volume, at nine o'clock sharp, as if keeping an appointment. He starts with scales — always the scales, that patient ascending ladder — and then whatever he's working on, and then, most nights, something with no name, something slow and wandering that I've come to think of as mine, because he only plays it when he knows I'm home.

I press my palm flat against the wall sometimes, feeling the wood hum. It's not the same as hearing. It's better, in a way. It's hearing with my whole body, the vibration traveling through plaster and paint and bone, and I think: *this is what music was always trying to do anyway. Get under the skin.*

*

On the first Saturday of March, Marisol declares — there is no other word for it, Marisol does not suggest, she declares — that we are going to the sea.

"The sea," Tomás repeats skeptically, from his chair by the shop door where he holds court over the crossword. "In March."

"In March," Marisol confirms, arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who has already won. "Ada wants to hear it again while it's cold. She told me so." She looks at me. "You told me so."

"I may have mentioned it," I say, which is true. It was item three on the list we made in January, taped inside Ines's kitchen cupboard: *hear the sea in winter.* We'd done it once, in the bad week after the hospital, when Ines carried me the last mile and I laughed so hard I cried, and I had wanted it again not because the first time wasn't enough but because the first time felt stolen — snatched between storms. This time I want to be present for it. All of me, as much of me as there is.

Priya arrives at eight with a thermos of her mother's chai and the imperious energy of a sixteen-year-old who has appointed herself logistics coordinator. She has opinions about blankets (we need three), about seating (I get the bench seat, no arguments), and about music ("no sad cello in the car, Mr. Halloran, I'm serious"). Ines submits to all of it with the docility of a man who has learned that resistance only encourages her.

He drives. Of course he drives — his ancient car that smells of rosin and old paper, the one with the dented fender he refuses to fix because, he says, the dent happened the day Priya's recital went well and he's superstitious now. I ride shotgun. This is also non-negotiable, established by treaty. Tomás and Marisol fold into the back with Priya between them, and by the time we clear the town limits we are arguing about whether the sea counts as an instrument.

"It's percussion," Tomás says. "Thirty years on the water, I should know. It keeps time whether you like it or not."

"It's a drone," Priya says. "A really smug drone."

"It's an audience," I say, and everyone goes quiet for a second, because they know what I mean, and then Marisol says, "Well, it's a rude audience, it talks over everything," and we're laughing again.

The coast road unspools grey and silver. I watch the hedgerows give way to gorse, the gorse give way to nothing but wind-bent grass and sky, and I do the thing I have trained myself to do on good days, which is to memorize them. Not photograph — memorize. The exact grey-green of the sea in March. The way Tomás points out a wreck marker buoy with the authority of a man pointing out an old colleague. The way Priya falls asleep mid-argument, her head tipping onto Marisol's shoulder, her hand still loosely holding the thermos lid. The way Ines drives with both hands at ten and two, careful as a man carrying water, glancing at me at the turns — not anxiously anymore, just checking, the way you check a fire is still lit.

At the beach, the wind is enormous. It takes my breath literally, pushes it back into my chest, and I stand there laughing at the sheer physical rudeness of it while Marisol wrestles blankets onto the sand and Tomás produces, from some infinite pocket, a flask of coffee laced with enough whiskey to be medicinal.

We walk to the waterline. I walk it myself, cane in hand but mostly ceremonial, the wet sand firm under my boots. The sea in winter sounds different than the sea in summer — less friendly, more honest. It isn't performing for anyone. It just keeps saying what it says, wave after wave, the oldest sentence in the world.

"What's it saying?" Priya asks. She's beside me, hood up, cheeks whipped pink.

I listen. Really listen, the way I listened to Ines practice Elgar at midnight, the way I listen to everything now, rationing nothing because there's no reason to ration.

"It's saying *again,*" I tell her. "It's just saying *again, again, again.*"

Priya considers this. "That's kind of depressing."

"That's kind of the whole point of everything," I say, and she laughs, and the sea says *again,* and I stand there in the cold with my whole strange family scattered along the tideline behind me, and I think: *if this is the shape of my life, look at the shape of it. Look how wide.*

Ines comes to stand next to me. He doesn't take my arm unless I offer it, which is one of the ten thousand things I love about him — he understands that help offered without asking is a kind of theft. We watch the water together. His coat smells of rosin and cold air.

"Thank you for driving," I say.

"You planned this," he says. "I just held the wheel."

"Same thing, in this family."

Something crosses his face when I say *family* — it always does, a flicker of wonder, like a man still surprised to find himself warm indoors. Three years ago this man rehearsed solitude like scales. Now he stands on a freezing beach holding a thermos lid for Tomás and being argued with by teenagers, and he hasn't run. I look at him sideways and store that away too: the set of his shoulders, easy for once. The good season is doing its work on all of us.

*

The good weeks keep coming, and I spend them like a woman who has finally stopped saving for a rainy day and realized it rains here constantly anyway.

I finish Priya's lessons — or rather, we finish them together, because teaching her has become less instruction than conversation. Her bow arm has loosened into something confident and slightly reckless, entirely her own. One Thursday afternoon, in my sitting room with the radiator ticking, she sets down the étude she's supposed to be working on and says, "Can I play you something? Not for a grade or anything."

"Always."

She plays Bach. The Sarabande from the Fifth Suite — the one written, people say, during some private grief of Bach's own, though no one knows what. She plays it slowly, imperfectly, with a seriousness that makes my throat close. There's a phrase in the middle where she lets a note die completely before moving on, and it's not technically what the score asks, and it's the most musical thing I've heard all year.

When she finishes, the silence sits with us for a moment, respectful.

"That pause," I say. "In the fourth bar of the second section. Where did you get that?"

She shrugs, embarrassed, pleased. "I dunno. It felt like it needed to stop for a second. Like it was remembering something."

"It was," I say. "That's exactly what it was doing."

She looks at me sharply — she's sharp-eyed, this girl, sharper than any of us would like — and I brace for the question, the one about why I play everything like it might be the last time, the one she must be circling closer and closer to. But she doesn't ask. Instead she says, "Will you write that down? What you just said? For when I forget it?"

So I write it on the inside cover of her notebook, in my best handwriting, the one that still obeys me: *Let the music remember things. That's its job. Yours is just to let it.* She reads it twice, nods like a general accepting terms, and packs up her cello.

After she leaves, I sit by the window and let myself feel the full weight of the afternoon. This is what I came here for, I think. Not the sea, not even Ines, exactly — though God, yes, Ines, always Ines — but this. To be known by a roomful of people. To matter in the specific, unglamorous way a teacher matters, a neighbour matters, a friend matters. I spent forty years being interesting to strangers and invisible to everyone else. I have six months, maybe, maybe more, and I am spending them on the only currency that ever mattered.

*

At night, the wall sings.

Nine o'clock, sometimes ten. Scales first, then études, then — increasingly — whole movements, played through, mistakes and all, because he has stopped polishing and started *saying*. I've taken to leaving my bedroom window cracked even in the cold so the sound has a path, absurd as that is, sound traveling through walls and not weather, but superstition is a comfort and I've earned mine.

One night I knock on the wall. Two knocks, soft. A pause. Then, from his side, three knocks back — our code, established in the stairwell weeks ago, meaning *I hear you, I'm here.* I knock twice more: *again.* And he plays the unnamed piece, the slow wandering one, and I fall asleep to it with my hand against the wall like a child at a séance, and dream of nothing at all, which is the best sleep there is.

In the mornings we have breakfast sometimes, when my hands cooperate — he makes eggs, terrible eggs, eggs punished beyond recognition, and I eat them with genuine joy because they're his. We read the paper aloud to each other, dividing sections like rations. He does the obituaries now without flinching, which took months, and I never comment on it, which took everything I have.

But grace, I know — I have always known, since the diagnosis, since long before — is not a promise. It's a loan. Some nights I wake at three and lie still, taking inventory: fingers, yes; feet, mostly; voice, present. And the inventory always comes back fine these days, fine fine fine, and I lie there in the dark knowing the ledger will turn over eventually, the way seasons do, and I refuse — I *refuse* — to spend the good weeks standing guard against the bad ones. That was my old life. Hiding the pills, hiding the limp, managing grief like a schedule. I did my time in that country. I emigrated.

Still. Some habits are load-bearing.

There's a letter on my desk that I've been writing for eleven days. It's addressed to Ines. It is not finished. Every evening I sit down at the little desk by the window — his Christmas gift to me, oak, with a drawer that sticks — and I add a line or two, and then I stop, because the letter keeps turning into the wrong letter. It keeps wanting to be instructions. *Keep the Sunday circle alive. Keep playing. Let people stay.* And those things are true, they're the truest things I know, but a letter of instructions is a letter written by someone who has already left, and I am not gone yet. I am here. My hands work. The sea said *again* and I intend to hold it to that.

So I write other things instead. I write down what Marisol said about her mother's garden. I write down the exact pitch of Tomás's laugh. I write down that Priya's Sarabande paused in the fourth bar because it was remembering something. I fill pages with the inventory of a good season, hoarding detail the way other people hoard money, and the unfinished letter waits underneath them like a foundation stone.

Tonight the wall is quiet — Ines is at the music school, his new Tuesday class, seven beginners aged nine to seventy, a job he accepted with the expression of a man signing away a kidney and discovering it didn't hurt. So I put on the kettle, and I sit at the sticking drawer, and I take out the unfinished letter, and I read what I have so far.

*Dear Ines. By the time you read this—*

No. I cross it out. Not yet. Not by the time. While. Everything, while.

I start again, and this time the pen moves easily, the words arriving like the tide, one after another, *again, again, again* — and outside the window the first of the spring rain begins, soft against the glass, and somewhere down the coast the sea is saying its one sentence to an empty beach, keeping my place until I can come back and hear it.
