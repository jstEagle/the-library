## 1. The Muscle That Doesn't Break

The string quartet was playing Vivaldi's "Winter," which Kestrel Underhill found either a private joke or an accident of taste, and he had learned long ago that accidents of taste were the only honest things at a wedding.

He stood in the shadow of the rose arbor with his hands folded behind his back, watching two hundred guests settle into white chairs on the great lawn of Hollis Manor like snow settling on a field — slowly, inevitably, each one finding their appointed place. The bride's mother had wept twice during rehearsal and would weep four times today; he had charted it. The groom's best man had already loosened his tie, so Kestrel had stationed a waitress with champagne within arm's reach of him for exactly this reason. The flower girl, six years old and ferociously proud of her basket, would freeze halfway down the aisle unless someone she trusted walked beside her, so he had assigned her grandmother to the second row and told the grandmother, quietly, to stand when the music began.

All of it moving. All of it handled.

"Kestrel." Priya, his floor manager, materialized at his elbow with her tablet hugged to her chest like a shield. "The caterer wants to know if the canapé service starts before or after the processional."

"After. Ninety seconds after. The guests need something to do with their hands while they wait for the vows."

"You've timed it?"

"To the second." He smiled the smile he kept for vendors — warm, brief, entirely load-bearing. "Tell Marco if the seared scallops sit longer than four minutes I will personally feed them to the swans."

Priya laughed and went, and Kestrel turned back to the lawn, and for one unguarded moment he let himself see it the way he never let clients see him seeing it: as choreography. As theater. Two hundred people dressed in their most expensive hope, gathered to witness a contract being signed with mouths instead of ink. He did not believe in what happened under arbors. He believed in what happened *around* them — the logistics, the light, the timing of tears. Love, in his professional opinion, was mostly weather. What you could actually build was shelter.

His therapist — three sessions, before he'd decided he could manage the work himself, which told its own story — had once asked him why he'd chosen this profession if he disdained it so thoroughly.

*Because I'm good at it,* he'd said.

*That's not an answer,* she'd said.

It was the only answer he had. He was good at it the way a swimmer is good at water: not because he loved it but because he had learned, early and completely, that the alternative was drowning.

His father used to say the heart was a muscle, and muscles were built by resistance, and if you trained it right it wouldn't break. His father had been wrong about nearly everything else, but Kestrel had kept that one, polished it, made it doctrine. Train the muscle. Don't use it for anything heavy. A heart that lifts only small weights never fails you in public.

The processional began. The grandmother stood. The flower girl, seeing her anchor rise, marched down the aisle with the grim determination of a soldier crossing a bridge, and somewhere in the third row a man Kestrel had never met wiped his eyes, and Kestrel felt the familiar thing happen — the small, involuntary ache behind his sternum, the one that meant the muscle was working whether he wanted it to or not.

He breathed through it. Four counts in, six out. He had a system for everything, including feeling.

* * *

The reception unfolded like a tide chart come true. Toasts at 6:40, first dance at 7:15, cake cut at 8:30 sharp because the bride had opinions about melting buttercream and Kestrel shared them. He moved through the ballroom the way water moves through a hull — everywhere at once, visible nowhere, fixing things before anyone noticed they were broken. A candle guttered; he relit it. A grandmother's chair sat too far from the table; he moved it eleven inches and the whole geometry of her evening changed. This was the work, the real work, the part no one thanked you for because they never saw it happen, and Kestrel had built an entire life inside that invisibility. It was comfortable there. Nothing could reach you in the dark between other people's happiness.

At 9:12, his phone buzzed against his ribs.

He almost didn't check it. Clients came first; the rule was absolute. But the name on the screen stopped his thumb mid-swipe, and he stepped through the French doors onto the terrace, into air that smelled of cut grass and cooling stone, before he answered.

"Margot."

"Kestrel Underhill." Her voice hadn't changed. Low, amused, with that faint rasp at the end of sentences like a match being struck. "Still answering calls during receptions? You haven't changed at all."

"We're not doing this."

"We absolutely are, briefly, and then I'm going to offer you more money than you've ever been offered, and you're going to hate how fast you say yes." A pause. He could hear wind on her end — sea wind, the hollow whistle of it over open water. "How are you?"

"I'm working."

"That's what I thought." No judgment in it, which was somehow worse. Margot Vance had been his ex-wife for six years and his friend for none of them, and yet she still spoke to him in the tone of someone reading his vitals off a monitor. "I'll skip the pleasantries. There's a wedding. Remote coastal inn, north of Half Moon Bay — the Gullwing House, if you know it. Private client. Very private. The family is paying triple your rate plus expenses, and they want the best, and despite everything, you're still the best."

"How much is triple?"

She named the number.

Kestrel looked out past the terrace rail, at the dark lawn where the string lights glowed like a constellation someone had knocked loose from the sky. The number was not simply large. It was *specifically* large. It was the number that lived in a folder on his laptop labeled HOLLOWAY HOUSE, next to photographs of a sagging porch and a roofline gone soft with rot — the house in Mendocino County where he'd grown up, sold out from under his family when he was nineteen, the only asset his father had ever owned and the first thing the creditors took. Eleven years of saving. Eleven years of weddings built from other people's joy, and he was still forty thousand short of buying back the only place he'd ever called home.

"The date?" he asked, keeping his voice level.

"Three weeks. Winter solstice weekend. Off-season, obviously — the inn's closed otherwise."

"A winter wedding on the coast. In December." He shook his head even though she couldn't see it. "Margot, nobody plans a destination wedding for solstice weekend unless they don't care about the weather, and nobody doesn't care about the weather unless—"

"Unless the date matters more than the comfort," she finished. "Yes. I noticed that too. You always did notice the wrong-shaped details."

"What family?"

Silence. Long enough that he pulled the phone from his ear to check the call hadn't dropped.

"Margot."

"They gave me a confidentiality agreement thicker than the marriage license," she said. "But you'll sign yours and then you'll know anyway, so I'll tell you this much for free: the name should mean something to you. The patriarch's name is Aldous Vane."

The terrace seemed to tilt, very slightly, the way a deck does when a wave passes underneath.

Vane.

Kestrel was nine years old again, standing in the doorway of his father's study, watching a man in a gray coat fold papers into his briefcase while his father sat very still at the desk with his hands flat on the wood, the way you keep your hands flat when you are holding yourself down. He hadn't understood the words then — *foreclosure*, *acquisition*, *the bank has no further interest in protecting you* — but he had understood the gray coat, and the smell of cold air coming off it, and the way his mother cried in the kitchen afterward without any sound at all, washing dishes that were already clean.

Aldous Vane had bought Holloway House at auction for less than it was worth and torn out the orchard the following spring. Kestrel had read about it in the county records, years later, with a kind of clinical calm that had fooled even him.

"Kestrel?" Margot's voice, distant now. "You still there?"

"Why," he said carefully, "does Aldous Vane want me planning his family's wedding?"

"He doesn't want *you*. He wants the best. And—" the match-strike rasp again, softer this time, "—there's a co-planner. Already contracted. The client insisted on two names on the agreement. I don't know who picked the second one, but Kestrel — when I saw the name, I thought of you immediately, and not for professional reasons."

The ballroom behind him swelled with applause — the first dance ending, the crowd surging toward the couple like warmth toward a fire. Inside, everyone was holding someone. Outside, the December air pressed cold against Kestrel's face, and the number in the folder labeled HOLLOWAY HOUSE sat in his chest like a stone he'd swallowed years ago and never passed.

"Tell me the name," he said.

"Wren Calloway."

The muscle didn't break. He'd trained it too well for that. But something in it tore — a small clean rip, deep tissue, the kind of injury you don't feel until you try to lift something heavy — and Kestrel gripped the terrace rail and watched his own breath leave him in a white ribbon and dissolve.

Wren. Who had loved him with a ferocity that frightened them both. Who had asked him, once, on a night he still refused to remember clearly, to choose — her, or the career, the climb, the flawless machine he was building himself into. Who had left before he could answer, or perhaps because he'd already answered with every choice he'd ever made.

"When do I start?" he heard himself say.

Margot laughed, low and unsurprised. "Three weeks. Pack warm, Kestrel. They say the storms up there come in off the water like nothing you've ever seen."

The line went dead. Behind him, the bride laughed at something her new husband whispered, bright as breaking glass, and Kestrel Underhill straightened his cuffs, fixed his smile in place, and walked back into the light to finish a wedding he no longer believed in — carrying, for the first time in eleven years, a weight the muscle wasn't trained to hold.
