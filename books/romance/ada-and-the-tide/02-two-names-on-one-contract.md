## 2. Two Names on One Contract

The inn announced itself the way old money always did — quietly, and with the assumption you'd already heard of it.

Kestrel Underhill came up the coastal road at dusk, the sea a flat pewter sheet to his left, the building rising out of the salt haze like something the tide had decided, after long consideration, to keep. Saltwood House, the sign said. Weathered shingles the color of driftwood. Windows lit amber against the coming dark. He had planned four hundred weddings and he could read a venue in ten seconds flat: this one was beautiful the way a locked room is beautiful.

He parked, checked his reflection out of habit rather than vanity, and gave himself the usual briefing. *You are here to work. The Halloran wedding is a contract, not a memory. You will be flawless, and you will feel nothing.*

The mantra had served him for eleven years. He recited it now like a man taking vitamins — not because he believed in it that afternoon, but because he'd stopped believing in things that worked only when you believed.

Inside, the lobby smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke and cold sea air dragged in on someone's coat. A fire burned low in a stone hearth the size of a garage door. And behind the front desk, arguing gently with a registration ledger as though it were a stubborn vendor, stood a woman with ink-stained fingers and a cardigan the green of deep water.

Kestrel's body knew her before his mind consented to.

It happened in the spine first — a straightening, an alertness, the posture of a man hearing his name called across a crowded room. Then the hands, which had been relaxed for exactly zero seconds of the last decade, went still. His heart did something he would later refuse to describe as anything but *a scheduling error*.

Wren Calloway looked up from the ledger.

For a moment neither of them moved. The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a door closed with the soft finality of a period at the end of a sentence.

"You have got," Wren said, "to be kidding me."

"Wren." Her name came out of him intact, unedited, which alarmed him more than anything else she might have said. He'd rehearsed nothing. He'd assumed he'd never need to.

"They didn't tell you either." She set down her pen with exaggerated care, the way she did when she was furious enough to want to throw something. He remembered that. He remembered everything, which was the problem with memories — they kept no calendar, observed no statute of limitations. "Halloran Events hired me three weeks ago. Co-planner, they said. They said the other planner was *local*."

"I'm told the other planner has fifteen years of experience and a waiting list."

"Fifteen years." She laughed once, without humor. "You counted."

"I count everything. It's the job."

"It was never just the job, Kestrel. That was the whole problem."

An older couple checking in glanced between them with the frank delight of people who had stumbled into theater. Kestrel smiled at them automatically — the wedding smile, the one that cost nothing and meant less — and steered Wren toward the corridor by the hearth, out of audience range.

"Whoever assigned us both," he said quietly, "knew exactly what they were doing."

"That's paranoid."

"That's logistics. Nobody hires two planners for a sixty-guest wedding by accident. Either they wanted redundancy or they wanted *us*. I don't love either option."

Wren crossed her arms. Up close she was thinner than he remembered, and sharper, her hair cut short now, silver starting early at her temples like frost claiming a window first thing in the morning. But her eyes hadn't changed at all — brown and quick and merciless, eyes that had once looked at him across a shared apartment and seen every corner he was hiding in.

"Why are you here?" she asked. "Genuinely. You used to turn down jobs that required leaving Manhattan for more than forty-eight hours."

"The fee was unusual."

"Everything about this is unusual. The bride hasn't answered a single planning email. The groom arrived yesterday and hasn't spoken to his own mother. The patriarch—" she lowered her voice further, "—Edmund Halloran requested your file specifically. Your *file*, Kestrel. Who requests a planner's file?"

Kestrel felt the floor tilt a degree, the way a ship's deck tilts when you've been standing still too long. "What did you tell them?"

"That I'd worked with you. That you were competent." A pause, precise as a scalpel. "I left out the rest."

"There is no rest."

"No," she agreed softly. "There's only what we buried. Different thing entirely."

Before he could answer — before he could decide whether that sentence was a wound or an invitation — a voice came down the corridor like a draft under a door.

"Ah. My two planners."

Edmund Halloran was seventy if he was a day, tall and dry as winter grass, dressed in a sweater that probably cost more than Kestrel's car and looked like it had been chosen by a man who considered clothing beneath consideration. His eyes were pale blue and utterly without warmth, and they moved between Kestrel and Wren with an expression Kestrel could only call *satisfaction* — the satisfaction of a man watching two pieces arrive on a board he'd set up years ago.

"Mr. Underhill. Ms. Calloway. I trust introductions won't be necessary."

"We've met," Wren said.

"So I understand." Edmund smiled. It did not reach anything above his cheekbones. "I want this weekend handled perfectly. My granddaughter is marrying into the Ashford family, and the merger — the *marriage*," he corrected himself, so smoothly Kestrel almost missed it, "deserves the best. You'll find the guest list in your offices. You'll be sharing the planning suite; the storm has compressed our accommodations."

"What storm?" Kestrel asked.

Edmund looked toward the tall windows, where the sky over the water had gone the color of a bruise. "The one arriving tonight. The roads will close by morning. Which means, Mr. Underhill, that for the next several days, no one leaves Saltwood House." He said it the way other men said *congratulations*. "I do hope that suits you both."

He walked away down the corridor, unhurried, a man who had never once needed to hurry because the world arranged itself around him instead.

Kestrel watched him go and felt, with terrible clarity, that he had seen this man before — not in person, but in the architecture of his own childhood. In the way his father had gone quiet at dinner whenever certain names came up. In the word *Halloran*, spoken once, in 1998, in a kitchen in Oregon, in the tone people reserved for weather disasters and cancer diagnoses.

"Kestrel." Wren's hand was on his sleeve. When had that happened? "You've gone white."

"I know that man's name."

"Everyone knows his name. He owns half the coastline."

"Not that kind of knowing." He turned to look at her, and the lobby, the fire, the sea beyond the glass all seemed to recede, leaving the two of them standing in the exact shape of a question he'd spent twenty years refusing to ask. "Wren, why did you really take this job?"

She held his gaze. Behind her, through the window, the first snow began to fall — fat, slow flakes, unhurried, like ash drifting down from some enormous, patient fire.

"Same reason as you, I'd guess," she said. "The money was strange, the client was stranger, and something in my gut said if I didn't come, I'd spend the rest of my life wondering." Her fingers tightened on his sleeve, then released. "Welcome to Saltwood House, Kestrel. Try not to ruin us both twice."

* * *

Wren had rehearsed this moment for eleven years, which was fortunate, because none of the rehearsals survived contact.

In her versions, she was composed. In her versions, she said the devastating thing at the perfect time and walked away with her dignity folded neatly over her arm like a coat. In reality she had said *you have got to be kidding me*, which was not devastating, and then touched his sleeve, which was worse than devastating, because her hand had remembered him — the specific weave of his coat, the warmth underneath — and her hand had no loyalty to her whatsoever.

Now she sat in the planning suite — one long room above the kitchen wing, two desks pushed together under a leaded window, boxes of wedding collateral stacked like sandbags — and watched Kestrel unpack.

He unpacked the way he did everything: in categories. Binders labeled by tab. A pencil case with individual slots. A small tin of mints he set precisely equidistant from both their desks, and the sight of it — that instinctive courtesy, that unconscious making of room for her — made her chest hurt in a way she filed immediately under *later*.

"Seating chart first," he said. "Then vendor confirmations, then we corner the bride. If she won't answer emails, she'll answer a woman standing in her doorway with a fabric swatch."

"You haven't changed."

"I've changed considerably. I'm much worse now."

Despite herself — despite eleven years and one unfinished conversation and a departure she still defended in her head at three in the morning — Wren laughed. It escaped before she could catch it, and she saw him register the escape, saw something flicker behind his eyes that was not professionalism.

She looked away, at the window. The snow was thickening, the world outside going soft and white and silent, the sea invisible now beyond the whiteout's curtain. Somewhere below, the inn's generator coughed and caught.

"Tell me about the bride," she said, businesslike, anchoring herself.

"Celeste Halloran-Ashford. Twenty-six. Heiress. Marrying Daniel Ashford tomorrow evening, weather permitting." Kestrel opened a folder. "Which brings us to the interesting part. Look at the RSVPs."

Wren took the pages. Guest list, sixty names, and beside each one a notation in Kestrel's narrow handwriting. She scanned it, then read it again, slower.

"Half these people are investors," she said. "Not friends. Not family. There are *fund managers* invited to this wedding. There are two men from a shipping consortium. Where are the bride's college friends? Where's anyone under fifty who isn't staff?"

"Now you see it."

"It's not a wedding. It's a shareholder meeting with flowers."

"It's a beautiful shareholder meeting with flowers," Kestrel said. "That's my specialty."

She looked up at him. He was watching the snow, and in profile he was still the man she'd loved at twenty-four — the stubborn jaw, the tired eyes older than the face — except now the tiredness went all the way down. She hated that she noticed. She hated more that some traitorous part of her had spent the drive up the coast composing sentences she'd never send, and that seeing him had made every one of them true.

"Why did you leave Manhattan?" she asked. "Really. You loved it there. You *were* it there."

A long pause. Snow ticked against the glass.

"My father's house is being sold," he said finally. "The one in Oregon. Bank-owned. The one we lost when I was fourteen, after—" He stopped. Recalibrated. She watched him do it, watched the athlete training the muscle, watched him lock the door on whatever lived behind it. "After some things. This job buys it back. That's all you need to know tonight."

"After *some things*," Wren repeated. "Eleven years, and you're still filing your life under tabs."

"And you're still reading other people's mail."

"Only when they leave it open."

They glared at each other across the joined desks, and the glare lasted perhaps four seconds before it collapsed into something else — something older and warmer and far more dangerous, the two of them suddenly aware of how small the room was, how the candle-colored lamplight fell, how the storm pressed at the windows like a crowd leaning in.

Wren stood first. "I'm going to check on the bride."

"She'll be asleep."

"Then I'll check on the files. Edmund Halloran gave us a full archive of family documents for place cards and table history." She pulled her cardigan on, not looking at him. "And Kestrel — if there's something in those files about your father, I'd rather hear it from you than from paper."

He said nothing. The silence said enough.

She was halfway down the back stairs, lantern in hand, the inn groaning around her like a ship, when she passed the library and saw, through the gap in the doors, Edmund Halloran standing at the fire with a folder open in his hands — a folder whose cover page, visible for only a heartbeat before he closed it, bore a name she recognized from a lifetime ago.

*Underhill.*

The snow sealed the last of the road behind her, and somewhere above, the lights flickered once, and held.
