## 8. Everything We Bury, the Tide Returns

Wren found the papers the way the sea finds things it has swallowed — by accident, and all at once.

The generator had stuttered back to life at dawn, just enough to wake the office printer, and she had gone down to the planning suite to reprint the seating chart that Kestrel's coffee had ruined two days ago. The folder was where he always left it, squared to the desk's edge with that maddening precision of his, everything in its place, everything under control. *Underhill*, she thought. *A man who alphabetizes his grief.*

She lifted the folder and the wind shifted against the inn's east windows, a long low moan like something turning over in its sleep, and three pages slid free from behind the seating chart. Heavy stock. Cream-colored. A solicitor's letterhead she didn't recognize but whose name she did — Halvard Voss had signed her own severance once, years ago, in another life with colder lighting.

*Deed of Transfer.* Her eyes went down the page the way a hand goes into cold water, fast, braced for the shock.

The Ashcombe house. The stone cottage on the harbor road with the blue door Kestrel had described to her at three in the morning, candlelit, wine-warm, his voice cracking on the word *home*. The house he'd said he wanted back more than he'd ever wanted anything. The house Voss had taken from his father along with his father's good name.

Signed. Dated yesterday. Kestrel Underhill's signature sat at the bottom like a small dark animal, curled and final.

Below it, in Voss's slanted hand, a rider: *In consideration of the full and timely delivery of the Merrow-Voss nuptial event.*

Delivery. As if the wedding were freight. As if Isolde Merrow were cargo to be moved from one life into another, whether she fit or not.

Wren stood very still in the gray light. The radiator ticked. Somewhere above her, footsteps crossed the landing — guests waking, storm-bound, alive — and she felt the floor tilt beneath her, not physically, nothing so kind as physics. It was the older tilt, the one she knew from the worst year of her twenties: the moment when a man you loved showed you the ledger where your heart had been entered as a line item.

He'd told her about the house. He'd held her hands and told her about the house, and she had thought — God help her, she had actually thought — that he was choosing. That telling her was the beginning of choosing.

But he'd already signed.

* * *

She found him at ten o'clock in the great room, because of course she did; the whole stranded household gravitated there now, the way heat gravitates to a window. The storm had spent itself into long white gusts, and the light through the mullioned glass was the color of skimmed milk. Someone had lit the fire. The bride sat wrapped in a quilt near it, looking smaller than her dress size, watching the flames with the flat calm of a woman who has already left a place in her mind. The groom stood at the sideboard with a tumbler he wasn't drinking, and old Halvard Voss presided from the wing chair by the hearth like weather given a body.

Kestrel was at the planning table, sleeves rolled, reworking the timeline in his beautiful ruthless handwriting. He looked up when she came in, and Wren watched his face do the thing it had done since they were twenty-six — the quick brightening, the guardedness arriving half a second late, like a lock catching after the door has already swung.

She laid the papers on the table between them. She set them down gently, which took everything she had.

"Tell me I'm wrong," she said. "Tell me this isn't what it looks like."

The room quieted the way rooms do — not all at once but in rings, conversation dying outward from the table like frost spreading across a pane. Kestrel looked at the pages. Then at her. And Wren saw him weigh it, saw the athlete in him flexing the muscle, the detachment rising up to meet the moment — and failing. For the first time since she'd known him, in this life or any other, Kestrel Underhill's face simply fell open.

"It's not—" He stopped. Started again. "It's exactly what it looks like. But it isn't what you think."

"You signed it." Her voice came out low and even, and she hated how much control was in it, how much of him she'd caught like a fever. "Yesterday. Before you told me. Before you sat with me in the dark and told me about your father, about the house, about—" Her breath snagged. "You let me think you were deciding."

"I was deciding." He stood, palms flat on the table, and the timeline slid sideways under his hands. "Wren, I signed it because it was the only door out. You don't know what he holds. What he did to my father wasn't just the house — he buried him. Ruin follows ruin; there are people still alive who believe my father was a thief because Voss made sure of it. This contract clears the name. It gives back the house. All it costs is—"

"Isolde." The bride's voice, from the fire. Quiet. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — *understanding*. "All it costs is me."

Isolde rose from the quilt. Up close, without the armor of the gown fittings, she looked young in a way the wedding industry had worked hard to disguise — young and tired and strangely relieved, as though the last forty-eight hours had been a long exhale.

"He offered you my wedding," she said to Kestrel, "in exchange for your father's grave. And you took it." She turned to the room, to the groom, to Voss. "Did everyone take it? Did anyone come here for a marriage?"

The groom set down his untouched glass. "I came here for the merger," he said, almost gently. "I assumed we all knew."

"We didn't," said Isolde.

"No," Voss said, from the wing chair, and the single syllable dropped the temperature of the room. He unfolded himself slowly, an old animal conserving its strength. "They didn't. Which is why the weekend required professionals." His pale eyes found Kestrel first, then Wren, and Wren understood, with a lurch, that they had never been hired to plan a wedding. They had been hired to be watched. Two names on one contract — two people with history, with leverage over each other, with wounds Voss could press like buttons. "Miss Calloway. You found the file with the elder Underhill's name in it. Curious, how things surface in storms."

"You put it there," Wren said.

"I put many things where they needed to be." Voss smiled without warmth, a negotiation smile, a closing-the-deal smile. "Mr. Underhill signed freely. Ask him. Everything in this room was chosen freely — the bride's silence, the groom's indifference, the planner's pen. Freedom is such a convenient word. It means no one can be blamed."

"And it means," Kestrel said, and his voice changed — Wren heard it change, heard twenty years of trained composure burn off it like fog off water, "that I'm free to tear it up."

He picked up the contract. Held it a moment, weighing it — and Wren knew what he was weighing, because she'd have weighed the same thing: the blue door, the harbor road, his father's name made clean again, the whole resurrected past sitting right there in his hands, one signature deep.

Then he walked to the fireplace, past Voss, past the bride, past her, and fed the pages into the flames.

They caught slowly, cream going brown going black, the solicitor's seal curling like a living thing. Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Outside, the wind threw a fistful of snow against the glass and moved on.

"The house stays lost," Kestrel said, to the fire, to his father, to the room. "Fine. It was never mine. But this—" he turned, and his eyes were wet and furious and more awake than Wren had ever seen them, "—this was never yours either. None of it. Not her marriage, not their silence, not us."

Voss regarded the ashes with something that might, in a lesser man, have been respect. "You've just cost yourself everything, Mr. Underhill."

"No," said Isolde Merrow, and she stepped forward out of the quilt and the firelight, spine straightening vertebra by vertebra, a woman assembling herself in real time. "He's cost *you* everything. Because I've been packing that suitcase since September, and I only needed a reason to say it out loud." She looked around the room, at the strangers the storm had made into witnesses, into family, into something none of them had planned. "So here it is, said out loud, in front of everyone: the wedding is off. Not postponed. Off."

The groom raised his glass to her — the first honest gesture Wren had seen him make — and drank.

Voss stood very slowly. "You will all regret—"

"No," Wren said, and heard her own voice come out steady, and realized she meant it the way Kestrel meant things now, wholly, untrained. "We won't. That's the thing about storms, Mr. Voss. They dig up everything you bury. And the tide brings the rest."

For a moment the old man simply looked at them — the burned contract, the freed bride, the two planners standing shoulder to shoulder — and whatever calculation was running behind those pale eyes, it had run out of room.

Then the front doors boomed open on a wall of white, and a figure staggered through them, snow-crusted, half-frozen, unmistakable.

Margot.
