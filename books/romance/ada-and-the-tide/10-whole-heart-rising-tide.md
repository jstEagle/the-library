## 10. Whole Heart, Rising Tide

**Kestrel**

The inn smelled different in spring. That was the first thing Kestrel noticed when he came up the drive in April — not the salt and woodsmoke of winter, but something greener underneath it, cut grass and thawing earth and the particular sweetness of the tide going out at low water, all that hidden life briefly exposed.

The sign over the door was new. *The Gull & Tide*, freshly painted, the letters still sharp-edged enough to smell if you stood close. Below the name, smaller: *Owned by the people who stayed.*

It had been Delia's phrase, the bride who'd packed light and then unpacked everything. On the worst night of the storm, huddled around candles with a wedding collapsed around them like a dropped cake, she'd said it half-joking: *We should just buy the place. None of us have anywhere better to be.* And then Marcus — the groom who drank alone because he'd believed he was marrying a merger — had laughed for the first time in three days, and someone had found a napkin, and by two in the morning eleven stranded strangers had scribbled numbers on it and pooled their money on the bar like a poker pot.

Kestrel had contributed the last of his savings. The house he'd been trying to buy back was gone now — sold at auction in March to a family who would fill its rooms with their own noise — but standing under that new sign, he found he didn't ache the way he'd expected to. His father's name sat clean in the county records these days, cleared by the documents Wren had pulled from the patriarch's files, and it turned out a name weighed more than a house. He was still learning how to carry things that light.

Inside, the lobby had been repainted a soft grey-green, the color of the sea before weather. Wren had chosen it. He could tell because it was exactly right in a way that made his chest hurt.

She was at the front desk with her sleeves pushed up, arguing cheerfully with the florist on the phone about ranunculus versus peonies, and when she saw him she didn't hang up — just held up one finger, finished her sentence, and set the receiver down with the unhurried confidence of a woman who owned the building she was standing in.

"You're early," she said.

"I'm always early."

"You're always *early*," she agreed, coming around the desk. "It's one of your most annoying features." She stopped in front of him, close enough that he could see the sun had already found freckles across her nose, and studied him the way she used to study contracts — looking for the clause buried in the fine print. "You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"Like you haven't slept since March."

"I've slept." He had, mostly. "I've been planning."

"That's what worries me." But she was smiling, and her hand came up and straightened his collar with an ease that undid him more than any kiss could have. "Come on, planner. We've got a wedding to build."

* * *

They worked in the same suite of rooms where the storm had once trapped them, though the boards had been pulled up and replaced, and the windows opened now onto a courtyard full of new green. Delia and Marcus were remarrying — properly this time, on their own terms, no patriarch's signature anywhere in the paperwork, no consolidation of anything except two people who had somehow fallen in love during the wreckage of their own arranged wedding.

"It should be small," Delia said, sitting cross-legged on the planning table itself, which would have given old Kestrel Underhill an aneurysm. "Just the owners. Just us."

"The owners are the guest list," Marcus pointed out. He'd stopped drinking; or rather, he drank now only in celebration, and there was a bottle of good champagne sweating in an ice bucket by the window for later. "That's forty-one people."

"Forty-two," said Wren, not looking up from her clipboard. "I'm counting myself."

"You're staff," said Delia.

"I'm *co-conspirator*," Wren said. "There's a difference. Ask my business partner."

And there it was again — that word, tossed between them so easily now. Partner. Kestrel had spent twenty years training his heart like an athlete trains a muscle: repetition, discipline, no wasted motion, nothing that couldn't be controlled. He had built a career on the belief that love was logistics, that feeling was a liability you managed like weather. And here was this woman, his best mistake, treating his whole rebuilt life like a joint venture.

He caught himself doing the math again — the seating chart, the tide tables, the risk assessment — and made himself stop. Made himself sit down next to Delia on the table, which creaked in protest, and say the thing he'd been rehearsing on the drive up.

"There's no invoice for this one," he told her. "No contract. I'm doing it free."

Delia blinked. "You don't do free."

"I'm aware." He turned the pen in his fingers. "Consider it — restitution. Your first wedding nearly cost me everything. This one should give some of it back."

Wren had gone very still by the window. He felt her watching him and didn't turn around, because if he turned around he would say the other thing too early, and he wanted — for once in his careful life — to do this in the right order.

* * *

**Wren**

She knew what he was going to do before he did it. That was the trouble with loving someone for fifteen years across two separate attempts: you learned their tells. The way Kestrel straightened papers that didn't need straightening when he was nervous. The way he looked at the door when he wanted to stay.

All through the afternoon of setup — the arch built from driftwood the guests had collected in February, the chairs arranged facing the water instead of an altar, because Delia wanted to marry the sea as much as the man — Wren watched him work without his armor. No earpiece. No laminated timeline. He'd written the schedule on a chalkboard, for God's sake, in his own handwriting, and twice he'd let the schedule slip entirely to help Tobias carry chairs, laughing at something, unguarded.

The tide was coming in when the ceremony ended. That had been Wren's contribution — high tide at six-fourteen, so that when Delia and Marcus kissed, the water would be at its fullest, rising, everything the storm had taken given back doubled. Forty-two people stood barefoot on the cold sand in the gold light of seven o'clock, and when the vows were done — short ones, real ones, Marcus's voice cracking on the word *choose* — the applause mixed with the sound of the surf and nobody moved for a long moment, as if they all understood that leaving would break something.

Then the champagne came out, and the bonfire was lit, and the owners of the Gull & Tide became exactly the kind of chaotic, generous, impossible family that Kestrel Underhill had once claimed was a myth invented by people who couldn't read a balance sheet.

Wren found him at the tide line, apart from the firelight, his shoes off and rolled trousers, standing where the water could reach him. She came and stood beside him. The foam ran cold over both their feet.

"You gave away your fee," she said.

"I did."

"And your savings went into the inn. And the house is gone." She watched his profile against the darkening water. "By my accounting, Kestrel Underhill is officially broke."

"By your accounting," he said, "I've never been richer. But the accounting was always the problem, wasn't it? I kept trying to price things that don't have prices." He turned to face her then, and the firelight caught one side of his face, and the sea took the other, and she thought: *there he is. The one under all the versions.* "I trained my heart like a muscle for twenty years. Strength without softness. Control without risk. And it worked — right up until a snowstorm put you in a room with me and the whole system failed in seventy-two hours."

"Seventy-one," she said. "You're exaggerating."

"Wren." Her name in his mouth, serious now, stripped of banter. "I have spent my whole life lending pieces of myself out and keeping the core locked away where no one could foreclose on it. My father lost everything because men like Aldous Vane treat love as leverage. So I decided I'd never hold anything anyone could take." The wave pulled back, dragging sand from under their feet, and he didn't steady himself, let the ground shift. "But you can't keep a whole heart safe. I know that now. It's not a thing you store. It's a thing you spend."

The fire crackled behind them. Somewhere in the dark, Delia was laughing.

"So here it is," Kestrel said. "Uncontained. Untrained. No contract, no exit clause, no co-planner arrangement, no logistics. I love you. I loved you at twenty-six and I loved you wrong, and I love you now at forty-one and I intend to do it right — which means badly, and openly, and every day, whether the tide comes in or goes out. I'm not offering you pieces anymore, Wren. I'm offering you the whole ridiculous, over-planned, under-slept thing. Take it or leave it, but either way, it's yours."

The water rose around their ankles. Wren discovered she was crying, which annoyed her profoundly, and laughing, which saved her.

"You rehearsed that," she said.

"For three weeks."

"On the drive up?"

"The entire drive up. There's a page of notes in my glovebox."

"Kestrel Underhill wrote *notes* for a marriage proposal—"

"It's not—" He stopped. Started over. "Is it a proposal? I hadn't actually—"

"It's a proposal," she said, and took his face in her salty hands, and kissed him while the tide climbed their shins and the fire threw sparks at the stars and forty-two people pretended very hard not to watch. When she pulled back, breathless, she rested her forehead against his. "Yes. Obviously yes. But I want it noted for the record that I'm the one who found the files, exposed the patriarch, saved the bride, and bought the inn."

"Noted. You're the better partner."

"I'm the better *everything*. You're just prettier."

The wave came in higher than the last, soaking them both to the knee, and neither of them moved. Behind them the party roared; ahead of them the water kept arriving, faithful as anything, giving back everything the winter had carried away.

"Mrs. Calloway-Underhill," Wren tested. "Underhill-Calloway. We'll fight about it later."

"We'll fight about everything later," he agreed happily. "For decades. I'll draw up a schedule."

"You will do no such thing."

"No," he said, and pulled her close, heart hammering untrained and uncontained against her cheek, worth every risk, worth the ruin, worth the wait. "No schedule. Just this."

Far down the beach, Delia shouted something about dancing, and the tide kept rising, and they went together toward the firelight — two planners with no plan, walking into the rest of their lives.
