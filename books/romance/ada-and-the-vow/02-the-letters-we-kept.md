## 2. The Letters We Kept

The truck arrived on a Tuesday, and Rowan Adeyemi had planned it down to the hour.

Not the traffic—that was luck, or the city's usual indifference—but the timing of everything else. The lease signed eleven months ago. The transfer request filed with an office that had never heard of Brightwater's. The boxes packed in an order that would let Rowan unpack the letters first: forty-one shoeboxes' worth, a decade of drafts addressed to *O* and sealed and never sent, because sending them would have ended the only honest thing Rowan had ever built.

*You will tell her,* the vow went, the one made at nineteen with a hand pressed flat against a cold window in a house that smelled of other people's money. *You will tell her what you know, and you will take whatever she gives you for it.*

It had taken ten years to keep.

Rowan stood on the sidewalk across from the café and watched Odile Brightwater through glass gone soft with steam. She moved like someone conducting an orchestra only she could hear—flour to bench, bench to oven, oven to counter where a line of morning regulars waited without speaking, because speaking would have broken whatever spell she worked by. Her apron was dusted white at the hip. Her hair was escaping its knot in three places, and she hadn't fixed it, because fixing it would have required stopping.

Ten years of letters, and Rowan had never once seen her work. It felt like trespassing, watching now. It felt like the most beautiful thing available on earth.

"You going to help or sightsee?" called the mover, a broad man balancing a dresser on his shoulder like a grievance.

"Helping." Rowan took the end of the dresser and carried it up the stairs of the narrow brick building beside the café—the one with the sagging awning over its own little stoop, the one Rowan had chosen precisely because its kitchen window faced hers across four feet of alley air.

Four feet. Close enough to smell bread. Far enough to keep lying.

* * *

The lie deepened by inches, the way rot does, the way sugar dissolves.

On the third day, Rowan fixed the awning. It was not subtle—Odile caught him at it, arms full of a flour sack the size of a sleeping child, and stopped dead on the sidewalk.

"That's my landlord's problem," she said.

"Your landlord is eighty-two and lives in Tucson. I checked."

"You *checked?*"

"I'm thorough," Rowan said, which was true, and a sin, and the whole architecture of his life. He tightened the last bracket and stepped back. "There. It'll hold through winter."

She looked at the awning, then at him, and something crossed her face too fast to name—a flicker, a recognition of nothing, a door opening onto an empty room. "You're the new neighbor."

"Rowan."

"Odile." She shifted the flour sack. "Everyone here already knows me, so I'll skip the part where I pretend I'm charming."

"You don't have to pretend anything," Rowan said, and heard, a half-second too late, how it sounded—how it sounded like a man who knew exactly what she didn't have to pretend, because he'd been reading her unguarded heart for a decade.

Her eyes narrowed a fraction. Then a customer called her name from inside, and the moment folded itself away like a letter into an envelope.

"Borrowed sugar's in the blue canister if you ever need it," she said over her shoulder. "Neighborhood rule. You borrow, you return the dish with something in it."

"I know," said Rowan, who knew every rule of her street, her block, her kitchen, her griefs. "I mean—I will. Remember that."

* * *

He returned the dish on a Friday with lemon curd in it, because he remembered—of course he remembered—that she'd written, seven years ago, in the letter where she described failing her pastry certification the first time: *I want to be the kind of cook who makes the thing nobody ordered. Lemon curd saved me once. It tastes like starting over.*

She held the dish, looked at the curd, and laughed—one short, startled note. "Lemon curd."

"Is that all right?"

"It's—" She shook her head. "It's a strange thing to know about a stranger."

*You're not a stranger,* he almost said. *You've told me about your mother's hands. You've told me about the night she died and how you kept proofing dough anyway because your hands needed a job that wasn't crying. You told me once that you believed love was trainable muscle, and I have been training mine for ten years in the dark.*

What he said was: "Lucky guess."

And she smiled, tired and real, and took the curd inside, and Rowan climbed his own stairs and sat down among his shoeboxes and hated himself with a sweetness that was nearly indistinguishable from joy.

* * *

It was the bank statements that undid him—not morally, he'd been undone morally for years, but practically. He learned the shape of her ruin the way he learned everything: sideways, accidentally, by paying attention.

The building next to hers housed a credit union branch, and its manager was a talkative man named Deshawn who frequented the same Saturday market stall as Rowan and had, within two weeks, adopted him as a confidant. "That Brightwater woman," Deshawn said one morning, weighing tomatoes, "is three payments from losing the whole operation. Bank's been patient. Patience runs out in March."

Three payments. Rowan set down his basket.

He thought of her letters—the ones from culinary school, when she'd been broke but unbowed; the ones after her mother died, when she'd written *the café is the only inheritance I understand*. And underneath those, older, the reason Rowan was here at all: the betrayal. The supplier contract her father had signed in good faith while a partner quietly bled the bakery dry, the partner whose silence had been bought, whose name appeared in none of the filings because he'd had help making it disappear. Help from a nineteen-year-old clerk who'd found the discrepancy, understood it, and said nothing—because saying something would have cost him the scholarship, the position, the future being purchased for him by the very family doing the bleeding.

Rowan had told himself, for ten years, that silence wasn't complicity. Then he'd told himself that distance was penance. Now he lived four feet from her kitchen window and watched her knead dough at five in the morning to save a business that men like his benefactors had taught her family the world would always take.

That night he wrote a letter he did not send. He was out of practice at not sending them; it was the one craft he'd perfected.

*O — I live next door now. I fix things badly and bake worse. Every kindness I do you is a coin stolen from a vault I helped lock. Tell me to go. You won't, because you don't know enough to. That's my doing too.*

He sealed it. Filed it in box thirty-eight, between the confession he'd drafted at twenty-three and the one he'd burned at twenty-seven.

Then he opened his curtains, and across the alley her light was still on, and she was standing at her counter with her head bowed over something small and white—an envelope, he realized. She was reading an envelope, and her shoulders were wrong, hunched in a way he knew from a decade of her handwriting, the posture she took when the news was bad and she was deciding whether to let it make her smaller.

Rowan pressed his hand flat against the cold glass.

*March,* he thought. *Tell her before March.*

Across the alley, Odile lifted her head and looked directly at his window—and for one suspended second their eyes met through glass and lamplight and ten years of unsent words, and Rowan understood with total clarity that whatever came next, he had run out of ways to hide behind a stamp.
