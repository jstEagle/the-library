## 19. Last Train

The envelope is cream-colored, heavy stock, and it arrives on a Tuesday, which Ansel will later decide was the universe's idea of a joke. Nothing that destroys your week should arrive on a Tuesday. Tuesdays are for proofing dough and wiping down the cases and watching Orsen sketch notes in the margins of a score with a pencil held wrong, between ring finger and middle finger, like they're afraid of committing to a grip.

Ansel opens it standing at the counter because opening mail sitting down has started to feel like surrender.

*Notice of Emergency Permit Hearing.* Meridian Holdings has filed for an expedited demolition review—some clause, some loophole, some mechanism Ansel's uncle found or paid for—and the city has scheduled the hearing for Friday at eight p.m. The building's fate, or at least the next stage of its fate, decided in a room above a parking garage on Delancey Street, while—

While.

Ansel reads the date three times. It doesn't change. Friday. Eight p.m.

The recital is Friday at seven-thirty.

They stand there while the ovens tick and the first batch of the morning threatens to overproof behind them, and they do the math they already know the answer to. The hall is forty minutes away by subway if every light turns green and every train arrives like a promise. The hearing room is fifteen minutes from the bakery. There is no version of this where one person stands in both rooms.

"Ansel?" Orsen's voice comes down the stairs, sleep-roughened, careful. They've learned to announce themselves. Eight months ago they used to drift through the bakery like a ghost who hadn't been told they were dead; now they knock on the doorframe even when the door is open. "You're up early. Even for you."

Ansel folds the letter in half, then in half again, until it's small enough to be a lie. "Permit thing came early."

Orsen crosses the kitchen floor in bare feet, takes the folded paper out of Ansel's hand without asking, and unfolds it. Ansel watches them read it. Watches the exact moment the date lands—the small stillness in their shoulders, the way their breath goes somewhere and stays there.

"Friday," Orsen says.

"Friday."

"The recital is—"

"I know what time the recital is." Ansel hears their own voice come out flat and clipped, the voice they use with suppliers, and hates it. "I've known what time the recital is since March."

Orsen sets the letter on the counter, smoothing it flat with one hand, an oddly gentle gesture for a document designed to hurt. Their hands. Ansel thinks about those hands—how they shook for eight months, how they shake less now, how on Sunday they played the first movement of Rose's husband's piece all the way through without stopping, badly in two places, bravely everywhere else.

"So we split it," Orsen says.

"No."

It comes out faster than Ansel intended, and Orsen looks up, surprised—not by the word but by the force of it.

"You play," Ansel says. "I go to the hearing. That's the whole plan. That's the only plan. You've spent five months getting your hands back and you are not going to stand on a stage thinking about me sitting in a folding chair across from a developer's lawyer."

"And you're going to sit in a folding chair alone against Meridian's entire legal team, arguing a continuance based on a trust petition that got *delayed*, not granted—"

"The architect is coming around. I told you about the architect."

"The architect is one person with a conscience and no authority." Orsen drags a hand through their hair. They do this when they're frightened, Ansel has learned. They think nobody notices. "Ansel. Listen to yourself. You're doing the thing."

"What thing."

"The disappearing thing." Orsen says it without cruelty, which is worse. "You hid the sale letter for two months. You fought the uncle alone. You let me find out about the buyout offer from a voicemail he left you. Every time something gets hard, you fold it up small—" they nod at the letter, folded into a square on the counter, "—and put it in your pocket and decide you're the only one who can carry it. I'm asking you to stop."

The ovens beep. Neither of them moves.

"I'm not disappearing," Ansel says. "I'm being realistic. One of us has to be in that hall. The piece premieres Friday whether I'm in the third row or not. Rose will be there. Your family will be there. Two hundred people came to hear a cellist who froze eight months ago try again—that story got out, don't look at me like that, Kenji talks to everyone—and if you walk onto that stage distracted, if your hands seize—"

"My hands," Orsen says quietly, "are fine."

"They were fine in March too."

That lands. Ansel sees it land, sees the old flinch move through Orsen's face like weather, and immediately wants to take it back, but some part of them—the part raised by a father whose failures were invoked like scripture—refuses to retract a true thing just because it's sharp.

"Okay," Orsen says. "Fine. Then here's my counteroffer." They pick up the letter again, hold it up. "I play the recital. And then I get in a cab and I come to the hearing, and I walk in during whatever the developer's lawyer is saying, and I sit down next to you, and we lose or win together. That's the plan."

"The hearing starts at eight. The piece runs twenty-two minutes. With bows, with the encore Kenji is absolutely going to bully you into—"

"Then I'll miss the beginning. I'll miss the boring part. Lawyers are the boring part."

"Orsen—"

"You don't get to fight alone anymore." Orsen says it simply, the way they say everything that matters, and Ansel understands suddenly that this is what love looks like when it stops pretending to be a favor: inconvenient, immovable, standing barefoot on flour-dusted tile at six in the morning refusing to accept a reasonable plan. "You didn't let me freeze alone in March. Remember? You sat in the empty bakery and made me play Bach to a room full of chairs. You didn't ask permission. You just decided I wasn't going to do it by myself."

"That was different."

"It was exactly the same."

* * *

They fight about it for two days, in the way they fight now—which is to say honestly, which took months to learn, which still costs them both something every time.

Wednesday night, Ansel tries diplomacy. They make the lemon cake Orsen likes, the one with the glaze that cracks like river ice, and they present the argument again over slices: logistics, probability, the cold arithmetic of two rooms and one body. Orsen listens with terrible patience, the patience of someone who has already decided, and says, "What time does the last train run?"

Ansel blinks. "What?"

"The last train. From Delancey to the concert hall. If the hearing runs long—if I'm needed there past ten—what's the last train?"

"Eleven-forty," Ansel says slowly. "Why?"

"Just checking." Orsen eats their cake. "So if the hearing goes late, I can still be there for the end of it and get home. Nobody has to choose anything except sleep."

"That's not—you'd be playing a recital and then sprinting across town to a municipal hearing. That's insane."

"Yes," Orsen agrees cheerfully. "Probably. I've been told I have a history of freezing under pressure, so I figure I should practice doing things under pressure whenever possible."

"Don't joke about that."

"I'm not joking about anything." Orsen sets down the fork. The humor drains out of their face and what's underneath is the thing Ansel fell for at the reunion, before either of them had a name for it: a steadiness that isn't calm, exactly. Calm is the absence of fear. This is fear, held. "Here's what I actually think. I think you believe that if you need me and I'm not there, it'll prove something true and awful—same as when my hands stopped working proved something true and awful. But it doesn't prove anything. People are in two places at once all the time. It's called showing up imperfectly. It's the only kind of showing up there is."

Ansel looks at the cake. At the crack in the glaze. "My father showed up imperfectly," they say. "Right up until he didn't show up at all."

"I know." Orsen's voice gentles. "And you're not him. You're the person who stayed. Who kept her shop running when she started forgetting to open it. Who memorized her recipes before she could forget them. You're the stayingest person I've ever met, Ansel. Let someone stay for you. Just once. Let it be me."

Thursday, Ansel tries authority. They tell Orsen the plan is final: Orsen plays, Ansel fights, no discussion. Orsen nods along, agrees to everything, and then Thursday evening Ansel finds them in the back room with Rose's sheet music spread across two chairs, practicing the transitions, and realizes with dawning horror that Orsen has never once agreed—they've just waited, patient as dough, for Ansel to run out of arguments.

"You're not listening to me," Ansel says from the doorway.

"I'm listening fine." Orsen doesn't look up. Their bow arm moves through the passage that gave them trouble on Sunday, slower now, cleaner. "You're just not saying anything I haven't already decided against."

Rose shuffles in behind Ansel, drawn by the music the way she always is now, and settles into her chair by the window. She watches Orsen play with her head tilted, and for a moment—Ansel would swear to this later—for a moment she is entirely present, entirely here, seventy years of mornings in her eyes.

"Walter used to argue with me like that," she says, to no one in particular. "About the rye recipe. He wanted caraway. I said no caraway, ever, and he argued for eleven years." She smiles at her hands. "He lost, of course. But he stayed for the whole argument. That's how I knew."

She looks up at Ansel, and the lucidity holds, barely, like a flame in a draft.

"Is the hearing Friday?" she asks.

Ansel's chest tightens. "Yes, Grandma."

"And the concert is Friday."

"Yes."

She considers this with the gravity of a woman deciding a recipe. "Well," she says. "Walter would have done both. He'd have played the first half and argued the second half, and he'd have been late and rude and right." She pats the arm of her chair. "Do both. It's what the building is for."

* * *

Friday morning, the bakery opens at six as always, because the world does not pause for your catastrophes and neither does sourdough. Ansel works the counter in a haze, and at nine-thirty the uncle walks in.

He doesn't come often anymore—not since the check, torn in half, returned in an envelope with no note. He stands in the doorway like a man visiting a property he already owns, which, legally speaking, is closer to true than Ansel likes.

"I hear you got your notice," he says.

"I got it."

"Emergency hearing. Smart filing. The city moves fast when you know which forms to use." He picks up a napkin, folds it, unfolds it. "Eight p.m. Friday. Funny timing."

"It's not funny."

"No," he agrees. "It's efficient." He looks around the shop—at the cases, the counter his mother built, the framed photo of Walter with his cello that Ansel moved to behind the register after Rose asked where it went one too many times. "You know what's going to happen tonight. My lawyers will bury your trust petition in procedure. The board will approve the permit. And Monday morning this becomes a construction site." He sets the napkin down. "Come to dinner Sunday. Bring the cellist. We'll talk severance, relocation, something dignified. You can't win, Ansel. All you can choose is how much dignity you keep."

"We'll see," Ansel says.

"That's not a no."

"It's not a yes."

He sighs, the sigh of a man burdened by other people's stubbornness, and leaves a twenty-dollar bill on the counter for a coffee he didn't order. Ansel leaves it there all day, a small white flag from a war that hasn't ended.

At noon, Orsen comes downstairs with the cello case and sets it by the door. They're dressed for rehearsal—dark clothes, hair tied back—and there's a new stillness in them that Ansel recognizes with a lurch: it's the stillness they used to have before concerts, before the freezing, the performer's mask. Except now, underneath it, instead of terror, there's something else. Something that looks like decision.

"Kenji's picking me up at four," Orsen says. "Sound check at five-thirty. House at seven."

"I know the schedule. I wrote it on the whiteboard."

"I know you wrote it on the whiteboard." Orsen glances at it—the schedule in Ansel's precise handwriting, two columns, two venues, two times, arrows that don't connect. "Walk me through tonight one more time."

"I go to the hearing at seven-forty-five. I speak when they call the Meridian item. I present the trust documentation, the heritage angle, the architect's letter. I stall. That's all I have to do—stall until the continuance process finishes. If the board approves the emergency permit anyway, I object, I request the record stay open, I buy days. Maybe a week. Maybe two."

"And if it goes bad?"

"Then it goes bad and you're on stage playing Walter's piece and none of it will be your fault."

"There it is," Orsen says softly. "There's the disappearing thing again. *None of it will be your fault.* Ansel, I don't want a night where nothing is my fault. I want a night where everything is ours."

The bell over the door jingles—a customer—and Ansel sells a woman two croissants and a loaf of rye, no caraway, ever, and when they turn back Orsen is standing closer, and their hands come up and frame Ansel's face, flour and all, and hold it like something breakable that refuses to break.

"New plan," Orsen says. "Final offer. I play the piece. Twenty-two minutes. The moment the last note ends, I bow once—once, Kenji can suffer—and I'm in a cab. The hearing starts at eight; I'm there by eight-forty. Whatever happens after eight-forty, we're in the same room. And if the last train leaves at eleven-forty and we're still in that room, then we ride it home together, and tomorrow morning I'm downstairs at six eating whatever you burn because you're distracted."

"I don't burn things."

"You burned the brioche Tuesday."

"I was *thinking*."

"You were carrying something alone." Orsen's thumbs move once across Ansel's cheekbones. "Stop it. I mean it. Whatever happens tonight—win, lose, continuance, bulldozers—you don't spend one more hour of it by yourself. That's the deal. That's the only deal on the table."

Ansel closes their eyes. Behind them the ovens tick, the cases gleam, the photo of Walter watches from behind the register with his cello and his half-smile, a man who argued for eleven years and stayed for all of it.

"If your hands seize," Ansel says, "in the middle of the piece—"

"Then they seize, and I keep going, and it won't be perfect, and Rose won't care, because she told me herself—she said Walter played it wrong every time and she loved every wrong note." Orsen leans in, forehead against Ansel's. "Play your part. I'll play mine. Same building, same fight, same night."

The bell jingles again. Outside, the city goes on being the city—trains under the street, cranes on the skyline, a developer's lawyers sharpening their procedures somewhere above a parking garage. The clock over the door says 12:40. Six hours and fifty minutes until curtain. Seven hours and five minutes until the hearing. Eleven hours until the last train, which has become, without anyone announcing it, the deadline for everything: the building, the music, the question of who they are to each other when the night ends.

"One more thing," Orsen says, pulling back, reaching for the cello case. "If I'm not in that hearing room by nine—don't wait to speak. Don't save anything for me. Say all of it. Everything you'd say if I were there. Promise me."

Ansel hesitates.

"Promise."

"I promise," Ansel says. "But you'll be there."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're the stayingest person I know," Ansel says, and Orsen laughs—startled, bright, real—and shoulders the case, and pushes out the door into the noon light, toward the hall, toward the stage, toward the night that's waiting for both of them.

Ansel watches them go. Then they flip the sign to CLOSED, untie the apron, and take the folded letter out of their pocket one last time—not to hide it, but to read it, straight through, every clause, every line, because tonight they intend to know it better than the lawyer who wrote it.

Seven hours, they think, sliding the letter into their bag, grabbing the folder of trust documents off the counter, killing the lights.

Seven hours until the last train.

And this time, nobody rides it alone.
