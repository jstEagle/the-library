## 7. Dinner With the Enemy

The restaurant Julian chose was the kind of place where the menu had no prices and the waiters remembered your name from a single visit. Tamsin knew it by reputation — Le Marchand, third floor, a view of the river that cost more per plate than her father used to pay a typesetter for a week's work.

She'd dressed carefully: charcoal trousers, a blouse that was neither inviting nor severe, her one good watch. Armor, but civilian armor. She would not give him the satisfaction of seeing effort.

He was already there when she arrived, which surprised her. Julian Ashford struck her as a man who made rooms wait for him. But he stood when she approached, and there was something almost boyish in the way he pulled out her chair, as if he'd rehearsed the gesture and wasn't sure it would land.

"You came," he said.

"You paid an advance against my invoice. I come very cheaply when properly incentivized."

His mouth curved. "One day I'll get you to say something honest at the start of an evening instead of the end."

"One day you'll earn it."

The waiter poured wine — something Burgundian, she guessed, because Julian waved off the list and said simply, "the Gevrey," with the ease of a man ordering water. Tamsin watched his hands as he did it. She'd spent three weeks watching those hands now, in interview sessions, in the archive of his life she was assembling chapter by chapter. Hands that had signed the acquisition papers that ended Vane & Sons. Hands that were, at this moment, steady and elegant and utterly unremarkable.

She hated how much time she'd spent thinking about them.

"To the book," Julian said, lifting his glass.

"To the book." She touched her glass to his and drank. The wine was absurd. It tasted like black cherries and money.

* * *

The first hour was work, or the performance of it. They went through the outline for Part Two — the years after his mentor Edmund Hale's death, no relation to Marcus, though the coincidence of names had made her flinch the first time she'd typed it — and Julian talked about the expansion into digital publishing, the acquisitions, the consolidation of the market that had made Ashford House what it was.

Made it what it was *out of the bones of smaller houses*, she thought, and kept her voice level. "You bought eleven imprints in four years. That's not growth, that's a feeding pattern."

"Is that what your research says?"

"That's what arithmetic says."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, and she felt the laugh land somewhere beneath her sternum and hated it. "God, you're merciless."

"I'm accurate. There's a difference, but I understand how they get confused at your altitude."

"Your altitude," he said, turning his glass slowly on the cloth. "You say things like that like you're standing in the valley looking up. But you're not, are you? You know this world too well. The margins, the imprint economics, who answers to whom. You didn't learn that writing obituaries for the *Courier*."

Tamsin took a slow sip of wine and let the silence do some of her lying for her.

"My father owned a small press," she said finally, which was true, and which she had decided — weeks ago, in a moment of either recklessness or self-sabotage she still hadn't diagnosed — she could afford to admit. It was even useful. A ghostwriter who understood the industry from the inside was more credible, not less. And if he ever learned the name Vane, well. Vane was not an uncommon name. That was what she told herself.

Julian set down his glass. Something moved behind his eyes — she'd learned to read them by now, the flicker of recalculation, the editor checking a fact against a file — but whatever he found there, he kept.

"What kind of press?" he asked.

"Small. Literary. The kind that loses money beautifully." She smiled, and it cost her. "It doesn't exist anymore."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. You weren't there."

*No*, said the voice inside her, the cold one, the one that had driven up from the archive basement with photocopies in her bag. *But you were somewhere. Weren't you.*

* * *

The second bottle arrived without anyone ordering it, and with it the conversation slipped its moorings.

They'd abandoned the outline entirely. Julian was telling her about his first job — nineteen, proofreading galleys in a basement office that flooded every spring — and he told it well, self-deprecating, precise, and Tamsin caught herself leaning forward, chin on her hand, actually *listening*, and corrected her posture like a woman catching herself reaching for a stove.

"Why publishing?" she asked. "You could have done anything. You have the kind of brain that does anything."

He looked at her for a long moment. The candle between them had burned down to a stub; the light made half his face gold and left the other half in question.

"My grandfather," he said. "Ansel. He ran a letterpress shop in Yorkshire. Nothing grand — wedding invitations, hymnals, the occasional pamphlet someone needed printed before the authorities got nervous about it." He turned the glass again. "He used to say ink outlives power. Kings issue decrees; printers keep the receipts. I believed him so completely that I built an empire to prove it, and then—" He stopped.

"Then?" she said quietly.

"Then I became the thing receipts are kept on." He laughed, but it was hollow, and he covered it by drinking. "That's not for the book. Strike that."

"It's the only part tonight worth printing."

"Tamsin." He said her name differently than he usually did — slower, tasting it. In sessions he was all business: *Ms. Vane, shall we continue.* Now, three glasses deep, it was just her name, and it sounded strange and warm in his mouth, and she felt the danger of it like weather changing.

"You asked me once why I agreed to write this memoir," he said. "I gave you the publisher's answer. Legacy, control of narrative, all of it." He shook his head. "The real answer is that I've done something I can't undo, and I'm sixty percent coward, and I thought if I put it in a book — if I made myself say it out loud to someone sharp enough to catch me lying — maybe it would count as penance."

The candle flame bent. Somewhere behind her a waiter laughed at something, ordinary and far away.

"What did you do?" she asked. Her voice came out steady. She was proud of that, later, and sickened by it.

Julian looked at her, and for a moment — she would swear to this, afterward, in the privacy of her own sleepless accounting — for a moment he was going to tell her. She saw the sentence form in him, saw his shoulders drop a quarter inch, saw the mask go soft the way ice goes soft.

And she wanted it. God help her, she wanted it — wanted the confession, yes, wanted the ammunition, the smoking page she could carry out of Le Marchand and into the offices of every journalist who owed her a favor — but underneath that, worse, deeper, she wanted it the way you want anything from someone you're beginning to—

No. She shut the door on that thought hard enough to rattle its hinges.

"Julian," she said, and leaned in, and heard her own voice drop into something gentle she hadn't authorized. "Whatever it is. Say it. I promise you I can hold it."

He opened his mouth.

His phone lit up on the table between them, buzzing, insistent, the screen glowing *MARCUS HALE* in white letters.

They both looked at it. The name sat there like a slug on lettuce.

"Ignore it," Julian said.

"It might be important."

"It's never important. He's a vulture who happens to own presses instead of a top hat." But he glanced at it again, and Tamsin watched the softness drain back out of his face, the ice re-forming, and she understood that the moment had passed — that moments like this don't wait, that they are doors that swing both ways and lock behind you.

"He'll call again," she said.

"He always calls again." Julian exhaled, dragged a hand through his hair, and for the first time all evening looked tired — genuinely tired, the exhaustion of a man who has been holding a door shut for seven years. "Another time, perhaps. When the wine is better and the interruptions are fewer."

"Another time," she echoed, and hated herself for how much it sounded like hope.

* * *

He stepped away to take the call in the corridor — because of course he took it, because Marcus Hale was a name she knew too, a name that had surfaced twice this week in her research notes with little red asterisks beside it, and because whatever Julian Ashford was afraid of, it wore that particular face.

Tamsin sat alone at the table with two dead candles and a bottle that had gone warm and considered her position.

Her bag was under her chair. Inside it, folded into quarters, was a photocopy she'd pulled from the 2018 archives three days ago: a kill memo, internal, Ashford House letterhead, spiking a story about irregularities in the Vane & Sons receivership. Spiked by J.A., initials in the margin, dated nine days before her father's press collapsed. Nine days. She had stared at those initials until the letters swam.

She could put it on the table right now. She could say *explain this*. She could watch his face do whatever it was going to do and know, finally, whether the man across from her was a monster in good tailoring or something more complicated — something she was increasingly, catastrophically afraid he might be.

Because here was the problem, the one she'd been refusing to look at directly for two weeks: the man across from her was also the man who wrote her letters. Or might be. The handwriting she'd glimpsed on his session notes had haunted her — the same forward slant, the same way of crossing a *t* like a man signing a treaty. And last Thursday, over the transcript of their third interview, she'd caught herself reading his spoken sentences in the cadence of her anonymous correspondent, and had to put the pages down and stand at her window for twenty minutes breathing like a woman talked down off a ledge.

If they were the same man, then the person who wrote her *I have done something unforgivable and I wake up every morning hoping today is the day I deserve less sleep* was the person who killed her father's press. Desire and vengeance, sitting down to dinner together, sharing a bottle of Gevrey.

The phone call in the corridor ended. She heard his footsteps coming back, unhurried, and slid the memory of the photocopy back down into the dark where she kept it.

"Sorry," he said, sitting. "Business."

"Of course."

"Where were we?"

"You were about to confess to something unforgivable," she said lightly, testing, watching. "And then salvation interrupted."

Something flickered across his face — pain, quickly filed. "Something like that."

"Well." She raised her glass. "There's always the next bottle."

He smiled, but it didn't reach anywhere important, and as they finished the evening — polite now, armored again, discussing delivery dates and travel schedules like two professionals who had never once leaned toward each other across a burning candle — Tamsin held her smile in place and thought about the photocopy in her bag, and about the letter in her coat pocket, unsent, that began *Dear stranger, I think I know who you are,* and about the deadline she had given herself in the archive basement, written in pencil on the back of a receipt:

*Two weeks. Then I decide what he is.*

Outside, the river moved dark past the window, carrying its cargo of reflected lights toward the sea, keeping its receipts.
