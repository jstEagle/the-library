## 17. The Plan Outside the Law

*Cyrus*

The kitchen smelled of burnt coffee and rain coming in off the water, and Marisol was drawing on the back of a takeout menu with the fierce concentration of someone defusing a bomb.

Cyrus watched her from the doorway for a moment before she spoke. "You know this is illegal."

"Probably," Marisol said, not looking up. She'd colored the lookout tower at the end of the pier a violent orange and was now adding seagulls with the precision of an ornithologist. "So is what Volkov did. So is whatever Ansel's been doing with all those folders. I've seen them. He thinks I don't look through things, but I look through everything."

"You're eleven."

"I'm almost twelve." She shaded a wing. "And you're going to do it anyway. Both of you. You've been doing that thing where you talk quietly in the other room and then stop talking when I come in, which is honestly insulting, because I can hear fine."

Cyrus leaned against the doorframe and let the truth of it settle over her like fog off the harbor. The girl was right, of course. For three days the cottage had hummed with a low electric current—Ansel on the phone in hushed tones, Cyrus rehearsing Volkov's tour program until her fingers ached, both of them orbiting each other in the careful way of people who had broken something precious and were learning to hold it again without cutting themselves.

They had not forgiven each other. That word still sat between them like a stone neither would pick up. But they had called a truce, and the truce had a shape: Volkov first. Everything else after.

"We haven't decided anything," Cyrus said.

Marisol finally looked up, and there was something in her face too old for eleven-almost-twelve, something that had survived more than any child should. "You decided the night you came home from that phone call and didn't eat dinner. I counted. You played the same eight bars for four hours and then you called him." She tapped the table. "I'm not asking to be in your club. I'm telling you I'm already in it."

The rain arrived then, sudden and silver against the windows, and Cyrus thought about all the doors she had closed in her life—the practice room door, the stage door, the door of her own chest—and how this child had walked through every one of them as if locks were a rumor.

"Sit down," Cyrus said at last. "If you're going to be part of this, you're going to hear all of it."

* * *

*Ansel*

He laid it out on the kitchen table the way he'd once laid out evidence for his editor: photographs, printouts, the stolen file fanned open like a hand of terrible cards. The rain drummed the roof. Marisol sat cross-legged on a chair pulled up to the corner of the table, officially present, officially silent, officially fooling no one.

"The signing is Thursday," Ansel said. "Volkov's lawyer flies in from Vienna. There'll be a contract, a press release, probably champagne. Cyrus walks in, signs the NDA, and the comeback tour is announced by Friday morning. After that, we're finished. Legally bound, publicly committed, and every word in my archive becomes defamation instead of journalism."

"So we go before Friday," Cyrus said.

"We go during." He slid a printed floor plan across the table—the concert hall's administrative wing, corridors marked in his own cramped handwriting. "Here's the shape of it. Cyrus wears a wire. Not a metaphor—an actual recorder, small enough to sit under a collar. She gets Volkov talking. He's vain; vanity is a door that opens from the inside. If he says even one sentence acknowledging what happened in Prague—if he so much as brags about protecting his protégé—we have him."

"And you?" Cyrus asked. Her voice was steady but her hands, folded on the table, were white at the knuckles. He wanted to reach across and cover them. He didn't. Not yet. Trust was a muscle they were both retraining.

"I broadcast." He tapped his laptop. "Everything. Ten years of documents, recordings, financial trails—the whole archive, live, unedited, the moment you give the signal. No editor to spike it, no lawyers to bury it. Once it's out, it's out. You can't unpublish the world."

"That's the part that scares me," Cyrus said quietly. "Not the wire. Not Volkov. That part. Once you press send, there's no version of our lives where this doesn't follow us."

Ansel met her eyes across the wreckage of paper. "I know."

"Do you? Because last time you had a plan involving me, you didn't ask me first. Ten years, Ansel. A decade of decisions made on my behalf." She said it without heat—that was the strange mercy of it, the fury burned down to its embers—but she said it, and he took it the way you take medicine: straight, unchased, because it was true.

"This time you decide," he said. "Every step. I'm the broadcast, you're the trigger. If at any point in that room you want out, you walk out, and nothing goes live. Nothing. I'll burn the archive myself if you tell me to."

Marisol made a small sound, quickly swallowed. They both looked at her.

"What?" she said. "It's just—you'd really burn it? All of it? Ten years?"

"If she asked me to."

Cyrus looked at him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes that he couldn't name—something between grief and gratitude, the exact weather of their whole history compressed into three seconds.

"No burning," she said finally. "We're past burning. We're doing this."

* * *

*Cyrus*

They spent Wednesday preparing, and it felt absurdly like rehearsal—which, Cyrus supposed, was exactly what it was. Ansel rigged the recorder to a thin silk scarf she could wear at her throat; they tested it in the cottage, playing back her own voice saying test sentences until the words lost meaning. *My name is Cyrus Zhou. My name is Cyrus Zhou.* Like a spell. Like an epitaph she refused to let it become.

The signal was the hard part. Phones would be searched or at least watched; Volkov's people were thorough, and Ansel had been thorough enough about them to know it. In the end it was Marisol who solved it, because children solve things sideways, without the weight of consequence slowing them down.

"The pier bell," she said. They were eating dinner—Ansel's attempt at paella, mostly successful. "When the ferry comes in, the old man rings it twice. Everybody knows it. Nobody thinks about it. So if it rings twice at, like, an unusual time—" she shrugged, mouth full, "—that's just the bell being weird."

Ansel stared at her. "That's actually brilliant."

"I know," said Marisol.

So it was settled: two rings meant broadcast. One ring meant abort. No ring meant Cyrus hadn't reached the bell, and Ansel would wait, sick with waiting, until she did.

After dinner, while Marisol did homework she claimed not to need help with, Cyrus and Ansel washed dishes side by side in the narrow kitchen, shoulders almost touching, the window above the sink black with rain and the reflected lamplight of their own small conspiracy.

"My hands are steady," she said. It wasn't small talk. It was the report of a lifetime. "All week, rehearsing his program, they've been steady. Isn't that the joke? They only shake when I'm not performing."

"They shook at the recital," Ansel said. "Marisol's recital. And it was the best playing I've ever heard."

She turned off the tap. Water dripped. Outside, the sea worked at the shore with its patient, ancient hands.

"When this is done," she said, not looking at him, "when it's Thursday and it's over and the whole world knows—I don't know what I'll want. I keep thinking I should know by now. Everyone will want something from me. The orchestras, the journalists, the people who forgot my name and the people who never did. And I won't know what I want except—" she stopped.

"Except?" he asked softly.

"Except that I want to come home to a kitchen where someone is doing something wrong to a paella." She handed him the last dish, wet and warm. "That's all I've got right now. But it's more than I had a month ago."

He took the dish. Their fingers overlapped on the ceramic and stayed there a beat longer than drying a dish required.

"That's enough," he said. "That's plenty."

* * *

*Ansel*

Thursday came in gray and windless, the kind of coastal morning that felt held, suspended, like the town itself was taking a breath. Ansel stood at the fence line between the cottages at six a.m., watching Cyrus load her cello into the car she rarely drove. She wore the dark green dress he knew from a photograph taken years ago—the one from the letter he'd written after Prague, describing the woman in green whose hands had been betrayed. She'd found the letter, read it, and chosen the dress anyway. That was Cyrus: turning even her wounds into armor.

Marisol ran down the path with a thermos of coffee and pressed it into Cyrus's hands, then stood very straight, chin up, the way she did when she was determined not to cry.

"Two rings," Marisol said. "Don't forget."

"Two rings," Cyrus agreed. Then she crouched, set the coffee aside, and hugged the girl—hard, fierce, longer than Cyrus ever allowed herself. Over the small shoulder her eyes found Ansel's, and held them.

"Signal at noon," she said. "If I'm not back by four—"

"I know."

"—then burn it," she finished, and almost smiled. "No. Don't burn it. Publish it anyway. Even if I fail. Especially if I fail. Promise me."

His throat closed. "I promise."

She got in the car. The engine turned. The taillights shrank down the coast road until the fog took them, and Ansel stood at the fence with Marisol's cold hand finding his, and the thermos still warm in his other hand, and ten years of letters and lies and love packed into a laptop bag at his feet.

Above the garage, the little schoolroom clock ticked toward noon. Marisol looked up at him, gray-eyed and grave.

"She's going to be okay," Ansel said—to her, or to himself, or to the sea.

"You don't know that," Marisol said. "But the bell's ready. And I rang it once already this morning, just to make sure it works." A pause. "Kidding. Mostly."

At 11:47, Cyrus's text arrived, one word, the word they'd agreed on:

*Ready.*

Ansel opened the laptop. The archive waited on the screen—ten years of truth, patient as tide. Down at the end of the pier, the old man climbed the ladder to the bell, checking his watch, waiting for the ferry that wasn't due for hours.

The fog began, very slowly, to lift.
