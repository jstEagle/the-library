## 2. Storm Warning

The ferry *Maren* smelled of diesel and old coffee and the particular salt-rot of a boat that had worked harder than any boat should. Ansel Voss stood at her wheel for the last crossing of the season and pretended his hands were steady.

They were not. He'd noticed it an hour out of Kestrel Point, when he'd reached for his thermos and the divorce papers slid off the chart table and fanned across the deck like a tarot spread. He'd gathered them up without reading them—again—and wedged them under the coffee tin where they could not look at him.

Eleven years of marriage, summarized in fourteen pages. Sign here, here, initial there. Astrid had been decent about it, in the way of people who had run out of patience but not kindness. *You've been somewhere else this whole time,* she'd said, not cruelly, sliding the papers across their kitchen table in a town two hundred miles south of anywhere that mattered. *I'd like you back, but I don't think you're coming.*

She was right, which was the worst part. He had been somewhere else. He had been at a dock in Harrow Bay on a November morning, watching a woman with flour on her sleeve wave to him from the shore, and he had turned away before she could see his face do whatever it did whenever he thought of her, which was apparently still, after all these years, something embarrassing and involuntary, like a knee jerking at the doctor's tap.

The letter was in his coat pocket. It had been in his coat pocket through three winters now, migrating from jacket to jacket like a stowaway he couldn't bring himself to throw overboard. The envelope had gone soft at the corners. The ink inside had probably faded to rumor. He had never sent it because sending it would have been a cruelty dressed as a gift: *here is why I left, here is what I protected you from, please admire my sacrifice.* No. Some letters were meant to be carried, not delivered. Pilgrims didn't mail their prayers.

"Ansel."

He looked down from the wheelhouse. Marius stood on the dock below, hands jammed in the pockets of a coat that had been new when their father died and had aged into something more architectural than fashionable. His brother's face wore its usual expression, which was the expression of a man who had been waiting eleven years to say something and had decided, daily, not to say it.

"Marius." Ansel killed the engine. The *Maren* sighed against the pilings. "You're supposed to be in Bergen."

"I'm supposed to be a lot of places." Marius caught the line Ansel threw and made fast with the economy of a man who'd been tying knots since he was six. "I came to see you off. Or on. Whichever."

"Off," Ansel said. "Last crossing. Then she goes into drydock till April, and I go—" He gestured vaguely at the horizon, which contained nothing helpful.

"That's the plan, is it? Another winter of being nowhere?"

"It's a good plan. Nowhere has excellent coffee."

Marius didn't laugh. Marius hadn't laughed at one of Ansel's jokes since roughly the age of nineteen, which Ansel had long ago stopped taking personally and had recently started taking very personally indeed.

* * *

The harbor office was warm in the way harbors are warm in November: reluctantly, and only near the radiator. Ansel poured two cups of coffee from the pot that was always on, always terrible, always free, and set one in front of his brother without asking. This was their entire relationship in miniature—one cup pushed across a table, accepted without thanks, both men pretending the gesture meant nothing.

"You've heard from the bank again," Marius said.

It wasn't a question. Ansel drank his coffee and let the silence do the answering, which it did competently.

"They called me," Marius pressed. "Did you know that? They can't reach you, so they call me. Your name is on the last of Father's paper, Ansel. Eleven years, and there's still a tail on it."

"There's always a tail." Ansel turned his cup a quarter-turn on the table, a habit he'd inherited from their mother along with her stubborn jaw and her way of leaving rooms mid-argument. "That's how debt works. It's like glitter. You pay it off and find it in your pockets for a decade."

"This isn't funny."

"No," Ansel agreed. "But if I don't make it funny, I'll make it loud, and then we'll be having the other conversation, and I'd rather have the coffee one."

Marius leaned back. Outside the office window, the water had gone the color of unpolished pewter, and the gulls were flying low and angry, which was the sea's version of clearing its throat. Ansel had read weather the way other men read faces for thirty years, and everything out there was saying something he didn't want to hear.

"The forecast turned," Marius said, following his gaze. "You saw?"

"I saw. Snow by evening. Maybe heavy."

"Maybe heavy." Marius repeated it flatly. "The radio says the worst storm in five years, and you say maybe heavy. You've been doing that your whole life. Calling catastrophes 'weather' and calling heartbreak 'logistics.'"

Ansel set down his cup. "I'm going to check the lines."

"You're going to avoid me."

"Both things can be true. That's the beauty of brothers."

* * *

The *Maren* needed less work than he gave her. He checked the mooring lines twice, coiled rope that was already coiled, inspected a hull scar he knew by touch the way he knew the scar on his own knuckle. Routine was a kind of prayer, his father used to say, though his father had prayed mostly at card tables and betting windows, so perhaps his theology deserved some skepticism.

The truth—the actual truth, the one he kept below the waterline—was that Harrow Bay did something to him. Every other port was just a port. But this harbor had a bookshop at the end of Front Street with a green door and a bell that rang high and bright, and behind its counter, ten years ago, there had been a woman who laughed at his jokes even when they were bad, especially when they were bad, and who had once told him, in the blue hour before dawn on the *Maren*'s deck, wrapped in his coat, that she intended to grow old in this town and that he was welcome to grow old in it too, if he could manage to stay put for five consecutive minutes.

He had managed eleven years. He'd simply done it somewhere else.

He had told himself the story so many times it had worn smooth as sea glass: *the debts, the threats, the men who came asking about the Voss family and its obligations, the night he understood that anyone who loved a Voss became collateral.* Leaving had been arithmetic. Freya minus Ansel equaled Freya safe. He had done the math on a dock at midnight with his duffel over his shoulder, and math didn't care that his hands shook, and math didn't care that she would wake to an empty harbor and spend years wondering what she'd done wrong.

Math didn't care. Ansel cared. That was the part the equation never accounted for, and the part that had followed him through every winter since, sitting in the wheelhouse beside him like a second captain who never took a shift.

He was still holding the rope when Marius appeared at the gangway, snow already gathering on his shoulders like the sky had started early, eager, unable to wait for the official forecast.

"Radio says it's turning," Marius called. "Harbormaster's closing the road over the headland tonight. If you're sailing out, sail now."

Ansel looked at the water. It had begun to move strangely, in long uneasy swells, the sea rolling its shoulders before the weight came down. A man could make the crossing in an hour, maybe ninety minutes, and be safely south of it all. Or a man could misjudge it, as men did, and spend the night riding out a whiteout forty miles from shelter, which was the kind of story that ended careers or ended captains.

Or—a third option, small and unreasonable and glowing at the edge of his mind like a lamp in a window—a man could miss the tide entirely. Could be stranded. In Harrow Bay. Where every room would fill by dark, because storms filled rooms, except—

Except there was no room. There couldn't be. There was a woman with flour on her sleeves and a life he had no right to, and the universe, whatever else it was, was not cruel enough to arrange—

"Ansel," Marius said, closer now, and there was something in his brother's voice that Ansel couldn't place, something almost like fear, though whether of the storm or of what the storm might trap them into, he couldn't say. "What are you going to do?"

Ansel looked at the sky, which had gone the color of the inside of a shell. He looked at the divorce papers, still wedged under the coffee tin in the office, unsigned, patient as stones. He thought of a green door and a high bright bell, and he hated himself a little for the hope that moved through him, quick and warm and entirely unwelcome, like a cat brushing against a stranger's legs.

"I'm going to wait for the pilot boat report," he said, which was captain-speak for *I don't know*, which was Ansel-speak for *I know exactly what I want and I'm terrified of it.*

Marius studied him for a long moment. Snow settled between them, fat and slow, flakes the size of coins.

"The harbor inn will be full by four," Marius said quietly. "And there's only one other building in this town with beds in it, Ansel. You know that."

Ansel knew. God help him, he knew. Somewhere up Front Street, behind a green door, a bell was waiting to ring, and the first flakes of the worst storm in five years were coming down over Harrow Bay like a decision being made on his behalf.

He picked up his duffel.
