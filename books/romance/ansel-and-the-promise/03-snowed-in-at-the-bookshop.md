## 3. Snowed In at the Bookshop

**Ansel**

The storm arrived the way bad news always did in Harrow Bay—first as a rumor on the wind, then all at once, everywhere, no room left to pretend you hadn't heard.

Ansel stood at the wheel of the *Maren Voss* and watched the sky go the color of wet slate. The last crossing of the season. Twelve passengers, a pallet of dry goods for the winter market, and one captain who had told himself for three days that docking here meant nothing.

He'd rehearsed it on the water. Dock, unload, turn around, beat the weather back to the mainland. He would not go up Harbor Street. He would not walk past the bookshop with its green door and its window display he could recite from memory even now, because he'd helped her build the shelves that held those books. He had a schedule and a tide table and ten years of practice at not looking.

The harbor had other plans.

By the time the cargo was off, the wind had swung north and turned mean, snow coming in sideways like it had a grudge. The harbormaster, a woman named Petra who had been seventeen and fearless when Ansel was twenty and stupid, took his mooring lines without meeting his eyes.

"Coast Guard says nothing moves until morning at the earliest," she said. "Maybe not till tomorrow night."

"I'll wait on the boat."

"Boat's getting hauled tomorrow for winter refit. You know that."

He knew that. He'd signed the paperwork himself, in another life, before everything. Ansel looked up Harbor Street, where the lamps were coming on one by one through the thickening white, and felt the particular dread of a man whose escape routes were closing politely, one by one, like doors in a house he used to live in.

"The Anchor's full," Petra said, reading his face. "Storm chasers got in on the morning boat. Everything in town's full. Well." She hesitated, and he watched her decide whether to say it. "Everything except—"

"Don't," he said.

"—the bookshop," she finished anyway, because Harrow Bay had never once let him off easy. "Freya keeps the loft over the shop. For visiting authors, mostly. It'll be cold, but it'll be dry."

Snow gathered on his shoulders. Somewhere behind him, the *Maren Voss* knocked gently against the pilings, patient as a debt.

"Does she—" he started, and didn't know how to end the sentence. *Does she still live there. Does she still leave the light on. Does she ever say my name.*

Petra just looked at him, and in her look was the whole town's memory, ten years deep.

"Go ask her," she said. "It's snowing. Nobody's going to make you say anything complicated tonight."

That, Ansel thought as he climbed Harbor Street with his duffel cutting into his shoulder, was the most generous lie anyone had told him in a decade.

* * *

**Freya**

The knock came at seven minutes past six, which was eleven minutes after Freya had flipped the sign to CLOSED and exactly the amount of time it took to pour the day's first cup of tea and believe, briefly, that the hard part of the evening was over.

Ingrid answered it. Of course Ingrid answered it; the child had the door-opening instincts of a golden retriever.

"Mom! There's a man and he's covered in snow and he looks lost but also like he lives here?"

"He doesn't live here," Freya said, setting down her tea, and then she saw him, and every ordered thing in her chest tipped over at once.

Ten years. She had imagined this moment maybe four hundred times—in the shower, on the ferry dock, at three in the morning—and in none of those versions had Ansel Voss stood in her doorway wearing snow like a second coat, looking at her as if she were a page he'd been searching for in a book he thought he'd lost.

"Freya." Just her name. His voice hadn't changed. That seemed like a violation of some natural law.

"Ansel." Her voice had changed, apparently. It came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice using her mouth.

Three seconds. She counted them later, lying awake. Three seconds of eye contact across her own threshold, long enough for her to notice the gray at his temples, the deeper lines around his eyes, the way he still stood like a man braced against weather. Long enough for him to see her take all of that in. Three seconds is nothing, unless it's everything.

Then Ingrid said, "Are you the ferry man?" and the world lurched back into motion.

"I'm the ferry man," Ansel said, crouching slightly, because he had always known how to talk to children, which was somehow worse. "Ansel Voss."

"Ingrid," said Ingrid, with the gravity of a queen receiving an ambassador. "You're dripping on our mat."

"Ingrid," Freya said faintly.

"It's true, Mom. Look at the mat."

Freya looked at the mat. Looking at the mat was easier than looking at the fact that Ansel Voss was standing in her doorway asking to be let in out of the storm, and that she was going to let him, because she was who she was, and ten years had not managed to sand that down.

"The loft's empty," she heard herself say. "Visiting author canceled in October. There's a bed, a heater that works about sixty percent of the time, and a bathroom down here that you'll share with me and my daughter, so if you snore, I want you to know I have opinions and a broom."

Something moved through his face—relief, and grief, and something warmer underneath both. "I don't snore."

"Marius snores," Ingrid offered helpfully. "Uncle Marius. He stayed with us in September. He snores like a walrus learning to sing."

The temperature in the doorway dropped several degrees that had nothing to do with the storm.

"Marius is here?" Ansel said.

"Marius is *stuck* here," Freya corrected. "His truck died Tuesday. He's been sleeping on my sofa and fixing my radiator and eating all my cinnamon rolls like payment." She crossed her arms. "So the loft has company already, technically. You two can fight over the good blanket."

She hadn't meant to say any of that. It came out of her the way truth does when you're too tired to guard the doors: fast, unpolished, dangerous. Ansel went very still, the way men do when they learn the war followed them home.

"Freya," he began.

"Take your boots off," she said, and stepped aside to let him in.

* * *

**Ansel**

The bookshop smelled the same, which was the cruelest part. Old paper and cedar shelving and the lavender thing Freya kept near the register that she swore worked and he swore was decorative. Ten years, and his body remembered the layout of the place better than his own apartment—he stepped around the low shelf by the door without looking, ducked under the hanging lamp, and caught himself reaching to straighten the stack of new arrivals that sat crooked, exactly the way it always sat crooked, because Freya alphabetized by heart and not by rule.

"You still shelve the Brontës wrong," he said, before he could stop himself.

"They're shelved by *mood*," Freya said, not turning around. "You wouldn't understand. You read engine manuals for fun."

"That's slander. I read tide tables too."

And there it was—the old rhythm, surfacing between them like something drowned coming up for air, both of them startled to find it still alive. Freya laughed, one short syllable, and then pressed her lips together like she'd sworn an oath against it.

Marius appeared from the back room holding a wrench and a dish towel, and Ansel watched his brother's face do something complicated: guilt first, then relief, then a kind of exhausted surrender, as if Marius had been waiting ten years for this exact terrible moment and had hoped for more warning.

"Well," Marius said. "The ferry man."

"The mechanic."

They stared at each other across the mystery section. Ingrid looked between them with the frank delight of a child sensing drama.

"You two know each other," she said.

"We've met," both brothers said at once, in identical tones of studied blandness, and Ingrid narrowed her eyes with her mother's exact expression, and Ansel thought: *we are all in so much trouble.*

* * *

**Freya**

By nine the storm had fully committed. Snow stacked against the windows in soft gray drifts, the power flickered twice and held, and Freya made chili for four because feeding people was the only language she trusted herself to speak tonight.

Dinner was a minefield walked at a polite pace. Ingrid narrated her school week. Marius asked questions about the furnace with the desperation of a man seeking shelter in small talk. And Ansel sat across from her at her own table, in the chair where almost nobody sat, eating her chili and saying "thank you" in a voice that cracked slightly on the second word, and Freya discovered that fury and hospitality could occupy the same body at the same time, the way a house can be burning while the kettle still whistles.

He'd left. He'd left without a word, without a note, without even the decency of a coward's letter, and she had spent a decade building a life around the hole he'd left shaped exactly like himself. And now he was passing her the salt.

"More?" she asked, holding the pot, and hated how steady she sounded.

"Please." He held out his bowl, and their fingers didn't touch, and the not-touching was louder than the storm.

Ingrid yawned enormously at half past nine, and Freya sent her up to bed with a book and a warning about reading past lights-out, and then the apartment above the shop went quiet, and the shop below held three adults and forty years of unsaid things.

"I'll take the sofa," Marius said.

"You always take the sofa," Ansel said quietly.

"Somebody has to," Marius said, and there was something under it, some weight Ansel couldn't read, and Freya watched the brothers look at each other the way people look across a frozen lake—testing, afraid of what's underneath.

"The loft's yours," she told Ansel, briskly, practically, a woman discussing heating arrangements and nothing else. "Towels are in the cedar chest. The heater rattles; kick it once on the left side and it behaves."

"Thank you, Freya." He said it like a man accepting a sentence, or a gift, or both. "For the door."

"You knocked," she said. "I opened it. That's the whole story."

But it wasn't, and they both knew it wasn't, and upstairs Ingrid lay awake listening to the strange music of two men's voices below, and the wind came off the water carrying snow, and somewhere in the dark of the reading loft Ansel Voss lay down in the bed where visiting authors slept, ten feet and ten years from the woman he'd left, close enough now that neither of them could pretend the distance was accidental anymore.

Below the loft, in the shadow of the stairs, Marius sat on the bottom step with his head in his hands, turning something over in his mind the way he'd turn a seized bolt—knowing it had to come loose eventually, knowing what it would cost, and knowing, with the terrible clarity of the sleepless, that he was the only one left holding the wrench.
