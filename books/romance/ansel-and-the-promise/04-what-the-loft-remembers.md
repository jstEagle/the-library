## 4. What the Loft Remembers

Sleep, it turned out, was a luxury the bookshop could not afford that night.

Freya lay in her narrow bed above the shop with the quilt pulled to her chin, listening to the storm batter the shutters and counting the sounds of a house that had doubled its population in a single afternoon. Below her floorboards: the creak of someone pacing the reading loft. The soft complaint of the old radiator. And beneath all of it, steady as tide, the knowledge that Ansel Voss was sleeping—was he sleeping?—twenty feet from where she lay.

She had put Ingrid down at eight, tucking her into the bed they shared on deep-winter nights when the pipes groaned and the wind sounded like the sea had opinions. Ingrid had fallen asleep mid-sentence, something about whether snow could get tired, and Freya had stood over her longer than usual, one hand on the small rising back, thinking: *You have no idea what is downstairs.*

Ten years. Ten years of telling herself she'd made peace with it, and now the man was below her like a stone in her shoe.

* * *

At some hour past midnight she gave up pretending and went down for tea, because insomnia was best conducted standing up. She moved through the dark shop by memory, past the biography shelf, past the children's corner with its crooked paper lanterns, and was filling the kettle when she heard the loft boards shift.

"I didn't wake you," Ansel said quietly.

"You didn't." She didn't turn around. "I don't sleep well before storms."

"Right." A pause. "You never did. You used to say the barometric pressure—" He stopped himself, as if the sentence had wandered somewhere off-limits.

"The pressure made my thoughts rattle around," she finished anyway, because apparently ten years hadn't taught her to leave things alone either. "Like dice in a cup."

The kettle began its low gathering hum. She lit the lamp behind the counter rather than the overhead light—the small mercy of dimness—and turned to find him sitting on the bottom step of the loft stairs, wrapped in the blanket she'd given him, looking like a man who had been exiled from his own sleep. His hair was grayer at the temples. There were lines around his eyes that hadn't been there at twenty-six. But the way he sat, elbows on knees, hands loose between them—that was exactly, unbearably the same.

"There's chamomile," she offered. "Or the emergency hot chocolate I hide from Ingrid behind the dictionaries."

"Behind the dictionaries," he repeated, and something almost like a smile touched him. "Clever. No child has ever voluntarily approached a dictionary."

"That's the theory." She took down two mugs before she could think better of it, then stood holding them, absurdly betrayed by her own muscle memory. She knew which mug he'd liked. She reached for it automatically—the blue one with the chipped rim—and caught herself, set it back, chose a plain brown one instead. He watched her do it. Neither of them said anything about it, which was its own conversation.

She made the hot chocolate. It seemed safer than tea; tea required questions about strength and steeping, and hot chocolate was just surrender in liquid form.

* * *

They ended up on opposite ends of the old reading couch, a careful ocean of cushion between them, mugs warming their hands. Outside, the storm threw handfuls of itself against the windows.

"Marius snores," Ansel said, after a while.

"He's in the armchair by the window?"

"He insisted. Said the loft was too drafty for a man of his constitution. Then he fell asleep in under four minutes, so I assume his constitution is doing fine."

Despite everything—a decade of everything—Freya laughed. Just a small laugh, quickly caught, but it escaped, and she saw it land on him like something fragile being handed across a gap.

"He used to do that," she said. "Fall asleep anywhere. He slept through the entire midnight showing of *Casablanca* at the community hall. Head on my shoulder. I had a stiff neck for two days."

"I remember." His voice went quiet. "I carried him out to the truck. You walked beside me and quoted the whole last scene from memory."

"*Here's looking at you, kid,*" she said, before she could stop herself, and then hated how the words hung there, aimed at him without permission.

Ansel looked down into his mug. "You still do that," he said. "Remember everything."

"Somebody has to. Somebody left the remembering to me." The words came out sharper than intended, and she watched him absorb them—not flinching, not defending, just taking the hit like a man who'd agreed long ago that he deserved it. That was somehow worse than an argument. Arguments you could win.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For—for all of it. For the way it happened. For the way it didn't happen."

"You can't even say it." She kept her voice low, mindful of the sleeping floors above and beside them. "Ten years, and you're going to sit in my shop and talk in circles around it. 'All of it.' 'The way it happened.' Say the word, Ansel. Say *why*."

The lamp flickered. The radiator knocked twice, like a fist on a door.

"I can't," he said finally. "Not tonight. Not because I don't want to tell you. Because if I start, I'll tell you all of it, and tonight—" he looked up at her, and his eyes in the lamplight were the same gray-green as the winter harbor, "—tonight I need you to be able to look at me tomorrow. And the day after. While we're stuck here. If I tell you now, you'll spend the rest of the storm hating me, and I—I wanted one night where you weren't."

"That's not your decision," she said softly.

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. I've gotten into trouble before, making decisions for other people." Something crossed his face then, quick and dark, gone before she could read it. "It's a habit I'm trying to break."

Freya set her mug down on the side table with a deliberate click. "Here's what I know," she said. "I know you loved me. I never doubted that, not once, not even in the worst year, and God knows I tried to doubt it because doubting would have been easier than the alternative. The alternative being that you loved me and left anyway. Which means whatever took you away was bigger than love. And I have spent ten years trying to imagine what could possibly be bigger than—"

She stopped. Breathed. Ingrid was asleep upstairs. Marius was asleep by the window. The words *bigger than loving you* stayed safely behind her teeth.

"Do you know what I did, the first spring?" she asked instead. "I wrote you letters. Eleven of them. I kept them in the drawer where I keep the gift wrap. I finally burned them in the wood stove when Ingrid was two, because I realized I was keeping a shrine in a stationery drawer, and I had a daughter who deserved a mother who wasn't half-haunted."

"What happened to the ashes?" Ansel asked. His voice had gone rough.

"They went up the flue. Very poetic. Ingrid thought I was burning receipts." She smiled, and it hurt. "That's the thing about you leaving, Ansel. Life didn't stop. It just got quieter. I built a whole life out of quiet. Good quiet, mostly. This shop. Her. Tuesday soup nights with Petra. I want you to understand that I wasn't waiting. I refuse to have been waiting."

"I believe you," he said.

"But?"

"But nothing. I believe you." He looked at his hands. "I just wish—" He stopped again. This man, who could navigate a ferry through ice fog by feel, could not finish a sentence in her living room.

"Wish what?"

"I wish I'd gotten to hear her laugh," he said. "Yours. When it escapes. When you try to hold it in and it gets out anyway, like just now." He glanced toward the ceiling, toward the small sleeping weight of her. "Hers too. Someday. If—if that's ever allowed."

Freya stood, because staying on that couch one more minute felt dangerous in a way the storm outside was not. She gathered the mugs, and at the sink, with her back to him, she said the truest thing she could afford:

"She laughs like I do. Escapes and all. You'd have known that."

Then she climbed the stairs to her daughter, and did not look back, because looking back would have undone her entirely.

* * *

Marius had not been asleep for any of it.

He lay in the wingback chair with his breathing arranged into slow, theatrical rhythm, and listened to the two people he had spent ten years lying to speak to each other in whispers—careful, courteous, circling the wreck like divers afraid to touch it. Every kind thing they said to each other landed on him like a fine. Every kindness was worse than anger. Anger would have been simple.

He heard his brother say *I need you to be able to look at me tomorrow*, and Marius pressed his palm hard against his eyes in the dark.

Because that was the lie, wasn't it. The whole architecture of it. Ansel had sacrificed everything—his name, his future, the woman he loved—and Marius had protected that sacrifice the way you protect anything precious: by burying it. Ten years of silence, framed as loyalty. Ten years of watching Freya Nystrom build a life out of quiet and telling himself it was mercy, letting her believe she'd been left for no reason at all, because the reason belonged to Ansel to give.

But Ansel wasn't giving it. Ansel would circle this shop until the ice melted and the ferry ran and he could sail away again with his sacrifice intact and unexplained, noble and mute as a headstone.

And Freya would spend another decade being graceful about a wound she couldn't even see.

Marius turned his face to the window, where the snow came down thick and white and endless, erasing the harbor, the road, every path out of town. Stranded. They were all stranded, and the storm had days yet to spend.

In the morning, he thought. Tomorrow, while the roads were still buried and no one could leave—while *he* couldn't leave—he would break the seal his brother had kept shut for ten years. He would stand in this warm, lamp-lit shop full of books and tell Freya Nystrom why Ansel Voss had vanished, and let the truth fall where it fell.

His brother would hate him.

Good, Marius thought, into the dark. Hate was a door too. At least it opened.

Above him, faint through the floorboards, he heard Freya's footsteps cross to her daughter's room, pause there a long moment, and move on. The shop settled into silence. The storm kept its own counsel.

Tomorrow, then.
