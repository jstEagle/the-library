## 5. Marius Breaks the Seal

Marius Voss had spent three days in a bookshop loft learning that he was, fundamentally, a man of the sea who could not sleep on land.

The cot beneath him creaked every time he breathed. The rafters above him ticked and settled like an old man complaining about his knees. And below—through the gap between the floorboards that no amount of wool blanket had ever fixed—he could hear his brother and Freya Nystrom whispering to each other in the dark.

Not arguing. Not confessing. Whispering. The way people do when they're circling something enormous and hoping it won't notice them looking at it directly.

Marius lay on his back with his hands folded on his chest like a man at his own wake and thought: *I have been lying down for ten years too.*

He'd told himself the silence was a kindness. That was the trick of it—the really elegant, really poisonous trick—that you could dress a lie up in sacrifice's clothing and wear it for a decade without once feeling the weight of it. He had kept Ansel's secret the way you keep a coal banked under ash: carefully, faithfully, telling yourself you were preserving warmth when all you were doing was making sure nobody could use the fire.

Below him, Freya laughed. Quietly, barely a breath of it, but Marius knew that laugh. He'd heard it across the harbor in another life, back when the Voss brothers were two halves of one reputation and Harrow Bay still called them *the boys* instead of *those Voss men*.

He sat up. The cot protested. He ignored it, the way he'd been ignoring things for ten years, and then—because apparently he was done with that now, because apparently three days of watching his brother move through this woman's shop like a ghost rehearsing for a haunting had finally cracked something loose in him—he put on his boots and went downstairs.

* * *

The bookshop at night was a different country. By day it was Freya's kingdom, ordered and bright, every spine aligned. By lamplight it was all shadow and possibility, the shelves rising up around the reading nook like a forest where anything might be living.

Ansel sat in the armchair by the cold fireplace. Freya stood by the window with her arms crossed—not defensive, exactly. More like a woman holding herself together with her own hands.

They both looked up when Marius came down the stairs. He saw the moment they registered his face, whatever it was doing, because they went very still. People who grew up around water learned to read weather. These two read him instantly.

"Marius," Ansel said, in the voice he used for talking a man off a ladder or out of a bad bet. "It's late."

"It is late," Marius agreed. "It's also been late for ten years, so I figure we're all well rested on the subject."

Freya made a small sound. It might have been a laugh. It might have been the other thing.

Marius looked at his brother. His brother looked back, and there it was—the warning, plain as a red flag in a gale. *Don't.* Ten years of *don't*, delivered wordlessly across galley tables and holiday dinners and their mother's funeral, where Ansel had stood at the edge of the cemetery like a man checking whether Harrow Bay was visible from there. It wasn't. He'd checked anyway.

"I'm tired," Marius said. "I'm tired in a way that sleeping isn't going to fix, and I think we all know it." He pulled out the second armchair—the good one, the one Freya had clearly been saving—and sat down heavily, the way a man sits when he's decided. "So I'm going to say the thing. And Ansel, you can hate me if you want. You've had practice hating me quietly. This way at least it'll be out loud."

"Don't," Ansel said, out loud this time.

"Your father owed money," Marius said, to Freya, because this was not a story for Ansel. Ansel had lived it. "A lot of it. To men who were not the sort who send polite letters. You remember what Harrow Bay was like that winter—the cannery closing, half the boats mortgaged. Our father had signed things he shouldn't have, with people from up the coast, and by November the interest alone was more than the *Vesta* earned in a season."

Freya had gone white. Not dramatically—she wasn't a dramatic woman; she was a woman who alphabetized her grief—but Marius watched her hand find the windowsill behind her and hold on.

"They came to the house in December," Marius went on. "Two of them. Sat in our kitchen drinking our coffee and explained, very reasonably, that debts of the Voss name belonged to anyone tied to the Voss name. Family. Crew. Anyone who—" he paused, and hated himself for the pause, "—anyone who loved us, I suppose you'd say. They said it plainly. They weren't threatening Ansel. They were threatening everyone *near* him. They said a man with nothing to lose was a man they couldn't collect from, but a man with something to lose—"

He stopped. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere upstairs, Ingrid turned over in her sleep, and the whole room listened to the small creak of it.

"He left to keep you off their list," Marius said. "That's the whole story. That's the secret I've kept since I was twenty-six years old. He walked away from you so that you would never be worth anything to those men. And I let you believe—" His voice did something he didn't authorize. He pushed through it. "I let you believe he just didn't want you. For ten years. Because he asked me to, and because keeping his secret felt like loyalty, and because it was easier than admitting that loyalty and cowardice can wear the same coat."

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved.

Then Ansel stood up, and Marius braced for the blow—not a fist; they'd never been fist men, but words, the cold precise kind Ansel kept holstered for emergencies.

What he got instead was worse. Ansel just looked at him with an expression Marius hadn't seen since they were boys, when Ansel had taken the blame for a broken window he hadn't broken, and Marius had understood even then, at eight years old, that his brother loved him in a way that would eventually cost somebody everything.

"You didn't have to carry that alone," Ansel said quietly.

"No," Marius agreed. "And she didn't have to carry yours. Funny how we divided it up. Two brothers, one woman, and we split the damage three ways and called it protection."

He turned to Freya, because she deserved to hear it from someone who wasn't the man who'd done the leaving.

"There's more, and you should have all of it. He didn't go straight to the ferries. First year, he worked the fish processors in Kittle Harbor under a name that wasn't ours. Sent money home through me—cash, unmarked, no return address—every month for two years until the debt was cleared. I paid it out for him. I got to be the one who buried it, which meant I got to be the one who watched your face at the market, every winter, every spring, waiting for you to ask me about him. And you never did. Do you know why? Because you were proud. You were proud and hurt and raising a baby alone, and you never once gave me the satisfaction of asking, and God help me, Freya, I *thanked* you for it. Every time you didn't ask, I thanked you, because every time you didn't ask, I got to keep my brother's secret a little longer and call it love."

Freya's eyes were bright and terrible. She wasn't crying. Marius suspected that when this woman finally cried, it would be structural.

"A baby," she said. Her voice was steady in the way ice is steady. "You knew about Ingrid."

"I knew you were pregnant when he left. Yes."

"And neither of you—" She stopped. Started again. "Ten years. There was a christening. There were school plays. There was a *pneumonia scare* when she was four, Marius, I sat in a hospital corridor for two nights, and the man I thought had abandoned me was forty miles away hauling nets, and you knew, and you let me sit there believing I was alone in the world."

"We believed we were keeping you safe," Ansel said.

Freya turned to him, and Marius watched her decide—watched it happen, the way you watch a woman decide to take a boat out in weather—exactly how much truth to spend right now.

"Safe," she repeated. "You'll forgive me if I need a minute before I have feelings about that word."

She walked to the stairs. Stopped. Put one hand on the rail.

"For the record," she said, not turning around, "the men with the list. Are they dead? Jailed? Retired comfortably? Because if any part of that threat is still standing, then the two of you have just handed me something new to be furious about, and I'd like to know which fury to schedule first."

"The debt died with Dad," Marius said. "The men moved on years ago. There's nothing left to protect anyone from. There hasn't been for a long time." He heard how it sounded even as he said it. "Which means the last ten years bought exactly nothing except—"

"Except," Freya finished, "a girl who doesn't know her own history and a man who taught himself to be a stranger." She climbed two steps, paused. "Ingrid asks about the photograph sometimes. The one behind the counter. I tell her it's the harbor in winter." Her voice frayed, just slightly, at the edge. "She deserves better arithmetic than that. So do I."

Her footsteps went up and away, and a door closed upstairs with the particular gentleness of a woman refusing to slam one.

* * *

Afterward, the brothers sat in the lamplight like two men in the aftermath of a storm that had passed through the room rather than over it.

"You could have let me keep it," Ansel said at last. "One more day. The ferry leaves at dawn."

"That's exactly why I couldn't." Marius rubbed his face with both hands. "Do you know what I realized tonight, listening to the two of you whisper through the floorboards like teenagers? I realized I've been guarding a vault that's been empty for years. The danger's gone. The debt's gone. All that's left in there is the lie itself, sitting in the dark getting heavier. And you were going to sail out tomorrow morning and leave her holding it again."

Ansel was quiet for a long time. Outside, the wind had dropped; the snow came down soft and businesslike, already burying the harbor road.

"She has a daughter," he said finally. It wasn't an objection. It was a man testing the weight of a door he was afraid to open.

"She has a daughter who laughs like you," Marius said. "I heard it through the floorboards tonight, and I nearly went through them. Ansel. Brother. You spent ten years being noble, and here's what nobility got: she thinks she was unlovable, the kid thinks the harbor in winter is a mystery, and I think I'm a liar. Nobody was saved. We just spread the drowning around."

Ansel looked at the cold fireplace for a long moment. Then he reached into his coat, hanging over the armchair, and took out an envelope—soft at the corners, travel-worn, addressed in handwriting Marius recognized from a hundred shipping manifests and had never once seen used for anything true.

The divorce papers, Marius assumed. Or the letter. He'd carried both for months, Ansel had admitted that much on the crossing north.

But Ansel held the envelope without opening it, turning it over once, twice, like a coin he was deciding whether to spend.

"Dawn ferry," Marius said gently. "Weather clears by morning. You could be on it."

"I know."

"You could also not be."

Ansel looked up at the ceiling—toward the loft, toward the stairs, toward the door that had closed so gently—and Marius watched his brother arrive somewhere he'd apparently been sailing toward for a decade.

Above them, faintly, a floorboard creaked. Freya, awake. Listening. Waiting to find out which version of the story she'd been given was the one that got to be real.

Marius held his breath, and waited to see which brother would speak first.
