## 6. The Arithmetic of Sacrifice

The revelation sat between us all through breakfast like a third guest nobody had invited.

Marius had said his piece the night before and then retreated to the loft with the wounded dignity of a man who had finally put down something heavy, only to discover his arms still remembered its weight. Now he was upstairs, ostentatiously not listening, and Ansel was standing in my kitchen washing dishes that didn't need washing, and I was sitting at my own table in my own house feeling like a stranger at my own funeral.

He'd washed dishes here before, ten years ago. He used to hum while he did it—sea shanties, mostly, terrible ones, half-invented. This morning he was silent, and the silence was worse than the shanties. The silence said: *I know what I did, and I would like to be forgiven for it without the inconvenience of a conversation.*

"Put the plate down, Ansel."

"It's already clean."

"Put it down anyway."

He set it in the rack with the care of a man disarming something. Then he turned around, and there he was—Ansel Voss, forty-one years old now, gray coming in at his temples like frost on a railing, standing in my kitchen as though the intervening decade had been a long weekend.

"I was going to come back," he said. "I need you to know that. The plan was always—"

"Stop." I held up a hand. "I'm going to say some things, and I need you to let me finish them, because I have been rehearsing them for ten years in the shower and at three in the morning and once, memorably, into a display of discount romance paperbacks, and I have never once gotten to say them out loud. So." I breathed. "Sit down."

He sat. He looked, absurdly, like a man at a job interview, hands folded, spine straight, as if good posture might count in his favor.

"Ten years ago," I said, "I woke up and you were gone. No note. No call. Nothing. I spent the first week thinking you were dead. There'd been a storm, and I made myself sick checking the coast guard reports. I called the ferry office and a woman I didn't know told me Captain Voss had taken a position elsewhere, and she said it in that voice people use when they know more than they're saying, and I spent the next month thinking you'd left me for someone else. Then I spent the next nine years and eleven months thinking—" my voice did something I hadn't authorized, cracked sideways, and I pushed through it— "thinking that I had been so easy to leave that leaving me hadn't even required a note. That's the part, Ansel. Not that you went. That I was so *disposable* you didn't think I'd earned an explanation."

"Freya—"

"I'm not finished."

He closed his mouth. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked—Marius, listening, and let him.

"And now I find out the truth, and do you know what the truth is? The truth is that you *did* love me. You loved me so much that you decided—*you*, alone, in your captain's head, without consulting the other adult in the relationship—that you got to choose what my life would be. You looked at me and you saw something fragile. Something to be *sheltered*. And you made the biggest decision of both our lives without ever once asking me whether I wanted to be saved."

"The threats were real," he said quietly. "You don't know what my father owed, or to whom. They came to the dock, Freya. They knew your name. They knew about the shop. They knew about—" he stopped.

"Say it."

"They knew about the baby. Ingrid. You'd only just told me." His jaw worked. "I did the math. That's all it was. Arithmetic. If I stayed, they'd use you. If I left, I was a debt walking around on legs instead of a hostage they could collect. I took the whole thing with me and I went. It wasn't a choice between you and the sea. It was a choice between you and *them*."

"And you never once thought," I said, and my voice had gone very steady now, the way it does right before I drop something heavy on a customer's foot, "that I might have wanted to make that choice *with* you?"

"There was nothing to make. You'd have insisted on staying. You'd have insisted on fighting. You'd have—" he gestured helplessly at the kitchen, the shop beyond it, the whole small warm architecture of my life— "you'd have done exactly what you're doing now, which is refuse to let me protect you."

"Yes," I said. "Exactly this. Congratulations. You've identified the problem."

"Freya." He leaned forward. "They broke a man's hand over four hundred dollars. Four hundred. What do you think they'd have done over what my father owed?"

"I don't know," I said. "And that's the point. I *don't know*, because you never gave me the chance to find out. You decided I was a thing to be kept safe the way you'd keep a first edition in glass—look, don't touch, too valuable for the real world. Ansel, I was twenty-six years old. I was already raising a child alone. I had already buried a mother and outlasted a father and kept a business alive in a town where the economy is basically weather. I was not fragile. I was *tired*, and there is a difference, and you looked at a tired woman and saw glass."

"You'd have come with me," he said, and there was something almost accusatory in it, as if my love were the crime. "You'd have left the shop. You'd have taken Ingrid onto a ferry and into whatever came next, and if they'd followed—"

"Then I'd have been there," I said, "when you needed someone. Instead of here, finding out from your *brother* a decade later that the man I loved didn't leave me, he *evacuated* me, without my consent, like furniture ahead of a flood." I stood up. My chair scraped. "You want to know the cruelest part? You didn't just take yourself. You took the *story*. For ten years I was the woman Ansel Voss left, and I had to carry that around like a stain, and it wasn't even true. You let me wear your sacrifice as my shame. You got to be noble and I got to be abandoned, and you call that arithmetic?"

"Yes," he said, and to his credit—he stood up too, and his voice rose to meet mine, and for the first time since the door opened he sounded like the man who used to argue with me about whether *Wuthering Heights* was a love story or a horror story. "Yes, I do. Because the other math was your blood on a dock somewhere, or Ingrid growing up looking over her shoulder, and I would rather you hate me for thirty years than—"

"You don't get to *prefer* things *for* me!" It came out louder than I intended, loud enough to rattle the teacups, and I realized my hands were shaking. "That's the whole—that's the *entire* point, Ansel. Love isn't a decision you make on someone's behalf. It's not a debt you pay in secret. It's standing next to someone in the storm and saying *what do we do*, not walking out into it alone and calling it devotion. You didn't protect me. You just made sure that when the storm came, I'd be standing in it *alone*, without even knowing why."

The kitchen went very quiet.

And then a small voice from the doorway said, "Why are you fighting about a storm?"

Ingrid stood there in her socks, hair sleep-wild, clutching the stuffed puffin she was far too old for and would defend to the death. She looked at Ansel. She looked at me. She had her father's eyes—she'd had them all along, and I had never once let myself think about that until this week, and now I couldn't stop.

"We're not fighting, sweetheart," I said, in the voice every mother on earth has used while actively fighting.

"You're doing the quiet-loud thing." She came into the kitchen and planted herself between us like a very small referee. She squinted up at Ansel. "Are you the one from the picture?"

The teacup in the rack suddenly became the most interesting object in the room.

"Ingrid," I said carefully, "this is Ansel. He's—"

"He's the one from the picture," she said with the terrible certainty of nine-year-olds, "and you never let anyone ask about him, which means he's important, which means you're fighting about something important, which means—" she took a breath, the way she did when she was about to deploy logic like a weapon— "which means you should probably not do it in front of me. But also I think you should do it. Grandma Solveig says the fights you don't have are the ones that follow you around."

Out of the mouths of babes, and into the middle of my kitchen.

Ansel had gone the color of old rope. He was looking at Ingrid the way a man looks at a lighthouse he thought had gone dark years ago—something lit behind his ribs, and terror of what it might cost to steer toward it. "Hello, Ingrid," he managed.

"You have my chin," Ingrid informed him, with the mercilessness of the young. "Mom says I get it from her, but I've seen photos of her at my age, and her chin is completely different. I've been doing an investigation."

"*Ingrid.*"

"What? It's true."

I pressed my fingers to my eyes. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard creaked again—Marius, retreating now, giving us what privacy could still be salvaged, which was approximately none.

Ingrid looked between us once more, satisfied that her work here was done, and announced: "I'm going to read in the window seat. You can finish being weird now." And she padded out, puffin under her arm, leaving a silence of the specifically detonated variety.

Ansel exhaled. "She's—"

"Don't," I said, but there was no armor left in it. I sat back down. The fury was still there, banked, real, and I wasn't finished with it—not by half—but grief had come up underneath it the way cold water comes up under warm, and for a moment I just let myself sit in the wreckage of the morning. "She asked about you once, you know. When she was six. She'd found the picture. She asked who the man at the harbor was, and I said *an old friend*, and she said, *you looked happy in a different way then*, and I changed the subject, and I have been changing the subject for ten years, and I'm so tired of it, Ansel. I'm tired of the subject."

"I know," he said. "I'm sorry. I am so sorry, and I know it's ten years too late and it doesn't fix the arithmetic."

"No," I said. "It doesn't. Because here's the thing you still haven't understood, even now, even after all of it." I looked at him across the kitchen, across the decade, across the dish rack full of clean plates. "You keep saying you saved me. But I wasn't in the fire, Ansel. I was *outside* of it, in the cold, for ten years, wondering why the person I loved had locked the door. You didn't save me from the storm. You just decided I'd rather be lost than rained on. And you were wrong."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And in his face I watched the arithmetic finally, finally fail—the whole ledger of it, ten years of careful sacrifice, collapsing under the one entry he'd never counted: what the protection had cost the person it was for.

Outside, the wind had begun to die. Through the front windows of the shop, the sky over the harbor was going thin and pale at the edges, the way it does before a clearing—and I thought, with a feeling I couldn't name, *the last ferry leaves at dawn.*
