## 7. Last Ferry of the Season

Ansel woke before the alarm, the way he always did, and for a moment he didn't know where he was. The ceiling above him was wrong—too low, too close, crossed by a beam that some long-ago carpenter had carved with a row of small birds. Then the smell reached him: old paper and woodsmoke and something faintly sweet, like cinnamon left too long in a jar, and his body understood before his mind did.

The bookshop. The loft. Freya's reading loft, with its sagging cushions and its one small window, where he had spent the night listening to her breathe one floor below him and pretending he wasn't.

He lay still and let the knowledge arrive in pieces. The storm had spent itself sometime after midnight; he'd heard it go, the wind loosening its grip on the eaves like a fist unclenching. Through the window now the sky was doing that thing Harrow Bay skies did after a blow—scoured clean, pale gold at the eastern edge, so clear it looked almost apologetic.

Clear skies meant the ferry could run. Clear skies meant the last crossing of the season left at dawn, and he was on it.

He sat up too fast and the loft complained, a creak from the floorboards that seemed to carry all the way down through the shop, through the shelves, into the room where Freya slept. He froze. Nothing stirred. Or if it did, she gave no sign, and he told himself that was mercy, though it felt more like being dismissed.

* * *

They had not spoken since Marius broke everything open.

Ansel dressed in the dark, moving around the loft's furniture like a man defusing something. His duffel was already packed—it had been packed since Tuesday, since before the storm, since before any of this—and he lifted it and stood at the top of the stairs and looked down into the shop.

Freya was there. Of course she was there. She stood behind the counter with a mug of coffee held in both hands, watching the light come up through the front windows, and she was wearing the gray sweater, the one with the loose cuff she used to pull down over her knuckles when she was thinking hard about something she didn't want to think about. Ten years and she still wore it like armor made of wool.

Marius was on the couch beneath the window, already awake, already dressed, his own bag at his feet. Their eyes met across the shop and Marius gave him a single nod—the nod of men who have said everything unsayable and have nothing left but logistics.

"Roads are plowed," Freya said, without turning around. Her voice was level, pleasant, the voice she used with customers. "Harald called the dock at five. You'll make the crossing."

"Thank you," Ansel said. "For—for the loft. For opening the door."

"You'd have done the same." She turned then, finally, and looked at him, and her face was calm in a way that frightened him more than fury would have. Fury would have meant he still mattered enough to burn over. This was something else. This was a woman who had taken her story back overnight and found it fit her better without him in it. "It's what anyone would do."

It was not what anyone would do. It was what she would do, because she was Freya Nystrom, and she had been leaving the light on for lost people her whole life, and he had been the worst kind of lost person—the kind who thanked her by disappearing.

Ingrid appeared at the top of the back stairs, hair sleep-wild, dragging a blanket shaped like a whale. She was nine going on forty-five, with Freya's exact eyebrows deployed at maximum skepticism.

"You're leaving," she said. Not a question.

"The season's over, sprout," Marius said gently. "Ferry has to go to winter harbor."

"I know how ferries work." She came down the stairs, bypassed both brothers entirely, and stood in front of Ansel with her chin up. "You knew my mom. Before."

"Yes," Ansel said. His voice cracked on a word of two letters, which felt about right.

"She doesn't talk about before much." Ingrid studied him the way she probably studied difficult vocabulary words, with narrowed eyes and total commitment. "But she keeps your picture behind the counter. The one at the harbor. So."

"Ingrid," Freya said softly.

"So I'm just saying," Ingrid finished, with the magnificent indifference of a child who knows exactly what she's saying, "that people who get kept in pictures usually matter. Even if everybody pretends they don't." She gathered her whale blanket and marched back upstairs, pausing only to add, over her shoulder: "Somebody should write that down. It's good."

* * *

The walk to the dock took eleven minutes and none of them spoke for ten of them.

Harrow Bay in early morning after snow was a town out of a story someone kinder had written. Rooftops white-capped, smoke standing straight up from chimneys in the windless cold, the harbor water lying flat and black as poured ink between the ice sheets at the shallows. The *Petrel* waited at the end of the pier, her hull crusted white, her stack breathing thin gray smoke, looking exactly as patient and exactly as lonely as Ansel felt.

He checked her lines because checking lines was what his hands were for when his heart wasn't working. Fuel: full. Ice on the deck rails: chipped away. Wheelhouse: cold, waiting, smelling of diesel and coffee thermoses and twenty years of his life.

Marius stopped at the gangway.

"I'm coming with you," he said.

Ansel looked at him. "You don't have to."

"Yeah," Marius said. "I do. Winter harbor needs two hands, and besides—" he huffed a laugh that didn't have any humor in it, "—I figure I've got about ten years of things to say to you and a whole winter with no distractions. You're welcome in advance."

"Marius—"

"No. Listen." And there it was, the thing under the gruffness, the thing Marius had carried so long it had worn a groove in him. "I stayed quiet because staying quiet felt like loyalty. Turns out it was just cowardice wearing loyalty's coat. I let you be the martyr because it was easier than being honest, and I let her believe—" He stopped. Started again. "I wrote something. On the boat. Don't read it while I'm watching, I mean it, I'll jump overboard."

He pressed an envelope into Ansel's hand. It was thick. It had clearly been written, reconsidered, rewritten, and sealed with the desperation of a man who knew that if he reread it one more time he'd never send it.

"Marius," Ansel said. "What happened in that shop—you didn't ruin anything. There was nothing left to ruin."

"That's the saddest thing you've ever said to me," Marius said, "and once you told me my chili was 'a hate crime against beans.' Get on the boat, Ansel."

* * *

The *Petrel* pulled out at 7:14, and Harrow Bay began to shrink.

Ansel stood at the rail with the envelope in his jacket pocket, unopened, because Marius was at the wheel and watching him in the reflection of the glass like a hawk watching a very disappointing mouse. The town fell away behind them in stages. First the dock, where Freya had stood until the bow line came off and then turned and walked back up the pier without waving, which was somehow worse than waving, worse than crying, worse than anything. Then the church steeple. Then the bookshop's crooked chimney, visible above the roofline, the chimney he'd stared at from the water a thousand times in another life.

Then the harbor mouth, and the open gray sea, and nothing.

He thought about duty. That was the strange part—he'd expected to feel relief, or grief, or some clean single emotion, and instead he felt like a man holding a receipt for something he'd paid everything for and never received.

Ten years ago, the arithmetic had been simple. His father owed men who collected debts in ways banks didn't. Anyone tied to the Voss name was a target, and Freya—pregnant, hopeful, twenty-three years old and trusting him with her whole soft heart—was tied to him in every way that mattered. So he'd done the math. One man leaves, one woman grieves, one child never learns to fear a knock at the door. Three losses balanced against one safety. He'd carried that equation like ballast for a decade. It had gotten him through divorce papers he couldn't sign, through a marriage that failed because he'd given its best rooms to a ghost, through every empty winter.

And now, standing at the rail with the town gone from view, the equation finally showed its work, and the work was garbage.

Because the threats had ended eight years ago. Because his father's creditors had settled, scattered, died, moved on. Because Marius had known it was safe to come home for three years and had said nothing, and Ansel had never asked, because asking risked learning he could return—and a man can't bear to learn he's free if freedom means admitting he chose the cage. Because Freya hadn't needed protecting. She'd needed a partner, and instead she'd gotten a guardian angel who quit without notice, and she'd spent ten years being brave alone in a town full of people who thought she'd been left because she wasn't worth staying for.

He hadn't saved her from anything. The debt had dissolved like salt in water. But the decade—that was real. Ingrid's first steps, first words, first day of school: real, and missed. Every winter market, every storm, every night she'd lain awake in the apartment above the shop deciding she must have been unlovable: real, and caused by him. He had sacrificed precisely nothing and stolen precisely everything, and called it love, and called it duty, and the victory of having kept her safe was so hollow he could hear the wind whistling through it.

"Dawn's behind us," Marius called from the wheelhouse, too casually. "You've been staring at nothing for an hour. Either read the letter or throw it overboard, but pick one, because the suspense is ruining my steering."

Ansel took out the envelope. His hands were steady, which surprised him. He opened it and unfolded pages covered in Marius's terrible slanted handwriting, the handwriting of a man who wrote like he talked, fast and too loud.

*Ansel. I'm sorry. Not for telling the truth—I'd tell it again—but for making you carry it alone, and for letting her carry it longer. Here's what I know that you don't seem to: you keep choosing absence like it's a virtue. It isn't. It never was. Leaving her wasn't sacrifice, it was surrender, and you dressed it up so well even you believed it. She opened her door to us in a storm because that's who she is. The least you can do is stop being a storm. Write to her. Come back in spring if she'll have you—not before, and not to decide anything for her. Ask. For once in your stubborn life, ask.*

*Your brother (still, despite everything),*
*Marius*

*P.S. The girl is right. Somebody should write it down.*

Ansel folded the letter along its creases, slowly, and looked back the way they'd come. Harrow Bay was long gone below the horizon, but he could see it anyway, the way memory keeps its own hours—the lit window of the bookshop, the gray sweater, the photograph behind the counter that she had never taken down, not once in ten years, not even to hide it when he walked into her shop out of a snowstorm.

She had kept him in the picture. He had kept himself out of the frame. Those were not the same thing, and only one of them was forgivable.

At the wheelhouse door, Marius watched him and waited.

"Marius," Ansel said. "Do we still have that stationery from the ferry office? The ugly stuff with the seagull on it?"

A slow grin spread across his brother's weathered face. "Top drawer. Right next to the pens that don't work."

"Get me one that works." Ansel turned toward the wheelhouse, toward the winter, toward the letter he was finally going to send. "I've got until spring to ask a question, and I intend to ask it properly."
