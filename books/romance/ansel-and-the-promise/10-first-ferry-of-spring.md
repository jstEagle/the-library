## 10. First Ferry of Spring

The ice went out on a Tuesday.

Freya heard it before she saw it—a low groan rolling up from the harbor at four in the morning, like the whole bay clearing its throat after a long winter's silence. She lay in bed listening to the crack and shift of it, the great plates of ice breaking apart and grinding against each other, and she thought: *there it is.* The sound she'd been waiting for since December, when a package arrived containing ten years of unsent words and one careful question.

*May I come back in spring, if you'll have me?*

She had read the letter aloud to Ingrid by lamplight, exactly as she'd promised herself she would—no hiding, no editing, no performing fine. Ingrid, who was eleven now and sharp as a gull after a dropped chip, had listened all the way through with her knees pulled to her chest, and when Freya finished, her daughter said:

"So he's my—" She stopped. Started again. "He knew you. Before."

"He did," Freya said. "Before you. He loved me then, and he made a terrible decision out of that love, and he's spent ten years learning why it was terrible."

Ingrid considered this the way she considered everything, thoroughly and slightly suspiciously. "Is he going to be weird about it?"

"Probably a little."

"Okay." A pause. "Can we get a dog if he moves here?"

"Ingrid."

"I'm just saying. Negotiation is a thing adults do. I've read about it."

Freya had laughed until she cried, which was its own kind of answer, and they both knew it.

Now it was April, and the harbor was open, and the first ferry of the season was scheduled to come through on Thursday morning. The whole town knew. Of course the whole town knew—Harrow Bay had a population of nine hundred and a gossip network that would put national intelligence agencies to shame. Petra at the bakery had already asked Freya twice whether she was "nervous," in the tone of someone who hoped very much that she was, because nervousness meant a story, and stories were the only currency worth anything in a town this size.

"I'm not nervous," Freya told her, arranging a display of spring titles in the front window. "I'm choosing."

Petra snorted. "That's what nervous people say."

"That's what *choosing* people say. Nervous people say 'fine' and change the subject."

"Mm." Petra handed her a cinnamon bun across the counter, unasked. "For the dock tomorrow. You'll want your hands busy so they don't shake."

"Thank you for your confidence."

"Anytime, dear."

* * *

Thursday dawned clear and cold, the kind of morning where the light came off the water so bright it looked manufactured. Freya walked down to the dock at half past eight with Ingrid beside her, the cinnamon bun warm in her coat pocket, and found half the town already there under the pretense of other business. Old Halvorsen was inspecting a mooring cleat that had been fine for thirty years. Two fishermen were having an extremely animated discussion about bait. Mrs. Okafor stood at the end of the pier with binoculars she absolutely did not need for a ferry that would be visible from space.

"You could just wave him in yourself," Freya said, passing her. "Save the ferry the trip."

Mrs. Okafor lowered the binoculars with dignity. "I am birdwatching."

"It's April. The birds are still in Portugal."

"Then I am watching for their return. It's called hope, Freya." And she raised the binoculars again, and Freya had to walk away quickly because she was laughing and something in her chest was too full to hold laughter and everything else at once.

The ferry appeared at nine-oh-six, right on schedule, a white shape coming around the headland with the last of the ice glittering in its wake. Freya stood at the rail-end of the dock, Ingrid's hand in hers, and felt the old fear rise—the familiar, well-worn fear that said *he will leave again, they always leave, you built a whole life around the leaving, didn't you?*

But that was the thing about ten years and one long winter and a letter read aloud by lamplight: the fear came, and she let it sit beside her like an old acquaintance, and it did not get to drive.

She had spent the winter learning the difference between being safe and being small. She'd told the story at the winter market—standing up in front of the tree lighting, cocoa in hand, and telling two hundred neighbors that Ansel Voss had left to protect her from his father's debts, and that Marius Voss had stayed silent to protect his brother's sacrifice, and that both of them had made the same mistake: deciding for her. She'd watched faces soften and shift. She'd seen Mrs. Okafor dab her eyes. She'd gone home and taken the faded photograph of Ansel at the harbor out from behind the counter and hung it on the wall by the poetry section, where anyone could ask about it, and she answered every question honestly, even the nosy ones. *Especially* the nosy ones.

The bookshop had changed too. There was a new shelf now—local writers, local stories, a little card that read *Every winter has a story. Tell yours.* People came in just to stand near it. Some of them told her things they'd never said aloud. The shop had become what it should have been all along: not a hiding place but a hearth.

The ferry horn sounded, once, low across the water, and the crowd on the dock went quiet in that way crowds do when everyone is pretending not to watch the same thing.

It docked. Lines were thrown. The gangway rattled down.

And then Ansel Voss stepped off the first ferry of spring.

He looked older—that was unavoidable, ten years does what ten years does—but he also looked *lighter*, somehow, as though he'd finally set down luggage he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it wasn't part of his body. He wore the same wool coat from the photograph. He carried one bag. No boxes, no plans, no paperwork. When Marius had put him on the phone in February, Ansel had said, *I'm not bringing a plan this time. Plans are how I broke it. I'm just bringing myself, if that's enough.*

Freya stepped forward.

The crowd pretended very hard to look elsewhere. Mrs. Okafor's binoculars swiveled away with tremendous discipline.

Ansel stopped three feet from her—the same distance, she noticed, as that first night in the snowed-in shop, when three seconds of eye contact had lasted a decade. His breath fogged in the cold air. His hands were at his sides, open.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi," she said.

"I didn't decide anything on the crossing," he said. "I want that on record. I got on the boat and I sat there and I let the boat do the deciding, and now I'm here, and I don't know where I'm sleeping tonight or what I'm doing tomorrow, and I have never in my life been this terrified or this—" He stopped. Swallowed. "You said you'd meet me at the dock on your terms. So I'm asking what your terms are. I'll take whatever they are. Even if they're—"

"Ansel."

"—even if they're just coffee, even if they're just—"

"*Ansel.*"

He stopped.

"My terms are these," Freya said, and her voice carried further than she intended, because the dock was quiet and the water was quiet and some sentences simply refuse to whisper. "One: you tell me things. All the things, even the ugly ones, especially the ugly ones. Two: when you're afraid, you say so instead of disappearing. Three—" she glanced at Ingrid, who was watching with the expression of a judge who had already made up her mind but wanted to see the defendant squirm a little, "—three, you meet my daughter properly, and you understand that she comes first, always, and that her trust is earned in months, not moments."

"Yes," Ansel said. "All of it. Yes."

"Four." Freya reached into her coat pocket and took out the cinnamon bun. "You share breakfast with us, because I walked all the way down here carrying this and my hands were shaking, and Petra was right, and I hate when Petra is right."

A laugh went through the dock—she heard Halvorsen chuckle, heard Mrs. Okafor make a sound that was definitely not birdwatching—and Ansel smiled, and it was the smile from the photograph, the one she'd kept behind the counter for ten years like a candle in a closed room, except now the room was open and the windows were down and the wind was coming through.

"And five," Freya said, and extended her hand—not forgiveness, because forgiveness was a door that swung both ways and took time, but choice, which was a door she opened herself, on purpose, every day if she had to. "Five: you stay. Not because the past demands it. Not because duty says so. Because staying is a thing you choose in the morning, and choose again at noon, and choose again at night, forever, and you never decide alone again."

Ansel looked at her hand. Then at her face. Then he took off his glove, slowly, like a man unwrapping something he'd waited ten years to touch, and he placed his bare hand in hers.

"I choose," he said. "This morning. And I'll choose tomorrow. And the day after. As long as you'll keep asking."

"Oh, I'll keep asking," Freya said. "I'm very persistent. Ask anyone."

Behind them, Ingrid cleared her throat with the delicacy of a foghorn.

"Mom. You're holding hands with the ferry man in front of the entire town."

"I am aware, Ingrid."

"Are you going to introduce us, or do I have to read about myself in his memoir someday?"

Ansel crouched down to her level, unhurried, unafraid—which surprised Freya, until she remembered he'd written her daughter a letter too, short and honest, mailed separately, and Ingrid had read it four times and told no one. "Hi, Ingrid. I'm Ansel. I knew your mom a long time ago, and I was stupid, and I'm hoping—I'm really hoping—you'll let me try to be better."

Ingrid studied him for a long moment. Then she stuck out her hand, businesslike, and shook his once, firmly.

"Okay," she said. "But I want you to know the dog negotiation is non-negotiable."

"What dog negotiation?"

"The one happening over breakfast." She turned and marched up the dock toward the bakery, radiating the confidence of a child who has already won. "Come on. Petra's making a big deal about it. Everyone's coming. Mrs. Okafor brought binoculars."

Ansel straightened up and looked at Freya, and the terror in his face had softened into something else—something like wonder, like a man standing on solid ground after years at sea, not quite trusting it yet.

"She's remarkable," he said.

"She gets it from me," Freya said. "The stubbornness, anyway. The negotiating skills are a mystery."

They walked up the dock together, not touching anymore but close enough that their shoulders brushed, past Halvorsen and his cleat, past the fishermen and their bait, past Mrs. Okafor, who lowered her binoculars at last and said, loudly, to no one in particular:

"The birds are back."

And behind them, out on the open water, the ice was gone—all of it, every plate and floe, broken up and carried out to sea—and the harbor lay blue and moving and ready, holding nothing back, waiting for whatever came next.

Which, as it happened, was breakfast. And after breakfast, everything else.

* * *

Later—much later, after cinnamon buns and introductions and Mrs. Okafor crying openly into her coffee while insisting she had allergies—Freya stood in the doorway of the bookshop and watched Ansel look at the wall by the poetry section, at the photograph of himself at twenty-five hanging in plain sight, labeled in her own handwriting: *Ansel Voss, Harrow Bay. He came back.*

"When did you write that?" he asked quietly.

"This morning," Freya said. "I finished it at four a.m., when the ice went out. I just had faith about the rest."

Ansel turned to her, and outside the window the spring light came off the water, and somewhere down the street Ingrid was loudly renegotiating the terms of a dog with a bemused fisherman, and Freya thought: *this is what it feels like when the story finally belongs to you.*

"Well," Ansel said, taking her hand again, no glove between them this time. "What happens in chapter eleven?"

Freya laughed, and pulled him inside, and locked nothing behind them.
