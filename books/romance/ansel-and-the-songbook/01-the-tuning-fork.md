## 1. The Tuning Fork

The tuning fork lived in the left pocket of Theo Amberdale's apron, and it had lived there for seven years, which was longer than most marriages lasted and considerably more faithfully.

She struck it against the counter's edge at six-fifty every morning — *B natural*, the note her old teacher had called "the honest one" — and held it to her ear while the kettle came to a boil in the back room. The hum traveled through the bones of her skull like a small, well-behaved earthquake. It was the only music she allowed herself before nine o'clock, and she had never once broken that rule, not even on the morning the pipes froze and she had to boil water over a camp stove like a pioneer with a conservatory degree.

This was how you survived, Theo had learned. Not with grand gestures or dramatic reckonings, but with small ceremonies performed so many times they wore grooves into the day, and you rolled along those grooves like a marble in a track, unable to veer off even if you wanted to.

She did not want to. That was the point.

* * *

Amberdale Hollow Music opened at seven sharp, though nobody but the gulls ever knew it. The shop sat on Tidewalk Lane between a bakery that smelled of cardamom at all hours and a bookbinder who was rumored to have once been a spy, though he denied it so blandly that everyone assumed he must be lying about denying it. Theo's window displayed instruments the way other shops displayed jewelry: a cello in the corner that she polished weekly and never played, violins on velvet hooks, a wall of sheet music organized by a system so idiosyncratic that Mrs. Fenwick from the post office had once called it "a love letter written in alphabet soup."

Theo took this as a compliment. She had built the system herself, shelf by shelf, in the first year after the shop opened, when sleep was a rumor and organizing things was the only activity that didn't require her to think. Every piece of music in the shop had been chosen by hand. Nothing arrived by accident.

That was the architecture of it, if anyone had asked — and no one asked, because Theo had arranged her life with the same deliberate invisibility as her filing system. The shop opened early because mornings were safe; the town was still asleep and the only witnesses were gulls. The cello in the corner stayed in its case-shaped silence because an instrument on display was furniture, and furniture made no demands. And the shelves — the shelves were complete. Every concerto, every sonata, every etude and folk arrangement and beginner method book a coastal town could reasonably want.

Every piece except one.

There was a gap in the Romantic section, between Brahms and Bruch, where a spine should have stood and didn't. The gap was exactly the width of one concerto. Theo dusted around it every Tuesday with the tenderness of a woman tending a grave, and if you had asked her about it, she would have said the distributor was unreliable, and you would have believed her, because she said it beautifully.

Seven years of beautiful saying. The walls held. They always held.

Until the morning they didn't.

* * *

It began, as catastrophes do, with weather so lovely it felt like a setup. October light came through the window in long gold bars and lay across the floorboards like something spilled. Theo had sold a recorder to a child named Poppy (with a parental lecture on practice schedules that Poppy endured with the grim patience of someone being told vegetables exist), re-strung a guitar for the fishmonger's son, and argued cheerfully with the bookbinder-spy through the shared wall about whether Debussy counted as impressionist or merely French.

At eleven-forty, the bell above the door rang, and a stranger walked in.

He was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, wearing a coat too fine for Amberdale Hollow and an expression of pleasant purpose. Tourists came through occasionally in autumn — leaf-peepers, mostly, people who thought a coastal town in October would be quaint and were routinely correct — and Theo had a whole routine for them: smile, recommend the bakery, sell them a harmonica as a souvenir. Harmonicas were her tourist trap. Nobody wept over a harmonica.

"Good morning," the man said. "I'm looking for something rather specific."

"We do our best." Theo set down her pricing pencil. "What can I find for you?"

"It's a concerto." He was already drifting toward the classical wall with the confidence of a man who knew his way around one. "For cello and orchestra. I heard it years ago — a performance I've never forgotten — and I've been trying to find a recording of it ever since. My wife says I'm sentimental. She's right."

Theo's hands went still on the counter.

"Which composer?" she asked, and her voice came out level, which she counted as a victory.

The man named it.

The name landed in the shop like a stone dropped into still water. Theo watched the ripples spread: across the counter, up the shelves, into the gap between Brahms and Bruch where the absence lived, the gap she had maintained with such care for seven years, the negative space that was less a hole in her inventory than a hole in her chest with inventory-shaped edges.

"I'm sorry," she said. "We don't stock that one."

"Oh." He blinked. "Really? It's quite famous."

"It is," Theo agreed. "Famously unavailable."

She said it lightly. She had practiced lightness the way other musicians practiced scales — daily, obsessively, until it required no thought at all. But the man was already frowning at the shelves, and she saw him doing arithmetic: the Dvořák here, the Elgar there, the Shostakovich wedged beside the Saint-Saëns like a guest who'd arrived drunk. A shop this thorough. A gap this precise.

"That's odd," he said mildly. "You seem to have everything else."

"The distributor," Theo said, "is unreliable."

And then — because the universe has a sense of humor, and hers had always been cruel — the man turned back toward her with the brightening face of someone who has just remembered a delightful fact. "You know, that performance I mentioned? It was extraordinary. A young soloist — this was maybe seven, eight years ago now. She was going to be somebody, everyone said so. And then—" He paused, and his delight dimmed into something gentler, sadder. "Well. You may have read about it. It ended badly. Mid-phrase, actually. She just— stopped. Right there on stage. I've never seen anything like it. I think about it more than I should."

The kettle in the back room, long cold, might as well have been screaming.

Theo became aware of several things simultaneously and with terrible clarity: the gold bars of light on the floorboards; the smell of cardamom through the wall; the cello in the corner, silent, complicit; her own heartbeat, which had picked up tempo without consulting her. Somewhere very far away, the man was still talking — something about how he'd hoped the soloist had found peace somewhere, somewhere quiet, somewhere she could be ordinary.

Somewhere, presumably, like a music shop on Tidewalk Lane, selling harmonicas to tourists.

"We don't stock it," Theo said again, and heard her own voice as if from underwater. "But the bakery next door does excellent cardamom buns, if you're hungry."

The man looked at her for a moment — a long moment, in which something flickered behind his eyes, some instinct arriving too late at the wrong conclusion or possibly the right one — and then, mercifully, he smiled and bought two harmonicas instead.

"For my granddaughters," he said at the door. "They'll drive their parents mad. That's what grandfathers are for."

The bell rang. The door closed. Theo stood in the gold light with her heart going like a metronome set to allegro and did not move for a full minute.

Then she walked to the classical wall, stood before the gap between Brahms and Bruch, and pressed her palm flat against the empty shelf, the way you'd press a hand to a wound to keep everything in.

The walls held. They always held.

But God, they were thin.

* * *

She closed for lunch, which she never did, and walked out to the seawall with a bun she didn't taste.

The harbor was doing its usual midday performance: fishing boats knocking gently against their moorings, gulls conducting arguments overhead, old Mr. Calloway repainting the same blue bench he'd repainted every October since Theo moved to town, on the theory that a bench ought to look its best before winter. He raised his brush to her in salute. She raised her bun. This was, more or less, the entire social contract of Amberdale Hollow, and Theo loved it the way you love a blanket — for its warmth, and for the way it keeps the rest of the world at a polite distance.

Except the world, apparently, had stopped keeping its distance.

*Mid-phrase,* the man had said. *She just stopped.*

Seven years, and strangers still carried the story around like a souvenir. She had imagined — she had *needed* to imagine — that the world had forgotten, that the news cycle had done what news cycles do and moved on to fresher disasters. But forgetting wasn't the same as the story dying. It just meant the story traveled without her name attached, a ghost in the repertoire, told in shops and concert halls by people who'd never known her. *A young soloist. She was going to be somebody.*

She had been going to be somebody. That was the unbearable part, looking back. Not the collapse itself, awful as it had been — the lights, the silence, the conductor's face turning toward her like a lighthouse beam, the single cough in the third row that had sounded, in that dead air, like a gunshot. No. The unbearable part was that she'd had a future, a real one, with a shape to it, and she had watched it walk off stage without her.

So she had come here. To a town small enough to hold her and uninterested enough not to stare. She had opened a shop and built her grooves and struck her tuning fork at six-fifty every morning, and for seven years the system had worked, because the system was designed by a woman who understood avoidance the way engineers understand bridges: completely, lovingly, with constant maintenance.

And now a stranger had walked in off the street and put his finger directly on the load-bearing wall.

"You're scowling at the water," observed Mr. Calloway, without pausing his brushwork. "The water hasn't done anything to you."

"It's smug," Theo said.

"Aye, it does have that look." He studied her a moment over the top of his paint tin. He was one of perhaps three people in town who had been here long enough to remember why she never played the cello in the corner, and he was kind enough to never mention it, which in Amberdale Hollow was the highest form of intimacy. "Council meeting last night ran late," he offered, conversationally. "Big doings for the centennial. They've hired someone from away to run a music project. Songbook, they're calling it. Whole town contributing songs, letters, that sort of thing. Very ambitious for a town that can't agree on bench paint."

"Mm," said Theo, whose interest in civic musical projects ranked somewhere below her interest in wet socks.

"Fellow's a conductor, they say. Real one. Big career, cities, orchestras." Mr. Calloway dipped his brush. "Name of Voss, I heard. Julian Voss."

The bun in Theo's hand went very still, the way prey goes still.

She knew the name. Of course she knew the name. There were perhaps five names on earth that could stop her heart mid-beat, and that was one of them — printed on programs she had burned, spoken in reviews she had forbidden herself to read, attached to the memory of a baton rising in the wings of the worst night of her life. The conductor. *Her* conductor, once, in another life, before the night the music stopped and they had both fallen off the edge of the world in different directions.

"He'll need supplies, I expect," Mr. Calloway went on, blissfully unaware that he was dismantling seven years of careful engineering with a paintbrush and a bit of gossip. "Music stands, paper, whatever it is conductors need. You'll get his trade, I shouldn't wonder. Shop being the only one for forty miles."

"Yes," Theo said. Her voice sounded fine. Voices were reliable that way; they held even when nothing else did. "I expect I will."

She finished her bun without tasting it, thanked Mr. Calloway for the weather report, and walked back down Tidewalk Lane with the wind coming off the harbor and the town humming quietly around her — cardamom and paint and gull-cries, the ordinary music of a place that had saved her life without knowing it.

In the shop, the gold light had shifted. The gap between Brahms and Bruch caught the afternoon sun and threw it back at her, bright and accusing.

Theo struck her tuning fork against the counter — off schedule, off ritual, a crack in the groove — and held it to her ear. *B natural.* The honest note.

"Honest," she said aloud, to the empty shop, and laughed at the absurdity of it.

The bell above the door stayed quiet. The afternoon stretched ahead of her, orderly and safe, shelves in their places, the cello in its corner keeping its eloquent silence. Everything exactly where she had put it.

Which was, she was beginning to suspect with the dread of a woman watching a hairline crack spread across a foundation, exactly the problem.

Because tomorrow — or the day after, or whenever conductors from away got around to needing rosin and music paper — the bell above her door was going to ring again. And this time, she suspected, the stranger walking in would not leave with two harmonicas and a recommendation for cardamom buns.

This time, she had the terrible feeling, the past was coming in to browse.
