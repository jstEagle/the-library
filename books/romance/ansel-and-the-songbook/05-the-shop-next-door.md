## 5. The Shop Next Door

The trouble began, as most trouble in Amberdale Hollow did, with Mrs. Fenwick's clipboard.

"Theo, love, the songbook committee needs manuscript paper. Reams of it. And string sets — we've got three fiddlers and a man who swears he plays the viola but nobody's ever actually *heard* him." She peered over her spectacles at the shop around her, taking in the narrow aisles, the wall of sheet music sorted by a system only Theo understood, the cello case that lived behind the counter like a sleeping dog nobody mentioned. "And Mr. Voss wants to come by himself to look at what you've got. He said something about 'assessing the inventory.'"

"Assessing," Theo repeated flatly.

"He's very thorough," Mrs. Fenwick said, in the tone of a woman who found thoroughness charming.

Theo found it insufferable, though she didn't yet have proof. The proof arrived on Thursday morning, ten minutes after she'd flipped the sign to OPEN, in the form of a tall man in a wool coat that cost more than her monthly rent, standing in her doorway with the expression of someone about to conduct an inspection rather than browse a shop.

"You must be Theo," he said. "Julian Voss."

"I know who you are. The whole town knows who you are. Mrs. Pemberton told me your résumé over scones, unprompted, twice."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a wince, but a neighbor of one. "Then I'll skip the part where I tell you I've conducted in Vienna."

"That would be a mercy."

He smiled. It was a good smile, which annoyed her immediately. Good smiles were a kind of currency, and she'd stopped carrying cash years ago.

"I'm told you have the best selection of manuscript supplies between here and the city," he said, moving down the aisle with his hands clasped behind his back like a general surveying terrain.

"I'm told a lot of things. Half of them are about my scones."

He stopped at the shelf of string instruments' accessories and picked up a rosin cake, turning it over with the absent expertise of a man who'd spent his life around musicians. "Do you play?"

"No."

The lie came out so smoothly it surprised even her. He glanced at the cello case behind the counter — because of course he did, because the universe had apparently decided to make this week difficult — and then, mercifully, back at her.

"Well," he said. "I'll take two dozen of these manuscript books. And whatever Mrs. Fenwick ordered."

* * *

He came back the next day. And the day after that.

By the following Tuesday, Theo had developed a theory: Julian Voss was incapable of buying anything in under forty minutes. He would come in for a single item — a metronome battery, a pencil sharpener shaped like a treble clef ("for the children's choir," he'd explained, as if that made it less ridiculous) — and somehow emerge forty minutes later having rearranged her entire understanding of the Sibelius shelving section.

"It's wrong," he announced on Tuesday, standing before the Sibelius with the gravity of a man announcing a war.

"What's wrong?"

"The organization. You've got the symphonies together, which is fine, fine, but then you've filed the *Kullervo* under 'Choral Works,' which is defensible, I suppose, but then—" he pulled out a score and held it up like evidence at trial— "*Finlandia* is filed under 'Patriotic Music.' Which is a category that shouldn't exist. *Finlandia* is a tone poem. It belongs with the other tone poems."

"*Finlandia* is filed under Patriotic Music because old Mr. Aldous used to play it every Fourth of July until the town council asked him to stop, and by then the filing was traditional," Theo said. "Tradition is a valid organizational principle."

"Tradition is how people end up unable to find anything."

"People find things fine. People find things *beautifully*. Do you know how many people have come into this shop looking for *Finlandia* and found it exactly where they expected?"

"And how many came in looking for the tone poems and left confused?"

"They weren't confused. They were *enriched*. There's a difference."

He put the score back — in the wrong place, she noted, with the deliberate care of a man making a point — and turned to face her fully. Up close, in the gray light through the front window, there was something tired around his eyes that hadn't been there during their first meeting. Something that looked almost like grief wearing a well-tailored coat.

"You really do run this place like a fortress," he said.

"I run this place like a *shop*."

"A shop with a drawbridge."

"Every good shop has a drawbridge. Keeps out the barbarians."

"And which am I?"

She looked him up and down with theatrical deliberation. "Hun."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, and for a second the tiredness around his eyes vanished entirely and he looked, absurdly, young. Then he seemed to catch himself, and the laugh folded away into something more careful, and he paid for his battery and left.

Theo stood in the aisle for a long moment, listening to the bell above the door settle, feeling inexplicably like she'd just missed a step going downstairs.

* * *

That night, she wrote to her pen pal.

Dear friend,

I have discovered a new species of human. It frequents music shops, purchases single items, and reorganizes shelves by opinion. It is called a conductor, and I believe there may be only one in captivity, recently relocated to our town, where it has begun marking its territory with pencil annotations and unsolicited commentary on Sibelius.

Today it informed me that my filing system is "defensible" — which I have learned, from context, is what condescending people say when they mean "wrong but I'm being polite about it." It also laughed at my joke, which was worse, because now I can't decide whether to be furious or flattered, and I resent being made to choose.

In other news: I told you once that I don't like being heard. What I didn't say is that I mind it less when it's in writing. When you read these words, you're hearing me, I suppose, but it doesn't feel like it. It feels more like... leaving notes in bottles. Someone finds them or they don't. The ocean decides. There's no face to watch while they're reading, no silence afterward to fill.

There was a moment today, in the shop, when the conductor laughed — really laughed — and I thought: oh no. Not *oh no* like danger. Oh no like a floorboard giving way. Like something I thought was solid turning out to be a door.

I don't like doors. Doors lead places.

Tell me something true about yourself. Anything. I feel off-balance today and I'd like to stand on something solid, even if it's just your handwriting.

Yours,
T.

* * *

His reply arrived two days later, and Theo read it three times before locking the shop, walking home along the seawall, and reading it twice more against the wind.

Dear T.,

Here is something true: I reorganize things when I'm nervous. Shelves, scores, sentences. If you ever see me straightening objects that are already straight, you may assume I'm terrified. This happens more often than I'd like to admit.

Your conductor sounds dreadful. Though I notice you remembered exactly what he said, word for word, including the pause before "defensible." We tend to memorize the people who irritate us. It's a peculiar form of attention. I'd examine that, if I were you. (I'm not you. Examine nothing. But I'd examine that.)

As for doors: some doors lead places worth going. The trick isn't avoiding them. The trick is finding out which ones open onto the sea and which ones open onto traffic.

I knew a cellist once — this is the true thing you asked for — who could make an instrument sound like weather. Not like music *about* weather. Like weather itself. Fronts moving in. I haven't heard playing like it since, and I've heard a great deal of playing. Sometimes I think about that sound the way other people think about home.

Write soon. The bottles are arriving safely.

Yours,
J.

Theo read the paragraph about the cellist four times. Something about it snagged in her chest like a fishhook — the specificity of it, the ache underneath the casualness. Whoever J. was, he wasn't writing about an abstract cellist. He was writing about a person he'd lost, or failed, or both.

She should have felt the warning prickle. She was too busy feeling seen.

* * *

Friday brought rain and Julian Voss, in that order, and by noon the shop smelled of wet wool and pencil shavings and Theo was beginning to suspect she was being haunted.

"Do you have any more of those erasers?" he asked, dripping slightly. "The white ones? The choir children have eaten them."

"Eaten them."

"Marjorie's boy claims they taste like vanilla. I have chosen not to investigate this claim."

"They're not food. They're barely stationery." She fetched the box from under the counter anyway, and he leaned against the doorframe to shake out his umbrella, and she noticed — involuntarily, unforgivably — that he had a small scar through one eyebrow and that when he was damp and unguarded he looked less like a conductor and more like a man who'd been rained on, which was somehow worse.

"Mrs. Fenwick says you've been in the songbook recordings," he said. "Playing."

"I'm helping. There's a difference."

"There is?"

"One holds a cello. The other makes decisions."

"Ah." He nodded gravely. "And which are you?"

"Currently? A hostage."

He smiled at that, and then, because he was constitutionally incapable of leaving well enough alone, he drifted toward the Sibelius shelf.

"Don't," Theo warned.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're thinking about doing something. I can hear it. It sounds like an argument you're winning in your head."

He paused, hand halfway to *Finlandia*, and had the grace to look caught. "It's just — if you alphabetized within the category—"

"If I alphabetized within the category, Mr. Aldous's ghost would haunt this shop, and he was a difficult man alive. I'm not risking it."

"Superstition."

"Tradition."

"Drawbridge behavior."

"Hun behavior," she corrected, and he laughed again, that startled real laugh, and she turned away to straighten a stack of manuscript books that did not need straightening, because her face was doing something she didn't authorize.

* * *

That evening's letter took her an hour, mostly because she kept crossing things out.

Dear friend,

The conductor was back. Today's offenses include: suggesting alphabetical reform (rejected), admitting the choir children eat erasers (concerning), and laughing at me twice (unforgivable). Twice! Once is accident. Twice is campaign.

Here's the thing I keep circling and can't land on: he's not what I expected. I expected — I don't know. Polish. Distance. The particular coldness of men who've stood in front of orchestras their whole lives and learned to treat people as sections. And instead he shakes out his umbrella like he's apologizing to it, and he knows the choir kids by name, and when he laughs it looks like it surprises him, like laughter is a language he used to speak fluently and has gotten rusty in.

I don't trust it. I want to be clear about that. Charm is a technique; I've been charmed before and it cost me seven years. But there's a version of me — a stupid, hopeful version — that keeps noticing things about him. That he's tired in a way that isn't about sleep. That he watches my hands when he thinks I won't see. That when I said I don't play, he looked at the cello case and then away, and the look on his face wasn't curiosity. It was something gentler and sadder, like he recognized a locked door because he lives behind one too.

Anyway. Tell me I'm imagining it. Or better, tell me something about yourself that's inconvenient. Everyone has one inconvenient truth. Mine is that I liked his laugh and I'm still angry about it.

Yours,
T.

* * *

She posted it Monday morning. His reply came Wednesday, and she saved it for her lunch break, sitting on a crate behind the shop with the sea doing its gray business beyond the seawall.

Dear T.,

An inconvenient truth, as requested: I am a coward in precisely one arena. I can stand in front of two hundred musicians and tell a world-class soloist she played the phrase wrong. I can fire a timpanist mid-rehearsal. I can deliver eulogies. But there is one conversation I have rehearsed for seven years and never once managed to begin. Every draft of it dies in my throat. So instead I write letters to a stranger in a coastal town, telling the truth sideways, hoping practice makes possible.

You asked if you're imagining it. I think you're noticing accurately and interpreting cautiously, which is the correct order of operations. Tiredness that isn't about sleep usually has a name. So does watching someone's hands.

As for the laugh: anger and liking are not enemies. They're neighbors. Sometimes they share a fence and pretend not to wave.

Be patient with yourself. Even fortresses have gardens.

Yours,
J.

Theo sat on the crate for a long time after finishing it, the letter in her lap, the wind pulling at her hair, thinking about fences and neighbors and the strange, specific loneliness of a man who rehearsed conversations for seven years without starting them.

She thought: whoever you are, J., somebody hurt you badly, or you hurt somebody badly, and either way you've been carrying it alone in a coat that fits too well.

She thought: I know that coat.

And then she thought, with a small, traitorous warmth she refused to examine: I wonder if the insufferable conductor knows how lucky this town is, getting him.

She went back inside, flipped the sign to OPEN, and did not reorganize anything.

Behind the counter, the cello case sat in its corner, silent as always. But that afternoon, passing it with an armful of string orders, Theo caught herself pausing beside it — just for a second, just long enough to rest her fingertips on the handle — before catching herself and hurrying on, ears burning, as if the case might tell.

Outside, the bell above the door chimed, and Julian Voss stepped in from the rain, shaking out his umbrella like an apology, and asked — with the air of a man embarking on a campaign he'd already lost — whether she'd reconsidered the alphabetical question.

Theo reached for the nearest thing to throw.
