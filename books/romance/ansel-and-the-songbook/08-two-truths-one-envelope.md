## 8. Two Truths, One Envelope

Theo's letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Theo would later decide was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day she restocked the rosin and nothing else of consequence ever happened.

*Dear Friend,* it began, in handwriting that slanted leftward like wind-blown grass. *You asked last week what I'm afraid of, and I've been sitting with the question like a stone in my pocket. Here is the honest answer: I am afraid of being heard.*

She read it standing behind the counter, the shop bell still swinging from the postman's exit, morning light laying itself across the sheet music bins in long gold stripes.

*I run a small business where I am surrounded by instruments all day, and I have not played mine — properly played it, the way you play something when someone might actually listen — in years. Isn't that absurd? A person can build an entire life out of almosts. Almost playing. Almost answering. Almost letting someone through the door of the actual room where they live.*

*When I read your letter about the night you ruined someone, I wanted to write back immediately: whoever they were, I suspect they'd forgive you if you let them. But then I thought, how dare I? I don't know what you did. Maybe forgiveness isn't mine to hand out by post to a stranger. So instead I'll tell you this: I think the worst thing we do to ourselves is decide, on other people's behalf, what they can bear.*

*My hands are fine, before you ask. It's not the hands. It never was.*

Theo set the letter down on the counter with exaggerated care, as though it might go off.

The trouble was — and she had been circling this trouble for two weeks now, the way you circle a bruise to check whether it still hurts — the trouble was that she had begun to look forward to these letters the way other people looked forward to sunrise. She woke knowing one might come. She saved them for the quiet hour after closing, when the shop smelled of wood polish and the light went amber, and she read them twice, three times, running her thumb along the creases as though the paper itself were company.

And the further trouble was that her correspondent was becoming, in the complete absence of a face or a name or even a reliably guessed gender, someone she was — there was no dignified way around it — falling for. Not romantically, she told herself. Not exactly. It was more like falling for a room. A warm room with good light, where you could say the true thing and no one flinched.

*It's not the hands. It never was.* She hadn't meant to write that. Her pen had simply gone ahead of her, the way pens do when the guard at the gate has fallen asleep. Now it sat there on the page, already posted, already delivered, already read by a stranger who knew nothing about her except that she owned a shop and was afraid of being heard.

Except — and here was the thing she could not stop turning over — the stranger had written back: *I don't think it's ever the hands.*

Which was either a coincidence or a miracle or a problem.

* * *

Julian read her letter four times, then folded it along its existing creases so precisely that the fold disappeared entirely, and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, like a man carrying contraband.

He was doing this more often. Carrying her letters around. He had taken to reading them in the green room before rehearsals, which was dangerous, because twice now he had smiled at nothing during a tempo discussion and Mrs. Ashcombe, the second violin, had given him a look usually reserved for men who talked to themselves at bus stops.

*I am afraid of being heard.*

He knew that sentence. He had heard its twin spoken aloud seven years ago, in a green room not unlike this one, by a cellist flexing her fingers and saying, too lightly, *If I get through the slow movement, Voss, buy me a drink worth drinking.* He had said something reassuring and hollow. He had been twenty-nine and certain that confidence was contagious.

Then the cue had slipped. His cue. The downbeat that never came, or came late, or came wrong — he had replayed it ten thousand times and it changed shape every time — and the orchestra had lurched, and she had lurched with it, reaching for a phrase that had moved, and the sound she made was not music. And then she was down. On the stage. And the hall made a noise he still heard sometimes when the radiators ticked at night.

He had never told anyone the whole of it. He had told his pen pal most of it, in careful anonymized pieces, the way you hand someone shards wrapped in cloth. And his pen pal — this dry, funny, wounded stranger who signed off only as *T* — had written back: *whoever they were, I suspect they'd forgive you if you let them.*

Julian sat with that for a long time.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not make come out even: the woman he was falling for on paper and the woman he was failing to be civil to in person occupied the same town, possibly the same street, and some traitorous corner of him kept suggesting they might occupy the same skin.

It was absurd. He knew it was absurd. T wrote about a music shop — *the owner of our local shop is a tyrant about shelving; she alphabetizes composers by era and glares at anyone who suggests genre sections* — and Julian had laughed aloud, alone in his rented rooms above the bakery, because he had personally been glared at over shelving on Thursday. But half the towns in England had a music shop, and half the music shops had a proprietor with opinions, and the probability that his pen pal and the cellist-turned-shopkeeper with the guarded eyes and the hands that shook when she thought no one watched —

Well. It wasn't impossible. That was the trouble. It was not impossible, and hope, being what it was, had already moved into the possibility and started arranging furniture.

He took out his pen.

*T — You've caught me in a strange mood, so forgive what follows. Today a woman in this town argued with me about Sibelius for forty minutes and won, mostly by refusing to concede anything, and I found myself thinking afterward not *what an insufferable person* but *I wish I'd argued longer.* Do you see the disease I have? I've started collecting disagreements like flowers. I think it's because arguing is a way of paying attention, and paying attention is the closest thing to tenderness I currently trust myself to offer anyone.*

*You wrote that it's not the hands. I believe you. In my experience it's almost never the part of us that failed — it's the story we wrote about the failure afterward, the one we recite until we can't hear anything else. I know a great deal about reciting.*

*Here is my confession for the week, since we seem to have established a tradition: I have begun to dread Thursdays, because Thursdays are the day I see someone I argue with, and I have begun to dread the days between Thursdays, because those are the days I don't. If you have a cure for this, send it by return post. If you have the same disease, don't tell me. Some diagnoses are kinder unread.*

He signed it *J*, sealed it, and addressed it to the post office box Mrs. Fenwick had assigned, and only after he'd dropped it through the slot did he realize he had essentially confessed to having feelings for a woman whose name he didn't know, to a different woman whose name he also didn't know, and that both confessions might — the thought arrived like cold water — land in the same pair of hands.

* * *

Mrs. Fenwick ran the post office counter the way Nelson ran the fleet: with total commitment, failing eyesight, and an unshakeable belief in Providence.

On Friday morning, Theo came in for her letters and found Mrs. Fenwick holding two envelopes aloft, squinting at them with the expression of a woman defusing a bomb.

"Now then," said Mrs. Fenwick. "Now then. Which of you is which."

"Sorry?"

"The songbook pairs." Mrs. Fenwick tapped the envelopes together, a little percussion section of impending doom. "I've got a J going to a T and a T going to a J, but I'll be honest with you, dear, my spectacles are at the optician's and I've been sorting by feel." She peered up. "You're the T, aren't you. Or are you the J. You've got rather a neutral face, if you don't mind my saying."

"I mind slightly," said Theo, "but I'm the T."

"Are you though." Mrs. Fenwick held the envelope closer to her nose, then farther away, then at arm's length like a fisherman judging a catch. "Only there was a mix-up in October with the church newsletters, and Doris Pym received the flower rota for six weeks and nobody had the heart to tell her, and honestly the geraniums have never been better, so one does wonder whether mistakes aren't sometimes—"

"Mrs. Fenwick."

"Yes, dear?"

"Is that my letter?"

Mrs. Fenwick looked at the envelope in her left hand. Then the right. Then, catastrophically, she began to turn the left-hand envelope over, and Theo saw — with the particular horror available only to people watching their secret approach daylight at walking pace — the beginning of her own handwriting on the flap.

"That one's mine," said a voice from the doorway, smooth as varnish, and Julian Voss stepped into the post office, shaking rain off his coat. "The left one. I'd know that slant anywhere; I've spent a month being insulted by it."

"You have not been insulted," said Theo, before she could stop herself, "you've been *corresponded with*," and then heard herself, and wished very much for the floor to open.

Julian looked at her. Something flickered across his face — she couldn't name it, and it frightened her slightly, how much she wanted to name it.

"Fenwick," he said easily, plucking the envelope from her hand, "you're a menace with other people's post. One day the geraniums will be the least of it." He dropped the letter in his pocket without looking at it again, which was either innocence or the finest acting Theo had ever witnessed, and nodded to her. "Thursday," he said. "Sibelius. Bring your concessions."

"Bring mine?" Theo said. "*You'll* need a wheelbarrow."

He grinned — actually grinned, and the grin did something structurally unsound to her afternoon — and left.

Mrs. Fenwick watched the door swing shut, then turned to Theo with the serene smile of a woman who suspected everything and understood nothing.

"Lovely man," she said. "Terrible with shelving, I hear."

* * *

That night Theo sat in the amber light after closing with her newest letter — collected Saturday, mercifully unexamined — and read it twice, and then sat very still.

*I have begun to dread Thursdays... and the days between Thursdays, because those are the days I don't.*

She read it a third time. Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

Because she knew that disease. She had diagnosed it in herself on Tuesday, standing in her own shop, watching Julian Voss leave through her door and catching herself smiling at the space where he'd been. She had spent the evening furious about it — furious at him for being funny under his insufferability, furious at herself for noticing, furious at the whole rotten architecture of attraction that paid no attention whatsoever to the fact that she was, in whatever way mattered, already spoken for. Already writing, three times a week, to someone who understood her better than anyone alive.

Two people. Two impossible people. One of them safe behind an envelope, one of them dangerous in a doorway, and both of them, apparently, capable of making her dread the days between.

She took out her pen.

*J — You asked for a cure. There isn't one. I have the same disease, terminal case, and the only treatment I know is honesty, so here is mine: there is someone. Someone infuriating, whom I argue with, whom I have recently begun to miss in the gaps between arguments, and I cannot decide whether this is a betrayal of you or simply proof that the heart is a badly managed institution with no respect for existing correspondence. Forgive me. Or don't. Tell me instead: when you ruined that person, all those years ago — did you love them? Is that why the guilt has teeth?*

*Answer carefully. I find I care about your answer more than is strictly wise.*

She sealed it. Addressed it. And lay awake half the night composing Julian's reply in her head, and hating herself, and composing it anyway.

Three streets away, Julian stood at his window above the bakery with her latest letter in his hand — *the owner of our local shop is a tyrant about shelving* — and read the phrase *someone infuriating, whom I argue with* for the sixth time, and felt the two halves of his life lean toward each other like magnets, close enough now that he could feel the pull.

One envelope. One point where everything converged.

Neither of them slept well. Both of them checked the clock.
