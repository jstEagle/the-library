## 11. Almost Told You

### Theo

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day the shop received its sheet music order and therefore the day Theo was already braced for small catastrophes.

She read it twice behind the counter, one eye on the door in case Mrs. Fenwick came shuffling in with more of her "helpful suggestions" about the songbook display. The second reading made her ears go warm.

*There's something I want to tell you,* her correspondent had written, in that careful, slanted hand she'd come to know better than her own grocery list. *At the unveiling on Saturday. I think it's time I stopped hiding behind this paper. I'll be there — I'm always there, apparently; you've probably seen me and never known. If you'd rather I didn't say anything, tear up this letter and we'll go on as we were. But if you don't tear it up, look for me near the stage. I'll be the one rehearsing a confession.*

Theo set the letter down on the counter with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a live thing.

He wanted to meet. Her anonymous pen pal — the stranger to whom she had confessed things she hadn't said aloud to her own reflection, the person who knew about the shaking hands and the silence and the fear of being *heard*, who had answered all of it without flinching — wanted to stand in front of her at the unveiling and become a face.

Her first feeling was terror, clean and simple.

Her second feeling, arriving half a beat later like an unwanted harmony, was that she already knew who it was.

Which was absurd. Impossible. She had no evidence. She had a suspicion assembled entirely out of handwriting analysis conducted over coffee at six in the morning, and the fact that Julian Voss had once used the phrase *"the architecture of a phrase"* in conversation exactly as her pen pal had in his third letter, and the fact that when he argued with her about Sibelius his eyes did something her pen pal's words did — went soft around the edges even while the argument stayed sharp.

And the fact that she had told her pen pal about the insufferable conductor next door, and the insufferable conductor had looked faintly strangled the following week when she'd mentioned the letter.

"You could just ask him," said Priya from the repair bench, not looking up from the violin whose bridge she was carving. She had an unnerving habit of narrating Theo's silences.

"Ask him what?"

"Whatever you're currently not asking him."

"I'm not asking him anything. I'm doing inventory." Theo picked up a clipboard to prove it. The clipboard had nothing on it. She put it down again before it could betray her further.

Priya blew wood dust off the bridge. "You've been doing inventory for ten minutes and the inventory is his shoulders."

"My inventory is *shelving*—"

"And yet here we are." Priya finally looked up, and her expression was the particular gentle mercilessness of someone who had watched Theo talk herself out of every good thing for seven years. "Saturday. He's going to be standing there waiting to be known by you. What are you going to do?"

What she was going to do, Theo decided that night, sitting cross-legged on her bed above the shop with every letter spread out around her like tarot cards, was tell him first.

That was the plan. She would write back tonight — *I'll be there too, and I have something to confess as well, and we can be brave at each other simultaneously, which is the only dignified way to do it.* She got as far as *Dear friend* before she stopped, because the word felt suddenly insufficient and also suddenly true in a way that frightened her more than any name would.

She tried again. *Dear—*

The nib hovered. Ink gathered at its tip like a held breath.

Outside, rain started against the window, soft and persistent, the same rhythm as the scales she used to warm up with a lifetime ago. Theo thought: *if I write my name, everything changes.* And then she thought the truer thing underneath it: *everything has already changed. I'm only deciding whether to admit it.*

She wrote: *Dear friend. Yes. Saturday. Near the stage. I'll find you — or rather, I suspect I already have, though I've been too much of a coward to check. There's something I should have told you weeks ago, and I'm going to tell you on Saturday, and if you laugh at me I shall deny ever writing this letter.*

She signed it with her real name.

Then she sat very still for a long time, heart going like a metronome set far too fast, looking at those four letters — T-h-e-o — as though they might detonate.

She sealed the envelope. She addressed it. She put it in her coat pocket to post in the morning.

And then, because the universe is a conductor with a cruel sense of timing, there came a knock at the shop door below — three raps, unmistakable, the knock of a man who had been told a thousand times that the shop closed at five and had never once believed it applied to him.

* * *

### Julian

Julian stood outside Amberdale Music in the rain telling himself, for the fourth consecutive minute, to walk away.

He had a letter in his inside pocket. It had taken him three drafts and most of a bottle of wine to write, and it said, in essence: *I know who you are. I have known for some time, and I have been a coward about it, because the truth is that I ruined your life and you wrote to me anyway, kindly, without knowing, and I couldn't bear for the knowing to stop. But it must stop being a secret. Meet me Saturday. Or don't, and I will leave you in peace, which is the least I owe you and the hardest thing I will ever give.*

The actual letter was better than the summary. He hoped. He had rewritten the middle section eleven times.

The problem was that he couldn't wait until Saturday. This was the flaw in his character that conductors' batons were invented to disguise: he could not abide an unresolved chord. Seven years ago he had resolved one badly, rushing a cue, dragging a soloist into disaster, and he had spent every day since learning that some chords you cannot rush. You have to let them ring until they decide to stop.

But it was raining, and her light was still on above the shop, and he was weak.

He knocked. Three raps. Then he heard footsteps on the stairs, and the door opened, and Theo Montrose stood there in a cardigan with a smudge of ink on her jaw, holding a letter in one hand, and Julian's entire prepared speech evaporated like breath on glass.

"The shop's closed," she said.

"I know. I—" He gestured vaguely at the sky, the street, the general injustice of existence. "I was walking. It's raining. Your light was on."

"That's three separate confessions and none of them explain why you're here."

"No," he agreed. "They really don't."

She looked at him for a moment longer — and there was something strange in her face, something almost startled, as though she'd opened the door expecting someone else and found, instead, the exact right person, which was worse. Then she stepped back. "Well. You're soaked. Come in before you dissolve."

The shop smelled of rosin and old paper and, faintly, of the tea she always burned. Julian stood dripping on the mat while she fetched a towel and threw it at his head with more force than hospitality strictly required. He caught it. Their fingers didn't touch, but the air between their hands did something complicated.

"I posted a letter today," Theo said abruptly.

Julian's stomach performed a maneuver usually reserved for descending aircraft. "Did you."

"To my pen pal." She was watching him with an intensity that made the room feel smaller. "Telling him I'd meet him. At the unveiling. That I'd tell him the truth about who I am."

The rain filled the silence. Julian became aware that his own confession was sitting in his inside pocket, directly over his heart, like a small rectangular tumor.

"What a coincidence," he managed.

"Mm," said Theo. "Isn't it."

* * *

### Theo

Here is what happened, as Theo would later reconstruct it: two people stood in a music shop after closing, each holding a letter that said *I know, do you?*, and neither produced theirs.

It wasn't cowardice, exactly. Or it was, but a refined kind — the cowardice of people who have rehearsed a duet so many times separately that performing it together feels impossible. She kept thinking: *if I hand him the letter and he doesn't understand, if I've built this entire cathedral of hope on handwriting and a fondness for the word "architecture," I will have to move towns.* And he kept looking at her the way he looked at a passage he was afraid of — like something he wanted and dreaded in equal measure.

So instead they did what they always did. They argued.

About shelving, first — he'd moved the songbook display copies to eye level, which was presumptuous, and she moved them back while he watched, which was petty, and both of them enjoyed it enormously. About whether the centennial program should open with the fanfare (his position) or the folk suite (hers). About Sibelius, inevitably, because arguing about Sibelius had become their love language and neither had noticed.

"You cannot honestly believe the Second Symphony is his best work," Theo said, arms crossed. "It's a man shouting until he feels better."

"Yes," Julian said. "That's why it's great."

"That is the *opposite* of why—"

"You shout until you feel better," he said, quieter now. "I've heard you. Through the floor. On Sundays, when you think no one's listening."

Theo stopped. The shop went very still around them, the way rooms do when they're waiting to see what you'll break.

"I don't play," she said.

"No," he agreed softly. "You don't."

And there it was — the thing circling beneath all their arguments for weeks, surfacing at last, blinking in the lamplight. He knew. Not everything — she could see that, could see he didn't know *what* had silenced her, only that something had. But he knew she had a cello up there that she touched on Sundays like a wound she was checking for infection, and he had never once said so, had adjusted tempos instead, had given her silence when she needed silence and presence when she needed presence, and Theo felt her eyes sting and hated him for it and wanted, with a ferocity that alarmed her, to kiss him.

Later she would understand that he was fighting the same impulse. At the time, all she knew was that he stepped closer — close enough that she could see rain still caught in his hair, close enough that the argument about Sibelius died somewhere between them unsung — and said her name.

"Theo."

Not *Ms. Montrose*. Not the careful, armored *Theo* he deployed in rehearsals. A different one. Lower. Like a note he'd been holding back from the score.

"Don't," she whispered.

"All right," he said, and didn't step back.

The lamp buzzed. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere upstairs, the cello leaned in its corner, keeping its counsel.

"We shouldn't," she said, meaning several things at once, none of which she could explain without letters, without Saturday, without the whole paper architecture she'd built between them.

"No," he said. "We shouldn't."

Neither of them moved. His hand rose — slowly, telegraphing every inch, giving her a lifetime to retreat — and tucked a strand of hair behind her ear, and his fingertips grazed her cheek, and Theo's breath left her in a way that had nothing to do with breathing.

They were close enough to touch the secret. Close enough that she could feel it radiating off him like heat off stage lights. One more inch and everything would spill — the letters, the name, the night seven years ago, all of it — and some treacherous part of her leaned in—

The shop telephone rang.

They broke apart like teenagers. Theo grabbed the receiver with a steadiness she absolutely did not feel. "Amberdale Music, we're closed—"

"Theo, dear! It's Edith Fenwick!" boomed Mrs. Fenwick, at a volume suggesting the telephone was a formality. "I know it's late, but I've had the *dreadfullest* thought about the pairing lists, and I simply won't sleep until—"

"I'll see you tomorrow, Mrs. Fenwick," Theo said, and hung up, and turned around.

Julian was at the door, coat on, letter-shaped bulge still in his pocket, expression carefully arranged into something polite and devastated.

"Saturday," he said.

"Saturday," she said.

He hesitated on the threshold, rain waiting beyond him like an orchestra awaiting the downbeat. For one naked moment his composure slipped entirely, and what showed underneath was so raw and hopeful that Theo nearly called him back, nearly said it all right there among the sheet music stands.

Instead he said, "Goodnight, Theo," and walked into the rain, and she stood in the lamplight watching him go, her letter still in her hand, unposted, unconfessed, burning through the envelope.

Upstairs, she took out her pen pal's letter again and read the last line once more. *I'll be the one rehearsing a confession.*

"So will I," she said aloud to the empty shop. "God help us both."

* * *

### Julian

Julian did not sleep.

He sat in his rented room above the bakery with the letter on his knee and the lamp burning, listening to the rain and replaying the evening with the obsessive frame-by-frame scrutiny he usually reserved for rehearsal recordings. *Don't,* she'd said. *All right,* he'd said. Neither of them had meant either word. He had touched her hair. She had leaned toward him — he was almost certain she had leaned toward him; he had replayed that half-second forty times and it survived every review — and then the telephone had fallen on them like a dropped cymbal.

And beneath all of it, the thought that would not resolve: *she posted a letter today. She's meeting her pen pal Saturday. She's going to tell him the truth.*

Which meant that by Saturday night, Theo Montrose would know everything — would know that her gentle, candid, infuriatingly perceptive correspondent was the man who had broken her career, standing three feet away with his confession in his pocket. Would know that every kindness in those letters had come from him. Would know, possibly, that he loved her, because Julian had never once managed to write to her without loving her leaking through the ink.

He picked up his pen one more time. He crossed out nothing. He added a single line at the bottom of the letter, beneath his signature — his real name, written plainly, no alias, no armor:

*I will be near the stage on Saturday, whatever you decide. Some of us have been rehearsing our confessions for seven years, and we are, at last, ready for the performance.*

He sealed it. In the morning, he would post it, and then there would be no taking it back — no more anonymity, no more safe distance, only Saturday and the truth and whatever mercy she chose to extend.

Across town, unknown to him, an identical resolution was sealing itself into an identical envelope, addressed in a hand he would recognize anywhere, if only he had ever been allowed to see it.

Two letters. Two secrets. One postbox, waiting for morning.
