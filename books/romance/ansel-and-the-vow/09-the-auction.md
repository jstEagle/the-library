## 9. The Auction

The courthouse steps smelled like salt and old stone, and Rosalind climbed them with the original will pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

Ansel had delivered it at dawn — not to her door, but to the sanctuary itself, standing in the parking lot in a suit that looked like it had been slept in, holding a manila envelope out at arm's length as if she might bite him. Inside: the true will, dated three years before the forgery. Inside: a recording of Marcus Hale, his mentor, explaining over drinks exactly how he'd altered the signature page, how much the developer client had paid, how easy it had been because *no one reads the back pages of an estate file*. Inside: a letter.

She hadn't read the letter yet. She'd told herself there wasn't time, but that was a lie. The truth was she couldn't read it alone, couldn't read it without hearing his voice, and hearing his voice was still dangerous. Two days ago she'd learned that the man who wrote her the kindest sentences of her life was the same man who'd handed her grandmother's life's work to the shredder. She needed a room full of witnesses between herself and whatever that letter said.

So here they were. Probate court, Room 2B, docketed at nine a.m., auction scheduled for noon across town at the developer's showroom. Judge Okafor had agreed to hear the motion to void the sale on an emergency basis, which meant Rosalind had until nine-fifteen to convince a stranger in robes that everything she'd fought for for four months hinged on a piece of paper Ansel Vow had pulled from his own firm's archives.

He sat two rows behind her. She could feel him the way you feel weather coming — pressure, static, the sense that something enormous was about to break open. He wore no tie. His hands were folded, and they were shaking. She noticed because she'd spent months learning his handwriting, and handwriting is just the body confessing on paper.

"Ms. Nystrom?" The clerk leaned out. "You're up."

* * *

It went faster than she'd expected, which should have frightened her more than it did.

The developer's attorney — a silver-haired man named Pruitt who smelled like money laundering — argued standing up, voice rich with indignation. Fabricated document. Disgruntled heir. Chain of custody impossible to verify. And then, when Rosalind's lawyer played the recording, Pruitt pivoted beautifully to attack the messenger: *Mr. Vow obtained this evidence through improper means; anything derived from it is fruit of a poisoned tree.*

Judge Okafor let him run out of air. Then she put on her glasses.

"Mr. Pruitt, your client's purchase agreement is contingent on clear title. There has been no clear title since the day this firm filed the forged instrument." She tapped the will. "The court finds the 2019 instrument to be a forgery, void ab initio. The property reverts to the trust established by Edith Nystrom, per the original instrument. The auction scheduled for today is cancelled. The sale is voided. And Mr. Pruitt—" she removed her glasses, "—I am referring this matter to the state bar and the district attorney. Your client may want to consult separate counsel."

Pruitt gathered his papers like a man bailing water. Behind him, the developer himself — Calloway, big jaw, bigger watch — stood up so fast his chair skidded. "This is insane. I have contracts. I have *financing*—"

"You have a subpoena," said the bailiff, pleasantly.

Calloway stormed up the aisle, shoulder-checking the door hard enough to rattle the seal of the State of Maine on the wall. Half the gallery turned to watch him go. The other half turned to watch the two men from Vow & Hale rise as the DA's investigator approached them.

Marcus Hale went gray. Rosalind watched it happen — watched sixty years of polish drain out of his face in real time as the investigator read him his rights in a low, courteous voice. He looked, in that moment, like any old man being arrested, confused and small, clutching his coat. For one treacherous second she felt sorry for him. Then she thought of her grandmother's last summer, driving the beach at dawn counting nests that were about to be bulldozed, trusting the lawyers to fix what the lawyers had broken.

Hale's eyes found Ansel across the courtroom. What passed between them wasn't anger. It was worse — it was *betrayal*, the mentor looking at the protégé like a father watching the son choose someone else. Ansel held the look. He didn't flinch, didn't look away, didn't apologize. His hands had stopped shaking.

The investigator led Hale out. The gavel came down. Court adjourned, and the room dissolved into noise — reporters, the coordinator from the pen-pal program (why was *Marguerite* here?), her lawyer shaking her hand, strangers congratulating her for a victory that felt less like winning and more like setting down something impossibly heavy only to discover your arms remember nothing else.

Ansel stood at the back, alone, already half-turned toward the door. Giving her the exit. Of course he was. Even now, even having burned down his entire career, he was leaving the door open so she wouldn't have to walk past him.

"No," Rosalind said, out loud, to no one, and then louder, "Wait."

* * *

The courtroom emptied slowly, the way courtrooms do, reluctant stragglers and a janitor with a mop and Marguerite, who had absolutely refused to leave and stood by the window with her arms crossed like a chaperone at a school dance. Rosalind walked to the front of the room, unfolded the letter Ansel had given her at dawn, and realized her hands were trembling now instead of his.

"I never read this," she said. Her voice carried strangely in the empty room. "I was going to. Last night. I sat with it for two hours and I couldn't open it, because I didn't know if it would be a lawyer's letter or a lover's letter or an apology or an alibi, and I couldn't take another version of you I hadn't asked for."

Ansel had stopped halfway down the aisle. "Rosalind—"

"So I'm reading it here. Where it's safe. Where there are witnesses." A weak laugh. "Marguerite counts as a witness. Probably several."

Marguerite, from the window: "I count as at least six."

Rosalind opened the envelope. Unfolded the page. Recognized the handwriting instantly — she'd known it for weeks before she'd known whose it was, that careful slanted script, the way he crossed his t's like he was signing confessions.

She read aloud.

*"Dear Wren—"*

Her breath caught. Wren. Her name in their letters, the name she'd chosen because wrens sing loud and live small. Hearing it in his actual written words, in this room, made her eyes sting.

*"By the time you read this, you'll know my name, and you'll hate it, and you'll be right to. So let me tell you the truth while I still can, in the only place I've ever been honest.*

*I joined the pen-pal program because my sister said I was turning into my job. I stayed because of you. Every night after I spent the day being the worst person in this town, I got to be someone else in an envelope, and the terrible thing — the thing I can't forgive myself for — is that being someone else with you was the most honest I've felt in ten years. You told me once that you trusted me more than anyone in Gullwing Cove. You were trusting a man who didn't exist. I'm sorry. I'm going to spend whatever's left of my career making sure the man who does exist earns one percent of what you gave a ghost.*

*Your grandmother built something that outlasts her. I helped try to bury it. Today I dug it back up, and it cost me everything except the only thing I care about keeping, which was never mine to keep.*

*If you ever forgive me, don't do it in a letter. Do it on the beach, at low tide, where you do your best thinking and I do my worst lying.*

*Yours — actually, finally, entirely yours —*

*Ansel"*

Silence. The janitor's mop had stopped. Somewhere a clock ticked with judicial patience.

Rosalind lowered the page. Her vision blurred; she let it. Then she walked down the aisle, past the bar rail, past the front pew, until she was standing close enough to see that his eyes were red-rimmed and he was gripping the back of a pew like a man in rough seas.

"That letter," she said quietly, "was almost perfect."

"Almost?"

"You ended it wrong. I don't forgive people on beaches, Ansel. I forgive them at hatcheries, at five a.m., holding a flashlight, when a nest starts boiling and there's too much to do to be eloquent." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the tears, done hiding them. "Be there tomorrow. Five a.m. Nest seventeen's due. Bring coffee and don't talk for the first hour."

His exhale shook. "And after the hour?"

"After the hour," she said, "we start over. Real names. Slowly. Because I meant what I wrote to you — whoever you were — and I intend to find out how much of it survives translation."

Behind them, Marguerite blew her nose thunderously and announced to the janitor, "Six witnesses. Minimum."

Ansel smiled — wrecked, grateful, terrified. "Five a.m. I'll be there."

Rosalind nodded once, then turned to go, because if she stayed one more second she was going to kiss him in a courtroom and she refused to give the State of Maine the satisfaction.

At the doors she paused. "Oh — and Ansel? The sanctuary needs a new co-director. Legal background preferred. References required." She glanced back. "Yours will need some explaining."

* * *

That night she drove out to the sanctuary alone and walked the high-tide line under a fat moon, checking nest seventeen's cage one last time. The sand was cool. The ocean did its ancient work. Tomorrow, at dawn, the sand would boil with ninety small lives running for the water, and she would stand among them with a man learning to be honest, in a place that was hers again.

She crouched, laid her palm flat over the nest, and felt — faintly, unmistakably — the first stirrings beneath.

They were coming early.
