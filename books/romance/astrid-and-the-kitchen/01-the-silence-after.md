## 1. The Silence After

The bus from the airport smelled of wet wool and someone's forgotten lunch, and Petra Underhill was grateful for both, because they were ordinary smells, the smells of a world that had no idea who he was supposed to be.

He had taken the bus on purpose. A man who once flew first class to Vienna, to Seoul, to a birthday gala for a shipping magnate whose name he'd never bothered to learn—that man took taxis with tinted windows and arrived at concert halls through loading docks. That man was dead now, or close enough, and his replacement rode public transit with a duffel bag, a garment bag containing one black suit he hoped never to wear again, and a cello case he had not opened in seven hundred and forty-three days.

Not that he was counting. Counting would imply the number mattered.

Bellhaven announced itself the way small coastal towns do: too suddenly, all at once, a curve in the road and then water, gray-green and patient, and a scatter of rooftops huddled against it like sheep against weather. Petra pressed his forehead to the cold glass and felt something in his chest shift—not grief exactly, more like a door swinging on a rusted hinge. He let it swing shut again. He was very good at that now. Two years of practice.

His aunt Marguerite had owned the bakery on Harbor Street for thirty-one years. He knew this because she'd told him every time he called, which was every Christmas and roughly half the Easters, and because she'd written it into her letters with the pride of a woman describing a child: *the ovens are temperamental but so am I, we understand each other.* He had visited twice in his adult life. Both times he'd stayed three days, played nothing, eaten an alarming quantity of cardamom buns, and left before anyone could ask him to perform at anything.

Now she was gone—eight months gone, though the lawyers had moved at the speed of continental drift—and the bakery was his. The apartment above it was his. The temperamental ovens were his. The whole improbable kingdom of flour and salt sat waiting for him to sign papers, list it with some agent, and vanish back into the large anonymous nowhere where he intended to spend the rest of his life.

That was the plan. Petra liked plans. Plans were load-bearing structures you could lean your whole weight against without falling through.

* * *

The key stuck, which felt right. Everything about the building felt right in its wrongness—the paint peeling in long curls off the window frames, the brass bell over the door that gave a single flat note when he shouldered inside, the smell that hit him like a hand to the sternum: yeast, butter, cinnamon, and underneath it all the faint mineral ghost of the sea. It was the smell of every childhood Christmas he'd spent here, the ones where Aunt Marguerite had pressed dough into his eight-year-old hands and said, *you have good hands, Petra, don't waste them.*

He hadn't wasted them. He'd spent them. There was a difference, and he'd learned it publicly, in front of eleven hundred people.

He climbed the stairs to the apartment, each step announcing him like a witness. The rooms were exactly as she'd left them—a quilt folded at the foot of the bed, a teacup in the drying rack as if she'd only stepped out to buy milk and simply never come back. On the kitchen table, a stack of mail addressed to her in handwriting that grew shakier down the pile. He set his bags down in the middle of the front room, looked at them, and did not unpack.

There was a piano bench under the window. He remembered it holding sheet music once, decades ago. Now it held a folded newspaper, a tin of buttons, and a photograph of himself at fourteen, mid-bow, in a tuxedo that fit him badly, looking simultaneously terrified and lit from within.

Petra turned the photograph face-down with two fingers, the way you'd close a stranger's eyes.

* * *

The first week, he established a routine, because routines were how you built a wall out of hours instead of bricks.

He woke at six without an alarm, a habit from twenty years of conservatory discipline that no amount of ruin could shake loose. He drank coffee standing up, at the counter, looking out the window at the harbor while fishing boats went out in the dark like slow animals following a scent. At eight he walked the length of Harbor Street and back, hands in his pockets, nodding to people who nodded back with the particular friendliness of a town that had already decided he belonged to it.

"You're Marguerite's boy," said the woman at the hardware store, handing him sandpaper he hadn't asked for. "You've got her chin."

"I have my mother's chin," Petra said.

"Your mother got it from Marguerite. Family chins travel." She rang him up and refused to tell him what the sandpaper was for, saying only, "You'll know when you need it," which struck him as either very wise or very annoying, possibly both.

At noon he ate whatever the deli had. At two he met with Mr. Okonkwo, the estate lawyer, whose office smelled of lemon polish and whose patience appeared to be geological in scale. They went through documents. The building. The accounts. A life insurance policy Marguerite had apparently maintained with the quiet stubbornness she applied to everything. Petra signed where indicated. His signature had changed since the last time anyone important needed it—it used to be a flourish, a performance in itself; now it was just letters, tired ones, walking single file.

"The building's worth more than you might expect," Mr. Okonkwo said on the fourth day, tapping a page. "Waterfront-adjacent property. There's been interest."

"Interest?"

"A chef. Local woman. She's been after the place since before Marguerite passed—wanted to buy it outright, turn the downstairs into a restaurant kitchen. Your aunt wouldn't sell. Said the bakery wasn't done being a bakery." He slid the file across the desk. "This Astrid Voss has been persistent. I'd expect her to be in touch."

"Then she'll be disappointed," Petra said. "I'm selling."

Mr. Okonkwo looked at him over his glasses with an expression Petra recognized from conductors, from critics, from his own reflection on bad mornings. The look that said: *I have heard many men say things they did not mean.*

"Of course," the lawyer said mildly. "I'll draw up the listing."

* * *

The cello stayed in its case.

This was, he wanted it understood, a decision, not a wound. There is a difference between a thing you cannot do and a thing you choose not to do, and Petra had spent two years constructing that difference brick by brick, mortaring it with logic. His hands were fine. The doctors had said so—no nerve damage, no injury, nothing structurally wrong at all. What had failed him that night in Prague had been subtler than tendon or bone. Something upstream. Some wire between the knowing and the doing that had simply, in front of eleven hundred people and one very expensive microphone array, stopped carrying current.

His left hand had frozen on the fingerboard during the second movement of the Dvořák, and the silence that followed had lasted four seconds. Four seconds is nothing. Four seconds is also geologic time, when you are the silence. He had heard, in those four seconds, a man in row G cough. He had heard his own heartbeat, absurdly loud, like footsteps in an empty house. And then he had done the unforgivable professional thing, the thing they train you for: he had picked up where he'd left off, flawlessly, and finished the concerto, and the audience had given him a standing ovation because audiences forgive everything except watching a man break.

He had walked offstage, put the cello in its case, and never opened it again.

Seven hundred and fifty days now. Still not counting.

* * *

On the ninth day, the oven made its opinion known.

He was not using the oven. He had no reason to use the oven; he subsisted on deli sandwiches and the emergency cardamom buns he'd found in the freezer, which he thawed one at a time and ate standing over the sink like a criminal. But at three in the morning the building emitted a sound—a deep, metallic groan, followed by a clank, followed by a silence so pointed it seemed accusatory—and Petra lay in his aunt's bed staring at the ceiling and understood that the building was going to make demands of him whether he'd agreed to tenancy or not.

He went down to the kitchen with a flashlight, wearing sweatpants and his coat over his pajama shirt, and found the old gas oven wheezing like an asthmatic dragon. The pilot light guttered. Somewhere inside, a valve ticked with the rhythm of a dying clock.

"I'm selling you," Petra informed it. "Whatever you're doing is pointless."

The oven clanked again, insolently.

He stood there in the dark kitchen, flashlight beam trembling slightly—his hands, always his hands, they trembled now at odd moments, another souvenir of Prague that no doctor could explain—and he thought about Aunt Marguerite kneeling right here at four a.m. for thirty-one years, coaxing heat out of this iron beast, and he felt the door in his chest swing on its hinge again.

He closed it. He went back upstairs. He did not sleep.

In the morning there was a note taped to the bakery's front door, and the note was not addressed to him. It read, in handwriting like a slap:

**Marguerite — I know you're gone and I know the nephew is supposedly in town. Tell him, or tell yourself, that I have a crew booked for demolition assessment on the 14th and a lawyer who says the lease terms are negotiable. This kitchen has been dead for a year. Let me bury it properly. — A.V.**

Petra read it twice. Then he took it down, folded it, and put it in his pocket, because it was evidence—evidence that the world outside his walls had opinions, schedules, crews booked for the fourteenth, and a lawyer.

He made coffee. He drank it standing up. Out the window, the harbor did its patient gray work, and somewhere behind him the oven ticked, and the folded note in his pocket weighed approximately as much as a summons.

Ninety days, the lease said. Ninety days, and then he could sign, sell, and vanish, and this town with its hardware-store philosophers and its demanding ovens and its slapping handwritten notes could become a story he never told anyone.

It was a good plan. Load-bearing. He leaned his whole weight against it, and waited to see what would give first.
