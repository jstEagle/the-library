## 2. Terms of the Lease

Astrid Voss had three rules for buying real estate, and she was about to break all of them.

Rule one: never fall in love with a building before the inspection. Rule two: never trust a seller who says "as-is" more than twice in one meeting. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, let yourself imagine the finished kitchen before you own it.

She had already broken rule one so thoroughly that she'd sketched the layout on a napkin at Dottie's Diner, drawn little steam curls rising off the range like the building itself was sighing with pleasure. She'd broken rule two by shaking hands with Gerald Pruitt, the estate lawyer handling the old bakery's sale, a man whose "as-is" count had reached four and climbing. And rule three—well. The napkin had steam curls. It was over.

"The structure's sound," Gerald said now, tapping the folder between them on the bakery's dusty front counter. "Roof's newer than it looks. Water heater's from this century, which around here counts as a miracle."

"And the kitchen?"

Gerald's pause lasted exactly one beat too long.

Astrid set down her coffee. "Gerald."

"There's a tenant," he said, in the tone of a man ripping off a bandage he'd hoped you wouldn't notice. "Upstairs apartment. Ninety days left on the lease."

"Ninety—" Astrid laughed, one sharp note. "Ninety days is nothing. I'll buy the building, renovate down here, and whoever it is can pack their boxes in peace."

"It's not quite that simple. The lease has a quiet-enjoyment clause. No construction noise during stated hours. And the tenant has first refusal on renewal."

"Who is it? Some snowbird who's never even here?"

Gerald flipped a page, squinting at it as though the answer might have changed since the last time he'd read it. "Underhill. Petra Underhill. Nephew of the late Mrs. Aldova." He said the name carefully, watching her face.

The name landed somewhere in Astrid's chest and sat there, heavy and familiar.

Petra Underhill.

She knew that name the way coastal towns know their weather—by heart, without checking. Twelve years ago, when Astrid was seventeen and working summers shucking oysters at the Bellhaven Yacht Club, the town had thrown its annual winter gala, and for once—not because they could afford it but because someone's cousin knew someone's wife—they'd booked an actual professional musician. A cellist. Young, serious, with a jawline that made the yacht club's grandmothers forget their grievances and a way of playing that had made Astrid stand in the back doorway with a tray of champagne going warm against her hip, forgetting entirely what the tray was for.

He'd played something slow and aching, and then something fast and joyful, and the whole room had leaned toward him like plants toward light. Astrid remembered thinking: *that's what I want to do to people.* Not with a cello. With food. Make a room full of strangers go quiet and then come back to themselves changed.

She'd told him so, afterward, in the kitchen while he packed up his instrument. He'd looked startled—as if no one had ever said anything like that to him—and then he'd smiled, and she'd thought, *oh no*, and then he'd said, "Then we're in the same business," and shaken her hand, flour on his cuff from helping himself to the buffet, and left for whatever glittering city took him next.

And now he was upstairs. In *her* building. With ninety days of lease and a quiet-enjoyment clause.

"No," Astrid said.

"I'm afraid yes."

"No, I mean—I'll talk to him. Man to—chef to cellist. Whatever. I'll make him an offer. Cash for early termination. People love cash."

"He turned down the library's request to use the downstairs for story hour," Gerald said mildly. "I hear he also complained about the church bells."

"The *church bells* are two blocks away."

"So I've heard."

Astrid stood, buttoned her coat with the crisp violence of a woman preparing for battle. "Where does he take his mail?"

"Astrid," Gerald said, with the weariness of long acquaintance—he'd known her since she was stealing day-old rolls from this very counter, back when the bakery belonged to old Mrs. Aldova, who used to chase her out with a broom and then, once Astrid was out of sight, leave a bag of them on the step anyway. "Be nice. He's grieving. Mrs. Aldova raised him, near enough. He's up there sorting through forty years of her things."

Something in Astrid's chest pinched. She ignored it. Feelings were for after closing.

"Grief," she said, "does not entitle anyone to stand between me and my kitchen."

* * *

The stairs to the apartment were behind a door marked PRIVATE in paint so faded it read more like a suggestion than a command. Astrid climbed them with the confidence of a woman who had stormed many rooms and won most of them.

She knocked. Nothing.

She knocked again, harder, and heard—faintly, from inside—a sound she couldn't place. Low, sustained, wavering. A creaking, almost. Like a floorboard being slowly persuaded.

"Mr. Underhill?" she called. "It's Astrid Voss. From downstairs. We need to talk about the lease."

The creaking stopped. Long silence. Then footsteps, unhurried, and the door opened six inches on a chain.

One eye appeared in the gap. Dark, tired, and—she registered with a jolt—unmistakably attached to the face of the man from the gala, twelve years deepened and dimmed. The jawline had survived. Everything else looked like it had been left out in weather.

"Ms. Voss," he said. His voice was low and careful, like someone walking on ice. "Gerald called ahead, didn't he."

"He did not. But I'm glad you're expecting me. Saves time." She held up the folded napkin sketch, absurd now, steam curls and all. "May I come in?"

The eye considered her. The chain slid free.

The apartment was Mrs. Aldova's apartment still—doilies on the armrests, a clock that ticked too loudly, the smell of lavender fighting a losing war against dust. Boxes everywhere, half-packed, labeled in handwriting so precise it looked printed. And in the corner by the window, a cello case, closed, with a coat draped over it as if to hide it. Or maybe just to keep it warm. Astrid couldn't tell which was sadder.

Petra Underhill stood in the middle of it all wearing a sweater with a hole at the elbow, looking less like a man guarding his territory than a man who had forgotten why anyone would want it.

"You're buying the building," he said. Not a question.

"In escrow, pending my attorney's review. Which is where you come in." She planted herself in front of the doily-covered armchair like it was a podium. "Here's what I'm offering. You walk away today, I pay your remaining lease value plus moving costs plus a month's rent anywhere in town. That's generous. That's more than generous. That's me being the kind of person my grandmother would be proud of."

"That's very specific about your grandmother."

"She chased me out of this bakery with a broom when I was nine and then fed me until I was twenty. I contain multitudes." She thrust the napkin at him. "Look. This is what happens down there. A real kitchen. A restaurant. Jobs for half the town's line cooks who currently drive forty minutes to work. Do you know what's in that kitchen right now? An oven older than both of us and a grease trap that predates the moon landing."

His gaze dropped to the napkin. Something flickered across his face—not hostility. Recognition, maybe. The look of a man seeing someone else's dream rendered in ballpoint and feeling the pull of it despite himself.

"It's a good drawing," he said quietly.

"It's a great drawing. I drew it at Dottie's and the waitress cried."

That almost got a smile out of him. Almost. It got as far as the corner of his mouth and then retreated, like a tide going out.

"But no," he said.

Astrid blinked. "You haven't even heard the number."

"I don't need to. I have ninety days. I'll be gone before your permits clear, most likely. You can't renovate around a quiet-enjoyment clause, I know, but you also can't evict a man for existing." He said it without heat. Without anything, really. That was the strangest part—the total absence of fight in him. He wasn't defending his ground; he was simply standing on it, the way furniture stands.

"Most people negotiate," she said.

"Most people want something."

"Everyone wants something. That's the entire engine of civilization."

He looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment the tiredness in his face rearranged into something sharper. Interest, maybe. Or recognition of a fellow creature. Hard to say.

"What do you want, Ms. Voss?" he asked. "Exactly. Not the napkin version."

The question caught her off guard, which almost nothing did. She opened her mouth to give the pitch—the jobs, the town, the revitalization speech she'd given the bank, the council, her mother—and found that in this dusty room full of boxed-up grief, none of it would come out right.

"I want a kitchen where nobody's ever told me to make it smaller," she said finally. "I've spent ten years cooking in other people's spaces, under other people's rules, trimming my menu to fit their equipment. This building is the first thing I've ever looked at and seen myself in. So yes, I want it. Desperately. And I resent having to want anything that badly."

The words hung there. She hadn't meant to say all of them.

Petra Underhill studied her for a long moment. Then he crossed to the window, where afternoon light came in gray and salt-flavored, and stood with his back to her.

"When I was nineteen," he said, "I played a gala in this town. Winter festival. Little hall above the yacht club, folding chairs, a radiator that clanked in triple time." He didn't turn around. "There was a girl working the room with a champagne tray who told me she wanted to do to people what music does. With food."

Astrid went very still.

"I've thought about that, occasionally, over the years," he went on. "How certain she sounded. How much I envied it." He turned. Up close, in the flat gray light, she could see what the years had done: not age exactly, but erosion. Something worn down to the bedrock. "So. Chef Voss. You got your kitchen?"

"Not yet."

"Then I suppose we're in each other's way." He said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "Ninety days. I won't renew. I'll be gone by spring, and you can have every nail in this building. Until then—" a small, exhausted shrug— "the clause stands. Eight to six, no construction noise. I'm sorry. I know that's not what you came to hear."

"It absolutely is not."

"I know."

They regarded each other across Mrs. Aldova's doilies, two people standing on opposite ends of the same stubbornness, and Astrid felt the strange, unwelcome sensation of respecting someone she'd come prepared to despise.

"For what it's worth," he added, as she turned for the door, "she'd have liked your napkin. Mrs. Aldova. She used to draw on everything. Receipts, bills, the backs of recipes. Little sketches of the harbor." A pause. "There's a drawer of them in the kitchen, if you end up gutting the place. Left side, under the window. Don't throw them out."

Astrid stopped with her hand on the latch.

"Why would you tell me that?" she asked. "You're supposed to be making this hard."

"I'm not making anything hard, Ms. Voss. Things are already hard. I'm just standing in one small part of it." He almost smiled again—that same aborted tide. "Good luck with the permits."

* * *

Downstairs, alone in the dead kitchen, Astrid pulled open the left-hand drawer beneath the window.

Receipts. Bills. Yellowed recipe cards. And on every single one, in pencil or pen or once, memorably, mustard: little sketches. The harbor in fog. Gulls arguing over a fry. A cat asleep in a bread basket. A young boy at a table, head bent over a cello, drawn with such tenderness that Astrid's throat went tight.

On the back of a 1987 flour invoice, Mrs. Aldova had drawn the kitchen itself—this exact room—but imagined new. New range. Open shelves. Steam curling off pots, in loops so like the ones on Astrid's napkin that she laughed out loud, alone, in the dust and the cold.

At the bottom of the drawing, in the old woman's looping hand: *Someday, someone will fix this place properly.*

Astrid stood there holding thirty years of somebody's hope, and upstairs, faintly through the floorboards, she heard it again—that low, sustained, wavering sound. The creaking. Except now she knew what it was. Not a floorboard.

A cello, being played so quietly it was barely a sound at all. One long note, held and held, like a man testing whether the water was safe to enter.

Or like a man who had forgotten how to swim and was standing at the edge anyway.

Astrid put the drawings back in the drawer, closed it gently, and made herself a promise involving ninety days, a quiet-enjoyment clause, and the absolute destruction of Marcus Hale's patience—no. Wrong name. Whoever the investor turned out to be. Details later.

First: the oven. Second: the permits. Third: figuring out how to get a grieving cellist to move out without becoming the villain of her own story.

She was still working on the order of operations when she heard his front door close overhead, and realized—with genuine irritation—that she was listening for him now.

That, she decided, was a problem for day four of ninety.
