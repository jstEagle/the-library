## 6. The Investor

The man who walked into the bakery on a Tuesday in November was wearing a coat that cost more than Astrid's entire kitchen budget, and he knew it.

She knew it too, the way she knew when a customer was going to send back a dish before they'd taken a single bite—something in the set of the shoulders, the practiced casualness of the entrance. Marcus Hale stood in the doorway of what was currently half gutted kitchen, half construction site, and looked around with the expression of a man appraising a horse.

"You must be Astrid Voss." He extended a hand. His grip was warm, dry, calibrated to exactly the pressure that said *I am trustworthy* without saying anything so vulgar as the words. "Marcus Hale. I've been hearing your name all over the county."

"From whom?" Astrid asked, wiping her hands on her apron. "I've been hearing my name mostly from the permit office, and not in a friendly way."

He laughed. It was a good laugh—she'd give him that. Easy, genuine-sounding, the laugh of a man who had never once had to fake amusement and had therefore gotten very good at faking it.

"The planning board meeting last month. I was there on other business, but I couldn't help noticing the woman who made three separate county employees look like they'd shown up to a knife fight with spoons."

"I prefer to think of it as a productive dialogue about zoning variances."

"I prefer to think of it as the most entertaining forty minutes I've spent in a government building in my life." He looked past her, into the kitchen, and Astrid watched his gaze travel over the exposed studs, the half-removed tile, the ancient range she was keeping out of sentiment and spite. "This is quite a project."

"It's quite a kitchen. It was. It will be again."

"May I?" He gestured, and when she shrugged, he stepped inside with the careful tread of a man who understood that chefs were territorial. He ran a finger along the doorframe, examined the original tin ceiling tiles she'd had restored, and paused at the window that looked out onto the alley. "Good bones. Good light. You kept the original layout."

"The layout is the point. The woman who built this place in 1948 understood that a kitchen is a room you live in, not a room you work in."

"Rosa Marchetti," he said.

Astrid went still. "You've done your homework."

"I always do my homework." He turned and smiled at her, and it was a genuinely lovely smile, and Astrid felt the first cold thread of unease go down her spine, which was annoying, because she wanted to like him. "Ms. Voss, I'm going to be direct with you, because I suspect you'd find anything else insulting. I invest in food businesses. Restaurants, bakeries, production kitchens. I've been looking at Bellhaven for eight months. I've looked at the cannery, the old hotel, two storefronts on Harbor Street. And every time, I come back to this building, and I think: someone is doing this right. Someone understands what this town is going to need in five years."

"Someone is," Astrid said. "And that someone has a financing plan."

"May I ask about it?"

"You may ask. Whether I answer depends on the question."

He laughed again. "There it is. That's exactly what I mean." He pulled a card from his coat pocket—not a business card, she noticed, but a heavy cream rectangle with his name embossed in the center and a single phone number beneath it. No company. No title. "I'm not going to pitch you in a construction site while you're covered in drywall dust. Come to dinner. My treat, no strings. There's a place in Rockport doing remarkable things with duck. We'll talk about Rosa Marchetti and what you're trying to build here, and if it's not a fit, you'll have had a very good dinner and a story to tell."

"And if it is a fit?"

"Then we'll talk numbers, and I think you'll find them worth the duck."

He left the card on the nearest sawhorse, weighted it with a scrap of copper pipe like a paperweight, and let himself out with a nod. Through the window, Astrid watched him cross the street, pause to say something pleasant to Mrs. Okonkwo from the yarn shop, and continue down the sidewalk like a man who owned the sidewalk and was letting everyone else use it.

She looked at the card for a long time.

* * *

That night, in the apartment above the kitchen, with a space heater fighting a losing battle against the coastal damp, Astrid sat at her kitchen table with a bowl of leftover stew and wrote to her stranger.

The letters had become the strangest, best part of her week. The program had matched her with someone called only "P," and the first letter she'd received had been so aggressively dull—two paragraphs about the weather, one about a book on maritime knots—that she'd written back purely out of spite. But the stranger had risen to the bait, and somewhere over the past month the letters had turned into something she didn't have a name for. He was funny in a dry, reluctant way, like humor was a muscle he was embarrassed to use. And he was tired. Not bored-tired. *Bone*-tired, the tired of a person who had once been very good at something and had stopped, and was now living in the wreckage of that decision and calling it peace.

She'd written to him about the demolition, about the oven she was rebuilding, about the upstairs neighbor whose existence she had described as "a personal attack by the universe." She had not named the neighbor. Some confidences were strategic.

She wrote now about Marcus Hale. She kept it light—*a man in a coat worth more than my kitchen came by today to admire my drywall*—and asked, half joking, whether men who invested in small-town food businesses were ever anything but a plot device.

The reply came three days later, and it was different from his usual letters. Shorter. The handwriting, usually so controlled it looked typeset, had a slight forward lean, like a man hurrying.

*Astrid—*

*I want to answer your question seriously, which is unlike me, so forgive the tone.*

*Men with perfect documents are usually hiding something. I've sat across from enough of them to know the type. The charm is real, but it's not for you—it's a tool, the way your knives are tools. Watch what he asks about. A genuine investor asks about your food, your margins, your people. A man with an agenda asks about the building, the title, the paperwork. The building is the thing he wants. The restaurant is just how he plans to get in the door.*

*I'm not saying he's dishonest. I'm saying that when everything about a man is smooth, look for the seam.*

*Also: duck in Rockport is good. That part at least is true. The duck is always true.*

*—P*

Astrid read the letter twice, then a third time, standing at the window with the space heater ticking behind her.

*The building is the thing he wants.*

She thought about Marcus Hale's finger on her doorframe. His eye on the tin tiles. The way he had said *Rosa Marchetti* like he'd been practicing it.

"Paranoid," she said aloud, to the empty apartment. "He's a paranoid man who writes beautiful letters and is afraid of investors. Probably a failed businessman. Probably got burned once and now thinks everyone's a villain."

The apartment did not answer. The apartment never did. But upstairs, faintly, through the floor, she heard a floorboard creak—her impossible neighbor, pacing at midnight again, the way he did, three steps and a turn, three steps and a turn, like a man rehearsing something he'd never say.

* * *

She went to dinner.

She told herself it was due diligence. She told herself that a chef with a suspended permit and a half-gutted kitchen and a savings account that had begun making a sound like a dying radiator could not afford to turn down dinner with a man who wore five figures on his shoulders. She put on the dress she saved for food critics and funerals, and she drove the forty minutes to Rockport, and the duck, as promised, was remarkable.

Marcus Hale was charming for two hours straight, which was its own kind of feat.

He asked about her training—Copenhagen, the two years under the woman Astrid still described as "my education and my trial." He asked about her menu philosophy, and listened, actually listened, leaning forward while she talked about seasonality and the fishing fleet and the fact that Bellhaven deserved a kitchen that took it seriously. He made her laugh twice, genuinely, with a story about a catering disaster involving a senator and a chocolate fountain. And then, somewhere between the cheese course and the coffee, he asked:

"The building. Is it on the market?"

And there it was. The seam.

"It's not," Astrid said. "It's in probate. The estate is being settled."

"Ah." He stirred his coffee, unhurried. "And the upstairs tenant?"

Astrid set down her spoon. "Why do you ask?"

"Because a building with a sitting tenant is a complicated acquisition, and I like to know what I'm walking into." He smiled, easy and warm. "The bakery downstairs is your dream. I understand that. But buildings are assets, Ms. Voss, and assets have histories. Has the estate been cooperative?"

"The estate has been dead, which presents its own challenges."

He laughed, delighted. "You're wasted in that kitchen. Truly." Then, leaning back: "Here's what I'd propose, and you can tell me I'm out of my mind. I fund the kitchen—equipment, build-out, working capital, the whole package. In exchange, a minority stake, no operational control, I want that stated in writing. You cook. I write checks. And if the estate ever decides to sell the building—" he lifted his coffee, a small toast, "—I'd like the right of first refusal."

"The building," Astrid said slowly, "is not part of the restaurant."

"The building is the restaurant. You can't pick up a kitchen and move it. The tin tiles, the layout, Rosa Marchetti's ghost—that's the whole value. The building is the brand." He said it gently, reasonably, the way a man explains tide tables to someone whose house is on the shore. "I'm not trying to take anything from you, Astrid. I'm trying to make sure that when opportunity knocks, you're the one who answers."

*The building is the thing he wants.*

She could hear her stranger's voice so clearly it was almost as if he were sitting at the table, in his imagined cardigan, looking at Marcus Hale with those tired, level eyes.

"Let me think about it," Astrid said.

"Of course." He didn't push. That was the polished part, the part she'd remember later: he didn't push at all. He paid the bill before she could reach for it, walked her to her car in the cold, and shook her hand at the driver's door. "Take your time. Good things are worth doing properly."

Then he stood on the curb and watched her drive away, and in the rearview mirror Astrid saw him take out his phone and make a call, and she saw him smile while he made it.

* * *

The permit office called the next morning to say her suspension had been lifted, effective immediately, no explanation given. Astrid stood in the half-built kitchen holding the phone and felt, for one full minute, the dangerous lightness of a problem dissolving.

Then she thought: *I never told him about the permit.*

She hadn't. She was sure of it. The dinner had been the first and only conversation, and she'd never mentioned the suspension, the inspections, the paperwork snarled in the county system. And yet the suspension had lifted the day after Marcus Hale's coffee toast, smooth as a hand smoothing a page flat.

She stood in the middle of her kitchen, in the good light from the alley window, and felt the unease settle into her bones like damp.

That night she wrote to her stranger. Not about Hale—she'd already written about Hale, and his reply sat in her lap like a stone she kept turning over. Instead she wrote about the oven, about the first test bake, about how the kitchen smelled like possibility when the heat came up. She kept the letter warm on purpose. Some part of her, she realized, wanted to prove him wrong. Wanted the world to contain a charming man with immaculate paperwork who was exactly what he appeared to be.

She sealed the letter, and then, on an impulse she didn't examine, she added a postscript:

*P.S. Your paranoid investor came to dinner. He was charming. The duck was excellent. He asked about the building twice and about my food once. I haven't said yes to anything. I wanted you to know that.*

*Also, I've decided that if you turn out to be wrong about him, you owe me a letter about something happy. Your choice of topic. You've been dodging happy subjects for a month and I've noticed.*

She mailed it Tuesday. The reply came Saturday, and it was the strangest one yet—warm, almost giddy in its restrained way, a letter from a man who had been handed permission to be pleased and didn't quite know what to do with it. He wrote about the sound the sea made at 4 a.m., which he claimed was different from the sound it made at any other hour, "lower, like it's talking in its sleep." He wrote about bread, at length, with the reverence of a man who had clearly been thinking about it for years. He did not mention Hale again.

But at the bottom, in that controlled handwriting with its slight forward lean, he'd written:

*You asked for something happy. Here it is: I believe you. Whatever you decide about the man with the coat, I believe you'll decide it with your eyes open, and that's rarer than charm, and worth more.*

*I'm glad the permit came through. You deserve a kitchen that works.*

Astrid read the last line and frowned.

*I'm glad the permit came through.*

She had mentioned the suspension in an early letter, weeks ago, before Hale. She checked. She checked twice, sitting on the floor of the half-built kitchen with the stack of letters spread around her like evidence. Yes—she'd written about the permit trouble in her third letter, venting about the county. So it was nothing. He'd remembered. He remembered things; that was one of the things about him, one of the things that made the letters feel less like correspondence and more like being *known*.

Still, she sat there a moment longer, in the good light, with the oven cold beside her and the tin tiles overhead, and felt the faintest pressure against the inside of her chest—like a finger tapping a window from the other side of the glass.

Above her, faint through the floorboards: three steps and a turn. Three steps and a turn.

"Pacing again," she muttered, and gathered her letters, and did not let herself wonder why the sound of it, tonight, felt less like an annoyance and more like company.
