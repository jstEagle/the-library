## 7. Duet, Unplayed

The request came on a Tuesday, delivered the way most dangerous things in Bellhaven were delivered: cheerfully, and with baked goods attached.

Petra opened his apartment door to find Marge Okafor standing on the landing holding a tin of shortbread and an expression he had learned, over three months of small-town residency, to fear. Marge ran the library, the pen-pal program, and — as far as he could tell — roughly sixty percent of the town's civic infrastructure through sheer force of will and cardigan.

"Petra." She said his name like they were old friends, which they were not, though she had extorted a pen-pal membership out of him in exchange for extending his lease, so perhaps *extortionate acquaintances* was more accurate. "I brought shortbread."

"I didn't ask for shortbread."

"No one ever does. That's what makes it a gift." She pushed past him into the apartment with the confidence of a woman who had never once been stopped by a door, and set the tin on the kitchen counter, next to the stack of unopened mail he was pretending not to see. "The winter festival committee met last night."

"Mm."

"Everyone's very excited. Ice sculptures this year. A bonfire on the pier. And we thought—" she turned, hands clasped, radiating innocence the way a lighthouse radiates light, "—since we have a *professional musician* living right here above the bakery, it would be wonderful to have live music. Something classical. Something elegant."

Petra's hands did the thing they always did now when someone said *music* to him — a small, involuntary contraction, fingers curling toward palms that hadn't held a bow in two years. He shoved them into his pockets where she couldn't see.

"No," he said.

"You haven't even heard the proposal."

"There's no version of the proposal I want to hear."

"Twenty minutes," Marge said. "One cello, one chair, one small amplifier because the acoustics by the pier are tricky. The children's choir opens, you close, everyone drinks cocoa, everyone goes home happy. It would mean a great deal to this town, Petra. You'd be giving back to a community that has—"

"Charged me for parking twice, fined me for an overflowing bin I did not fill, and asked me at least four times if I'm 'the cellist guy'?" He kept his voice flat. Practiced. "No."

Marge studied him for a long moment. She had the particular patience of a librarian who had spent thirty years waiting out difficult patrons, and it was worse than any pressure could have been. She simply looked at him, kindly, like she could see straight through to whatever was rotting underneath.

"The offer stands," she said finally. "Until the fifteenth." She patted the shortbread tin. "Eat something. You look like a coat hanger."

Then she left, and Petra stood in his aunt's apartment — his aunt who had played piano badly and joyfully every Sunday morning until the neighbors complained and she played anyway — and felt the tremor in his hands slowly subside, the way a storm drains out of a sky.

He did not eat the shortbread. He sat down instead at the window that overlooked the bakery's back lot, where Astrid Voss was currently directing two men in the demolition of a wall, gesturing with a length of rebar like a general who had personally invented warfare. Even from up here he could hear her voice cutting through the cold: *"That's load-bearing, Gary, I know it's load-bearing, that's why I hired you to MOVE it—"*

He watched her longer than he meant to. Then he made tea he didn't drink and told himself, as he had told himself every day for two years, that his heart was a muscle and muscles only atrophied if you let them.

* * *

The problem with refusing to play, Petra discovered that evening, was that his body refused to cooperate with the refusal.

He tried to read. The words slid off the page. He tried to sleep. His hands lay on top of the blanket like two dead birds, except they weren't dead, were they, because at 1 a.m. they started moving without his permission — flexing, reaching, shaping themselves around a bow grip that existed only in memory.

He got up. He told himself it was to get water.

Instead he found himself standing in front of the closet where the cello case lived, under a folded quilt, behind a box of his aunt's Christmas decorations he had never unpacked. He hadn't opened it since he'd carried it off the stage in Vienna — no. Before that. Since he'd carried it out of the concert hall in Vienna while a thousand people watched him fail in real time, his left hand simply *stopping*, mid-phrase, mid-Dvořák, the notes dying in his fingers like a radio losing signal.

Focal dystonia, the doctors called it. A cruel little neurological betrayal, common among musicians, incurable, unpredictable. His brain had decided, somewhere deep below the level of his consent, that it no longer trusted his third finger. And once your own mind turns against you in front of two thousand paying customers, you learn something about yourself: that you were never really playing for them at all. You were playing for their belief in you. And belief, once dropped, shatters on the floor of the concert hall where everyone can see it.

He didn't open the case. He put his hand flat against the lid instead, the way you might touch the door of a house where someone you loved used to live.

Then he went downstairs.

Not all the way down — just to the landing outside the bakery's back kitchen, where the old radiator clanked and the smell of sawdust and plaster hung in the air. Astrid had left the work lights on. Through the doorway he could see the gutted room: half a wall gone, studs exposed like ribs, her grandmother's ancient range sitting alone in the middle of the chaos like a survivor.

It should have been ugly. It wasn't. It looked like a room mid-sentence, a room brave enough to be unfinished.

He went back upstairs and sat down at the small desk by the window, and pulled out the stationery the library gave him for the pen-pal program — cream-colored, with a little stamped bell in the corner, Bellhaven's idea of whimsy.

His correspondent — *Wren*, according to the program's alias system, because Marge believed anonymity freed the soul or something — had written to him eleven times now. Petra had answered ten. The first letter he'd written had been deliberately, weaponized-ly boring: the weather, the price of eggs, a paragraph about gutter maintenance. He'd expected the program to quietly drop him.

Instead Wren had written back: *Gutters. You wrote me a letter about gutters. Either you're the most fascinating man alive and this is a test, or you're hiding something enormous behind a discussion of downspouts. I respect both options. Try again.*

So he'd tried again. And somehow, letter by letter, without meaning to, without noticing the slope beneath his feet, he'd found himself telling this stranger things. Not everything. Never the thing. But adjacent things. The shape of things. He'd written about how a town can feel like a waiting room. About how silence gets comfortable, the way a bad mattress does — you stop noticing it's ruining your back.

Tonight, after Marge's shortbread and the closet and the unfinished kitchen, he picked up the pen and the wall cracked.

*Dear Wren,* he wrote. *You asked me once why I moved to a town where I knew no one. I gave you some answer about rent. Here is the real one.*

The pen hovered. His hand shook — not the dystonic tremor, the other kind, the honest one.

*I used to be somebody. Not famous-famous, but enough. Enough that strangers recognized my name, enough that my calendar was full eighteen months out, enough that when I walked on stage people leaned forward before I'd played a single note. I was a cellist. Past tense. Two years ago, in the middle of a concert, in front of two thousand people, my hand stopped working. Just — stopped. Like a machine deciding it was done. I finished the piece badly. I bowed. I walked off. I never played again.*

*Here's the part nobody tells you about losing the thing that made you you: everyone wants to comfort you, and none of them can, because what they say is always some version of "at least you still have..." and the sentence ends with anything but the thing. At least you're alive. At least you're young. At least you could teach. As if the loss were a budget shortfall and not an amputation.*

*So I came here, where nobody knows my name, and I decided I would become no one. It seemed restful. No one asks a no one to perform. No one looks at a no one's hands.*

*Tonight someone asked me to play at the winter festival. I said no before she finished the sentence. My hands were shaking in my pockets like a coward's. And then I stood in front of my cello case for twenty minutes and couldn't open it, and I realized the shaking wasn't fear of playing. It was fear that I'd open the case and discover I don't miss it. Or worse — that I do.*

*You're the first person I've told. Isn't that absurd? You don't exist. You're a pseudonym and a postmark and handwriting that slants like it's always in a hurry. And you know me better than anyone alive.*

*Don't write back something kind. I'll know it's kindness and it won't count. Write me something true.*

He read it over twice. Every instinct he owned — two years of instincts, polished smooth, dedicated to the single project of being no one — screamed at him to burn it. To write about gutters instead. To seal the wall back up before anyone noticed there'd been a crack.

He signed it *Lark* — his program alias, chosen in a fit of irony he no longer found funny, since larks sing at dawn and he hadn't managed a sunrise performance in years — and folded the pages, and did not seal the envelope.

He left it on the desk overnight, face-down, like a verdict he wasn't ready to hear.

* * *

In the morning, Astrid Voss was hammering something at six a.m., because of course she was, because the woman appeared to regard sleep as a personal failing in others. Petra stood at his window with coffee and watched her wrestle a sheet of plywood across the lot, alone, stubborn, magnificent in the way that disasters are magnificent — all momentum and no plan.

She caught him looking. She raised the hammer in something between a greeting and a threat. He raised his mug. She shouted something he couldn't hear through the glass, but from the shape of her mouth he was fairly sure it was either "good morning" or "your lease ends in fifty-one days," and knowing Astrid, it was probably both.

He went back inside and sealed the letter.

At the library drop-box, his hand hesitated over the slot for exactly four seconds — long enough for Marge, watching from behind her desk with the omniscience of her profession, to raise one eyebrow at him over her glasses.

"It's a letter," Petra said defensively. "People mail letters."

"They do," Marge agreed serenely. "Some even play music at festivals. Miracles abound."

He dropped the letter in and walked out into the cold, hands in his pockets, lighter than he had been in two years and terrified of exactly that.

Three days later, the reply came.

*Dear Lark,* it began, in that slanted, hurrying hand. *I've read your letter nine times. I read it in the bath, which was a mistake, because now it's slightly wrinkled and smells like rosemary, and I refuse to apologize for either.*

*You asked for truth, not kindness. Fine. Truth: I have spent the last six months renovating a kitchen that everyone — the bank, my ex-business partner, my mother, one specific insufferable tenant — believes will fail. I swing a hammer at five a.m. partly because I love the work and partly because if I stop moving, the doubt catches me. So when you say you became no one on purpose, I understand it better than you'd believe. Some days "no one" sounds like a spa retreat.*

*But here's the other truth, the one you won't like: you said you never played again. Liar. People who never play again don't stand in front of closed instrument cases at midnight. People who never play again don't describe a Dvořák phrase dying "like a radio losing signal" — that's not a memory of failure, that's a musician's ear, still working, still listening, still yours. Your hand quit. You didn't. There's a difference, and someday you're going to have to figure out which one you actually lost.*

*Also: the festival. Go. Not for the town. For the cocoa.*

*— Wren*

P.S. If you tell me the name of the piece, I'll find a recording. I want to hear what broke you. I think it matters.

Petra read the letter standing in his doorway, the cold coming in around his ankles, and laughed — a short, startled sound, rusty from disuse, that echoed strangely off the stairwell.

Because the piece had been Dvořák's Cello Concerto. And because Astrid Voss was, at that exact moment, visible through the window below him, hammering plywood in the frost with her headphones in, and because he was almost certain — impossibly, absurdly certain — that she was listening to a recording of it right now.

No. That was coincidence. That was ego. That was two years of loneliness teaching him to see her everywhere.

He folded the letter carefully and put it in the drawer with the others, and told himself the warmth spreading through his chest was just the coffee.

Below, the hammering paused. Astrid looked up at his window — directly at it, for a beat too long — then went back to work.

Petra stood there a while, watching, his traitor hands curled loosely at his sides, almost relaxed. Almost remembering.
