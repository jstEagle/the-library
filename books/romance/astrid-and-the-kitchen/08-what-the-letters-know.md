## 8. What the Letters Know

The phrase arrived on a Tuesday, which Astrid would later decide was fitting, because Tuesdays were when the bakery's plumbing chose violence and her patience was already spoken for.

She was standing in the half-gutted kitchen with a mug of coffee going cold in one hand and a letter in the other, reading it for the third time. The morning light came through the plastic sheeting over the window in that particular coastal way—gray, forgiving, making even sawdust look romantic—and she had been enjoying a rare moment of peace with her pen pal's latest offering when the sentence stopped her cold.

*Grief isn't a room you leave. It's a room you learn to furnish.*

Astrid lowered the letter.

She knew that sentence. Not the words themselves—those were new—but the *shape* of them, the cadence, the way it turned a platitude inside out like a sock. She'd heard something just like it four days ago, standing on the bakery's back stairs at eleven at night, holding a bottle of wine she'd claimed was for cooking and Petra Underhill had claimed he wasn't interested in, while they'd argued about whether the oven could be saved.

*You don't get over it,* he'd said, not looking at her, his long fingers worrying the strap of his watch. *You just redecorate around it.*

Redecorate. Furnish. A room you live in.

Astrid set down the mug and told herself, firmly, that she was being ridiculous.

Because here was the thing: coincidences happened. Phrases drifted through the culture like pollen. Half the population talked about grief as architecture—it was practically a genre of greeting card. And besides, her pen pal was nothing like Petra Underhill. Her pen pal was funny in a dry, self-deprecating way that snuck up on you. He asked questions like he actually wanted the answers. He admitted to things—real things, embarrassing things, the kind of things men like Petra Underhill would sooner eat glass than confess to a stranger.

Petra Underhill, as far as she could tell, confessed to nothing. He was all deflection and dry remarks and that infuriating way of looking at her like she was a piece of sheet music he was deciding whether to sight-read. He fixed her oven at three in the morning without being asked and then pretended he hadn't. He wore flour-dusted sweatpants like a man who had given up on being perceived, which was absurd, because the man had once played cello for two thousand people in her hometown and she had been seventeen and had cried during the encore, though she would be buried alive before telling him that.

No. Her pen pal was someone else entirely. Someone gentler. Someone who wrote:

*I burned the rice again. Third time this week. I want you to know that I am a grown man with functioning hands and I have been defeated by rice.*

And she had written back: *Rice is humbling. That's why restaurants fear it more than lobster.*

And he'd replied: *I've started setting a timer and then ignoring it out of spite. We are in an abusive relationship, me and the rice.*

That was her pen pal. That was not the man upstairs who alphabetized his spice jars—she'd seen them through the stairwell door, labeled in beautiful small handwriting, which was its own kind of suspicious, but plenty of people had nice handwriting.

"Stop it," she said aloud to the empty kitchen. A pigeon on the windowsill gave her a look. "We are not doing this."

The pigeon flew off, unconvinced.

* * *

But the thing about a splinter is that once you know it's there, your thumb keeps finding it.

All Wednesday, Astrid caught herself listening. Not to anything specific—to the texture of things. When the plumber's apprentice said *it's a room you learn to work around* about the pipe situation, she flinched. When Mrs. Okonkwo from the flower shop used the word *furnish* in casual conversation about her sunroom, Astrid gripped the counter.

By Thursday she had constructed a full case against herself. She made a list, mentally, of everything her pen pal had revealed: he was new-ish to town, or at least new to writing letters; he was awake at strange hours; he had lost something large and unnamed; he cooked badly but thought about food constantly; he had, per last week's letter, "a neighbor who hates him, possibly deservedly."

And Petra Underhill: new to town, awake at three a.m. fixing ovens, clearly carrying some unspoken loss behind those careful gray eyes, ate her leftover focaccia like a starving man while claiming bread was "not really his thing," and lived directly above a woman who had tried to evict him.

The evidence arranged itself into a shape she didn't like.

"No," she told the sourdough starter, feeding it with more force than necessary. "He signs his letters *Yours, anonymously*, which is the least Petra thing imaginable. Petra wouldn't write to a stranger. Petra barely writes emails. I've seen him hold a phone like it owes him money."

The starter bubbled, noncommittal.

There was only one way to settle it, and Astrid Voss did not become the youngest sous-chef in her culinary school's history by leaving questions unsettled. If she wanted to know whether her pen pal was the insufferable cellist upstairs, she would simply run an experiment. Controlled conditions. Single variable. Scientific method, applied to emotional espionage.

She spent Thursday evening composing the test. It had to be natural—something she could drop into conversation without it landing like a trap. Something specific enough that no accidental overlap could explain it away. Something that would mean nothing to anyone except the person who had read her letters.

Her pen pal knew, from her third letter, about the disaster of her first restaurant job: how she'd worked a double shift with a 103-degree fever because calling out felt like surrender, and how Chef Broussard had found her swaying over a stockpot, put a hand on her shoulder, and said, *"Voss. The pot will still be there tomorrow. You will too, if you sit down."* How she'd sat down on a milk crate in the alley and cried, and how that sentence had become the thing she whispered to herself on every bad day since. She had never told anyone else that story. Not her sister. Not her business partner. Nobody.

So on Friday afternoon, when Petra came down the stairs at four o'clock—drawn, as always, by the smell of the day's test bake, wearing the gray sweater that made him look like a depressed academic—she was ready.

"Underhill," she called, without turning from the counter. "Opinion."

"I have several," he said, arriving at the doorway and stopping there, always at the doorway, never crossing the invisible line into her territory. "Mostly unfavorable."

"The oven's running hot again. Left side." She slid a tray of cardamom buns toward him. "Tell me if these are ruined."

He leaned in, took a bun, bit into it. Chewing, he frowned at the ceiling in the manner of a man consulting a higher power. "Ruined," he said. "Tragically. I'll have to finish it."

"You're a parasite."

"A scavenger. Parasites are attached to hosts; I'm merely opportunistic." He reached for a second bun, and Astrid watched his hands—those famous hands, the ones the internet still argued about, long-fingered and precise even dusted with sugar—and made herself say it, casually, leaning back against the counter with her arms crossed like a woman discussing weather.

"You know what my first chef used to tell me?" she said. "When I was killing myself over a stockpot? He said, *the pot will still be there tomorrow. You will too, if you sit down.*"

It was a small thing, the pause. Most people wouldn't have caught it. But Astrid had spent ten years reading the micro-expressions of line cooks trying to hide a dropped plate, and she caught it—the half-beat where Petra's hand stopped halfway to the tray, where his eyes went somewhere else, somewhere internal, before coming back to her face with an expression of polite blankness so carefully constructed it might as well have been signed.

"That sounds like a smart chef," he said.

"He was. Mean as a hornet, but smart." She held his gaze. "Funny the things strangers tell you that end up mattering."

"Mm," said Petra, and reached past her for a third bun, which was either the act of a man completely at ease or the act of a man performing complete ease with the precision of a stage magician. "These are genuinely good. The cardamom's doing something interesting."

"It's doing cardamom," Astrid said. "Don't deflect onto the pastry."

"I would never." He was already retreating toward the stairs, bun in hand, moving with the unhurried urgency of a man exiting a building he suspected was on fire. At the bottom step he paused. "Left side of the oven runs hot. Have you checked the thermostat?"

"Have you checked your pulse?" she called after him. "Because you're acting guilty."

The staircase swallowed him. From above came the sound of his door closing—gently, always gently, the man closed doors like he was afraid of disturbing the silence—and then nothing.

Astrid stood alone in her kitchen with a tray of buns and a heart beating faster than any bun warranted.

* * *

Here is what she should have done, by any reasonable standard: marched upstairs, knocked, and said, *Are you my pen pal? Yes or no. Also, explain the spice labels.*

Here is what she actually did: she washed the tray, wiped the counter, fed the starter, prepped tomorrow's dough, and thought about it for six straight hours.

Because the problem—the real problem, the one under the problem—wasn't whether it was him. By Friday night, folding proofing cloths in the quiet kitchen, she had to admit the experiment had settled it. There had been no other explanation for that pause. No other reason a man's hand would stop mid-reach at the mention of a story he couldn't possibly know unless he *did* know it, intimately, from the inside of an envelope.

The problem was what it meant if it was him.

It meant that every letter she'd written—the fever story, the story about her father teaching her to taste with her fingertips before she could read, the letter where she'd admitted, at one in the morning, wine-assisted, that she was terrified the restaurant would fail and everyone who'd doubted her would be right—that he had read all of it. That he knew her in a way almost no one did. That the person she'd been most honest with in five years was the man she sparred with across a staircase, and neither of them had known they were already acquainted.

It meant he'd heard her vent about her impossible upstairs neighbor—*he fixes ovens at 3 a.m. like some brooding ghost of bakeries past, who does that*—and had said nothing. Had let her complain about him to his own anonymous face.

It meant—

Astrid set down the cloth.

It meant he'd read the letter where she'd written, sleepless and reckless: *Sometimes I think the loneliest thing isn't having no one. It's having someone right upstairs and not being able to say any of this out loud.*

Oh.

Oh, no.

She pressed both palms flat against the cool steel of the prep counter and breathed, the way Chef Broussard had taught her, four counts in, six counts out, the technique for steadying hands before service.

Because here was the truly dangerous part, the part she kept circling like a tongue finding a chipped tooth: she wasn't angry. Or rather, anger was present, yes, filing paperwork somewhere in the building, but it was being dramatically outshone by something warmer and considerably less convenient. Because if it was him—if the dry, gentle, devastatingly honest letter-writer was the same man who brought her deadbolts from the hardware store without being asked and remembered she took her coffee black and looked at her, sometimes, like she was music he'd forgotten how to play—then the two halves of her winter had been the same person all along, and the sum of him was so much more than either part that it made her chest hurt.

And if she confronted him now, it would stop. Whatever this was—this slow, ridiculous, unwritten correspondence between the woman downstairs and the man upstairs—it would collapse into embarrassment and defensiveness and Petra Underhill retreating behind that polite blankness until she got nothing but *mm* and *the thermostat needs checking* forever.

No. Astrid Voss knew about timing. You didn't pull a soufflé early because you were impatient. You waited. You watched. You let it rise.

So she said nothing.

* * *

Saying nothing, it turned out, was its own full-time occupation.

On Saturday she caught herself testing him again, shamelessly, unable to help it. She mentioned, apropos of nothing, that she'd always wanted to see the northern lights—"since I was a kid, it's the one stupid dream I've never chased"—and watched him go very still, because her second letter had contained exactly that confession, word for word, *stupid dream* included. Petra studied the label on the olive oil bottle for a long moment and said, "You should go. Alaska's best in February," and his voice was perfectly level and his ears had gone faintly pink, and Astrid had to turn to the sink so he wouldn't see her smile.

On Sunday he left a bag of hardware-store spoons on her counter—"they were out of the ones you broke, these are close enough"—and she said thank you and he said *don't mention it* and she thought, *oh, I intend to mention it, daily, for the rest of your life,* and then nearly dropped a whisk, because *the rest of your life* had arrived in her head fully formed, uninvited, wearing no disguise whatsoever.

Monday, the librarian waved at her through the window of the bookshop, and Astrid watched Petra cross the street to avoid the conversation, and she thought: *pen-pal program. Lease extension. Oh, you absolute coward, that's how they got you.*

She laughed out loud, alone in the kitchen, and had to pretend afterward that she'd been laughing at the yeast.

The worst of it—the very worst—was that knowing changed nothing and everything. They still fought about the oven. He still stole her buns. She still complained about him to her pen pal, sitting at the little table by the window at midnight, and it had become the strangest ritual of her life: writing *my impossible neighbor did the dishes again without asking, who raised this man* to the impossible neighbor himself, sealing the envelope, and walking it to the library drop box in her slippers, grinning like an idiot the whole way.

Because now there was a second letter underneath every letter. Now when she wrote *you're the only person who doesn't want anything from me*, she was also writing it to the man whose rent she'd tried to triple. Now when she signed off *yours, anonymously*, she pressed the pen down harder than the words required.

Tuesday evening, a week after the phrase in the kitchen, she found him on the back stairs. He was sitting on the step below hers with a mug of tea, wrapped in a blanket despite the fact that it was forty degrees, watching the harbor lights come on. He didn't ask her to join him. He just shifted slightly, making room, which was somehow worse.

She sat. The wood was cold through her jeans. For a while neither of them spoke, and the town went gold below them, one window at a time.

"My pen pal wrote something today," she said, watching his profile very carefully.

"Oh?"

"Said grief is a room you furnish." She kept her voice light, idle, a woman remarking on tides. "Which is nonsense, obviously. Grief is a room you burn down."

Petra was quiet for a moment. Steam rose off his tea.

"Maybe," he said finally, "some rooms are worth furnishing."

And there it was again—that cadence, that sideways tenderness, the exact way he turned a hard thing over until you could see its soft underside. Astrid looked at him, at the careful slope of his shoulders and the steam curling past his jaw, and felt the secret settle deeper into her chest, heavy and warm and utterly unsustainable, a soufflé she could feel beginning to fall.

"Maybe," she allowed.

Above them, faintly, through the floorboards, her phone buzzed with the notification she'd set for the county permit portal. She ignored it. Down in the harbor, the last boat came in, trailing gulls.

What she didn't know—what she couldn't have known, sitting on those cold steps guarding her warm secret—was that Marcus Hale's immaculate paperwork was already moving through offices in the county seat, and that within the week, the name signed at the bottom of her aunt's property deed would stop being her aunt's, and the question of who knew whom, and who owed whom the truth, was about to stop being a private game between two people on a staircase.

She only knew that when she stood to go inside, Petra said, "Goodnight, Astrid," and said her name the way he said almost nothing else—like it was worth pronouncing properly—and she climbed the stairs to her apartment smiling at the floor.

She also knew that tomorrow's letter was going to be the hardest one yet to write honestly, now that she knew exactly who was reading.

She wrote it anyway. Some experiments, once begun, insist on their conclusion.
