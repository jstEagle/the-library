## 9. Sourdough Diplomacy

The storm came in off the water around four o'clock, the kind of gray wall of weather that made Bellhaven feel like the last town on earth. Petra watched it from his kitchen window while the lights flickered once, twice—like a nervous performer checking the wings—and then gave up entirely.

He lit the two candles he owned, both stolen from a church rummage sale, and stood in the dark listening to the wind try to peel the roof off the bakery. Below him, he could hear Astrid moving around her half-built kitchen, the clank of pots against a camp stove she'd apparently decided was worth keeping through construction.

Then, silence. Then footsteps on the stairs.

He knew it was her before she knocked. He'd learned her tread by now—the brisk heel-strike of a woman who considered stairs a personal insult. The knock came anyway, three sharp raps, as if the door had done something wrong.

"Underhill." Her voice through the wood. "Tell me you have power."

"I have candles."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's not not what you asked."

A pause, long enough for him to imagine her expression: the narrowed eyes, the mouth doing arithmetic with his stupidity. Then the door opened without her waiting for permission, because of course it did, and there she stood in the candlelight wearing an enormous rain jacket over her apron, flour in her hair like she'd been snowed on indoors.

"The generator's dead," she said. "And I have forty pounds of dough mid-proof that needs a hot oven in the next hour or it's compost."

"Tragic."

"Don't say tragic like that. You don't know what proofing is."

"It's when dough gets hopeful," Petra said. "I read about it."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, more like a smile being detained for questioning. "My oven's gas but the igniter's electric. Your aunt's old bread oven downstairs is wood-fired. Except the flue hasn't been cleaned since roughly the Nixon administration, and I can't get it to draw."

"You want me to fix a chimney."

"I want you to *try* to fix a chimney. It's your building. Technically. For another sixty-one days." She said the number like a countdown to an execution she was very much looking forward to attending.

Petra looked at his candles. At the rain hammering the glass. At the woman dripping on his floorboards who had, three days ago, told him his face looked like a Sunday crossword clue nobody wanted to solve.

"If I fix it," he said, "you owe me dinner."

"You'll be eating my bread out of charity. That *is* dinner."

"Dinner and wine."

She stared at him. The rain filled the pause. "Fine. One bottle. If the chimney draws."

* * *

It took him ninety minutes, most of them spent flat on his back in the soot-black throat of the old oven with a wire brush and a flashlight clenched in his teeth, thinking dark thoughts about every decision that had led him here. Astrid supervised from a safe distance, offering commentary.

"You've missed a spot."

"You can't see from there."

"I have excellent eyesight."

"You have excellent opinions. Those are different organs."

But the flue cleared—a decade of creosote and one ambitious bird's nest—and when he struck the match and fed in kindling, the draft caught with a low satisfied roar, and heat began to pool in the old brick like something waking up.

Astrid went quiet. He glanced back and found her watching the fire with an expression he'd never seen on her: unguarded, almost young.

"My grandmother had an oven like this," she said. "In Trondheim. Everything important that ever happened in our family happened next to it."

"Important things happen near ovens?"

"Everything happens near ovens. People just pretend otherwise." She shook herself, brisk again, and started hauling dough. "Get out of my way, Underhill. This is a two-person job now and you're the second person."

* * *

By nine o'clock the storm had knocked out the whole grid, the whole coast, possibly the whole century, and the kitchen smelled like heaven's loading dock. Loaves cooled on racks in the firelight. Astrid found a bottle of wine in the pantry—some dusty red left by a contractor as a bribe—and poured it into two mismatched mugs because all the glasses were packed in boxes labeled FRAGILE in handwriting that suggested optimism rather than experience.

They sat on upturned milk crates by the oven, which threw more honest light than any chandelier Petra had ever played under.

"To the flue," Astrid said, raising her mug.

"To the bird," Petra said. "Whose nest we destroyed. May it find better architecture."

She laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of her, and then immediately suspicious of itself, like she'd caught her own hand in the cookie jar. Petra filed the sound away somewhere load-bearing.

"So," she said, after a while. "Sixty-one days. Where do you go? After."

He turned his mug. "I don't know. Somewhere no one knows my name."

"That's a strange ambition for someone with such a famous name."

"It's the only thing about me anyone remembers. It seemed fair to want a place where they'd forget it."

Astrid studied him across the firelight. "You know what I think? I think you didn't come here to be forgotten. I think you came here to be *unwitnessed*. There's a difference. Being forgotten is passive. Unwitnessed is a choice. It's hiding with good posture."

The words landed harder than she could know—or maybe she knew exactly; she had that kind of aim. Two years since the concert in Vienna, since his left hand had simply stopped answering mid-Brahms, since eight hundred people had watched him sit frozen on stage while his career politely excused itself from the room. He had never told anyone the full truth of that night. He wasn't going to start beside a bread oven.

"Careful," he said lightly. "That almost sounded like insight."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly flour."

"And exhaustion."

"Oh, exhaustion especially." She stretched her legs toward the warmth, and for a moment her shoulder rested against his—briefly, accidentally, in the way of small rooms and big fires. Neither of them moved away. That was the remarkable part. Neither of them moved away.

"Do you ever miss it?" she asked quietly. "Before?"

"Which before?"

"Whatever before you're not talking about."

Petra looked into the fire. "Every day," he said. "Like a phantom limb. Itching where nothing is anymore."

Astrid didn't offer sympathy. That was the thing about her—he was learning this—that she never offered the cheap version of comfort. She just nodded slowly, as if filing it under true, and refilled his mug, and let the fire talk for a while.

* * *

The moment, when it came, arrived the way weather does—gradually and then all at once.

She had reached past him for the last heel of the first loaf, and their hands collided on the crust, and neither withdrew, and suddenly the space between their faces had become a country he was standing in without a visa. Her eyes were gray-green in the firelight, flecked with gold like something expensive. A smudge of flour sat on her cheekbone. His hand rose without authorization.

"Petra," she said. Not Underhill. *Petra.* First time. It sounded different in her mouth—softer, like a note played piano instead of forte.

"Astrid," he answered, because it seemed only polite to return fire.

Her chin tilted up a degree. The fire popped. Somewhere in his chest, an instrument long out of tune began, treacherously, to sound.

And then his phone rang.

Not a gentle ring. The landlord's ringtone—Petra had never changed it from the factory default, a brassy fanfare that announced itself like a cruise director. The spell shattered like a dropped glass. Astrid jerked back as though scalded, and Petra fumbled the phone out of his pocket, hating it with his entire soul.

"Hello?"

"Petra! Thank God. Power's out everywhere, I'm calling to check the building—" Mr. Okafor's voice, warm and oblivious, narrating insurance concerns, generator advice, a story about the flood of '09. Petra said "mm" and "yes" and "of course" while Astrid stood, gathered mugs, and became extremely busy with a dish towel.

When he finally hung up, she was already halfway to the stairs.

"Bread's on the rack," she said, not turning around. "Wine's yours. Thanks for the flue."

"Astrid—"

"Goodnight, Underhill." And she was gone, her footsteps climbing fast, the door above shutting with a click that managed to convey an entire paragraph.

Petra sat alone by the dying fire with two mugs and forty dollars' worth of perfect bread and the distinct sensation of having been interrupted by God personally, who apparently took sides in real estate disputes.

* * *

He did not sleep. Instead, at midnight, he sat at his aunt's old desk with a pen and the cream-colored paper of the pen-pal program, and wrote:

*Dear Stranger,*

*There was a power outage tonight. I fixed a chimney. I ate bread that could make a grown man weep, in a kitchen lit by firelight, with someone who talks like a whetstone and laughs like she's breaking her own rule.*

*I nearly kissed her.*

*I'm telling you this because you don't exist. Because you're paper, and paper doesn't raise an eyebrow, and if I say it aloud anywhere else in this town it will reach her ears inside of an hour, probably embellished.*

*Here's what frightens me: I've spent two years practicing not wanting things. It's a discipline, like scales. You do it daily and eventually the fingers stop reaching. But tonight my hand moved before I authorized it, and now the whole regimen feels like what it always was—not healing, just hiding with good posture.*

*Someone said that to me tonight. Hiding with good posture. I hated her for it. I wrote it down the moment I got upstairs.*

*Do you ever meet a person and feel your whole careful architecture creak?*

*Yours,*
*S.*

He read it over. Every line was a confession wearing a trench coat. He should soften it. He should cut the part about the kiss, the part about the creaking architecture, the part where he'd quoted her to her own anonymous self.

He sealed the envelope instead, and put it in the outgoing pile, and lay down, and stared at the ceiling until the storm wore itself out.

* * *

Across the hall of the same building—in the apartment Astrid rented from the same landlord, a fact the universe had arranged with its usual heavy hand—she sat cross-legged on her bed with a lamp balanced on a stack of cookbooks, the power still out, writing by actual candlelight like a Victorian ghost.

*Dear Stranger,*

*Tonight I got trapped in my own kitchen by a storm with the world's most infuriating man, and I need you to understand that I am reporting this as a complaint.*

*He fixed the flue. He complained the entire time and then fixed it anyway. He ate my bread and said nothing for a full minute, which from him is a standing ovation. We drank terrible wine out of mugs and he said something about phantom limbs that I haven't been able to shake, like a song stuck in your head except it's a person stuck in your chest.*

*We almost kissed. There. I said it. To you, who are legally required to be fictional.*

*And then his phone rang and he answered it, and I stood there feeling like a fool in a novel, and I left, and now I'm writing to a stranger about a man I can't stand, and the worst part—the genuinely humiliating part—is that I don't know which sentence in this letter is the lie.*

*The man I can't stand. Or the rest of it.*

*Tell me what to do. You're the only person I know who doesn't want anything from me. Isn't that funny? My most honest relationship is with someone whose name I don't know.*

*A.*

She stopped. Read it back. Her pen hovered over the paragraph about the kiss for a long time.

She thought about crossing it out. About how a letter was a door you couldn't unopen, how the librarian would deliver this to some random soul in Bellhaven who might be anyone—might be Mrs. Castellano, might be the boy at the hardware store, might be—

She thought, briefly, absurdly, of Petra's phrasing at dinner last week, *hiding with good posture*, and felt a strange shiver she chose not to examine.

"No," she said aloud to the empty room. "Absolutely not. The universe isn't that cruel."

She addressed the envelope, sealed it, and set it on the sill for morning delivery, right beside the window where the storm was finally letting go of the town, and where—two floors down and one wall over—another sealed letter sat in the dark, saying almost exactly the same thing.
