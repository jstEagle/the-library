## 11. The Forged Signature

The box arrived on a Tuesday, which Astrid would later think of as the last ordinary Tuesday of her life.

It sat on the counter of the half-built kitchen like a gift—kraft paper, twine, a card in Marcus Hale's looping, expensive handwriting: *Everything you need to close. Welcome to the big leagues, Chef V.*

She'd been up since four, as usual, though there was no oven to bake in yet, so "up" meant standing at the steel prep table she'd bought secondhand from a defunct diner in Coos Bay, sharpening knives that had nothing to cut, drinking coffee that had gone bitter an hour ago, and staring at the wall where the pass-through window would eventually be. The drywall was up. The plumbing was roughed in. The hood system was scheduled for Friday. She was six weeks from opening, if everything held.

*If everything held.* That phrase had started following her around like a stray dog ever since Marcus Hale walked into her life with his camel coat and his easy laugh and his checkbook.

She untied the twine with a paring knife because scissors were somewhere under the sawdust, and lifted out the closing packet with both hands, the way you'd lift something holy or explosive. Contracts, disclosures, the financing summary, the title work. Three hundred pages, give or take, tabbed and initialed where her lawyer said to initial. Her lawyer, Deb Okafor, was thorough in the way of women who had been burned before and had decided never again, and she'd flagged every page with a sticky note: *Read this. No, actually read it.*

Astrid read them. That was the thing people didn't understand about her—that the sharp tongue came packaged with a stubborn, unglamorous patience. She could reduce a food critic to tears in four sentences, but she could also sit for three hours reading boilerplate about escrow accounts, because her aunt Rosalind had raised her on two commandments: *never serve anything you wouldn't eat yourself*, and *never sign anything you haven't read twice*.

Aunt Rosalind. Who had left her the building. Who had been dead eleven years and still managed, from beyond whatever grave she occupied, to be the most sensible person in Astrid's life.

Astrid was halfway through the title abstract, coffee gone fully cold, when she found it.

It was on page 214, in a schedule of prior conveyances—a list of everyone who had ever owned or sold interest in the parcels making up the bakery property. Standard stuff. Her name, obviously. Before her, Rosalind Voss, 2009, inheritance from her husband. Before that—

Astrid stopped.

Before that, there was a conveyance dated March 2014. A sale of the rear parcel—the strip behind the bakery where the old loading dock was, the bit of land she'd planned to pave for a patio. Sold by *Rosalind Voss* to a holding company whose name Astrid didn't recognize, for a sum that was insultingly small.

Astrid set down the pages very carefully, the way you do when your hands want to shake and you refuse to let them.

Her aunt had died in January of 2014.

She read it again. The date didn't change. March 14, 2014. Two months after the funeral. Two months after Astrid had stood in the rain outside St. Brigid's watching a casket go into ground that was already freezing over, while half of Bellhaven showed up because Rosalind Voss's cinnamon rolls had gotten them through three divorces and one flood.

And there, at the bottom of the conveyance record, was the signature. She'd know it anywhere. She'd grown up watching it at the top of grocery lists and birthday cards and the recipe cards that lined her own kitchen now. *Rosalind Voss*, in that slanted, confident hand, the R always looking like it was about to walk off the page.

Except it wasn't quite right.

Astrid got up, went to the office in the back—still technically a closet, still full of boxes—and dug out the tin where she kept the letters. Real ones, not pen-pal ones. Aunt Rosalind's last letter to her, written three weeks before the stroke, full of complaints about her knees and instructions about the sourdough starter and a postscript that said, *Don't let that Hale boy's father talk you into selling the back lot. It's worth more than he thinks.*

Astrid hadn't thought about that postscript in years. Now it rang in her ears like a fire alarm.

She laid the letter beside the photocopied signature page and compared them under the desk lamp. The R. The slant. The way the *oss* trailed off. Whoever had done this was good. They'd practiced. But Rosalind had broken her right wrist in 2011, badly, and after that her signature had changed—the crossbar of the *t* in Voss had shortened, gotten cramped, a little hitch of pain preserved in ink forever. Every letter after that had it. This forgery had the long, easy crossbar of a woman with two working wrists, signing from memory. Signing from a birthday card from 2008.

Astrid stood in her closet-office for a long moment, breathing.

Then she did what she always did when the world handed her something unbearable: she put it down, picked up something manageable, and worked until her brain caught up. She washed dishes that were already clean. She reorganized the spice shelf by height instead of alphabet. She texted Deb Okafor: *Can you look at something today? Not hypothetical. Actual problem.*

Deb called back in twenty minutes. "Send me photos of everything," she said, after Astrid talked for five minutes without stopping. "Don't sign anything else. Don't confront anyone."

"Define 'anyone.'"

"Astrid."

"Deb."

A pause. Deb Okafor had known Astrid since they were nine, when Deb had defended her in the schoolyard against Tommy Lindqvist and lost a tooth doing it. She knew exactly how well "don't" worked on Astrid Voss, which was to say she knew better than to waste her breath pretending.

"At least wait until I've looked at it," Deb said. "Twenty-four hours. If I'm right about what this is, we do this properly, with the county recorder and a forensic document examiner and all the boring machinery that makes things stick. If you storm in there tonight, he lawyers up and we've got nothing but your word and a dead woman's wrist."

"I hate that you're right."

"I know. Send the photos."

* * *

She sent the photos. Then she made bread, because her hands needed occupation and her heart needed rhythm, and kneading dough was the only prayer she'd ever learned. She worked the sourdough—Rosalind's starter, thirty-one years old, older than some of her knives—and let the town's gossip come to her, because it always did. The bakery's front room was still closed for renovation, but she'd set up a folding table by the door selling loaves and coffee to whoever wandered in, and Bellhaven wandered in constantly, because Bellhaven would find a reason to talk to you even if you sold nothing but gravel.

By noon she knew three things. One: Marcus Hale had been seen having lunch at the Anchor with Councilman Petrossian, laughing, ordering the good scotch. Two: the permit office downtown had suddenly developed a mysterious backlog regarding her kitchen inspections. And three—delivered by Mrs. Okonkwo from the hardware store, who considered herself the town's central nervous system—"That nice young man upstairs from you, the cellist? He was asking Ed about property records yesterday. County annex, not the library. Funny thing to be curious about, isn't it?"

Funny indeed. Petra Underhill, who barely spoke, who moved through the world like a man trying not to disturb the furniture, asking about property records. Astrid filed it away next to the other oddities—the way he'd looked at her that morning in the flour-dusted sweatpants incident, like he'd been caught doing something tender; the letter in his beautiful handwriting that she'd once glimpsed and couldn't stop thinking about; the strange, specific tenderness of her anonymous correspondent, who wrote about exhaustion like a man who had been publicly destroyed by it.

She wasn't ready to line those things up yet. She told herself she wasn't. Some suspicions are load-bearing walls—you can't pull them out to inspect them without risking the whole house.

* * *

Marcus Hale found her at four o'clock, which told her he'd heard she was asking questions, which told her someone in town reported to him, which was its own small horror.

He came through the side door with the confidence of a man who had never once been unwelcome anywhere, carrying a bottle of wine—something French, something with a label that probably cost more than her first car. "Chef Voss. I heard you got the packet." He set the wine on the prep table without asking. "I wanted to deliver it personally, but my assistant beat me to it. She's efficient. Terrifyingly so."

"It arrived."

"And?" He smiled. He had a good smile. Astrid had noticed that the first day—it was the smile of a man who had learned early that being liked was cheaper than being honest. "Any questions? Closing is a formality at this point. You're going to have the best-funded kitchen between here and Portland."

"I have one question."

"Name it."

Astrid wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, giving herself the extra second. "Page 214."

Something crossed his face. It was fast—faster than most people could catch—but Astrid spent her life reading faces across a pass window, catching the flicker of disappointment before a customer lied and said the dish was fine. She caught it.

"What about it?" he said pleasantly.

"The rear parcel. The March 2014 conveyance. My aunt sold that lot two months after she died."

Hale laughed. Actually laughed, a warm, rueful sound, like she'd told him a charming joke. "Clerical error. These title companies, I swear—they're copying records forward from old databases, dates get garbled. It'll be corrected before closing, I promise you. I'll have my people fix it this week."

"The date isn't the problem, Marcus. The signature is."

The laugh died. Not all at once—he let it fade gracefully, like a host turning down music when the conversation turns serious. He leaned against the prep table, and for the first time since she'd met him, he looked at her without performing. What was underneath was worse than the performance. It was patient.

"Your aunt," he said, "was a lovely woman. I remember her. My father tried to buy that lot from her for fifteen years. She told him no in more creative ways than anyone I've ever met. Once she mailed him a pie with *NO* baked into the crust in raisins." He shook his head, almost fond. "So believe me, Chef, I understand the sentimental attachment. But the record shows the sale happened. If there's a discrepancy in the signature, that's unfortunate, but these things get litigated for years, and meanwhile permits stall, investors walk, contractors file liens. Buildings rot while lawyers bill." He picked up the wine and turned it in his hands, studying the label. "You're six weeks from opening. Maybe seven, given the permit backlog—I hear downtown is swamped. How's your runway?"

"My runway is none of your business."

"It's entirely my business. I'm the investor." He set the bottle down again and straightened, and his voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Here's what I actually came to say, Astrid. May I call you Astrid? Thank you. Here's what I came to say: towns like this run on reputation. Yours is excellent. *Beloved chef returns home, rebuilds grandmother's bakery*—it's a beautiful story. People repeat beautiful stories. But stories are fragile. One bad chapter and nobody remembers the beginning." He smiled again, softer now. "Take your upstairs neighbor. Lovely man. Tragic story. Everyone in town knows it, even if they pretend they don't—the great Petra Underhill, hands that failed him in front of eight hundred people. Do you know what I hear, when I ask around? I hear a man who drinks. I hear a man who was difficult with conductors. I hear rumors he froze because he was using, or because he was unstable, or because he never deserved the career in the first place. None of it may be true. All of it is repeatable."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed and gave up.

"Why," Astrid said carefully, "are you telling me about my tenant?"

"Because you talk about him." Hale shrugged, apologetic, a man delivering bad news he wished he didn't have to. "To your friends. To your pen pal, apparently—which is sweet, by the way, the whole winter letters program, very Hallmark. The librarian runs it, doesn't she? Mrs. Alvarez?" He watched Astrid's face, and whatever he saw there, he catalogued it; she felt it happen, felt herself become information. "Small town. People mention things. Your correspondent sounds like quite a writer, from what I gather. Very... perceptive about men with perfect documents."

The floor did not move, but Astrid felt it tilt anyway.

Only three people knew what her pen pal had written her about Marcus Hale. Herself. The librarian, who sealed and sorted the mail and had never once in forty years betrayed a confidence. And Petra Underhill—who had stood in her kitchen ten days ago, flour on his sweatpants, and said, unprompted, in that low careful voice, *"Men with immaculate paperwork are usually hiding the mess somewhere else."*

She had dismissed it. She had told herself it was coincidence, that the whole town distrusted smooth outsiders, that Petra simply had the same instinct she did. She had tested him once, casually, with a detail from her letters—the bit about her mother's cast-iron skillet—and watched him go carefully blank in a way that careful people go blank, and she had said nothing, because saying something would make it real, and because some part of her had wanted to protect the one person who wrote to her like she mattered.

Now Marcus Hale was quoting her private letters back to her in her own kitchen.

"You went through her mail," Astrid said. Her voice came out flat and cold, which surprised her; she'd expected it to shake.

"I went to lunch," Hale said mildly. "People tell me things. That's my entire skill set, if I'm honest. I listen, and then I remember." He pushed off the table and buttoned his coat, unhurried, a man leaving a dinner party he'd enjoyed. "Here's my advice, free of charge. Sign the closing documents. Keep the timeline moving. Don't go hunting through conveyance schedules for problems that a clerical correction will resolve amicably. Because if this gets contentious—if lawyers get involved, if hearings happen—then everything becomes discoverable. Everything. Letters, reputations, histories. And I would hate for this town to learn things about people you care about that aren't true, just because you were angry about a date on a form."

He picked up the wine and pressed it into her hands before she could refuse it. His fingers were warm and dry and perfectly steady.

"The wine's a Barolo," he said. "Drink it when you close. Celebrate." At the door he paused, and glanced back at her with genuine, horrible warmth. "Oh—one more thing. Your neighbor. The cellist. If he comes to you with any concerns about our arrangement—any at all—you might ask yourself why a man with nothing to lose is suddenly so interested in what you stand to lose." He tapped the doorframe twice, a little goodbye knock. "Men like that will trade anything for a chance at their old life, Astrid. I've seen it before. It's sad, really. But it's human."

Then he was gone, and the door swung shut behind him, and Astrid stood alone in her half-built kitchen holding a bottle of wine worth more than a week of her revenue, shaking—not her hands, she refused that, but something underneath her hands, some deeper structural element, a foundation crack.

* * *

She didn't cry. She cooked. It was nearly midnight when she finally sat down on an overturned drywall bucket with a bowl of the plainest pasta she knew how to make—garlic, oil, salt, nothing to hide behind—and let herself think.

He knew about the letters. Not guessed—*knew*. Which meant either the librarian had talked, which Astrid refused to believe, or Petra had. And if Petra had told Marcus Hale what his pen pal wrote to her, then the man who wrote her those letters—the ones she'd read forty times, the one that said *you strike me as a woman who has fed everyone else her whole life and forgotten she's allowed to be hungry*—that man had sold her out to the investor trying to take her kitchen.

Or.

Or he was being squeezed. Or he was scared. Or there was a version of this where Petra Underhill, who flinched at loud noises and fixed her oven at 3 a.m. and played nothing for two years, had been offered something he wanted so badly he couldn't breathe, and hadn't yet said yes, and hadn't yet said no, and was sitting upstairs right now in the dark with his ruined hands and his unsent letters, drowning slowly.

Astrid looked at the ceiling, as if she could see through the joists into his apartment.

"Don't you dare," she said quietly, to the ceiling, to him, to whichever of them needed to hear it. "Don't you dare be who he says you are."

Upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked. Someone pacing. Or someone standing very still by a window, listening to a woman in an empty kitchen talk to a ceiling.

Astrid washed her bowl, dried it, and put it away. Then she took out her stationery—the good paper, the paper she saved—and began a letter to her stranger, because tomorrow Deb would call with the forensic examiner's number, and the county recorder would open at nine, and the war would start whether she was ready or not. But tonight, she had one more letter to write, and she found, as the pen touched the page, that her hand was steady after all.

*Dear Stranger,* she wrote. *Tell me something true. Tonight. Anything. I'll know if it isn't.*
