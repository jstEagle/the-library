## 12. Intermission

The envelope Marcus Hale left on the bakery counter was cream-colored, heavy stock, and smelled faintly of money—the way expensive paper always does, like a bank vault that had been taught cologne.

Petra didn't open it for six hours.

He told himself this was strategy. Deliberation. The measured pace of a man weighing an offer on its merits. In truth it was the same thing he'd been doing for two years: standing at the edge of a high dive, inventing reasons to check the water temperature.

He finally opened it at the kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold beside him, because tea was what his aunt Ruth would have poured, and because he needed her ghost sitting in one of those mismatched chairs more than he wanted to admit.

The contract was beautiful. That was the first thing he noticed. Beautiful documents were Hale's signature move—clean typography, generous margins, clauses arranged like place settings at a dinner party where you already knew which fork to use. *The Voss Masterclass Series in Chamber Music.* Funded in full. Venues in Vienna, Amsterdam, Lyon. His name above the title, not as a performer—he wasn't ready to be a performer, maybe never would be—but as artistic director. Curator. The man who chose who got heard.

It was, in its way, more than his old life had ever offered him. His old life had offered applause. This offered *authority*. A seat at the table instead of a chair on the stage.

All he had to do was sign.

And then he read clause fourteen, subsection C, and the tea went cold in him too.

*The undersigned affirms, as a material condition of this agreement, his willingness to provide testimony, should it be requested, regarding any knowledge of irregularities pertaining to property transactions associated with the premises known as 14 Harbor Lane, including but not limited to the awareness of said irregularities by parties operating said premises.*

He read it three times. It was written in the language lawyers use when they want to say something terrible without getting their fingerprints on it. But underneath the varnish, the meaning sat there plain as a dead fish on a dock:

*You will tell them Astrid knew.*

*You will swear that Astrid Voss knew her investor's paperwork was forged—that she knowingly built her kitchen on someone else's stolen signature—and your name, your famous ruined honest name, will be the stamp that makes it stick.*

Petra set the pages down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

* * *

He hadn't said yes. That was the thing he kept returning to, pacing the apartment at midnight with the floorboards announcing him to the whole building. He hadn't signed anything. He'd told Hale he needed time—"a few days," he'd said, in a voice he barely recognized, a voice that belonged to a man negotiating rather than refusing—and Hale had smiled the smile of a fisherman watching the line bend and said, *Of course. Take all the time you need. Though I should mention the offer has a shelf life.*

Everything about Hale had a shelf life. Including, Petra suspected, people.

But here was the knife of it, the part he couldn't stop turning over: for one full, shameful minute—maybe two, if he was honest, and honesty was apparently a muscle he'd have to rebuild from nothing—he had *considered* it. He had stood in Hale's rented car outside the marina and let himself imagine Vienna. Imagine walking into a rehearsal hall and having people stand up. Imagine being Petra Underhill again instead of the man who froze mid-Brahms in front of two thousand people while his hands turned to stranger's hands.

Two minutes. That was how long he'd been for sale before his conscience caught up.

And now Astrid was downstairs, sawing through a load-bearing wall, trusting him—or not trusting him, he honestly couldn't tell anymore—with whatever it was they were becoming. And he was up here holding a document that required him to choose between the only two things left in his life that still made sound.

* * *

The trouble with avoiding someone in a building with one staircase is physics. There is no third option. So when Petra came down the next morning—ostensibly to fix the radiator valve that had been clanking like a ghost with opinions—he was doing so knowing full well she'd be there.

She was standing at the counter with blueprints spread out and a pencil behind her ear, and she looked up, and for one unguarded second her face did something warm. Then it remembered itself and went back to flint.

"Radiator," he said.

"I know. It sounds like it's dying."

"It's air in the line. It's fine." He crouched by the valve with his wrench and immediately understood why lying to her in person was impossible: she watched. Not idly—she watched the way he used to watch conductors, cataloguing every hesitation for later use.

"You were at the marina yesterday," she said. "With Marcus Hale."

The wrench slipped. Not badly, but enough.

"He told you?"

"He tells everyone everything. It's called charm." She tapped her pencil against the blueprint. Twice. "What did he want?"

"Nothing important."

The silence that followed had texture. He could feel her deciding whether to press, and he could feel—worse—that some part of her had already decided something else entirely. People who grew up around liars developed sonar. He'd seen it in musicians who'd survived bad orchestras; they could hear a false note before it sounded.

"Huh," she said, and went back to her blueprints, and the *huh* was worse than an interrogation.

He bled the radiator. The clanking stopped. Neither of them mentioned the letter program, or the outage, or the bread, or the wine, or the fact that they had stood four inches apart in the dark ten days ago and he had thought about kissing her with a completeness that frightened him. They were both very good at not mentioning things. It was, he realized, their entire relationship architecture: two people building a house out of everything they refused to say.

"Your pen pal," she said suddenly, not looking up. "Still writing?"

His stomach dropped through the floor joists.

"Why do you ask?"

"No reason." Pencil tap. "Mine's gone quiet lately. Shorter letters. Like he's distracted." She finally looked at him, and her eyes were doing arithmetic. "I worry about strangers."

*Don't*, he almost said. *Don't look here.* But he'd stalled once already today, and stalling has a momentum all its own, and so he said, "Some people are just busy," and watched her face close like a shop pulling down its shutters.

"Sure," she said. "Busy."

* * *

That afternoon he walked to the county records office—not to do anything, just to see the building, which was a stupid thing to do and he did it anyway. It was a squat brick building with a flagpole out front and a notice board by the door. Halfway down the board, pinned under a plastic sleeve, was a public notice of hearing: *In re: alleged irregularities, parcel 118-C, Harbor Lane.* Date: eleven days out.

Eleven days. Hale's shelf life, expiring right on schedule.

An older man in a canvas jacket was reading the same notice, lips moving slightly. When Petra stepped closer, the man glanced at him and said, "Voss girl's kitchen. Shame. Best thing to happen to this street in twenty years."

"You know her?"

"Everybody knows Astrid." He squinted at Petra. "You're the nephew. Ruthie's boy. You play the cello."

"I used to."

"That's not what I asked." The man nodded at the notice. "Whatever they're saying about her, it's not true. I sold my boat shop lot to her aunt in '09. Clean as anything. If somebody's got papers saying different, somebody made them." He said it simply, the way people state weather, and walked off, leaving Petra standing in front of the notice board with his heart hammering.

Because that was the other half of clause fourteen, wasn't it. It wasn't just that testifying against Astrid would destroy her. It was that the truth—the actual truth, the one the boat-shop man carried around in his head like a spare key—was sitting right there, available, free. All Petra had to do was walk into that hearing and say what he'd seen: Hale in the bakery, sliding the folder across the flour-dusted counter, saying *signatures are such old-fashioned technology.* Say the bribe. Say it all, under oath, with his name attached.

And lose Vienna. Lose Amsterdam. Lose the masterclass series with his name above the title. Lose the last exit ramp off the road his life had been stuck on for two years.

Or sign, testify, and watch her face in the courtroom when he did it.

There was no version of clause fourteen where he kept his hands clean. That was the design. Hale hadn't built a trap; he'd built a mirror, and put Petra in front of it, and waited to see which man looked back.

* * *

He wrote to her that night. His pen pal letters had gotten shorter lately—he knew it, she'd said it, the guilt of it sat in his chest like a swallowed stone—but tonight the words came fast and crooked, and he let them.

*Dear Stranger,*

*I've been thinking about what courage actually costs. We talk about it like it's free—like brave people just reach down and find it somewhere, no receipt. But I think most courage is a purchase, and the price is always something you can't get back. You pay with a version of yourself you were hoping to keep.*

*I'm holding something I shouldn't be holding. Someone offered me the thing I want most in the world, and the price is a lie about someone I—*

He stopped. Pen hovering. The sentence had a cliff edge in it and he could feel the drop.

*—about someone I care about. And the worst part isn't the choice. The worst part is that I hesitated. Two minutes. Maybe three. Long enough to be ashamed of. What do you call a person who hesitates? Is there forgiveness for the pause? Or does the pause count?*

*Tell me what to do. You're the only person whose answer I'd believe.*

He read it back. It was the most honest thing he'd written anyone—including her, face to face, ever—and he was mailing it anonymously to a woman who might already suspect who he was, asking her to save him from a decision he was too cowardly to make out loud.

He sealed the envelope. Then he took it to the mailbox at the corner of Harbor and Pike, and stood there with the little door open, and did not mail it.

He walked home with it inside his coat, over his heart, like a letter bomb he'd defused by keeping it.

* * *

Astrid found him two mornings later.

Not in the kitchen—she'd stopped expecting him in the kitchen—but on the back steps, where he sat with his tea watching gulls argue over something in the tide line. She came out the kitchen door in her apron, flour to the elbows, and stood over him with her arms crossed, and he knew before she spoke.

"The hearing notice went up," she said. "Parcel 118-C. My aunt's parcel. The one I never sold because it was never mine to sell, because my aunt never sold it, because—" Her voice snagged and she hauled it back. "Hale filed forged papers, Petra. Real ones, notarized, with my aunt's signature on them. And the county clerk says the hearing is in nine days and my permits are frozen until then."

"I know. I saw the notice."

"And here's what I keep coming back to." She sat down on the step above him, which somehow made it worse; now she was talking to the back of his head. "Hale approached you. At the marina. Before any of this went public. And you came home and said *nothing important,* and you've been walking around ever since like a man carrying a piano upstairs." She paused. "So I'm going to ask you one question, and I want you to look at me when you answer it."

He turned. Her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and terrified, and underneath all of it, still—God help them both—hopeful.

"What did he offer you?"

And here was the moment. Here was the entire chapter of his life reduced to a single hinge. Nine words' worth of truth would burn the bridge to Vienna forever and probably get him laughed out of the county records office, and would also—might also—save her. All he had to do was say: *He offered me a masterclass series with my name on it if I'd swear you knew about the forgery.*

Instead, what came out of his mouth was: "Astrid—"

"Don't." She was already standing, already retreating toward the door, and her face was doing the closing-shutters thing again, only this time all the way down. "You know what the worst part is? I wouldn't even care about the offer. Take the money. Take Europe. Take whatever he dangled. I'd think less of you, but I'd understand—you lost everything, I get hungry for things too." Her hand found the door handle. "What I can't survive is watching you decide. Standing here watching you weigh me against your comeback like I'm a line item. Because I've read enough letters to know what honesty looks like, Petra, and it doesn't look like *nothing important.*"

"Astrid, wait—"

The door shut. Not slammed. Shut, quietly, which from Astrid Voss was the sound of a verdict.

Petra sat on the steps with his cold tea and the unsent letter burning a hole through his coat lining, and understood, with terrible clarity, that he had just done the one thing worse than choosing wrong.

He had refused to choose at all—and she had counted the seconds.

Inside the apartment, on the kitchen table, Hale's cream-colored contract waited with a pen beside it, because Petra had left the pen there deliberately, the way you leave a loaded question.

Nine days.
