## 13. Cold Oven

Astrid had learned to read ovens the way other people read faces. A cold oven told her everything she needed to know about a person's character. A cold oven meant someone had walked away mid-promise—left the dough to die on the peel, left the proofing basket abandoned under cloth, left the whole small architecture of a morning's work to collapse in on itself.

She was thinking about cold ovens at six in the morning because the bakery's old deck oven, the one she'd spent three weeks coaxing back to life, the one that had finally held temperature like a promise kept, was cold. Stone dead. And the reason it was dead was standing in her kitchen doorway in a coat that cost more than her entire knife roll, looking like a man who had rehearsed an apology in the shower and then forgotten every word of it on the stairs.

"You're up early," Petra said.

"I'm always up early. I'm a chef. We're like vampires with better skin." She didn't look at him. She kept her hands busy with the mise en place she didn't need to do—fanning out shallots, aligning the knife parallel to the board's edge, small precise movements that kept her from doing something with her hands she'd regret. Or wouldn't regret. That was the problem. She couldn't tell anymore which impulses were the ones to distrust.

"Astrid."

"Don't." The word came out flat as a sheet pan. "Don't say my name like that. Like you're the one who gets to be gentle right now."

He came into the kitchen anyway, because of course he did. That was the whole trouble with Petra Underhill: he moved through the world like a man who had already been forgiven for everything, not out of arrogance but out of some deep, ruined conviction that nothing he did could matter much either way. It had been charming once. It had been charming for weeks. She'd catalogued the charm the way she catalogued flavors—smoke, salt, something sweet underneath, something burnt at the edges.

"I heard about the permits," he said. "The eviction notice. I came down as soon as—"

"As soon as you could." She set down the knife. Carefully. The way her aunt had taught her to set down a knife when she wanted to throw it. "Funny thing about that. Hale's lawyer filed papers Thursday. The hearing's Monday. And you—" she finally turned, and let him have the full weight of her looking at him, "—you've been upstairs the whole time. I watched your kitchen light go on and off. On and off. Like a man deciding something."

"I wasn't deciding anything."

"Petra." She laughed, and it came out wrong, too sharp, the laugh of a woman who has just found something spoiled in the back of the walk-in. "You're a terrible liar. You know that? It's actually remarkable. A man with your face should never have attempted dishonesty. Your face files a complaint every time."

Something flickered across that face—the complaint being filed, presumably—and she hated how much she wanted to read it, how much she had trained herself on his expressions these past months the way she'd trained herself on the oven's moods. The twitch at his left eye when he was amused. The way his jaw set when he was protecting something. The way his hands, those famous hands, went quiet in his pockets when he was frightened.

His hands were in his pockets now.

"Hale came to see you," she said. It wasn't a question. It had stopped being a question three days ago, when she'd found Hale's card on the bakery's front step, face down, like something a cat had dragged in and abandoned. "He came to see you and you didn't tell me."

"He came to see me," Petra admitted. "Yes."

"And?"

"And it's complicated."

"Everything's complicated. Sourdough is complicated. Love is complicated. *Complicated* is what people say when they're choosing not to be honest." She heard herself say *love* and watched it land on him like a dropped pan—heard it land on herself, too, a dull ringing ache—and refused, absolutely refused, to pick it up or apologize for it. "I have spent my whole adult life around men who think charm is a substitute for integrity. I built a kitchen to get away from them. I let you in anyway. That's on me. But I will not stand in my own kitchen and watch you do the thing where you go quiet and dignified and noble about it, like silence is some kind of virtue. Silence is just silence, Petra. It's what's underneath it that counts."

The kitchen was very quiet. The pilot light on the range hissed its small constant complaint. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked, the building's old bones settling, and Astrid thought absurdly that the building had better instincts than either of them—it knocked when something was wrong.

"You think I did something," Petra said slowly.

"I think you were offered something." She crossed her arms. "I think you were offered the thing you want most in the world, and it came wrapped in paper with my name on it, and you didn't say no fast enough. And now there's a forged signature on a property deed and my permits are suspended and you can't look at me, which, for the record, you're currently failing at, because you're looking at the flour bin."

He looked at the flour bin. Then, with visible effort, at her. "Astrid, I swear to you—"

"Don't swear. Swearing is for people whose word means something."

That one landed. She saw it land—saw the exact place in him it hit, some load-bearing wall she'd been leaning on without knowing it was there. He went pale in the particular way of a man who is being punished for a crime he hasn't committed yet, or has, or is about to; she couldn't tell, and the not-telling was its own answer.

"I have been honest with you," he said, and his voice had gone quiet in that way that used to undo her, low and careful, each word set down like glassware. "From the beginning. Everything I—everything between us has been honest."

"Has it." She picked the knife back up. Not to use it. To have something in her hands that had a clear purpose. "Then tell me what Hale offered you. Right now. Plain words, no cello-player poetry, no looking out the window at the harbor like the answer's going to sail in. What did he offer you?"

The silence stretched.

It stretched and stretched, and Astrid stood in it and let it, because she had learned this much in her forty-one years: you could tell everything about a person by what they wouldn't say in the first thirty seconds. The truth came out fast or it didn't come out at all. Everything else was marinade.

Petra's mouth opened. Closed. He looked at the cold oven, at the eviction notice she'd pinned to the walk-in door like a dartboard, at the half-built wall where the new hearth oven would go—*would* go, she corrected herself, *will* go, because she had not lost yet and she refused to use the past tense about her own kitchen.

"I can't," he said.

"You can't."

"There are things I can't tell you right now. Not because—" His hands came out of his pockets at last, and she hated herself for noticing them, the long fingers, the faint tremor he could never quite suppress, the hands that had made her think, in the beginning, *oh no, not this one, this one could actually break something in me.* "Not because I'm hiding. Because telling you now, in this way, would hurt you worse. I need a little time. I need—"

"You need." She nodded. "Of course. The famous Petra Underhill needs time. The whole town tiptoes around the maestro's needs. Did you know Marisol at the library still won't shelve the classical section while you're in the building? Everyone's so careful with you. Everyone handles you like you're already broken." She was saying too much. She was saying exactly as much as she meant. "I wasn't careful with you, Petra. That was the whole point. I handed you the sharp knives and the hot pans and I said, here, this is who I am, burn yourself if you're going to. And you stood there holding everything I gave you and you never once got burned, did you? Not really. Not in any way that cost you."

"You don't know what I—"

"I know your letters stopped being honest a long time ago."

The words were out before she could decide whether to say them, and the moment they were in the air she understood their weight—not the weight she'd intended, but a heavier one, a strange one, because Petra went absolutely still. Not wounded-still. *Caught*-still. The stillness of a man who has heard a word he recognizes from a room he thought was sealed.

"Letters," he said. Carefully. Too carefully. "What letters?"

And there it was. The thing she'd been sitting on since the night of the power outage, since the wine and the bread and the almost-kiss and the phone call that had sent them both scurrying to their corners like animals who'd forgotten, briefly, that they were animals. The thing she'd tested a week ago, casually, over coffee—*funny, my pen pal used that exact phrase, "grief is just love with nowhere to put itself"*—and watched him go white and say, *common phrase, probably,* with the conviction of a man reciting a line he'd rehearsed.

She had known for eleven days. She had known and said nothing, because knowing was a knife and she hadn't decided yet whether to use it or keep it sharp.

"Never mind," she said now, and was surprised to find her voice steady. "It doesn't matter."

"Astrid—"

"It doesn't *matter*." She turned back to the board. Picked up a shallot. The first cut went through clean, and she focused on that, on the papery skin and the sharp clean smell, on anything other than the sound of him not leaving. "Here's what matters. Monday there's a hearing. Hale's lawyer is going to stand up in a county records room and explain to a panel of bureaucrats why my aunt's signature on a deed from 2019 is legitimate, and everyone in that room is going to be very polite about it, and unless someone in that room knows something—*witnessed* something—my kitchen dies on the vine. So the only question I have for you, Petra, the only one, is this: did you see him? Did he offer you something? And is there anything—*anything*—you're carrying that belongs to me?"

The knife rested on the board. The shallot lay in neat crescents. The oven stayed cold.

"I can't tell you," Petra said again, and his voice cracked on *can't*, actually cracked, a hairline fracture running through the word, and some soft traitorous part of her wanted to cross the kitchen and put her hand over that crack like putty over plaster. "Not yet. Please believe that there is a version of this where I—"

"No." She said it quietly, which was worse than shouting, and she knew it was worse, and she said it quietly anyway. "Here's what I believe. I believe you're a good man standing in a burning building trying to decide which photograph to save. And I believe you've been in there so long you've forgotten the building isn't yours." She wiped her hands on her apron. "I can't do this with you, Petra. I can't stand in my own kitchen and beg a man to be honest with me. I've done that. With Marcus, with the bank, with my whole goddamn life. I'm done auditioning people for the role of someone who stays."

"I'm not Hale."

"No," she agreed. "Hale never made me want to believe him. That's what makes you so much worse."

He flinched. Good, some cold part of her said. Then the rest of her, the larger part, the part that had memorized the sound of his footsteps on her stairs, said nothing at all, because there was nothing left to say that wasn't a surrender.

Petra stood there a moment longer. She watched him decide not to speak—watched it happen in real time, the breath he took and swallowed, the sentence he built and demolished, the whole silent architecture of a man protecting something at the cost of everything else. She would think about that later, that look, that swallowed word. She would take it out and examine it in the small hours the way she examined a failed sauce, turning it, tasting it, trying to find where it had broken.

"I'll be at the hearing," he said finally. "Monday."

"Will you?" She kept her eyes on the shallots. "So will half the town. That's not the same as being *there*."

She heard him go. The door, the stairs, the street door, the bell above it that she kept meaning to oil and never did. Then the kitchen was hers, all hers, the way she'd wanted it before she'd ever met him—cold and quiet and entirely without him—and she stood in the middle of it and discovered that winning the argument felt exactly like this: like a cold oven at dawn, like dough that would never rise now, like a room that had finally stopped holding its breath.

She worked. Of course she worked. She made stock from the bones in the walk-in, because bones were honest, bones gave up exactly what they had and no more. She wrote a list on the butcher paper: *lawyer, Marisol, the Hollis brothers re: deed, call Aunt Renata's notary.* She pinned it to the wall. Under it, without deciding to, she wrote a second line, smaller:

*He said "letters." I never said letters.*

She stared at it a long time. Then she didn't cross it out.

Upstairs, through the old floorboards, she could hear him moving. Not pacing—Petra didn't pace, he *hovered*, a man built for hovering—and then, faintly, the sound she'd been waiting two years to hear and had never once heard, not through all these months of shared walls and shared bread and shared almosts.

The cello.

One note, low and wavering, like a man testing ice. Then another. Then a phrase, halting, unfinished, the musical equivalent of a letter begun and abandoned.

Astrid stood in her cold kitchen with flour on her hands and a knife within reach and listened to the sound of Petra Underhill failing to say something, again, in the only language he had left.

Monday, she thought. Two days.

She picked up her phone and dialed the county clerk, and did not look up at the ceiling, and did not listen, and heard every note.
