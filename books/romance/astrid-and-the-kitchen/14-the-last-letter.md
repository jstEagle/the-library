## 14. The Last Letter

The apartment above the bakery had never felt like his. For ninety days it had been a waiting room—his aunt's furniture, his aunt's curtains, his aunt's view of a harbor he'd learned to watch the way other men watched television. But tonight, with the lamp turned low and a fresh sheet of paper squared in front of him like an exam he intended to fail honestly, the room finally belonged to him. It was the room where he was about to ruin everything.

Petra Underhill had written forty-one letters to a stranger. He knew the number because he kept them—not hers, those lived in a shoebox under the bed that he touched more often than he'd admit to anyone, including himself—but his own drafts. The failures. The letters begun and abandoned in the blue notebook that had once held fingering diagrams for the Elgar. Forty-one finished, maybe sixty abandoned, and not one of them true.

That was the arithmetic that had kept him awake since Marcus Hale shook his hand on the bakery steps three days ago. Not the offer itself—he could have survived the offer, could have filed it away with all the other temptations that came dressed as rescue. It was how easily he'd said *let me think about it*. How his mouth, which had spent two years practicing silence, had found the words of a collaborator without any trouble at all.

*Let me think about it.* Four syllables of cowardice, and Hale had smiled like a man who'd already won.

So: the last letter.

He picked up the pen. Put it down. Picked it up again. Outside, the harbor did its patient breathing, and somewhere below him Astrid Voss was either asleep or lying awake hating him, and he genuinely could not have said which possibility hurt worse.

*Dear Stranger,* he wrote, because that was how they began, always, though neither of them was a stranger anymore and hadn't been for months.

*I have started this letter eleven times. I know because I counted, and counting is what I do instead of feeling things. You've called me out on this twice now, so consider this my formal surrender.*

He stopped. Read it back. It sounded like him—the letter-him, the one she'd laughed at and argued with and, once, in a sentence he could recite in the dark, the one she'd said she trusted. That was the problem. Everything he wrote sounded like him because it *was* him; there had never been another version. There was only the version with the important parts left out, the way you can play a piece with every note correct and still be committing fraud.

He crossed out nothing. He kept going.

*Here is what I have never told you, in the order I should have told it, which is to say immediately.*

*My name is Petra Underhill. You know this already, probably—I heard it in your voice the day you asked me about the gala, that careful flat way you said "the cellist," testing me, giving me a door to walk through. I stood in that doorway like an idiot and let it close. I'm sorry. I'm sorry for the small lie and for the eighty larger ones built on top of it, load-bearing, all of them.*

*You should also know that when you vented to me about your impossible upstairs neighbor—the man who plays nothing, speaks little, and glares at your contractors like they're personally dismantling his childhood—you were describing me to me. And I let you. Worse: I enjoyed it. There is no lower form of vanity than being flattered by someone's honest anger while hiding behind a pseudonym.*

The pen scratched. Downstairs, a pipe knocked twice, the old building settling into its bones, and he thought absurdly: *she'll need to fix that before winter.* He thought it the way he thought everything about her now—automatically, helplessly, the way his left hand still shaped chords against his thigh when he wasn't paying attention.

*Third confession. The night we got trapped in the dark kitchen with the sourdough and the terrible wine—*

He paused. His ears went hot, alone, at a desk, at midnight, over a sentence. Ridiculous. He was thirty-four years old and blushing at his own stationery.

*—when the lights came back on and I stepped away from you like the moment was something to escape: that wasn't indifference. I have replayed that half-second roughly four hundred times. In most versions I don't step back. In several of them I say something articulate. In one memorable draft of reality I am charming. You had to be there for the actual version, which featured me knocking over the corkscrew and saying "well" in a voice I have never heard myself use before or since.*

*Fourth. This is the hard one, so I'm going to write it fast, the way you pull off a bandage.*

He pulled off the bandage.

*Marcus Hale came to see me. Three days ago. He offered to fund a European masterclass series with my name on it—a return, he said. A resurrection. All I had to do was sign away my lease quietly and provide some testimony regarding whether you knew about certain signatures on certain documents. Testimony I would have been inventing, Astrid. He wanted my signature the way he wanted your aunt's: as a tool, applied where it doesn't belong.*

*And here is the part I can barely write: I didn't say no.*

*I said "let me think about it." I stood on those steps with my whole ruined career held out to me like a coat in cold weather, and I wanted it. God help me, I wanted it so badly my hands shook—and not the bad shaking, not the tremor, just want, plain and human and ugly. Two years I've told myself the silence was grief. Some of it was. But some of it was this: a man who learned that wanting things gets people hurt, and decided the safest performance was no performance at all. I didn't stop playing because my hands failed, exactly. I stopped because if I never played again, I could never fail again. It has been very quiet in here. I mistook it for peace.*

*Then you came along with your flour and your fury and your letters that read me like a difficult score, and it turns out the heart is a muscle after all, and mine has been waking up like a foot coming back from numbness—all pins and needles and pain, and alive, unbearably alive.*

*I love you.*

There it was. Three words on paper, and the room did not catch fire, and the harbor did not stop breathing, and the only sound was his own pulse deciding to make itself known in his ears. He stared at the sentence. He did not cross it out. Crossing it out would be one more small lie, and he had retired from that business approximately ten minutes ago.

*I love you, and I have loved you since roughly the third letter, when you told me exhaustion wasn't weakness but inventory, and I sat in this chair and thought: whoever this is, they are going to wreck me. I love you in person too, which is less convenient, since in person I am a coward who lets doors close and says "let me think about it" to devils. I love the way you argue with contractors. I love that you salt everything before tasting it and then complain it's salty. I love that you fixed the oven yourself rather than ask me, even though I'd already fixed it, and we have both pretended not to know this for six weeks.*

*Fifth and final confession: I don't deserve an answer to this letter. I'm not writing it to get one. I'm writing it because you taught me that honesty isn't a performance you give when it's safe—it's the thing you do with shaking hands, and then you play the next note anyway.*

*If you never speak to me again, that will be fair pricing. If you do—if by some miracle you can stand the sight of the actual man and not just the voice on paper—then I will tell you the rest in person, all of it, starting with Hale and ending wherever you want it to end.*

*Until then, I remain—not anonymously, not anymore, not ever again—*

*Yours,*

*Petra Underhill*

He sat back. The chair complained, as it always did, in his aunt's voice almost, and for a second he let himself smile at that—she would have liked Astrid. She would have liked Astrid enormously and told him so in the blunt way she'd told him everything, and she would have taken one look at this letter and said, *Petya, mail the damn thing.*

He read it through once more, hunting for exits, for softeners, for the clever deflection that had protected him for two years. There were none left. He'd used them all up. The letter was naked from the first line down, and reading it felt like standing on the concert stage again—except this time there was only one person in the hall, and she was the only audience he'd ever actually wanted.

Now: send it, or burn it.

The town's pen-pal program ran through the library, bless and curse Mrs. Okonkwo forever. Letters dropped in the box by noon were delivered by four. It was—what, nearly one in the morning? He could sleep, such as it was, and walk it down in the morning like a civilized man. Or he could take it out to the sink, hold a match to the corner, and watch two years of caution go up in useful light, and wake tomorrow as the same careful ghost he'd been when he arrived.

He weighed the envelope in his hand. It weighed nothing, of course. One page. The smallest thing he'd ever made and the heaviest.

He thought about her hands dusted white at 3 a.m., arguing with an oven. He thought about her face when she'd said *I know your letters stopped being honest long ago*—no. She hadn't said that yet. That hadn't happened. What she'd said, three days ago on the stairs, was worse in its way: nothing at all, just a look, cool and measuring, the look of a woman doing math she didn't like. She'd seen him waver. Of course she had. Astrid Voss missed nothing that happened in her kitchen, and apparently the whole town qualified.

She thinks I'm already his, he thought. She thinks I took the coat.

Which meant every hour he sat here composing perfect sentences was an hour the story wrote itself wrong. Confession delayed was confession denied; she'd taught him that without ever saying it, in the way she attacked a day, a menu, a broken pipe—now, immediately, with whatever tools were at hand.

Petra stood up too fast. The chair skidded. He grabbed his coat, shoved the letter into the inside pocket where it lay against his chest like a second heartbeat, and was at the door with his hand on the latch before he remembered—

Before he remembered that the library box wouldn't be collected until morning anyway, and that she lived twenty feet below him, and that he had spent his entire adult life delivering things through intermediaries when the person was right there.

His hand stayed on the latch. Below him, faintly, a light clicked on in the kitchen—the 3 a.m. hours were hers too, he'd learned; insomnia was the one thing they'd never needed letters to share.

*Tomorrow,* he told himself, and hated how much it sounded like *let me think about it.*

The envelope burned a hole through his coat pocket all the way back to the desk, where he set it down, weighted it under the Elgar notebook, and lay on top of the covers fully dressed, staring at the ceiling, rehearsing the twelve steps between this door and hers.

Outside, far down the coast, the first gray hint of a morning he was no longer sure he deserved began assembling itself over the water.
