## 15. Eviction Notice

The notice was taped to the bakery door at six-forty in the morning, and Astrid knew it before she read a single word because of the way the light hit it—flat, official, the particular matte white of paper that had been printed by someone with a laser printer and no soul.

She stood on the sidewalk with two coffees, one for herself and one she'd been carrying out of habit for Petra, and stared at it.

*NOTICE OF EVICTION AND SUSPENSION OF COMMERCIAL PERMITS.*

*Pursuant to filing #C-4471 with the Bellhaven County Records Office...*

Her aunt's name was on it. *Eleanor Voss, grantor.* Her aunt's signature, reproduced in a crisp little box at the bottom like a stamp on a passport, dated eleven months after Eleanor Voss had died.

Astrid set both coffees down on the windowsill very carefully, the way you do when what you actually want to do is throw them through the glass.

"Well," she said, to no one, because the street was empty except for Gus Delacroix's dog, who had followed her from the café and now sat regarding her with the solemn sympathy only dogs and librarians can manage. "Well, that's just rude."

* * *

By seven-thirty there were nine people in her kitchen, which was eight more than usually fit.

Deb from the hardware store had arrived first, still in her bathrobe, holding a cordless drill for reasons no one questioned. The Okafor twins came next, then Marisol from the fish market, then a slow accumulation of others drawn by the gravitational pull of small-town catastrophe—the same force that gathered crowds at fender-benders and bake sales, often simultaneously.

"They can't do this," Deb said, for the fourth time. She said it the way people say *it's not raining* during a flood: as if repetition might alter physics.

"They did do it," Astrid said. She had the filing spread across her prep counter, weighted down with a marble rolling pin and a bag of flour that was slowly leaking into a pale drift around the edges. "Filed Tuesday. Permits suspended pending review of 'disputed title.' Which means I can't run gas lines, can't pass inspection, can't open. And the eviction—" she tapped the notice, "—gives me thirty days to vacate the building. Both floors."

"Both floors," Marisol repeated. "Including your tenant?"

"Including my tenant." Astrid heard her own voice go flat and strange on those words. She thought of Petra's face last week, that awful shuttered look when she'd told him his letters had stopped being honest—when really she'd been begging him, in the least honest language available to either of them, to tell her something true. He hadn't. And now they were going to be evicted together, side by side, two strangers sharing a sinking ship.

She found she didn't have the energy to be angry about him this morning. That frightened her more than Hale's paperwork did. Anger at Petra had been fuel. Its absence felt like running out of wood in February.

"The signature's forged," said Tom Okafor, squinting at the document through reading glasses he wore purely for dramatic effect. "I sat next to Eleanor Voss at every potluck for twenty years. She signed her name like she was stabbing it." He pointed. "That's not a stab. That's a curtsy."

"It doesn't matter that it's forged," Astrid said. "What matters is proving it, which means a records hearing, which means—" she checked the date stamped in red at the top, "—March ninth. Six weeks. After my permits are dead and my lease is voided and Marcus Hale has already filed an application for his own renovation permits, which I guarantee are already approved, because men like Hale don't file paperwork unless they know which way the wind blows."

The kitchen went quiet. It was, Astrid reflected bitterly, the quietest her half-built kitchen had ever been. No saws, no compressor, no radio. Just the drip of the temporary sink and nine people breathing.

Then Deb picked up her drill and started unscrewing a cabinet door.

"What are you doing?" Astrid asked.

"I don't know," Deb admitted. "But standing around feels worse than drilling."

It was, Astrid would think later, the truest thing anyone said all day.

* * *

The lawyer was named Priya Raman, and she worked out of an office above the pharmacy in Millbrook, forty minutes inland, surrounded by ferns that had clearly given up on ambition. Astrid drove out that afternoon with the forged filing in a manila folder clutched like a weapon.

Priya read it twice. Made a small sound. Read it again.

"This is sloppy," she said finally. "Which is good news. Notary stamp is from a county two hundred miles away. The grantor's signature is dated post-mortem—which whoever assembled this clearly didn't notice, or didn't care, which tells me they were in a hurry. Men in a hurry make mistakes." She looked up over her glasses. "Men in a hurry with money make expensive mistakes. Either way, we can beat this. But."

"But."

"But 'we can beat this' takes time. Motion to stay the permit suspension, motion to challenge the filing, discovery, the hearing itself. Six weeks minimum if nothing goes wrong. Something will go wrong." Priya folded her hands. "In the meantime, Ms. Voss, legally speaking, you cannot operate a commercial kitchen in that building. You cannot hold events there. You cannot sell so much as a loaf of bread out the front door without handing Mr. Hale a gift-wrapped injunction."

"So I'm supposed to just stand there while he takes it."

"I'm supposed to tell you that if you fight fair, you win in March." Priya's voice gentled, which Astrid distrusted on principle. "What you do is between you and your conscience."

Astrid drove back along the coast road with the window down despite the cold, salt air scouring her face, and thought about her conscience, and about how little it had ever gotten her.

* * *

The town rallied. Of course it did. This was Bellhaven, where grief and casseroles arrived in the same car.

By Friday there was a petition at the library with two hundred signatures. By Saturday, someone—Astrid suspected Deb, whose handwriting was on every surface of the town including, once, memorably, the mayor's boat—had stenciled SAVE THE KITCHEN on a bedsheet and hung it across the bakery's second-story railing, directly beneath Petra's windows. Astrid watched him stand at the glass that morning, coffee in hand, looking down at it for a long time. She couldn't read his expression. She'd stopped being able to read his expression weeks ago, and hated how much she missed it.

Marisol organized a fundraiser at the fish market. The Okafors offered their garage as a staging area for the kitchen equipment, since Astrid couldn't legally install any of it. The librarian, Mrs. Ferro, appeared at the bakery door with a printout of county code annotated in three colors of ink and the expression of a woman who had personally destroyed better men than Marcus Hale using less.

"There's a provision," Mrs. Ferro said, tapping the page, "for hardship stays when suspension causes demonstrable community harm. We get statements. Signatures. Testimony at the hearing. You'll want character witnesses, property witnesses—" she paused, and her eyes flicked up, sharp as a tack, "—and anyone who witnessed Mr. Hale's conduct firsthand. Bribery leaves fingerprints even when paperwork doesn't."

Something cold moved through Astrid. "Why would you say bribery?"

"Because a man who forges one signature has usually offered to buy several." Mrs. Ferro closed her folder with a snap. "Ask around. Quietly."

Astrid asked around. Quietly. And what she heard made the cold thing spread: a rumor that Hale had met privately with the upstairs tenant before the filings landed. A rumor that the tenant had been seen leaving the Harbor Inn with papers under his arm. A rumor, passed from Gus Delacroix to his dog to anyone who'd listen, that the famous cellist upstairs had been offered a way back into the world, and that the price had been the kitchen below him.

She didn't believe it. She wanted very badly not to believe it.

But she remembered the way Petra had gone silent when she'd accused him of dishonesty. The way he hadn't defended himself. The way a man who had nothing to hide would have simply said *I don't know what you're talking about*, and Petra had never once said anything that simple.

* * *

Sunday night, after the fundraiser crowd went home and the petition sheets were stacked in her office and the bedsheet flapped its stupid hopeful message in the wind, Astrid stood alone in the middle of her half-built kitchen.

It looked terrible, honestly. Studs exposed where drywall waited. Wires hanging like vines. The big range sat crated in the corner where it had been delivered three weeks ago, still wrapped, still dreaming. Her aunt's old oven—the one Petra had fixed at three in the morning, flour-dusted and swearing softly, the image she'd carried around like a coal in her chest ever since—stood dark against the far wall.

She crossed to it and opened the door. Cold. Of course cold. Everything was cold now; the gas was off, part of the suspension, as if the county feared her oven might commit fraud.

"You should see this place," she said aloud, to Eleanor, to the ghost of every woman who'd ever stood in this room with burned hands and stubborn hope. "You should see what they did to it."

The kitchen didn't answer. Kitchens never did. They just held what you gave them—the smoke and the salt and the shouting and the singing—and waited to see if you'd come back.

Astrid pressed her forehead against the oven door. The metal was cool and slightly greasy and utterly familiar, and she stood there for a long time, furious and alone in exactly the way she'd promised herself years ago she'd never be again, because needing people was how you got hurt, and look, look how right she'd been—

Except.

Except Deb with the drill. Except two hundred signatures. Except Marisol donating a week of fish-market profits, and the Okafors' garage full of her equipment, and Mrs. Ferro's three-color assault on county code, and a whole town that had decided her kitchen was *their* kitchen without asking her permission first.

She wasn't alone. That was the trap of it. Being loved by a town was like being loved by the ocean—you could drown in it and still feel unsupported.

And underneath everything, quieter than the wind against the bedsheet, the other thing. The letter-shaped hole in her week. Her pen pal, who had understood exhaustion better than anyone alive, who had written her sentences she'd reread until the paper went soft—who had gone silent, or dishonest, or both, somewhere between the power outage and the forged signature, and whom she had cut off with words she'd chosen precisely because they'd wound.

*I know your letters stopped being honest long ago.*

She'd meant it as a test. A door left open. Say something true, she'd begged him silently, anything, and I'll forgive all of it.

He hadn't walked through the door. And now there were rumors, and papers under arms, and a bedsheet flapping under his window like an accusation.

Astrid straightened up. Wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, businesslike, the way she did everything.

"Six weeks," she told the kitchen. "We hold for six weeks."

From upstairs, faintly, through the old floorboards, came a sound she hadn't heard in two years and had never heard in this building: a cello. One long note, wavering, imperfect, unmistakably real. Then another. Then a phrase, halting, gathering, like a man remembering how to walk.

Astrid stood in her ruined kitchen with her arms crossed and listened to the whole trembling, unfinished thing, and could not have said, if you'd asked her, whether it filled her with hope or broke her heart clean in two.

Probably, she thought, as the last note faded into the sound of the sea, it was both. Probably that was the whole trick of this miserable, beautiful life.

Upstairs, silence again. But this time it felt less like a wall and more like a held breath.

She turned off the work lights and locked the front door behind her, past the white notice with her dead aunt's forged name on it, and she did not take the bedsheet down on her way out.

Some things, she decided, were worth leaving up.
