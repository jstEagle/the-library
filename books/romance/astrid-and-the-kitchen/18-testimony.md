## 18. Testimony

The county records hearing was held in a room that smelled of floor wax and old paper, with the kind of fluorescent lighting that made everyone look like they were testifying against their own character. There were eight folding chairs for the public, a long table for counsel, and a witness box that might once have been dignified but now resembled a large wooden lectern with delusions of grandeur.

Petra arrived forty minutes early because his hands had started shaking at six in the morning and he had learned, over two years of practice, that the only cure was to arrive before the fear did and let it find him already seated.

Astrid was not there yet.

He told himself this meant nothing. Hearings started at nine; it was twenty past eight; she was probably arguing with a delivery driver or reorganizing her spice rack by some private taxonomy only she understood. He told himself this twice, three times, and then gave up on self-deception as a discipline he'd never been good at anyway.

The room filled slowly. A clerk with reading glasses. Two county officials whose names he'd learned from the notice taped to the bakery door. Hale's lawyer—a compact man named Brandt with a tan achieved indoors and a briefcase that closed with an expensive click. And Marcus Hale himself, who sat at the defense table like a man attending the opening night of a play he'd financed, adjusting his cuffs, smiling at people he didn't know.

Petra recognized the smile. He'd spent fifteen years smiling it himself—the performance smile, the one that said *everything is under control* while your hands quietly came apart beneath you.

At eight fifty-two, Astrid came through the door in a charcoal blazer over what he suspected was her only non-flour-dusted shirt, her hair pinned back with the severity of a woman who had decided that if the world wanted to take her kitchen, it would have to do it while looking her in the eye. She scanned the room, found him, and her expression did something complicated—softened, then armored itself again.

She sat in the second row. Not beside him. Not across the aisle. Second row, directly behind where he would sit, close enough that when he turned to check, he could see the small crease between her brows that appeared whenever she was frightened and refusing to admit it.

"Mr. Underhill." The hearing officer, a gray-haired woman named Okafor with the unhurried voice of someone who had heard every lie the county could produce, nodded toward the stand. "Whenever you're ready."

He wasn't ready. He stood anyway.

* * *

The oath was shorter than he expected. His voice held. Small victories.

"State your name and occupation for the record."

"Petra Underhill." A pause. "I'm—I was a cellist. Currently I'm between occupations."

Brandt looked up at that, and Petra saw the flicker behind his eyes: *between occupations*, filed away, sharpened for later use.

Okafor asked him how he knew Marcus Hale. Petra took a breath and began.

"He approached me on the fourteenth of December. Outside the bakery. Around seven in the evening—it was dark, the streetlight near the door was out, which I later understood was not an accident. He said he'd been trying to get a meeting with me for weeks."

"And what did Mr. Hale propose?"

Petra had rehearsed this part every night for eleven days, lying in bed, saying the words aloud to the ceiling until they lost their power to choke him. It hadn't worked entirely, but it had worked enough.

"He proposed that I sign an affidavit stating that Ms. Voss had knowledge of a property transfer involving her aunt's parcel on Harbor Road. In exchange, he would fund a masterclass series in Europe. My name on it. Twelve cities. He said—" and here Petra's memory supplied the exact words, unbidden, in Hale's warm baritone, "—he said, 'You've been invisible for two years, Underhill. I'm offering to make you visible again. All it costs is one signature and one sentence.'"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere behind him, Astrid exhaled.

"Did he say anything else about the affidavit?"

"He said the statement would be true 'in spirit.' He said Ms. Voss's aunt had been confused at the end of her life, so really, anyone could have believed anything. He said—" Petra's jaw tightened. "He said reputations were like bread. They go stale fast, and nobody mourns them."

A dry sound from the second row—not quite a laugh. Astrid.

"Did you sign?"

"No."

"Why not?"

And there it was. The question he'd rehearsed least, because the honest answer was the one he most dreaded giving. Okafor waited. Brandt waited, pen poised, patient as a spider.

"Because I wanted to," Petra said finally. "That's why not. I want to be very clear about that. He didn't threaten me. He offered me exactly the thing I've been starving for since my hands failed, and I sat with that offer for three days, and I wanted it so badly I could taste it. I didn't sign because of—" he almost said *her*, caught himself, "—because of what it would have done to someone who didn't deserve it. But I want the record to show that the decision was close. Closer than I'm comfortable admitting. If I tell you otherwise, I'm just performing, and I've retired from that."

Okafor regarded him for a long moment. Something in her face shifted—not warmth, exactly, but recognition. The look of a person who had expected a witness and received a human being instead.

"The record will show whatever you tell it, Mr. Underhill. That's rather the point of records." She glanced down. "Mr. Brandt, cross-examination."

* * *

Brandt rose like a man unfolding himself from first class. He smiled at Petra with genuine, professional charm.

"Mr. Underhill. Let me begin by saying I have enormous respect for your career. The Brahms recording from 2019 is still taught, I understand."

"Thank you."

"So let's talk about that career." Brandt produced a folder and opened it with ceremony. "The Vienna Philharmonic, March 2021. You walked off stage mid-performance. Is that correct?"

"It is."

"The reason given publicly was illness."

"That was the reason given publicly."

"What was the actual reason?"

Petra felt the old cold move up his arms. This was the trap—he'd known it would be, had lain awake mapping its shape. Brandt didn't need to prove Hale innocent. He needed to prove Petra unreliable, and the fastest route was through the wreckage everyone in this town half-remembered from the news.

"My hands stopped working," Petra said. "Mid-phrase. I couldn't feel the strings. I finished the movement out of stubbornness and then I couldn't continue."

"Neurologists found no pathology."

"Correct."

"So a world-class musician, at the height of his powers, abandons a performance—and then tells us his hands simply *stopped*. And we're asked to accept his account of events today without question?" Brandt spread his hands apologetically. "Mr. Underhill, isn't it possible that your perception—in Vienna, or on a dark street outside a bakery—isn't entirely trustworthy?"

"It's possible," Petra said. "I've thought about that every day for two years."

Brandt blinked. He had prepared for denial, deflection, wounded pride. He had not prepared for agreement.

"But here's what I know," Petra continued, and his voice steadied in a way that surprised him. "In Vienna, I was the only one who experienced what happened to my hands. Today, there are documents. There's a property record with a forged signature. There's a phone call Hale made to me on the twentieth of December, which I recorded, because after three days of wanting his offer, I decided I should protect myself from my own wanting. You can question whether I perceive reality accurately. You cannot question a recording."

Brandt's smile thinned by a millimeter. "We'll address the recording's provenance in due course. Let me ask you about motive. You co-signed a lease on the property in dispute. You have a personal relationship with the complainant. Isn't it true that you benefit directly from Ms. Voss prevailing here?"

"Yes."

"You *admit* it."

"I admit everything today, counselor. It's cheaper than the alternative. I tried the alternative for two years—it cost me a career."

A murmur moved through the folding chairs. Old Mrs. Halloran from the library, third row, nodded slowly, as though Petra had finally returned a book on time.

Brandt pressed harder. He took Petra through the concert history piece by piece—the canceled tour, the withdrawn commissions, the agent who'd dropped him, the reviews that used words like *unmoored* and *a shadow of*. Each fact landed like a small, precise blow, and Petra let them land, because they were all true, and because he had discovered somewhere between chapter sixteen of his own life and this wooden box that the truth, even ugly, weighed less than its concealment.

"Let the record reflect," Brandt concluded, turning to Okafor, "that the complainant's sole corroborating witness is a man whose last public act was to walk off a stage in front of two thousand people, who has no medical explanation for it, and who stands to gain financially and personally from today's outcome."

"Noted," Okafor said. Then, mildly: "Anything further?"

"One moment." Brandt leaned in. "Mr. Underhill. One final question. If Mr. Hale withdrew his offer tomorrow—if the masterclass series vanished, if you never played another note in Europe—would your testimony change?"

Petra looked at him. Behind Brandt, Hale watched with his cuffs adjusted and his face arranged into mild concern, and Petra thought: *there it is. The audience. Fifteen years of playing for rooms full of people checking their programs, and the last one of them is sitting right there.*

"No," Petra said. "It wouldn't change. Because for the first time in my life, I'm not testifying for what I might get back. I'm testifying for what's actually true. It turns out those are different skills. I was better at the first one. I intend to be better at the second."

He stepped down. His hands were shaking—but then, they always shook now. The difference was that today, shaking, they had done exactly what he asked of them.

* * *

The hearing ran until nearly two o'clock. The recording was played—Hale's voice, smooth and damning, filling the wax-smelling room while the clerk's tape recorder captured it capturing him. The forged signature was examined. An appraiser testified. Mrs. Halloran produced, from some archival instinct, a 1987 deed that established the aunt's ownership beyond argument, and presented it to the panel with the satisfaction of a woman returning a forty-year overdue fine.

Through all of it, Astrid sat in the second row and did not look at him once—which he understood, because looking at him would have meant feeling something, and feeling something in that room would have meant showing it, and she had come armored for war.

When Okafor announced that the panel would rule within ten days and that the eviction filing was suspended pending review, the room exhaled. Hale gathered his coat, paused beside Astrid's chair to say something low and pleasant that made her knuckles whiten on her bag, and left. Brandt followed, clicking his case shut.

Then the room emptied, and it was just the two of them and the janitor starting on the far windows.

Astrid stood. She walked up the aisle and stopped one step below him, so that for once in their acquaintance they were nearly the same height.

"You said 'closer than I'm comfortable admitting,'" she said.

"I did."

"Three days. You sat on it for three days."

"Four, technically. I counted the fourth as the day I called the county clerk's office and asked how affidavits work. That was the day I decided."

She studied him. Outside the tall windows, Bellhaven glittered under a hard blue January sky, gulls wheeling over the harbor like punctuation marks.

"In my letters," she said quietly, "my pen pal once wrote that courage isn't the absence of wanting to run. It's running in the wrong direction first, and then turning around where people can see you do it." Her voice caught slightly. "I assumed that was a philosophy. I didn't know it was a confession with a date attached."

"Astrid—"

"Ten days," she said. "They rule in ten days. And I have a kitchen with no permits, an investor who knows things he shouldn't know, and a—" she stopped, recalibrated. "A neighbor. Who apparently records phone calls now, like a person with a future."

"Like a person learning," Petra said.

Something almost softened in her face. Almost. She picked up her bag.

"There's flour in my car and a proofing schedule that doesn't care about county panels," she said. "Are you coming or not? Somebody has to hold the ladder while I re-tape the oven thermometer, and you're the only person in this town tall enough."

It was not forgiveness. It was not even absolution. It was an invitation to stand next to her while the verdict waited somewhere in a file, and it was, Petra understood, the most Astrid thing she had ever said to him.

He followed her out into the cold, toward the bakery, toward the ten days—where, neither of them yet knew, the ruling was already being written, and it was not going to be the one anyone expected.
