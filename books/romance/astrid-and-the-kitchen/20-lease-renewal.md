## 20. Lease Renewal

The lawyer's office smelled of lemon polish and old paper, and Petra Underhill sat in it with his hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a verdict, which was ironic, because the verdict had already come in—two weeks ago, in a county hearing room that smelled of nothing at all—and this was merely the paperwork. The paperwork, he was learning, was where life actually happened. All the drama of testimony and forged signatures and Marcus Hale's immaculate smile collapsing like a soufflé in a slammed oven door—that had been the performance. This was the living.

"Mr. Underhill." The lawyer, a compact woman named Doris Fenwick who had known his aunt better than Petra ever had, slid a folder across the desk. "The estate settlement. Final disbursement."

Petra looked at the number on the page and felt something he hadn't felt in two years: possibility with weight to it. Not the airy, theoretical possibility of a masterclass series in Vienna with his name embossed on the brochure—Hale's offer, dead now, buried with Hale's reputation—but the heavy, grounded kind. The kind you could spend.

"You could buy a very nice apartment in the city," Doris said, watching him with the frank appraisal of a woman who had watched him mope around Bellhaven for the better part of a year. "Or several mediocre ones."

"I don't want an apartment in the city."

"No," she said. "I didn't think so." She tapped the folder. "So what does the famous cellist want to do with his aunt's money?"

And there it was—the question he'd been circling for two weeks like a dog trying to find the exact right spot to lie down. Because the answer involved Astrid Voss, and involving Astrid Voss in anything financial felt roughly as wise as handing a lit match to a fireworks factory. She would either laugh him out of her half-built kitchen or she would look at him with those gray eyes that saw entirely too much, and either way he'd have to stand there and take it.

He'd rehearsed the conversation eleven times. In the shower. On his morning walk along the seawall. Once, disastrously, out loud, to his own reflection, which had gone so badly he'd considered moving to Portugal.

The problem was that every version began with *I'd like to invest in your restaurant* and ended, somewhere around sentence three, with *because I love you*, and he wasn't sure he was allowed to say the second part yet. They had kissed among the sawdust. They had fought about the fact that she'd known he was her pen pal since chapter eight of their acquaintance, as it were, and let him hang himself with his own elegant phrasing for months. They had made peace with all of it, mostly, over approximately forty cups of coffee. But money was different. Money was where people showed you what they actually believed about the future.

* * *

He found her in the kitchen at seven in the morning, which was not difficult, because Astrid Voss occupied the kitchen the way weather occupies a coastline—totally, and without apology.

She was standing at the new counter—hers now, legally, irrevocably, thanks to a jury of twelve sensible county residents—staring down at a stack of menus that were, Petra noted, beautiful. Heavy cream stock, letterpress type, a small woodcut of the building on the cover that made the bakery look like something out of a storybook.

"You're staring at them like they insulted you," he said from the doorway.

"They did." She didn't look up. "The printer put 'Astrid Voss' in italic. I'm not italic. I'm roman. I'm the most roman person alive."

"You're shouting at cardstock at dawn."

"I'm *negotiating* with cardstock at dawn. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of us is a professional." She finally looked up, and whatever she saw in his face made her set down the offending menu. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The face. The one where you've composed something in your head and you're waiting for the acoustics to be right." She wiped her hands on her apron—flour-dusted, always flour-dusted, even though the ovens weren't running yet; he suspected the flour was load-bearing at this point, some kind of emotional support powder. "Out with it, Underhill. I've got a health inspector at nine and a printer who thinks I'm a font."

So much for the acoustics. He had planned to do this over dinner. He had planned candlelight, possibly. He had planned to ease into it the way one eases into cold water, with dignity and controlled breathing. Instead he heard himself say:

"I want to co-sign the kitchen."

Silence. Somewhere outside, a gull screamed what Petra chose to interpret as encouragement.

"My aunt left me more than the apartment," he went on, before his nerve could file for eviction. "There's the settlement. And I've been thinking about what to do with it, and I keep coming back to—" He gestured helplessly at the room, at the copper pots hanging like chandeliers, at the big window where morning light was just beginning to pour across the floorboards. "This. You. Us. The whole ridiculous enterprise. I don't want my name on a masterclass brochure in Vienna. I want it on a lease in Bellhaven. Next to yours."

Astrid was quiet for a long moment. Long enough that Petra's heart performed a small, undignified solo.

"You've been rehearsing that," she said finally.

"Eleven times."

"It shows. You said 'the whole ridiculous enterprise' like you'd written it down."

"I did write it down. I wrote it down four times and burned three versions."

Something moved through her face—something soft and startled and quickly corralled, because Astrid Voss did not permit unsecured emotions on the premises. She picked up one of the menus, straightened it against the stack with unnecessary precision, and said:

"There are terms."

"Of course there are terms."

"Fifty-one percent is mine. This is my kitchen. I have been dreaming about this kitchen since I was nineteen years old and burning my first roux in my aunt's trailer. You are investing, not colonizing."

"Agreed."

"And if this fails—if the restaurant goes under—you don't get to disappear upstairs and write bitter letters about it. We fail out loud, together, in public, like adults."

"That sounds horrifying."

"It is. That's the price of admission." She stuck out a floury hand. "Do we have a deal?"

He took it. Her grip was strong and warm and slightly gritty, and Petra thought: *this is the best contract I will ever sign, and I once signed with a label whose logo is on concert halls in six countries.*

"We have a deal," he said.

* * *

Doris Fenwick drew up the paperwork in three days, which Petra suspected was a personal record driven partly by professional pride and partly by her desire to see how this disaster of a romance resolved itself on paper. They signed at her desk on a Tuesday afternoon—Astrid in her good blazer, which still had a faint smell of rosemary; Petra in the only jacket he owned that hadn't been near a sawdust pile.

"Sign there," Doris said, "and there, and initial every page, because Mr. Hale ruined simple documents for everyone in this county forever."

Petra signed. His handwriting came out steady. He noticed this with a small private astonishment—there was a time when his signature shook so badly he'd stopped signing programs altogether, when fans asked. His hands, these days, only trembled for good reasons: nerves before kissing her, nerves before playing, the ordinary electricity of being alive in front of people.

Astrid signed last, then blew on the ink out of habit, then caught herself doing it and glared at both of them as though daring comment.

"Well," Doris said, stamping the final page. "Congratulations. You're business partners. Legally bound. God help you both." She closed the folder and added, with the ghost of a smile, "Your aunt would be insufferable about this, Petra. She used to tell me the bakery needed music. Said bread rises better when someone's playing."

"She never told me that."

"She told everyone that. You weren't listening yet."

* * *

The argument happened that same evening, which surprised neither of them, because they were who they were.

They were sitting at the counter with takeout containers between them—Astrid refused to cook in her own kitchen until opening night, some superstition Petra had learned not to question—and she spread the draft menu across the marble like a general deploying troops.

"Dishes are settled. Wine list is settled. Which leaves," she said, tapping the bottom corner, "music."

Petra perked up. This was, absurdly, the first territory in the entire project where he was the expert. "Live cello on weekends. Solo repertoire—Bach, mostly. The suites were written for rooms like this. Warm wood, low ceilings, people eating—"

"No."

"—and the acoustics in here are going to be extraordinary once the—wait. No?"

"No Bach."

"This is my area of expertise, Astrid."

"And this is my dining room. I've eaten in a hundred restaurants with a string quartet sawing away in the corner, and you know what it does? It makes everyone whisper. It makes dinner feel like church. I want people laughing. I want butter on chins. I want a table of tourists singing happy birthday off-key and nobody shushing them because there's no solemn soundtrack telling them food is serious business." She crossed her arms. "No live classical. Recorded. Something warm. Something with a pulse."

Petra stared at her. Two years ago—no, ten years ago—he would have taken this as a verdict on his entire art form. He would have delivered a lecture on the history of the suite form, cited venues, invoked dead composers as character witnesses. Some wounded, defensive orchestra conductor in his chest raised its baton, ready to begin.

But that conductor had retired. What remained was a man who had learned, slowly and painfully and with tremendous help from a certain anonymous correspondence, that being listened to mattered more than being admired—and that listening went both directions.

"Okay," he said.

Astrid blinked. "Okay?"

"Okay. Recorded music. With a pulse." He turned a fork over in his fingers, thinking. "But can I make a case for one thing? Not a rebuttal. A case."

She narrowed her eyes, but nodded.

"Sundays. One hour, mid-afternoon, between services. I play—not Bach, if Bach makes people whisper. Whatever makes people stay. Standards, folk tunes, that thing you hum when you think nobody's listening, which I have heard through the floor and which I will be transcribing whether you consent or not." He held up a hand before she could object. "One hour. If the room goes quiet in the bad way—the church way—I stop. If people start humming along, we keep it. Deal?"

Astrid looked at him for a long moment. He watched her run the calculation—pride versus curiosity, control versus trust—and he recognized the expression, because he'd worn it himself for the better part of a year, behind envelopes and stamps and a false name.

"One hour," she said. "Sundays. And if anyone whispers reverently, you're fired from your own restaurant."

"Our restaurant."

"Our restaurant." She said it like she was testing the weight of it, and then, apparently satisfied, reached across the counter and stole the last dumpling out of his container with the serenity of a woman who had just won two negotiations in one day.

"That was mine."

"Finders keepers. It's in the lease. Section twelve."

"There is no section twelve."

"There will be by Friday. I'm adding clauses while you sleep."

* * *

That night, Petra climbed the stairs to the apartment—his apartment, technically, until the end of the month, after which he would move his things down the hall into what had been his aunt's sewing room, because Astrid had opinions about commuting across a shared wall that he'd decided were charming rather than alarming.

He paused at the window that overlooked the harbor. Below him, through the kitchen's big glass door, he could see Astrid still at the counter, lamp on, menu in hand, mouthing corrections to the printer's italics. Small. Determined. Utterly certain of who she was in a way Petra had spent forty years and one catastrophic career failing to be.

He thought about Doris's words. *She told everyone that. You weren't listening yet.*

His cello stood in its corner by the bookshelf, open case like a patient mouth. He crossed the room and lifted it out—not to practice, exactly. Just to hold it. To feel the familiar weight settle against him, the neck fitting his hand the way it always had, the way it had even in the worst weeks, even when his fingers wouldn't obey and the sound came out broken.

He played a few bars. Nothing ambitious. The folk tune Astrid hummed—the one she'd deny humming, the one he'd been quietly working into something with a melody line that rose at the end like bread proving in a warm oven. It sounded rough. It sounded unfinished.

It sounded like a beginning.

Downstairs, the lamp clicked off. Through the floorboards he heard her footsteps cross the kitchen, pause beneath his window, and then—faint, almost certainly accidental, absolutely going to be denied—a few bars of humming. The same tune. Slightly ahead of where he'd been playing it, as if she'd been carrying the next phrase around all evening, waiting for him to catch up.

Petra smiled and wrote it down.

Three days later, the framed letters went up behind the counter—eleven months of correspondence, anonymous no longer, glass-fronted and slightly crooked because Astrid hung them and refused to admit the level was broken. Customers would ask about them. Astrid would say, "Ask the cellist," and Petra would tell the story badly, leaving out the best parts, and Astrid would correct him from the pass, louder than necessary.

But that was spring. That was later.

On the night the lease was signed, there was only this: a man with a cello in a small apartment above a kitchen that was finally, officially, theirs—writing down a melody he'd stolen fair and square from the woman below, and finding, to his considerable surprise, that his hands didn't shake at all.

Except, he admitted, examining them honestly in the lamplight, maybe a little. Maybe exactly enough.

The winter festival committee had called that morning. Left a message with the bakery's new answering machine, cheerful and relentless as gulls: *Mr. Underhill, we understand congratulations are in order, and also that the program has one slot left, and also that Mrs. Pruitt says you owe the town a performance, and also—*

He hadn't deleted it.

He played it again, twice, standing in the dark with the phone against his ear and the folk tune open on the stand in front of him, and thought: *one slot. One hour on Sundays. One song at a festival in front of the whole town.*

Apparently, he was learning to count in smaller numbers. Apparently, that was how courage worked—not as a grand gesture, but as practice. As scales. As showing up.

He dialed the bakery's machine downstairs, waited for the beep, and left a message for Astrid, who was thirty feet away and would hear it in the morning:

"It's me. Tell the festival committee yes. And tell them—tell them I'll need someone in the front row who isn't afraid to hum along."

Then he put down the phone, picked up the bow, and began again from the top.
