## 21. Winter Festival

Petra's POV

The cello case had not left the closet in two years. It had lived there the way some people live in guest rooms—present, tolerated, never quite at home. When Petra finally lifted it out on the morning of the festival, it came with a small avalanche of dust and a single forgotten sock that had no business being anywhere near a cello.

He held the case against his chest and stood in the middle of his aunt's old apartment, listening to the building below him. Astrid's kitchen was already awake. He could hear the low percussion of her radio, the clatter of a sheet pan going into the oven, her voice raised in cheerful tyranny at someone—probably Dev, probably about the transport of the soup urns. The whole town would smell like woodsmoke and cardamom by noon. Bellhaven took its winter festival seriously: strings of lights along the pier, a tree the size of a modest lighthouse, and a stage built each year on the harbor green by men who argued about load-bearing capacity the way other men argued about football.

Two months ago he had refused this stage. Refused it flatly, in front of the librarian, who had accepted his refusal with the serene expression of a woman who knew she'd get her way eventually. Librarians, Petra had learned, played the long game.

His hands were shaking now. Not badly—not the catastrophic tremor of that final concert in Vienna, when the D minor had slipped through his fingers like water through a net—but enough that he noticed. Enough that he sat down on the edge of the bed, opened the case, and looked at the instrument for a long moment before touching it.

"Hello," he said, because Astrid talked to her sourdough starter and claimed it improved the flavor, and he was willing to try anything once.

The cello said nothing, which he respected.

He had been practicing for six weeks. In the apartment, quietly, with the window cracked so the sound leaked out over the harbor like something contraband. Then less quietly. Then one evening Astrid had come up the stairs without knocking, stood in his doorway with flour on her cheekbone, and said, "You know these walls are paper, right?" and turned around and gone back down before he could answer. After that he stopped pretending to practice quietly. There seemed little point.

The piece was Bach—the Sarabande from the Fifth Suite. Three minutes long. He had chosen it precisely because it was short, because if his hands betrayed him they could only betray him for three minutes, and because it was the first thing he had ever learned properly, at eleven years old, in a practice room that smelled like floor wax. If he was going to fall off a cliff in public, he wanted it to be a cliff he knew.

He dressed carefully, which for Petra meant the same black sweater he owned four of, and went downstairs.

Astrid was in the middle of the kitchen, orchestrating chaos. She'd volunteered to run the food stall—of course she had—and the counters were a battlefield of covered trays, labeled containers, and one enormous pot of spiced cider that burped steam like a friendly dragon. She was wearing her good coat over her apron, which meant she'd gotten dressed twice, which meant she cared, though she would deny it under oath.

"You're up early," she said, not looking at him, sliding a tray of cardamom knots into a carrier with the precision of a surgeon.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Liar. You slept fine. You just woke up scared." She glanced at him then, and her eyes did the thing they did—went sharp, then soft, in the space of half a second. "Hands?"

"A little."

"Good." She crossed the kitchen, took both his hands in hers—floury, warm, absurdly strong—and held them up between them like evidence. "Shaking means you're paying attention. I've watched a hundred cooks freeze on a bad service night. The ones whose hands don't shake are the ones who've stopped caring what comes out of the kitchen." She squeezed. "You care. That's all courage is. Caring while terrified."

"That's not a saying. You made that up."

"I make up excellent sayings. Write that one down; it'll be framed behind the counter someday." She released his hands, picked up the cider pot handles, and nodded toward the door. "Come on, cellist. Walk me down. If I drop this, you catch it. That's the deal."

* * *

Astrid's POV

The harbor green was transformed. Astrid had lived in cities her whole adult life—Brig, then Lyon, then a decade of brutal, glorious kitchens in Chicago—and she had forgotten what a small town could do with two hundred dollars' worth of lights and genuine enthusiasm. The tree glittered. Children ran everywhere with sparklers their parents would regret. Old Halvorsen's brass band was tuning in a way that suggested tuning was more of a philosophical position than a practice.

She set up her stall with Dev and Mrs. Okonkwo from the library, and by four o'clock the line for cardamom knots stretched past the chowder stand, which Astrid tried not to feel smug about, and failed completely.

"He's going to be fine," Mrs. Okonkwo said, appearing at her elbow with two cups of cider and handing her one unasked.

"I didn't say anything."

"You've checked your phone four times in ten minutes and you keep looking at the stage like it owes you money."

Astrid drank her cider. "He hasn't performed in two years. Not for an audience. What if—" She stopped. She didn't finish sentences like that anymore; she'd spent too many years in kitchens where finishing the pessimistic sentence was considered a professional skill. "What if it goes wrong?"

"Then it goes wrong in front of people who brought folding chairs and thermoses and love him." Mrs. Okonkwo shrugged. "I've sat through forty years of school recitals. A wobbly note is nothing. A man hiding in an apartment—that's something."

Astrid looked at the stage. Petra was already up there, sitting alone with his cello in the pool of lamplight, adjusting the endpin, talking quietly to the sound man. From here he looked calm. She knew better. She knew the specific stillness he wore when he was frightened, the way his hands went precise and careful, like a man defusing something. She'd seen it the night of the power outage, when the landlord called and he'd retreated behind politeness like a drawbridge going up. She'd seen it again during the hearing prep, when his lawyer told him Hale's people would drag his career through the mud and Petra had said, evenly, "They can try."

The town had turned out for him. Not out of curiosity—or not only. Word had traveled after the hearing, the way word travels in small places, slightly embellished: the cellist who gave up everything to tell the truth. Half of them didn't know the details. All of them knew he'd stood up in a county records hearing and put his name on the record against a rich man's lawyers, knowing exactly what it would cost him.

The mayor—a retired ferry captain with a magnificent beard—took the microphone and delivered remarks of truly heroic length. Astrid sold cider through all of it. Finally, mercifully, the mayor said, "And now, folks, a man who needs no introduction, but is going to get one anyway—"

"Please don't," Astrid murmured.

"—Mr. Petra Underhill!"

Applause rolled across the green. Astrid abandoned her stall entirely, ignoring Dev's protest, and pushed through to the front row, where she planted herself directly in front of the stage with her arms folded, as if daring the universe to try anything.

Petra settled the cello against him. And she saw it—even from ten feet away, even in the flat winter light. His hands, poised above the strings, trembling.

The whole green saw it. A hush went through the crowd, the particular hush of people deciding, collectively and instantly, to be kind.

He looked down at his hands. He looked out—at the crowd, at the lights, at her. Astrid did the only thing she could think of. She unfolded her arms and pressed her hand flat against her sternum, the way she did in the kitchen when a dish came out right, and mouthed, *Caring while terrified.*

Something moved through his face—half a laugh, half a surrender. He closed his eyes. Brought the bow to the string.

And began.

* * *

Petra's POV

The first note came out rough. Not wrong, exactly, but grainy, the way a voice sounds on a cold morning, and Petra felt the old panic rise in his throat like floodwater—*there it is, there's the crack, everyone can hear it*—

And then he heard something else underneath the panic. The crowd. Two hundred people holding their breath for him. Not waiting for him to fail. Waiting for him to play.

He let the second note answer the first. The Sarabande is a slow piece, a grave piece, music that moves the way light moves across a floor—imperceptibly, and then all at once. He had played it thousands of times in halls where the audience listened with their expertise, their comparisons, their memories of other cellists. This crowd listened with nothing but themselves. He could feel it, the difference, like playing in a room versus playing outdoors. No walls to bounce back judgment. Just air, and cold, and attention.

In the fourth bar his left hand fumbled a shift and the note landed flat—a small, unmistakable crack in the line.

The old Petra would have died there. Would have felt the whole architecture of the performance collapse, would have spent the remaining bars in a white-hot fury of self-erasure, would have gone home and not touched the instrument for another two years.

Instead, Petra did what Astrid did when a sauce broke: adjusted, kept moving, trusted the next thing. The following note came out true. The one after that, truer. By the middle section his hands had warmed, and the tremor hadn't vanished—it never fully vanished now, he suspected it never would—but it had become part of the sound somehow, a faint human vibration under the music, like the grain in wood.

*The note that falters matters less than the one that follows.* He'd written that to her, months ago, in a letter signed *Yours, P.*, back when the letters were the bravest part of him. He hadn't known he was writing instructions.

He opened his eyes near the end—he couldn't say why, except that the last phrase felt like something you should witness rather than survive—and found Astrid in the front row, hand on her heart, watching him with an expression he had no defenses against. Behind her, the whole town: Mrs. Okonkwo with her mittened hands clasped, the librarian openly weeping with zero shame, Dev holding a tray of knots aloft like an offering, old Halvorsen's brass band standing silent and reverent, the mayor's beard frosted with cider steam. Everyone he'd hidden from. Everyone he'd testified for. Everyone who had shown up anyway.

He played the final chord and let it ring out over the harbor until the sea took it.

For a moment: silence. The terrifying, bottomless silence.

Then the green erupted. Not polite applause—cheering, stamping, someone's dog barking joyfully, Mrs. Okonkwo blowing her nose like a foghorn. Petra sat with his bow still raised, blinking, feeling something crack open in his chest that was not grief for once.

He stood. Bowed. Looked down at Astrid.

She was smiling the way she smiled at bread coming out right—with her whole face, with her whole history, with everything.

He mouthed it back to her: *Caring while terrified.*

"You made that up," she shouted over the noise.

"It's an excellent saying! Someone should frame it!"

* * *

Astrid's POV

Afterward the green dissolved into festival proper—carols, cider, children dragging adults toward the tree-lighting. Astrid found Petra at the edge of the crowd, cello cased on his back, looking slightly stunned, like a man who'd been underwater for two years and had just remembered how breathing worked.

"Well?" she said.

"I think my hands shook the entire time."

"They did."

"You're supposed to lie."

"I'm a chef, not a magician." She reached up and straightened his collar, an excuse to touch him, and he let her, which after everything still felt like a small miracle. "It was the best thing I've heard in my life, and I once ate a thirty-course tasting menu in Lyon. Don't argue with me. I have witnesses."

"The note in the fourth bar was flat."

"It was honest." She poked his chest. "There's a difference, and you finally know it, and I'm not letting you forget it."

He laughed—an easy, unguarded laugh, a sound she intended to collect more of—and pulled her in by the waist, flour and sawdust and cider and all, and kissed her in front of God and the whole town of Bellhaven, which cheered again, because apparently they'd all been waiting for that too.

When they broke apart, Mrs. Okonkwo was standing beside them holding a thick envelope, wearing the expression of a woman delivering a verdict.

"What's that?" Astrid asked.

"Official county post." Mrs. Okonkwo handed it over. "Came to the library addressed to both of you. Jointly. Which around here means gossip already knows something we don't."

Astrid turned it over. The return address was the county clerk's office. Beneath it, stamped in red:

**NOTICE OF FINAL RULING — ENCLOSURE: RESTORED DOCUMENTS & LEASE CERTIFICATION**

And in the corner, handwritten by someone at the clerk's office, almost apologetically:

*Congratulations — and our apologies for the delay. Enclosed also: a personal letter forwarded to this office, misdelivered months ago. We believed you'd want it with the rest.*

Astrid looked at Petra. Petra looked at Astrid.

Together, in the glow of two hundred fairy lights, they opened the envelope—and Astrid went very still, because the handwriting inside was one she recognized.

It was her aunt's.
