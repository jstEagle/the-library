## 22. Astrid and the Kitchen

**Petra**

The first thing Petra Underhill learned about opening a restaurant was that it had nothing to do with music and everything to do with panic.

The second thing he learned was that Astrid Voss, at seven in the morning, three days before opening, was a force of nature wearing an apron that said KISS THE COOK OR ELSE.

"Petra." Her voice carried up the stairs of the bakery apartment the way her voice carried everywhere—like it owned the building, which, technically, half of it did. "The framers are here. The letters. Where are the letters?"

He was already halfway down, the flat wooden box in his hands. Twenty-two letters, give or take—the ones that had survived. Some had been written in the dark of a power outage by candle stub. One had flour pressed into its creases from a night he'd written it on the kitchen counter while bread proofed beside him. One had a wine ring on it that Astrid had refused to let him clean, declaring it *provenance*.

The framers had come from Portland, two patient women who kept exchanging glances whenever Astrid issued an instruction, which was constantly, which was the point.

"These go behind the counter," Astrid said, tapping the wall she'd cleared for them. Bare pine, freshly sealed. "Facing the dining room. I want people to eat their breakfast looking at them and wonder."

"Wonder what?"

"Whether they're love letters."

"And if they ask?"

She took the box from him, lifted the lid, and looked down at their handwriting—his careful, hers slashing—mixed together like the two of them had been all along. "Then we tell them the truth," she said. "That we were cowards for six months and it worked out anyway. It's a good menu item."

"That's not a menu item."

"It's the *special*."

The framers laughed. Petra felt the strange, still-new thing happen in his chest that happened now maybe fifty times a day: nothing hurt. He kept waiting for the catch, the old mechanism of dread to engage, and it kept not engaging. His hands, resting at his sides, were quiet.

The letters went up one by one behind glass. Anonymous no longer. The whole town had known by February anyway—Mrs. Okafor at the library had figured it out in November and told exactly everyone, which was her love language—but there was a difference between a town knowing and a wall admitting it.

The last letter they hung was the first one he'd ever written. *Dear Stranger. I am told this program builds community. I do not have much to say. The weather here is damp.*

Astrid stood beside him, shoulder against his shoulder, and read her own reply through the glass. *Damp is a choice, Stranger. So is being boring. Try harder.*

"I was so rude to you," she said fondly.

"You were rude to a version of me I didn't know I could be. It was very helpful."

She kissed his jaw, flour and all, and went back to yelling at the framers about sightlines, and Petra stood in the half-built kitchen—the kitchen he had co-signed, the kitchen that had nearly been taken from her by a man with perfect documents and a rotten signature—and thought: *this is what the music was always trying to say, and I was saying it to the wrong rooms.*

* * *

**Astrid**

The trouble with feeding a town was that the town kept showing up.

Astrid had planned for forty guests at the soft opening. She'd planned for sixty, after Mrs. Okafor's newsletter went out. What she had not planned for was ninety-one, including the entire congregation of St. Brendan's, the high school jazz band (uninvited, unrepentant), and a food blogger from Seattle who kept saying the word *rustic* in a way that made Astrid's eye twitch.

"More benches," she told Dev, her sous chef, who had followed her from the city on the promise of "coastal chaos and a boss who means it." "And if the jazz band plays one more bar of anything, feed them. Feed them until they can't hold their instruments."

"That's not discipline, Chef. That's a reward system."

"Everything I do is a reward system. That's the secret of hospitality."

The kitchen hummed around her—her kitchen, *their* kitchen, the ovens she'd fought a corrupt investor and half the county records office to keep, now breathing heat into the room like a living thing. The forged signature on Hale's documents had unraveled in April the way rotten things do, all at once and embarrassingly. The settlement had covered the last of the build-out. The permits hung framed by the door, next to the eviction notice she'd also framed, because Astrid Voss did not believe in throwing away evidence of her own victories.

Through the pass she could see the dining room filling, and behind the counter, the letters in their glass, and in front of the letters, a small crowd of children.

She knew why they were there. Everyone knew why they were there. It was Saturday.

Petra had started teaching cello in the bakery's back room in March—one student, then three, then eleven, because in a town like Bellhaven, a famous musician teaching children in a bakery was not a program, it was a *phenomenon*. He'd been terrified of it. She'd watched him rehearse his first lesson four times in their apartment, pacing with an invisible bow, muttering about pedagogy.

"You played Carnegie Hall," she'd said.

"Carnegie Hall doesn't wiggle."

"Then teach them not to wiggle."

"That's not—it's not how children *work*, Astrid—"

And then eleven children had shown up, and he'd knelt down to adjust the smallest one's bow hold, and his hands—the hands that had failed him in front of two thousand people—had been perfectly, gently steady, and Astrid had stood in the doorway with her heart doing something undignified, and gone back to her bread.

Now the last lesson of the morning was letting out, and the children were migrating toward the smell of cinnamon, and Petra came through the pass door carrying a cello case and an expression of a man who has been thoroughly defeated by an eight-year-old.

"Marisol," he said, by way of explanation, "has informed me that vibrato is 'just shaking with confidence.' She's not wrong. I don't know what to do with that."

"Put it on the wall. We have a wall for true things."

He looked at the letters behind the glass, then at her—flour to the elbows, hair escaping its knot, ninety-one guests and counting—and something in his face went soft and wondering, the way it still did, the way she suspected it always would.

"You fed the jazz band," he said.

"I fed the jazz band."

"You're a menace."

"I'm a *success*. There's a difference and it's about forty covers."

* * *

**Petra**

The soft opening, in the end, was not about the food, though the food was extraordinary—Astrid's cooking had a quality Petra could only describe as *listening*, as though she'd tasted what the town was hungry for and given it back. It was not about the letters, though people stood in front of them all afternoon, and Mrs. Okafor read them aloud to her granddaughter in a voice of great drama.

It was about the moment, late in the golden afternoon, when Marisol Reyes—eight years old, three months of cello, vibrato described as "shaking with confidence"—walked up to Petra with her quarter-size instrument and said, "Play with me. The one from the festival. The slow part."

Every adult within earshot went quiet. Petra felt the old flinch start in his shoulders, the ghost of the concert hall, the memory of the note that wouldn't come.

But this was not a concert hall. This was a kitchen. There was flour in the air and a jazz band eating their weight in cardamom buns and Astrid at the pass, watching him with an expression that said, plainly, *you can. You know you can.*

So he took his cello out of its case, and he sat down on a bench among the tables, and Marisol sat beside him, and they played the slow theme from the festival piece—her thin, wobbly, entirely confident line underneath his, shaking with confidence exactly as advertised. He flubbed a shift in the second phrase. Nobody minded. Marisol grinned at him like flubbing was the whole point, and maybe it was.

When they finished, the room clapped the way rooms clap for children and brave men: without any polish at all. And Petra looked up and found Astrid leaning in the kitchen doorway, and the look on her face was the one from the empty bakery the night after the verdict, when she'd sat down in the middle of the floor to listen to him play for no one—the look of a person doing the thing no one had ever done for him.

He'd spent two years thinking his hands had betrayed him. It had taken a town, a kitchen, a stack of letters, and one sharp-tongued chef to teach him the truth: his hands had been fine. He had simply been playing for people who weren't listening.

These people were listening. This woman had always been listening, even when she was only reading.

Later, after the last guests drifted out into the spring evening and the jazz band was bribed into going home, they locked the door together and stood in the empty dining room, the letters glowing behind the glass in the lamplight.

"We should frame one more," Astrid said.

"We framed all of them."

"Not one of the letters." She reached into her apron pocket and produced a piece of parchment paper, folded, flour-dusted. "One of the *notices*. The eviction. It should live next to the permits. So every time I have a terrible day, I remember that we won."

"You framed the eviction notice already. It's by the door."

"This one's different." She unfolded it. It was not an eviction notice. It was a lease—the new one, the co-signed one, his name beside hers—and in the margin, in her handwriting, she'd added a line: *Renewable annually, in perpetuity, by mutual argument over menu music.*

Petra read it twice. His throat did something inconvenient.

"You can't amend a lease in pen," he managed.

"Watch me." She held out the pen.

He signed the margin. It was, legally speaking, meaningless. It was, in every other sense, the most binding document he had ever executed.

* * *

**Astrid**

Spring turned toward summer the way it does in coastal towns—reluctantly, beautifully, one fog-burned morning at a time.

The kitchen found its rhythm. Saturdays meant cello class in the back room and cinnamon rolls in the case, because Astrid had learned that parents would attend anything if you fed them. Petra's students gave a recital in June that was, objectively, a disaster, and subjectively the best afternoon of Astrid's year: eleven children, eleven cellos of varying sizes, one piece played in eleven different keys simultaneously, and a standing ovation from a room that included three fishermen and the mayor.

Petra cried a little. He pretended it was the onions. Astrid, who had no onions in the building, let him have it.

They still wrote to each other, sometimes. Not through the library—Mrs. Okafor had retired from her matchmaking post with great ceremony—but by hand, left under each other's pillows or propped against the salt cellar. Short letters now. *You were right about the rye. I'm never telling you that again. —A.* And: *Marisol asked if we're married. I said the paperwork was pending. She said "that's not an answer." She's right. —P.*

It was on one of those June mornings, fog burning off the harbor, that Astrid came down to open the kitchen and found the mail slot stuffed with a thick envelope, postmarked Portland, addressed to *Petra Underhill and A. Voss*.

She set it on the counter next to the espresso machine and made coffee and tried not to look at it, which lasted four minutes.

Petra came down at seven, hair still damp, and saw the envelope, and went still in the doorway in a way she recognized—the old flinch, the ghost posture.

"It's from the festival committee," she said. "I checked. Not Hale. Hale is *gone*, love. He's somebody else's forged signature now."

Petra opened it at the counter. She watched his eyes move, watched his breath do the thing it did.

"What is it?" she asked.

He set the letter down. He was smiling, but it was the complicated smile, the one with history in it.

"The Vienna masterclass program," he said. "The one Hale promised me—the real one, the one that existed before he touched it. They heard about the hearing. They heard about—" he gestured vaguely at himself, at the kitchen, at all of it, "—everything. They're offering me a summer residency. Teaching. Playing. In Vienna."

The kitchen hummed around them. The ovens ticking as they warmed. The harbor fog lifting off the water outside the windows.

"When?" Astrid asked, and was proud of how steady her voice was.

"July. Six weeks." He turned the letter toward her, and she saw that beneath the printed offer, in his own handwriting, he had already written a reply in the margin—the way she had amended the lease, the way they did everything now, in margins, in pen, in front of each other. It said: *Honored. I can't leave the kitchen. But send me your students, one summer, and I'll send them home.*

"You answered it already," she said.

"I answered it already." He looked up at her, and his hands—those quiet, steady, forgiven hands—were completely still. "I spent two years running out the clock on a lease. I'm not going to spend the rest of it running out the clock on anything else. Besides." He nodded at the framed letters behind the counter, at the eviction notice by the door, at the whole improbable, flour-dusted, hard-won life of it. "I have a kitchen to run."

Astrid Voss, who did not cry, who had not cried through the forgery or the hearing or the recital, wiped her eyes with a flour-dusted wrist and said, "You have a *kitchen* to run. And a class in twenty minutes, and Marisol will shake with confidence whether you're here or not."

"Then we'd better open," Petra said, and flipped the sign on the door to *OPEN*, and the bell over it rang out into the spring morning like the first clear note of something with many, many movements yet to play.
