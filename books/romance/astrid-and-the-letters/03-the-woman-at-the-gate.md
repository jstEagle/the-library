## 3. The Woman at the Gate

I had rehearsed the drive for three days, which was ridiculous, because you cannot rehearse a driveway. But I'd done it anyway—every version of my opening line, every tone from brisk to conciliatory to the one where I pretended we were two strangers who happened to share a piece of paper and a grandmother's worth of history between them.

*Good morning. I understand we're co-owners.* Too formal. Sounded like a summons.

*You're probably wondering why I'm here.* No. He wasn't. Nobody wonders why Astrid Vane shows up anywhere; they wonder why she took so long.

*I'm not here to apologize.* That one, at least, was true, so I kept it, folded it into my chest pocket like a receipt I intended to present at the appropriate moment, which was never.

The orchard looked worse than grief. That was my first thought, pulling up at the rusted gate just after seven in the morning, when the light comes in low and honest and forgives nothing. Half the trees had gone up in dead white limbs that caught the sun like bones. The grass stood knee-high and going to seed. A drainage ditch along the eastern row had silted shut years ago, and the standing water had turned the low ground into a swamp that smelled of rot and iron—the particular smell of a place deciding whether to die.

And there, in the middle of it all, a man I recognized by his shoulders before his face.

Lior Brightwater had always carried himself like an apology being made in advance. In school he sat in the back of rooms as if he'd been assigned there by some committee of his own shame. But this man at the gate was different. This man stood with his feet planted and his sleeves rolled and a pair of work gloves hanging out of his back pocket, surveying my grandmothers' orchard—I say *my* because possession is nine-tenths of spite—with the expression of someone who has already decided the place is his.

He heard the truck before he saw it. Of course he did. He turned, and I watched four years hit him all at once, watched it land in stages: recognition, then disbelief, then something rawer that he smothered before it could reach his face. He was good at that. He'd always been good at that. Lior could hold a note steady through anything except the truth.

"Astrid." My name in his mouth sounded like a key turning in a lock that hadn't been oiled in years.

"Lior." I got out of the truck. My legs were steadier than I deserved. "You're blocking the gate."

"I'm standing near the gate."

"You're blocking it. There's a difference, and it's about eighteen inches."

He didn't move. Neither did I. Somewhere in the grass a bird was conducting its morning business with more diplomacy than either of us managed.

"I suppose you got the letter," he said.

"From the county? Yes. And I got the deed. My copy arrived Tuesday." I reached into the cab and pulled the envelope out, held it up between two fingers like evidence at a trial. "So before you get any ideas about fences or surveys or whatever it is people do when they inherit half of something and pretend the other half doesn't exist—"

"I wasn't pretending anything."

"You've been here how long? Three days?" I nodded at the barn, where I could see, through the open door, the shape of a cot and a kettle and a man's whole life arranged in miniature. "Three days, and already the gate's yours."

"The gate is rusted open," he said. "It can't close. It hasn't closed in probably a decade. So technically neither of us owns a functioning gate, which feels thematically appropriate."

I hated him a little for that. I hated him more because the joke was good, and because his voice hadn't changed, still that low careful instrument, and because hearing it did something to my chest that I had traveled four hundred miles specifically to prevent.

* * *

We argued about drainage first, because drainage was safe.

"That ditch needs clearing," I said, walking the east row while he trailed me like a skeptical foreman. "The water's backing up into the root zone. See the yellowing on the lower leaves? That's not disease, that's drowning. Those roots have been sitting in soup for two seasons."

"And you know this because?"

"Because I spent every summer from age eight to age twenty-two learning this orchard from a woman who could diagnose a tree by the sound its leaves made in wind. Because I have a degree in soil science that you apparently don't remember. Because it's obvious, Lior. It's been obvious since the parking lot."

"Edith knew this place," he said quietly. "My grandmother. She kept it alive alone for the last ten years."

"I know who Edith was." I stopped at the worst of the standing water, where the mud had gone the color of old bruises. "I knew her better than you think."

That landed. I saw it land. He opened his mouth and then closed it, and for a moment we stood on opposite sides of the flooded ditch like two armies observing a truce neither had signed.

"She wrote to us," I said, before I could stop myself. Which was true, and also not mine to say, and I heard myself stepping over a line I'd drawn in my own head somewhere on the highway. "Never mind. The point is the ditch. Clear it, grade it toward the culvert, and the low rows dry out by June. Don't, and you'll lose the Gravensteins on this end within three years."

"*You'll* lose them," he said. "*We*. Apparently it's 'we' now."

"It was always 'we.' That's rather the point of a deed with two names."

"Then explain to me—" and here his voice finally cracked its careful casing, and underneath it was the boy I'd known, furious and hurt and trying so hard to be reasonable about it, "—explain to me why it took a county clerk and a piece of registered mail for you to remember that. Five years, Astrid. You left without—"

"Don't." 

"—a word. Not a letter. Not a phone call. I stood in your mother's driveway and she told me you'd gone and that it was better this way, and I believed her, because what else does a nineteen-year-old do with a sentence like that—"

"I said don't." My voice came out flat and hard, a door closing. "You want to talk about the past? Fine. Here's my past: I got a letter from a lawyer saying Edith Brightwater left me half an orchard, and I drove here because a deed doesn't care about feelings. That's the entire conversation. Everything else is weather."

"Everything else is *weather*," he repeated.

"Yes."

"You left me like a storm front and I'm supposed to call it weather."

"If the boot fits."

He laughed then—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a man finding the exact shape of a wound. "God. Four years of conservatory taught you nothing about subtlety."

"Conservatory teaches you exactly one thing: how to keep playing when everything falls apart around you. I learned it well."

Something shifted in his face at that—some flicker, quickly buried, of a knowledge I couldn't name. Later I would learn what it was. Later I would learn a great many things. But that morning I only saw him go carefully blank, the way he used to go blank whenever anyone mentioned his hands, and I filed it away in the drawer where I kept everything I refused to think about regarding Lior Brightwater.

* * *

We moved to the dead limbs because arguing about dead limbs was easier than arguing about us.

"There," I said, pointing at a apple tree near the fence line, its crown full of pale brittle wood. "Dead. All of it. Fire blight, probably, years ago, never cut out. It'll spread if you leave it."

"It looks fine from here."

"It looks fine from there because you don't know what you're looking at. Come here."

He came. He came reluctantly, with the exaggerated patience of a man humoring a lunatic, but he came, and when I snapped a dead twig off and handed it to him he took it, and the brush of his fingers against mine lasted maybe half a second and rearranged my entire nervous system.

I kept my eyes on the twig. Dry, gray, snapping clean. "Feel that? Dead wood snaps. Live wood bends. That's your whole test. Snap or bend."

"Bend," he said, looking at me instead of the branch.

"The *branch*, Lior."

"I know what you meant."

I took a step back. The morning had gotten warm, or I had. "There are forty-one trees on this property. I counted from the road. At least a dozen need deadwood cut out, six are candidates for removal entirely, and the rest are salvageable if we clear that ditch and get air moving through the canopy. That's the situation. That's what I came to say."

"You drove four hundred miles to deliver a horticultural report."

"I drove four hundred miles because half of this land is legally mine and I refuse to watch it rot under the management of a cellist who thinks dead wood 'looks fine.'"

"A former cellist," he said, and the words dropped between us like something heavy into deep water.

I waited for him to explain. He didn't. He just stood there with the dead twig still in his hand, turning it over, and I understood suddenly that I had stumbled onto something—that the collapse I'd read about secondhand, the ruined concert, the silence afterward, was not a story he told but a wound he kept, and that I had just pressed on it without meaning to.

For one stupid, treacherous moment, every argument I'd built on the drive fell down. I wanted to ask. I wanted to sit down in the wet grass and say *tell me what happened, tell me everything, I read about it in a music blog at two in the morning and cried into a dish towel like an idiot.* Instead I said:

"Well. Former cellists can still operate a pruning saw."

"Can they."

"It's mostly grip and stubbornness. You have at least one of those."

His mouth twitched. He fought it. He lost. And for about three seconds we stood in the ruins of my grandmothers' orchard almost smiling at each other, and it was the most dangerous moment of the entire morning, more dangerous than the shouting, because smiling was the thing we used to do before anything else.

I killed it deliberately. "I'll be staying at the motel in town. I'll come out at seven tomorrow to start on the ditch. You can help or not."

"This is my—this is *our* property. You don't set the schedule."

"Somebody has to. You've had three days and produced a cot and a kettle."

"The kettle is significant."

"The kettle is not drainage."

"Astrid." He said my name differently this time. Slower. Like a man setting down something heavy to free his hands. "Why are you really here?"

And there it was. The question I'd driven four hundred miles to avoid, standing in the middle of the path like a fallen tree.

Because my mother had spent twenty-three years teaching me that love was a debt you paid by disappearing. Because I had a folder of unsent emails and a savings account that was a rounding error from zero. Because the orchard was the last place on earth I had ever been unafraid, and I had just learned it was dying, and I could not—could *not*—stand in my rented apartment and let it go under without me.

"Because I got a letter," I said. "From a lawyer. Saying Edith Brightwater left me half an orchard."

"You said that already."

"I'm a woman of limited material. I repeat myself."

He studied me for a long time. The light had come up fully now, and I could see him properly for the first time in five years—the new lines around his eyes, the way he'd filled out through the shoulders, the small scar on his jaw I didn't know the story of and hated that I didn't. He looked like a man who had been holding his breath for years and had forgotten he was doing it.

"Seven o'clock," he said finally. "There's a wheelbarrow in the barn with a bad wheel. And the shovel handles are rotten. If you're going to run this orchard, we'll need actual tools."

"Noted. I'll bring tools."

"And Astrid—" I was halfway back to the truck. I stopped but didn't turn around. "The Gravensteins. The ones by the ditch. Edith grafted those herself. She'd want them saved."

"I know," I said. "That's why I started there."

I drove out with the gate rattling behind me—it still couldn't close, that broken thing, that open wound of a gate—and only when I hit the county road did I let myself feel it, the thing under the anger like water under ice.

Relief. Stupid, enormous relief. He was real. He was real and angry and alive and making jokes about kettles, and for five years I had carried him around as a ghost, a boy-shaped absence, a debt I paid in installments of not-calling. Now he was a man with mud on his boots and grief in his jaw, and ghosts, whatever else you say about them, cannot stand in a gateway and block it.

I made it two miles before I had to pull over, and I sat in the truck on the shoulder of the county road with both hands on the wheel, shaking, laughing, crying, possibly all three, absolutely certain of only one thing:

Tomorrow at seven o'clock, I was going to see him again.

And God help me, some traitorous part of me had already started looking forward to it—which is when I remembered the other letter, the one waiting in my glove box, the one from Halloran Development offering cash for the land, and understood that the hardest conversation hadn't even started yet.
