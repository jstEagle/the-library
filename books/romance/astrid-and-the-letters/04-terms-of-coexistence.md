## 4. Terms of Coexistence

The problem with sharing a house with a ghost is that ghosts don't knock. Astrid Vane, it turned out, did worse than knock—she made lists.

I found the first one pinned to the kitchen door of the farmhouse on my third morning at the orchard, written in a hand so precise it looked typeset: *Drainage ditch behind the north rows is choked. Two dead apples by the fence line need felling before they fall on something we like. We should talk terms.*

*We should talk terms.* As if we were two corporations negotiating a merger instead of two people who had once—well. As if.

I read the note three times, hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something I could refuse. They didn't. That was the trouble with Astrid; she had always been able to make the reasonable thing sound inevitable. In the old days she'd used this power to get me to eat vegetables and attend her mother's interminable dinners. Now she was using it to conscript me into orchard maintenance, which felt like a crueler fate than either.

She was already outside when I came down, standing in the mud at the edge of the north field with her hands jammed in the pockets of a canvas coat that had seen more honest years than either of us. The morning was gray and low, the kind of sky that presses down on a place like a hand on a piano's strings, muting everything.

"Terms," she said, without turning around. She'd heard the screen door. Of course she had. Astrid missed nothing; it was one of the many things I'd spent five years trying to forget about her.

"You left a note."

"I leave notes because you don't answer letters." She turned then, and her face was carefully neutral, the way a stage is neutral before the curtain rises. "I thought we might avoid the ambiguity."

"Ambiguity," I repeated. It was a good word. It covered a lot of ground, the way floodwater covers a field—everything still there underneath, just invisible.

"The deed has two names on it," she said. "Yours and mine. Neither of us is leaving—I've watched you try for three days, and you're terrible at it, by the way, you keep packing the same bag and unpacking it again—and the land won't take care of itself. So. Terms."

I wanted to tell her that I hadn't been packing and unpacking anything, that my bag sat by the bed exactly where I'd dropped it, but that would have been admitting I'd noticed her noticing, and noticing each other was not on the list of permitted activities. Not yet. Possibly not ever.

"There's a table inside," I said finally. "And paper."

"And pens," she said. "I checked."

*

We sat across from each other at the kitchen table with a legal pad between us—the same table, I couldn't help noting, where my grandmother had once fed me bread and honey and told me that hearts were like orchards, they needed pruning or they went wild. Grandmother Eriksen had been full of aphorisms like that. I'd found them tiresome as a boy. I found them unbearable now, sitting across from the one person who could make my heart feel less like an orchard and more like a demolition site.

Astrid clicked her pen. "Work first. We split it. You take the barn side, I'll take the house side, and we alternate on the drainage."

"Fine."

"We split costs. Tools, seed, whatever the trees need. Receipts in the tin on the shelf."

"Fine."

"We don't sell without both signatures."

That one landed harder than it should have. I looked at her and she looked at me, and for a moment something moved behind her eyes—something quick and guarded, like a bird startled off a branch. Then it was gone.

"Fine," I said again. My vocabulary had apparently regressed to age six.

"Seasons," she said, writing. "You're here spring through autumn. I'm here winter into spring. Overlap in—" She paused, pen hovering. "March."

"Why March?"

"Grafting season," she said, as if I were an idiot. Perhaps I was. "My grandmother taught yours. Or yours taught mine. Someone taught someone, and now we both know how, and March is when it happens. Unless you've forgotten."

I hadn't forgotten. I wished violently that I had. Grafting meant her hands near mine over the same cut branch, meant the particular concentration on her face that I had once loved from across rooms, meant—

"March," I said. "Fine."

"Last item." She set the pen down. Her voice changed—not softer, exactly, but flatter, the way water goes flat before it freezes. "We don't discuss the past."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere under the floorboards, a pipe ticked as it warmed.

"That seems," I said, "practically unworkable."

"It's entirely workable. People do it all the time. Entire countries are founded on it."

"Countries also collapse."

"Then we'll be very efficient about our collapse." She wrote it anyway, in her typeset hand: *No discussion of the past. This includes but is not limited to: why I left, what happened after, and anything said or unsaid prior to either.* She underlined *unsaid*, which felt like a knife laid gently on the table, handle toward me.

"You understand that's most of what there is between us," I said.

"No," she said. "Most of what there is between us is forty acres of dying apple trees and a roof that leaks in three places. The past is just weather. We work around weather all the time."

I should have argued. Some part of me, some honest part buried deep under the scar tissue, knew that a truce built on silence was a wall built on sand, and that walls on sand do a great deal of damage when they come down. But the larger part of me—the part that had stood on a concert stage eleven months ago while four hundred people watched my hand seize and my career die in real time—that part heard the word *never discuss* and felt something like relief. Relief with teeth in it, but relief.

Because here is what I had learned, in the long humiliation of that year: feelings were a muscle, and muscles could be trained. Train them enough, deny them enough, and they went quiet. Stillness was achievable. I had achieved a kind of stillness that most people would call peace and I called survival, and Astrid was offering to codify it. To give it structure. A trellis for the vine of my avoidance.

"All right," I said. "Sign here."

She signed. I signed. The ink dried between us like a small, formal wound.

*

For nine days, the agreement held beautifully, which should have told us both everything.

We worked in parallel orbits. I took the barn side as contracted—cleared the fallen limb off the equipment shed, dragged three rotted crates out of the loft, discovered that mice had been living better in my grandmother's barn than I had been living anywhere for a decade. Astrid worked the house side, and I tracked her progress by sound: the scrape of her trowel, the slam of the well bucket, once, unmistakably, her singing—two bars of something old, cut off the moment she realized the windows were open.

At noon we ate at opposite ends of the porch with the legal pad between us like a demilitarized zone, marking tasks complete. At dusk we retreated to separate rooms. It was, I reflected, the most functional relationship I'd had in years, which said something devastating about my standards.

On the ninth day, the letter came.

I saw it first—a thick cream envelope in the mailbox at the road, wedged among the seed catalogs. My name on the front, in a font chosen by someone paid to make greed look friendly. I almost threw it away. Instead I carried it up the drive, and by the time I reached the gate I had already torn it open, because some instincts are older than caution.

*Lior Brightwater,* it began. *Meridian Development Group is pleased to offer...*

I stopped reading on the porch steps. Astrid came out with two mugs of coffee—one for me, I realized with a small internal jolt, though neither of us acknowledged it—and saw my face.

"What."

I handed her the letter rather than summarizing it, because summarizing would have required speaking, and speaking would have required a voice that didn't shake.

She read fast, lips slightly parted. *Meridian Development Group is pleased to offer $340,000 for the parcel known as Brightwater Orchard, contingent on rezoning approval, with additional consideration for expedited closing...*

"Three hundred forty thousand dollars," she said flatly.

"For dirt," I said. "For dead trees and a leaking roof and a drainage ditch full of silt."

"For your grandmother's trees," she said quietly, and then seemed to hear herself, and closed her mouth.

We stood there while the wind moved through the orchard—the living trees and the dead ones alike, no distinction in the sound they made, which struck me as either deeply democratic or deeply depressing—and I watched Astrid do arithmetic I couldn't see. Whatever she was calculating, it wasn't money. Or it wasn't only money. There was a tightness at the corner of her jaw that I remembered from another lifetime, the look she got when duty and desire pulled in opposite directions and she was deciding, alone, which one to bleed for.

"We don't sell without both signatures," I said. "Item four."

"I know what I wrote." She handed the letter back. Her fingers didn't touch mine; she'd engineered the handoff to guarantee it. "I'm saying we should think about what we're actually doing here. This land is worth nothing as farmland. You know that. I know that. Meridian knows that. The only reason anyone wants it is location."

"The only reason anyone wants it," I said, "is that it's ours."

"Is it?" She looked out at the rows, and her voice did something complicated. "Your grandmother left it to both of us, Lior. Both of us. After everything. Did you ever ask yourself why?"

And there it was—the past, arriving through the back door of a question, despite item seven, despite the underline beneath *unsaid*. I felt my jaw set. I felt the old machinery engage, the smooth deflection, the charming nothing-to-see-here smile I'd worn through dinners and interviews and one catastrophic post-concert reception.

"I assume she wanted the trees cared for," I said.

"Did she." Astrid picked up her coffee. "Or did she want us cared for, and the trees were just the excuse?"

I had no answer for that, so I gave her the one I'd been rehearsing since the county clerk's office: "It doesn't matter. We have an agreement."

"We have an agreement," she agreed, and there was something in her tone that made the words sound like the beginning of an argument rather than the end of one. She went inside. The screen door banged with a punctuation I chose not to interpret.

*

That night I lay awake in my grandmother's narrow bed listening to the house settle, and I thought about stillness.

Eleven months ago, mid-phrase, in front of four hundred people, my left hand had simply stopped obeying me. Not pain—worse, a kind of absence, a deadness, as if the nerve between heart and hand had been cut. The Elgar Adagio poured on without me for two full seconds, which is an eternity on a stage, an eternity long enough for every face in the hall to turn from rapture to pity. I had finished the piece. I remember being proud of that, afterward, for about a week. Then I remembered the reviews, the word *fragile* appearing in print attached to my name, and pride packed its bags.

Since then I had perfected the training regimen. No cello. No music halls. No conversations that went below the waterline. The heart was a muscle; I had exercised it into stillness; I had, I told myself, succeeded.

But lying there, I kept hearing Astrid's voice: *did she want us cared for?* And underneath it, fainter, the two bars of song she'd cut off at the window, and underneath that, deeper than all of it, the memory of her at nineteen, standing in her mother's doorway, saying *I can't, Lior, please don't ask me to explain* — and me, twenty-one and proud and wounded, not asking. Not following. Writing her one furious letter and then, when it came back unopened, writing nothing ever again.

Five years of silence, formalized into a legal document with seven items. Item seven: no discussion of the past. We had shaken hands on avoidance and called it terms.

The letter from Meridian sat on the dresser, cream and patient. Three hundred forty thousand dollars to walk away clean. No trees, no Astrid, no March grafting season with her hands near mine. A life with the volume permanently turned down—which was, I supposed, what I'd been shopping for since the stage.

I fell asleep composing counterarguments to a woman who wasn't in the room, which is a form of practice, if nothing else.

In the morning there was a new note on the kitchen door, in her typeset hand:

*Re: Meridian. We should talk. Not about the past. About the future, which apparently has a price tag.*

And beneath it, smaller, as if added later, in different ink, almost against her will:

*P.S. The ditch is cleared. Your half of the fence is next.*

I stood there holding the note, and I understood two things with sudden clarity. First: the truce was already cracking, and we both knew it, and neither of us was going to say so, because saying so would violate item seven, which governed the very fact of item seven's failure—an elegant trap, really, worthy of my grandmother.

Second: Astrid Vane had made me coffee this morning without being asked, and had cleared half the drainage ditch that was, by contract, mine.

Whatever we had signed on that legal pad, it was not holding. Something else was growing in its place, quietly, the way things grow in orchards—without permission, without announcement, and with absolutely no respect for the terms.

I got my coat. The fence wasn't going to fix itself, and March was coming.
