## 5. What the Trees Remember

The storm came through on a Tuesday night, the kind of wind that sounds like it has a personal grudge, and by Wednesday morning half of Row Nine was lying in the mud like casualties.

I stood at the gate with my coffee going cold in one hand and counted the damage the way I'd learned to count damage generally: quickly, without flinching, saving the flinching for later when no one could see. Two big limbs down off the oldest pippins. A young tree snapped clean at the trunk, which was almost merciful—nothing to save there, just something to grieve and remove. And one of the elders, the huge apple at the corner nearest the road, had split partway down its trunk, hanging open like a coat off one shoulder.

Lior came out ten minutes after I did, hair still wet, moving like a man who hadn't decided yet whether this land was his problem. He looked at the fallen limbs for a long moment.

"I'll get the saw," he said.

That was it. No argument about whose row it was, no reference to the agreement we'd drafted two weeks earlier with all the warmth of a lease termination. Just: *I'll get the saw.* I decided not to examine why that small sentence landed somewhere under my ribs. Some things you don't poke at. You've learned that. You learned it the hard way, five years ago, poking at nothing until it became everything.

We worked most of the morning without talking much. There's a particular silence between two people who used to talk constantly—a silence with furniture in it, history in it—and we moved around inside it carefully, handing each other tools, dragging brush, not quite touching. He was better with the chainsaw than I expected. I didn't tell him so. Compliments were not covered under the terms of coexistence.

By early afternoon we'd cleared the broken rows and dragged the salvageable limbs to the barn, and only the split elder remained. It was too valuable to simply cut down—one of the original trees, planted by hands neither of us had ever met—and too damaged to leave standing without doing something. Lior circled it twice, frowning.

"You'd want to brace it," he said. "Cable the two halves together before the next blow finishes it."

"Or take it out," I said. "It's a hundred years old. It's had a good run."

He looked at me like I'd suggested drowning a kitten. "It's *the* tree. Your grandmother—" He stopped himself. The name sat unspoken between us, one of the many we'd agreed not to say.

"My grandmother what?" I said, because apparently I was in the mood to poke at things today.

But he just shook his head and went to the barn for the cable, and I stayed with the tree, and that's how I came to be alone with it when I found what I found.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about old fruit trees: they are documents. They are written on, amended, annotated, corrected. Every pruning scar is a margin note. Every graft is a signature. My grandmother taught me to read them when I was small enough to ride her hip, back before either family stopped speaking, back when the Brightwaters and the Vanes spent more time in each other's orchards than their own kitchens.

So while I waited for Lior, I did what I always do with an injured elder. I put my palm flat against the split trunk—the wood cold and damp, smelling of rain and something faintly sweet, like cider remembered rather than tasted—and I read.

The lower trunk was all weather, all lichen, illegible. But up higher, where the bark had pulled apart along the split, the interior was exposed, and there they were.

Graft marks.

Not modern ones. Not the sloppy wedge cuts of some hired crew from thirty years ago. These were old, deliberate, beautiful—whip-and-tongue grafts made by someone who knew exactly what she was doing, the tongues interlocked like folded hands, healed over decades into smooth wooden seams you could barely find unless you knew to look. Three of them, maybe four, spaced up the trunk where a storm or a disease had taken the original crown and someone had rebuilt it, branch by branch, variety by variety.

I knew those grafts. God help me, I knew them the way you know your own handwriting.

My grandmother made cuts like that. She'd learned from her mother, who'd learned from—well, from wherever such things come from, the long female chain of hands teaching hands. She used to hold my fingers over a scion and say, *You don't force them together, Astrid. You fit them. You make the match so exact the tree forgets it was ever two things.* I was eight years old and thought she was talking about apples.

And here was the thing that stopped my breath, here in the wet cold with my hand on another woman's work: these weren't Vane grafts. Or—they were, but they weren't only. Because I recognized the technique, yes, the angle of the tongue cut, the particular patience of it—but I also recognized the *varieties*, now that I looked at the surviving branches above each seam. That one was a Brightwater pippin. I'd know the long spurs anywhere; half our side of the valley had grown from Brightwater stock. Which meant someone had taken Vane hands and Brightwater wood and joined them, deliberately, lovingly, on this tree, decades ago.

Someone had grafted my family onto his.

"Astrid."

I jumped. Lior had come back with the cable and a coil of wire, and he was looking at me looking at the tree, and whatever was on my face made him stop three steps out like I'd raised a hand.

"What," he said. Not a question. An invitation I could refuse.

Instead I took his wrist—not his hand, his wrist, I'm not ready for hands—and pulled him to the split. "Look. Here. And here."

He leaned in. I watched his face do what mine had done: the frown first, then the recognition arriving slowly, like light coming up over water. His fingers found the seam of the lowest graft and traced it, careful as a man touching a scar he'd forgotten he had.

"That's a whip-and-tongue," he said quietly. "That's—my grandmother did those. On our trees. I used to think she invented it." He moved to the second mark, then the third, and I watched him arrive at the same arithmetic I had. "This is a Brightwater pippin. Grafted onto—" He looked up at me. "Who did this?"

"Your grandmother," I said. "Or mine. Or both of them, which is what I'd bet." I kept my voice level, professional, the voice I use for drainage and dead limbs. It cost me something. "They worked together, Lior. Before any of it. Before us, even. This whole orchard is—" I gestured at the rows, the mud, the sky doing its gray afternoon thing overhead. "It's a collaboration. It always was. We're not the first two stubborn people to try to make this ground work."

He didn't answer right away. He stood with his hand flat on the trunk the way mine had been, and I watched something move across his face that I couldn't name and wasn't invited to name. Grief, maybe. Or the specific ache of learning that a story you've been telling yourself—the clean story, the story where your family was one thing and theirs was another and the line between them was bright and fixed—isn't true, never was.

"They never told us," he said finally. "Any of it. That they were—" He stopped. Started again. "I grew up thinking the Vanes were practically strangers. That's what I was told. That we barely knew each other."

"We spent every harvest together until I was twelve," I said. "You don't remember?"

"I remember *you*," he said, and then went red, and busied himself with the cable coil as though it had personally wronged him.

I let him have the escape. I was holding enough new things already. But I turned back to the grafts, because there was one more thing nagging at me, one more annotation in the wood, and I wanted to look at it properly before the light went.

There, at the top of the highest graft, where the seam had healed smoothest, someone had carved something small. Not initials—I'd seen initials, teenagers carve initials, initials mean nothing. This was deliberate, tiny, precise: a single letter cut into the healed wood years ago, when it was still soft enough to take a knife. An *A*. And beside it, older, weathered nearly to invisibility, an *L*.

I didn't point it out to him. I don't know why. Maybe because it felt private, a message passed between two women across sixty years, and I hadn't been given permission to forward it. Maybe because I was afraid of what it would do to his face if I showed him—that small carved hope, that *A* and *L* set into living wood by hands that must have believed the two names belonged together, believed it so firmly they wrote it into a tree.

Maybe because I was afraid of what it would do to mine.

* * *

We braced the tree before dark. It took both of us—he held the split closed while I set the cable, then we swapped, and somewhere in the middle of it we stopped being careful about touching, because you can't cable a hundred-year-old apple tree while maintaining diplomatic distance. His shoulder against mine. My knee against his boot. The ordinary physics of shared work, which is a language older than speech and harder to pretend you don't speak.

When it was done we stood back and looked at it: the old tree laced together, its two halves bound into one crown again, holding.

"It might take," Lior said. "If we're lucky. If the next storm isn't worse."

"The next storm is always worse," I said. "You brace anyway."

He glanced at me sideways, and I heard what I'd said a half-second after saying it, the way you do—heard how it sounded, how it applied, how it was the sort of thing you say about trees when you're actually saying it about everything else. He didn't call attention to it. Neither did I. The agreement held. Never discuss the past. We were very good at the agreement. We were so good at the agreement it was starting to feel like the most strenuous work either of us had ever done.

He gathered the tools. I raked the last of the mud flat. And as he walked toward the barn, he paused, and without turning around, said:

"My grandmother kept letters. I found a box of papers when I was clearing the cellar shelf last week. I haven't read them yet." A beat. "I just thought you should know they exist."

Then he was gone, and I stood alone in the blue evening with the braced tree creaking softly above me, and I thought: *letters.* Letters between whom? About what? And why did the word go through me like a splinter finding its depth?

Above me, in the healing wood, an *A* and an *L* held each other in the dark, the way they had for sixty years, patient as anything planted by women who knew that some matches take seasons to take—if you fit them exactly enough, if you refuse to let the storm finish the split.

I went inside and did not sleep, and did not stop wondering.
