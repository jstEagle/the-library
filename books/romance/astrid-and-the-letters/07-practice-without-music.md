## 7. Practice Without Music

The cello case had been sitting in the corner of the barn for six days, and I had developed a relationship with it not unlike the one I'd once had with a tooth that needed pulling. You don't look at it directly. You circle it. You tell yourself you'll deal with it tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, until the thing itself becomes a kind of furniture—present, patient, and quietly accusatory.

The orchard was better company. The orchard didn't ask anything of me except work.

I'd found the pruning shears in the toolshed on day three, hanging on a nail exactly where my grandmother must have left them a decade ago, the wooden handles worn smooth and pale at the grip. There was something in that wear that steadied me. Someone had held these for years and years, and the trees had kept growing anyway, indifferent to every crisis she'd ever brought to them. It seemed like useful information.

So instead of practicing—which was a word I could no longer say without hearing the sound of two thousand people inhaling at once—I pruned.

I should be clear about what my pruning actually looked like, because Astrid would later describe it to me with a cruelty she probably believed was kindness: like a man fighting an octopus. I approached each tree with the shears open and the manual open—the county extension office had a pamphlet, *Pruning the Home Orchard*, illustrated with cheerful line drawings of trees that bore no resemblance to the actual gnarled, opinionated creatures in front of me—and I cut where it seemed right, which is to say I cut where the branches annoyed me.

By the fourth morning I had done genuine damage. I'd taken a flush cut to the collar of the old Gravenstein by the well, the one with the split trunk, and sap was weeping down the bark like the tree was crying about it personally. I stood there holding the shears and felt the familiar heat climb my neck. Even here. Even three hundred miles from any concert hall, I could find new ways to be incompetent in front of an audience of one tree.

"You're cutting the tree's wrist."

Astrid's voice came from behind me, and I did not startle, which I want noted for the record took considerable discipline.

She was standing at the gate with two mugs of coffee and the particular expression of someone who has been watching longer than they've been willing to admit. Her hair was tied back with what appeared to be a strip of baling twine. She looked, infuriatingly, like she belonged here—like the orchard had been waiting for her specifically and was merely tolerating me as a temporary arrangement.

"The tree doesn't have a wrist," I said.

"Everything with branches has a wrist." She came through the gate without asking, which was apparently our arrangement now, this co-ownership extending itself into my mornings without negotiation. "The branch collar. Right here." She touched the swollen ridge where the limb met the trunk, her fingers quick and certain. "It's the tree's own healing tissue. Cut through it and the wound never closes. Cut just outside it—" she made a short, angled gesture in the air, "—and it seals itself by summer."

"I read the pamphlet."

"The pamphlet was written by someone who learned from a book." She handed me a mug. "Your grandmother learned from her mother, who learned from a man named Oskar Vane."

"My grandmother learned from your grandfather?"

"My great-grandfather." She said it lightly, but I watched her face while she said it, the way you watch ice on a pond in March. "The families traded techniques back and forth for sixty years. Didn't she ever tell you?"

"She told me stories," I said. "I mostly didn't listen. I was fourteen and I thought stories about grafting were a punishment."

Something moved across her face—not quite a smile, more like the memory of where smiles used to live—and she turned to the damaged Gravenstein before I could see where it went.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't only take the obvious things. When my career collapsed in that hall—when my left hand simply stopped answering, mid-phrase, in front of everyone—it didn't take just the performance. It took the practice room. It took the four a.m. fingering exercises. It took the specific pleasure of a difficult passage finally yielding, which had been, since I was seven years old, the only reliable happiness I knew. All of it gone, because every version of it now ended in the same image: my hands, failing, publicly.

But the body is a hoarder. It keeps old skills in drawers you forgot existed.

"Give me the shears," Astrid said, an hour into the morning, after I'd butchered a water sprout situation on the third row. "Watch."

She took the tool the way she used to take nothing, because Astrid Vane never held instruments—but watching her now, I understood she'd been holding them all along, somewhere else, in some other life I hadn't been invited into. Her grip was high on the handles, thumb loose, and when she cut, the whole motion came from her shoulder, one clean arc, no hesitation at the moment of contact. The branch fell. The cut surface was pale and perfect, angled so rain would run off it.

"You're gripping like it's a weapon," she said. "You're strangling it. The blade does the work; your hand just decides where."

"It's a metaphor for something, surely."

"Everything is." She put the shears back in my hand, and this was the part I wasn't prepared for: she didn't let go immediately. She adjusted my fingers—one off the top of the handle, thumb freed, wrist rotated a few degrees—and her hands were cold from the morning and rough at the fingertips in a way they hadn't been at twenty-two, and I stood very still and let a woman who had once destroyed me re-teach me how to hold a tool.

"There," she said, stepping back. "Again."

I cut. The branch came away in one motion, clean, almost silent. Something in my chest unclenched half a degree.

"Where did you learn this?" I asked. "Actually."

"Orchards," she said vaguely, moving to the next tree. "Various."

That was an answer shaped like a door closing, and I recognized the craftsmanship, because I'd been building those doors myself for five years.

* * *

We fell into it over the following days without ever agreeing to. That was the strange part—that neither of us proposed it, and yet every morning by eight I'd hear her boots on the gravel and the coffee would already be made, two mugs, because somewhere around day two I'd stopped making one. We worked the rows in a loose rotation, her calling corrections, me executing them, and gradually the corrections got fewer, which I chose to interpret as improvement rather than her giving up.

She corrected my grip the way a conductor corrects a section player: never with explanation, always with demonstration, and always, always with the assumption that you already knew how and were simply being lazy about it. "Shoulder," she'd say, when I started hacking from the elbow again. "Angle." Once, just "Lior," in a tone that contained an entire masterclass.

And here is the thing I keep circling and can't land: it felt like music. Not performing—God, not that—but the other thing, the thing underneath, the chamber-music thing, the wordless negotiation between two people playing the same phrase. When we worked a tree together, she'd hold a limb steady while I cut, and there was a rhythm to it, a call and response, her weight shifting to counterbalance mine, and twice we moved simultaneously in a way that should have required discussion and didn't, and both times we pretended not to notice.

Bodies are sentimental. That's the trouble with them. Mine remembered her—remembered the specific choreography of sharing space with her, the way you hand a person things before they ask, the way you can feel them deciding to move a half-second before they move—and it kept offering up these memories at inconvenient moments, like a friend who shows you photos of your ex at a dinner party.

On the sixth morning, we pruned the old pears along the north wall, the ones planted so close together their branches had grown into each other, fused in places, two trees sharing limbs like conjoined arguments. My grandmother had told me once—they were her favorites—that you couldn't prune them separately. You had to decide, cut by cut, which tree each branch belonged to.

"They're a mess," I said, standing under them.

"They're forty years of decisions nobody finished," Astrid said. She reached up and traced a junction where two limbs had grown together, bark seamless, no visible seam at all anymore. "See, you can't even tell where one ends."

"No."

She dropped her hand. We stood there a moment too long, both looking at the tree, neither looking at each other, and then she said, briskly, "Left side's yours," and the moment closed like water over a stone.

* * *

On the seventh evening I made a mistake. I was tired in the good way, the way I hadn't been tired in months—muscle-tired, honest-tired, tired from work instead of dread—and I walked back through the barn to put away the tools, and the cello case was there, and because my defenses were down and my hands were pleasantly ruined, I opened it.

I didn't take the cello out. Let's be clear. I lifted the lid, and the instrument lay there in its green velvet, strings dull, fingerboard dusty, looking less like a cello than like a photograph of one, and I looked at it for maybe ten seconds and closed the case again and stood in the dark of the barn breathing like a man who'd opened a door onto a long drop.

When I came out, Astrid was at the gate, locking up, and she looked at me—at whatever my face was doing—and said nothing. But the next morning there were three mugs of coffee on the well wall instead of two, and when I raised an eyebrow at her she said, "For after. Your hands will shake if you go straight from the shears to caffeine withdrawal," which was a lie so transparent and so obviously kind that I drank the coffee and thanked her and hated how much I meant it.

That afternoon, working the last row, she said, out of nowhere, "Show me how you used to stand."

"What?"

"When you played. Your grandmother described it once—she came to a recital, before—" She stopped, recalibrated. "She said you stood like the cello was telling you secrets. Show me."

"I don't—there's nothing to show. I don't play."

"You're holding the shears wrong again," she said, "so apparently you do nothing well right now, which means you have nothing to lose. Show me."

And because I was exhausted, and sun-stupid, and because some traitorous part of me wanted to stand that way again even empty-handed, I did it. I set my feet apart, left foot forward, and curved my arms around a cello that wasn't there, and settled into the old stance, knees soft, spine long, chin resting on air.

The silence where the instrument should have been was enormous.

Astrid watched me for a long moment. Her expression did something complicated—grief and recognition and something fiercer underneath—and then she stepped forward and put one hand flat against my shoulder blade, between the wings, and pressed gently down and in.

"You're collapsing here," she said quietly. "You always did, at the end of a long program. She mentioned it. Open the back. Play from here." Her hand stayed. "The sound comes from behind you. From the part of you that you can't see. That's why it scared you, I think. You had to trust a room full of strangers with the part of yourself you couldn't watch."

My eyes were burning, which I attributed to sawdust, and she withdrew her hand before I could be caught at anything, and picked up her shears, and said, "The Bartlett needs topping or it'll split in the next storm," in a perfectly ordinary voice, and we went back to work.

But I stood that way for the rest of the afternoon, whenever I remembered to—shoulders open, back strong, arms curved around nothing—and the nothing began, very slowly, to have a shape.

* * *

It was nearly dusk when I found it. I'd gone into the root cellar for the ladder—we'd been propping a heavy limb with a board—and while I was maneuvering in the dark my elbow caught the rotted shelf unit against the east wall, and the whole thing shifted, and a section of backing board crumbled away from the studs, leaving a gap perhaps eight inches wide.

Behind it, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone brittle as pastry, was a tin.

Not a small tin. A biscuit tin, dented, rust-bloomed at the corners, and heavy—heavy with paper. I stood there in the cellar dark with dust sifting down through the single shaft of light from the door, and I knew whose handwriting was inside before I untied anything, the way you know a piece of music from its first two notes.

I carried it up into the last of the daylight and set it on the well wall, and I sat beside it for a long time without touching it, while somewhere behind me Astrid raked the lanes, the scrape of her rake keeping a slow, patient time.

My grandmother's letters. Addressed, I could see now through the cloth, in her upright schoolteacher's hand—to the Vane family. To *the Vanes*, plural, over and over, year after year by the look of the postmarks' absence. Unsent. A correspondence conducted entirely with herself, spanning decades, sealed behind a rotted shelf in a cellar neither family had entered in years.

"Lior?" Astrid's voice, from the lane. "It's getting dark. Are you—"

I put my hand flat on the tin's cold lid, feeling the ridges of it like piano keys under my palm, and called back, "Found something. In the cellar."

Her footsteps stopped.

I heard her cross the yard, slow, and come to stand at the end of the wall, and look at the tin, and look at me, and the dusk gathered around us like a held breath. Whatever was in that tin, it belonged to both our families. Whatever my grandmother had spent thirty years writing and never sending, it had been written to *her* people.

"Well," Astrid said finally, and her voice was not quite steady. "Are you going to open it?"
