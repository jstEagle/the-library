## 4. Terms of Coexistence

The bell above the café door did its small brass damage at seven-forty in the morning, and Jonah Voss walked through it carrying a leather folder the way other men carried grievances — close to the body, both hands.

Hazel had known he would come. She had spent the night telling herself she didn't care whether he came, which was a different thing entirely from knowing, and by dawn she had reorganized the pastry case twice, wiped down the espresso machine until it gleamed like a confession, and baked a batch of scones she had no intention of serving him. The scones were for her. Armor you could eat.

"Sit anywhere," she said, because the alternative was saying his name, and she wasn't ready to find out what it did to her mouth after fifteen years of disuse.

He sat at the corner table by the window — the good table, the one with morning light and a view of the maples going copper along the street — and Hazel hated him a little for choosing it without knowing it was the good table, or maybe knowing, because he had sat there before, once, when they were nineteen and the world was still a place that made promises it intended to keep.

She poured two coffees from the urn. The urn coffee was her shame: burnt at six a.m., held at temperature for hours, tasting faintly of the metal it lived in. She served it to contractors and county road crews and anyone who ordered without looking at the menu. She served it now with something close to satisfaction.

Jonah wrapped both hands around the mug like a man warming himself at a fire he didn't trust.

"You look well," he said.

"Don't." She sat across from him, spine straight, apron still on because taking it off would mean this was a meeting between equals rather than an ambush she was hosting. "Let's not do the part where we pretend we're people who say things like that to each other."

Something moved behind his eyes — hurt, maybe, or its more patient cousin — and then it was gone, filed away behind the same careful blankness he'd worn yesterday in her doorway. He opened the folder.

"The offer expires in thirty-one days," he said. "Meridian Development. Marcus Hale's outfit. They want the whole parcel — forty acres, orchard, farmhouse, the creek frontage. Cash sale, as-is."

"How much?"

He told her. The number was obscene. It was the kind of number that paid off a bank, bought a new oven, replaced the awning, hired Priya full-time with benefits, and left enough over to never again stand at three in the morning doing arithmetic on a napkin while proofing dough went soft and sad beside her. Hazel felt the number land in her chest like a stone dropped into a well, and she waited for the splash, and it didn't come, because the well was deeper than she'd thought.

"That's for both halves," Jonah said. "Split down the middle, per the deed."

"Per the deed," she repeated. "Which is per the postcard."

His jaw tightened. "You've seen it, then. The boundary amendment."

"I've seen it." She turned her mug a quarter-turn on the table, a nervous habit she'd inherited from a woman who'd never been nervous in her life. "And I'm telling you, Jonah, my grandmother did not write that postcard. The cursive is wrong. Astrid Bellamy crossed her sevens. Whoever wrote that didn't cross a single one. And she dotted her i's like she was stabbing them. That postcard has no dots at all. It's all smooth little ticks, like someone practicing."

Jonah looked at her for a long moment. Outside, a delivery truck ground past, and the light shifted across his face and showed her, briefly, how tired he was — not the tiredness of travel but the older kind, sedimentary, laid down in layers over years.

"You remember her handwriting that specifically," he said. Not a question. Something under it, though. Something almost gentle, which was worse than hostility, because hostility she knew how to hold.

"She taught me penmanship every Sunday for eleven years. I remember everything about that woman." *Everything except,* said a small voice in the back of her skull, and she stepped on it before it could finish.

"So you're contesting the amendment."

"I'm saying it's forged."

"And if it is," Jonah said, leaning forward, forearms on the table, and up close she could see the scar on his knuckle — the one from the fence wire, the summer they were sixteen, when she'd laughed so hard she couldn't help him wrap it — "then the original boundaries stand, and the water rights go back to shared, and Meridian's appraisal drops by forty percent, and they walk, and we're both stuck owning half of something neither of us can afford to run alone."

The word *we* sat on the table between them like a live coal.

"There is no *we*," Hazel said. "There's a you and a me and a piece of paper that says we have to be in the same county for a month. That's not a we. That's a sentence."

"A sentence with a deadline." He almost smiled. It was terrible, seeing the ghost of the old smile on the new face — the same crooked architecture, weathered. "Thirty-one days. Then Hale's offer lapses and he moves to the Hollis farm, and whatever happens here happens slow, through lawyers, at four hundred dollars an hour."

"I don't have four hundred dollars an hour. I barely have four hundred dollars a month."

It was out before she could stop it, and she watched him hear it — watched his gaze flick, quick and involuntary, to the case of pastries, the peeling paint on the window frame, the espresso machine she'd polished so hard it looked new because new was what it wasn't. She saw him do the math she did every night. She saw pity start to assemble itself behind his eyes, brick by brick.

"Don't," she said again, sharper.

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You were about to feel sorry for me, and I will pour this coffee over your survey maps if you do."

He raised both hands, palms out, surrendering, and for one unguarded second the gesture was so purely, stupidly *himself* — the boy who used to raise his hands the same way when she caught him stealing green apples off the kitchen windowsill of the farmhouse — that Hazel had to look out the window at the maples until her face remembered its shape.

* * *

They talked terms the way countries talk terms: formally, through intermediaries of silence, each concession announced like a troop withdrawal.

One month. Thirty-one days, minus the ones already burned. In that time, they would settle the question of the postcard — verify it, contest it, or, failing both, negotiate a partition. Hazel would engage a document examiner; Jonah would pull the original deed from county records. Neither would sign anything with Meridian. Neither would speak to Hale alone.

"And the orchard itself?" Hazel asked. "Who's running it during the month? Because I drove past yesterday and block four is a jungle. If those apples aren't thinned by next week you'll lose half the crop regardless of who owns them."

Jonah's cup paused halfway to his mouth. "I've been thinning since Tuesday."

"At five in the morning?"

"Somebody has to. You're—" He gestured at the café, the room, her whole visible life. "Busy."

"I grew up in that orchard, Jonah. I can graft a branch in my sleep. Don't you dare stand there and imply I've forgotten how."

"I wasn't implying anything. I was stating a fact about your calendar." But his voice had changed key, gone quieter, and when he looked at her there was something in it that wasn't about apples at all. "You used to follow me around those rows like a shadow. You'd climb higher than me. You fell out of the Gravenstein when you were nine and got up and climbed it again the same afternoon, and Aunt Ruth had to hold you down to put ice on your ankle."

"I remember."

"Do you?" he asked, and it was too soft, too curious, a crowbar slipped into a seam, and Hazel felt the whole conversation tilt toward the place where the floor gave way — toward the last summer, the barn, the night the sheriff's car idled outside the Voss house and Jonah's truck was gone by morning with no note, no call, nothing but a fifteen-year silence shaped exactly like an answer.

Neither of them mentioned it. The silence had weight; she could feel it pressing down on the sugar bowl, on the salt, on the space between their hands on the tabletop, eight inches of oak that might as well have been the creek in flood.

"We split the work," she said, dragging the conversation back onto solid ground. "Orchard and deed, fifty-fifty. I take mornings here, afternoons there. You keep your five a.m. heroics if you must, but I'm not paying for your coffee if you drink the urn stuff all day."

"This coffee is a war crime."

"It's free."

"It's punishment." He drank it anyway, all of it, grimacing, and she caught herself cataloguing the grimace — the way his nose wrinkled, the way he set the mug down with exaggerated care, as though it might apologize — and stopped herself with the internal equivalent of biting through her own lip.

Rules, she told herself. We need rules.

"Ground rules," she said aloud. "One. We talk business only. Two. We don't discuss—" the pause lasted half a heartbeat too long, and they both heard what filled it, "—the past. Three. If either of us wants out, we say so plainly, no disappearing acts."

Something flickered across his face at *disappearing acts*, fast as a fish turning under water, and was gone. "Agreed," he said, and his voice was level, and she understood suddenly that he had expected rule three, had perhaps built his whole approach around surviving it, and her saying it out loud had cost him something he'd budgeted for and still hadn't saved enough to pay.

Good, she thought. And then, immediately, treacherously: *oh, stop.*

"Four," she said. "We tell each other the truth about the money. Both sides. No surprises. If Hale comes at either of us sideways, we hear it within a day."

"He already has."

The words dropped into the room like a glass hitting tile.

"What?"

"He called my motel last night. Left a message." Jonah pulled out his phone, scrolled, and set it on the table between them, screen up, speaker off, message open. Hazel read it twice. It was brief, courteous, surgical: an acknowledgment of her financial situation — *which we understand may make a timely resolution attractive to all parties* — and an invitation to discuss options regarding *her specific interest* in the parcel.

Her specific interest. Her half, severed from his. The stone in her chest finally splashed, and the splash was cold.

"He's trying to cut you out," she said.

"He's trying to cut us apart. There's a difference, and the difference is that cutting us apart works better." Jonah took the phone back. His thumb hovered over the message and then he deleted it, deliberately, holding her eyes while he did it. "I'm telling you because of rule four. Not because I think you'd take it."

"But you think I need the money."

"I know you need the money. I did the math on your window frames before you finished pouring the coffee." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow crueler. "Hazel. I'm not here to save your café or sink it. I'm here because my father left me half an orchard and a mess, and I want it settled clean. That's all. Whatever else you've decided about me — and you've clearly decided plenty — decide this too: I don't lie about numbers."

*But you lied about everything else,* she thought. *You lay down one night inside a friendship and were gone before the dew dried, and I have spent fifteen years building a person who doesn't need you, and that person is sitting right here drinking your terrible opinion of my coffee, and she would like you to leave.*

What she said was: "Then we have a truce."

"Armed neutrality," he agreed, and there it was again, the ghost-smile, and she stood up abruptly because if she stayed seated one more minute she was going to smile back, and smiling back was a breach she could not afford in week one of a thirty-day war.

* * *

He helped clear the mugs. She noticed this the way you notice a splinter — a small wrongness under the skin. He stacked them the way restaurant people stack them, bottom-up, handles aligned, and she wondered briefly where a man learned that, and then refused to wonder anything else about him, ever, as a matter of policy.

At the door he paused, folder under his arm, morning light full on him now, and for a moment he looked like he wanted to say something that wasn't on any term sheet. His mouth opened. Closed.

"Your scones are burning," he said instead.

She whirled. The oven timer hadn't gone off — she'd forgotten to set it, first time in ten years — and the smell reached her a half-second later, dark and acrid, and by the time she'd wrenched the tray out onto the rack, black smoke curling off twelve ruined scones, the bell over the door had already chimed him out.

Through the window she watched him cross the street to his truck. He stopped halfway, looked back at the café — at her, framed in the glass, holding a tray of charcoal — and lifted one hand. Not a wave, exactly. More like a salute between opposing officers.

Hazel set down the tray. She scraped the scones into the compost, every one, and stood with her hands braced on the counter, breathing the burnt smell, feeling it settle into her hair, her clothes, the fabric of the morning.

Thirty days. She could do thirty days standing on her head. She had survived worse than a month of Jonah Voss — she had survived his leaving, hadn't she, had built a whole life on the far side of it, a life with a menu and a mortgage and a mantra about trainable hearts.

So why, she asked the empty café, did the truce feel less like a treaty and more like a fuse?

On the counter by the register, where he'd set it down without her noticing, lay the leather folder. She flipped it open. Survey maps, the Meridian letter, a copy of the deed — and beneath them, folded into quarters, a page torn from a spiral notebook, written in Jonah's blunt, slanted hand:

*Original boundaries attached. Postcard amendment filed 2019. Check who filed it.*

Filed 2019. Four years ago. Six years after her grandmother died.

Hazel stood very still in the smell of burnt sugar, and somewhere down the street a church bell rang the half hour, and she thought: *someone wanted this sale badly enough to forge a dead woman's hand.*

The question that followed her into the kitchen, that stayed with her through the lunch rush and the wiping down and the last lock of the night, was simple and cold:

*Someone besides me.*
