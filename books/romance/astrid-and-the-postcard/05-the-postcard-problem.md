## 5. The Postcard Problem

The postcard arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day Hazel's grandmother had done her errands, the day she wore her good coat to the bank and bought a single peach from Delgado's stand because, as she said, *a woman should buy one beautiful thing a week and eat it standing up.*

The postcard was not beautiful. It was a photograph of the orchard itself — some stock image, probably licensed off a website, the rows too green, the sky too blue, the light falling in that generic golden wash that no actual afternoon in Sutter County had ever produced. On the back, in faded blue ink, was a message.

Hazel read it three times at the counter while the espresso machine hissed behind her and Priya called out an order for two cortados and a slice of the almond cake. The café noise moved around her like water around a stone.

*Dearest Hazel,* it began. *If you're reading this, then Mr. Okonkwo has finally done his job. The boundary lines have been changed as discussed. Block Four goes to the Voss side, all of it, and I've signed what's needed. Don't fight it. Some fences are meant to move. All my love, Astrid Bellamy.*

Dated eleven years ago. Postmarked from right here in town.

Hazel set it down on the counter, next to the register, next to the little ceramic owl her grandmother had given her when she was nine — *for wisdom, and because owls are the only birds that look like they're listening* — and she stared at it the way you stare at a word you've written so many times it stops meaning anything.

"Boss?" Priya said. "The cortados are getting sad."

"Give them to table four," Hazel said. "Tell them the sadness is intentional. Rustic."

Priya looked at her a beat longer than necessary, then went. That was the thing about Priya — she knew when to push and when to simply take the tray and go, and in four years she had developed an unerring instinct for which was which. Hazel suspected the instinct ran both directions, and that Priya could read the state of the café's finances off Hazel's face the way sailors read weather off water. She kept waiting for the question. It hadn't come yet. She was grateful and ashamed in equal measure, which was becoming a familiar pairing, like salt and caramel.

She picked up the postcard again.

Here was the situation, as Mr. Okonkwo — her grandmother's lawyer, now hers, a patient man with reading glasses he cleaned with his tie — had explained it over the phone that morning: the deed to Bellamy Orchard was not a simple half-and-half. It would have been, except for this postcard. Filed with the county eleven years ago, accompanied by a notarized statement and a copy of the card itself, it documented Astrid Bellamy's intent to alter the boundary between the two halves of the property. Block Four — twelve acres, the oldest trees, the ones with the deep roots and the heavy fruit, the block that bordered the creek and therefore held the water rights — would pass entirely to the Voss share. Her grandmother had supposedly sent the card to Hazel herself, care of the café's old address, and the filing relied on its existence as evidence of communicated intent.

"So the orchard isn't half mine," Hazel had said.

"The orchard is half yours," Okonkwo had said carefully, "and then complicated. If the postcard is valid, your half is worth considerably less than Jonah Voss's half. If it's invalid, we're back to fifty-fifty, clean."

"And how do I find out if it's valid?"

"That," he'd said, "is the question."

Hazel turned the postcard over in her hands. The paper was wrong first — too glossy, too modern, the kind of card sold in racks at gas stations and gift shops, not the heavy matte stock her grandmother had ordered by the box from a printer in Sacramento, cards printed with her own photographs, her own apple blossoms, her own handwriting pressed into every note she'd ever sent. But fine. People changed printers. People bought convenience cards at the pharmacy when the good ones ran out. Hazel made allowances. She was, professionally speaking, a maker of allowances; her entire business model rested on them.

Then she read the message again, slowly, and stopped making allowances.

Because Hazel Hartwell had been taught cursive by Astrid Bellamy, and taught the way old women teach things — relentlessly, lovingly, with a wooden ruler across the knuckles of your self-esteem until your letters stood up straight or you cried trying. Every summer from the time she was seven, an hour at the farmhouse kitchen table, the sun through the lace curtains making stripes on the paper, her grandmother's hand closed warm over hers: *No, sweetheart, the tail on the g goes below like water off a roof. Again.* By ten Hazel could forge her grandmother's signature well enough to sign school permission slips during the years her father couldn't be bothered. By thirteen she could reproduce the whole alphabet of the woman's hand — the particular way Astrid crossed her t's low and flat, like a lid on a box; the way her capital A never quite closed at the top, leaving a small beak of an opening; the way her r's ended in a tiny upward flick, impatient, like a bird taking off.

The handwriting on the postcard did none of these things.

The t's were crossed high and looping, showy, a flourish Astrid Bellamy would have mocked into oblivion over a single kitchen-table hour. The capital A was closed tight as a fist. The r's just lay there. And the ink — Hazel held the card up to the window light, tilting it, watching the way the blue sat on the paper rather than in it — the ink looked recent. Not eleven years recent. Not even five. The kind of blue that came out of a pen bought last month at the office supply store, the kind that sat on top of cheap glossy cardstock instead of bleeding into it the way real ink bled into real paper, the way her grandmother's ink always had, feathering at the edges like roots into soil.

Hazel put the card down. Picked it up. Put it down again.

"You're doing the thing," Priya said, appearing at her elbow with a rag and the expression of someone who had decided the cortados could wait. "Where you hold an object and your face does arithmetic."

"It's nothing."

"It's a postcard."

"It's a—" Hazel stopped. Started over. "It's supposedly from my grandmother. It changes who owns what in the orchard."

Priya's eyebrows went up. "And?"

"And the handwriting's wrong."

"Wrong how? Like, forged-wrong?"

"I don't know yet." Which was a lie, and they both heard it. Hazel slid the card into her apron pocket, where it lay against her hip like something with a pulse. "I know it's wrong. I just don't know what it means yet."

* * *

She closed early that night — a sin she'd been committing more often lately, since the numbers had started their slow slide, though she told herself it was inventory — and drove out to the orchard alone, because there was a person she needed to see and it wasn't Jonah Voss.

Astrid Bellamy had been dead six years, but she had never entirely left the farmhouse. She was in the wallpaper, in the water stain shaped like Portugal on the ceiling of the front room. She was in the kitchen most of all, in the hooks by the stove where her cast iron still hung, in the drawer that held the wooden ruler, which Hazel had never thrown away and never used and could not explain why she kept. Hazel let herself in with the key on the nail inside the porch light fixture — a hiding place so obvious it had survived forty years of burglars, or more accurately forty years of no burglars, because this was Sutter County and the worst crime anyone committed was parking across two spaces at the Feed & Seed.

The house smelled of dust and apples and, underneath, faintly, impossibly, of her grandmother's soap — lye and lavender — though the bars of it had been gone for years. Memory did that. Memory furnished empty rooms.

Hazel sat at the kitchen table, at the spot where she'd learned her letters, and laid the postcard down under the window where the evening light came through slantwise and gold, the same light, the same angle, the same hour her grandmother had always said was the only honest light for judging anything. *You want to know if a thing is true, you look at it at five o'clock in October. Everything lies at noon.*

She took out her phone and photographed the card, then opened the folder of scanned letters she kept in the cloud — a habit from the year after the funeral, when she'd digitized everything in the cedar chest, unable to throw away a single envelope. Forty-one letters. Birthday cards. Notes tucked into shipments of jam. One long letter written the week after Hazel's father died, which Hazel had read exactly once and then not again, because grief was a muscle too and hers had cramped.

She spread three of the letters on the table beside the postcard and compared them the way she'd compare doughs — texture, elasticity, the give of the thing.

Her grandmother's cursive leaned slightly right, but not much; it walked, it didn't run. The postcard's writing sprinted. Her grandmother's words were spaced evenly, each line measured out like she was setting plants in a row — *even spacing, sweetheart, even spacing, the eye trusts order* — while the postcard's lines drifted, crowding at the start of each sentence and loosening toward the ends, the rhythm of someone writing fast without caring, or writing carefully without knowing the hand they were imitating. And the loops — Astrid's descenders on y and g and j were shallow, clipped, practical, a woman who grew up in drought years and wasted nothing, not water, not ink, not motion. The postcard's descenders plunged, extravagant, dragging tails halfway into the line below.

It wasn't her grandmother's hand. It was someone's idea of her grandmother's hand, assembled from a distance, from memory, from maybe a signature on a document. It was a costume. It was her grandmother's voice spoken by a stranger doing an impression.

Hazel sat very still in the gold light and felt the strange cold weight of it settle onto her shoulders.

Someone had written this. Someone had wanted Block Four to belong to the Voss side of the ledger — wanted it badly enough to commit fraud, to file papers with the county, to invent a dead woman's blessing. And the obvious answer, the answer that sat there fat and ugly and convenient, was Jonah. Or Jonah's family. The Voss side gained twelve acres and the water rights; the Voss side had motive written all over it in fake cursive.

But.

But her grandmother had died six years ago, and the postcard was dated eleven years ago, filed shortly after. And something about the dates snagged in Hazel's mind like a burr in a sock — the way the card was *supposedly sent to her*, care of an address she hadn't lived at since she was twenty-two. The way it had surfaced through the lawyer, through the county, through channels, instead of arriving anywhere real. The way it read less like a letter from a grandmother and more like a legal instrument wearing a grandmother's perfume.

And underneath all of it, quieter, worse: *don't fight it. Some fences are meant to move.* Her grandmother had spent seventy years fighting for every fence line on that property. Astrid Bellamy had once gone after a surveyor with a garden hose over six inches of disputed boundary near the well. The woman in that postcard — placid, surrendering, philosophizing about movable fences — was not a woman who had ever existed.

Hazel gathered the letters, squared their edges, and put them back in her bag. She left the postcard lying on the kitchen table in the honest light, because it seemed right somehow to leave the lie sitting in the place where the truth had been taught.

Outside, the orchard was going purple at the edges, the rows running away into dusk, and somewhere down in Block Four the old trees stood in the dark holding their fruit the way they'd held it for sixty years, indifferent to deeds and boundaries and the small ambitions of people. Hazel stood on the porch a moment, breathing the cold apple air, and understood that the ground had shifted under her twice now — once when the inheritance landed, and once tonight, when the inheritance revealed itself to be built on sand.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop turning over, walking back to the car with her breath fogging: if the postcard was forged, then someone had gone to enormous trouble to make sure the land divided unevenly. And if it was forged recently — and the ink said recently, said *within years, not decades* — then whoever did it hadn't done it for money eleven years ago. They'd done it for money soon. They'd done it knowing the orchard might come up for sale.

They'd done it knowing *she* might come back.

Hazel drove home with the windows cracked despite the cold, the orchard shrinking in her mirror, and by the time she reached the café her hands had stopped shaking and her mind had done what her mind always did under pressure: it made a list. Tomorrow, call Okonkwo. Ask who filed the postcard, who notarized it, whose name was on the paperwork. Ask about the county clerk's office, records, dates. Find out whether forgeries left fingerprints the way pastries left crumbs — and they did, everything left crumbs, you only had to be willing to get on your knees and look.

She parked behind the café and sat in the dark car a moment. Through the kitchen window she could see Priya's bike locked to the rack, and the light she'd left on over the proofing bench out of habit, and she thought about how two weeks ago her biggest problem had been a bank letter, and how problems, like sourdough, only ever grew.

One thing she knew with total certainty, and it steadied her the way the ruler across the knuckles had once steadied her: Astrid Bellamy had not written that card. Whatever else was true — whatever Jonah Voss had or hadn't done, whatever games were being played with fences and water rights — her grandmother's hand was her own, and Hazel would know it blindfolded, underwater, at the end of the world.

Somebody had borrowed a dead woman's voice. And Hazel intended to find out who, and why, and what else they'd lied about — starting first thing tomorrow, with a phone call, and ending, she suspected, somewhere she couldn't yet imagine.

She locked the car. The postcard stayed behind her in the kitchen, lying in the dark on the old table, in the house that smelled of lavender and dust, waiting like a question nobody had finished asking.
