## 6. Rows and Ruin

The orchard smelled different in the morning than it did at dusk, and Jonah had forgotten that.

At dusk it was all sugar and shadow — the apples giving off their last warmth of the day, the air thick enough to taste. But at seven in the morning, with dew still hanging in the grass and the light coming low and gold through the rows, the orchard smelled green. Clean. Like something just beginning instead of something ending. He stood at the edge of Block Two with a thermos of coffee he'd bought from the gas station because he couldn't bring himself to walk into Hazel's café again so soon, and he breathed it in, and for one unguarded second he was seventeen years old and everything ahead of him was still possible.

Then Hazel came down the row with a pair of Felco pruners holstered on her hip like a sidearm, and the second ended.

"You're up early," she said. Not unfriendly. Not friendly either. She said it the way she might have said *the weather's happening* — an observation about conditions beyond anyone's control.

"Couldn't sleep." True, but incomplete. He'd slept four hours and dreamed about the barn fire twice, which was two more times than usual, and both times he'd woken with his heart going like a trapped bird and the taste of smoke in his throat that wasn't there. Fifteen years, and his body still hadn't gotten the memo that the fire was over. That most things were over.

Hazel nodded and kept walking, and he fell into step beside her because there was nowhere else to be. That was the arrangement they'd made over her terrible coffee three days ago: one month, shared access to the property, no discussion of the past. Armed neutrality. He'd thought it would be easier than this. He'd thought wrong.

They walked Row 12 together without speaking, their boots making parallel prints in the wet grass, and Jonah catalogued what fifteen years had done to the trees the way another man might catalogue damage after a storm. The Honeycrisps in Block Two were older now, their trunks thickened, some of them leaning the way old men lean. There was a gap in Row 9 where a tree had died and been pulled out — he could see the pale circle of the stump, already softening into the earth. Someone had replanted it. A young tree stood there, staked and thin, maybe three seasons into its life. He found himself absurdly moved by it, this small stubborn replacement standing where its predecessor had failed, and he didn't examine why.

"Grandma Astrid kept planting," Hazel said, following his gaze. She said it flatly, but he heard what was underneath it, the same thing he felt underneath his own silence: *she's gone and we're both still here and neither of us knows how to say that out loud.*

"She always said an orchard isn't land," Jonah said. "It's a promise you make to people you'll never meet."

"I remember." And then, quickly, as if she'd given away too much: "She said it to everyone. It wasn't special."

"No," he agreed. "It wasn't."

But it had been special when she'd said it to him, the summer he turned seventeen, standing right here in Row 12 with her hand on his shoulder and cider-sticky fingers, and he had believed — with the total, unexamined certainty of a boy who has never yet been broken — that he belonged here. That this place was as much his future as it was hers. That Hazel would follow him anywhere, because why wouldn't she? They were Jonah-and-Hazel, Hazel-and-Jonah, a single unit the town had long since stopped treating as two people. Mrs. Calloway at the post office gave them one envelope between them at Christmas. Old Man Petrosian paid them jointly for picking, splitting the cash down the middle without counting. They were not a couple, exactly — or they were, in the way that mattered more, the way that comes before romance and survives after it: best friends, co-conspirators, two halves of one plan.

And the plan had been simple. Jonah would go away for a year — agricultural school, a certificate, something to make him useful beyond strong arms and good instincts — and then he'd come back, and Astrid Bellamy was already talking about succession, about how the orchard needed young blood, and Hazel would run the kitchen side, the jams and the cider donuts and the little café counter she'd been dreaming about since she was twelve, and Jonah would run the trees, and they'd grow old arguing about irrigation the way Astrid and her late husband had grown old arguing about everything else, loudly and with enormous affection.

He had been so certain. That was the thing that still stunned him, all these years later — not the leaving itself but the certainty that had preceded it. Seventeen years old and certain Hazel Hartwell would follow him anywhere. Certain the world was a corridor with doors that opened in the direction you walked. He could still feel it, that certainty, like heat from a stove you've moved away from: the ghost of warmth on skin that's since learned cold.

"Your pruners are dull," Hazel said.

Jonah looked down. "They're fine."

"They're dull. You're going to crush the cambium instead of cutting it, and you'll get canker in half these cuts by spring." She held out her hand, palm up, imperious, and after a moment he surrendered the pruners. She produced a small whetstone from her jacket pocket — of course she carried one; of course she did — and sharpened them right there in the row, three strokes per bevel, quick and exact, while he watched her hands and tried not to remember every other time he'd watched her hands.

That was the trap of this place. Everything here was memory. He couldn't pick up an apple without remembering the summer they'd eaten windfalls behind the barn, sitting in the crook of the Gravenstein that had since split in the hailstorm year, daring each other to eat the sour ones. He couldn't look at the packing shed without seeing Hazel at fourteen, furious, having thrown his hat onto the roof over some argument he no longer remembered — only the fury, only the way she'd glowed with it, and the way he'd laughed until she laughed too, because that was the whole architecture of them: she burned, he laughed, and the fire went out.

Except once. Except the one fire that didn't go out. Except the night everything burned and he stopped laughing and left, and the laughing never came back, and neither did he.

"There." She handed the pruners back. "Try not to butcher anything."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you're cutting wrong."

He blinked. "I haven't cut anything yet."

"You're holding them wrong. Thumb through the big handle, not the little one. You'll have less control." She demonstrated in the air, brisk, not looking at him, already turning toward the next tree. "Angle cut, quarter inch above the bud, outward-facing. You know this."

"I know this," he said, stung despite himself. He was thirty-four years old. He had managed orchards in Yakima and Wenatchee, hundreds of acres, crews of forty. He knew which end of a pruner was which.

But his hands obeyed her anyway, thumb shifting, angle adjusting, and that was somehow worse — that after fifteen years and two hundred miles and everything that had happened and everything that hadn't, his body still took direction from Hazel Hartwell without consulting him first.

* * *

They worked the morning in a rhythm that felt dangerously like the old rhythm.

That was the thing nobody warned you about grief, Jonah thought, somewhere around the eleventh tree: it wasn't the absence that got you. It was the echoes. The way absence had holes shaped exactly like what used to fill them, and if you weren't careful you'd fall into one without noticing. They fell into one now, working down Row 12 — he cut, she dragged brush to the pile, he cut, she dragged, and somewhere in the middle of it they started talking without deciding to, the way water starts moving downhill.

"The Mutsus are crowding each other," she said. "Astrid kept meaning to thin that block and then her hip went bad."

"So I noticed. We should open the centers up before the festival traffic walks through."

"Festival?" Her head came around. "What festival?"

"The centennial. In October." He realized, saying it, that she genuinely hadn't known — that she'd been so buried in bank letters and burnt madeleines that the town's hundredth birthday had passed under her radar. Something in his chest twisted. The Hazel he remembered had organized the fourth-grade float, the high-school harvest dance, the pie contest that got so competitive the county paper wrote it up. "You really didn't know?"

"I've been busy." Defensive, fast. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "A hundred years. Grandma Astrid would have—" She stopped. Started over. "The committee will want the orchard. They'll want it for the dance, the cider pressing, all of it. They always have."

"Probably."

"And we can't give it to them looking like this." She surveyed the block with sudden fierce attention, and Jonah recognized the expression because he'd spent his adolescence studying it: Hazel finding a problem she could solve because the unsolvable ones were eating her alive. "The deadwood in Rows 4 through 9. The trellising on the Galas is rotting. If we're going to let strangers walk these rows in six weeks—"

"If," he said carefully. "We haven't decided anything yet."

"We haven't decided nothing, either." She yanked a dead branch free and threw it on the pile harder than the branch deserved. "Don't do that. Don't stand there being reasonable at me."

"I'm not being reasonable at you, I'm—"

"You're doing the thing where you agree with me in a tone that means you think I'm wrong." She wiped her hands on her thighs, leaving green streaks, and finally looked at him straight on, and her eyes were the same gray-green as the leaves around her, and it was unbearable. "Say what you mean, Jonah. You've had fifteen years of practice not saying what you mean. Take the morning off from it."

The words landed harder than she could have known. He kept his face still — he'd gotten very good at keeping his face still; it was practically a profession — and said, "I mean the offer expires in a month. Hale's buyer wants an answer by the festival. Whatever we do with the deadwood, we should know what we're doing it for."

"Saving trees is its own purpose."

"Not when someone's paying to take them out."

The silence that followed had weight, temperature, texture. Somewhere down the hill a crow said something rude. Hazel picked up her pruners and went back to work, and the conversation was over, and they both pretended it hadn't happened, and the pretending was so practiced on both sides that it went smooth as a hinge.

* * *

By noon they'd cleared two full rows, and Jonah's shoulders ached in the specific satisfying way that meant honest work, and he'd almost — almost — forgotten to be careful.

That was the danger of her. That had always been the danger of her. Not the sharpness, though she was sharp; not the temper, though she had one like a struck match. The danger was this: that after twenty minutes of shared labor he forgot to guard himself, and the words started coming out unfiltered, and the person across the brush pile from him was seventeen again and so was he, and the wound closed over without scarring, seamless, like it had never been there at all.

"Remember when we grafted the whole east row wrong?" he said, before he could stop himself. "Every single scion. Upside down."

Hazel snorted. "You grafted them upside down. I told you they were upside down."

"You handed them to me upside down."

"I was twelve. You were seventeen. Whose job was quality control?"

"Yours, apparently, forever." He grinned before he could help it, and she almost grinned back — he saw it start, the corner of her mouth, the crinkle beginning at her eye — and then he watched her catch it. Watched her put it away, deliberately, like folding a letter back into its envelope. The grin died unborn and her face went neutral and she turned to the next tree, and the temperature of the morning dropped ten degrees in the space of a second.

Because that was the other thing about the echo. The hole was shaped like what used to fill it, but the filling was gone, and when you reached for it your hand came back empty, and the emptiness hurt worse than the wound ever had.

Jonah stood among the trees with sap on his hands and grief in his throat like a stone he'd swallowed years ago and never passed. This was what he'd come back to. Not the paperwork, not the deed, not the developer's money. This: working beside her in the rows, close enough to touch, separated by fifteen years of silence that they carried between them like a pane of glass — everything visible through it, nothing passable. He had spent his whole adult life telling himself the story that made the leaving bearable. *It was necessary. It protected her. There was no other door.* And he'd mostly believed it, the way you mostly believe the load-bearing walls of any house you live in, because the alternative is checking the foundation and finding out what the house is actually standing on.

But walking these rows with her, hearing her correct his grip without looking at him, watching her fold away a smile rather than risk sharing it — he understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the friendship hadn't died when he left. It had died earlier, or differently, or it had been murdered, and he was one of the suspects, and he could no longer remember with confidence whether he'd done it.

"Hazel." His voice came out rougher than intended.

She didn't turn. "What."

"Nothing." He looked at the tree in front of him, at the clean angle cut, quarter inch above the bud, outward-facing. Doing it right because she'd told him to, and hating that he was doing it right because she'd told him to. "Never mind."

She worked another minute. Then, without looking at him, in a voice stripped of everything except fact: "Your father used to prune like that. Same bad habit. Thumb through the small handle." A pause. "I keep forgetting he's gone too."

And there it was — the thing they never mentioned, surfacing sideways, the way grief does when you refuse it the front door. His father. Dead eight months now, and Jonah had come back to an orchard his father had schemed against and a woman his father had threatened, carrying a ledger of secrets that included both. He opened his mouth to say something — he didn't know what, an apology-shaped nothing, a sound to fill the space — and that was when his phone buzzed in his pocket.

Marcus Hale's name on the screen. *Lunch tomorrow? Bring your sister-in-sellerhood. I think we can make everyone happy.*

Jonah read it twice. Down the row, Hazel had gone back to work, her silhouette sharp against the bright green wall of leaves, and he thought about the word *everyone*, and who Hale imagined it covered, and what it cost to make a man like Hale happy, and who usually ended up paying.

He pocketed the phone without answering, and picked up the pruners, and followed her down the row — toward whatever came next, whether he was ready for it or not.
