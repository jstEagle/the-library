## 8. What the Café Costs

The email arrived on a Tuesday, which Hazel would later decide was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day she'd built her whole mythology around. *Tuesdays we make madeleines. Tuesdays we're fine.* She was elbow-deep in a fifty-pound sack of flour when her phone buzzed against the counter, and she read the message with flour-white fingers and a heart that had, by now, been trained to absorb blows like a boxer absorbs jabs — head down, shoulders up, keep moving.

*Dear Ms. Hartwell, effective immediately, Fairmont Provisions will be unable to continue extending credit on your account. All outstanding balances are due within fourteen days. We regret—*

She didn't read the rest. She didn't need to. *We regret* was the sound every closing door made in her life lately, and she had become fluent in the language of regret, could hear it coming three sentences before it arrived.

"Boss?" Priya's voice from the kitchen doorway, where steam curled around her like a question. "The butter's here. Well. Half the butter's here."

"Half."

"Half." Priya held up the delivery slip like evidence in a trial. "They shorted us. Again. Third time this month."

Hazel wiped her hands on her apron, an apron that had once been white and was now the color of every long day she'd ever worked, and she smiled. That was the trick of it, the muscle memory of it — the smile came first, before the thought, before the fear, the way her hands still shaped dough without her asking them to. "I'll call them."

"You've been calling them."

"I'll call them again."

Priya looked at her for a long moment, that steady dark-eyed look she'd been perfecting for six years, the look that said *I see you, Hazel Hartwell, all the way down*, and then she turned back into the kitchen without another word, which was somehow worse than an argument. Arguments you could win. Silence just sat there, patient as rising dough, waiting to be proven right.

Hazel called Fairmont. She got a man named Denny who had taken her orders for nine years and who now spoke to her in the careful, flattened tone of a man reading from a script written by someone in accounts receivable. She heard *policy* twice and *unfortunately* three times, and when she asked, very reasonably, very calmly, if nine years of prompt-ish payments counted for anything, Denny said, "I'm sorry, Ms. Hartwell. I really am." And the terrible thing — the thing she'd carry around like a stone in her shoe for the rest of the day — was that he sounded like he meant it.

She hung up and stood in her kitchen, her beautiful kitchen, the one she'd rebuilt with her own hands at twenty-six, copper pots hanging like promises she'd made to a younger self, and she did the arithmetic she always did, the arithmetic that had become a kind of prayer: *If I sell out by Thursday. If the weekend is good. If the festival brings foot traffic. If.*

The *if*s stacked up like madeleine shells in a mold, and she knew — she had known for months, in the place where she kept things she didn't look at — that the *if*s were not going to hold.

* * *

That afternoon she did what she always did when the numbers turned feral in her head: she worked. She made three kinds of tarts, a batch of brioche she had no butter for and would have to improvise around, and a double order of the honey-lavender scones that had once been written up in a regional magazine that no longer existed. The café filled and emptied around her in its rhythms — the morning regulars, the lunch rush, the two o'clock lull when the light came through the west windows and turned everything the color of cider.

She was boxing a lemon tart for Mrs. Okafor when she overheard it, the way you overhear the things that are going to rearrange your furniture.

"—she's covering it again," one of the line girls whispered to the other, by the espresso machine. "Friday's payroll. Priya put in her own money again. My cousin works at the credit union, she saw the deposit."

Hazel's hands went still around the pastry box.

She did not turn around. She finished the box, tied the string, smiled at Mrs. Okafor, told her the tart had extra lemon because Mrs. Okafor deserved extra lemon, and then she walked back into the kitchen with the box string still caught on her finger like a thread she couldn't shake loose.

"Priya."

"Mm?" Priya didn't look up from the station where she was piping cream with the precision of a surgeon and the calm of someone who had never once, as far as Hazel could tell, doubted that the world would hold.

"Did you cover Friday?"

The piping bag did not pause. "Cover what?"

"Priya."

"Cover what, boss?" And now she looked up, and her face was a masterpiece of innocence, and Hazel understood suddenly that she was not talking to her sous-chef. She was talking to the one person in her life who had stopped believing her a long time ago and loved her anyway.

"You put your own money into payroll."

"I did not."

"Priya."

"Hazel." Priya set down the bag. She wiped her hands. She came around the steel counter until they were standing close, close enough that Hazel could smell the vanilla on her, and when she spoke her voice was low and even and devastating. "You think I don't check? You think I don't see the invoices you hide in the drawer under the recipe cards, like I can't smell a lie the way I smell scorched sugar? I've known how bad it is since March."

The kitchen was very quiet. The proofing cabinet hummed. Somewhere out front, the espresso machine hissed like something small being wounded.

"Since March," Hazel repeated.

"Since March." Priya crossed her arms. "And every week since March you've stood up in front of all of us and said *we're fine, keep going, we're fine*, and I've let you say it, because I thought you needed to say it. But it's October, Hazel. And Friday's payroll came out of my savings account. And I need you to look at me and tell me the truth, because I can't keep covering for you if you won't even cover for yourself."

Hazel opened her mouth. And here is the thing about a lie you've told so long it becomes load-bearing: when it starts to go, it doesn't crack. It doesn't crumble. It simply stops being there, all at once, like a wall you lean against that turns out to have been painted on — and you fall, and the falling is quiet, and the floor is further down than you thought.

"I don't know how bad it is," she said, and her voice came out strange, small, a stranger's voice. "I mean — I know exactly how bad it is. I do the numbers every night. I just — I don't let them be true."

Priya's face softened, which was somehow harder to bear than her anger. "I know," she said. "I know, boss."

"The inheritance—" Hazel started, and stopped, because what could she say? *I own half an orchard with a man who broke me, and the deed is haunted by a forged postcard, and a developer is circling like a hawk that's learned to wear a suit?* "It's complicated."

"Everything worth having is complicated." Priya picked the piping bag back up, and just like that the moment was over, folded away, because Priya understood — better than Hazel ever had — that you could hold a truth and keep working at the same time. That was the whole secret. That was the thing Hazel had never learned. "Friday's covered. Next Friday isn't. So you've got a week to decide what you're actually fighting for."

She piped cream. Hazel stood there in her kitchen, in the smell of vanilla and yeast and the ghost of every Tuesday she'd ever promised herself, and felt the shame rise in her like heat from an oven floor — not the sharp shame of being caught, but the older, deeper kind, the shame of a woman who had built her entire self on the story *I am the one who holds it together*, and who had just discovered, in front of the only person whose opinion could actually burn her, that she was the one falling apart.

* * *

She closed late that night. She always closed late; it was a point of pride, a superstition, a penance. She wiped down the counters twice. She wrapped the unsold brioche for the food bank, because some rituals you keep even when the ground is giving way, especially then. She turned off the lights section by section, front to back, the way her grandmother had taught her — *leave the kitchen light for last, Hazel-bird, so the dark has to chase you* — and she stood for a moment in the dark kitchen with just that one bulb burning, and she let herself do the thing she never did.

She let the numbers be true.

Not the café numbers. Those she could recite in her sleep, and did, some nights, waking with the figures printed on the inside of her eyelids like a debt. The other numbers. The real ones. *How long have I known? Since spring. How many people have I told? No one. How many times did I smile and say fine?* She counted. She lost count. The shame didn't come all at once; it came in layers, like peeling an apple she couldn't stop peeling, and under every layer was another layer, and under the last one — under all of it — was a question she'd been avoiding since the day the lawyer called:

*If the orchard saves me, what am I saving?*

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the worm in the fruit. She had spent fifteen years building a life on the story of what had been done to her — the boy who left, the summer that ended, the heart she'd trained to be a closed fist — and now there was an orchard with her name on it and a man with Jonah's face walking her rows, and the café was drowning, and she had just discovered that the person she'd been lying hardest to was the person in the mirror.

She thought of Priya's savings account. She thought of Mrs. Okafor's lemon tart. She thought of Denny's sorry voice, and the half delivery of butter, and the drawer under the recipe cards where she hid invoices like a child hides report cards.

She thought of her grandmother, who had left her an orchard instead of an apology, and wondered — for the first time, really wondered — what Astrid Bellamy would have said about all this. About the lying. About the cost.

The kitchen light hummed. Outside, October pressed its face against the windows, all gold light and coming frost.

Hazel turned off the last light, and stood in the dark, and made herself a promise she wasn't sure she could keep: *One week. I tell the truth for one week.* Starting — she checked the clock, it was nearly midnight, close enough — starting tomorrow.

Her phone buzzed on the counter. A message from Jonah, of all people, all business, no warmth: *Met with the surveyor today. Found something at the boundary line you should see. Tomorrow morning?*

She stared at it for a long time. *Found something.* The words sat there like a door left ajar, and through it she could smell — absurdly, unmistakably — apples and old smoke, and something in her chest that she had trained very carefully not to feel began, against all her training, to move.

She typed: *Tomorrow. Early.*

And then she stood in her dark café, in the cost of everything, and waited for morning like a sentence she had finally agreed to serve.
