## 9. Cider and Confessions Adjacent

The press smelled like October even in September — that was the thing nobody told you about cider, that the apples gave up their future before they gave up their juice. Jonah Voss stood in the old pressing shed with his hands stained brown to the wrist and thought: *this is the hour. This is the hour you tell her.*

He had been thinking that for eleven days.

*You tell yourself you're waiting for the right moment,* said the voice in his head, the one that sounded suspiciously like his father, dry as seed husks. *But there is no right moment. There never was. There's only the moment you keep not choosing.*

"Block Four's drops are sweeter," Hazel called from the doorway, a crate balanced on her hip like she'd been born carrying it, which in a sense she had. "Wormy, though. You'll want to cull them."

"I know how to cull," he said.

"You know how to do everything," she said, and set the crate down, and something in her tone made him look up — not sharpness exactly, not warmth either, but that third thing between them that had no name yet and might never get one. "That was almost a compliment."

"It was an observation."

"You've been making a lot of observations lately." She pulled off her gloves finger by finger. "Observing my pruning. Observing my culling. Observing my coffee."

"Your coffee deserves observation. It deserves a witness. Possibly a coroner."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, like she'd stepped on a stair that wasn't there — and turned away too fast to hide that she'd laughed at all, and Jonah felt the old treacherous lift in his chest, the one that had gotten him into this whole catastrophe of a life, the one that said *she still laughs at your jokes, she still*—

*Don't,* said the voice. *You have one job tonight.*

The pressing shed sat at the orchard's western edge, past the farmhouse, close enough to the creek that you could hear water working under the dark. His grandfather had built it in 1961 out of salvaged barn timber, and the walls still held the smell of every season since — fermented apple and machine oil and, underneath it all, faintly, smoke. That smell again. It got into everything in this valley. He'd spent fifteen years in cities where nothing smelled of smoke unless something was actually burning, and he had told himself he liked it that way.

Hazel had brought apples from the café — bruised ones, cosmetically ruined, the ones she couldn't sell. "Waste not," she'd said, dumping them into the hopper with the efficiency of someone who had run numbers on waste her whole life. Her apples made a darker cider than his. She claimed it was the variety. He suspected it was the desperation.

The press itself was a rack-and-cloth monster, wooden slats gone silver with age, and it required two people the way certain doors require two hands. That was the trap of it. That was maybe why he'd suggested pressing tonight instead of just signing whatever paperwork Marcus Hale's people kept sending — because the press needed four hands, and four hands meant proximity, and proximity meant the evening would stretch long and lamplit and full of the exact silences where confessions live.

Or that was what he told himself he wanted. The truth was less flattering.

*Here is what you actually want,* said the voice. *You want to tell her everything and be forgiven before you finish the sentence. You want absolution without the part where you sit in the mess you made. You are thirty-four years old and you still want the fire without the burning.*

The grinder ate the first bushel with a sound like distant thunder. Pomace and juice, sweet-sharp, filling the shed with a fog of apple that made the single hanging bulb look soft. Hazel worked the far side of the press, sleeves pushed up, hair escaping its tie in wisps that caught the light. She had flour on her forearm still, from the morning's baking, a pale smear she didn't know about, and Jonah watched it and felt something crack quietly inside his ribs.

"You press every year?" she asked. "Even when—" She stopped herself. Even when you weren't coming back. Even when this place was just a line item. "Even alone?"

"First few years I paid a crew. Then I did it alone." He fed another layer of apples. "It's better with two. The cloths need centering. You can't see both edges by yourself."

"No," she said. "You can't."

They layered the next cloth in silence, her hands and his crossing over the same slats, and it was so much like being nineteen that he had to grip the frame to stay in the present tense. She used to stand exactly there. She used to hum while she worked — show tunes, badly, on purpose — and he used to time his movements to hers without meaning to, the way you fall into any rhythm long enough.

"Do you remember," she began, and then stopped, wiping her hands on her thighs.

"Probably."

"The summer we pressed the whole east block in one night. Your dad said if we finished by midnight he'd drive us to the lake."

"He drove us to the lake."

"We were in the water until three in the morning. I got a leech."

"You screamed loud enough to wake the Hendersons."

"I did *not* scream."

"Hazel." He looked at her across the press, juice-wet and lamplit and fifteen years younger for a second. "You screamed. I carried you out on my back. You made me check your entire leg twice."

She was smiling. He could see her fighting it and losing. "I was seventeen."

"You were seventeen," he agreed, and the words came up his throat before he could stop them, riding the momentum of the memory like a cart downhill: "Hazel, I need to tell you why I—"

His phone rang.

The sound cut through the shed like a blade through cloth — that specific trill he'd assigned to one contact only, the one he could never bring himself to change and never wanted to hear. The old ringtone. His father's ringtone, from back when his father called weekly to ask about yields and never once about anything else.

Jonah's hands went still on the press.

Hazel noticed. Of course she noticed; she'd always been able to read him faster than he could read himself, which was precisely the problem, historically speaking. "You should get that," she said. "Late for a business call."

"It's nothing."

"It's ringing."

"It can ring." He meant it. God help him, in that moment he meant it completely. The confession was right there, loaded, aimed — *why I left, why I never came back, what your father said to me on the porch with the screen door open and the kitchen light behind him* — and he could feel the whole shape of it pressing against the backs of his teeth.

The phone stopped ringing. Then started again.

"That's persistent," Hazel said lightly, but her eyes weren't light. They'd gone watchful, the way they went watchful whenever the subject bent toward that last summer, toward the missing thing between them, the wound neither of them named. "You'd better answer. If it's the lawyer about Hale's offer—"

"It's not the lawyer."

"Then who—"

He answered it. Because answering was easier than explaining, and explaining was impossible, and because some cowardly reflex older than his own conscience reached out and took the call.

"Dad," he said.

* * *

His father's voice came through thin and tinny, all elbows. "You're still up there, then."

"It's harvest."

"It's always harvest somewhere. That's the beauty of harvest." A pause, the sound of a television in the background, muted or nearly. "Hale called me. Says the girl's stalling."

"She's not stalling. We agreed on terms."

"Terms." His father said the word like it was something stuck to his shoe. "Jonah. Son. I'm going to say this once, and then I'm going to let you make your own mistakes, which is more than I ever got. That orchard is worth more as dirt than it will ever be worth as fruit. You know it. Hale knows it. The only person who doesn't know it is the Hartwell girl, and she doesn't get to not know it forever on your dime."

"She co-owns it."

"She co-owns half of a liability." Another pause, longer. When his father spoke again his voice had dropped, gone quieter, and somehow that was worse. "There are things about that property you don't understand. Things I handled. Things that need to stay handled. You sell your half, you walk away clean, and you let the past stay where we put it."

"Where *you* put it," Jonah said.

Silence. Then, softly, in a tone Jonah hadn't heard since he was a boy caught doing something unforgivable: "Mind yourself, boy."

The line went dead.

Jonah stood holding the phone, the shed humming around him, the press dripping its slow metronome into the bucket. Through the open door he could see Hazel at the wash bench, rinsing cloths, her back to him, the line of her shoulders saying *I heard enough of that to know it wasn't the lawyer.*

*Well?* said the voice. *This is it. She's standing right there. Tell her who called. Tell her what he said. Tell her about the things that need to stay handled. One sentence leads to the next; that's how sentences work. You could be free by midnight.*

He put the phone in his pocket.

"Everything okay?" Hazel asked, not turning around.

"Yeah." The lie came out smooth, worn smooth, a river stone of a lie, polished by years of use. "My dad. Checking on the harvest numbers."

She turned then, drying her hands, and looked at him with an expression he couldn't fully parse in the bad light — something between disappointment and confirmation, like a scientist watching a hypothesis hold. "How is he?"

"Same."

"Still keeping score?"

"Always."

She nodded slowly. "Funny," she said. "Fifteen years, and he never once called me. Not after the fire, not after—" She stopped. The word *fire* hung in the shed air among the apple-fog, and Jonah felt it land in his chest like a swallowed stone. "Never mind. Cloths are ready."

*You just let her say it,* the voice said, almost admiring. *You let her stand there and mention the fire and you said nothing. Do you understand what you are? You're not protecting her anymore. You haven't been protecting her for years. You're protecting yourself. The protection ran out around year five and you just kept paying the premium.*

They pressed in near-silence after that. The work saved them, the way work always had — the crank and the layers, the pomace coming out flat and spent, the juice running amber-dark into the settling tank. Hazel's hands knew the motions even after fifteen years away; muscle kept its own records, its own stubborn archives. Once their fingers touched reaching for the same corner of cloth and neither of them pulled away fast enough, and once she said "center it more on your side" in exactly her mother's cadence, and once the press groaned so hard at the turn of the screw that they both laughed at it, startled, like two people jumping at the same thunderclap.

By midnight the tank held forty gallons of dark sweet cider, and the shed smelled so entirely of autumn that the present tense barely existed anymore.

Hazel poured two tin cups from the first draw — cloudy, unfiltered, warm from the day. "To Block Four," she said.

"To Block Four."

They drank. The cider was rough and honest and better than it had any right to be, and Hazel closed her eyes at the taste, and Jonah watched her do it and understood with terrible clarity that he was going to lose her again — not to distance this time, but to the truth, whenever he finally found the spine to speak it. There was no version of the story he could tell her that wouldn't cost him the way she was looking right now, unguarded, almost trusting. Every road led through the fire. Every road led through her father's kitchen light and the words *I'll see that boy charged* and the choice Jonah had made at nineteen that he had been re-making, silently, every single day since.

Unless he lied his way through this too. Unless he sold his half, took the money, walked away clean, and let the past stay where his father put it.

"Hazel," he said.

She opened her eyes. "Hmm?"

"There's something I should—"

"Don't." She said it quickly, quietly, holding up one hand, and her face had changed — closed like a shop gate at closing time. "Whatever it is. Not tonight. Tonight was—" She gestured at the tank, the shed, the cups. "Tonight was good. Let it be good. Tomorrow we can go back to being lawyers at each other."

And because he was what he was — because the cowardice fit him like an inherited coat — he said, "Okay."

She rinsed the cups. She gathered her keys and her gloves and the empty crate. At the door she paused, half-turned, lit from behind by the yard floodlight, and for one moment she looked like she might come back and say something true herself — about the postcard, maybe, whose wrong handwriting had been bothering her for days; he'd seen her mouthing the letters to herself like a rosary. About the last summer. About anything.

"Goodnight, Jonah," she said instead.

"Night."

Her taillights crossed the orchard and shrank and vanished at the county road, and Jonah stood alone in the pressing shed with forty gallons of cider and zero gallons of courage, listening to the creek work under the dark.

*Second person, now,* said the voice, and it sounded tired, tired of him, tired the way his father was tired. *Because first person lets you hide in the grammar. Here is what you did tonight: you had the truth in your mouth and you swallowed it for a telephone. You lied to her face about a man who has been lying to you both for fifteen years. You chose the smooth lie over the hard sentence because the hard sentence costs and you have never once in your life paid retail. You will sleep fine. That's the worst part. Cowards always sleep fine.*

Jonah picked up the hose, hosed down the press, racked the cloths, swept the pomace. He did all the closing-up things his grandfather had taught him, in order, thoroughly, because ritual was a place to put your hands when you couldn't put them anywhere honest.

On his way out he stopped at the shed door and looked back at the dark rows, Block Four black against the stars, the oldest trees standing like witnesses who'd seen everything and testified to none of it.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow he'd find the words.

He was already rehearsing tomorrow's excuses as he locked the door.
