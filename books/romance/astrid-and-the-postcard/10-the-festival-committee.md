## 10. The Festival Committee

The notice went up on the community board outside the feed store the same week the first apples began to blush, and Hazel read it three times before she understood what she was looking at.

**CENTENNIAL FESTIVAL COMMITTEE — OPEN MEETING. THURSDAY, 7 PM, GRANGE HALL. ALL WELCOME. VENUE TBA.**

Venue TBA. As if it were a small thing, a blank to be filled in with a shrug. As if a hundred years of a town's history could be housed anywhere at all.

She was supposed to be buying flour. She stood in the aisle of Hartwell's old account — no, that was her account now, hers alone, the penmanship in the ledger all hers and getting shakier by the month — and she thought about venue TBA the way she'd been thinking about everything lately: as arithmetic. A festival needed a place big enough for crowds, parking that didn't exist downtown, shade for August, and enough romance in the setting that people would open their wallets for commemorative jam and hand-painted signs. There were exactly two candidates in Willow Creek. The fairgrounds, which flooded, smelled like livestock, and had all the charm of a parking garage. And Bellamy Orchard.

The orchard where she'd grown up. The orchard she now owned half of, on paper, beside a man she hadn't spoken to in fifteen years until six weeks ago, when he'd walked through her café door carrying survey maps and a buyer's offer like a bouquet of dead flowers.

"You're doing the face," said Priya, appearing at her elbow with the flour Hazel had come in for and a look Hazel knew too well.

"What face?"

"The one where you've already decided something terrible and you're waiting for the math to agree with you."

Hazel put the notice back on its hook. "It's just a meeting."

"Mm." Priya set the flour in the basket with the tenderness of someone disarming a bomb. "You know what I love about you? You always say 'it's just' before the worst decisions of your life."

* * *

The Grange Hall smelled of floor wax and forty years of potlucks, and by seven-fifteen every folding chair was taken. Hazel arrived late — the madeleines had refused to release from the pan, a small mutiny she'd taken personally — and had to stand along the wall, which meant she saw him before anyone else did.

Jonah sat near the back with his arms crossed, wearing the gray shirt she hated herself for noticing. He'd been coming out to the orchard four mornings a week now, their truce holding the way a dam holds: technically, and with constant low pressure behind it. They walked rows together. He'd learned to make coffee without being asked. Neither of them had mentioned the night he left, and the silence between them had grown so large and so lived-in that it had begun to feel almost like furniture.

He caught her eye across the room. Something passed over his face — not surprise; he'd known she'd be here, probably — but a kind of bracing. She felt her own shoulders do the same.

Mayor Odette Crane called the meeting to order with a gavel that was actually a wooden spoon, and the centennial festival unfolded before the town like a quilt of ambitions: a parade route down Main Street, a historical exhibit in the library, a pie contest — "Hazel," Odette said, finding her against the wall with a look of pure predation, "we'll be counting on you" — and then the agenda item everyone had come for.

"The venue," Odette announced, and the hall settled into the particular silence of people who already know the answer and want to watch someone else say it. "The fairgrounds are available, as always."

A groan, good-natured but real. Someone said "the smell," someone else said "my mother fainted at the fairgrounds in '94 and we've never recovered," and Odette raised both hands.

"I'm open to alternatives. Genuinely. But they need to be realistic. We need acreage, we need access, we need—" she consulted her notes, though she didn't need to, "—shade. It's August. We're not baking the town's elderly on asphalt."

And there it was. The opening. Hazel felt it the way you feel a current under still water.

She should have stayed quiet. Every practical bone in her body — and she was a woman built almost entirely of practical bones, a skeleton of schedules and supplier invoices and trained muscle — told her to stay quiet. Volunteering the orchard meant publicity, and publicity meant eyes, and eyes meant Marcus Hale, who had bought her lunch three weeks ago and smiled at her desperation with the precision of a man reading an X-ray. It meant the whole town watching whatever fragile, armed neutrality she and Jonah had constructed, taking sides, asking questions. It meant more mornings in the rows with Jonah, more of his silence, more of the way her heart behaved around him like something unsupervised.

It meant staking the festival — the town's hundredth birthday, the biggest thing to happen here in a generation — on land whose deed hung on a forged postcard and whose future hung on a developer's expiring offer.

"It's reckless," she murmured to herself.

"Then don't," said a voice beside her, and she turned to find Dot Ferrell, eighty-one years old, smelling of lavender and cigarettes, watching her with bright bird eyes. "Or do. Reckless is just hope that's stopped asking permission."

"That's not wisdom, Dot, that's a fortune cookie."

"I've outlived three husbands and one fire marshal. I'm allowed to plagiarize." Dot nodded toward the front, where Odette was saying, "Well, if there are no other proposals—" and Hazel heard herself, before any part of her brain authorized it, say:

"The orchard."

The word came out steady. Her hands were not.

Every head turned. Somewhere in the back a chair creaked, and she knew without looking that it was Jonah sitting forward.

"Bellamy Orchard," she said, because apparently she was committed now, committed the way you're committed once you've stepped off a dock. "It has the acreage. It has the shade. It has—" she heard her own voice soften and couldn't stop it, "—a hundred years of this town in it already. My grandmother used to say the trees remember everyone. Half the families in this room have a photo somewhere of a kid up one of those trees. Let's take the picture again."

Silence. Then Dot began to clap, slowly, deliberately, the way old women clap when they intend to start a movement, and the hall followed her like a tide.

Odette was radiant. Odette was also, Hazel noted, already writing it down, which meant it was real, which meant there was no version of the evening where Hazel walked it back. "Bellamy Orchard! Of course. Perfect. Now, logistics — access, parking, coordination with the owners—"

"Owners," said a voice from the back, dry as August grass, and the hall turned again. Jonah stood, unhurried, and Hazel watched him walk the length of the room with fifteen years of unreadable expression. "Plural. In case anyone's wondering how I'll be voting."

"And how is that?" Odette asked, though her spoon-gavel was already tilting toward yes.

Jonah looked at Hazel. Just looked, for a beat too long, long enough that the room noticed, long enough that she felt heat climb her throat. Whatever moved behind his eyes wasn't anger. That was the worst part. It was something older and more tired and more careful than anger.

"It's the right venue," he said. "I'll help however I can."

And that was that. The hall erupted into the satisfied noise of a problem solved, chairs scraping, Odette assigning subcommittees with the spoon, Dot Ferrell declaring herself chair of lanterns ("there will be lanterns in those trees or I will die trying"), and Hazel stood in the middle of it feeling the strange vertigo of having gotten exactly what she'd impulsively wanted.

Jonah reached her last, as the crowd thinned. Up close he smelled like cut grass and cider apples, and there was a splinter of evening light from the high windows catching the gray at his temple that she absolutely did not have time to notice.

"You didn't ask me first," he said. No edge in it. Almost gentle.

"Would you have said no?"

He considered that honestly, which was somehow worse. "No."

"Then I didn't need to ask."

"No," he agreed. "But you might have wanted to." And there it was — the whole history of them folded into nine words, offered flatly, without accusation, and taken back before she could touch it. He picked up his cap from the chair beside him. "Festival committee meets Sundays. I'll be there."

"I know," she said. "I volunteered us into it."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. An ancestor of one. "Yeah," he said. "You did."

* * *

She walked home the long way, past the dark windows of her own café, and let herself feel it — really feel it, standing under the streetlight where no one could see — the recklessness of what she'd done. Not the business risk. She could price the business risk; she'd priced worse. What frightened her was smaller and larger: that she had chosen, publicly, in front of the whole town, to spend a season beside Jonah Voss. That some part of her had stood up in that meeting before her caution could catch it, and that part had sounded, unmistakably, like hope.

Hope was not trainable muscle. Hope was the opposite — it was the one muscle that only weakened with use, that tore a little each time you stretched it, and she had spent fifteen years learning to leave it alone.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Odette, already: *Subcommittee assignments attached!! You + Jonah = site & grounds (obviously). So excited!!!*

Obviously. The town had decided. The town, which remembered them at nineteen, which had its own versions of the story — versions Hazel suddenly realized she'd never corrected because correcting them would mean telling them — had looked at the two of them in that hall and seen a reunion. Had seen, maybe, what Dot Ferrell saw, clapping like a woman starting a movement.

Hazel stood a moment longer under the streetlight, thumb hovering over the reply.

Then, from the direction of the orchard road, she heard it: a car slowing, stopping. Headlights swept the hedge. And Marcus Hale stepped out into the amber glow with a folder under his arm and his smile already assembled, and said, pleasantly, "Evening, Hazel. Heard the news. Got a minute? I think you're going to want to hear what this means for your half."

She looked at the folder. She looked at the orchard road, dark and full of trees that remembered everyone.

"Walk with me," she said, and hated how much the folder looked like an answer to a prayer.
