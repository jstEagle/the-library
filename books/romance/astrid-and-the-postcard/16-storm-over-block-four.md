## 16. Storm Over Block Four

### Hazel

The sky turned the color of a bruise at four in the afternoon, and by five Jonah Voss was standing in her kitchen doorway with his phone in one hand and a look on his face she hadn't seen since they were teenagers — that particular urgency he used to wear when a summer storm rolled in off the ridge and every kid in the orchard ran for cover while he ran the other way.

"Radar says hail," he said. "Big cells. Golf ball, maybe bigger. It'll hit Block Four first."

Hazel set down her piping bag. The éclairs could wait; they'd waited all week already, like everything else in her life.

"How long do we have?"

"Ninety minutes. Maybe less." He was already pulling his jacket back on. "The nets won't hold it. But if we get tarps over the young trees in Four and Five, if we shake what we can loose before the ice comes down—"

"You want to stand under falling hail."

"I want to not lose the harvest," he said. "It's the first real crop this orchard's given anyone in years. It's—" He stopped himself, jaw working. "It matters."

She should have said no. She had a café to close up, dough proofing overnight, a supplier letter she still hadn't answered sitting in her office drawer where she'd hidden it like contraband. She had fifteen years of reasons to let Jonah Voss fight his own weather.

Instead she heard herself say, "I'll get my boots."

* * *

Block Four was the youngest block, the trees barely taller than Hazel, planted — she realized as they dragged tarps from the barn — the spring after she left town. Someone had planted them anyway. Someone had gone out into those empty rows year after year with no idea whether she would ever come back, and put saplings in the ground.

She didn't ask who. She didn't have to, and neither of them wanted to be having that conversation under a bruised sky.

They worked in the strange green light that precedes violence. The air tasted metallic, like sucking on a coin. Wind came down through the ridge in long shoves, flattening the grass between rows, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet but was gathering itself somewhere above them, patient and enormous.

"Tarp here," Jonah called, and they stretched it over two trees together, his hands and hers meeting at the corners, tying down to stakes he seemed to know were there without looking. Of course he knew where the stakes were. He'd probably driven them himself.

"The apples are still small," Hazel shouted over the wind. "Will they—"

"They'll survive hail better than ripe fruit. It's the branches I'm worried about. The old tree in the corner—" He glanced toward the far edge of Block Four, where the oldest tree in the orchard stood, an enormous gnarled thing that had been dropping apples into Hazel's childhood hands before she could read. "If the ice takes a limb off that one, it's half the tree. Maybe worse."

"Then we shake it," she said. "Loose fruit falls, the branches lighten, maybe they bend instead of break."

He looked at her then — really looked, rain just starting to speckle his face — with something that wasn't quite surprise. Something closer to recognition, which was worse, or better. She couldn't tell the difference anymore.

"That's what my dad used to say," he said quietly.

"I know," Hazel said. "I was there."

* * *

The shaking was brutal work. They climbed the lower limbs of the old tree and hauled on the branches until the whole canopy shuddered and released its small hard apples in rattling showers, hundreds of them drumming down onto the grass, onto the tarps they'd spread beneath, onto their shoulders and heads. The wind rose. The light went from green to a strange bruised violet, and somewhere west, low and continuous, came a sound like a freight train that never arrived.

"Hail's coming," Jonah said. "We need to be inside in ten minutes."

"One more row." Hazel was already moving. Her hands were raw, her hair plastered to her neck, and she felt more alive than she had in months — than she had in years, if she was honest, and lately honesty kept ambushing her at inconvenient moments. "Row twelve. If we lose row twelve Priya has nothing to press for the festival."

"Priya doesn't even—"

"She's been helping me plan the pastry stall. Cider-glazed everything. She has a spreadsheet, Jonah. You don't argue with a woman who has a spreadsheet."

Something cracked across his face that might have been a laugh. They hit row twelve together as the first drops came down fat and cold and wrong — too cold, each drop stinging where it landed, and Hazel understood a half-second before her mind caught up: these weren't drops anymore.

"Ice," she said. "Jonah, it's ice—"

"Barn!" He grabbed her wrist and they ran, hail beginning to fall in earnest now, bouncing off the packed earth around them, off the tarps, off the young trees they'd covered and the ones they hadn't. A pellet the size of a marble caught her shoulder blade and she yelped. Another cracked against the trunk beside her head with a sound like a gunshot's smaller cousin.

Then they were through the barn door, into the sudden hush and hay-smell of the dark, both of them breathing hard, soaked through, hail hammering the tin roof above like the sky had declared war on the building specifically.

And Hazel started laughing.

She couldn't help it. It came up out of her like water finding a crack — helpless, gasping laughter, the kind she hadn't produced since she was nineteen years old standing in this same barn with this same boy, both of them drenched and triumphant after some smaller storm, some gentler catastrophe.

"What?" Jonah said, but he was fighting a smile and losing.

"We looked like idiots," she managed. "Running. Two grown adults running from the sky like—"

"Like people who don't want concussions?"

"Like children." She pushed wet hair out of her eyes. "You used to run *toward* storms. What happened to you?"

He leaned against the wall, arms crossed, watching her with an expression she couldn't name in the dimness. Rain-light came through the gaps in the boards in thin silver lines.

"You did," he said.

The laughter died in her throat. Outside, the hail redoubled, a thousand small fists on the roof, and in the sudden silence between them Hazel became acutely aware of how close the barn walls made them — six feet, then less, the dark doing what darkness does, erasing the years and leaving only the shapes of things.

"Jonah—"

"Don't." His voice was rough. "You don't have to say anything. I shouldn't have—"

"Why did you leave?"

It came out of her before she could stop it, the question she'd sworn a truce around, the armed neutrality they'd maintained for weeks of terrible coffee and shared ledgers and careful distance. The storm shook the roof and she stood in the shaking and asked it anyway.

His face did something complicated. For one unguarded second he looked like a man standing at the edge of a very high place, deciding.

"Hazel," he said, "the night I left—"

Lightning took the world apart.

It came through the board gaps white and total, and the thunder arrived so fast behind it that the two sounds were one sound, one enormous concussion, and the old tree at the corner of Block Four — she knew it before she saw it, knew it by the way Jonah's head snapped toward the window, by the groan of wood that followed, deep and mortal — the old tree had been struck.

They were outside before either of them decided to be, hail still falling but softer now, spent, sliding off their shoulders as they ran down the row. The lightning had split the old tree straight down its heart. Half the crown leaned at an angle that made Hazel's stomach drop, held by a hinge of bark no wider than her hand, and the air smelled scorched and sweet, burnt sugar and rain.

"Oh," Hazel said. Just that. Just the smallest syllable of grief.

Jonah put his hand on the wounded trunk, gently, the way you'd touch a horse with a broken leg. Steam rose faintly where the split exposed the heartwood to the wet air.

"It can live like this," he said finally. "Split trees can live. But that hanging half has to come off tomorrow, clean cut, or it'll tear the rest down in the next wind." He looked at her across the ruined tree, rain running off his chin, and whatever he'd been about to say in the barn was gone — folded away again, tucked back into whatever locked room he kept it in. "Get some sleep, Hazel. Tomorrow we prune."

* * *

### Jonah

He didn't sleep. He sat in his motel room with the lamp off and listened to the last of the storm rattle the window unit, and he thought about the half-second in the barn when he'd almost told her everything.

Almost. His whole life had been built out of almosts, stacked like cordwood.

The problem was that the truth wasn't one thing. It was a braid: the fire, her father's face in the hospital waiting room, the threat delivered in a voice low enough that nineteen-year-old Jonah understood instantly that it wasn't a bluff. And underneath all of it, the part he'd never said aloud to anyone — that he'd gone willingly, in the end. That when her father offered him exile in exchange for her safety, some traitorous part of him had felt *relief*. Because staying meant watching her choose, and he'd been afraid — even then, even certain as he was that she'd follow him anywhere — afraid she wouldn't. Afraid that being chosen was a test he'd fail.

So he'd taken the choice away from both of them and called it sacrifice, and fifteen years later he still couldn't tell which it had been.

The hail stopped sometime after midnight. At dawn he drove out to the orchard with the chainsaw in the truck bed and found Hazel already there, thermoses lined up on a tailgate like an offering, wearing her grandfather's old canvas coat three sizes too big, squinting up at the split tree with her hands on her hips.

"You're supposed to be at the café," he said.

"Priya opened. She said, quote, 'Go save your tree, I have the counter.'" Hazel handed him a thermos. Coffee, black, exactly how he took it — a fact she had no business remembering and clearly did. "Also she said if we're going to keep having emergencies, we should invest in a proper first-aid kit, because the one in the barn expired during the Obama administration."

The morning was rinsed and gold, the orchard dripping, hail-melt pooling in the wheel ruts. The old tree looked worse in daylight — the split ran eight feet down the trunk, and the leaning half hung like a broken wing. Jonah circled it twice, planning cuts, and Hazel circled with him, and somewhere in the third circuit they fell into the old rhythm without meaning to: he'd point, she'd nod or shake her head, she'd see a hazard he'd missed, he'd adjust. Fifteen years collapsed into something efficient and wordless and dangerously comfortable.

"You tie off the hanging half first," she said. "Before you cut the hinge. Otherwise it swings when it goes."

"I know."

"You always tied off too late. You liked the drama."

"I liked—" He stopped. Laughed despite himself. "Okay. I liked the drama. One time. I was sixteen."

"You dropped a limb on Mr. Petrelli's fence."

"On the *fence line*, which was technically—"

"Which was his fence." She was grinning at him, rain-wet hair drying into waves, and the sun came through the dripping canopy and lit her up like something he'd dreamed about for fifteen years and convinced himself he'd invented.

The work took most of the morning. He climbed, she belayed the ropes, and when the hanging half finally came free and swung down controlled and heavy into the open lane between rows, the whole remaining tree rocked once and settled, lighter, balanced, alive. They stood together in the fresh-cut smell of sap and sawdust, looking up at the wound in the trunk, clean-edged now, already beginning its slow work of sealing over.

"It'll scar funny," Hazel said. "But it'll seal."

"Most things do," Jonah said, "if you cut them clean."

She turned and looked at him then, and the morning went very quiet. The orchard dripped. Somewhere a mourning dove said its one sad word over and over. She was close enough that he could see the sawdust caught in her lashes, and her expression had opened in a way he hadn't seen since the barn, since the question — *why did you leave?* — that was still hanging between them, unanswered, patient as weather.

"Jonah," she said. "Last night. In the barn. You were going to tell me something."

He was. He is. Say it. Say any of it. Start with the fire, start with your father, start with the relief, start anywhere—

"Hazel," he said, and reached up, slowly, giving her every chance to step back, and brushed the sawdust from her cheek with his thumb. Her breath caught. She didn't step back. The space between them narrowed to a decision, and her eyes dropped to his mouth and stayed there, and the whole orchard seemed to lean in—

Her phone rang.

She flinched like the lightning had come back. The moment broke and scattered into the morning, and she pulled the phone from her coat pocket, and Jonah watched her face change as she read the screen — watched the warmth drain out of it and something guarded slide into place.

"It's Marcus Hale," she said.

"He can wait."

"He says it's about the deed." She looked up at him, and the stranger was back behind her eyes, the woman who ran numbers she already knew were fatal. "He says he's found something in the county records. Something about the postcard."

The word landed in Jonah's chest like a stone dropped down a well. The postcard. The forged thing sitting at the center of the deed like a spider at the center of a web — and Hale, circling, smiling, holding lunch meetings and firm handshakes, now claiming to have found something in the records.

"Hazel," Jonah said carefully, "don't meet him alone."

"He asked me to lunch."

"Then don't have lunch alone either." He heard how it sounded — possessive, panicked — and couldn't find a better version fast enough. "Whatever he thinks he's found, he's not telling you because it helps us. Men like Hale don't share information. They sell it."

She was already typing a reply, thumbs moving, and when she looked up again she wore the polite closed face she'd worn the day he walked back into her café.

"I can handle Marcus Hale," she said. "I've handled worse."

*Have you?* he almost asked. *Have you ever actually looked at what you've been handling?*

But the moment was gone, sealed over like the cut in the tree, and she was walking back toward the tailgate to collect the thermoses, and the only thing Jonah could think, watching her go, was that the storm had spared the harvest and split the oldest tree wide open — and that he had a feeling, the way you feel weather coming, that the next thing to split was going to be the truth.
