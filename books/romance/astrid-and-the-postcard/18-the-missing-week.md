## 18. The Missing Week

The night Jonah Voss decided to leave Bellamy Orchard forever, the fireflies were out in numbers that would have made Hazel laugh.

He remembered that, later, in all the years of remembering — how she'd stood on the porch steps the summer before, arms spread like a conductor, and said, *Look at them, Jonah. It's like the orchard is breathing.* She'd been sixteen then, seventeen, some age when wonder was still legal. And he had looked, because looking at things with Hazel was its own kind of religion. You didn't have to believe in anything else if you believed in the way she saw the world.

The night he left, the fireflies were out and nobody was watching them, and that seemed to him, even at nineteen, even with his hands shaking on the steering wheel of his father's truck, like the whole story in miniature. The orchard breathing. Nobody watching.

* * *

It had started, as most catastrophes do, as an ordinary evening.

August, the summer they were nineteen. The air had that thick green weight it got in late August, apples swelling on the boughs, the light going amber earlier every day. Jonah had spent the afternoon up a ladder in Block Four, thinning the crop, his shirt stuck to his back, while Hazel sat below against the trunk reading aloud from a paperback she'd bought for fifty cents at the church sale — some novel about a woman who ran away to the sea. Every so often she'd stop and say, *Listen to this,* and read a line again, slower, the way you'd taste something.

"You'd never run away," he'd called down to her. "You couldn't leave this place."

"I could," she said, not looking up. "I just never would."

That was the difference between them, he'd thought then. He was always half-packed. She was rooted so deep that pulling her up would kill her, and she knew it, and she said it plainly, the way she said everything, and he loved her for it in the wordless, terrified way of boys who don't yet know that love is a thing you can lose by keeping quiet.

That evening there was a bonfire planned. Not at the orchard — at the Kettle Creek lot behind the Hendricks place, the usual end-of-summer thing, crates and pallets and somebody's uncle's guitar. Hazel had promised to bring pie. She'd been baking since noon; the café kitchen — her father's kitchen, really, though Astrid Bellamy's hands had shaped everything in it — smelled like browned butter and burnt sugar, and Jonah had stolen a piece of crust off the cooling rack and been swatted with a dish towel for his trouble.

They were supposed to go together. That was the plan. That was always the plan.

But Hazel's father had come home early from the co-op in a mood, and there was a phone call, and then Hazel appeared at the truck window with flour still dusting her forearms and an apology already forming. "Rain check," she said. "Dad needs me to inventory the cellar. He says tomorrow we'll do something, just us." A small grimace. "Father-daughter bonding. Pray for me."

"Want me to stay? Help you count jars?"

"And listen to him sigh at you all night?" She leaned through the window and kissed him, quick and floury, at the corner of his mouth. "Go. Be social. Tell Marcus Hale's dad I said his tractor is an abomination."

So Jonah went alone. And that — that single ordinary divergence, a girl counting jars in a cellar instead of sitting beside him on a tailgate — was the hinge on which the next fifteen years swung. He would lie awake in a hundred rented rooms afterward, in a dozen cities, doing the arithmetic of it. *If she had come. If I had stayed. If the rain hadn't held off.*

If, if, if. The math never came out any different.

* * *

The bonfire was fine. That was the thing he could never make anyone understand, in the years after, when there was no one to tell: the bonfire was *fine*. Beer passed around in paper cups, Danny Hendricks butchering "Wagon Wheel" on a guitar missing two strings, someone's little sister catching fireflies in a mason jar and letting them go one by one like tiny lanterns being released back into service. Jonah stayed until eleven because leaving early meant questions, and questions meant admitting he'd rather be anywhere Hazel was, and at nineteen he still had enough pride left to hide that.

He drove home slow with the windows down, the truck bed rattling with empty pallet wood he'd promised to return. The orchard road was dark under the trees, that particular darkness you only get in fruit country in August, when the canopy closes overhead and even the stars come through in pieces.

He saw the glow before he understood it.

Orange, low and wrong, flickering through the trunks off to the east — toward the old barn. Not the bonfire; the bonfire was miles behind him now, dying down to embers. This was closer. This was *theirs*.

He didn't remember stopping the truck. He remembered running. Gravel to grass to the soft churned earth of the barnyard, and the heat hitting him like a wall, and the sound — God, the sound, that wet roaring crackle of old timber going, the barn's tin roof popping like gunfire as it expanded and buckled. The Bellamys' barn. The barn Astrid Bellamy had painted red every third summer until her arms gave out, the barn where Hazel had learned to drive a stick shift at fourteen and cried laughing the whole time, the barn that held forty years of tools and tackle and the hand-lettered sign from the first festival, *BELLAMY ORCHARD, EST. 1924*, and the loft where —

Where he and Hazel had —

The heat drove him back three steps. He stood in the yard with his mouth full of smoke and ash and screamed her name, screamed it until his throat tore, because she lived fifty yards away across the orchard grass and if she was in that building —

She wasn't. He found that out thirty seconds later when the house door banged open and lights came on and her father's voice cut across the yard like a blade: "She's inside. She's fine. Where the hell were *you*?"

And Jonah, nineteen years old, smoke in his lungs and terror in his mouth, heard the question underneath the question. Heard what the man was really asking. And made the mistake — the first of the night's mistakes, though it wouldn't feel like a mistake for years — of answering honestly.

"I was at the Hendricks'. There was a bonfire. Half the town saw me there."

*Half the town saw me there.* An alibi, offered freely, before anyone had asked for one. He'd think about that phrasing for fifteen years too. Why had he led with that? Because some animal part of him had already understood, standing in that burning heat, that alibis were about to matter.

* * *

The fire burned itself out by dawn. There was nothing to save; the volunteer crew mostly just kept it from jumping to the tree lines. By morning the barn was a black ribcage against the pink sky, steam still rising off the collapsed roofline, and the whole town came to look the way towns do — in trucks, slowly, hats off, saying *shame* and *shameful* and *any idea how it started?*

Any idea how it started.

Jonah stood with Hazel in the orchard grass while the neighbors murmured, and she was gray-faced and shaking and wouldn't hold his hand, which he took for shock, because it was shock, but it was also something else he didn't have a name for yet. Her eyes kept going to the ruin and sliding away from it, like a tongue finding a broken tooth.

"I was in the cellar," she kept saying. "I was in the cellar the whole time. I counted the jars twice because Dad wanted exact numbers."

"I know," he said. "I know, Haze."

"The last thing I remember is the cellar. It's like —" Her brow creased, that small frown she got over a recipe that wouldn't behave. "It's like the evening has a hole in it. I came upstairs and Dad was shouting about the barn and I don't remember walking up the stairs. Isn't that strange?"

"It's shock," he said, with the confidence of someone who had no idea.

Because here is what Jonah did not know, what he would not know for fifteen years, what he was not equipped at nineteen to even imagine: that holes in an evening are not always simple shock. That the mind, faced with something unbearable, does not always merely flinch — sometimes it edits. Sometimes it takes the unbearable hour and lifts it cleanly out, the way you'd lift a rotten apple from a crate so the rot doesn't spread, and sets it somewhere the conscious self will never think to look, and seals the wall over it, and goes on.

Hazel Hartwell had come up from that cellar and walked into the barn — he would learn this much later, in pieces, from a deputy's report he was never supposed to see — had walked into the barn carrying a hurricane lamp to fetch a jar of her grandmother's preserved cherries for the fair, because her father had snapped at her that the cellar inventory could wait, actually, because Astrid wanted the cherries tonight, and the lamp's filler cap had been loose, and she'd set it on the hay-bale edge while she climbed, and the flame had followed the spill down into the straw the way fire does, greedily, instantly, without malice or intent or any human wickedness at all.

A girl. A loose cap. A lamp. Dry August hay.

And then the mind had done what minds do, and the hole opened, and Hazel came up the stairs into her father's shouting remembering nothing but the cellar, twice-counted jars, the smell of browned butter still on her arms.

* * *

Raymond Hartwell came to the Voss farmhouse on the third day.

Jonah's father let him into the kitchen and then, with a look at his son that said *stay*, went and stood by the sink with his arms folded. Raymond sat down without being asked, which was itself a message. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with Hazel's same dark hair going silver early and none of her warmth anywhere in his face. He set a document case on the table and did not open it.

"Do you know what accelerant residue means, boy?"

Jonah's stomach dropped through the floorboards of the world. "There wasn't any —"

"Lamp oil." Raymond said it flatly, watching him. "Kerosene, from a fill lamp. Found soaked through the straw by the loft ladder. Now, I've been asking myself who had reason to be in my barn that night with a lamp. And I keep coming back to the fact that my daughter was home, safe, counting jars in my cellar — and you were nowhere anyone can account for between eleven o'clock and whenever you came tearing up the lane screaming your head off."

"I told you. The bonfire. People saw me leave —"

"People saw you at a bonfire, yes. Bonfires end." Raymond opened the document case now, unhurried, and drew out papers he had clearly already shown to someone — the sheriff's office, Jonah would learn later, informally, over coffee, a man laying groundwork. "Arson with endangerment is a felony in this state. A nineteen-year-old with a record doesn't just do time. He loses any future worth having. College money, gone. Any job that asks, gone. And a Voss conviction in this county — well. You know how this town keeps books."

The kitchen was very quiet. His father hadn't moved from the sink. Jonah understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Raymond Hartwell had rehearsed this. Had practiced it in his truck on the drive over, maybe. Had chosen *boy* and *records* and *keeps books* the way a carpenter chooses nails.

"There were matches in that barn older than me," Jonah said. "Anyone could've —"

"Here's what happens now." Raymond wasn't listening. Men like him never listened; listening implied the possibility of revision. "You're going to leave. Tonight, tomorrow, I don't care which, but soon, and far. You'll write whatever you like to my daughter — no, actually, better if you write nothing at all. Cleaner. A clean break reads like guilt, and guilt is what I want this town to read. And in exchange, the sheriff's file stays a conversation we had over coffee. No charges. No record. You get to become whoever you're going to become somewhere that isn't here."

"Why?" The word came out of Jonah cracked down the middle. "Why would you want your own town thinking I burned your barn?"

And Raymond Hartwell looked at him then — really looked, and for one unguarded second something moved behind his eyes that Jonah spent years misreading as hatred and would eventually, much later, understand as fear — and said:

"Because my daughter loves you. And if this goes to trial, she testifies. She sits in a courtroom and relives the worst night of her life in front of strangers, and either she defends you and this town decides she chose a boy over her own family, or she doesn't and it breaks something in her that won't ever mend. Either way I lose her a little. So no. There won't be a trial. There'll be a departure." He stood, buttoned his jacket, picked up the empty case. "You seem like a boy who understands sacrifice. Prove it."

At the door he paused, without turning around. "She'll hate you for a while. That's all right. Hate heals faster than grief. Give her something solid to hate and she'll get through this." A beat. "Better than she'd get through the truth."

Then he was gone, and the screen door slapped, and Jonah's father turned from the sink and asked his son what Raymond Hartwell had wanted, and Jonah — nineteen, terrified, and already learning the shape of the silence he'd carry for fifteen years — said, "Nothing, Dad. Nothing that matters."

* * *

He wrote the letter anyway. Of course he wrote the letter. Fourteen drafts, the last one burned in the same sink where his father washed dishes, because every version either told too much or explained nothing, and a letter that explains nothing is just a knife with your name engraved on the handle.

*By the time you read this I'll be gone. Don't look for me. Some things are better finished than fixed. — J.*

He left it under the stone on the porch rail where she'd once hidden a birthday present for him, because he wanted her to find it the way she'd found that present — surprised, and certain it was meant only for her. Then he packed a duffel bag, took two hundred dollars from the coffee can his mother kept for emergencies, and stood for a long moment in the farmyard with the fireflies coming up out of the grass all around him, hundreds of them, the orchard breathing its slow green breath, nobody watching.

He did not look at the barn. He couldn't. Even ruined, even a skeleton, it felt like looking at her.

The truck started on the second try. He drove out past Block Four, past the bridge over Kettle Creek where she'd taught him to skip stones and he'd taught her to swear properly, past the county line sign he'd spray-painted with Danny Hendricks when they were sixteen and gotten a week of chores apiece for it. He watched all of it in the mirror until the road bent and took it away.

He was nineteen years old. He had been given a choice between destroying a girl he loved and disappearing, and he had chosen disappearance, and he had believed — he had *needed* to believe — that it was the braver option. That taking the blame clean, without explanation, without defense, was a kind of gift. Let her hate me, he'd told himself, driving through the dark with his headlights picking moths out of the night. Let her hate me and heal. Raymond said hate heals faster.

What he had not counted on — what no one had counted on, because no one knew — was that Hazel's memory had already done the cruelest work of all. That she would wake the next morning with a hole where the truth should be, and build herself a story to fill it, and the story would starve her for fifteen years. That she would remember a betrayal with no content, a wound with no weapon, and that she would aim the wound at him because he was the one who vanished, and vanishing looks exactly like guilt.

He had taken the exile to spare her the fire.

And she had spent every year since believing the fire was him.

* * *

Fifteen years later, Jonah Voss stood in the doorway of Hartwell's Café holding survey maps and a buyer's offer, watching Hazel Hartwell laugh at something Priya said behind the counter, and felt the whole buried architecture of that August night shift and groan inside him like the barn roof had shifted and groaned, one more degree of heat, one more pop of stressed tin, before the collapse.

She looked up. She saw him. Fifteen years fell on both of them at once.

And Jonah thought, with a clarity that felt like the first cold drop before a storm: *she doesn't know. She genuinely doesn't know what happened that night — and sooner or later, one of us is going to have to tell her.*

He just hadn't yet figured out how to tell her that the person who started the fire was her.
