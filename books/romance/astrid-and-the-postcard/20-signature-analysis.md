## 20. Signature Analysis

The graphologist worked out of a converted garage in Corvallis, behind a house with wind chimes made of spoons, and her name was Dr. Imogen Reyes-Ferris, which she had printed on a business card along with the words *Forensic Document Examination* in a font that looked like it had been chosen by someone who wanted very badly to be trusted.

Hazel sat across from her at a workbench lit by two articulated lamps, the postcard lying between them under glass like a specimen. It had been three days since the archive — three days of carrying the knowledge of water rights around inside her like a stone she couldn't set down or swallow. Three days of telling Jonah almost nothing over the phone, because some instinct older than sense told her to hold this close until she understood its shape.

"You said on the phone you were taught cursive by the writer," Dr. Reyes-Ferris said. She was sixty-something, silver hair cropped short, hands that moved with the economy of someone who had spent decades looking at what other people missed. "Tell me about that."

"She raised me, half the time." Hazel kept her voice level. It took effort. "Summers at the orchard. She believed handwriting was character made visible. She'd have me copy passages out of her recipe book until my letters matched hers. Slant, pressure, the way she crossed her t's — low, flat, like she was keeping them from flying off."

"And did they match?"

"Eventually. For a while, I could sign her name better than I could sign mine." Hazel heard how that sounded and didn't apologize for it. "She thought it was funny. Said forgery was just attention wearing a costume."

Something flickered across the examiner's face — approval, maybe, or recognition. "Then you'll understand what I found faster than most clients." She adjusted one lamp so the light raked sideways across the postcard's face, low and golden, throwing every stroke of ink into relief. "Look here. The capital A in *Astrid*."

Hazel leaned in. She had looked at this postcard a hundred times since the lawyer first produced it — the cheerful message about boundary lines being settled, the signature looping confidently at the bottom, dated eleven years ago, filed with the county as though it had always existed. She had hated it. She had studied it. But she had never seen it under raking light.

"The entry stroke starts too high," Hazel said slowly. "Grandma started her A's from the baseline. She said a letter should rise like something growing, not drop like something falling."

Dr. Reyes-Ferris smiled without warmth. "Go on."

"And the crossbar—" Hazel's breath caught. "It's drawn, not written. You can see the pen hesitate. Grandma never hesitated. She wrote like she talked, all forward motion, even when she was wrong."

"Drawn, not written. That's exactly right." The examiner pulled a magnifier on a swivel arm down over the card. "Whoever wrote this knew your grandmother's hand well enough to imitate its general architecture. The slant is correct. The letterforms are largely correct. But imitation lives in the conscious mind, and handwriting lives below it. Under magnification, the conscious mind leaves fingerprints everywhere." She tapped the glass gently. "Tremor in the downstrokes where your grandmother's hand would have been fluid. Pen lifts in connected words — see, here, the *ll* in Bellamy, there's a microscopic break where a real writer would have carried through. And the spacing drifts wider toward the end of each line, which happens when someone is copying rather than composing. They run out of model and start inventing, and invention takes more room."

Hazel sat back. Her heart was doing something strange and slow, like a clock winding down. "You're certain."

"I'm certain enough to put it in writing, which is what you're paying me for." Dr. Reyes-Ferris slid a sheet across the bench — already typed, already signed, waiting only for Hazel to be ready to receive it. "The postcard was not written by Astrid Bellamy. In my professional opinion, it was produced by a skilled imitator working from exemplars of her handwriting — probably several — with access to personal details that lent it authenticity. The reference to the east fence line, for instance. That's not public record."

"No. It isn't." Only family knew about the east fence. Only family, and whoever the family had talked to.

"There's more, and it's the part I'd want you sitting down for, if you weren't already." The examiner turned back to the card and lifted it, tilting it against the light so the surface caught like oil on water. "Ink. Ballpoint, standard dye-based formulation. Do you know anything about ink dating?"

"I know paper yellows. I didn't know ink did anything interesting."

"Ink does everything interesting. Dye-based ballpoint inks fade and undergo chemical change on a predictable curve — solvents evaporate first, then the dyes themselves degrade. There are laboratories that can take a small sample — we're talking a pinprick, barely visible — and place an ink within a production window based on its formulation." She paused, letting Hazel follow. "This particular formulation wasn't manufactured until four years after the date on this postcard."

The garage went very quiet. Outside, the spoon chimes turned over in a breeze Hazel didn't feel.

"Say that again," Hazel said.

"The ink is younger than the document claims to be. Whoever wrote this postcard wrote it no earlier than seven years ago — likely less. Your grandmother had been dead for years by then." Dr. Reyes-Ferris set the card down with the care people reserve for things that might bite. "This isn't an old mistake. This isn't a confused old woman misremembering a conversation. Someone manufactured this document recently, deliberately, and filed it with the county to change the legal reality of your land. And they were good enough at it that it held for years."

Hazel's hands were flat on the workbench. She watched them as if they belonged to someone calmer. "Filed it how? Documents don't just walk into a recorder's office."

"They walk in attached to transactions, amendments, title corrections. Somebody has to submit them, and somebody has to accept them." The examiner's eyes were sharp and not unkind. "I examine documents, Miss Hartwell. People are above my pay grade. But I'll tell you what the document tells me about its author: patient, methodical, familiar with both your family and county procedure. This wasn't opportunism. This was planned."

* * *

The drive back took ninety minutes and Hazel spent all of them talking to herself in the car, out loud, because the silence kept filling up with images she didn't want.

Seven years ago. She ran the arithmetic again and again, hoping it would come out different. Seven years ago she was thirty-one, running the café on loans and optimism, and her grandmother had been dead for four years, and the orchard had been sitting in probate limbo like a body nobody would bury. Seven years ago, someone had decided the limbo needed resolving — in their favor.

The orchard road unspooled ahead of her, gold and green in the late light, and she thought about how many times she'd driven it this season without once thinking of it as something a stranger might want to take apart.

By the time she reached town she had a list. Not written down — she wasn't ready to write it down, writing made things real in ways that couldn't be taken back — but arranged in her head with the same care she gave a mise en place.

Three names.

The first was Dale Voss, Jonah's uncle, who had managed the orchard's affairs during the probate years and who had, according to Jonah, spent those same years arguing that the property should be sold to the highest bidder and the proceeds split before the trees rotted where they stood. Dale had access. Dale had motive. Dale had sat across from Hazel at two funerals and one awkward Thanksgiving and told her, both times, that sentiment was a tax on the living.

The second was Gerald Finch, the retired county clerk whose signature — or whose stamp, anyway — appeared on the recording page where the postcard had entered the public record. Hazel had called the records office twice this week asking casual questions, and both times the woman on the phone had gotten slightly vaguer whenever Finch's name came up, the way people do when a name carries weather.

And the third—

Hazel parked behind the café and sat in the driver's seat with her forehead against the wheel.

The third name was Marcus Hale, or rather the machinery around him: his acquisition team, his title company, his lawyers, the smooth apparatus of a man who bought land the way other people bought groceries. He'd been circling the Bellamy parcel for two years — she'd learned that from Priya, who read the business section the way other people read gossip columns. Two years of inquiries, appraisals, letters of interest. What if the postcard hadn't been the opening move of a family squabble at all? What if it had been groundwork? A poisoned deed planted years in advance, waiting for a buyer sophisticated enough to know exactly what a clouded water right was worth — and worth making disappear?

It sounded like a conspiracy theory. She knew that. She also knew that Dr. Reyes-Ferris had said *planned*, and *patient*, and *familiar with county procedure*, and that none of those phrases fit a grieving nephew or a sloppy clerk half as well as they fit a professional.

Inside, the café smelled of cinnamon and closing time. Priya was wiping down the pastry case, and she looked up with the expression she wore when she'd been waiting for Hazel to come home to herself.

"Well?" Priya said.

"It's forged." Hazel hung her coat on the hook by the door, a gesture that required steadiness she had to manufacture. "Completely. And the ink is newer than the date. Someone wrote it within the last decade and slipped it into the record."

Priya set down her cloth. "Someone specific."

"Three someones, maybe." Hazel came behind the counter, tied on an apron purely for the comfort of the knot at her back, and finally let herself say the names aloud, one after another, tasting each for rot. "Dale Voss. Gerald Finch. And whoever works for Marcus Hale."

"Hale." Priya repeated it flatly. "The man buying you lunch last month?"

"The man buying everyone lunch. That's how he operates." Hazel pulled the day's ledger toward her and stared at numbers she could recite in her sleep. "He told me cash now, condos later, and he smiled while he said it, and I hated myself for doing the math."

"And now you think he might have forged a dead woman's handwriting to make the math easier."

"I think someone did. And I think the list of people who could is short." Hazel looked up. "There's a records clerk who processed the filing. Finch. He's retired now. Widowed, I think — there was an obituary in the Gazette a few years back, his wife. He lives out past the grange hall."

Priya studied her for a long moment. "You're going to go knock on his door."

"I'm going to bake first. Then I'm going to go knock on his door." Hazel opened the flour bin, and the familiar motion of measuring steadied her voice. "A man alone in a quiet house will talk to anyone who brings a pie. My grandmother taught me that too."

"That woman," Priya said, "taught you crime."

"My grandmother taught me *attention*." Hazel cracked eggs one-handed, watching the yolks tremble and hold. "Wearing a costume."

* * *

She baked until midnight. It was the old ritual, the one that had carried her through her grandmother's funeral and the café's worst winter and every night since Jonah Voss walked back into her life with survey maps under his arm: butter cut into flour, sugar measured by weight, the oven ticking as it climbed. Her hands knew the work so thoroughly that her mind was free to do its darker arithmetic.

Dale Voss. She laid him out like a case file. Access: total. Motive: documented — he'd wanted the orchard sold for a decade, and a forged postcard muddying the title would depress the price, scare off competing buyers, and let him snap up the whole parcel cheap through whatever shell he favored. Method: plausible. Dale was cheap, and cheap men sometimes found expensive shortcuts.

Gerald Finch. Access: absolute — nothing entered the record without passing his desk. Motive: unknown, unless it was money, and money was always a candidate. Method: trivial. A clerk doesn't forge; a clerk just stops looking closely, or looks closely and says nothing, for a fee.

Hale's people. Access: they'd need a source inside the family or the county — but they had money, and money buys sources the way water finds cracks. Motive: the strongest of all, because a clean title was the only thing standing between Marcus Hale and forty acres of developable river-valley land, and men like Hale didn't wait for obstacles, they manufactured solutions years in advance and let them ripen.

Three names. And underneath them, unwelcome as a draft under a door, a fourth possibility she refused to write down even in the privacy of her own skull: that the answer might implicate the Voss family in a way that would hand Hazel a weapon she didn't want to hold. Because she had spent fifteen years believing Jonah had abandoned her, and she had begun — carefully, resentfully, in the small hours — to wonder whether that belief was load-bearing. If the Vossses had done this, if Dale had forged her grandmother's hand, then her hatred had been pointed correctly all along, and she could keep it, polished, familiar, hers.

She caught herself wanting that. Wanting the forgery to be Dale's, wanting it to be simple, wanting her fifteen years of anger to turn out to have been justified — and understood, standing in the blue light of the oven window, that this was exactly the kind of lie she'd built her whole life out of. The comfortable version. The version that flattered her.

The madeleines came out gold-edged and perfect. She wrapped six of them in wax paper, put the kettle on for nothing, and stood in the empty kitchen holding a warm box like a talisman.

Tomorrow she would drive out past the grange hall and knock on Gerald Finch's door. Tomorrow she would smile at a retired clerk and watch his hands while he talked, because Dr. Reyes-Ferris had taught her today that the truth lived in the small movements people didn't know they were making.

Tonight she allowed herself one call. Jonah picked up on the second ring, wide awake — she could hear the orchard wind behind him, and the creak of the farmhouse porch swing he'd been repairing all week.

"It's forged," she said, without preamble. "The postcard. And the ink is younger than the date. Someone filed it within the last ten years."

Silence, long enough that she checked the screen to see if the call had dropped.

"Ten years," Jonah said finally, and his voice had gone somewhere she didn't recognize. "That's— Hazel, that's not grief. That's not confusion. That's—"

"A crime. Yes."

Another silence, and then, quieter, almost to himself: "My uncle handled everything back then. Everything."

"I know." Hazel looked at the wax-paper box on her counter, six madeleines destined for a stranger's table, and felt the investigation tighten around her like a corset. "Jonah. Who else knew about the east fence line?"

The question landed in the dark between them, and she heard him draw breath to answer — and stop. She heard the stopping. Fifteen years ago she'd have missed it. Now she catalogued it, filed it, and waited.

"Hazel," Jonah said carefully, "there's something I need to tell you about who else had access to my father's papers."

The wind moved through the receiver like a held breath, and Hazel stood very still in her kitchen, listening.
