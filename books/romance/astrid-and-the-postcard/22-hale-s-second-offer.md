## 22. Hale's Second Offer

The rain had been coming down since dawn in that thin, needling way that made the whole town feel like it was being sifted through a screen. Hazel watched it from behind the café counter, watching it bead and run down the window beside the door where she'd taped the festival flyer — *CENTENNIAL CELEBRATION, BELLAMY ORCHARD, ONE NIGHT ONLY* — the paper already going soft at its corners, the ink beginning to bleed like something wounded.

She should have taken it down. She knew that. If Marcus Hale walked past this window — and he would; he'd been walking past this window for three days now, slow as a man measuring a coffin — he would see that flyer and understand exactly what she was doing with his orchard. Because that was how he thought of it. His orchard. She could tell by the way he said *the property* on the phone, by the way he'd started sending flowers to the café, absurd white lilies that filled the whole room with their funeral sweetness.

The bell over the door chimed at eleven-forty, and there he was, shaking rain off an umbrella worth more than her espresso machine.

"Hazel Hartwell." He smiled the smile she'd come to think of as his working face — warm at the edges, calculating at the center, like a ledger with a pretty cover. "You look tired."

"Thank you, Marcus. I've been sleeping beautifully."

He laughed, delighted, and hung his umbrella on the hook by the door as if he owned the hook, the door, possibly the building. He wore charcoal gray today, no tie, the top button of his shirt undone just enough to suggest a man who didn't have to try. Everything about him was calibrated. Even his cologne arrived a half-second before he did.

"Coffee," he said, settling onto the stool at the end of the counter, the one farthest from Priya's station. "Whatever you recommend. And I'll take the seat with the best view of you, if that's available."

"It isn't." But she poured the coffee anyway, because throwing him out would be a scene, and scenes were what he wanted. A scene meant witnesses. Witnesses meant leverage. She'd learned enough about him in the last two weeks — from the clerk's widow, from the county records, from Jonah's tight jaw whenever Hale's name came up — to know that Marcus Hale never entered a room without wanting something from it.

She set the cup down. He wrapped both hands around it like a man warming himself at a fire he'd lit himself.

"So," she said.

"So." He sipped. His eyebrows went up, honest surprise this time. "That's actually good. I expected burnt."

"You expected me to be failing."

"I expected your books to be failing. There's a difference." He turned on the stool to face her fully, and the warmth drained out of his voice so smoothly it was almost impressive, like watching someone close a drawer. "Hazel. Let's not do the dance again. You know why I'm here. I know why I'm here. The only question is whether we're going to keep pretending this is about coffee."

* * *

She wiped down the counter while he talked, because her hands needed something to betray or not betray, and a rag gave them somewhere honest to go.

"The new number is four hundred thousand," Hale said. "For your half. Cash, thirty-day close, no contingencies beyond title."

Hazel kept wiping. Four hundred thousand. The number landed in her chest like a stone dropped into a well — she heard it fall and fall and never heard it hit bottom. Four hundred thousand dollars. Enough to pay off the bank twice over. Enough to replace the ancient oven that ran hot on the left side. Enough to fix the roof before winter, to give Priya a raise she'd earned three times over, to stop doing arithmetic in the shower, stop lying awake cataloguing debts the way other people counted sheep.

Enough to make all of it — the forged postcard, the archive dust, the graphologist's report sitting in a folder under the register like contraband — unnecessary.

"That's up from three-twenty," she said, keeping her voice flat.

"It is."

"And the reason for the increase?"

Hale set down his cup. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and the flyer's bleeding ink seemed to spread another millimeter.

"Because I know what you found in the county archive," he said.

There it was. No preamble, no softening. He said it the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*, and Hazel felt a strange flicker of respect beneath the fear — he wasn't going to insult her with pretense. Not anymore.

"The water rights," she said. "If Astrid Bellamy's postcard is valid, Block Four can't be rezoned for residential. Your condos die on the vine. So the land's only worth anything to you if the postcard is worthless." She folded the rag over the rail of the sink. "And the postcard is a forgery. Filed eleven years ago, ink dated after my grandmother died, pushed through a clerk who took money from everybody's lawyers including yours. I know, Marcus. I've known for nine days."

Something moved behind his eyes — not surprise. Confirmation. The look of a man checking a box.

"Then you also know," he said quietly, "that a forged document doesn't help you. It hurts you. It poisons the whole chain of title. Which means—" he spread his hands, generous, reasonable, monstrous, "—that deed you and Jonah Voss are sitting on isn't clean. It hasn't been clean for a decade. And I have attorneys who are very, very good at making dirty titles dirtier."

"You're threatening to contest it."

"I'm offering to buy it." He smiled again, but the smile had teeth in it now, actual enamel, and Hazel understood that she was seeing the real architecture of the man for the first time: the charm was the façade, but underneath the façade was more façade, and underneath *that* was this. "Contesting is what happens if you say no. Those are different conversations. I'd hate for us to have the second conversation when the first one is still on the table."

* * *

Priya came out of the kitchen mid-sentence, saw Hale, read the room in the way she read everything — instantly, completely, without appearing to look — and vanished again. Ten seconds later the smell of cardamom began drifting from the ovens, deliberate, a lifeline thrown across the counter. *I'm here. Keep talking. I've got the room.*

Hazel gripped that lifeline.

"How long?" she asked. "If you contest. Realistically."

"Oh, Hazel." He said her name like a man handling something fragile he intended to break. "Realistically? Two years minimum to get in front of a judge who understands riparian water law. Three if your attorney's any good, because mine will bury them in discovery until they quit or bill you into next decade. Depositions. Subpoenas. Expert witnesses on handwriting, on hydrology, on probate procedure from 1974. Every apple you pick during harvest season, some lawyer sends a letter about it. Every festival flyer—" his gaze slid, casual as a knife laid on a table, toward the window, "—becomes evidence of occupancy under contested title. Do you know what that means?"

"It means you'd sue me for picking apples."

"It means I'd *file motions*. Whether they go anywhere is almost beside the point. Motions cost money to answer. Letters cost money to receive. Time costs everything." He leaned forward, elbows on the counter, and lowered his voice into something almost kind, which was the cruelest register he had. "You have a café with three missed payments. I checked — of course I checked, don't look scandalized, banks sell that information to anyone with a fax machine. You have a business partner—" the pause he left around Jonah's name was surgical, "—who has already lost this valley once and doesn't strike me as a man eager to lose it slower. And you have a town that needs this festival to happen, which means you need it to happen, which means every month you fight me is a month you're spending money you don't have to protect a piece of paper that was forged by somebody neither of us married."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere back in the kitchen, Priya dropped something metal and swore softly, and the small human sound of it steadied Hazel like a hand on her shoulder.

"And if I sign," Hazel said, "what happens to the forgery investigation?"

"What investigation?" Hale spread his hands. "A criminal referral is a rumor. Rumors die. Deeds don't." He reached into his jacket — she flinched before she could stop herself, and hated the flinch, filed it away to be angry about later — and produced not a gun but a folder. Cream-colored. Expensive paper. He laid it on the counter between them without opening it, the way a gambler lays down cards he already knows are winners.

"Four hundred thousand. Thirty days. And in exchange, everyone walks away clean. No contest. No motions. No letters about apples. The referral goes quiet — not because I'd threaten anyone, Hazel, I would never threaten anyone—" the smile again, brief and bright and empty as a show home, "—but because once the sale closes, there's simply nothing left to investigate. Motive evaporates. Interest fades. Towns forget. That's what towns are *for*."

* * *

She thought about her grandmother then, suddenly and violently — not Astrid the legend, Astrid of the framed postcard-to-be and the cursive lessons at the farmhouse table, but Astrid's hands, flour to the wrist, pressing a rolling pin into nine-year-old Hazel's grip and saying, *you don't fight the dough, sweetheart, you convince it.* She thought about the smell of that kitchen. She thought about how Astrid had signed things — slowly, deliberately, each letter a small act of ownership — and how someone had taken that hand and worn it like a mask to steal from her grave.

And she thought about the well inside her chest where the number four hundred thousand was still falling, falling, and how much easier everything would be if she just let it hit bottom and stay there.

"I need to talk to Jonah," she said.

"Of course." Hale closed the folder but didn't take it back. He left it on the counter, cream and patient, a guest that intended to spend the night. "Take the week. Take ten days. But Hazel—" he stood, buttoned his jacket, became charming again the way other people put on coats, "—understand what you're holding. You're not holding a deed. You're holding a lawsuit wearing a deed's clothes. I can either buy the lawsuit or file it. Both options end with me owning Block Four. The only difference is how many years of your life it costs you to get there."

He collected his umbrella. At the door he paused, hand on the bell, and looked back at her with something that in a better man might have been regret.

"For what it's worth — and it isn't worth much — I think you're the best baker in three counties, and I'd genuinely rather this ended well for you." The bell chimed. "Think about it."

* * *

The door closed. The rain kept on. Hazel stood behind her counter with a cream folder in front of her and her grandmother's ghost at her shoulder, and she did not touch the folder. She made herself not touch it. Instead she untaped the bleeding flyer from the window, smoothed it flat against the stainless steel, and taped it back up — straighter this time, corners reinforced with two extra pieces of tape, an act so small and so stubborn that it nearly undid her.

Priya emerged wiping her hands on her apron. She looked at the folder. She looked at Hazel.

"Well?" she said.

"He raised the price," Hazel said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her; apparently the heart really was trainable muscle, trainable even for this, trained by fifteen years of practice at sounding fine. "And showed his teeth."

"And?"

Hazel pulled the folder toward her — not to open it, but to carry it, to get it off her counter, out of her café, into the rain, into the truck, up the muddy lane to the farmhouse where Jonah was probably repairing the irrigation line again because repairing things was the only language he trusted himself to speak.

"And I'm going to go show him," she said, pushing through the door into the downpour, "exactly what we found in the walls."
