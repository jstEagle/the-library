## 23. Festival Preparations

The string lights went up on a Tuesday, which Hazel would later remember as the last easy day of the season.

They came in boxes from the hardware store — four hundred feet of warm white bulbs, the kind that made everything they touched look like a memory of itself. Priya had ordered them without asking, slapping the receipt on the counter beside the espresso machine like a dare. "You volunteered the orchard for the centennial," she said. "You don't get to half-commit."

Hazel had volunteered. That was the strange part. She'd raised her hand at the festival committee meeting before her brain could file an objection, and now there were minutes with her name in them, and a subcommittee — God help her — a *lighting subcommittee*, and Jonah Voss standing in her café at seven in the morning holding two ladders like a man reporting for duty.

"You take the low branches," he said.

"I know how ladders work, Jonah."

"I'm sure you do." He set one down with a clatter that turned every head in the café. Mrs. Okonkwo looked up from her crossword. The two retirees by the window paused mid-argument about the price of eggs. In Kettle Falls, a ladder was news; Jonah Voss carrying one was a headline.

"Low branches," Hazel repeated, because arguing felt too much like flirting and flirting felt too much like something she couldn't afford. She took the other ladder anyway.

* * *

Jonah had forgotten what the orchard looked like from above.

Not literally — he'd walked these rows all summer, he knew every tree the way you know the scarred knuckles of your own hand — but from the top of a ladder, with a coil of lights over his shoulder and the whole valley opening out beyond Block Four, it was different. It was nineteen again. It was the year he'd climbed the water tower on a dare and seen his entire future laid out below him like a map: the orchard, the town, Hazel's kitchen window with its crooked curtain, all of it small enough to hold in one hand.

He'd been so certain futures worked that way. You looked down, you chose, you kept.

"Hold it taut," Hazel called up from three trees over. Her voice carried the way it always had, clear as a bell across open ground. "You're letting it sag."

"It's supposed to sag a little. It's festive."

"It's supposed to be *taut*, and then it sags naturally under its own weight, which is different from starting out sloppy."

He grinned at the trunk in front of his face. Fifteen years, and she still corrected him like he was seventeen and pruning wrong. He'd hated it in June. Somewhere between the irrigation line and the hailstorm — between hauling pipe in wordless rhythm and standing soaked under a split sky while lightning took the oldest tree in the county — he'd stopped hating it. Now it landed somewhere under his ribs, warm and unwelcome, like cider drunk too fast.

He clipped the next strand and let himself look at her. She was halfway up her ladder, hair escaping its tie, a strand of lights draped over one shoulder like a sash. She'd bitten through the plastic on a zip tie and was holding it between her teeth while she worked with both hands, and the sight of it did something unfair to him. She caught him looking.

"What?"

"Nothing. Your technique's fine."

Her eyes narrowed. "That sounded like an insult."

"It wasn't." He descended, coiling slack. "It was the opposite of an insult. I've decided your technique is flawless and I'll be taking no further questions."

She laughed — a short, surprised sound, quickly smothered, but real. He heard it all the way down the ladder.

* * *

By Thursday the whole town had gotten involved, the way Kettle Falls got involved in anything: completely, and with opinions.

Dolores Marsh from the historical society arrived with a folding table and a banner she'd sewn herself — CENTENNIAL HARVEST FESTIVAL, one hundred years, in gold thread gone slightly green with age, because apparently she'd made it for the fiftieth and been saving it. The Fergusons donated hay bales. The high school jazz band promised to play, contingent on access to an electrical outlet, which became Hazel's problem, which became a long extension cord snaking from the farmhouse like an umbilical cord.

And the pie contest. The pie contest deserved its own chapter in the town chronicles. Sign-ups opened Monday morning at the café and by noon there were eleven entries and a dispute. Ruth Calloway had entered her apple crumb, the same recipe that had won 1974, 1987, and 2003, and Marisol Vega had entered a salted caramel apple galette that Ruth publicly referred to as "not technically a pie," igniting a definitional debate that consumed the comment section of the Kettle Falls community page for two full days.

"They're going to riot," Priya said, watching Ruth and Marisol circle each other near the register like weather fronts. "Over fruit and pastry. Actual violence."

"Good," Hazel said. "Let them. It's the first time this town has argued about anything that isn't the orchard or my love life."

Priya gave her a look — the look, the one she'd been giving her since the kitchen confrontation, the one that said *I see you and I'm not done* — but let it go. For now.

Because here was the thing Hazel hadn't said out loud: the town had stopped whispering. Or rather, they'd started whispering something else. Not *poor Hazel Hartwell, café going under, and now sharing land with that Voss boy.* Instead: *have you seen them putting up lights? Have you seen them at the farmers market, laughing? Did you hear they saved the harvest through the storm?* The narrative had turned without her permission. The town had decided, in its collective and meddlesome heart, that this was a story with a particular ending, and they were rooting for it loudly enough that she couldn't pretend not to hear.

She should have minded. She did mind, a little, the way you mind someone reading over your shoulder. But underneath the minding was something worse and warmer: she didn't mind it at all.

* * *

Friday night, after the volunteers went home, they stayed.

It wasn't planned. Dolores left at six, the Fergusons at seven, the extension cord was coiled, the jazz band's outlet tested, and then Hazel stood in the middle of Block Two with a roll of lights still unstrung and thought, absurdly, *just one more row.* And Jonah, who should have gone back to the motel, picked up the other end.

The orchard at dusk was a country unto itself. The light came down through the canopy in long amber shafts, and the grass smelled cut and sweet, and somewhere off toward Block Four the damaged old tree stood with its lightning scar black against the evening, wearing its wound like the rest of them wore theirs.

They worked without talking much. That was the miracle of it, the thing Hazel kept noticing and refusing to examine: they didn't need words anymore. Sometime over the summer the silence between them had changed composition — from the loaded, barbed silence of June into something easier, a silence with furniture in it, rooms you could move around in. He held the ladder. She climbed. She called measurements; he trusted them. When a bulb shorted and the whole strand went dark, they cursed in unison and then looked at each other, startled, and laughed until the laugh hurt.

"There," Jonah said finally, when the last row was strung. They stood back in the grass, necks craned, and he reached over and plugged the strand into the portable battery pack, and the lights came on all at once — warm white, hundreds of them, threaded through the dark rows like the orchard had grown its own constellation.

Hazel didn't say anything. She was looking up with her mouth slightly open, and the light lay across her face in little coins, and Jonah thought: *this is the part where I say something I can't take back.*

"This is the part," he said instead, "where we test the emergency lighting protocol."

"The what?"

"In case of outage during the festival. Standard procedure." He unplugged it. Plugged it back in. The lights blinked on, off, on. "See? Flawless. No further questions."

"You're ridiculous."

"I contain multitudes. Mostly ridiculous ones."

She bumped his shoulder with hers — actually bumped it, deliberate, the way she used to when they were kids walking the fence line — and neither of them moved away afterward. They stood there shoulder to shoulder in the lit-up dark, and the orchard hummed around them, moths ticking against bulbs, the far-off sound of the creek doing its endless arithmetic.

"Hazel." His voice came out lower than he intended.

"Don't," she said softly. Not sharp. Almost gentle. "Not yet. Let me have tonight being simple."

He nodded. And because he was learning — slowly, at thirty-four, the way some people learn languages late — he let it be simple. They stayed another hour, testing nothing, fixing nothing, just walking the rows under the lights they'd hung together, and when they finally packed up, their hands brushed passing the battery pack, and neither of them apologized, and neither of them mentioned it, and both of them drove home thinking about it.

* * *

Saturday was pies.

The judging table went up under the biggest apple tree in Block One, and by ten a.m. the orchard held more people than Hazel had ever seen on the property — not counting the storm night, which didn't count, that had been survival and this was something else. This was celebration. Kids ran the rows shrieking. The jazz band played something upbeat and slightly ragged. Dolores Marsh presided over a history table of photographs, including one from 1951 showing Astrid Bellamy — young, sun-hatted, unmistakable — standing exactly where the judging table now stood, holding a pie aloft like a trophy.

"She won that year," Dolores told anyone within earshot. "Astrid Bellamy. Best apple pie in three counties. Said the secret was patience and a hot oven." She smiled at Hazel. "And stubbornness. Lots of stubbornness."

Hazel touched the edge of the photograph, careful not to smudge it. Her grandmother looked back at her from seventy years away, grinning, and Hazel felt the familiar ache — grief and gratitude braided so tight she couldn't tell which was which.

The contest itself was carnage. Eleven pies, five judges, one increasingly heated dispute over whether a galette qualified (it did; Ruth sulked magnificently). Hazel entered at the last minute, on a dare from Priya, with a brown butter apple tart she'd thrown together at five a.m. in a fit of insomnia-borne inspiration. She didn't win — Ruth Calloway took the ribbon, again, to no one's surprise and everyone's satisfaction — but she placed third, and Priya hugged her hard enough to crack a rib, and for one whole afternoon Hazel Hartwell forgot to run numbers in her head.

Jonah pressed cider at a hand-crank press the Fergusons lent, and children lined up to turn the crank, and he let them believe they were doing all the work. Hazel watched him from the tart table — watched him crouch to a six-year-old's height, show her how to hold the handle, watch the girl's whole face light when the first juice ran copper into the bucket — and felt something in her chest come loose and refuse to go back.

"You're staring," Priya murmured, sliding in beside her with two cups of cider.

"I'm supervising."

"You're staring at that man like he's the last warm thing in October."

"Priya."

"No, no. I'm saying it once and then I'm never saying it again." Priya handed her a cup. "Whatever you're waiting for — permission, a sign, the deed to clear, the stars to align — it isn't coming. There's just Tuesday, and then Wednesday. Take one of them."

Hazel drank her cider and didn't answer. But she didn't argue either, which from her was practically a signature on a contract.

* * *

The festival committee meeting Sunday evening ran long and happy, and afterward Hazel found Jonah in the farmhouse kitchen, washing cider presses in the old stone sink because he was the kind of person who cleaned equipment nobody asked him to clean.

"Committee voted," she said, leaning in the doorway. "Festival's officially on the orchard. Full day. Fire marshal signed off. Dolores is already talking about a commemorative plaque."

"A plaque."

"For the hundredth harvest. Apparently the Bellamy orchard is historically significant."

"It is," Jonah said, not looking up from the sink. "Your grandmother would have hated the attention and secretly loved the plaque."

Hazel laughed. Then, because the kitchen was small and warm and smelled like apples and dish soap, and because Priya's voice was still in her ear — *there's just Tuesday, and then Wednesday* — she crossed the room and picked up a towel and started drying presses beside him. Their elbows knocked. Neither moved.

"My grandmother used to make us press cider right here," she said. "You remember."

"I remember everything about this kitchen." He handed her a press plate, fingers brushing hers. "I remember being twelve and you daring me to drink the scrapings straight from the press. I remember you crying when the first frost took the tomatoes. I remember—" He stopped. The water ran.

"You remember what?"

"I remember the summer I left." He said it quietly, to the sink. "I think about it every time I'm in this room."

The towel went still in Hazel's hands. The old reflex rose — the flinch, the wall, fifteen years of practiced armor sliding into place — and then, unexpectedly, didn't. Because the kitchen was warm, and his shoulder was six inches from hers, and the truth was she'd been thinking about that summer too, lately, turning it over like a stone with something glinting underneath.

"Jonah—"

But the screen door banged. Dolores Marsh's voice, bright and breathless: "Hazel? Sorry to interrupt — I was driving past and saw lights — oh, good, you're both here." She came in waving an envelope like a telegram. "Mail came to the society office by mistake, addressed to the orchard. Official-looking. I assumed it was festival permits, but—" she peered at the front, and her voice shifted, cooled, "—actually, it's from a law firm. Hale Development Group. Addressed to *both* of you."

Jonah shut off the water.

Hazel took the envelope. It was heavy, cream-colored, expensive paper — the kind of stationery designed to make you feel smaller before you opened it. Through the window behind Dolores, the string lights in Block Two burned on in the dark, patient and golden, the most beautiful thing the orchard had worn in a hundred years.

"Well," Hazel said, and heard her own voice go flat and steady, the trained-muscle voice. "Open season, apparently."

She slit the envelope with a paring knife from the drawer — her grandmother's drawer, her grandmother's knife — and read the first line, and felt the warm kitchen begin, very slowly, to grow cold.
