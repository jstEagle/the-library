## 25. What You Did

The tin box sat on the passenger seat of Hazel's truck like a small, patient animal.

She had driven back from the orchard with the windows down even though it was October and cold enough to make her knuckles ache on the wheel, because she could still smell smoke — not real smoke, she knew that, but the ghost of it, the way a room holds a perfume long after the person wearing it has gone. The matches were in her jacket pocket now. Three of them, red-tipped, swollen with fifteen years of damp and dark, rattling against each other with every step as she crossed the motel parking lot.

Jonah's room was the last one on the end, the one facing the orchard road. Of course it was. She had noticed that weeks ago and told herself it meant nothing, that he'd simply been given whatever room was free, that there was no poetry in a motel clerk's shuffle of keys. But standing under the flickering vacancy sign now, watching the light behind his curtain — he was awake, then; it was nearly eleven — she felt the old certainty rise in her again, the one from when they were seventeen and she could read his moods through walls: *he is thinking about me.*

Or he was thinking about the harvest. Or the deed. Or Marcus Hale's lawyers. There had been a time when she would have trusted the first answer without question. That time had cost her everything.

She knocked before she could talk herself out of it.

Footsteps. The chain. The door opened six inches and Jonah looked out at her, and whatever he saw in her face made him go very still — the stillness of a man who has heard a sound in the night and is deciding whether to reach for a light.

"Hazel."

"I found something today," she said. "In the barn foundation. Under the storm-fall." She held up the tin box so the porch light caught its dented lid, the faded picture of a sailboat she'd once loved because it wasn't an apple. "It's mine. It was mine. I hid it under the floorboards when I was nine because I thought nobody would ever look under there, and nobody ever did, until the lightning brought the tree down and you started clearing branches like a man who can't leave a mess alone."

He didn't move to take it. He didn't move at all, except for his eyes, which went from the box to her face and stayed there.

"Can I come in," she said. It wasn't quite a question. She was already stepping past him, into the small room that smelled of cheap coffee and the cedar blocks he'd always kept in his duffel, and she heard him close the door behind her with the care of someone closing a door on weather.

* * *

The room was exactly what a motel room should be: a bed made too tightly, a chair with a woven seat, a lamp that buzzed faintly like an insect trapped in glass. Jonah stood by the window with his arms folded, and Hazel set the tin box on the little table between them and sat down across from it, and for a long moment neither of them spoke.

"You opened it," she said finally.

"Yes."

"Tell me what was inside."

His jaw worked. "Matches. A ribbon. A photograph of—" He stopped.

"A photograph of us," Hazel said. "At the cider press. I'm holding a cup and you're making a face because I dared you to drink it straight. I remember taking that picture. I remember the ribbon too — it was off a pie box from my mother's bakery, the year before she stopped baking altogether." She turned the box over with one finger, slowly, like turning a card she already knew. "I don't remember putting those things in there. Isn't that strange? I remember being nine and hiding the box, and I remember everything after that except the part where I filled it. It's like a book with pages torn out, and the tear is clean, and you'd never know unless you knew."

"Hazel—"

"Tell me about the fire."

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere down the row of rooms a television laughed at itself, canned and distant. Jonah unfolded his arms and refolded them, and she watched him do the arithmetic she had watched him do all season — how much truth, how much shelter, how much of her could bear how much of this — and something in her went cold and hard and clear, colder than it had ever been, because she understood suddenly that he had done this arithmetic before. Many times. For years.

"The night of the bonfire," he said quietly. "August. You were nineteen. So was I."

"I know what August it was, Jonah. I've carried that August for fifteen years. I was there when you left. I stood in your driveway while you put your bag in your father's truck and you wouldn't even look at me, and the next morning my father told me you'd gone, and that some people are like that, and that I should be grateful to find out early." Her voice was steady. She was surprised by how steady it was. Underneath the steadiness, something vast was moving, the way water moves under ice. "So tell me about the fire. Because I've spent fifteen years believing I know why you left, and yesterday I found three matches in a box I hid when I was a child, and I smelled smoke in a foundation that's been cold since before I was born, and I am *done*, Jonah. Do you hear me? I am done being the last person to know the story of my own life."

He came away from the window then. He pulled the other chair around, not quite opposite her, angled the way people sit when they want to be close but not cornering, and he sat down with his forearms on his knees and his hands hanging loose between them, and he looked at the tin box instead of at her.

"There was no bonfire," he said. "That's the first thing. Everyone remembers a bonfire because there was supposed to be one — the end-of-summer thing, the whole town coming up the hill. But it rained that afternoon. Hard, for an hour, and your father called it off, and everyone went home, and the orchard emptied out." He paused. "Except us."

Hazel felt the rain of that afternoon land on her shoulders as if it had never stopped falling. She said nothing.

"We were in the east barn. You wanted to show me something — you were always showing me something, you had this whole—" His mouth twisted, almost a smile, gone immediately. "You had a plan for everything. The festival, the café you were going to open someday, the grafts you wanted to try on the north slope. You had a notebook. You used to read it to me like scripture." He took a breath. "There was a lantern. An oil lantern, the old kind, from the loft. We weren't supposed to have it lit — your father had a rule about open flame in the barns after the dry summer — but it was dark in there with the storm clouds, and you were reading to me, and we weren't doing anything wrong, we were just—" He stopped again. Started over, plainer. "The lantern fell. Or it got knocked. I've gone over it ten thousand times and I honestly don't know which. There was a gust through the door, or my elbow, or — it doesn't matter. The hay caught. It went up faster than anything I've ever seen, before or since. Dry August hay and a draft and it was a wall in about ninety seconds."

The cold thing inside Hazel turned over. "That's not what happened," she heard herself say. "There was no fire. My father never—" But even as she said it, she felt the sentence hollow out from the inside, because her father *had* — she remembered the word itself arriving, the hush of it, the way the town said *fire* that autumn in lowered voices, the black skeleton of the east barn standing all winter like a rib cage. She had known there was a fire. She had simply never once, in fifteen years, asked herself where she had been during it.

"Get out," she said, before she could stop herself, and then, because that was absurd, they were in his motel room, "No. Keep going. Finish it."

Jonah's hands had gone white where they gripped his knees. "The fire department came from two towns over. By the time they got there the roof was gone. And your father—" Here his voice changed, flattened, became careful in the way of a man walking on river ice. "Your father came to our house the next morning. Before sunrise. I know because I hadn't slept, and I heard his truck, and I went down, and he was sitting at our kitchen table with my father, and he had papers. Photographs. From the insurance investigator, he said, though I never believed there was an investigator, I think the photographs were his own, taken that night." Jonah finally lifted his eyes to hers. "They were photographs of the lantern. Of where it fell. On your side of the loft ladder, Hazel. Your reading spot. The place only you sat."

"No," Hazel said.

"He told my father he could have me charged. Arson. I was nineteen, I had a juvenile record from the spring — the stupid stuff, the mailbox, you remember — and he said a jury in this county would take one look at a Voss boy with a record standing in a Bellamy barn and it would be over before lunch. He said the only alternative was for me to leave. That night. Permanently. And if I ever came back, if I ever contacted you, he'd reopen it. He'd file the charges. He'd make sure I burned for it, one way or the other." Jonah's voice cracked on the last words and he let it crack; he didn't try to smooth it over. "And my father — God rest him, and God forgive him — my father looked at me across that table and said, *Son, sometimes you carry a thing so someone else doesn't have to.* And I thought he meant the Voss name. I didn't understand until years later that he meant *you.*"

"Stop." Hazel was on her feet. She didn't remember standing. "Stop it. Stop talking."

"Hazel—"

"This is a story," she said, and her voice came out strange, high, thin, a stranger's voice. "This is a story you've built. Fifteen years, and you come back with survey maps and a buyer's offer, and now there's a fire and my father with photographs and you, the noble exile, carrying my sins up a hill like — like some kind of—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, hard, as if she could hold her ribs shut. "I was *there*, Jonah. I remember that summer. I remember the rain. I remember—" But the memory she reached for slipped sideways as she grabbed it, the way a dream does in the first seconds of waking. She remembered the rain ending. She remembered the smell of wet dust on the orchard road. She remembered walking somewhere, or being walked home, and her father's kitchen light, and — nothing. A soft gray blank where the middle of the night should have been, smooth as poured concrete, and she had lived her whole life on top of it without once noticing the ground beneath was missing.

Because who questions the ground?

"You're lying," she whispered. "You're lying to make yourself the hero of it. You left because you wanted to leave. Because I was a small-town girl with a notebook full of plans and you couldn't wait to get out, and you didn't even have the decency to tell me goodbye, and you have spent fifteen years letting me believe I wasn't worth a stamp, a phone call, a single postcard — and now that there's money in the ground and a developer circling, now you need me to think well of you, so you invent this. This *sacrifice*. You take the worst thing you ever did to me and you turn it into the best thing anyone ever did, and you expect me to thank you."

Every word landed on him. She watched them land. She watched him take each one without flinching, without defending himself, his hands loose and open on his knees like a man who had decided long ago that whatever she needed to throw, he would stand there and be hit — and that, more than anything, more than the photographs and the lantern and the gray blank in her memory, that frightened her. Because guilty men flinch. Guilty men argue. Only men telling the truth stand still.

"Say something," she demanded.

"What do you want me to say?"

"Say you're lying. Say you made it up. Say the lantern was yours, say you knocked it, say any version of this where it isn't—" Her voice broke and she hated it, hated the crack running through her steadiness like a fault line through porcelain. "Where it isn't *me*, Jonah. Because if it's me, then my father didn't protect me, he *used* me. He threw a nineteen-year-old boy off his land to hide what his own daughter did, and he let me hate you for fifteen years, he fed me that hatred at Sunday dinners, he called you *that Voss boy* like you were a stain — and I ate all of it, I built my whole life on it, I made *hating you* into a load-bearing wall—" She was crying now, furiously, without permission, tears she hadn't cried even over the bank letters, even over the burnt madeleines, and she wiped at her face with the back of her hand like a child. "And if it's me, then the fire is mine. The barn. The trees that burned. Old Man Hollis's bees that swarmed off the hill because the smoke reached them — I read about that, I read about it in the paper and I *felt bad about it*, I felt bad the way you feel bad about weather, and it was *me*—"

"Hazel." He rose slowly, hands still open. "Listen to me. You were nineteen. It was an accident. A lantern, a gust, ninety seconds — there's no villain in that barn except the wind. What your father did afterward, that's on him. What I did — leaving without a word, staying gone, letting you carry the hating because the hating was easier for you than the grieving — that's on me, and I'll answer for it as long as you want me to. But the fire—" He shook his head. "The fire was nobody. The fire was just fire."

"Don't comfort me." She backed toward the door. "Don't you dare stand there and comfort me. You don't get to be gentle. Not after — you don't get to—"

Her hand found the knob behind her. The chain was off; he must have unhooked it when he let her in, or maybe he'd never hooked it, maybe some part of him had been waiting all evening for exactly this knock. The door swung and the October air came in cold and clean and smelling of apples and distance, and she stepped through it backwards, keeping her eyes on him because turning around felt like surrender.

"I don't believe you," she said. "Do you understand me? Whatever this is, whatever you think you're doing — I don't believe you. I was there. I would *know*. If I had done a thing like that, I would know."

Even as she said it, the matches in her pocket shifted against her hip, three small bones knocking together, and she saw his eyes flick down to the sound and then away, and she saw him decide — visibly, deliberately, the way he'd decided everything all season — not to say what he was thinking.

"Goodnight, Hazel," he said softly. "The door's never locked. You know where I am."

She walked. Across the parking lot, past the ice machine humming its one note, to the truck, and she got in, and she did not start the engine. She sat in the dark with her breath fogging the windshield and her hands shaking on the wheel, and she took the matches out of her pocket and held them in her palm under the parking lot light, and they were just matches. Child's matches, souvenir matches, the kind that came free with a box of kitchen candles. They proved nothing. They proved everything. They were the most terrible object she had ever held and she could not make herself put them down.

*I would know,* she had told him. *If I had done a thing like that, I would know.*

But she hadn't known about the box. She hadn't known about the torn pages, the clean tear, the ground beneath the ground. And a voice that sounded terribly like Priya, and underneath Priya another voice that sounded terribly like her grandmother, said quietly, in the dark of the truck: *How would you know, Hazel? How would you know, when you've spent your whole life training your heart to hold only what it can carry?*

She drove home with the windows up. She did not cry again. She washed her face, she checked the locks on the café door twice, she lay down in her narrow bed above the kitchen and stared at the ceiling where the streetlight made its familiar pale coin, and she waited for sleep the way you wait for a verdict.

It did not come. Instead, unbidden, into the gray space between waking and dreaming, came the smell of that afternoon — wet dust, cut hay, the sweet rot of fallen apples — and beneath it, faint and growing, unmistakable, the smell of smoke.

She had been loyal to her memory for fifteen years.

Lying in the dark, wide-eyed, Hazel began — carefully, the way you probe a sore tooth — to wonder what else it had lied to her about.
