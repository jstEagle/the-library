## 37. Festival Night

### Hazel

The lanterns went up at golden hour, and the orchard turned into something out of a story she would have been too embarrassed to write.

Hazel stood in the middle of Row Twelve with a roll of twine over her shoulder and watched the light happen. Priya and three teenagers from the high school had spent the afternoon stringing paper lanterns along the guide wires between trees — amber, cream, one defiant teal that Priya refused to take down on the grounds that *aesthetic monotony is cowardice* — and now the sun was dropping behind Block Four and the whole orchard was catching fire gently, row by row, like the trees themselves had decided to bloom out of season.

A hundred years ago, she thought, someone had stood in these same rows doing this same thing. The centennial committee had found photographs in the archive: lanterns strung low, women in white cotton, men with their sleeves rolled, apples piled in bushel baskets painted with the year. 1925. The faces were grainy and solemn, because nobody smiled for cameras then, but you could see it anyway — the pride of it. *We made this. We grew this. Come see.*

She had volunteered the orchard six weeks ago with her heart hammering like a shoplifter's, certain the town would say no, or worse, say yes and then watch everything collapse. Instead they had come. They had come in droves. Mrs. Okonkwo from the library had organized a heritage table under the biggest Baldwin tree. The FFA kids were running a cider-pressing demonstration with Jonah's old press, which he'd rebuilt in four days with parts he'd driven two counties to find. Someone's cousin's bluegrass band was tuning up on a flatbed near the barn foundation — the new barn, the one that had replaced the burned one before Hazel was born, its boards silvered soft as felt.

And her pastry stall — her little striped awning and folding tables borrowed from the church basement — had sold out at seven fifty-eight.

"Sold out," Priya said again, appearing at her elbow with the empty cash box held up like a trophy. "Do you understand what happened here? You sold out. Of everything. Including the things I told you we didn't have enough of."

"I heard you the first nine times."

"Because it bears repeating." Priya set the box down and looked at her, and Hazel braced herself, because Priya had a look she used when she was about to say something true, and true things still landed on Hazel like weather. But all Priya said was: "Go. Walk around your own festival. Eat something that isn't a rejected éclair. That's an order from your sous-chef, which is a real position with real authority that you gave me in writing."

"You made me write it during a flour shortage."

"Desperate times." Priya flapped a dish towel at her. "Go find the cider man. He's been looking at you all evening like a man who keeps forgetting to breathe."

"He has not."

"Hazel. I have eyes. I have *training*. Go."

Hazel went — mostly to escape the towel — drifting down Row Twelve with the lanterns swinging overhead and the smell of woodsmoke and cut grass and warm sugar everywhere, and it struck her, somewhere between the pie contest table (Mrs. Okonkwo's latticed apple taking first by acclamation) and the bridge, that she could not remember the last time she had stood in a crowd and wanted nothing from it. Not approval. Not rescue. Not even the small daily mercy of being left alone. She just wanted to be in it, this swarm of her town, eating kettle corn under her grandmother's trees, and the wanting-nothing felt so unfamiliar she kept checking herself for symptoms.

The bridge came up on her left before she decided to walk toward it.

It was only a footbridge, really — planked, hand-railed, sagging slightly toward the creek that divided the orchard from the far pasture. Her grandfather had built it the summer her mother turned ten; Astrid Bellamy had painted it the green of new leaves and repainted it every few years until her hands couldn't hold a brush, and the last coat, faded and peeling now, was still hers. Hazel had crossed it ten thousand times. She'd sat on its rail at nineteen with her heart in pieces, and at twenty-five, and at thirty-one, each time telling herself she was over it, each time measuring the wound like a child measuring a height mark on a doorframe — *am I bigger yet? am I past it?*

Tonight the lanterns reached even here, two strings sagging across the water, and the creek ran dark and quick beneath them, carrying broken coins of light.

Jonah was already on it.

He was leaning on the rail with his sleeves rolled, forearms dusted with dried cider must, watching the current, and the sight of him did the thing it always did now — not the old lurch, not the drop, but something quieter and more frightening: a settling. Like a house at night making the sound of itself becoming home.

He heard her boots on the planks and turned, and smiled, and the smile was not careful anymore. That was the difference. Six weeks ago every expression he wore had been load-bearing, holding something up. Now he just looked glad, plainly, the way you'd look at rain after a dry spell.

"Sold out," he said.

"So I'm told. Repeatedly."

"Cider's gone too. Nine o'clock, on the nose. Forty gallons." He shook his head like a man recounting a miracle he didn't believe in. "Mr. Aldous bought six cups and told anyone who'd listen that it tasted like 1974. I don't know if that's a compliment. He said it very emotionally."

"It's a compliment. Everything about 1974 is a compliment, coming from Mr. Aldous."

They stood shoulder to shoulder at the rail, not quite touching, and below them the creek talked to itself, and somewhere behind them the band swung into something slow, a waltz worn smooth by a hundred years of county dances. Fireflies had come up out of the pasture grass in loose constellations. A child shrieked happily near the pie table. The lanterns breathed in the breeze.

"The hearing's Thursday," Jonah said quietly.

"I know."

"And Hale's attorney filed his response today. Denying everything. Calling the wall letters 'unverified personal correspondence.'"

"They're his own father's letters."

"They're *inconvenient* is what they are." Jonah's jaw worked. "Thursday could go either way. I need you to know I know that. I'm not going to stand here and promise you the board does the right thing, because I've watched that board—"

"Jonah." She put her hand on his arm — the first time tonight — and felt him go still under her palm, the whole man pausing like a held breath. "Thursday can wait until Thursday. Tonight is the hundredth anniversary of this orchard, and my stall sold out, and your cider made an old man cry about 1974, and I am standing on my grandmother's bridge with you, and I have spent fifteen years learning how to be unhappy efficiently. I'm taking the night off."

He looked at her for a long moment. The lantern light moved on his face.

"Taking the night off," he repeated, like a phrase in a language he was relearning.

"That's right. It's in my contract. Priya wrote it."

"She sounds thorough."

"Terrifyingly."

He laughed — low, surprised out of him — and the sound went through her like warmth through cold dough, rising her up from places she'd thought were packed flat forever. And then neither of them said anything, because there was nothing structural left to say, and the band played, and the creek ran, and the fireflies stitched their slow bright seams through the dark above the water, and Hazel Hartwell stood on a bridge with the boy who had carried her worst secret out of town so she could grow up unburdened, and let herself lean, just slightly, until her shoulder rested against his arm.

He didn't move away. He leaned back.

* * *

### Jonah

Later — after the band's last set, after Mrs. Okonkwo pressed a slice of second-place peach pie on him with the imperiousness of a woman who had decided he belonged to the town now and would brook no appeal — Jonah walked the rows alone while Hazel helped Priya break down the stall.

He hadn't meant to be alone. He'd meant to help. But she'd waved him off with flour still on her cheekbone and said, *Go, you've done your shift, I'll find you,* and the words *I'll find you* had gone through him with a force entirely disproportionate to their size, so he'd gone, and now he was walking Block Two in the lantern-dark with his heart doing something young and stupid in his chest.

The trees were heavy. First real crop since the storm year, and the hail-scarred limbs of Block Four had healed over in hard ridges like knuckles, and the fruit hanging there was the sweetest in the orchard — everyone said so, the stressed trees always made the best cider, his father used to swear by it. His father. Whose letters, pulled from the farmhouse walls in a cloud of plaster dust, were sitting in a folder on the kitchen table waiting for Thursday, proof that the water rights had always run shared, that the forgery had been exactly what it looked like, that the Voss name — his name — had been dragged through the mud of it by a man who wasn't even alive to answer for it.

He stopped under the oldest Baldwin. The lightning-split one, the one they'd almost lost in the storm, propped now with steel cables and stubbornness. Half of it still bore fruit. Trees were like that, he was learning. They broke and kept going. Nobody taught them shame about the scar tissue; they just grew around it and ripened anyway.

He heard her boots on the grass before he saw her.

She came down the row with her hair coming loose from its knot and a lantern glow behind her and she stopped when she saw where he was standing, and something passed over her face — not pain, exactly. Recognition. This was the tree they'd sheltered under during the hail, soaked and laughing, the almost-kiss the lightning had interrupted. They both remembered it. It lived between them now like a third person.

"Everyone's gone," she said. "Priya took the van. Mrs. Okonkwo tried to adopt you, I hear."

"She got my address. I feel like I've been processed."

"You have. Welcome to Maple Falls. There's no appeals process." She came closer, stopped an arm's length away. Above them the split crown of the Baldwin creaked softly against its cables, and the last lanterns swayed, and the orchard was so quiet he could hear the creek from here, and his own pulse, loud and unreasonable.

"Hazel," he said.

"I know," she said. "I know what you're going to say. Or — I don't, actually. That's the thing. Fifteen years ago I knew everything about you, and then I knew nothing, and now I know the important things again but I've lost all the small ones. I don't know if you still hate cilantro. I don't know what you do on Sunday mornings. I don't—" She stopped, pressed her lips together, started over, quieter. "I don't want to guess anymore. That's all. I'm tired of guessing."

"I don't hate cilantro anymore," he said. "I grew out of it. Sunday mornings I walk the blocks. All forty. It takes two hours. I started doing it the first spring after I came back and I haven't missed one." He swallowed. "I used to do it before, too. Everywhere I lived. Denver, Spokane. Wherever there were trees. People probably thought I was surveying."

"Were you?"

"No." The truth came up easy now, the way it had begun to lately, like water finding a channel long dug. "I was homesick. For thirty years I was homesick and I called it ambition. And then I came back and stood in these rows and realized the whole time, the entire time, some part of me was walking toward this. Toward—" He gestured, helpless, at the trees, the lanterns, her. "Toward the place. Toward you. Both. I don't know where one ends."

The fireflies drifted between them. Somewhere far off, a dog barked twice and gave up.

"Hazel," he said again, because her name was the truest word he owned. "I'm not going to make a speech. I've written enough speeches in letters I burned. I'm just going to ask, plainly, the way I should have asked when we were nineteen and I was too much of a coward to ask anything. Can I kiss you? Here? No lightning required?"

Her breath caught — he saw it, the small bright hitch of it — and then she laughed, wet-eyed, shaking her head at him.

"You waited fifteen years," she said, "to ask permission."

"I'm told the heart is trainable muscle."

"Oh, *shut up*—"

And she crossed the distance herself, fists in his shirt, and kissed him.

It was not the almost-kiss from the storm. That one had been adrenaline and rain and the world ending pleasantly. This was slower. This was a man and a woman standing in a century-old orchard with lantern light on their closed eyelids, kissing like people who had finally stopped bracing. Her mouth tasted faintly of salt and sugar and the cider she'd been sneaking all evening, and her hands slid up to his jaw, and he made a sound against her lips that he would deny under oath, and the oldest Baldwin creaked overhead like it approved, and the creek kept talking, and the fireflies rose, and nothing split, nothing interrupted, nothing ended.

When they broke apart she stayed close, forehead against his, breathing.

"Well," she managed.

"Yeah," he agreed.

"That was—" She huffed a laugh. "Fifteen years. I had built it up. In my head. I want you to know I had built it up *significantly*, and somehow it still—"

"Overshot?"

"*Exceeded projections.*"

He laughed and kissed her again, briefly, because he could, because the muscle memory of it was already writing itself into him, and when they parted this time she tucked herself under his arm and they stood facing the dark rows together, her head on his shoulder, his chin resting in her hair, the way they might have stood at nineteen if the world had been kinder, or the way they were standing now, at thirty-four, which was better, because now they knew exactly what it had cost and chose it anyway.

"Thursday," she said at last, drowsy against him.

"Thursday," he agreed. "Whatever happens, we walk in together."

"Together," she said, like she was testing the weight of it. Finding it good.

Above them, in the highest fork of the old Baldwin, where the lightning scar met the living wood, something glinted — a scrap of paper wedged deep in the crevice, fluttering once in the night wind, pale as a moth. Neither of them saw it. The orchard kept its secrets a little longer, patient as trees, holding one last postcard in its branches like a hand held out across a hundred years.
