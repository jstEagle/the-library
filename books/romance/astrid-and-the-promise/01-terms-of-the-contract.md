## 1. Terms of the Contract

The tasting room at Vane Vineyards smelled of oak barrels and old money, and Odile Crane hated it on sight.

Not the vineyard itself—she could admit, standing at the tall windows with a clipboard she wasn't reading, that the place was beautiful. The hills rolled gold and green into a haze of September heat. The estate house sat on its rise like something painted by a man who'd never known hunger. But beautiful venues were just beautiful problems wearing better clothes, and Odile had spent eleven years learning to see the problems first.

She clicked her pen. Clicked it again.

"Ms. Crane?" A young woman in a linen apron hovered at her elbow. "Mrs. Vane-the-elder wanted to know if you'd confirmed the string quartet for Saturday."

"The quartet is confirmed. The florist arrives Thursday at six. The tent goes up Friday morning, weather permitting, and if the weather doesn't permit, I have a contingency plan that involves a barn and four hundred candles." Odile smiled the smile she kept for clients' staff—the one that said *I am competent and pleasant and please stop talking to me.* "Tell Mrs. Vane everything is handled."

The girl fled. Odile turned back to the window and let the smile drop off her face like a mask slipping its hook.

Everything was not handled. Everything was barely holding. Her lead florist had quit by text on Tuesday. Her sister hadn't answered a phone call in three weeks. And Odile's bank account contained exactly enough money to cover October's rent if she ate nothing but rice and didn't drive anywhere, which was a problem, because this wedding was forty miles from her apartment and she'd already burned through half a tank getting here.

One last job. That was what she'd told herself when Marguerite Vane's assistant called—a society wedding for the vineyard heiress, big budget, bigger family, the kind of commission that could float her through the winter while she decided whether she ever wanted to look at another bridal bouquet again. She'd taken it because she had to. She'd told herself the cynicism was temporary, a coat she could hang by the door when the season ended.

But coats like that don't come off. They grow into the skin.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. She checked it out of habit, out of the same reflexive hope she'd been checking it with for three weeks, and felt the familiar drop when she saw the name: *Priya.*

*Just checking you're alive. Call me back or I'm driving up there.*

Odile typed *Alive. Busy.* and put the phone away before Priya could weaponize punctuation at her. Her sister's silence was worse than anger. Anger would mean Camille was feeling something again. Silence meant the thing Odile had watched happen at the altar—that long, terrible pause while three hundred guests waited and the groom simply did not appear—had settled into her sister like sediment.

*You should have known,* Camille had screamed at her, once, in the wreckage afterward. *You plan weddings. You knew him. You knew something was wrong and you said nothing because the client was paying.*

And the worst part, the part Odile carried around like a stone sewn into her coat lining, was that Camille was right. She *had* known. A flicker here, a hesitation there—small things, deniable things—and she had filed them away under *not my problem* because Daniel Whitmore's family was writing a very large check and Odile Crane did not bite the hand.

She'd stopped believing in weddings somewhere around year six, she realized now. She'd just kept building them, gorgeous and hollow, like a jeweler making paste diamonds and calling them real.

"Ms. Crane."

The voice came from behind her, low and unhurried, and Odile turned to find a stranger watching her from the doorway of the tasting room.

Tall. Dark hair pushed back like she'd been running her hands through it all day and had given up fighting it. A charcoal blazer over a white shirt with the sleeves rolled, and wine-dark eyes that took Odile in with an assessment so frank it bordered on rude. There was a tiredness under the polish—not the surface tiredness of a bad night, but the deep-set kind, structural, load-bearing.

"You're the wedding planner," the woman said. Not a question.

"I'm the woman keeping this wedding from collapsing into chaos, yes."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. "Astrid Vane."

Of course. The bride. Odile had seen photographs in the planning file—younger, blonder, smiling at some charity gala—but the photographs hadn't prepared her for the way Astrid Vane looked at people, as though she were reading fine print.

"Ms. Vane. Congratulations." Odile extended her hand. "Your grandmother has very strong opinions about peonies."

"My grandmother has strong opinions about oxygen." Astrid shook her hand. Her grip was cool and brief. "I need to talk to you. Privately."

"This is privately. Everyone else is at the rehearsal dinner venue arguing about chair covers."

"Not privately enough." Astrid glanced toward the open doors, then stepped closer, lowering her voice. "Is there somewhere we can go? Ten minutes."

Every instinct Odile had honed over eleven years of managing other people's disasters whispered that this was irregular. Brides did not pull their planners aside two days before the wedding and ask for private conversations unless something was wrong—money, cold feet, a family scandal, a lover in the wings. And whatever it was, it would become Odile's problem. It always became her problem.

But she was tired, and curious, and the combination had always been dangerous for her.

"The cellar," Odile said. "It's climate-controlled and no one goes down there until Friday."

* * *

The cellar was long and dim and cold, rows of barrels receding into shadow like sleeping animals. Someone had strung work lights along the ceiling for the rehearsal dinner setup, and they cast a warm amber glow that made everything look staged—which, Odile supposed, was fitting.

Astrid walked the length of the aisle between barrels, trailing her fingers along the oak, and didn't speak until they reached the far wall, where a single window slit let in a blade of evening light.

"Do you know why my grandmother is throwing this wedding?" she asked.

"Because you're getting married?"

"I'm not getting married." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you'd correct someone on a date. "There is no groom. There hasn't been a groom since March, when Marcus decided he preferred his business partner to me, in every sense of the word."

Odile kept her face still. Eleven years of practice. Inside, something cold turned over. *Here it comes.*

"The wedding is real," Astrid continued. "The invitations are real. Two hundred guests, most of them family, arriving tomorrow for what my grandmother is calling a reunion weekend, except the reunion is a battlefield and the wedding is—" She exhaled. "My grandmother needed a reason to gather everyone. A celebration. Something to hold the family together while she decides how to divide the estate. So I agreed to be the bride. And then Marcus left, and I needed—"

"A groom," Odile said.

"A date," Astrid corrected. "A smaller ask. Someone on my arm who makes the weekend survivable. Someone who can handle my grandmother's interrogations, my half-brother's contempt, and forty-eight hours of questions about why Astrid Vane is finally settling down." She turned to face Odile fully, and in the amber light her exhaustion was unmistakable now, stripped of polish. "Someone professional."

"No."

"I haven't made the offer yet."

"You're about to ask me to pretend to be your girlfriend at a wedding I'm coordinating. The answer is no. It's a conflict of interest, it's unprofessional, and I don't do theater."

"It's five thousand dollars."

The number landed in the cellar like a dropped glass. Odile stood very still, letting it shatter, letting herself count the pieces. Five thousand dollars was October's rent and November's rent and a tank of gas and a phone call to Camille that started with *I'm sorry* and maybe, finally, got past it.

"That's an obscene amount of money for a date," Odile said carefully.

"It's an obscene amount of money for what I'm actually asking, which isn't a date. It's a performance. Three days. You'll be excellent at it—you're already performing right now. The competence, the clipped sentences, the smile you gave the kitchen girl that didn't reach your eyes. You've been doing it since I walked in." Astrid's voice wasn't cruel. It was precise, the way a surgeon is precise, and somehow that was worse. "I need someone who can lie beautifully. From everything I've heard about you, Ms. Crane, lying beautifully is your profession."

The stone in Odile's coat lining grew heavier. *You knew something was wrong and you said nothing.*

"And when the weekend ends?" she asked. "When your family scatters and the guests go home? What happens to the fiction?"

"Then it ends. We shake hands. You disappear back into your life and I disappear into mine, and neither of us ever has to explain anything to anyone." Astrid held out her hand. "Terms of the contract. Cash, half upfront. No romantic expectations. No emotional entanglements. You play the woman I've been quietly seeing for four months, and I make sure my grandmother doesn't gut this family like a fish in front of two hundred witnesses."

Odile looked at the offered hand. Long fingers, a faint ink stain on the side of the index finger—someone who still wrote things by hand. A tremor in the wrist that Astrid was controlling, mostly, the way you control a fire by sitting very close to it.

Two people at the end of their ropes, Odile thought. That was all this was. Two women dangling from fraying cables, each mistaking the other for solid ground.

She should say no. Every professional instinct, every scar left by Daniel Whitmore and her sister's ruined face, said *no.* You don't build lies inside weddings. You especially don't move into them, sleep under the same roof, wear them like a dress for three days. Lies in weddings metastasize. She'd watched it happen. She'd *caused* it to happen.

"Half upfront," Odile said.

Astrid blinked. "You—"

"Cash. Half tonight, half Sunday when the last guest leaves. I keep my planner role—I am not abandoning a live event to play house with you. Publicly, we met through the wedding preparations. I'll craft the timeline myself; I know how families interrogate a story, and yours will be seamless." Odile took the hand. Cool fingers closed around hers, and that controlled tremor passed between them like a current. "And one more term, Ms. Vane. If anyone asks me directly whether it's real, I won't confirm it. I deflect. I redirect. I charm. But I don't look your grandmother in the eye and lie. I've done enough of that for one career."

For a moment something moved behind Astrid's eyes—surprise, maybe, or the flicker of a person recognizing another person's damage and deciding not to mention it.

"Deflecting," Astrid said. "That's the whole job description of my family. You'll fit right in."

"Where do you want to have met? Four months ago, you said."

"The harvest festival in town. You were there for work, I was there avoiding mine. We argued about champagne." Astrid was already building it, quick and fluid, and Odile understood suddenly that she'd rehearsed this—that the offer had been composed long before tonight, waiting for the right moment, the right desperate freelancer. "You thought the sparkling was overpriced. I agreed, which offended me, because it's my family's overpriced sparkling."

"Fine. I'll flesh it out tonight and send you the details to memorize." Odile released her hand and reached for her clipboard, needing the weight of it, the ordinary rectangle of her actual life. "Rehearsal dinner seating chart is due to your grandmother by ten a.m. I assume you want to be seated next to me."

"I assumed you'd insist on it."

"I insist on controlling variables." Odile turned toward the stairs, then paused, the question leaving her before she could stop it—the first crack in her own professionalism all week. "Why me? You could have hired anyone. An actress. A friend. Why pay a stranger five thousand dollars to stand next to you?"

Astrid looked at her across the amber-lit dark, and for a moment the fine-print reader was gone, and what remained was just a tired woman in a cold cellar, holding a lie out like a life raft.

"Because a stranger is the only person I can trust," she said. "Everyone who loves me wants something from my family. You only want the money. It's the cleanest transaction I've been offered in years."

She walked past Odile toward the stairs, her heels clicking on stone, and paused at the bottom step without turning around.

"Dinner with my grandmother is at eight tomorrow. Wear something that says *serious intentions.* She'll have you investigated by nine."

Her footsteps faded up into the light, and Odile stood alone among the barrels with five thousand dollars half-promised and a stone in her chest, telling herself the cold feeling settling over her was just the cellar air.

It was not the cellar air. It was the particular chill of a story beginning—one whose ending, eleven years of experience should have taught her, she would not be able to control.
