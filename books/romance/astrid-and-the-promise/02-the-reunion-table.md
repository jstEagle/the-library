## 2. The Reunion Table

The Vane estate announced itself the way it always had—too loudly. Astrid felt it in her chest as the car crested the last hill and the house came into view: limestone walls gone honey-gold in the late afternoon, the cypress rows standing like sentries along the drive, the vineyards falling away behind everything in green waves toward the river. Her father's kingdom. Her father's grave, almost, given how much of him was buried in that soil.

Beside her, Odile Crane looked out the window with the expression of a woman appraising a venue.

"Good sightlines," she said. "Terrible parking situation."

"That's what you have to say? We're about to walk into a war zone and you're critiquing the parking?"

"I'm a professional." Odile turned to look at her, and Astrid caught again that quality she'd noticed at their first meeting—the flat, assessing calm of someone who had seen every kind of disaster a wedding could produce and no longer found any of them interesting. "Wars are just logistics with worse lighting. Sit up straight. Hold my hand when we get out. Not before—we want it to look spontaneous."

Astrid stared at her. "You've rehearsed this."

"I rehearse everything. It's why I'm expensive." A pause, and then something flickered across Odile's face—almost humor, quickly suppressed. "Your hand is going to be sweaty. That's fine. Sweaty hands read as nerves, and nerves read as love. Nobody questions a nervous lover."

"That's the most cynical thing I've ever heard."

"Thank you," Odile said, and got out of the car.

* * *

Her grandmother received them on the terrace, because Marguerite Vane did not receive people; she held court and permitted them to approach. She was eighty-one years old and dressed as though the estate might be sold out from under her at any moment and she intended to be photographed for the obituary looking magnificent. Her eyes went to Odile first—the stranger, the anomaly—and stayed there for three full seconds while Astrid's stomach performed slow rotations.

"So," Marguerite said. "You brought someone."

"Grandmother, this is Odile Crane. Odile—my grandmother, Marguerite Vane."

"Madame Vane." Odile extended her hand, and Astrid watched, fascinated, as her grandmother—who had once made a sommelier cry at Christmas—took it. "Astrid speaks of you often. She says you're the only person in this family who terrifies her, which I understand to be a compliment."

Marguerite's eyebrows rose a fraction of a millimeter. In Vane terms, this was open delight. "And what do you do, Odile Crane, that gives you such a firm handshake?"

"I plan weddings."

A silence. Somewhere below the terrace, a tractor coughed to life among the vines.

"How fitting," Marguerite said dryly. "Given the occasion. Come inside. The family is already circling."

* * *

The drawing room smelled of beeswax and old roses, and it was full of exactly the people Astrid had spent six hundred miles dreading. Her aunt Camille, who asked questions like an auditor. Two cousins whose names Astrid always had to reconstruct from context. And Julien.

He stood by the window with a glass of the estate's own red—he always drank the estate's own red, as if staking a claim—and when he saw Astrid come through the door with a woman's hand in hers, his mouth curved into something that wasn't quite a smile.

"Astrid." He crossed the room unhurriedly. He had their father's height and none of his warmth, and he wore his half-brotherhood like a title he'd won in a lawsuit, which, in a sense, he had. "You didn't mention you were bringing anyone."

"You didn't mention you were coming early to get your version of events on record first."

"Family habit." His gaze slid to Odile, cataloguing her: the dark dress, the watchful eyes, the hand still loosely laced with Astrid's. "And you are?"

"Odile Crane." The same handshake, the same level gaze. "Astrid's partner."

"Partner," Julien repeated, tasting the word. "In what business?"

"The wedding next weekend," Astrid said. "Odile's coordinating it. She's also—" and here she made herself smile, made herself lean slightly into Odile's shoulder, an act so audacious she expected lightning, "—mine."

Julien's glass paused halfway to his lips. Across the room, Aunt Camille's head lifted like a hound catching scent.

"Well," Camille said, gliding over. "How wonderful. How long?"

"Eight months," Odile said, without a heartbeat's hesitation.

Astrid nearly choked. Eight months. They had met eleven days ago in a hotel bar where Odile had been reviewing a seating chart and Astrid had been three glasses into deciding her life was unsalvageable.

"And how did you meet?" Camille pressed.

"At a wedding," Odile said. "I was working. Astrid was a guest who'd been seated, deliberately, I suspect, at the table of people she disliked. She came to find me to complain about the floral arrangements. Peonies out of season, drooping within the hour. She was right about them." A small shrug. "I fired the florist. She bought me a drink. The rest is arithmetic."

It was perfect. It was seamless. It was delivered with the weary authority of absolute truth, and Astrid watched her family absorb it—Camille nodding, a cousin laughing, even Julien's suspicion losing a degree of its edge—and felt something complicated move through her chest. Gratitude, yes. But underneath it, something more dangerous: the sudden, irrational wish that it had happened. That there had been a wedding, a bad florist, a drink. That the story were true.

She pushed the feeling down where she kept all the others.

* * *

Dinner was served in the long room under the portrait of Astrid's father, Henri Vane, painted at fifty, glass of wine in hand, smiling with the confidence of a man who believed his family would hold together out of sheer affection. The will had been read four months ago. The portrait watched over the table like a man trying to remember what he'd done wrong.

It was Camille, naturally, over the fish course, who opened the subject.

"It's simply unusual, isn't it," she said, to no one and everyone, "the way the shares were divided. Controlling interest to Julien, when Henri always said—" she paused delicately, "—well. What he always said."

"Camille." Marguerite's voice carried the weight of a cellar door closing.

"I'm only asking. Astrid, darling, you must have questions. You were his—everyone knew you were his favorite. The vines, the harvest, all of it was to be yours. Everyone assumed."

"Assumptions aren't legal instruments," Julien said pleasantly.

"No," Camille agreed. "But signatures are. And that second signature page appeared so very conveniently after the funeral—"

"Enough." Julien set down his fork. The pleasantness had drained out of his voice, leaving something harder beneath. "The will has been probated. It's done. Rehashing it at dinner is vulgar, and frankly, Camille, your husband's debts are not my concern, so I'd think twice about stirring the pot."

The table went silent. Camille flushed. A cousin studied her plate with scholarly intensity. And Astrid felt the old heat rise in her throat—the fury, the grief, the certainty that something about that document was wrong, wrong, wrong—that she had been swallowing since the day the lawyer had slid the papers across his desk and refused to meet her eyes.

She opened her mouth to say something she would regret.

"He's right that it's vulgar," Odile said.

Every head turned. Astrid turned too, alarmed.

Odile set down her wine glass with a soft click and continued in the mild, unbothered tone of a woman discussing napkin folds. "Not the topic—the timing. My professional opinion. I've sat through two hundred of these dinners, give or take. Family money plus grief plus alcohol is a combination I've seen end engagements, businesses, and one memorable fistfight in a marquee. If the Vane family wants to litigate its ghosts, do it tomorrow, in daylight, with coffee. Tonight there's a reunion, there's a wedding in nine days, and there is—" she gestured gracefully at the table, "—an genuinely excellent Burgundy that nobody is tasting because everyone is busy performing outrage. Which is a waste. Henri Vane chose this wine. I'd think honoring that would matter more than re-fighting a probate."

Silence. Absolute, ringing silence.

Then Marguerite began, very quietly, to laugh—a dry, rusted sound, like a gate opening after years. "Well," she said. "Henri would have adored you. He hated a scene at his table." She lifted her glass. "To the wine, then. And to whoever taught this family its manners tonight."

Glasses rose around the table, ragged but rising. Even Julien lifted his, though his eyes never left Odile—cool, recalculating, filing her away somewhere. Astrid saw it and understood: whatever Odile had just defused, she had also made an enemy of its architect. She'd walked into the middle of a family war and planted a flag without knowing which side held the high ground.

Under the table, Astrid's hand found Odile's knee and squeezed—thank you, be careful, thank you.

Odile, to her credit, did not react at all. Except that, a moment later, her hand came to rest over Astrid's, warm and steady, and stayed there.

* * *

Later, in the blue guest room with its view of the dark vineyards, Astrid stood at the window with a glass of water she wasn't drinking and listened to Odile move around behind her, unpacking with the crisp efficiency of someone setting up a field hospital.

"You lied beautifully," Astrid said. "The peonies. The eight months. Where does it come from?"

"Practice." A drawer closed. "People don't fact-check love stories. They want them to be true, so they stop listening for details. That's the whole trick."

"And the part about my father choosing the wine?"

Odile paused. In the window's reflection, Astrid watched her consider the question, watch something guarded pass behind her eyes.

"Lucky guess," Odile said. "Fathers who own vineyards choose the wine."

"My father died in March. Four months ago. And the will—" Astrid stopped. Pressed her lips together. There it was, sitting on her tongue: the second signature page, the date that didn't fit, the lawyer who wouldn't look at her. Four months of carrying it alone because saying it aloud would make it real, and real meant war, and war meant losing whatever was left of her father's memory to lawyers and forensics.

She almost told her. Standing ten feet away, in a borrowed room, with a woman she'd hired eleven days ago—she almost told her everything.

Instead she said, "Thank you. At dinner. You didn't have to do that."

"Yes, I did," Odile said. "It's what you're paying me for." But she said it more gently than the words required, and when Astrid glanced at the reflection again, Odile was looking at her—not appraising, not planning. Just looking. Like a woman noticing, for the first time, that the person in front of her was not a client.

"Get some sleep," Odile added, turning back to her suitcase. "Tomorrow your grandmother wants a tour of the cellars, and I intend to be brilliant at caring about barrels."

Astrid laughed despite herself—a short, surprised sound—and turned back to the window, to the vines moving in the dark like a sea holding its breath.

Below, a light burned in the estate office. Through the gap in the curtains she could see Julien's silhouette at the desk, hunched over papers, turning pages by lamplight long past midnight.

Watching him, Astrid's hand drifted unconsciously to her own bag, where the photocopy she should have destroyed sat folded in an interior pocket, its suspicious date pressing against her hip like a coal.

She hadn't told Odile. She told herself it was prudence.

It was the first lie of many, and they both felt it settle into the house between them, quiet as dust.
