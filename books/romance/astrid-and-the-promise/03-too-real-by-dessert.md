## 3. Too Real by Dessert

The trouble with pretending, Odile thought, was how quickly the pretending stopped feeling like work.

She had planned four hundred and eleven weddings. She knew the choreography of a reception the way a surgeon knew the body: the moment the champagne hit, the moment the toasts began to run long, the moment the mother of the bride's smile calcified into something that would need managing by the cake course. She could read a room like a floor plan. And so, seated at the Vane family table with a linen napkin in her lap and Astrid Vane's hand resting—casually, convincingly, *possessively*—on her knee, Odile read this one and found it fascinating in the way a house fire is fascinating.

Grandmother Marguerite Vane presided from the head of the table like a portrait that had learned to speak. She wore emerald silk and a brooch the size of a fist, and when she lifted her glass for the third toast of the evening, the entire table went quiet with the obedience of long habit.

"To my son Étienne," Marguerite said, her voice a polished stone skipping across still water, "who would have hated to see us all dressed up with no one to scandalize."

Laughter, brittle and required. Odile felt Astrid's fingers tighten on her knee—a small, involuntary pressure, there and gone, like a pulse.

"To Étienne," the table echoed.

Astrid drank. Odile watched her throat move and thought: *she's counting these toasts like I count place cards.* Each one a task. Each one survivable.

Across the table, Julien Vane—half-brother, per the briefing Astrid had given her in the car, though the word *half* had been delivered with a blade in it—raised his glass a beat too slowly and a degree too high. "To Étienne," he said, "who left us all so much to sort out." His eyes moved around the table as he said it, collecting reactions the way a toll collector collected coins. When they landed on Odile, they lingered. Appraising. She'd seen that look before, on grooms doing arithmetic about prenups.

She smiled at him with the exact warmth she'd give a difficult vendor. He smiled back like a man who'd already decided what she was worth.

* * *

By the time the fish course cleared, Odile had catalogued the table's fault lines the way she catalogued seating charts: Marguerite and Julien against the room; a cousin, Sylvie, who kept glancing at Astrid with something between pity and hunger for news; an uncle who had already drunk past the point of discretion and kept saying the word *shares* in a voice calibrated to be overheard. And Astrid at the center of it, spine straight, glass mostly untouched, performing the role of the composed heiress so flawlessly that Odile—who knew performance when she saw it, who had built a career on it—felt something twist behind her ribs that was not part of the arrangement.

Astrid leaned in. Her breath brushed Odile's ear, and Odile was ashamed of how her body responded to it, a shiver arriving uninvited like a guest without a plus-one.

"Cellar," Astrid murmured. "Five minutes. If I have to smile at Uncle Bertrand's harvest anecdote one more time I'm going to put my fork in my eye."

"Professional assessment," Odile murmured back, "the fork would be an improvement to the table setting."

Astrid laughed—a real one, short and startled, nothing like the polished sound she'd been dispensing at the table all evening—and Odile felt it land somewhere she hadn't authorized.

They slipped out between the toasts and the dessert service, the way Odile had slipped out of a hundred receptions, except that this time she wasn't escaping to fix a crisis. She *was* the crisis, or half of one, walking hand-in-hand down a stone corridor that smelled of cold earth and oak and a century of patience.

* * *

The cellar opened beneath them like the inside of a cathedral built for drinking. Candles—Astrid had lit some earlier, Odile suspected, in preparation, or perhaps the cellar simply always waited lit, the way churches do. Barrels slept in rows. Bottles lay racked along the walls in their thousands, dusty and patient, each one a decision someone had made years ago about what the future would taste like.

"This is where he used to bring me," Astrid said, before Odile had chosen a safe conversational direction. Her voice had shed its drawing-room polish entirely. "My father. I'd sit on that barrel—" she pointed, "—the one with the dent, I kicked it when I was nine—and he'd pull samples with the thief, that long glass tube, and make me guess the vintage. I was terrible at it. I once said a '98 was 'tastes like pennies and disappointment,' and he laughed so hard he had to sit down."

Odile said nothing. She had learned long ago that grief was a door you did not knock on; you waited to be invited in.

"He'd have liked you," Astrid added, and then winced. "That's a strange thing to say. You've been my fake girlfriend for thirty-six hours."

"I've been called worse by people who've known me longer."

That earned her another real laugh. Astrid drifted down the row of barrels, trailing her fingers along the oak staves, and Odile followed, aware of the candlelight doing things to Astrid's profile that the candlelight had no business doing. Somewhere above them, the toasts would be resuming. The dessert Odile had personally approved—poached pear, almond cream, the last of the estate's honey—would be going untouched. She found she didn't care. That was the alarming part. Four hundred and eleven weddings, and she had never once abandoned a dessert course. Dessert was sacred. Dessert was the whole point.

"Can I ask you something?" Astrid said. "And you're allowed to refuse. I know what I bought. I know the terms."

*What I bought.* The words should have been a comfort. Boundaries. Clarity. Instead they stung in a way Odile examined and declined to examine further.

"Ask."

"The way you work. The way you read a room, the way you deflected Julien at dinner—did you see that? He went for you, *and who exactly are you*, and you turned it into a joke about the wine list and he just—deflated. That's not charm. That's armor. Where did you get it?"

Odile looked at a bottle of '89, dusty and dark, and considered lying. It would have been easy. It was, in fact, the service she was paid for.

"My sister," she said.

Astrid went still.

"Her name is Colette. Three years ago she was engaged. I planned the wedding—it was going to be the best thing I'd ever done, and I don't say that lightly, I once planned a wedding on a private island during a typhoon." Odile heard her own voice go flat and efficient and couldn't stop it. "Two weeks before, the groom's brother came to me. Privately. He told me his brother had been seeing someone else for a year. He had proof. He asked me—begged me—to tell Colette."

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere overhead, muffled by stone, a fork rang against a glass. Another toast.

"And you didn't," Astrid said. Not an accusation. Worse. Understanding.

"I told myself it wasn't my place. That it was hearsay. That Colette was happy and my job was to protect the event, not interrogate the marriage." Odile's laugh came out wrong. "I protected the event. It was beautiful. The flowers were peonies, in November, flown in at obscene expense. She found out about the other woman on the honeymoon. From a text. He didn't even have the decency to—"

She stopped. Astrid had crossed the space between them and taken her hand. Just that. Her grip was warm and slightly rough at the palm, a working hand, a hand that had held a vineyard thief and kicked a dented barrel at nine years old.

"Colette doesn't speak to me now," Odile said. "Not entirely. She sends a card at Christmas with a signature and nothing else in it. And I kept planning weddings, because it's what I'm good at, and I told myself the next one would be different, that love was real *somewhere*, statistically, in principle—" She shook her head. "You asked where I got the armor. I forged it myself. It's very good. It's held for three years."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight it's leaking."

The word hung in the candlelight. Astrid's thumb moved once across Odile's knuckles, an unconscious motion, and Odile watched her realize she was doing it and decide not to stop.

"My father knew he was dying for eight months," Astrid said. "Eight months, and he told no one. Not Marguerite. Not me. He spent them in meetings with lawyers, and then he spent one perfect afternoon with me in this cellar, teaching me how to read the color at the edge of a glass, and he never said a word. I keep thinking—if I'd known, I'd have—" Her voice caught. "I'd have said things. And now the only things he said to me at the end were about *tannins*, and I'm supposed to stand at that table and toast him and smile, and I can't tell if I'm grieving him or grieving the conversation we never had."

"Both," Odile said quietly. "It's always both."

Astrid looked at her then—really looked, the performance stripped away, the heiress and the client and the employer all set down like coats—and Odile understood, with the clarity of a woman watching a structural failure in slow motion, that the cellar had stopped being an escape.

"You don't believe in weddings anymore," Astrid said.

"I believe in *events*."

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard."

"I know."

Astrid stepped closer. The candle nearest them guttered in some cellar breath, and the shadows leaned in. Odile could smell the wine on her, and beneath it something green and mineral, the vineyard itself clinging to her skin. Her hand was still in Astrid's. She could have taken it back. There was still a version of this evening where she took it back.

"Astrid," she said, and her voice came out lower than intended. "The terms—"

"Forget the terms."

"That's exactly the problem. I can't. I never forget the terms. The terms are the only thing that's ever kept me—"

"Kept you what?"

*Safe. Alone. Unfired, unjilted, unbetrayed.* She didn't say it. Astrid was close enough now that saying anything at all felt like a structural risk. Her eyes dropped to Astrid's mouth and she hated herself for it and did not correct it, and Astrid's breath caught—a small sound, barely anything, and Odile would remember that sound later with the precision of a woman cataloguing evidence against herself—

Astrid tilted her head. One degree. Two.

The cellar door banged open.

"—*there* you are." Julien Vane filled the doorway, tie undone, wineglass somehow still in his hand, swaying with the careful exaggeration of a man who had been drinking to survive this evening too and was losing. His eyes moved from Astrid's face to Odile's to the space between them—three feet of candlelit air that suddenly looked, to a drunk man, like a confession. "Hiding from the family. How touching. Or—" his mouth curled, "—is this what the money's for? Marguerite said you *hired* one. I thought she was being colorful."

"Julien," Astrid said, and her voice had gone to ice so fast Odile felt the temperature drop. "You're drunk."

"I'm *observant*." He came down two steps, unsteady, and pointed the glass at Odile. "And you. The planner. You've got a look about you. You look at things. At *papers*, I'd guess. At tables. At whatever's lying around." His smile widened, and beneath the wine Odile saw something colder moving, something sober and watchful, and every instinct she'd honed across four hundred and eleven weddings stood up and took notice. "I'd hate for you to see something you weren't supposed to."

"Goodnight, Julien," Astrid said.

He held Odile's eyes a moment longer—long enough to make it a message, not a glance—then turned and climbed back into the light, humming something tuneless, his footsteps lurching up the stairs.

The cellar door swung shut. The candles steadied.

Neither of them moved. The near-kiss hung in the air between them, interrupted but not withdrawn, and Odile found that her heart was not slowing down, and that it had nothing to do with Julien Vane.

"We should go back," Astrid said softly. "Before they miss us."

"Yes," Odile said, and did not move.
