## 4. The Second Signature

The study smelled the way it always had—dust and cedar and the ghost of my father's pipe, though he'd been dead eleven months and had quit smoking a decade before that. Grief keeps its own habits. I'd come in to find the deed to the north parcel for Grand-mère, who wanted it "for her records," which meant she wanted to hold it over Julien's head at Sunday lunch. Instead I found the will.

Not the will—the *file*. The leather folder that had sat in the bottom drawer of my father's desk since the reading, the one the lawyer, Maître Roussel, had told us contained only ancillary documents. Property schedules. Insurance riders. Nothing of consequence.

I hadn't meant to open it. That was the lie I would tell myself later, standing in the dark with my heart going like a bird against glass. I had meant to open it. Some part of me had been circling that drawer for months the way you circle a wound, pressing it to be sure it still hurts.

The folder was heavier than paper should be. Inside: the property schedules, as promised. The insurance rider. And beneath them, clipped separately, a signature page.

One page. One signature—my father's, unmistakable even in photocopy, the heavy downstroke on the *V* of Vane that I used to trace with my finger as a child while he signed bottles at harvest. Below it, a date. Three weeks before his death.

And below *that*, a clause I had never heard read aloud.

*In the event of incapacity or death, controlling interest in Domaine Vane—fifty-one percent of shares held by Édouard Vane—shall pass to Julien Marchand.*

Julien. Not me. Not Grand-mère. Not the trust that had been discussed at every family dinner since I was old enough to pour wine without spilling it.

I sat down in my father's chair because my legs stopped being interested in holding me. The leather exhaled under me, familiar, and for one stupid second I waited for him to walk in and explain it. He was good at explaining things. He'd spent my whole childhood explaining why Julien got the vineyard lessons and I got the books, why Julien's name went on the barrels and mine went nowhere, why fairness was a luxury a working estate couldn't afford. I had stopped asking. I had built a life in Lyon out of not asking.

But this—he'd never said this. And the date. Three weeks before he died, when he was already sick, when the morphine made his hands shake so badly he couldn't hold a pen, let alone make that confident downstroke. When Maître Roussel came twice and both times left with his briefcase clutched to his chest like a hymnal.

I turned the page toward the window light. Photocopy, yes—but photocopies preserve what originals try to hide. There, along the edge of the date line, a faint shadow. A place where something had been altered, or where two versions had been laid over each other and the machine had caught the seam. Ink doesn't lie about its age; paper doesn't forget what was written on it first. I didn't know if that shadow proved anything. I knew it looked like proof, and I knew the difference between those two things could ruin me.

Because here was the arithmetic of it, cold and simple: the estate was in trouble. Everyone knew it and no one said it. The 2021 frost, the hail last spring, the bank's letters that arrived in envelopes too thin to contain good news. If this page were real—if my father had truly cut me out three weeks before dying—then the family's fragile peace rested on a document worth fighting over, and a fight was the one thing the Domaine couldn't survive. Grand-mère would go to war. She'd been rehearsing that war since Julien was born to the wrong mother and the right ambition. And the harvest—the first harvest under our own label, the one that was supposed to save us—would rot on the vines while lawyers picked through the cellar.

If the page were forged—and God, some traitorous part of me hoped it was, because a forged page meant my father hadn't done this, hadn't chosen Julien over me with three weeks to live—then someone in my family had committed a crime wearing my father's name like a stolen coat.

Either way, telling the truth cost everything. Silence cost only me.

I put the page back exactly as I'd found it, aligned the folder's corners, closed the drawer. My hands were steady. I remember being surprised by that.

* * *

Odile found me an hour later on the terrace steps, two glasses of the estate rosé sweating in her grip, and I understood again why hiring her had been the only sane decision of my life. She moved through my family's chaos like water finding level—soothing Grand-mère's suspicion with a compliment about the table linens, absorbing Tante Sylvie's third interrogation about our "engagement" with a smile that gave nothing away. She was magnificent. It was beginning to be a problem.

"You have the face," she said, sitting beside me without asking, handing me a glass.

"What face?"

"The face of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like." She looked out at the rows, the late light turning the vines to bronze wire. "I see it at every wedding. Right before the toast."

"It's nothing." The word *nothing* tasted like copper. "Family business."

"Ah." She drank. "The most dangerous kind." A pause, and then, quieter, in that voice she used maybe once a day, stripped of all its professional polish: "You know you can tell me things. That's not in the contract, but it's included anyway. Free of charge."

And I almost did. That was the terrible thing—I felt the whole of it rise in me like floodwater, the drawer and the folder and the impossible date, my father's name on a page that might as well have been written by a stranger. Odile Crane, who had seen every family detonate from the inside, who knew exactly what secrets do to weddings and reunions and harvests. Who owed me nothing but a fee and was giving me more than that for free.

I opened my mouth. I looked at her—at the tired eyes behind the competence, the woman who had told me over bad coffee in Lyon that she'd stopped believing in weddings, and who had come here anyway, to mine, to stand between me and my family's teeth—

—and I thought: *if I tell her, it becomes real.* Real enough that she might feel obligated to act. Real enough that it could reach Grand-mère, reach Julien, reach the lawyer whose name I hadn't yet let myself say aloud. Real enough that whatever fragile thing was growing between us—this fake engagement with its increasingly un-fake pulse—would be buried under it. Odile had been burned by one family's betrayal already. I would not hand her a second one wrapped in ribbon.

"I can't," I said. "Not yet. Please don't ask me."

Something crossed her face—hurt, quickly filed away behind the professional mask, and I hated myself for making her use it. "Okay," she said. "But Astrid. Whatever it is—secrets at weddings don't stay buried. I've watched them surface at the worst possible moment, in front of the most possible people. If there's a bomb under your table, better you find it than it finds you."

"I know where it is," I said. "That's the problem."

She studied me for a long moment, and I let her, because looking away would have confirmed everything. Then she touched my wrist—brief, warm, gone—and stood. "Dinner's at eight. Your grandmother has seated us next to your brother. I'd call that a hostile act."

"A declaration of war," I agreed, and smiled, and the smile held until she walked inside.

Then I sat alone on the steps with my untouched wine, watching the light die over the vines my father might—or might not—have taken from me, and I understood what I had just done. I had chosen the secret. I had chosen it over her, over the one person in this house who wasn't playing an angle, and I had done it smiling, with her hand still warm on my wrist.

Below the terrace, the first grapes hung heavy in the dark, waiting. Everything here was waiting—waiting to be picked, waiting to be pressed, waiting to become either wine or vinegar depending on what we did next.

And somewhere in the house, in a locked office with a leather briefcase, Maître Roussel was sleeping soundly, believing his secret was safe.

He didn't know I'd found the page.

He didn't know that Odile Crane—who saw everything, who missed nothing, who had spent three days cataloguing this estate down to the candlesticks—would be walking those same halls tomorrow, opening those same drawers, doing the logistics that kept this wedding alive.

I told myself I had time. I told myself I'd decide before anyone else found anything.

I told myself a great many things that night, lying awake in my childhood bed, listening to the wind move through the vines like a tide going out.

None of them were true.
