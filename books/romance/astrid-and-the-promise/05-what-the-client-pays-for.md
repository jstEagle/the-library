## 5. What the Client Pays For

The document room at Vane House smelled of dust and old money—paper, leather, and beneath it all the faint mineral tang of wine that had soaked into the walls over a century. Odile had been given access to it on her second day, when Marguerite Vane had swept in with a ring of keys and the expression of a woman granting a favor she intended to be repaid in deference.

"You'll need the floor plans for the tent placement," the grandmother had said, unlocking a cabinet with a key that hung from her neck like a relic. "And the estate maps. Everything else—" a thin smile, "—is family business."

Odile had smiled back the way she smiled at brides' mothers who wanted peonies in December. *Of course. Naturally. I would never dream of touching anything else.*

She had meant it, too. That was the thing people misunderstood about her profession. Wedding planners were not gossips by trade; they were vaults. She had kept worse secrets than seating charts—affairs discovered mid-reception, prenups signed in tears, a groom who'd confessed to his best man an hour before the ceremony that he wasn't sure. Her discretion was the product she sold alongside the flowers and the lighting design. It had never once cost her anything to keep it.

Until now.

* * *

It happened on Thursday, four days before the wedding, because everything in Odile's life lately happened sideways, in the margins of other people's business.

She was hunting for the easement paperwork—the access road to the east field where the tent would stand, because the caterer's trucks needed legal right-of-way and the county had a way of choosing wedding weekends to remember that roads were public. The filing system in the document room was a monument to three generations of lawyers who believed in paper and disbelieved in order. She'd been at it for two hours, kneeling on the cold flagstones in her good trousers, when she pulled a folder labeled *ESTATE — VANE, EDMOND — FINAL* and felt the wrongness before she saw it.

The folder was too thick.

Wills, in her limited but sufficient experience, did not come with appendices. She should have put it back. She told herself later, standing in front of Julien Vane's lawyer with her heart hammering, that she should have simply set it down and walked away, and that the woman who did walk away was someone she used to know.

But her hands opened it.

The first copy was the will she'd heard whispered about at dinner—Edmond Vane's final testament, leaving the vineyard in trust to Astrid, with provisions for Julien that read, even to Odile's untrained eye, like an afterthought. A cottage. An annuity. A pat on the head in legal language.

The second signature page was behind it, clipped loose.

Her stomach dropped before her mind caught up. She knew what she was looking at the way she knew a cake that had been iced over a crack—a beautiful surface laid deliberately across a fault line. It was a transfer instrument. Controlling shares of Vane et Fils, fifty-one percent, conveyed from the estate to Julien Ambrose Vane. Dated March the fourteenth.

Three days after Edmond Vane died.

She sat back on her heels in the dust and read it twice, three times, looking for the trick of it. The signature was Edmond's—or was so nearly Edmond's that only love or fraud could tell them apart, and Odile had neither the expertise nor the desire to guess. But the date. The date was the wound. A man did not sign away controlling interest in the vineyard his daughter loved three days after his death. And if he had somehow managed it—if some deathbed codicil existed—then why was this page loose, unfiled, hidden behind the will like a letter slipped under a door?

There was another explanation, and it arrived in her mind fully formed, wearing a name: **Perrin**.

Alain Perrin, the family's attorney, was also—she checked the engagement contract again that night, though she didn't need to—*her* employer. The client of record. The man whose firm had hired Crane Events, whose retainer was paying the fee Astrid had promised her, the fee she needed so badly she'd agreed to pretend to be in love with a woman she was beginning to actually—

She stopped that thought at the border and posted guards around it.

* * *

Friday morning she watched Perrin across the breakfast terrace and understood that she had never once looked at him properly.

He was sixty-ish, silver-templed, charming in the way of men who had spent forty years being trusted with other people's worst days. He kissed Marguerite Vane's hand. He complimented the coffee. He asked Odile intelligent questions about the tent and the string quartet and whether the forecast rain would threaten the ceremony, and every question was a small performance of innocence, and now she couldn't stop seeing it.

"Rain," he said, "is the great equalizer of weddings. Even the best planner can't fight the sky."

"No," Odile said. "But we can plan for it." She held his gaze a half-second longer than courtesy required, and something flickered behind his eyes—not guilt exactly. Recognition. The look of a man checking whether a lock he thought was sound still held.

That afternoon she went to the county records office in town, telling herself it was about the easement, which was even true, mostly. While she was there, she asked—casually, she hoped—for the recording dates on any instruments filed against the Vane estate in the past year. The clerk, bored and helpful, pulled the index.

The transfer to Julien Vane had never been filed.

A document dated March fourteenth, unrecorded in June. Either it was invalid—and someone was keeping an invalid instrument in a locked cabinet as leverage—or it was waiting. Sitting in the dark until the moment served. Until after the wedding, perhaps. Until Astrid had publicly, happily, irreversibly committed herself to a future built on sand.

Odile walked out into the hot noon light of the village square and stood very still beside a fountain shaped like a grape cluster, which under the circumstances struck her as a personal insult.

Here is what her profession demanded, and she recited it to herself like a catechism: *You are contracted to the client of record. You do not disclose client matters. You do not involve yourself in family disputes. You make the wedding beautiful and you go home.* Perrin's firm had retained her; the confidentiality clause ran both directions; and if she breathed one word of what she'd seen, she wouldn't just lose the fee. She'd lose the reputation that was the only asset she owned outright. Twenty years of being the vault. One breach and every planner's network from Napa to Provence would close to her like a fist.

And here is what her heart demanded, and it didn't bother with rhetoric at all. It simply produced Astrid's face from two nights ago—Astrid in the cellar doorway, candlelit, laughing at something Odile had said about centerpieces, the laugh catching halfway through and turning into something rawer, more surprised. *I haven't laughed like that since Dad got sick,* she'd said, and then looked horrified at having said it, and Odile had wanted, with a ferocity that frightened her, to spend the rest of her life earning that laugh.

Astrid suspected. Odile had seen it in her at dinner—the way she watched Julien, watchful as a deer at a water hole. She was carrying the same dread alone, in the dark, and Odile could end it with a sentence. *There's a second page. Check the dates.*

Or she could say nothing, collect her fee, and become one more person who knew the truth about the Vane family and let Astrid find out the way everyone finds out eventually: too late, and in public.

* * *

Saturday night, two days before the wedding, she found Astrid on the terrace steps below the east wing, barefoot, a glass of wine untouched beside her, watching the vines go blue-black in the dusk.

"The rehearsal lunch ran long," Odile said, sitting down at a careful distance. Careful distances were getting harder to maintain. That was its own alarm bell.

"Aunt Colette has opinions about the seating chart."

"Your aunt has opinions about oxygen. It doesn't mean we have to breathe them."

Astrid laughed—that caught-halfway laugh—and then it faded, and she turned, and in the last light her face was open in a way that made Odile's chest ache. "Can I ask you something? As my fake girlfriend, professionally speaking?"

"Professionally speaking, yes."

"If you found out something about someone you cared about—something bad—something that could blow up their whole life—would you tell them?" Her voice was light. Her eyes were not. "Or would protecting them mean keeping quiet?"

Every instinct Odile had honed over twenty years said: *deflect, charm, redirect.* Every hour she'd spent in the document room said: *she knows, or she suspects, and she's asking you because she trusts you, and God help you both.*

"I think," Odile said slowly, "that secrets are like debts. They don't get smaller with time. They accrue."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have right now."

Astrid studied her for a long moment, and Odile felt the inspection like a searchlight, felt how much of her was lit up and visible—the sleepless nights, the document dust still somehow under her fingernails, the war being fought openly across her face. But Astrid only nodded, and picked up her wine, and said, "Two days. Then whatever happens happens," and touched Odile's hand briefly on the step between them, and went inside.

Odile stayed on the terrace until the stars came out over the vineyard, doing arithmetic she already knew the sum of.

If she spoke, she lost the fee, the career, the version of herself that had survived her sister's humiliation by learning never to take sides. If she stayed silent, she kept all of it—and handed Astrid over to the wreckage intact, unwarned, the way the world had handed Delphine over to a jilting groom while Odile stood in the back of the church with her mouth shut, telling herself it wasn't her place.

*Not your place,* she thought. *You've built a whole life out of those three words.*

She went inside. She checked the timeline for the ceremony one more time. She confirmed the florist, the quartet, the road clearance for the trucks. She was very good at her job.

Then she opened her laptop and drafted an email to Alain Perrin—*Confirming final logistics ahead of Saturday; please advise if any outstanding estate matters may affect the weekend schedule*—and stared at the sentence *outstanding estate matters* for a long time, a message written in a code only two people could read, a warning dressed as professionalism, a cowardice dressed as diligence.

She sent it.

His reply came within minutes, smooth as poured wine: *No matters whatsoever. Enjoy the celebration.*

Odile closed the laptop and pressed her palms hard against her eyes and hated herself with a thoroughness that felt almost professional.

Down the hall, she could hear Astrid moving through the rehearsal notes, humming. Two days. Whatever happened, happened.

And Odile already knew, with sick certainty, that she was going to let it happen to her.
