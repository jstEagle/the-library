## 6. Harvest Eve

The wedding was, by every professional measure, a triumph.

Astrid knew this the way she knew the ripeness of grapes—by instinct, by years of watching her father walk the rows and pinch a skin between thumb and forefinger and pronounce judgment. She stood at the edge of the terrace as the last light went amber over the valley, and she watched Odile Crane move through her own creation like weather through wheat: inevitable, invisible until you looked, and then all you could see.

The long tables ran the length of the lawn, dressed in linen the color of cream poured slow. Candles in mismatched brass holders—Odile had sourced them from three different estate sales, Astrid had learned, because "perfection that looks assembled is dead; perfection that looks found is alive." The florals were cut from the vineyard's own gardens, marigold and late lavender and those dark, wine-colored dahlias her mother had planted the summer before she left. Odile had asked about them once, quietly, notebook open. *Your father's favorites?* And Astrid had said yes, though they'd been her mother's, and Odile had written something down and never mentioned it again, and somehow that had felt like being known.

The bride was radiant. The groom wept at his vows. Grandmother Marguerite, who had not wept since the Eisenhower administration, had pressed a handkerchief to her eyes during the string quartet's second piece and pretended it was allergies.

And Astrid stood apart from all of it with a glass of the estate's young rosé she wasn't tasting, watching the woman she'd hired to pretend to love her, and understanding—with the flat, clean clarity of someone reading their own diagnosis—that she had failed completely.

She was in love with Odile Crane.

It hadn't happened all at once. That was the lie people told about falling, that it was a drop, a plunge, a single catastrophic moment. It hadn't been. It had been incremental as fermentation. A look across the reunion table when Odile deflected Aunt Camille's third interrogation about grandchildren with a story so smooth no one remembered what they'd been asking. The night in the cellar, interrupted, when Odile had talked about her sister in a voice stripped of all its armor, and Astrid had wanted—violently, helplessly—to put her hands on either side of that face and say *you didn't fail her, you were twenty-four, you were alone.* The mornings since, when Odile appeared in the kitchen at seven with two coffees and handed one over without asking how Astrid took it, because she'd already learned.

Small things. Fermenting things. Until one day you pressed your thumb to it and it gave, sweet and irreversible, and there was no going back to grape juice.

"You're staring," said Julien, materializing at her elbow with the timing he'd always had for other people's worst moments.

"I'm supervising."

"At your fake girlfriend." He swirled his whiskey. He'd been drinking since the ceremony; she could hear it in the looseness of his consonants. "You know, most people hire caterers. You hired a whole woman."

"She's very efficient."

"Mm." He watched Odile too, now, and something in the quality of his attention made Astrid's neck prickle. It wasn't envy. It was appraisal—the way he looked at ledgers, at contracts, at anything he intended to use. "Where did you find her, exactly?"

"A wedding expo. Why?"

"No reason." But he smiled at his glass, and the smile stayed too long, and Astrid felt the evening tilt a degree beneath her feet.

Across the lawn, Odile caught her eye. Just for a second. Just long enough to do the thing she did—this small, private lift of one eyebrow, *surviving?*—and Astrid felt her mouth answer before she could govern it, felt the whole treacherous warmth of her face give her away. Odile's expression flickered. Something startled moved through it, quickly buried, and then she turned back to the caterers and the candlelight and the beautiful machine of the evening.

*She knows,* Astrid thought, and didn't know if the thought was hope or terror.

* * *

Later, when the dancing started and the string quartet gave way to a playlist and the guests blurred into happy indistinction, Astrid walked out past the terrace to where the vines began.

Harvest Eve. Her father had called it the vineyard's true new year—not the calendar, not the solstice, but the night before the first pick, when everything the year had built hung waiting on the vine and nothing yet had gone wrong. He used to walk the rows alone on this night. She'd found him once, twelve years old, trailing him through the dark, and he hadn't sent her back. He'd just lifted her onto his shoulders and said, *Listen.* And she'd listened, and heard nothing, and he'd said, *Exactly. One quiet night. Tomorrow it all changes forever.*

The vines stood in rows under a fat moon, heavy with fruit, patient. Somewhere out here tomorrow morning, forty pickers would descend and the whole future of Vane would come off the stems in buckets. Or wouldn't. Depending on a signature. Depending on a date. Depending on whether the document sleeping in her desk drawer—the second signature page, the wrong date, the ink that sat too black on paper that should have yellowed around it—was what she believed it was.

She still hadn't told Odile.

She'd almost done it a dozen times. In the kitchen, coffee steaming. On the drive back from the florist, when Odile had hummed along to the radio without noticing she was doing it. Last Tuesday, 2 a.m., when they'd ended up on the same sofa with the same blanket and Astrid had opened her mouth and said instead, "You should sleep," like a coward, like a woman guarding a door.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: the will was the family's wound. If she was right—if the controlling shares had been forged into Julien's name—then saying it aloud would detonate everything. The reunion, the fragile peace, Grandmother's health, the harvest itself. And Odile—Odile whose fee Astrid was paying, whose presence at this wedding was a fiction Astrid had purchased—would be dragged into the blast radius of a truth that wasn't hers to carry. Astrid had told herself this was protection. Protection of Odile, protection of the weekend, protection of the delicate impossible thing growing between them that she refused to name while it was still safe inside the lie.

But standing among the vines, she admitted what it actually was.

Fear. Plain fear. Because once she told Odile, the thing between them would have to be real or nothing, and Astrid had spent four years learning that real things could be taken.

"Hey." Odile's voice behind her, soft. Astrid turned. She'd changed out of the plum dress into jeans and a sweater, hair loose, holding two glasses of the good Burgundy from the cellar—*the good stuff*, she said, "the client's paying, and tonight I'm counting myself staff." She handed one over. "You vanished. I do a final walkthrough at ten. It's in my contract."

"It's in your contract that you check on me?"

"It's in my contract that I check on everything." Odile came to stand beside her, shoulder almost touching hers, facing the same dark rows. For a while neither spoke. The music drifted faintly from the house. Somewhere an owl worked the hedgerow.

"Tomorrow's the harvest," Astrid said finally.

"I know. I scheduled the brunch around it. Your grandmother nearly bit me when I suggested nine-thirty."

"That tracks." Astrid turned the glass in her hand. The wine caught the moon. "My father used to say this was the only honest night of the year. Everything's decided but nothing's happened yet. All the work, all the weather, all the luck—it's all in the grape. You just don't know which way it breaks till you pick."

Odile was quiet a moment. "That's either very wise or very cruel."

"He was both." Astrid laughed, and it cracked in the middle, and she heard it crack, and Odile heard it too, and turned.

The moonlight did her no favors and every favor. Astrid could see the tiredness under her eyes, the week of eighteen-hour days, the small crease between her brows that appeared whenever she was calculating and disappeared whenever she forgot to calculate. She was looking at Astrid with an expression Astrid had never seen her wear at any of the forty weddings she'd apparently orchestrated—unguarded, questioning, a little afraid.

"Astrid." Just her name. Nothing after it.

"Don't," Astrid whispered. Not *don't say it*. She meant *don't stop.*

Odile didn't stop. She set her glass down on the low stone wall with a click that sounded enormous in the quiet, and she reached up, and her hand found Astrid's jaw—cool fingers, sure grip—and Astrid leaned in and the distance closed and—

The porch lights of the house flared. Voices. Someone laughing too loudly, stumbling toward the side gate, and they broke apart like teenagers, like conspirators, Odile stepping back with her hand pressed briefly to her own mouth as if to hold the moment in.

Julien came through the gate.

He stopped when he saw them. His eyes moved from Astrid to Odile and back, and Astrid watched him assemble something behind his eyes—a conclusion, a plan, a weapon being oiled. He smiled with all the warmth of a signed contract.

"Don't let me interrupt the site inspection," he said. "Miss Crane. A word before you go? Business."

"Julien—" Astrid started.

"It's fine," Odile said. Her voice was level. Her hand, at her side, was not quite steady, and Astrid saw it, and hated that she saw it. "We can talk business."

He held the gate for her. She went through it. And Astrid stood alone among the vines with two glasses of wine and the ghost of a touch on her jaw, watching the porch lights swallow them both, feeling the first cold edge of a night that was supposed to be quiet.

* * *

From the terrace Astrid could see them at the far end of the lawn, near the equipment shed, two silhouettes against the moonlit grass. Julien talking. Odile listening. His posture loose and expansive; hers very still, the stillness of a woman keeping her hands from clenching.

Five minutes. Seven. Astrid told herself it was logistics—final payments, vendor confirmations, the kind of conversation planners had at every wedding. She told herself this while her heart performed a slow, sickening arithmetic of its own, because she had seen that oiling-the-weapon smile before, at funerals and family dinners and the reading of the first will, and she knew exactly what Julien looked like when he was closing a deal.

At eleven minutes, Odile turned and walked back across the lawn alone. Fast. Faster than Odile ever walked.

She passed within ten feet of the terrace without seeing Astrid in the shadows—or pretending not to—and Astrid watched her go, watched her press the heel of her hand hard against one eye, once, briefly, like someone erasing something.

Then Astrid looked back at the shed, where Julien still stood, and he raised his glass to her across the distance. A toast. To what, she couldn't imagine.

The owl called again from the hedgerow. Tomorrow the pickers would come, and the grapes would come off the vine, and everything hidden would begin coming into the light.

Astrid picked up both glasses and drank her father's good Burgundy standing alone in the dark, and it tasted like the night before everything changes forever.
