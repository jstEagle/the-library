## 7. The Person in the Mirror

The guest cottage smelled of someone else's lavender.

Odile noticed it the way she noticed everything now—too much, too sharply, the way a person notices details when they are trying very hard not to notice themselves. The diffuser on the vanity had been left running by the house staff, some small courtesy of the Vane estate, and it filled the room with a sweetness that felt like an accusation. Weddings always smelled like this. Flowers and candles and someone's idea of romance, piped through the air like a promise.

She set her clutch on the dresser and did not turn on the lamp.

Outside, the wedding was still going. She could hear it—muffled strings from the quartet, the low ocean-roar of two hundred guests laughing at something the band leader said, the distant pop of a cork. Her masterpiece, humming along without her. She had built it to run without her. That was the whole point of being good at this: you built a machine so elegant that your absence didn't matter, so that you could slip away during the cake course and no one would ever know you'd gone.

No one knew she'd gone. Except Julien Vane, who had found her by the equipment shed twenty minutes ago with wine on his breath and his father's eyes narrowed into something meaner than grief.

*You know what you saw in that office. And you know what happens to planners who talk.*

He hadn't even raised his voice. That was the worst part. He'd said it the way you'd discuss a seating chart—reasonable, almost kind—and then he'd smiled, and the smile had been the smile of a man who had already checked the locks.

*Stay silent or lose the fee and her reputation.* He hadn't used those exact words. He'd used better ones. He'd talked about Odile's *professional standing*, about how the family lawyer, Marchetti, was *well connected*, about how a certain planner's sister had once been left at the altar and wasn't that such a *sad story*, he'd hate to see another Crane woman humiliated in public.

That last part—that was when her hands had gone cold.

Because he shouldn't have known about Camille. She had never told him. Which meant Astrid had told him, or their grandmother had, which meant the story traveled through this family the way everything did—as currency, as leverage, as something to be spent against you when the moment was right.

Or it meant she'd told Astrid herself, weeks ago, in the cellar, with the near-kiss still hanging between them like a held breath—and Astrid had passed it along without thinking, the way people do, the way families do, and there was no malice in it at all. Odile couldn't decide which possibility hurt more. She suspected the truth was the second one, and that the first one only existed because she needed someone to blame who wasn't herself.

Odile stood in the dark cottage and listened to the wedding she had built, and hated it.

Not the wedding itself—the linens were perfect, the string lights over the terrace caught the dusk exactly as she'd promised the bride's mother they would, the cake had been carried out at 8:47 precisely because she'd scheduled it for 8:47. It was flawless. It was the best work she'd done since before Camille's wedding, maybe ever. And that was the thing she couldn't stop looking at, the splinter under the skin of the whole night: she had poured six months of genius into a celebration for a family whose inheritance was built on a forged page, and she had known, and she had smiled, and she had charged them for the candles.

Her phone buzzed in her clutch. She didn't look. It would be her assistant confirming the vendor load-out, or her mother asking if she was eating enough, or Astrid—

It might be Astrid.

Astrid, who had kissed her knuckles at dinner like it was nothing, like it was habit, like they were real. Astrid, who had whispered *you're extraordinary* against her ear while her grandmother watched them across the table with an expression Odile still couldn't read. Astrid, who trusted her. Who had said, three nights ago, on the cellar steps, *I don't know why I can tell you things I can't tell anyone else,* and Odile had held the confession about the will in her mouth like a stone and said nothing, and let her keep trusting.

Odile turned on the lamp.

The mirror above the vanity was old, estate-old, its silvering gone cloudy at the edges so that whoever stood before it appeared framed in fog, dissolving at the borders. She looked tired. She looked, she thought, exactly like what she was: a woman in a beautiful dress doing ugly arithmetic.

*Stay silent or lose everything.*

And here was the thing—the thing that made her stomach turn over, here in the quiet, with no one watching but the fogged glass: the threat barely mattered. Oh, she needed the fee; the business account had forty-one days left in it before the overdraft line ran dry, she'd done the math in the car on the drive up like she did every month, a little funeral rite of numbers. But money was survivable. Reputation was survivable. She'd survived worse. What Julien had actually taken from her, standing by the shed with his reasonable voice, was the fiction she'd been living inside for six weeks—the fiction that staying quiet this time was different from staying quiet last time.

Because last time there had been a man too. Marcus. Camille's groom, with his soft apologetic eyes, who had pulled Odile aside two days before the wedding, behind the venue, smelling of cigarettes he swore he'd quit, and said: *I'm not sure I can do this. But if you say something, and it blows up, she'll never forgive either of us. Maybe give me until tomorrow. Maybe I'll be braver tomorrow.*

And Odile—who had planned that wedding down to the sugar flowers on the cake, who had a professional obligation, who had a sister's happiness balanced on her tongue like a coin—had given him until tomorrow.

There was no tomorrow. There was four hundred guests and a string quartet and Camille standing alone at the end of an aisle she'd walked toward a lie, and there was Odile in the back row knowing, having known for thirty-six hours, saying nothing, doing her job, keeping the machine running smooth.

Camille had forgiven her. That was the cruelest part. Camille had cried on her shoulder at the reception—Odile had stayed, God help her, Odile had *worked* the disaster, had rerouted the toast schedule and gotten the band to play something upbeat and turned a catastrophe into a story people told gently—and Camille had said, *It's not your fault, Odie. You couldn't have known.*

But she had known. And Camille didn't know that she'd known, and Odile had let her go on not knowing for three years, and the silence had hardened into a personality. Cynicism, she called it. Professional detachment. *I don't believe in weddings anymore,* she told people, and it sounded like wisdom, world-weary and chic, and everyone nodded because everyone loved a jaded romantic. But it wasn't wisdom. It was a hiding place. If you believed in nothing, then you owed nothing—not truth, not courage, not the ugly risk of speaking. Believing in nothing was just cowardice wearing black.

And now here she was again. Same aisle, different church. A forged will instead of a faithless groom. An Astrid instead of a Camille. And the same voice in her head, Marcus's voice now wearing Julien's cologne: *Maybe give it until tomorrow. Maybe you'll be braver tomorrow.*

She pressed her palms flat against the vanity. The wood was cool. Her reflection stared back at her through the fogged silver, half-dissolved, and Odile thought: *there she is. The woman who lets other people walk into things because walking into them is their business, not hers. The woman who plans other people's promises because she's too afraid to make one of her own.*

"You knew," she said aloud, to the mirror, and her voice came out strange in the empty cottage. "You've known for a week."

The reflection didn't argue. That was the trouble with mirrors; they only ever repeated you back to yourself, and she'd been repeating the same sentence for seven days: *it's not my place, it's not my client, it's not my family, it's not my fight.* Seven days of finding new ways to phrase a cowardice until it sounded like ethics.

Marchetti's ledger was in her email. She'd found it Tuesday, cross-referencing florist invoices against the estate account, and there it had been—a disbursement dated eleven days after Étienne Vane's death, paid to a document courier, initialed M.M., with no corresponding file. Eleven days after death. And the signature page transferring controlling shares to Julien bore a date from before it. Ink doesn't travel backward. Paper doesn't sign itself. She had sat in the estate office with the evidence glowing on her laptop screen and closed the lid, gently, like closing a casket.

*Professional ethics demand discretion,* she'd told herself. She was hired to plan a wedding, not audit a legacy. Interfering could void her contract, expose her to liability, blow up the event mid-production. All true. All reasonable. All, she understood now, standing in the lavender dark, the same architecture of excuse she'd built around Marcus, load-bearing walls of logic around an empty room.

The music outside swelled—first dance. She'd timed it for 9:15, golden hour's last light through the western windows. Somewhere out there, Astrid was watching the bride and groom sway, and Odile knew what Astrid's face did when she watched people dance; she'd catalogued it without meaning to, the way her mouth softened, the way she went somewhere else, somewhere hopeful, a country Odile had stopped believing existed.

Astrid thought Odile was brave. She'd said it once, drunk on one glass of the estate's own pinot, laughing: *You walk into these families like a general. Nothing rattles you.* And Odile had accepted the compliment like all the others, letting people mistake armor for courage, letting them mistake silence for strength.

The fee. Forty-one days. Julien's smile. Marchetti's connections. She laid them all out in her mind like invoices, and found, examining them honestly for the first time, that they weighed nothing. They were paper threats. The real debt—the only one with interest accruing—was the one she already owed, three years past due, to a woman in Portland who thought her sister hadn't known.

Odile took off her earrings. Set them down beside the lavender diffuser. In the mirror her face was bare and unforgiving and finally, finally honest.

"I'm not doing it again," she told the woman there. "Whatever it costs."

Her heart was pounding like she'd already said it out loud to the family, like the confession had already detonated, but that was tomorrow's work. Tonight there was only this: the decision, made in private, witnessed by no one but her own dissolving reflection. Forgiveness, she understood suddenly—it arrived to her whole, like a chord—forgiveness didn't start with Camille forgiving her, or Astrid forgiving her, or God, or time. It started here, alone, with a woman choosing to stop being the person who stayed quiet. You forgave yourself in advance, on credit, and then you went out and earned it.

She picked up her clutch. Checked the time. 9:22—the toasts would begin at 9:30, and after the toasts, the rehearsal dinner tomorrow, where the whole Vane clan would gather around one long table under the pergola she'd strung with lights, and where Julien, flushed with his victory, would be unable to resist announcing the will's terms like a coronation.

Let him announce them.

Odile opened the cottage door onto the noise and gold of the wedding, and stepped through it, and did not look back at the mirror.

Tomorrow, she thought, descending the gravel path toward the light, I tell them everything.

She just hoped, with a cold twist beneath her ribs, that she could find a way to make Astrid hear it from her first—before the wreckage, before the table, before the truth arrived the way truths always do in this family:

as an ambush.
