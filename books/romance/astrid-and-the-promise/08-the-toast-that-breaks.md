## 8. The Toast That Breaks

The rehearsal dinner was held in the long gallery of the east wing, beneath my grandmother's collection of hunting paintings—dead foxes, dead stags, dead things rendered in oils and hung with pride. Odile had transformed it anyway. Candles in amber glass along the tables, garlands of grapevine and late roses trailing down the linen, the light turned low and gold so that even the portraits seemed to soften, to forgive the room its history.

It was her masterpiece. I knew that before anyone said it. I watched her move through the room in a slate-blue dress I'd never seen, checking sightlines she'd already checked twice, touching a napkin here, a candle there, and I thought: *this is what devotion looks like when it has nowhere honest to go.* She had built beauty into every corner of a weekend built on lies, and I loved her for it, and I hated myself for loving her, because I was still carrying the second signature page like a stone sewn into my coat.

I hadn't told her about the will. She hadn't told me whatever she wasn't telling me—we were two locked boxes passing each other across a table, each convinced the other's silence was innocence.

"Smile," Odile murmured as she passed me at the head table, her hand brushing my shoulder, brief and electric. "Your grandmother is counting your smiles."

"She's counting my failures."

"Then give her arithmetic trouble." And she was gone again, into the golden room, leaving warmth on my skin like a brand.

Dinner was quail and the estate's own pinot, the vintage Papa had blended the summer before he died. My grandmother sat enthroned at the center of the table, wearing pearls and the expression of a woman presiding over her own canonization. Julien sat across from me, flushed with wine and something brighter underneath it—a triumph he hadn't yet announced but couldn't stop leaking from his pores. He kept looking at me the way you look at a clock whose time is nearly up.

And beside me, radiant and false, my fake fiancée laughed at my uncle's jokes and touched my wrist at intervals calibrated for maximum believability, and every touch landed like a question I couldn't answer.

Then Julien stood, and tapped his glass, and the room went quiet the way rooms do when everyone senses the weather turning.

"A toast," he said. "To family. To legacy." His eyes found mine. "And to truth—which, as our lawyers keep reminding us, always comes out at rehearsals."

Grandmother's smile thinned. "Julien."

"I've been patient," he said, still smiling, still tapping the glass, the sound small and obscene in the hush. "Truly. But tomorrow we sign papers, and it seems dishonest to let my sister walk into it blind. So—" he raised his glass higher, "—a toast to the will. The real one. The one that gives controlling interest in Vane Vineyards to me."

The words dropped into the room like a chandelier falling.

Someone's fork clinked against china. My aunt Madeleine made a small sound, half gasp, half laugh, waiting to be told it was a joke. Grandmother set down her wine with terrible precision.

"You will explain," she said, "what you think you're saying."

"I'm saying it plainly, for once in this family's history." Julien's flush had deepened; his voice climbed with it. "Papa amended the estate eight months before he died. Controlling shares to me. It's signed, witnessed, filed. Astrid gets the house and a stipend—generous, really, considering—and the vineyard goes where the work is. Where I am." He spread his hands, magnanimous as a politician. "I didn't want it to come out this way. But someone in this room has been sitting on secrets, and I'm tired of being the only one who plays fair."

Every eye swung to me. The candles guttered in a draft from the doors, and for one vertiginous moment I saw the room as Odile must have designed it—as a stage—and understood that my brother had just burned her set dressing to the ground.

"That document is a forgery," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"It's dated, signed, and notarized."

"So was the Protocols of the Elders of Zion." I stood. My chair scraped back and the sound ran down the table like a crack through ice. "You forged Papa's signature, Julien. You forged his *name*."

"And you forged an engagement!" He flung his arm toward me, toward Odile, toward the whole gilded lie of us. "Shall we compare documents? Shall we put everything on the table tonight, since we're all so committed to truth?"

"Enough." Grandmother's voice cut like pruning shears. "Astrid. Is this true—the amendment?"

I opened my mouth to say *I found the page, I suspected, I was going to—*

And then I looked at Odile.

She had gone very still by the far door, a tray of champagne glasses balanced on one hand, and her face—the face I had memorized in a cellar three nights ago, in candlelight, an inch from mine—was doing something I had never seen it do. It was closing. Shutters coming down over windows. And behind the shutters, just for a heartbeat before they slammed: guilt.

Not surprise. Not confusion. *Guilt.*

"You knew," I said.

The room tilted. "Odile." Her name came out of me stripped of everything soft. "You knew about this?"

"Astrid—" She set the tray down on the sideboard with exaggerated care, as if the world might be saved by not breaking glassware. "Can we—not here."

"When, then?" I laughed, and heard how ugly it sounded, how much like him. "At breakfast? After the signing? You've been in this house for weeks. You've been in my *bed*, figuratively speaking, since the moment you walked in with your contract and your fee, and you knew my brother was holding a forged will over my head and you said *nothing*?"

"I didn't know what it was. Not at first." Her voice was low, controlled, the professional register, and hearing it now was like being slapped with a glove. "I found a page while doing logistics. I didn't know if it meant anything. I was going to—"

"To what? Tell me?" I took a step toward her and the whole table watched, forty Vanes and Vanes-adjacent, feasting on it. "Or tell *him*? Which client were you protecting, Odile? Because I've seen your rate card. I know exactly what loyalty costs."

"That's not fair."

"No," I agreed. "None of this is fair. That's rather the theme of the evening."

"Girls." Grandmother, ice-cold. "This is neither the time nor—"

"It's exactly the time." Julien was grinning now, openly, wine-drunk and victorious, and I understood with terrible clarity that this—this detonation, this wreckage—was what he'd wanted all along. "Go on, Astrid. Finish it. You're good at endings."

So I finished it.

I turned back to Odile, and I took off the ring—I'd forgotten I still wore it, the prop ring, the cheap garnet she'd bought in town to sell the story—and I set it on the table in front of my grandmother's place setting, where it lay small and ridiculous among the silver.

"The engagement is over," I said, loud enough for the room, because if we were doing wreckage we would do all of it. "The contract is void. Whatever you were paid, keep it—it was never enough for either of us, was it? That was the whole problem."

Odile's face did not crumble. That was the worst part. It simply went white and smooth and closed, a door shutting in a house I'd thought was becoming mine.

"For the record," she said quietly, "I was going to tell you. Tonight. After dinner."

"After dinner," I repeated. "After the toast. After the ruin. How generous."

"Astrid—"

"Get out of my father's house."

She held my eyes for one more second—one second in which something moved behind them, vast and drowning, something I would spend weeks refusing to name—and then she straightened, lifted her chin, and walked out through the doors she'd dressed with garlands, past the candles she'd lit, out of the beautiful room she'd built for a family that ate its own.

The doors sighed shut behind her.

Silence, total, ringing. Forty people pretending to study their plates.

"Well," said Julien, raising his glass at last. "To truth."

I picked up my own glass and threw the wine—not at him, though God knows I wanted to—at the floor, where it spread dark and red across Odile's perfect parquet like something harvested too early, like blood, like the end of the season.

* * *

**Odile**

I made it eleven steps down the service corridor before my legs informed me they were done performing.

I put my hand against the wall—the plaster cool, smelling faintly of the lilies I'd ordered from a grower in Napa who owed me a favor—and I breathed the way I teach panicked brides to breathe, four counts in, six counts out, except no one teaches you the version where you are the disaster.

*Get out of my father's house.*

She'd worn the ring without being asked. She'd laughed at my jokes in front of her grandmother, real laughter, badly disguised. In the cellar she had leaned close enough that I could have counted her eyelashes, and she'd said, *Tell me something true,* and I had told her about Margot, about the groom who jilted my sister at the altar while I stood in the back row holding the emergency tissues and saying nothing, and Astrid had taken my hand and said, *You were twenty-four. Silence was survival then.*

But it isn't survival anymore. That's the thing I learned standing in front of mirrors and hotel bathroom tiles and now, apparently, in a corridor that smelled of funeral flowers. Silence stopped being survival the moment I started charging for it.

I had found the page. I had recognized the lawyer's letterhead, the ink, the date that didn't fit. I had done the math—fee versus reputation versus the look on Astrid's face—and I had chosen wrong, and I had told myself *tonight, after dinner, after the toast* as if timing could launder cowardice into discretion.

Somewhere behind me, muffled by two walls, I heard the crash of thrown wine and the rise of voices, the Vane family detonating precisely on schedule, and I pressed my forehead against the cool plaster and thought: *you planned this wedding down to the sugared violets and you never once planned how to survive it.*

My phone buzzed in my clutch. A text from Julien, sent minutes ago, timed like everything else he did:

*That was unfortunate. Your fee will still clear Friday. We should talk before you leave the property.*

I stared at it. At the offer dressed as kindness, the leash dressed as a lifeline. Somewhere in this house was a ledger, a notary, a date on a page that proved everything, and I was the only person alive who knew where all the pieces were buried.

Astrid had told me to get out of her father's house.

I wiped my face with the heel of my hand, smearing mascara I'd applied for a man who wasn't my boyfriend at a wedding that wasn't a wedding, and I turned—not toward the exit, not toward my car, but deeper into the east wing, toward the cellar stairs, toward the dark where I'd first almost kissed her and where, if there was any justice left in this rotten, gorgeous season, the truth was still waiting to be dug up like the last row of grapes before frost.

They could fire me. They could sue me. They could take the fee and the reputation and the career I'd bled twenty years into.

But nobody—nobody—was going to bury the truth in my presence twice.
