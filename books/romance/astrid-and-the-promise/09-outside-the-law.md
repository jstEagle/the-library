## 9. Outside the Law

The morning after the rehearsal dinner, Odile Crane did the one thing she had never done in eleven years of wedding planning: she broke a client's confidence on purpose.

She had spent the night awake in the cottage above the winery, Julien's threat replaying against the memory of Astrid's face as it came apart in front of forty guests—the moment Astrid's eyes had found hers across the long table and understood, all at once, that Odile had known and said nothing. *You were paid to be here,* that look said. *And you were paid for something else, too.*

Odile had packed her car twice and unpacked it twice. Then, at three in the morning, she had sat on the edge of the bed and spoken aloud to the empty room, to her sister three thousand miles away, to the woman she'd been at nineteen watching Marguerite stand alone at an altar while everyone pretended it was fine.

"I stayed quiet then," she said. "I'm not doing it twice."

She did not call lawyers. Lawyers were what people hired when they wanted the truth to die slowly and expensively in a filing cabinet. She called no one at all. Instead she spent the morning doing the thing she was best at—logistics. She printed the photographs of the second signature page. She photographed, with a steady hand, the page in Ambrose Kell's ledger she had found wedged in the back of the estate office during wedding logistics: an entry dated eleven days *after* Edmund Vane's death, billing "estate consultation—contingency," billed to Julien Vane. She photographed the ink itself under her phone's flashlight, the way the date on the transfer page sat in a slightly different black than the signature beside it, the pen lifted and returned, the hand tired or trembling or simply not Edmund Vane's.

Then she went looking for the family.

* * *

She found them the way families scatter after a detonation—Grandmother Vane in the drawing room with her spine like a fence post, Julien's wife on the terrace pretending to read, cousins drifting through the halls like ghosts who'd outstayed their welcome. Astrid was nowhere. Odile's chest tightened at that, and she made herself keep moving.

She went to the cellar.

It was the one room in the estate that belonged to no faction. The Vane family had been arguing in the drawing room for a hundred years, but the cellar was where they all came eventually, because the cellar was where the dead of the family lived—Edmund's barrels, Edmund's father's barrels, the dust and the cool dark and the smell of oak and patience. Odile set the folding table from the wedding storage room in the center of the aisle between the racks, laid out her evidence in a planner's neat sequence—documents left to right, the way a guest would read a place card—and then she went and asked, quietly and without apology, for everyone to come downstairs.

Grandmother Vane refused, of course. For eleven minutes.

Then Odile said, "Ma'am, you can hear this in the cellar or you can hear it in a courtroom, but you will hear it today, because I have spent my whole career making other people's lies look beautiful, and I am done."

Something in the old woman's face shifted—the flicker of a woman who had once been young and scared in this same house. She came downstairs. They all did.

* * *

The cellar held them the way a confessional holds a congregation: Julien at the back near the stairs as if he might still leave, his wife beside him not touching him; the cousins on upturned crates; Astrid standing apart with her arms crossed, her face a locked door, refusing to look at Odile. Odile felt that refusal like a hand around her throat. She made herself stand straighter.

"I'm not a lawyer," Odile began. "I'm not a detective. I'm the woman you hired to pour champagne and make your family look like it loved itself for one weekend. So believe me when I tell you I know exactly what a performance looks like. I've staged dozens." She let that land. "And I know a forgery when I'm standing next to one."

She turned the first page toward them. Edmund Vane's will—or the page that had surfaced three days ago, the one transferring controlling shares of the vineyard to Julien.

"This is dated the fourth of September. The day your father died." She moved her finger to the ink. "Look at the date. Look at the signature. Two different pens. Two different pressures. The signature was made when his hand could still write—the hospital records show he signed his final instructions on the first of September. The date was added later. Someone wrote the fourth of September over a signature that had been on this page for weeks."

"Whoever you are," Julien said, "you have no idea what you're—"

"Shut up, Julien." It was his wife. She hadn't looked at him when she said it. She was looking at the page.

Odile laid down the second document: the ledger page. "This is from Ambrose Kell's own book. Eleven days after Edmund died—eleven days after this page was supposedly signed—a billing entry. 'Estate consultation, contingency.' Billed to you, Julien. Contingency for what? The estate was already settled. Unless something was still being *made* settled."

The cellar had gone very quiet. Somewhere above them a door banged in the wind. Grandmother Vane had gone the color of the limestone walls.

"And this," Odile said, and her voice finally shook, and she let it, "is the part that isn't about ink at all. Your father spent his last month telling anyone who'd listen that this vineyard would go to Astrid. The workers heard it. The neighbors heard it. You heard it, Julien. You heard it and you couldn't live with it, so you bought yourself a page of paper and a lawyer with a flexible conscience, and you let your sister sit at a table for a year being told she was the heir while you waited to take it from her."

"I was owed something!" Julien's voice cracked open, and everything behind it spilled—ugly, human, small. "Do you know what it's like being the son who *stays*? I worked these vines. I bled in these rows while she was in Paris being his favorite, being the one he *showed off*—and then the will comes and it's her, all of it, her, and I'm the caretaker. Forever the caretaker. I just—I wanted what I'd earned—"

"You wanted what you *stole*," Astrid said.

It was the first thing she'd said. Her voice was steady and that was worse than shouting. She came forward into the lamplight and looked at the documents, then at her brother, and Odile watched her hold herself together by sheer will.

"You didn't even forge it well," Astrid said, and something almost like a laugh broke in her. "Papa would be embarrassed. He'd be embarrassed for you."

Julien's face did something terrible and then something worse: it collapsed. He sat down on a barrel ledge, put his head in his hands, and this man who had threatened Odile in the dark twelve hours ago began, in front of his whole family, to weep.

"I didn't want to take it from her," he said into his hands. "I wanted him to *see* me. Once. I thought if I had the shares, he'd—he'd have to—"

"He's dead, Julien," Grandmother Vane said. Her voice was ash. "He's dead and you forged his name to win an argument with a ghost."

She rose. She was eighty-one years old and she crossed the cellar floor like a woman crossing a battlefield. She took the transfer page in both hands, looked at it for a long moment—and then, deliberately, in front of everyone, she tore it in half. And in half again. And let the pieces fall.

"That document never existed," she said. "As far as this family is concerned, as far as any court or lawyer or ledger is concerned, Edmund Vane's will says what he always said it said. The vineyard goes to Astrid. The shares go to Astrid. Julien—" her voice caught, and Odile saw that she was not only furious but grieving, grieving the boy she had apparently not known, "—Julien will account for what he did to Ambrose Kell, because I will not have that man's crimes buried under my son's. But the vineyard was never his to lose, and it was never yours to take."

Julien looked up at her, wrecked. "Maman—"

"Don't," she said. "Not today. Maybe not this year. Go."

He went. Up the stairs, past the light, out. His wife did not follow him. She stayed, and Odile thought: *there's a marriage with a long road ahead of it,* and then thought of nothing else for a while, because Astrid was looking at her.

* * *

The family filed up the stairs in ones and twos, dazed, and the cellar emptied until there were only two people left in the cool dark among the barrels of the dead.

Astrid didn't speak. She stood with the torn pieces of paper at her feet and looked at Odile with an expression Odile couldn't read—too many things layered in it, anger and exhaustion and something underneath that hadn't decided what it was yet.

"You knew," Astrid said finally. "Before the toast. You knew and you let him do it. You let him stand up there and gut me in front of everyone."

"Yes."

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"I could explain it," Odile said. "I could tell you about the threat, the fee, the reputation, how I told myself I was protecting the wedding, protecting you, all the beautiful lies I'm professionally gifted at telling. But the truth is I was a coward for one more night, and you paid for it, and I'm not going to dress it up. I've spent my whole life dressing things up. It's the only thing I know how to do, and I'm trying—God, I'm trying—to stop."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the racks above, a barrel ticked as the wood settled, the sound the cellar made when it was thinking.

"You tore down my fake engagement in front of forty people," Odile said. "You were right to. It was a contract. I was the best performance you ever bought." She took a breath. "But I need you to know one thing, Astrid, and then you never have to speak to me again. Everything else was fake. The date was fake, the ring was fake, the family-weekend girlfriend was fake. The night in the cellar was not fake. Watching you at the rehearsal dinner was not fake. I have planned two hundred weddings and I stopped believing in any of them years ago, and then you happened to me, and I don't—"

"Stop," Astrid said.

Odile stopped. Her heart was going like a bride's.

Astrid looked at her for a long moment, and then she bent down, picked up one of the torn quarters of the forged page, and held it out.

"You kept evidence," she said. "You had it all morning. You could have run. You could have sold it to Julien, or mailed it to a lawyer and disappeared. Instead you stood in front of my entire family and burned your own career to the ground to give me back my father's name." Her voice frayed. "That's not nothing, Odile. That's not—"

"It's a start," Odile said. "It's the smallest possible start."

Astrid almost smiled. It was a battered thing, that almost-smile, but it was real, and Odile held onto the sight of it like a rope.

"The harvest starts tomorrow," Astrid said. "First light. The whole family's supposed to be in the rows—Grandmother's orders, everyone, no exceptions." She turned toward the stairs, then paused, not looking back. "I'm not forgiving you tonight. I want to be clear about that. I'm not *not* forgiving you, either. I just need—" she pressed her hand briefly to her sternum, "—I need a night, and I need my father's grapes in my hands, and I need to see who shows up at dawn."

"And if I show up?"

Astrid climbed two steps, and the lamplight caught the edge of her profile, and she said, quietly, without turning:

"Then you'd better bring gloves. Harvest waits for no one—not even for us."
