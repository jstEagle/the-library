## 10. First Harvest

The vines had turned the color of forgiveness—deep, bruised purple under a sky rinsed clean by three weeks of autumn rain.

Astrid stood at the top of the south slope with a pair of shears in her hand and dirt already ground into the knees of her jeans, and she could not stop looking at her family. All of them. That was the miracle of it, the small and stubborn miracle: they were all there.

Her grandmother Margaux moved down the row ahead of her, slower than she would ever admit, her silver hair pinned up beneath a straw hat that had belonged to Astrid's father. She had refused to wear gloves. "My hands pruned these rows before you were born," she'd said that morning, daring anyone to argue. No one had. But when she reached the end of the first row, she had paused, laid one bare palm against the trunk of the oldest vine, and stayed there long enough that Astrid had looked away to give her privacy.

Julien was four rows over, working alone by unspoken agreement, his shirt dark with sweat. He had shown up at six in the morning without being invited, standing at the edge of the gravel lot like a man waiting to learn whether he was still allowed through the gate. It was Margaux who had finally nodded at him—not warmly, not yet, but with something more durable than warmth. Permission. He hadn't spoken much since. He cut each cluster carefully, inspected it, set it in the crate with a precision that looked, to Astrid, like penance performed one grape at a time.

She didn't hate him anymore. That surprised her most of all. The fury had burned hot for two weeks—the forged signature, the lawyer's ledger, the humiliation of the rehearsal dinner—and then, somewhere between the cellar confrontation and the quiet morning when he'd appeared on her doorstep with his resignation from the family trust typed out and signed, the fire had simply run out of fuel. What remained underneath wasn't love, not exactly. It was something older and less dramatic: the memory of a boy who had taught her to drive the tractor, who had cried at their father's funeral harder than anyone, who had been so terrified of being left with nothing that he'd tried to take everything. She could not forgive him fully. But she could let him stay, and let him earn what he'd broken, row by row, season by season. It would take years. She found she didn't mind the length of the sentence. Some things were worth serving time for.

"Astrid." Her grandmother's voice carried down the row. "You're staring at your brother instead of your fruit. The pinot won't wait for your feelings."

Astrid laughed—a real laugh, loose and unguarded—and bent to the vines.

The clusters were heavy this year, tighter than last season's, the skins taut with sugar. Her father had planted this block the year she was born; he used to lift her onto his shoulders and tell her the vines could taste the weather, that a good harvest remembered every kind day it was given. She had thought it sentiment when she was young. Standing here now, with the whole fractured family moving through the rows around her like a slow tide, she understood he had been describing something else entirely. A harvest was just a record of everything that came before it—the rain and the drought, the pruning and the neglect, the patience of people who kept showing up. You couldn't rush it. You couldn't fake it. You could only do the work and trust the season.

The morning stretched gold and unhurried. Crates filled. Someone—Cousin Théo, probably—started singing an old Provençal harvest song, badly, and no one stopped him. Margaux directed operations from the shade of the tractor with a thermos of coffee and the air of a general who had decided, provisionally, that the war was over.

It was nearly noon when Astrid straightened, pressed a hand to the ache in her lower back, and saw Odile.

She was at the far end of the block, near the stone wall where the slope dropped toward the river, a wicker basket hooked over one arm. Not picking—she'd been strictly forbidden from labor, as a guest—but walking the rows slowly, trailing her fingers along the leaves, her face tilted up into the sun. She wore a linen dress the color of cream and no shoes, because Odile Crane had apparently decided that after twenty years of heels, her feet were entitled to a sabbatical. Her hair was down. She looked, Astrid thought, like someone who had finally been introduced to herself and liked the acquaintance.

Three weeks since the cellar. Three weeks since Odile had stood before the whole Vane family with the ledger open in her hands and said, *I should have told you sooner, and I'm telling you now because you deserve the truth more than I deserve your forgiveness.* Three weeks since Astrid had watched the woman she loved choose honesty over safety, knowing it might cost her everything, and felt her own anger collapse into something helpless and enormous.

They had taken it slowly since. Dinners. Walks along the river. One evening on the terrace when Odile had fallen asleep against Astrid's shoulder mid-sentence, exhausted from rebuilding her business from the wreckage of the Vane scandal, and Astrid had sat perfectly still for two hours rather than wake her. They had not said the word yet. Both of them were people who knew the price of words said too soon.

But Astrid was done waiting for perfect conditions. Her father had taught her that too, though it had taken her thirty years to hear it.

She set down her shears, wiped her hands on her thighs, and walked down the row toward the stone wall.

* * *

Odile heard her coming before she saw her—that particular stride, quick and decisive, the walk of a woman who had grown up on this land and would die arguing with it. She turned, and there was Astrid, sun-flushed, grape-stained to the elbows, beautiful in the way that only honest work made people beautiful.

"You're supposed to be resting," Astrid said. "Guests don't pick."

"I'm not picking. I'm supervising. It's called delegation. I invented it."

"Mm." Astrid stopped an arm's length away. Close enough that Odile could smell crushed grapes and sunshine and the faint green scent of the vines themselves. "Can I tell you something strange?"

"Those are my favorite kinds of things."

"The last wedding I planned here—" Astrid glanced back up the slope, toward the house, the terrace where the tents had stood, the aisle where a bride had walked through Odile's white roses. "I spent that whole weekend watching you work. Every crisis, every collapsed tent pole, every drunk uncle—you handled all of it like it was nothing. And I remember thinking, *she makes it look easy, but nobody's that calm. Something must be broken in her.*" She smiled, rueful. "I was right. And I fell for you anyway. Maybe partly because of it."

"That's either very romantic or very insulting."

"Both. I contain multitudes." But the humor faded from her face, and she took Odile's hand—grape-sticky, warm—and held it between both of hers, the way you hold something you're afraid to drop. "Odile. I've been rehearsing this for three weeks, which is ironic, given how you feel about rehearsals."

"Deeply ironic," Odile agreed. Her heart was doing something undignified.

"So I'm just going to say it." Astrid breathed out. "I don't want a date for a reunion. I don't want a contract, or a fee, or a performance. I want you to stay. Not for a weekend. Not for a season. Stay because you want to be here when the barrels are racked in March, and when the frost comes, and when my grandmother says something unbearable at Christmas and we have to escape to the kitchen together. Stay because—" her voice caught, steadied— "because I love you. And I've learned the hard way that the things worth having are the things you can't guarantee. So I'm asking anyway."

The wind moved through the rows. Somewhere up the slope, Théo was still singing, gloriously off-key.

Odile looked at her—at this woman who had inherited a vineyard and a family's wreckage and had chosen, every single day since, to tend both instead of walking away—and felt the last locked door inside herself swing open.

There had been a time, not long ago, when Odile Crane believed weddings were theater and love was a product and second chances were a story sold to people who hadn't been paying attention. She had built a career on that cynicism and hidden a wound inside it. Her sister's face at that altar. Her own silence afterward, dressed up as professionalism, as discretion, as *not my place.* She had spent years mistaking numbness for strength.

And then a stranger had offered her money to lie, and instead of a lie, she had found the truest thing she'd ever touched.

"I believe in weddings again," Odile said quietly. "Did you know that? Actual ones. The whole ridiculous enterprise. The vows and the cake and the uncle who cries during the toast. I got it back somewhere between your grandmother threatening me with a corkscrew and Julien confessing in front of forty people." She stepped closer, until the space between them was just breath. "So yes. I'll stay. For the harvest, and the racking, and the unbearable Christmas. For all of it. I love you, Astrid Vane—I think I started loving you somewhere around dessert on the first night, which frankly ruined my professional reputation forever, and I've never once regretted it."

Astrid kissed her among the vines, tasting of sun and grapes and the particular sweetness of a thing that has survived its own breaking.

When they broke apart, Astrid was laughing, tears and all. "You know my grandmother is watching us from the tractor."

"Of course she is. I planned her granddaughter's wedding. I know exactly how that woman surveils." Odile lifted her chin and called up the slope, clear and unashamed: "Madame Vane! Your granddaughter has excellent taste!"

Margaux raised her coffee thermos in salute. "Finally," she called back. "I was beginning to think I'd have to intervene."

Up the hill, Julien set down his shears, looked at the two of them for a long moment—and then, hesitantly, almost shyly, raised one hand. Astrid raised hers back. It wasn't absolution. It was a beginning, and beginnings were enough.

The bells of the village church rang noon across the valley, thin and bright, and the whole Vane family turned toward the house for lunch—leaving the two of them alone in the long gold light, hand in hand among the vines their love had grown from.

Odile squeezed Astrid's fingers and nodded at the rows stretching away toward the river, heavy with fruit, ready at last.

"Well," she said. "Shall we bring in the harvest?"
