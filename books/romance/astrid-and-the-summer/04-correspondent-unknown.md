## 4. Correspondent Unknown

The cottage on Larkspur Lane had been rented furnished, which Elias had taken as a mercy at the time and now understood as an insult. Everything in it belonged to someone else's life: a sofa the color of weak tea, a lamp with a fringed shade that shed light like an apology, curtains printed with a pattern of birds so generic they might have been any bird at all. He'd been in Alder Creek eleven days and he had not yet made the space his own, because making a space your own was a thing you did when you intended to stay, and Elias Marchetti had built an entire career on never intending to stay.

He sat at the kitchen table — pine, wobbly, older than he was — with a case file open in front of him and his laptop closed beside it, and he was bored in a way that felt almost physical, like a stone lodged under the ribs.

That was the strange part. The work itself was good work, by the only measure he'd ever allowed himself to matter. Meridian Development paid its invoices before they were due. The Whitlock acquisition was the kind of file junior associates dreamed about: clean structure, cooperative bank, a foreclosure angle so tidy it practically wrote its own briefs. Two hundred and forty acres of valley floor, water rights, a tasting room within driving distance of three population centers. On paper it was beautiful.

On paper.

In person it was a woman in mud boots standing in a cellar full of her father's unsold wine, looking at him like he was a bill she couldn't pay. He'd thought about her more than was professionally advisable. Not the way men in bars thought about women — or not only that, though he wasn't going to pretend her face hadn't stayed with him, all sharp angles and tired defiance — but the way you thought about a move you'd already made on a chessboard and couldn't quite stop reexamining. She'd looked at the land like it was a person. He'd written *sentimental attachment* in his notes and the phrase had sat wrong on the page ever since, like a shoe on the wrong foot.

He got up, poured two fingers of the cottage's previous tenant's whiskey — decent stuff, better than he deserved — and stood at the window over the sink. Rain was coming; the air had that green, bruised smell of it. Across the lane, a dog trotted past with something dead in its mouth, unhurried, a creature with no deadlines whatsoever.

Eleven days, and what did he know about Alder Creek? He knew the diner put too much butter on everything and charged too little for it. He knew the council members smiled at him with their mouths and measured him with their eyes, because towns like this could smell a developer's lawyer the way cattle smelled weather. He knew the librarian was named Mrs. Okonkwo and she had looked straight through his charm on day one as if it were a coat he'd forgotten to take off at the door.

And he knew that at night, in this borrowed house full of borrowed furniture, the silence came down like snowfall, total and weightless, and he lay in a bed that wasn't his and listened to a town breathe around him without including him in it.

He was thirty-four years old. He had won forty-one consecutive motions. He had a corner office waiting in Sacramento with his name already etched on frosted glass, and he could not remember the last time he had said anything true to anyone.

The thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome, and he drank the whiskey down to be rid of it.

* * *

It was Mrs. Okonkwo who put the idea in his head, though she would deny it later with great satisfaction.

He'd gone into the library on Thursday to pull county records — the assessor's plats, the old water easements, anything that might complicate Meridian's title theory — and found the place transformed. Bunting. A folding table by the front desk with a hand-lettered sign: **ALDER CREEK FRIENDS OF READING — ANONYMOUS PEN-PAL PROGRAM. WRITE TO A STRANGER. BE A STRANGER TO NO ONE.**

"That sign is self-contradictory," Elias said, because he couldn't help himself.

Mrs. Okonkwo looked up from her stamping with the expression of a woman who had been waiting all week for someone to say exactly that. "It's aspirational," she said. "You must be the new lawyer."

"You have me at a disadvantage."

"I don't have anyone at a disadvantage. I have everyone at an advantage. I run the library." She set down her stamp. "You're here for the records room. Second floor. Don't sneeze on the 1970s plats, the mold is load-bearing." Then, as he turned: "We need letter writers. Half the town signed up and half of those are children asking each other about video games. The adults are supposed to write to shut-ins and newcomers and anyone who needs a line a week that isn't a bill. It works better than you'd think."

"I'm not really—" He gestured vaguely at the table, the bunting, the entire concept.

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You're really not. That's why you should do it." And she went back to her stamping as though the conversation were over, which, in her jurisdiction, it apparently was.

Elias took the stairs to the records room and told himself the exchange meant nothing. But an hour later, elbow-deep in plat maps, he caught himself thinking about it again. *A line a week that isn't a bill.* When had anyone sent him one of those? His inbox was a cathedral of demands. His mother called twice a year and both times wanted to know when he was coming home, meaning Sacramento, meaning nowhere. His colleagues were friendly the way rival soldiers are friendly between offensives.

He pulled the Whitlock plat from 1987 and saw, in the margin, in faded surveyor's ink, a note he didn't expect: *G. Whitlock requests amended access per agreement w/ F. Marchetti — see correspondence.* 

Marchetti.

He stared at it for a long moment. Common name. Italian families had been in this valley since the thirties; there were probably a dozen Marchettis in the county archives. His father had died four years ago in a condo in Roseville with a storage unit full of boxes Elias still hadn't opened. There was no reason — none — to think the name in the margin meant anything.

He photocopied the plat anyway, and filed the copy in his briefcase instead of the case folder, which was a thing he noticed himself doing and declined to examine.

On his way out, the pen-pal table stood between him and the door like a tollbooth. Mrs. Okonkwo watched him approach it with the serene confidence of a fisherman watching a trout swim toward the net on its own recognizance.

"Sign-up sheet's right there," she said. "Pseudonym optional. Address goes through the library. No small talk about weather — that's a rule, people waste whole letters on weather."

"And if I change my mind?"

"Then you'll have changed your mind, and I'll have a spare envelope." She slid the sheet toward him. "Pen's attached. People always steal the pens otherwise. It tells you something about people."

Elias looked at the sheet. Fourteen names in various hands. He picked up the pen.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. A town's inner correspondence was a soft intelligence asset; you learned things in letters that never surfaced in depositions. That was the professional justification, and it was even partly true, and he wrote *Correspondent Unknown* on the line for pseudonym because it sounded like a case caption and amused him, and then he hesitated over the blank space where the first letter was supposed to go.

"Take it home," Mrs. Okonkwo said. "First letters written under pressure are terrible. Like first drafts of anything." She handed him a stack of stationery — actual paper, cream-colored, slightly rough — and an envelope pre-printed with the library's post office box. "Due back whenever. We're not monsters. Well. Some of us are."

He walked out into the late-afternoon light with the stationery under his arm feeling, absurdly, like a man leaving a confessional with the booth under his arm.

* * *

That night the rain arrived, drumming on the cottage roof, and Elias sat at the wobbly pine table with a legal pad full of hearing strategy he couldn't make himself care about and the cream-colored paper in front of him, blank.

He wrote six false starts.

*Dear stranger,* — too much like a form letter. *I've been told I should write to you* — too much like an apology. *I am a lawyer* — God, no, that was how you cleared a room.

He poured a little more of the dead man's whiskey and tried again, and this time he stopped trying to sound like anyone at all, and what came out surprised him:

*I don't know what I'm supposed to put in one of these. The instructions say no weather. That eliminates ninety percent of my conversational range, which tells you something sad about my life.*

*I'll tell you what today was, since apparently that's the assignment. Today I sat in a room with a view of a vineyard and worked out how to take it away from someone, and the work was good, and I kept looking up at the vines instead of the numbers. They're trained along wires in rows so straight they look drawn on. Somebody spent decades teaching those plants to grow exactly that way. You can't see that from a spreadsheet. I can't stop seeing it, and I don't know what to do with that information, so I'm giving it to you. Consider yourself a landfill for inconvenient observations.*

*Here's another one: I'm good at what I do, and lately being good at it feels like being fluent in a language I no longer want to speak. I win for people I wouldn't invite to dinner. I've gotten very fast at not noticing that. Tonight I noticed.*

*You don't have to answer any of that. The librarian says most people just want to hear that somebody read them.*

*— Correspondent Unknown*

He read it over twice. Every instinct he owned told him it was reckless — candid, unguarded, the kind of writing that ends up as Exhibit C. He sealed it anyway. Mailing it felt like stepping off a ledge in the dark and finding, halfway down, that the dark was holding him up.

* * *

Her reply came nine days later, forwarded in a plain envelope, and he knew it was hers before he opened it — not by any mark, but by the way his pulse behaved, quick and stupid, like a boy's.

*Dear Landfill,*

*I laughed out loud at "landfill for inconvenient observations," which startled the cat. I don't have a cat. It startled me.*

*You wrote that you keep looking up at the vines instead of the numbers. Keep doing that. The numbers will still be there. The vines are the ones doing the actual work of staying alive.*

*Since we're trading inconvenient observations: I inherited a mess recently. Not money — the opposite of money. A whole architecture of debts wearing the costume of a legacy. Everyone in this town has a story about my family and most of the stories end badly, and the worst part is they're mostly true. I came here to bury someone and instead I find myself defending him to strangers, and I don't even know if he deserves it. That's the part nobody warns you about grief. It comes with homework.*

*Here is my question for you, since you offered yourself as a repository: when you look up at the vines instead of the numbers — what do you feel? Be honest. Nobody's grading.*

*— Your correspondent, also unknown*

Elias read it three times. Then he sat very still in the lamplight with the fringe shadow falling across the page, aware of several things at once: that this stranger had handed him something real; that he wanted, badly, to answer honestly; and that somewhere in the back of his mind a quiet voice noted that whoever she was, she was probably one of the fourteen names on a sheet in a small town where he was currently dismantling someone's inheritance — and that he had no way of knowing which.

The voice was faint. He let it stay faint.

*What do I feel,* he wrote that night. *Like I'm trespassing. Like the rows were laid out by people who expected to still be here, and I'm the interruption. I grew up around ambition, not agriculture. Ambition doesn't ask anything of you except more. A vine asks you to show up every single season and gives you nothing for years and then, if you're patient, gives you everything. I've never been patient. I'm telling you this like it's a character flaw, which I suppose it is, though I've mostly gotten paid for it.*

*About your father: defend him anyway. Not because he deserves it. Because the story other people tell about a man is never the whole man, and someone should hold the part they can't see. If no one else volunteers, I volunteer.*

*— C.U.*

* * *

They fell into it the way water finds a channel.

A letter a week, sometimes two. Hers came in handwriting that started careful and loosened as the pages went on, as though she forgot mid-sentence that she was writing to a stranger. His came typed at first — habit — until she teased him (*Do you type your grocery lists? Who hurt you?*) and he bought a fountain pen from the drugstore and discovered his own handwriting after fifteen years of keyboards, stiff and slanted and strangely intimate, like hearing a recording of his own voice.

She told him about the cellar, about the rows of unsold vintages with dust on the shoulders of the bottles like epaulettes. About Ruthie, the foreman, who spoke in verdicts. About the way the town looked at her in the diner, tallying her against her father's failures. She told him she was afraid of becoming her father — of loving something so much she'd ruin herself keeping it — and she wrote it plainly, without drama, the way you report weather to no one, and Elias sat with that letter for an hour before he could answer.

He told her things he had never told anyone. That he'd chosen law because it was the one arena where being right mattered more than being liked, and that he'd discovered too late the game was rigged the other way. That his father had been a quiet man who'd wanted something from him he'd never identified and now never could. That he lay awake some nights in a rented house doing arithmetic on his own life and the sums kept coming out wrong.

*You asked once what I feel when I look at the vines,* he wrote in the fourth week. *I lied by omission. What I feel is envy. They know what they're for.*

She answered: *Then come stand in a vineyard sometime, Correspondent. Envy is curable. I have the prescription on-site.*

He laughed alone at the kitchen table, and the sound surprised him, and the lamp with the fringed shade threw his shadow long across the wall of the borrowed house, and for the first time in eleven days — in eleven years, if he was honest, and with her he was trying to be — the silence didn't come down like snowfall.

* * *

On a bright Tuesday morning, Mrs. Okonkwo sorted the week's mail behind her desk with the efficiency of a card sharp. Two envelopes bore the program's return address, one incoming, one outgoing, and she held them side by side for a moment, frowning at nothing in particular — a flicker of something, a half-formed suspicion, the librarian's equivalent of a fish rising to inspect the fly.

Then the phone rang, and a child wanted to know if the library had books about volcanoes, and the moment dissolved unexamined into the ordinary noise of the day.

She slipped Hazel Whitlock's letter into the outgoing mail for *Correspondent Unknown*, and Elias Marchetti's letter into the pile for the vineyard, and stamped them both without a second glance, having never once checked her sign-in sheet against anything at all.

At the vineyard, Hazel opened hers at the kitchen table and smiled at the first line. At the cottage on Larkspur Lane, Elias opened his standing in the doorway, briefcase in hand, running late for a meeting with the bank that held Whitlock Vineyard's mortgage — and read the opening sentence twice.

*Dear Correspondent — I met the enemy yesterday. He has good manners and cold eyes, and I hated how well he fit his suit. Tell me something true about bad men, since you seem to collect inconvenient observations. Do you think they know what they are?*

Elias stood in the doorway a long moment, the letter trembling very slightly in his hand, the morning bright and indifferent outside.

Then he folded it away, got in his car, and drove to do the thing the letter was asking him about.
