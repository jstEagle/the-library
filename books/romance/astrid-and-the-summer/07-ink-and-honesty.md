## 7. Ink and Honesty

Hazel wrote the second letter at three in the morning, which told her something about herself she wasn't sure she wanted to know.

The cellar was cool and smelled of dust and old oak and the ghost of her father's cigars, and she sat on an overturned crate between rows of bottles no one had ever bought, the lantern turned low so the shadows pooled in the corners like standing water. Somewhere above her, the farmhouse ticked and settled. Somewhere beyond that, the vineyard ran dark to the tree line, four hundred acres of sleeping things that didn't know yet whether they'd be alive next summer.

*Dear Stranger,* she wrote, because the librarian had been firm on the rules — no names, no addresses, no small talk about weather — and because the word *stranger* had begun to feel less like a warning and more like a door left open a crack.

*I told you I came here to bury a man I didn't like. What I didn't tell you is that I'm afraid I'm him.*

She stopped. Read it back. In the lantern light the words looked like something pulled out of her chest with forceps, still wet. She almost crossed it out. Instead she kept going.

*He wasn't cruel, exactly. He was just a man who kept choosing the easier lie. He told the bank the harvest would be better than it was. He told my mother he'd stop drinking. He told me, the last time we spoke, that everything at the vineyard was fine, and I believed him because I wanted to, and by the time I learned otherwise he was past apologizing. And here's the thing that keeps me up at night — I'm good at the easier lie too. I'm good at telling people I'm fine. I've been telling everyone, including you, that I came here for closure. I came here because I had nowhere else to go and a mortgage I can't pay.*

*So that's my confession this week. I am not the brave widow of the vineyard. I'm a woman in a dead man's cellar, talking to a stranger because strangers can't be disappointed in me yet.*

*Tell me something true back. That's the price of reading mine.*

*— H.*

She sealed it before she could regret it and left it in the tin box at the library before the town was awake, feeling lighter and more exposed than she had in years, as though she'd walked through Alder Creek in her nightgown and no one had noticed but her.

* * *

Elias read her letter on the ferry dock of his own conscience, so to speak — which is to say, standing at the window of his rented cottage with the letter in one hand and a coffee going cold in the other, watching fog burn off the hills above Whitlock Vineyard.

He'd meant to answer her first letter with something charming and deflecting. He was very good at charming and deflecting; it was practically his bar admission. But her second letter arrived and dismantled the plan entirely.

*Tell me something true back. That's the price of reading mine.*

He sat down at the little desk under the window — the desk he'd never used for anything but case files — and found, to his mild alarm, that his hand already knew what it wanted to say.

*Dear Stranger,* he wrote. *You want true. Fine. Here's true: I'm good at winning for people I don't respect.*

*That's the whole job, mostly. You find the version of events that serves your client and you build it into something a judge or a town council can't refuse. I used to think that was craft. Lately it feels more like being a very expensive translator for people who only speak money. Last month I won a zoning fight that put forty families out of a building so a client could put up a parking structure he'll never visit. I got a bonus. I ate a very good dinner alone. That's the true thing you asked for: I'm excellent at my work and I can't remember the last time I was proud of it.*

*You said you're afraid of becoming your father. I'll trade you a fear for a fear: I'm afraid I already became the thing I was running from. My old man was a lawyer too — small practice, honest, poor, dead too young. I swore I'd never be broke like him. Nobody warns you that the opposite of broke isn't rich. It's just a different kind of poor, the kind with a nice watch.*

*There. We're even. Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation as a man with no soft spots, and it's the most valuable thing I own.*

*— E.*

He read it twice before sealing it, and the second reading frightened him a little, because it was the most honest document he'd produced in a decade, and he'd written it to someone whose name he didn't know, in a town he was in the process of legally dismantling.

He told himself the irony was noted. He told himself it didn't matter. He mailed the letter anyway.

* * *

Hazel's third letter came back to him smelling faintly of wine and pencil shavings.

*Dear E,*

*You win the confession contest this week, and I resent it, because now I have to go deeper to keep up.*

*Here's what I didn't tell you about my father: he used to sing to the vines. Badly. Italian songs his mother taught him, off-key, at dawn, and the whole valley could hear him and the whole valley pretended they couldn't. When I was nine I thought he was the most embarrassing man alive. When I was nineteen I thought he was the most disappointing. I'm thirty-one now and I stood at his grave last month and realized I couldn't remember his singing voice at all — just that it existed, just that people complained about it, the way you'd remember a weather pattern from a place you left.*

*Grief is stupid like that. It doesn't take the whole man. It takes one specific thing you didn't know you were still holding, and it takes it in public.*

*I'm telling you this because you said you have a reputation with no soft spots. I want to tell you that reputations are just scars other people can see. Mine is: the daughter who left and came back too late. Everyone in this town has a version of that story and they're all slightly different and all of them are right.*

*Question, since we're being honest: what did you want to be, before you became excellent?*

*— H.*

Elias read that letter four times. The question sat in him like a splinter — *what did you want to be, before you became excellent* — and he caught himself, two days later, standing in the county records office pulling files he didn't need, thinking about a summer when he was eleven and had announced to his father that he wanted to write novels. His father had laughed, kindly, and said the law paid better, and Elias had never mentioned it again, and had somehow, over twenty years, let that single exchange build his entire life.

*Dear H,* he wrote. *You're a menace. That question has been following me around for three days like a dog I fed once.*

*Answer: I wanted to write. Don't laugh. I was going to be the next great American something. My father said the law paid better and I was eleven and believed him, and that was that. Now the only things I write are motions and these letters, and I notice that I look forward to these letters the way I never once looked forward to a motion. Draw your own conclusions. I'm choosing not to.*

*Your father sang to his vines. Mine read case law aloud at the dinner table like it was poetry — he loved the old dissents, the judges who lost the argument but won the future. I used to think it was the most boring thing in the world. I'd give a lot to hear it now.*

*New rule: we're allowed one cowardice per letter. Mine this week — I'm in this town on business, and the business is not the kind you'd like, and I'm not going to say more, and you're allowed to be annoyed about it.*

*— E.*

* * *

Hazel read the paragraph twice, and something in her chest went tight and strange — not suspicion, not yet, just the small animal awareness that everyone in Alder Creek was on somebody's payroll and the town was small enough that her stranger might be anyone, might be the feed store man, might be the new lawyer everyone at the diner kept mentioning, the one with the good suits and the council's ear.

She put the letter down. Picked it up again. The rest of it was so plainly, painfully true that she couldn't make the one paragraph outweigh it.

*Dear E,*

*Noted, and filed, and yes, I'm annoyed, and no, I'm not going to interrogate you about it, because everyone in this town is on somebody's payroll and if I disqualified my pen pal for that I'd have to write to myself. One cowardice per letter. Mine: I checked the mailbox twice today before your letter came, and when it came I sat in the truck in the driveway and read it instead of going inside, and it was the best forty minutes of my week. That's not a confession about you. It's a confession about how thin my life has gotten, and how much room there turns out to be in it.*

*You wanted to write. So write. Right here. Consider this your venue. I'm the only reader who can't reject you, because I already like you, which is either the easiest thing I've done all year or the most dangerous.*

*— H.*

* * *

They wrote through June and into July, and the letters stopped being letters and became a place.

Hazel wrote to him from the vine rows at dusk, describing the way the light came down the valley like something being poured. Elias wrote back from a diner booth at midnight, from the courthouse steps, once from a hotel in the city during a client dinner he'd stepped out of to sit in the parking garage because her letter had come that morning and he couldn't wait.

*The foreman here is a woman named Ruthie who has worked the land forty years and treats me like a very slow student,* Hazel wrote. *Today she had me thin the canopy for three hours in the heat and I complained the entire time and she said, "Good, the vines can't hear you, but you needed to say it." I think she might be the wisest person I've ever met and I think she'd deny it with violence.*

*Here's what I'm learning that I hate learning: I like the work. My hands are wrecked. I fell asleep at the kitchen table at 8:40 p.m. like a farm animal. And when I woke up my first thought wasn't dread. It was the east block, and whether the mildew got it in the night. I've never in my life had a first thought that wasn't dread. I don't know what to do with a morning that starts with something other than fear. It's like being handed a tool I don't know the name of.*

*Dear H,* Elias wrote, *I read your letter in a parking garage in the city, sitting on the hood of my car next to a concrete pillar, and a man asked me if I was having car trouble and I said no and he gave me a strange look and left, and I realized I was smiling at a piece of paper in an underground garage at nine in the morning, and that is the cowardice portion of this letter, delivered in full.*

*You asked once what I wanted to be before I became excellent. Update: this week I drafted the strategy for the biggest deal of my career, and it's good, it's airtight, and I sat there looking at it and felt the way I imagine a forger feels looking at his best fake — proud of the work, sick about the picture. My client is not a good actor. I keep telling myself the deal will help the town. Some of it might even be true.*

*I can't say more. Rule stands. But I'll tell you this: I've started walking past your valley on purpose. There's a road above the east ridge where you can see the whole property in the evening, the rows going gold, and I've driven it four times and told myself each time it was a shortcut. There is no shortcut there. I checked the map.*

*— E.*

Hazel read that one standing in the vineyard with the evening coming down, and she read the line about the road above the east ridge and felt her whole body go warm in a way that had nothing to do with the sun, and she thought: *whoever you are, you drive up and look at my vines and lie to yourself about why, and I do the same thing at my mailbox, and we have never seen each other and I would know your handwriting in a crowd of a thousand.*

She wrote back: *Come to the ridge at harvest. Whoever you are. I'll leave a bottle on the fence post at the north corner. You don't have to say who you are. You don't even have to knock. Just — come see it up close, instead of from the road. The vines are better in person. So, I'm told, is honesty.*

* * *

Elias read that letter on the east ridge itself, parked, engine off, the valley going amber below him, and the request landed in his chest like a stone dropped into a well he'd thought was bottomless.

*Come see it up close.*

He was, at that moment, holding a folder on the passenger seat containing the draft foreclosure notice for the property below him. Hearing date: late August. Three weeks before first pick.

He looked at the folder. He looked at the valley. He took out his pen, and on the back of the letter — the only paper he had — he began to write the reply he would actually send, and the words came out of him before he could stop them, honest as blood:

*Dear H,*

*I'm coming to the ridge. I'm coming past the ridge. I have to tell you something soon and I'm afraid of the day I do, and I'm more afraid of the day I don't. Until then — leave the bottle. I'll bring the honesty.*

*— E.*

He sealed it, and drove down toward town, and did not see the lights of the farmhouse come on below him, or the woman standing at her kitchen window looking out at the dark valley, wondering — with a hope that was beginning to feel less like a risk and more like a fact — who exactly was about to walk into her life.

Three days later, at the library, the librarian pinned a flyer to the corkboard: *ANNUAL HARVEST FUNDRAISER — AUGUST 9TH — TOWN HALL — ALL WELCOME.*

Hazel read it, and thought of her stranger, and thought of the man from Meridian whose name she'd begun hearing at the diner, and had no idea that by Saturday night she'd be pouring bad catered wine for both of them at once.
