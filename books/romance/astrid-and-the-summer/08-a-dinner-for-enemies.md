## 8. A Dinner for Enemies

The invitation arrived on cream cardstock with a gold-embossed grape cluster in the corner, which was funny, because the last time anyone in Alder Creek had raised money for anything, it had been a bake sale to fix the church roof, and the roof still leaked.

*The Alder Creek Harvest Fund Gala — Benefiting the Volunteer Fire Department & Community Hall Restoration. Saturday, seven o'clock. Black tie optional (we know who you are).*

Ruthie read it over my shoulder at the kitchen table and snorted.

"We know who you are," she repeated. "That's Marisol's handwriting under the print. She means me."

"You're coming."

"I own one dress, and it's been to three funerals."

"Then it's due a celebration." I smoothed the card flat. Part of me wanted to burn it. A town fundraiser meant the whole valley in one room — the council members who'd smiled at Elias Marchetti across their folding tables, the neighbors who still called my father *that Whitlock boy* like he'd never grown up, just gone to seed. It meant being looked at. Measured. Found either pitiful or proud, and no way to tell which until it was too late.

But it also meant Meridian Development would be there. The notice had come Tuesday — certified mail, thick as a phone book, every page of it designed to make a person feel small. *Notice of Default. Notice of Intent to Foreclose. Hearing scheduled for the twenty-ninth day of August.* And on the signature line, in confident blue ink: *Elias Marchetti, Esq., Meridian Development Group.*

I'd read that name so many times the letters had stopped meaning anything. Then they'd started meaning everything.

"He'll be there," Ruthie said, watching my face do whatever it was doing. "The lawyer. Marisol told me Meridian's sponsoring the wine table. Their money's all over this thing."

"Of course it is." I set the invitation down. "They buy the hall, they buy the council, they buy the wine. Why not buy the fire department while they're at it? Everything burns eventually anyway."

"That's your father talking."

I looked up. Ruthie was buttering toast she had no intention of eating, the way she did when she wanted her hands busy and her eyes free to pin you down.

"It's arithmetic," I said.

"It's armor," she said. "And it doesn't fit you. Go put on something that does."

* * *

What fit, as it turned out, was a dress I hadn't worn since Chicago — deep green, bought for a book launch I'd attended on the arm of an editor who'd later described my manuscript as *promising but uncommercial*, a phrase I'd come to understand was publishing's way of saying *no, but politely*. The dress still buttoned. That felt like a small victory over the entire preceding year.

The community hall had been transformed in the way small towns transform things: crepe paper streamers in burgundy and gold, borrowed tablecloths, candles in mason jars, and a banner over the stage reading WELCOME TO THE HARVEST FUND GALA in letters that had clearly been traced by hand, and lovingly. Someone had strung white lights along the rafters, and against the dark ceiling they looked less like decoration than like a sky someone had remembered to bring indoors.

It was beautiful. I hated how much it moved me.

"Hazel Whitlock." Marisol Vega materialized at my elbow in a red dress and librarian's glasses pushed up into her curls. "You came. I had a bet going with Pastor Dunn."

"What were the odds?"

"Two to one against. You owe him nothing; I bet on you." She pressed a glass into my hand. "Catered wine. Don't taste it, just hold it. It's from a box."

She swept off before I could thank her, and I stood at the edge of the room holding my terrible wine and watching the town turn. There was Ruthie, indeed in the funeral dress, laughing with the fire chief. There were the Hollis brothers, whose orchard bordered our north field, pointedly not looking at me. There was Councilman Brandt, flushed and expansive, holding court near the dessert table.

And there, by the wine table — naturally, inevitably, the way water finds the lowest ground — was Elias Marchetti.

He wore a dark suit with no tie, collar open, which should have read as underdressed and instead read as a man so certain of himself he didn't need the costume. He was listening to Councilwoman Reyes with his head tilted, attentive, amused at some private frequency, and even from across the room I could see the exact mechanics of his charm: the slight forward lean, the pause before he answered, the smile that arrived a half-second late, as if he found things delightful only after careful consideration.

I had seen that smile in my imagination more times than I cared to admit. In person it made my jaw tighten.

"Hazel." He'd crossed the room without my noticing — that was the first unsettling thing. The second was that he said my name like he'd been saying it for years. "You look—" He stopped himself, recalibrated. "Like Chicago agrees with you."

"Chicago spat me out months ago."

"Then it has poor judgment." His eyes went to my glass. "You're not drinking that."

"Marisol warned me."

"She undersold it. That wine is a hate crime against grapes." He flagged down a volunteer and exchanged my glass for another from a bottle behind the table — a real bottle, dusty, unlabeled. "Councilwoman Reyes brought it from her cellar. She doesn't know what she has. Don't tell her."

"Why are you being kind to me?"

The question landed between us like a dropped glass. Around us the gala went on — laughter, the scrape of chairs, someone's child weaving between tables with a sparkler — but our small patch of air had gone still.

Elias didn't flinch. I'd give him that. Most men, caught flat, would have laughed it off or turned it into a joke. He just looked at me, and for a moment the charm dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was harder to read and much more interesting.

"Because you asked," he said finally. "Nobody asks me anything. They assume."

"They assume you're here to take my land."

"Yes." He said it plainly, no apology in it, and somehow that was worse than a lie. "That is literally why I'm here. But it's not the only reason I'm here, and I suspect you know the difference, even if you don't like it."

"The notice came Tuesday." I kept my voice level. Low. This was not a conversation for an audience, though I could feel the audience assembling at the edges of it anyway — Ruthie watching from the fire chief's side, Marisol pretending to shelve nothing behind the book-sale table she'd set up in the corner. "Twenty-ninth of August. Right before harvest."

"I know when it is."

"Do you?" I stepped closer, and now we were close enough that I could smell his cologne, cedar and something darker, and I hated myself for cataloguing it. "Because from where I stand, Mr. Marchetti, the timing looks surgical. Foreclose before the grapes come in, and the crop — the one good thing my father left — becomes collateral instead of income. Is that in the strategy memo? Or did you figure that out all on your own?"

Something flickered across his face. Not guilt. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man hearing his own thoughts said back to him in a voice he hadn't authorized.

"You've been reading," he said quietly.

"I've been *surviving*. Reading came first."

For a moment neither of us spoke. The string lights hummed faintly overhead. Somewhere near the stage, the pastor tapped a microphone and it shrieked, and half the room laughed, and the sound broke whatever spell had settled over us.

"Dance with me," Elias said.

"No."

"It wasn't a romantic overture. It was tactical. Half this room is watching us right now, and if we keep standing here looking like a deposition, they'll write the story themselves by Sunday service. If we dance, we're just two people being civil, and civility bores them inside of four minutes."

"And after four minutes?"

"After four minutes," he said, "you can step on my foot and leave with your dignity intact. I'll even make it easy. I'll say something unforgivable."

Against every instinct I owned, I heard myself laugh — one short, unwilling syllable — and I saw him register it, file it away, treasure it in whatever ledger he kept behind those gray eyes.

"One dance," I said. "And then you say the unforgivable thing."

* * *

He was a better dancer than he had any right to be. The band was three retirees and a high schooler on drums, playing something slow and slightly out of tune, and Elias held me at a distance that was correct in every particular and somehow intimate anyway, his hand light at my waist, his attention — this was the strange part — entirely on me. Not scanning the room. Not performing. Looking at me like I was the only text worth reading.

"You grew up here," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Until I was eighteen. Then I did my best to become someone else."

"How did that go?"

"The someone else filed for bankruptcy in March."

His mouth curved. "You joke about it."

"If I stop joking about it, I'll have to feel it, and I'm scheduled to feel it in August. I'm booking appointments."

"That's the healthiest thing anyone's said to me all year." He turned us neatly past the Hollis brothers, who watched with open mouths. "Tell me about the vineyard. Not the finances. The vines."

"Why?"

"Because everyone in this room talks to me about the finances. I want to hear about the part you actually care about."

So, God help me, I told him. About the old block on the south slope, the one my grandmother Astrid planted, Cabernet franc on rootstock older than any living person in the valley. About how the vines remember every season — the frost year, the drought year, the year my mother died, when nobody pruned properly and the fruit came in wild and strange and, my father always swore, the best wine the estate ever made. I heard myself talking and couldn't stop, and Elias listened the way people listen in confession, without interrupting, without judging, his thumb moving once, absently, against my waist.

When the song ended, he didn't let go immediately. Neither did I. That was the moment I understood I was in genuine danger, standing in a room full of witnesses, wanting the music to start again.

"Four minutes are up," he murmured.

"So they are." I stepped back. My hand felt cold where his had been. "Say the unforgivable thing."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide — watched the choice move through his face like weather. Whatever he'd planned to say, whatever easy cruelty or necessary truth, he set it aside.

"Miss Whitlock." Louder now, pitched to carry, and the nearest conversations faltered as heads turned. "I'm sorry about your father. For what it's worth — and I know exactly what it's worth coming from me — Alder Creek lost something when it lost him. And so did you."

It wasn't unforgivable. It was worse. It was *kind*, publicly, from the mouth of the man holding the foreclosure papers, and I watched the room absorb it — Ruthie's narrowed eyes, Marisol's raised eyebrows, Councilman Brandt's sudden interest — and I understood that I had been maneuvered, gracefully, into owing him something in front of everyone.

"Mr. Marchetti." My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "Save your condolences for the hearing. They'll carry more weight in writing."

A ripple of laughter, nervous and delighted. Elias inclined his head like a duelist acknowledging a touch.

"Until August, then," he said.

"Until August."

* * *

I lasted forty minutes longer at the gala. I ate rubber chicken, donated twenty dollars to the fire department, and let Marisol introduce me to three eligible widowers, all of whom talked about their boats. Then I claimed a headache and walked out into the night, because the air inside had gotten too warm and too full of him.

The parking lot was nearly empty. My truck sat under the one working streetlight, and beside it, hands in his pockets, jacket over his shoulder, stood Elias Marchetti, looking up at the stars like a man checking the time.

"You followed me out."

"I left first. You followed *me*." He turned. Under the streetlight his face was stripped of everything he'd worn inside — the charm, the polish, the performance. What remained looked tired. "I wanted to say something without an audience."

"Then say it."

He opened his mouth. Closed it. When he spoke, the words came slowly, like a man walking on ice and testing each step.

"The twenty-ninth of August," he said. "Don't come to that hearing thinking it's already decided. However the paperwork reads. However it *looks*."

"Is that legal advice?"

"It's the only kind I can give you." He stepped closer, and the streetlight cut his face in half, lit and shadow. "Hazel. When the time comes, you're going to learn things about me. Some of them will be true. I need you to remember that I told you — right here, tonight — that not all of them will be."

My heart was doing something stupid and arrhythmic. "That's not an apology. That's a riddle."

"It's a warning." He backed toward his car, silver under the stars, parked crooked like a man who'd stopped caring about such things. "There's a storm coming Thursday. Tie down your trellises. The old block on the south slope — Astrid's block — it takes water differently than the rest. Your father knew that. Check the drainage tiles before the rain, not after."

"How do you know about the drainage tiles?"

He paused, one hand on the car door, and for a heartbeat he looked almost young, almost frightened, almost honest.

"Goodnight, Miss Whitlock," he said, and got in, and the taillights slid away down the county road until they were just two embers, then nothing.

I stood in the empty lot a long time, the cold coming up through my thin dress, turning his words over like stones. *You're going to learn things about me.* A warning, a riddle, a hook — and beneath all of it, absurdly, unmistakably, the tone of a man who knew my grandmother's vines the way I did.

Who talked about my land like it was already his to worry over.

Who had said *however it looks* — as if appearance and truth were different documents, and he'd signed both.

I drove home with the windows down and the night rushing past, and somewhere between the hall and the vineyard gate I realized I was composing a letter in my head — the kind of letter you write to someone who knows you, someone safe, someone who has never once lied to you —

and I had no idea, yet, who that person was.

But Thursday was coming, and the storm with it, and Elias Marchetti would be at my gate with papers in hand when it broke.
