## 16. The Father's Ultimatum

The call came at six in the morning, which in Marco Marchetti's world was not early but punctual.

Theo was still asleep, or something adjacent to sleep—the shallow, restless kind where you dream in zoning maps—and the phone buzzed against the nightstand like an insect with a grudge. He knew before he answered. There are only so many reasons a man calls his son at dawn, and none of them begin with *I'm proud of you*.

"Theo." His father's voice had that terrible calm it developed before storms. "My office. Nine o'clock."

"It's Saturday."

"Yes," said Marco Marchetti, as if this were a point in his favor.

The line went dead. Theo lay staring at the ceiling of the guest room in the villa—his childhood room, technically, though it had been redecorated twice since he'd left and now resembled a hotel that suspected its guests. Somewhere below, the housekeeper was starting coffee. Somewhere beyond that, the vineyard rows caught the first light, the grapes going soft and dark in the way they did in these last weeks before harvest.

His mother's grapes. Planted by her hands, or at least under her direction, back when the vineyard had been a dream instead of collateral.

He got up. He showered. He put on the shirt his father liked—the blue one, the one Marco once called *executive*—and he hated himself a little for doing it, for reaching even now for approval like a reflex he couldn't unlearn.

Downstairs, the kitchen smelled of espresso and nothing else. His father didn't cook. Hadn't, Theo suspected, since the funeral, when cooking had been one of the many things that belonged to Elena Marchetti and therefore had to be packed away with the rest of her.

"Nine o'clock," Theo reminded him anyway, pouring his own coffee.

His father looked up from his tablet. For a moment—one strange, flickering moment—he looked tired. Old, even. Then the armor came down over his face, smooth as poured concrete, and he returned to his screen.

"I know what time it is," Marco said. "I've known what time everything is since before you were born."

* * *

The office above the winery had been his mother's tasting room once. Theo could still see the ghost of it if he squinted—the long oak table where she'd hosted neighbors, the shelf where she'd kept the bottles too imperfect to sell but too loved to pour out. Now there were two monitors, a leather chair, and framed renderings of the Marchetti Commons development: glass, steel, a plaza with a fountain no one would sit near because fountains in plazas are always more concept than comfort.

His father stood behind the desk. Not sitting. Standing, arms crossed, which meant this was to be conducted like a sentencing.

"Do you know what I did this morning?" Marco asked.

"Slept, briefly."

"I called Alder County Records." His father lifted a printout from the desk. "And I found something interesting. The proxy votes. Four property owners on the café block changed their positions in the final week. Do you know how hard it is to flip four votes in a week, Theo? It requires phone calls. Visits. Promises." He set the paper down with terrible gentleness. "It requires someone who knows exactly which levers to pull, because he grew up pulling them."

Theo said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be either a lie or a confession, and he'd spent enough of his life doing both.

"You told them about the easement on the Delgado property," his father continued. "You told the Hendersons their lease had a renewal clause nobody noticed. You sat in my own meeting room and dismantled my acquisition strategy piece by piece, and then you came home and kissed my cheek at dinner."

"You weren't eating your salad," Theo said. "I was worried."

Something moved behind his father's eyes—not amusement, never quite amusement, but its distant cousin. It vanished immediately.

"Do you understand what you've done?"

"I do, actually. I think I understand it better than you do."

"What you've done," Marco said, stepping forward, voice low and level, "is cost this family eighteen months of negotiation. You've cost us the anchor tenants. You've cost us—" he stopped himself, jaw working, and when he resumed, his voice had dropped into something quieter and far more dangerous. "You've cost me the only thing I have left to build."

"There it is," Theo said softly. "*Me.* Everything is always what it costs *you*."

"Don't."

"No, let's say it plainly, since we're being executives this morning." Theo heard his own voice rise and didn't care. "You want to pave over the block where Mrs. Okafor has run her tailor shop for thirty years. Where Hazel—" he caught himself, a half-second too late.

His father's head tilted, slow and predatory. "Hazel."

"A person. A baker. She owns the café at the corner."

"The one who spoke at the zoning meeting." Marco's mouth curved without warmth. "The pretty one who made the council feel like villains. Yes. I remember her." He let the name hang there, letting Theo twist on it. "Interesting company you keep."

"She's nobody's company of yours."

"No. But she's somebody's company of yours, apparently. Enough that you'd burn eighteen months of work for her." His father straightened, and when he spoke again, the calm had returned, final and glacial. "Here's what happens now. You will take over as project lead. Publicly. You'll stand in front of the council and the press and you'll make this right—you'll rebuild every deal you torched, and you'll do it under your own name, so the town understands the Marchetti commitment isn't one man's stubbornness but a family's future."

"And if I don't?"

Marco walked to the window. Below them, the vineyard spread out in the morning haze, rows running downhill toward the river like lines on a page.

"The vineyard is leveraged," he said simply. "Has been since your mother got sick. The development is how we survive. If you won't lead it—if you insist on being a tourist in your own inheritance—then I'll sell. All of it. The vines, the cellar, the name. There's a hospitality group out of Sacramento that's been circling for a year. They'd love the label. They'd rip out the old blocks and plant what sells." He turned back. "Your mother's vines, Theo. That's the price of your conscience."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the old building ticked and settled, the way it always had, the way it used to sound like breathing when his mother worked late below.

"You'd sell her vineyard," Theo said. His voice came out strange. Flat. "To punish me."

"To save it," his father said. "That's the difference between us. You think love means protecting things from change. I know it means doing the ugly arithmetic so there's something left to protect." He picked up the printout, squared its edges against the desk, precise as ever. "You have until Monday. Lead the project, or sign the sale authorization. Either way, you'll finally choose something instead of hiding in letters you're too cowardly to sign."

Theo went cold all over. "What did you say?"

"Did you think I didn't know?" His father almost smiled. "The library program. Your mother did the same thing when we first moved here—wrote to strangers because she hadn't found her people yet. I recognized the handwriting on the outgoing mail months ago. I just wondered how long you'd take to make it matter." He handed Theo a folder—the Sacramento offer, thick with legalese. "Monday, Theo. Nine o'clock. Bring me an answer, not an excuse."

* * *

He didn't go back to the villa. He drove instead to the far end of the property, past the modern crush pad and the stainless tanks, to the old stone building half-swallowed by blackberry bramble at the vineyard's edge: his mother's winery.

It had been closed for eleven years. The county had condemned the roof after the storm of '19, and his father had never repaired it, because repairing it would mean admitting it existed, and admitting it existed would mean admitting she had built something here that wasn't part of his plan.

Theo shouldered through the brambles, wincing as thorns raked his forearms, and pulled open the door with its hinges screaming like something waking up.

Inside, the dark smelled of dust and oak and time. He found the light switch out of pure muscle memory—his hand knew this room better than his head did—and one bare bulb stuttered to life overhead, illuminating rows of barrels gone gray with age, a crushing trough furred with cobwebs, and the chalkboard on the wall where his mother had tracked each vintage in her looping, joyful hand.

*Elena's Block — pick Thursday if the rain holds off.*
*Ask Theo if he wants to stomp grapes this year (he says yes every year).*

He stood in front of that last line for a long time.

He'd been nine when she wrote it. Maybe ten. He remembered the stomping—cold must between his toes, purple to the knees, his mother laughing so hard she had to hold onto the barrel staves. He remembered thinking, with the absolute certainty of a child, that this was what his life would be. This room. These rows. Her.

Then she got sick, and the arithmetic started, and his father did the ugly math until there was nothing left of her anywhere except a leveraged vineyard and a son trained to inherit the debt.

Theo lowered himself onto an overturned crate beside the crushing trough and put his face in his hands.

This was rock bottom, he supposed. He'd read about it in novels—in *her* letters, actually, the anonymous correspondent who wrote about debt the way other people wrote about weather, briskly, bravely, cataloging catastrophe like ingredients. *Flour, sugar, salt, ruin.* He'd admired her for it. He'd fallen for her through it, honestly, helplessly, one envelope at a time. And now he sat in his mother's ruined winery with a choice between betraying a town and losing the last living thing his mother had touched, and he understood, finally, what Hazel had been trying to teach him all along:

There is no version of this where you get to be clean.

His phone buzzed. Priya—no. It was a number he didn't know, and then a text from the library's pen-pal coordinator, cheerful and oblivious: *Reminder! Final letters can be exchanged anonymously OR you may include contact info. No pressure either way! Happy writing! 📬*

He almost laughed. Almost.

Instead he opened the folder from Sacramento and read the first page without seeing a word of it. Then he took out his notebook—the one he'd started carrying everywhere, the one filled with drafts of letters he'd never send—and clicked his pen.

*Dear Wren,* he wrote, using the handle she'd chosen, though by now he suspected she knew. By now he suspected a great many things, including that she'd tested him with a false detail weeks ago and he'd walked straight into it like a fool in love, which was, he supposed, accurate.

*I'm writing to you from the floor of a ruined building, which feels appropriate.*

He told her everything. About the ultimatum. About the leverage, the sale, the threat dressed up as salvation. About his mother's chalkboard and the line that had undone him. He wrote until his hand cramped and the bulb overhead buzzed and the night outside went fully dark, and when he reached the end, he wrote the only true sentence left in him:

*I don't know yet what I'm going to do Monday. But I know I'm done choosing between the people I love and the person I am. One of those things has to survive, and for the first time in my life, I've decided it won't be the inheritance.*

He signed it with his full name. *Theodore Elias Marchetti.* Then, after a moment, he crossed it out.

Not yet. Not in a letter.

Tomorrow, in front of everyone—at the harvest festival eve gathering, where the whole town would be watching—he'd say it out loud.

He folded the letter into his jacket, looked up at his mother's chalkboard one last time, and answered the question she'd written for a ten-year-old boy fifteen years ago.

"Yeah, Mom," he said to the empty cellar. "I want to stomp grapes this year."

Outside, the brambles scraped at his sleeves as he pushed through, and somewhere across town, in a dark apartment above a dark café, a woman was probably awake too, holding an envelope of her own.
