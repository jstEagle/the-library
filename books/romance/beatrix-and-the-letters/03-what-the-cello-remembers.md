## 3. What the Cello Remembers

Ada Reyes had survived three conservatory professors, one disastrous marriage, and a decade of New York apartment neighbors, and she had learned this: you could tell everything about a person from their hands.

She was watching the baker's hands now, and they were wrong.

Not wrong the way a broken thing is wrong—wrong the way a locked door is wrong, wrong the way a question is wrong when it has been deliberately left unanswered. The woman—Kestrel, she'd said, a name like a bird that lived in storms—moved through her small kitchen with the economy of someone who had memorized every inch of it. She kneaded dough with a rhythm so practiced it had become something else entirely, something closer to breathing. But her hands. Ada had spent her life around musicians, and hands were the first thing she noticed about anyone, the way other people noticed shoes.

Bakers had calluses on the pads of the fingers, on the heels of the palms, burn marks along the wrists from oven racks. Ada had seen a thousand such hands in a thousand small towns, hands that told stories of flour and heat and early mornings.

Kestrel Lockridge's fingertips were callused in a way that no oven had ever caused. Hard, polished ridges on the very tips of the left-hand fingers, four of them, in a neat descending line. And the way she held her wrists—loose, floating, even while working dough, even while exhausted—was a way of holding them that had to be trained into a body over years, over thousands of hours, over a devotion so total it reshaped the person carrying it.

Ada knew that devotion. She had it herself. It was the reason she was currently stranded in a blizzard in a town she couldn't find on a map, competing for a commission that would either resurrect her career or bury it.

"So," Ada said, leaning her elbows on the counter, "how long have you played?"

The kneading stopped for exactly one beat. Then resumed.

"Played?" Kestrel didn't look up.

"Piano. Guitar. Whatever it is." Ada kept her voice light, conversational, the way you'd toss a ball to someone you suspected might not catch it. "Those calluses on your left hand. Fingertips only. That's strings. Cello, I'd guess—the spacing's too wide for violin, and violists hold their hands like they're apologizing for something."

The dough received a particularly violent fold.

"I bake," Kestrel said. "It's rough on the hands."

"Mm." Ada watched her. The woman had a face built for hiding—plain at first glance, until you caught the architecture underneath, the cheekbones, the eyes that were the gray of a lake before ice. She was beautiful the way winter was beautiful: you had to be willing to stand in it. "My mother was a seamstress. Her hands were ruined by the work. Calluses everywhere, scars from pins and shears. Work leaves marks everywhere it touches. But yours—" she gestured with her own hand, an open palm, an offering, "—yours are marked in exactly four places. Fingertips. Left hand only. That's not work. That's *practice*. That's someone who pressed strings for hours a day, years a day."

The oven ticked. Outside, the wind threw snow against the windows in fistfuls, like a child demanding attention.

"I think your bread's burning," Kestrel said.

It wasn't. Ada let her have it anyway.

* * *

The blizzard had swallowed the world sometime in the night, and by morning it showed no intention of giving anything back. The power had flickered twice and held. The bakery's little apartment above the shop had given up its bed and blankets to the guests, and now the three of them occupied the front of house like survivors of a shipwreck who had washed ashore on the same improbable island.

Julian Voss was pacing by the front window, phone held aloft in the universal posture of the modern stranded, as though he could intimidate a cell tower into existence through sheer force of will. Ada watched him over the rim of her tea and felt the old, familiar heat of rivalry bank itself in her chest.

They had met exactly twice before this weekend—once at a festival in Helsinki where his orchestra had programmed her chamber work and he had conducted it beautifully and then, in the reception afterward, referred to it as "a promising early effort" to a donor within her hearing; and once in the offices of the Meridian Foundation, where they had both been shortlisted for the Harrow Commission, a hundred and fifty thousand dollars, a premiere with a major orchestra, a name in the program that would outlive them both. They had shaken hands. They had smiled. They had each privately calculated the other's obituary.

"Still nothing," Julian said, lowering the phone. "The roads are closed through the pass. Train's suspended. They're saying forty-eight hours, minimum."

"Tragic," Ada said. "However will you survive without an audience."

His jaw tightened, a small satisfying motion. "I'm trying to be gracious about this, Reyes."

"Are you. I hadn't noticed."

"I'm trying," he repeated, with the strained patience of a man reciting his own resolutions, "to be gracious. We're guests. In her home." He nodded toward the kitchen, where Kestrel was pulling a tray of rolls from the oven, and something in his face shifted—softened, went almost boyish. "She didn't have to take us in."

"No," Ada agreed. "She didn't."

And there it was again, that flicker. Julian Voss was many things—brilliant, vain, ruthless in the bloodless way that ambitious men are ruthless—but he was not, as far as Ada knew, a sentimental man. Yet he had been looking at their host all morning with an expression Ada couldn't quite name. Recognition, maybe. Or the ache of almost-recognition, the tip-of-the-tongue torment of a face you knew you knew.

Ada had her own theories about faces. She had spent the evening cataloguing their rescuer: the way she moved, the way she flinched—not visibly, never visibly, but in the set of her shoulders—whenever Julian spoke above a certain volume. The way she had gone very still, the first night, when Julian had mentioned, in passing, in that way men mention the centers of their own universes, that he was in town for the Harrow Commission announcement. The way she had said, "Congratulations," in a voice with nothing in it at all, a voice like a room with the furniture removed.

And the hands. Always the hands.

* * *

In the afternoon, the storm thickened until the windows went blue-gray and the world beyond the glass simply stopped existing. Ada found Kestrel in the back room, restocking flour with the focused intensity of a woman who would rather organize sacks of milled wheat than make conversation.

Ada picked up a broom. "Put me to work. I'll go insane otherwise. I compose. Unstructured time is my natural predator."

"You don't have to—"

"I will alphabetize your spice rack. I will do it badly. I will put the cumin with the cinnamon and you will never forgive me. Save us both."

Something happened at the corner of Kestrel's mouth. Not a smile. The ghost of where a smile used to live, like a dent in a pillow long after the head has gone. "The cumin's already next to the cinnamon," she said. "My system is my own."

"Monstrous," Ada said, and began sweeping.

They worked in silence for a while, and Ada let the silence do its work. She had learned this from her mother, too: you didn't drag a secret out of a person. You swept beside them until the secret got bored of being carried.

"You're from the city," Kestrel said at last. It wasn't a question.

"Born there. Escaped to Helsinki for a while. Came back. The city's like an ex you keep sleeping with because at least you know where everything is."

The ghost-smile again, stronger this time. "And the commission? The Harrow? That's a big deal, isn't it."

"It's the deal." Ada leaned on the broom. "Every five years, one composer, one major work. It's the difference between being a name people argue about and being a name people remember. Julian's been circling it for a decade. So have I." She paused. "You know who he is, then. You recognized the name."

"Everyone in music knows who Julian Voss is."

"You said that like a woman reading from a script."

Kestrel set down the flour sack with great care, as though it were an instrument being cased. "What do you want me to say, Ada?"

"The truth. Whatever version of it you can afford." Ada held her gaze. "I noticed your hands the first hour I was here. I've been a musician my whole life—I know what they look like when they've been trained, and I know what they look like when they've been *kept*. Yours are kept. Like something in a drawer that someone takes out and holds at night and puts back before morning." She softened her voice, gentled it, the way she'd approach a stray. "I'm not asking you to perform. I'm not even asking you to explain. I'm asking because I've spent thirty years around people who gave everything up, and not one of them ever looked at their hands the way you do. Like they're someone else's. Like they're evidence."

The room went very quiet. The storm pressed against the walls. And Kestrel Lockridge looked at Ada with an expression that was not shyness—Ada saw that now, saw it with a small cold shock—something older and more armored than shyness, something that had been built deliberately, brick by brick, over years. A survival structure. A hiding place with load-bearing walls.

"You're wrong about me," Kestrel said quietly. "I'm just a baker."

"Of course," Ada said, and meant: *I see you, and I will keep seeing you, and when you're ready, I'll be here.*

She went back to sweeping. Behind her, she heard Kestrel's breath come out slowly, the way a person breathes when they've been holding it for twelve years and have just decided to hold it a little longer.

* * *

It was near midnight when Ada came downstairs for water and found the bakery dim, one lamp burning, and Julian Voss standing in the back room with a box in his hands and a look on his face that she had never seen on him in ten years of professional acquaintance.

He looked like a man reading his own autopsy.

The box was old, cardboard gone soft at the corners, stuffed with envelopes. Dozens of them. Scores of them. And even from the doorway, even upside down, Ada could read the name inked on the topmost one in a hand she now recognized—the same hand that had written the label on the flour sacks, the same hand that had pressed strings for years a day:

*J. Voss.*

"Julian," she said.

He didn't hear her. He was staring at the letter in his hand, at the signature at the bottom of the page, and his lips were moving around a name, and Ada—sharp-tongued, observant, wrong about so many things and right about this one—felt the whole weekend tilt beneath her feet like a floor becoming a stage.

"Julian," she said again. "Who is she?"

He looked up at her, and his face was gray as the storm.

"Kestrel Lockridge," he said. "She's Kestrel Lockridge."
