## 16. Beatrix's Own Ruin

The hotel in Lisbon had a mirror in the elevator, and Beatrix Vale had spent four years avoiding it.

It was a small vanity of hers—she took the stairs whenever she could, told herself it was for her health, her discipline, the same way she told herself she'd come to Portugal for the light. But on the night everything ended, the elevator was broken anyway, and so she climbed eleven floors with a cardboard box against her hip and arrived at her own door breathing hard, and there was no mirror anywhere to catch what her face did when she set the box down and understood that it was lighter than it should have been.

She had left things behind on purpose. That was the trick of leaving—you could make it look like tidiness.

* * *

Twelve years was a long time to be away from somewhere, and not nearly long enough to become someone else.

She had learned this slowly, the way you learn a language badly—through embarrassment, through repetition, through small daily failures that added up to fluency in nothing at all. Geneva first, because Geneva had a job: a restoration consultancy that wanted someone young and hungry and unattached, someone who could be sent to Venice or Vienna or Vilnius at a week's notice without anyone asking inconvenient questions about roots. She had been all of those things. Unattached most of all. She had worn it like a coat with the tags still on, proof that she had chosen it, proof that leaving Aldermoor had been an act of will rather than an act of flight.

Her mother had cried at the airport. Her father had shaken Wren Eastwick's hand two weeks later at the funeral of old Mrs. Prewitt and said nothing, which was its own kind of verdict. And Beatrix had sat in a departure lounge reading the same paragraph of a novel for forty minutes, waiting for the grief to hit, waiting for the great rending she felt she owed him—the man who had put her on a train and told her not to wait—and when it didn't come, when what came instead was a strange weightless anger, she had decided that was her answer. She had been right to go. He had made it easy. The train had been his idea; the distance had been his gift; and she would spend the rest of her life refusing it gracefully.

That was the story she told. Stories are load-bearing structures. You don't inspect them if you want to keep standing.

* * *

Geneva gave her competence. Competence was a mercy—it filled the hours, it paid the rent, it gave her hands something to do besides shake. She became very good at saving buildings that belonged to other people. Fourteenth-century facades, baroque staircases, a convent library in Coimbra where the mold had gotten into the vellum and she had worked seventy-hour weeks for a year to pull three hundred manuscripts back from silence. Photographs of her work went into brochures. Her name went into footnotes. At conferences, men older than her father quoted her methodology back to her without attribution, and she learned to smile in a way that cost her nothing because it reached nothing.

It was in Coimbra that she met Tomás.

He was an architect, or he had been one once, before the commissions dried up and he reinvented himself as a consultant who talked beautifully about adaptive reuse while doing very little adapting or reusing. He had dark hair going silver at the temples and a laugh that made rooms lean toward him, and when he found out she was English he performed delight—*an Englishwoman who saves Portuguese books, how marvelous, how improbable*—and she, starved as she was for someone to look at her like she was remarkable rather than merely reliable, mistook the performance for sight.

They married in the second year. It rained, which everyone said was lucky, and she believed them because believing was easier than examining why the rain felt like a warning.

Here is what she would tell no one, not the solicitor, not the friends she collected and shed in equal measure across three countries, not even herself on the bad nights: she married Tomás because he was easy to leave. Some part of her, some cold bookkeeping part, had run the numbers. A marriage to a charming, feckless, fundamentally unserious man required nothing of her heart. It was a house with no foundations—nothing to crack when the weather turned. She had already lost the one love that would have demanded everything, and she had learned from Wren Eastwick that demand was the prelude to abandonment. So she chose a man who would never ask her to stay, and then, when he eventually asked her to stay, she discovered she had been wrong about him after all, and the discovery broke something she hadn't known was still whole.

Because Tomás, in the end, wanted children. Wanted a home. Wanted her *present*—not her salary, not her prestige, not the impressive wife at the dinner parties, but her, at a table, eating dinner, answering questions about her day. And Beatrix stood in the kitchen of their apartment above the Baixa with a glass of wine she wasn't drinking and realized that she did not know how to be present. She had spent twelve years practicing absence. She could restore a six-hundred-year-old wall but she could not sit across from another human being and let them see the state of the interior.

"You are always halfway out the door," Tomás said, not cruelly. That was the worst of it—he said it sadly, the way you'd note a structural flaw in a building you loved. "Even when you are in the room, querida. Even then."

She could have fought for it. People did. There were counselors and conversations and second chances, whole industries devoted to the rescue of marriages. But fighting meant staying, and staying meant being seen, and being seen meant telling him about Aldermoor, about the orchard, about a boy with ink-stained fingers who had kissed her under a tree they'd both pretended was theirs, and about the morning she woke to find him gone and a letter that said *don't wait* in handwriting she'd once traced with her fingertip like a blind woman reading a face.

She couldn't do it. So she did what she always did. She left first, cleanly, with the boxes labeled and the accounts separated, and she let him keep the friends, and she flew to Lisbon for a job that didn't exist yet, and she told herself the marriage had failed for ordinary reasons that happened to ordinary people every day.

The divorce papers came on a Tuesday. She signed them in a café with a pen the waiter lent her, and her hand did not shake, and she thought: *there. Proof. Nothing in me can be broken by losing anything anymore.*

It took her three more years to understand that this was not strength. This was the ruin itself, dressed well.

* * *

Lisbon hollowed her out politely.

The career flourished—that was the trap of it. Promotions, publications, a keynote in Rotterdam where eight hundred people applauded a woman who went back to her hotel room afterward and ate a room-service sandwich standing up because sitting down alone at a table felt like a confession. She restored a palace, a lighthouse, a chapel whose frescoes had been whitewashed by some frightened priest in 1755. She was, by any external measure, a success. By any internal measure, she was a building with the façade propped up by scaffolding, and the scaffolding was work, and work does not hold forever.

Her mother's illness arrived the year she turned thirty-four, quietly, the way illness does—first a tiredness blamed on age, then a word spoken carefully over the phone (*lymphoma*, said the way you'd say *subsidence*, as though naming it gently might limit the damage), then the long arithmetic of treatments
