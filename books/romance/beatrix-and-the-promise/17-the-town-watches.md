## 17. The Town Watches

The trouble with a small town, Wren thought, was that it had no skin. Everything that happened in Aldermoor sank straight through to the bone of it, and stayed there, and was passed around like a coin too old to spend.

He'd known this for twelve years. He had built his whole life on knowing it—on being the man who kept his head down, who wound the shop's clock and swept its step and answered every question about himself with a question about someone else's book. Invisibility wasn't cowardice, he'd told himself all these years. It was courtesy. It was keeping the peace.

By Thursday morning, the peace had been spent.

It started, as everything did now, with the orchard. Mrs. Prewitt from the bakery had seen them pruning on Saturday—Wren up a ladder, Beatrix below with the saw, Ivy running figure-eights between the rows—and by Sunday the story had grown a second act: they were *together*, they had been together all along, and the twelve years in between were either a lovers' quarrel or a scandal, depending on which kitchen the story landed in.

Wren heard the first version at his own counter. Mr. Hobbes, who came in every Tuesday for westerns and never bought anything else, set down his coin and said, too casually, "Heard you've got company out at Vale's old place."

"Co-owner," Wren said. "Legally speaking."

"Legal's one word for it." Hobbes collected his paperback, tipped an imaginary hat, and left the sentence hanging in the shop like smoke.

* * *

By Wednesday it was worse, because it had reached the schoolyard, and what reached the schoolyard reached Ivy.

She came home with her collar crooked and her face doing the thing it did when she was trying very hard to be fine—the same tight brightness her mother used to wear, God help him—and she set her satchel down with exaggerated care and said, "Papa, is Beatrix your sweetheart?"

The kettle was just coming to the boil. Wren stood very still and let the question sit there, ticking.

"Who told you that?"

"Tom Fenwick." She examined her shoelace. "He said you left her at the train station twelve years ago and she went away crying and everybody knows it. He said—" Ivy's voice thinned, and she pushed through it the way she pushed through everything, chin first, "—he said that's why I don't have a mama, because you ruin things you love."

The kettle screamed. Wren didn't move toward it. He crossed the little kitchen instead and crouched in front of his daughter, whose eyes were wet and furious and eight years old, and felt something in his chest go from cold to molten in the space of one breath.

"Look at me," he said. "Tom Fenwick doesn't know anything. Not one true thing."

"He knew about the train station."

And there it was—the past, not knocking this time but walking straight through the front door, into his kitchen, into his daughter's mouth. He had spent twelve years believing that if he kept quiet enough, small enough, the old story would stay buried where he'd left it. He had never once considered that the story didn't belong to him alone. That it had edges, and people standing on them, and that one day it would reach out and touch the person he loved most in the world.

"That part might be true," Wren said quietly, because he had decided long ago he would never lie to her, even when the truth was a stone in both their hands. "I did say goodbye to someone at a station once. And it was the worst thing I've ever done, and I've been sorry for it every single day since. But I have never—not once—ruined anything I love. Do you understand me? Whatever else is wrong with your papa, that isn't."

Ivy's lip wobbled. Then, being Ivy, she asked the question that mattered: "Do you love her? Beatrix?"

The kettle shrieked again. Outside, rain had started against the window, soft as static.

"I'm going to make tea," Wren said, which was not an answer, and they both knew it, and her face closed a little as she turned toward the stairs.

That night he stood in her doorway after she'd gone to sleep and watched the rise and fall of her breathing and understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own verdict, that invisibility had a price and the bill had finally come due. He could bear being the town's cautionary tale. He had borne it for years; he'd earned it, hadn't he, in his own accounting. But Ivy had earned nothing. Ivy was paying for a story she'd never been told, in a currency she couldn't count yet.

He could not keep her safe by staying small. Staying small was how Tom Fenwick's version got to be the only version.

* * *

Thursday brought the regulars, and the regulars brought sides.

It was strange, Wren thought, watching them filter in out of the drizzle—strange and a little terrible—to realize that his shop had become a parliament. Old Nan Corrigan took her usual armchair by the poetry shelf and announced to the room, in the voice she used for announcing deaths and good pie, "Beatrix Vale came into the bakery Tuesday. Bought a loaf and stayed an hour talking to my niece like she'd never left. Good blood, that girl. Always was." Which was, in the dialect of Aldermoor, a declaration of war on behalf of Beatrix.

Mr. Quill, retired schoolmaster, defender of lost causes, countered from the history section: "A woman comes back after twelve years and everyone assumes the man must be at fault. Perhaps she left *him*. Perhaps there are things we don't know." He looked directly at Wren when he said it, and Wren pretended to alphabetize a stack of maps.

Hobbes, back again two days running for the first time in years, planted himself at the counter and delivered the neutral position: "Orchard's worth money now. That's all this is. Thorne's sniffing round it, everyone knows. Rest is village entertainment."

At the name Thorne, something shifted in the room—a collective stiffening, subtle as weather. Everyone in Aldermoor knew what Marcus Thorne's interest meant. The man bought things the way rot buys wood: slowly, politely, and then all at once.

"Entertainment," Nan Corrigan repeated, witheringly. "Is that what it was, Wren? Twelve years ago?"

And there it was. The direct question, in his own shop, with the bell over the door still trembling from the last customer's exit. Every eye in the room found a way to look at him without looking at him.

Wren set down the maps. His heart was going hard, but his voice, when it came, surprised him by being steady.

"It was the worst mistake of my life," he said. "I hurt her badly, and I've never told anyone why, because the why is mine to carry and hers to hear first. That's all any of you get until I've given her more than that." He picked the maps back up. "Now. Did anyone actually come in to buy a book today, or shall I start charging rent for the chairs?"

A silence. Then Quill laughed—one short bark—and asked after a biography of Nelson, and the parliament dissolved back into customers, but something had changed in the room's air, and Wren knew it had changed in him too. He had spoken. Publicly. He had drawn a line around the truth and posted a guard at its edge, and the sky had not fallen.

Nan Corrigan paused at the door on her way out, small and sharp-eyed under her wool hat. "About time," she said. "You've been a ghost in this town for twelve years, Wren Eastwick. Ghosts don't get to keep the girl."

Then she was gone into the rain, leaving him staring at the door.

* * *

Friday, he walked Ivy to school.

It was not their custom—he usually let her run ahead with the other children while he lingered at the corner like a man casing a bank—but today he walked her all the way to the gate, hand in hand, under an umbrella that was really too small for two. The mothers on the wall watched. Let them watch.

At the gate, Tom Fenwick materialized the way boys do, all elbows and certainty, flanked by two lieutenants. "Your dad's the one who—" he began, and Wren crouched down to the boy's level, umbrella dripping between them, and looked him in the eye.

"Tom," he said, calmly, "the story you've been telling isn't yours. It belongs to other people, and some of it's wrong, and all of it hurts someone standing right here." He nodded at Ivy. "If I hear it again, I won't be angry. I'll just be disappointed, and I'll tell you so in front of everyone, and you'll wish I'd been angry instead. Fair warning."

Tom's ears went red. One of the lieutenants snickered, which finished him. He muttered something and peeled away, and Ivy looked up at her father with an expression he'd never seen on her before—something between awe and suspicion, as if he'd turned out to be a character from one of his own shelves.

"You told him off," she said.

"I defended our family."

"Our family," she repeated, testing the words. Her eyes went wide with her own audacity. "Does that mean Beatrix is—"

"Bell's going," Wren said, standing, pointing at the schoolhouse clock, retreating with what dignity remained. "Go on. Learn things."

She went, but she looked back twice, and the second time she was smiling.

* * *

He meant to go straight back to the shop. Instead his feet took him the long way, past the market square, past the noticeboard where a fresh flyer curled in the damp—**THORNE DEVELOPMENTS: A MODERN FUTURE FOR ALDERMOOR, PUBLIC CONSULTATION, TUESDAY 7PM**—and on, without his permission, to the lane that led to the orchard.

Beatrix was there, of course. She seemed to live in the gaps of his days now, appearing wherever he wasn't looking. She was at the gate with a clipboard and a frown, counting something—trees, losses, maybe years—and when she saw him she lowered the clipboard, wary.

"This isn't our workday," she said.

"I know." Rain dripped off the umbrella. He realized he had no speech prepared, no plan beyond the fact of her, and pressed on anyway. "Ivy came home yesterday with a story. A boy at school told her I ruined you. That it's common knowledge." He watched the color leave her face and return, quickly, rearranged. "I've spent twelve years letting this town believe whatever it liked about me because silence felt like penance. But it's not penance if other people pay it. And she shouldn't pay it. Neither should you."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked on the gate's iron scrollwork—the gate whose key still hung on his ring, though neither of them had mentioned that in weeks.

"What did you tell her?" Beatrix asked finally.

"The truth. Or the shape of it. That I hurt you, and I'm sorry, and the reasons are mine to give you first." He met her eyes. "Which means I need to give them to you. Soon. Before this town does it for me, badly."

Something moved behind her expression—fear, or hope, or the place where those two share a border. "Wren—"

"Not today," he said. "I'm not ready today. But I will be. And until then—" he lifted the umbrella higher, offering half of it, an absurd small shelter in all that grey weather, "—until then, I'm done hiding. If Aldermoor wants a story, it can watch us restore an orchard. It can watch us do it well. Let them talk about that for a change."

Beatrix studied him for a long time, rain gathering in the folds of her collar, and whatever she saw in his face made her step under the umbrella beside him—close enough that their shoulders touched, the first deliberate contact either had allowed in months.

"The consultation is Tuesday," she said. "Thorne's people. They'll be at the festival committee meeting too, sniffing around. If we're going to be watched, we should decide what they see."

"Partners," Wren said. "In everything the deed requires, and nothing it forbids."

Her mouth curved, barely. "Careful, Eastwick. That almost sounded like a plan."

They stood a moment longer in the rain, two people sharing one umbrella at the gate of everything they'd lost, and Wren felt the old fear shift inside him—not gone, nothing so kind as gone, but moved, reassigned, made smaller than the thing it had guarded. He had spent twelve years protecting himself from the town's eyes. Now he would stand in them, on purpose, for her and for Ivy, and let them see exactly what he was.

Above them, along the orchard's high hedge, a rook lifted off into the wet wind, and somewhere down the lane, a car engine slowed—too slow, idling, the particular predatory patience of a black Mercedes taking its measurements.

Thorne had started watching too.
