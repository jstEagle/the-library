## 6. Arrivals

The morning of the arrivals, Beatrix woke at five to the sound of the sea and lay there cataloguing everything that could go wrong.

It was a habit from her performing years—the pre-concert inventory. The hall's acoustics. The conductor's temperament. The state of her hands. She had done it for fifteen years, standing in green rooms with her cello between her knees like a shield, running through catastrophe after catastrophe until the actual concert arrived and proved, usually, to be survivable.

She had thought she'd left that habit behind with the career. Apparently not. Apparently it had only changed instruments.

*Delia will arrive first,* she thought, watching the ceiling lighten. *Delia always arrives first, because Delia believes arriving first is a form of winning. She'll have the fiancé with her—Preston? Prescott? Something with a P—and he'll be wearing linen that costs more than my car. Marcus will come late, or not at all, or late enough that not-at-all would have been kinder. And Aunt Wren—*

Aunt Wren didn't bear thinking about before coffee.

She got up, dressed in the dark so as not to wake Cyrus in the room across the hall, and went down to the kitchen, where she stood at the counter buttering toast with the focus of a woman defusing something explosive.

"You're going to wear a hole in that floor," said Mrs. Pell, who had cooked at the inn since Beatrix was nine and who considered herself exempt from all boundaries of employment.

"I'm fine."

"You've walked the same line between the stove and the icebox eleven times. I counted." Mrs. Pell cracked eggs into a bowl without looking up. "Your grandmother used to do the same thing before every family dinner. Right there. Same track. She said pacing was how she prayed."

"Grandmother didn't pray. Grandmother *planned.*"

"Same thing, in that kitchen." Mrs. Pell nodded toward the window, where the morning fog was peeling back off the water. "He coming down soon, your young man?"

Beatrix's hand paused on the toast knife. *Your young man.* Two days of practice and the phrase still landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there, warm and inconvenient.

"He sleeps like the dead," she said. "Lighthouse keepers' hours, he says. Up half the night, useless till ten."

"And you've been up since when?"

"That's different. That's nerves."

"Mm." Mrs. Pell slid a plate across the counter—eggs, toast, a rasher of bacon she hadn't been asked for and wouldn't apologize for. "Eat. You can't lie to that family of yours on an empty stomach. Takes fuel."

Beatrix ate because arguing with Mrs. Pell was a losing campaign with a long history of casualties. The toast helped. Everything helped slightly less than she needed it to.

The plan, such as it was, had been rehearsed on the porch two nights ago: they'd met at the farmers' market in June—no, May, right after his boat's engine died and she'd helped him carry parts; they'd been together three months; no, four, long enough to be serious but not so long that the family would ask why they hadn't met him sooner. He liked sailing and old boats and cooking badly. She'd taught him nothing about music yet but planned to. They were taking it slow. They were happy.

It had sounded thin on the porch, in the dark, with the crickets going. It sounded thinner now, in daylight, with three cars' worth of family due by evening.

At nine-thirty, footsteps on the stairs—Cyrus, hair still damp, wearing the blue shirt she'd pointedly not noticed him buy in town. He looked, infuriatingly, calm.

"You're up early," he said.

"I'm always up early."

"You're up *anxious* early. There's a difference. Your shoulders are up around your ears." He poured himself coffee and leaned against the counter beside her, close enough that their elbows nearly touched—a thing they'd been doing more of lately, this casual nearness, as if two days of fiction had already worn grooves of habit. "What's the worst that happens?"

Beatrix stared at him. "You want the list?"

"I want the top item."

She took a breath. "Delia looks at you, then at me, and does that thing with her mouth—you know the thing—and says, 'Well. Isn't this convenient,' and somehow by dessert everyone believes we're lying, and by Sunday I've lost the inheritance and whatever dignity I had left, which wasn't much to begin with."

Cyrus considered this over the rim of his mug.

"All right," he said. "And what's the worst that happens if she does?"

"She—" Beatrix stopped. "I lose the inn."

"The inn isn't yours to lose. Not yet. Not alone." He set the mug down. "Here's what I know about your cousin, having met her exactly once, at the funeral, where she spent forty minutes telling me about her gallery: people like that are loud because they're afraid of quiet. You grew up in this house. You know its quiet better than anyone alive. That's worth more than any performance we put on today."

It was, Beatrix thought later, an absurdly good answer for a man who was being paid to be here. She pushed the thought away before it could grow teeth.

"Also," Cyrus added, "your shoulders are down now. So."

* * *

Delia arrived at noon precisely, in a white Range Rover too clean for the salt road, and Beatrix watched from the porch as it crunched up the drive with the slow, deliberate grandeur of a ship coming into harbor.

Her cousin emerged first—of course she did, letting Preston park—wearing cream linen and sunglasses pushed up into her hair like a crown she was saving for later. She was beautiful in the way that well-tended things are beautiful: pruned, watered, defended against weather. Behind her came Preston—*Preston*, Beatrix remembered with relief, Preston Ashcombe—who was tall and pleasant-faced and had the handshake of a man who practiced it.

"Beatrix." Delia kissed the air near both her cheeks. Her eyes were already moving past, scanning the porch, the yard, the windows. Cataloguing. "You look well. Rested. Coastal life agrees with you."

"It agrees with everyone until February."

"Mm." The sunglasses came down off her hair, not onto her eyes but into her hand, a prop deployed. "And where is he? Marcus texted that you'd brought someone. Marcus never texts good news, so naturally I assumed it was a joke."
