## 9. Grandmother's Letters

The attic of the Fairweather Inn smelled the way all attics should: of cedar and dust and the faint ghost of lavender sachets that had given up their fight against time sometime during the previous administration. Beatrix climbed the last stair with a flashlight in her teeth and a mental list in her head—twelve linen napkins, the good tablecloth, the one with the embroidered tide pattern that her grandmother had commissioned from a woman in Port Clyde who charged by the hour and delivered by the season.

Aunt Wren had issued the request at breakfast with the particular sweetness she reserved for tasks she wanted done properly. "The good linens, dear. In the cedar chest at the back. And do mind the floorboards near the north window; they've gone soft."

Beatrix had volunteered without thinking about it. That was the trouble with hiding inside a life—you ended up doing all the errands, because everyone assumed you had nowhere better to be.

She was not complaining, exactly. The attic was cool despite the August heat pressing on the roof, and there was a strange comfort in the clutter of it: her grandfather's fishing rods bundled like sleeping herons, boxes of Christmas ornaments labeled in her grandmother's upright hand, a dressmaker's mannequin wearing nothing but a cobweb shawl. Every object here had been loved once and then set down gently, which was more than most things could say.

The cedar chest sat where it always had, beneath the round window that looked out over the harbor. Beatrix knelt, worked the brass latch—it stuck, then surrendered with a click—and lifted the lid.

Mothballs and memory. She breathed it in before she could stop herself.

The linens were on top, wrapped in tissue paper, exactly as promised. Twelve napkins. The tablecloth with its embroidered tides, each wave stitched so finely that when you ran your fingers across it, you could almost feel the water moving. Her grandmother had been many things—a formidable cook, a merciless card player, a woman who could end an argument with one raised eyebrow—but above all she had been patient with beautiful work. She believed some things deserved to be made slowly.

Beatrix was folding the tablecloth into its tissue nest when her knuckles struck something hard at the bottom of the chest.

Not linens. A box. Rosewood, small enough to hold with two hands, with a brass clasp gone green at the edges. She knew it instantly, the way you know a face from childhood—the box that had lived on her grandmother's writing desk for as long as Beatrix could remember, the one her grandmother locked whenever the grandchildren came thundering up the stairs, not out of secrecy exactly, but out of ceremony. *Some things keep,* she used to say. *That's what locks are for.*

Beatrix sat back on her heels.

She should put it back. She knew that with the clarity of someone who had spent two years becoming very good at knowing which doors to leave closed. But her hands were already lifting the lid, and the latch gave way as if it had only been waiting for permission.

Inside, on a bed of yellowed cotton, lay seven envelopes.

Seven. One for each heir. Beatrix counted them twice, though counting was hardly necessary—she recognized the handwriting on the first envelope she touched, the strong forward slant of it, the way the capital B always looked like a sail catching wind.

*For Beatrix.*

Her name. Her grandmother's ink. Sealed with a dab of wax that had cracked along one edge but held.

Beneath hers, six more envelopes, each addressed in the same hand. *For Delia. For Marcus. For Wren. For Edmund.* Two more at the bottom whose names she couldn't read without shifting the others, and she didn't shift them. It felt like eavesdropping just to look.

And pinned to the inside of the lid, a note in her grandmother's hand, the paper soft with age:

*To be opened at the reunion's end. Not before. I mean this, and you know how I feel about meaning things.*

Beatrix laughed—a startled, unsteady sound that bounced off the rafters. Even dead two years, even sealed in rosewood, her grandmother could still issue an instruction that made you want to obey it and slam the door at the same time.

*You know how I feel about meaning things.*

Oh, Beatrix knew. She had been eleven years old, practicing scales with her ankles crossed on a kitchen chair because her feet didn't reach the pedals, when her grandmother had stood in the doorway listening and said, "Again. You rushed the third bar." And when eleven-year-old Beatrix had protested that nobody would ever notice, her grandmother had said, "I noticed. Play it again." She had meant it. Beatrix had played it again. This was the entire architecture of their love: built from demands that were also gifts.

She looked down at the envelope with her name on it.

It was heavier than the others.

That wasn't sentiment. She weighed Delia's envelope in her palm first—light, papery, a single sheet perhaps—and then Marcus's, similar, and then hers, which had a density to it, a gravity, as though something more than words lived inside. A second page. An enclosure. Something folded flat and kept.

Her heart did a thing she didn't authorize.

*To be opened at the reunion's end.*

The reunion had three days left. Three days of Delia's needling and Marcus's careful distance and Aunt Wren watching everything with those pale, patient eyes, and Cyrus—

Cyrus, who had kissed her in the shallows yesterday with salt water in his eyelashes and both hands cradling her face like she was something breakable, which was either very sweet or very wrong depending on what he actually saw when he looked at her. Cyrus, who agreed too easily to everything. Who had said *yes* to her ridiculous proposal before she'd finished offering it, the way a man says yes when he already knows the shape of the question.

She pushed the thought down where she kept the other thoughts she wasn't ready for.

The attic light shifted through the round window, going amber. Somewhere below, the house creaked through its afternoon routines—Marcus on the phone in the study, his voice low and clipped; Delia laughing at something her fiancé said, that laugh like breaking glass wrapped in silk; children chasing each other across the porch boards. And under all of it, faintly, the sea doing what the sea does, which is remind you that everything moves whether you consent or not.

Beatrix turned her envelope over. The wax seal caught the light.

What would her grandmother have written to her? To any of them? The will itself had been strange enough—that condition, *accompanied by a committed partner*, which had sent the whole family into a flutter of arrangements and accusations. Delia had produced a fiancé within the week, a pleasant, forgettable man named Graham whom Beatrix suspected had been hired by the hour, though she was in no position to throw stones, or indeed to be anywhere near glass houses, given the arrangement currently sleeping down the hall in the blue room, ostensibly because the mattress in the lighthouse cottage was broken.

*Ostensibly.* What a useful word. She'd been building her whole summer out of ostensibly.

But the letters were different. The will was law—her grandmother's lawyer had read it aloud in that dry, careful voice while the family sat rigid around the dining table—but the letters were whatever her grandmother had chosen to say when no one was making her say anything. The true will. The one written in ink instead of obligation.

And hers was heavier.

She thought about her grandmother's hands. Big hands for a small woman, capable hands, hands that had gutted fish and kneaded bread and, once, long ago according to family legend, slapped a rude conductor across the face at a benefit concert in Boston. Hands that had held Beatrix's face after the collapse—not at the hospital, not in the weeks of tabloid coverage afterward, but months later, here at the inn, one gray morning in October when Beatrix had come down for coffee and found her grandmother waiting with two cups and no questions.

"You don't have to tell me," she'd said. "But someday you'll have to tell yourself."

Beatrix hadn't known what she meant. She still didn't, entirely. But the sentence had followed her around for two years like a cat that wouldn't be fed elsewhere.

*Someday you'll have to tell yourself.*

Was that what waited in the envelope? Some version of that sentence, expanded, footnoted, final?

She became aware that she was holding the envelope to her chest, the way you hold something you're protecting or something that's protecting you, and she made herself lower it.

Don't open it. Not because of the instruction—though the instruction mattered, mattered enormously, because breaking faith with her grandmother was the one transgression she couldn't stomach—but because of what opening it might mean. Letters like this arrived at the ends of things. They were the last conversation, the closing argument. As long as it stayed sealed, her grandmother was still, in some stubborn grammatical sense, speaking. Still mid-sentence. Still about to say something.

Two years of silence had taught Beatrix the value of a sentence that hasn't ended yet.

She repacked the chest with hands that weren't quite steady. Linens first, tissue paper smoothed, napkins counted twice—twelve, yes, all present. Then the rosewood box, lid lowered, clasp clicked shut. She hesitated, then took her own envelope out again.

Just hers. Just to look at. That wasn't opening it. That was reconnaissance.

She carried it down the attic stairs balanced on her open palm like something volatile, and she was halfway to her room—planning to hide it in the drawer with her spare strings, a location she considered unimpeachable—when she rounded the corner past the linen closet and nearly collided with Cyrus.

He had paint on his forearms and sawdust in his hair and the particular expression of a man who has spent the day arguing with a window frame and lost. He steadied her elbow automatically, and the touch went through her like current.

"Whoa. You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Worse. Stationery." She said it lightly, and it almost worked, except Cyrus had always been able to hear the seam in her voice, the place where the performance stopped. He'd had that gift since conservatory, sitting second chair behind her, hearing every phrase before she finished shaping it.

His eyes dropped to the envelope. To her name on it. Back up to her face.

"That your grandmother's writing?"

"It is."

"She seal it herself?"

"Ages ago. They're in the will—everyone gets one at the end of the reunion." She turned it over in her fingers, buying time. "Mine's heavier than the others. Isn't that strange?"

Something moved across his face—quick, there and gone, like a fish turning under the surface. If she hadn't been watching for it she'd have missed it entirely. But she had been watching for it, lately, watching him the way you watch weather.

"Heavier how?" he asked.

"More pages. Or—" she shrugged, aiming for casual and landing somewhere short of it, "enclosures. Something extra. Grandmother never did anything without a reason. If she made my letter weigh more than Delia's, she wanted me to know it weighed more."

Cyrus was quiet a moment. Down the hall, a door closed; the house resettled.

"And you're not going to open it early?" he asked. "Peek?"

"I'm offended you'd suggest it."

"You're holding it like a live grenade."

"I'm holding it like a letter." She smiled, and it cost her something. "Grandmother said to wait. So I'll wait. Three days isn't so long."

"No," Cyrus said. "Three days isn't so long."

But he said it strangely—gently, with a weight underneath that she couldn't parse, the way he sometimes said ordinary things as though they had a second meaning he was carrying for her until she was ready to hold it herself. Like he knew something about those three days. Like he was counting them differently than she was.

He touched the envelope once, briefly, with one sawdust-rough finger, almost a salute.

"Whatever's in there," he said, "it was written by someone who loved you. Heavy or not."

Then he went on toward the stairs, paint and sawdust and easy stride, leaving Beatrix standing in the hallway with her heart beating oddly and her grandmother's last words sealed tight in her hands.

That night she hid the envelope beneath her spare cello strings, in the drawer that opened with a stick-and-a-half of resistance, and lay awake listening to the tide come in.

Heavy, she thought. Why heavy?

And why, when Cyrus had looked at it, had he looked away so fast—as if he'd already read it?
