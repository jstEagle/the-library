## 1. The Anonymous Correspondence Act

The notice went up on the community center corkboard on a Tuesday, which is the day most bad ideas are born, and by Thursday it had migrated to the café counter like a stray cat that had decided it lived here now.

*WINTER LETTERS: ISLAND PEN-PAL PROGRAM. COMBAT SEASONAL ISOLATION. ALL PARTICIPANTS MATCHED ANONYMOUSLY. FIRST LETTERS DUE FRIDAY.*

I read it twice while wiping down the espresso machine, which is the only appliance I've ever loved without reservation, and I said, out loud, to no one, "Absolutely not."

"Absolutely yes," said Marta Okafor, from the doorway of the kitchen, holding a clipboard the way other people hold weapons.

Marta owns this café. She also, three years ago, pulled me out of a snowbank outside the ferry terminal—literally, a snowbank, I had gotten off the boat with two suitcases and no plan and a face that I imagine looked like an unmade bed—and she fed me soup and gave me a job and never once asked a question I couldn't answer. This is the arrangement: she saved my life, and in exchange I run her kitchen and she pretends not to notice that I flinch at certain words. *Reception. Registry. Centerpiece.* Words that used to be my native language and are now, to me, what Latin is to most people: beautiful, dead, and best left buried.

"Read the flyer, Marta," I said. "It says *all participants*. I am not all participants. I am a specific participant, and that participant is declining."

"It's a good program. Doctor Lindqvist started it after last winter. Remember last winter?"

I remembered last winter. Last winter the island went dark for eleven days and I learned that I could go seventy-two hours without speaking to another human being before the silence started doing something architectural to me, building rooms inside my head that I did not want to live in.

"That was weather," I said. "This is correspondence. I can weather weather. Correspondence is a choice."

"Seb." She came behind the counter and took the cloth out of my hands, which is a violation of at least three of my personal bylaws. "You alphabetize the spice rack when you're lonely. I've seen you do it. You did it twice last week and once you did it backwards. Nobody alphabetizes backwards unless they're lonely."

"That was efficiency."

"That was a man writing letters to paprika."

I took the cloth back. "Who's it even for? The program. Who signs up for anonymous letters with strangers?"

"Forty-one people so far." She tapped the clipboard. "Retirees. The lighthouse keeper. Half the winter crew at the cannery. People who get quiet when the boats stop running. And you."

"I didn't sign up."

"You're signing up now. I signed you up Tuesday. You're 'S.' Your match is 'M.' First letters are due Friday, and Lindqvist collects them personally, and if there's a gap in the box she'll stand in my doorway and look at me with those eyes until I confess to crimes I haven't committed. She's very good at it. It's why she runs the program."

"Marta—"

"You'll write one letter. One. If you hate it, you tell me, and I'll tell Lindqvist your match fell through, and that'll be the end of it." She was already backing toward the door, because Marta knows that negotiations are won by leaving before the other party finishes his sentence. "Friday. The box is by the post office. And Seb—"

She paused in the doorway, and her voice did the thing it does maybe twice a year, the thing that isn't the boss voice or the friend voice but the third voice, the one that saved my life in a snowbank.

"Nobody's asking you to be anybody. That's the whole point of it. Anonymous. You write as 'S.' That's all. Just a letter."

Then she was gone, and I stood there holding a dishcloth and resenting her, which is how I spend a meaningful percentage of my time, and loving her, which is the rest of it.

* * *

Here is what Marta doesn't know, and what the island doesn't know, and what the man called Seb has spent three years making sure no one will ever know:

Seb is not my name. It is the top third of my name, amputated at the syllable, the way you'd shorten a rope you no longer trusted. The rest of it—the rest of it died in a courtroom ten years ago, in a room with bad lighting and a judge who wouldn't look at me, and I have never once said it aloud since, not even to myself, not even in the shower where men confess to things.

I was, in another life, very good at weddings. Not good the way a caterer is good, or a florist is good. Good the way a conductor is good. I could walk into a room with a bride's mother crying and a caterer quitting and a hurricane offshore, and within four hours the hurricane would be a footnote and the mother would be laughing and the flowers would look like they'd grown there on purpose. My name was on a door in the city. People waited eighteen months for me. I had a waiting list and a signature color and a reputation for being, as one magazine put it, "impossible to rattle."

Then the Halloran wedding happened, and something went wrong that I did not do, and I was the one holding the bag, because the bag had been handed to me by people who were very skilled at handing bags to other people. There was an investigation. There were headlines. There was a prosecutor whose name I learned the way you learn the name of weather—a thing that arrives and ruins the season and cannot be argued with. I lost the case. I lost the business. I lost the name. I very nearly lost the rest of it, too, and I won't go into the night I didn't, except to say that a woman with a clipboard and a bowl of soup has no idea what she interrupted, and I intend to keep it that way.

The prosecutor. I don't think of her often. That's not true—I think of her exactly as often as is structurally unavoidable, which is whenever anyone says the word *prosecutor*, or *testimony*, or on bad nights, *trust*. She stood up in that courtroom and she said my name—the whole name, the long one, the one with the syllables I've since amputated—and she said it in a voice like a verdict, and I have spent ten years making sure no one would ever say it like that again.

So no. I was not going to write letters to a stranger. Letters are how this starts. Letters are how a person becomes legible. I had spent a decade becoming illegible, and I was rather proud of the work.

* * *

Thursday night, after close, I found the flyer on the counter again, and under it, in Marta's handwriting, a note: *One letter. That's the deal. —M.*

I made tea. I do not drink the tea; I hold it, because holding something warm is a documented technique for calming the nervous system and also because it gives my hands a job other than shaking. I sat at the corner table, the one by the window where you can see the harbor, which was doing its winter thing, gray water hitting gray rocks with the enthusiasm of a debt collector.

One letter, I thought. I'll write something so boring she— he— *they*—will never write back. That was the strategy. I would be so aggressively uninteresting that 'M.' would file my letter under *correspondence, dead* and move on to the lighthouse keeper, who at least had a profession. I would write about the weather. I would write about bread. I would write one paragraph and sign it 'S.' and be done with the whole enterprise, and Marta could tell Lindqvist whatever she liked.

I got the good paper. This is a confession, so let me make it quickly: I keep good paper in the flat above the café, heavy cream stock, bought in the city in another life, and I have never had occasion to use it because good paper implies a recipient and I have not had one. I took out a sheet, and the weight of it in my hands did something to my chest that I decided to ignore.

*Dear M,* I wrote.

I stopped. Looked at it. *Dear* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. I crossed it out.

*M,* I wrote.

Better. Colder. I could work with cold.

I am going to reproduce the letter here, because it matters, because it is the first dishonest thing I ever said to a person I would come to love, and because the shape of the lie tells you everything about the shape of the man.

*M —*

*I've been told I have to write this, which you should know, since honesty is apparently the theme. I run a kitchen on this island. I bake bread at five in the morning because the schedule suits me and because the harbor at that hour is the only view I've ever seen that doesn't want anything from me. I am not a sociable man. I am writing this under protest, at the request of my employer, who has leverage over me in the form of wages and affection.*

*I don't know what one puts in a letter to a stranger. I assume you'll write back about your own weather, and I'll write back about mine, and we'll do this until the program ends in spring and we can both stop. If that suits you, fine. If it doesn't, I understand. Most things don't.*

*— S.*

I read it over. It was perfect. It was a locked door with a welcome mat, which is the specialty of the house. I signed it, folded it, and put it in an envelope, and I felt nothing, which I took as proof of my good judgment.

Then I sat there for another hour, holding the cold tea, looking at the harbor, and I did not get up to go to bed, and I could not have told you why. The letter was done. The obligation was met. And yet there was a feeling in the room, sitting across from me at the table like an uninvited guest, and it took me a while to name it, because I hadn't seen it in so long.

It was the feeling of having written to someone. Of having said *here is my weather, what's yours* and meaning, underneath it, in the sub-basement of the sentence, *is anyone there? Is anyone reading? Does anyone out there want to know?*

I had spent ten years making sure no one wanted to know. It turns out that a man can do a thing perfectly for ten years and still, on a Thursday night, with good paper and cold tea, want it undone.

I put the letter in my coat pocket, because I was not going to sleep anyway, and I walked down to the post office in the dark, and I dropped it in the box, and the sound it made—paper on paper, one small fall—was louder than it had any right to be.

I told myself it was nothing. I told myself I'd never think about it again.

I was already composing my answer to her reply before I reached the corner.

* * *

Friday, Marta found me re- alphabetizing the spice rack, front to back, like a normal person, and she looked at the post office box on her way in—the box by the door, the community one, where Lindqvist's collection envelope had been—and she saw it was empty, and she saw my face, which I had arranged into its usual expression of mild grievance, and she smiled the smile of a woman who has just won a bet with the universe.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"How was it?"

"It was a chore," I said, tying my apron. "It was a tax. I paid it. It's done."

"Mm," said Marta, tying her own apron, which is a smaller, more dignified apron, because she is the owner and understands hierarchy. "Lindqvist says your match turned hers in first thing this morning. Before eight. She was at the post office when it opened."

I kept my hands very busy with the cinnamon. "Good for her."

"Very eager, apparently. Lindqvist said she wrote four pages."

The cinnamon tin slipped. I caught it, which is the only athletic thing I have done in a decade.

"Four pages," I said, "is excessive."

"Uh-huh," said Marta, and went to open the shutters, and let the gray winter light come in over the harbor, and I stood there among my alphabetized spices with my heart doing something I had specifically instructed it not to do.

Four pages. From a stranger. From *M.*

I would not think about it. I would not count the hours until Marta brought the forwarded envelope home, which she would, because Marta was enjoying this, and because the program ran through the community center and the community center ran through Marta like a spine.

I would not think about it, I told myself again, and then I caught myself wondering—stupidly, dangerously, with the whole gray day still ahead of me—what a person who writes four pages to a stranger in winter might possibly have to say.
