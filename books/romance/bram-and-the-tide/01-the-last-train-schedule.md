## 1. The Last Train Schedule

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Orsen Thackeray would later consider the last ordinary Tuesday of his life, though of course he didn't know it at the time. He knew only that it was raining, that the till held eleven pounds forty and a button, and that Wren's school had sent home a form requiring — in the cheerful, merciless language of all school forms — *urgent completion*.

He stood behind the counter of Marchetti & Thackeray Booksellers — the sign still said Marchetti, though no Marchetti had set foot in Saltmere in nine years — and read the letter twice.

*Dear Mr. Thackeray,* it began, in Aunt Cordelia's handwriting, which was as upright and unforgiving as a fence post. *You are cordially invited to the annual Thackeray family reunion at Gullwing House, Saturday fortnight. Dress: smart. Appetite: moderate. There will be matters to discuss concerning the future of the property.*

*The future of the property.* Five words, and each one weighed about as much as an anchor. Orsen set the letter down on the counter next to Wren's form and looked at them both the way a man looks at two waves approaching from opposite directions, knowing they're going to meet somewhere in front of him and splash him thoroughly.

"Papa." Wren appeared at the end of the poetry section, where she'd been shelving books by a system known only to her. She was eight, with her mother's dark hair and Orsen's habit of squinting at things she disapproved of. "Miss Prewitt says the form is important."

"I know, love."

"She said if you don't fill it in I can't go on the tide pool trip."

"Then we shall fill it in," Orsen said, "with the urgency it deserves," and pulled a pen from the cup by the register, three of whose remaining five pens did not work, which was, he reflected, a fair summary of his current circumstances.

The shop was quiet in the way it always was on rainy Tuesdays — one customer browsing the nautical histories, an elderly spaniel asleep in the armchair by the window that everyone pretended belonged to the shop rather than to Mrs. Dunmore, who would collect it at four. Rain ran down the windows in long silver threads, and beyond the glass, the harbor was a smudge of gray with boats bobbing like toys left out overnight.

It was, Orsen thought not for the first time, a good shop. It was a good home, too, since he and Wren lived above it, in the flat that smelled permanently of old paper and toast. The trouble was that none of this goodness belonged entirely to him. His grandmother had left the building to be shared among the Thackerays, and for nine years the family had tolerated his tenancy the way one tolerates a cat on a good sofa — with affection, mostly, and occasional remarks about shedding.

Lately the remarks had been coming more often, and mostly from Cordelia.

He was staring at the letter again when he heard the floorboard creak near the back stairs — the third one up, which he kept meaning to fix and never did — and remembered, with a small start, that he had a lodger now.

Bram Vale had arrived six weeks ago with two suitcases, a research grant, and a manner of speaking that suggested she found most things mildly amusing but was too polite to say so. She was studying tide pools — or, more precisely, the small creatures that lived in them and endured being studied — and she needed a room near the water, and the back room of the bookshop was cheap and dry and came with a landlady-adjacent landlord who asked very few questions. She paid on time. She fixed the kettle when it broke. She had once, unprompted, reorganized the marine biology section into something Orsen described as "disturbingly logical."

And now here she was, sitting cross-legged on the floor between the science shelves, surrounded by tide charts spread out like tarot cards, a pencil tucked behind her ear, frowning at the sea as though it owed her money.

Orsen watched her for longer than was strictly necessary for a man who claimed to merely be passing through his own shop.

She had a way of reading that he found — interesting. That was the word he settled on. Interesting. Most people read charts the way they read bus timetables: quickly, functionally, with their eyes already moving on. Bram read them the way other people read letters from old friends. Her finger traced the curve of the low-tide line, and her lips moved slightly, counting something, and once she made a small sound of satisfaction — *hah* — as though the tide had finally admitted something under questioning.

"You're staring," she said, without looking up.

"I'm observing," Orsen said. "There's a difference. Staring is rude. Observing is what shopkeepers do."

"And what does the shopkeeper observe?"

"That you've been down there for two hours, and the tea has gone cold twice, and you haven't sold a single chart."

"They're not for sale." She looked up then, and the pencil behind her ear slipped and she caught it without seeming to notice she'd done it. "These are last year's. I'm comparing them against this year's to see how the spring tides shifted. The pools on the north side are changing faster than the models say they should."

"Is that bad?"

"It's *interesting*," she said, and something in the way she said it made Orsen think that interesting was the highest compliment she paid, and that he had just been promoted to the same category as tidal anomalies.

Wren materialized beside her the way children do, without any transition from elsewhere. "Can I look?"

"Careful. Those are older than your father."

"Nothing's older than Papa," Wren said solemnly, and sat down beside Bram and began, with terrifying confidence, to point at the charts and ask questions. What was a neap tide. Why did the moon bother doing all that. Did anything live in the really deep pools, like octopuses, because she would accept nothing less than an octopus.

And Bram answered every question. Not the way adults usually answered children — with the patient, airless tone of someone waiting for the conversation to end — but properly, as though Wren were a colleague who happened to be short. She showed her how the chart lines meant height, and height meant how long a pool stayed wet, and how long a pool stayed wet decided who got to live there.

"So the pools are like houses," Wren said.

"Exactly like houses," Bram said. "Some of them get flooded out twice a day, and some of them hold on."

Orsen turned back to the counter and picked up Wren's school form, and found himself thinking about holding on, which was not a subject he liked to think about, because thinking about it led to thinking about the letter, and the letter led to Gullwing House, and Gullwing House led to Cordelia saying *the future of the property* in that voice she used, the one that made even the word *the* sound like a legal proceeding.

The truth — the plain, unglamorous truth he kept in the same drawer as the broken pens — was this: Cordelia believed the shop should be sold. She had believed it for years, politely, the way one believes in the weather. A building like that, she said, was wasted on books. And lately her politeness had begun to develop edges, because Orsen was thirty-six, widowed, running a business that earned, as Felix had put it at Christmas, "a charming little loss," and raising a daughter alone in a flat above a shop in a town where, according to Felix, nothing ever happened except rain.

*A stable household,* Cordelia had written in last year's reunion follow-up note, and Orsen had read those words so many times they had worn smooth, like stones. As though stability were a thing you could show someone. As though it were a pie you could bring to the table and say, *here, taste, it's sound.*

He filled in Wren's form. Emergency contact: himself. Secondary contact: he paused, pen hovering. For four years the secondary contact had been nobody, and nobody had a phone number he couldn't remember. He wrote *N/A* and hated the neatness of it.

Behind him, Wren laughed — a real laugh, the kind that came out of her whole body — and he glanced over his shoulder and saw Bram showing her how to fold a paper boat from the corner of a superseded chart, and the two of them were bent over it together, heads nearly touching, arguing about whether the boat would survive the harbor or immediately disgrace itself.

Something loosened in Orsen's chest. He didn't have a name for it. Warmth, maybe. Or the memory of warmth. It had been a long time since the shop had sounded like this — since it had sounded like more than one person and a dog.

Mrs. Dunmore collected her spaniel at four, as she always did, and the customer bought a history of Cornish lighthouses, and the rain thinned to a drizzle, and Orsen locked the till — eleven pounds forty, minus the button — and carried the teapot to the back room, where Bram was pinning her charts to the wall in a grid.

"The tea's cold," he said. "Again. I could make fresh."

"You could," Bram agreed, "and I could pretend to be surprised by how much sugar you put in mine."

"That was one time."

"It was four times." But she smiled when she said it, and took the cup, and blew on it, and looked at him over the rim with those eyes that seemed to find everything mildly amusing. "Bad day?"

Orsen considered lying. He was, he felt, entitled to one lie per day, given the state of the till. But she was looking at him with the same attention she gave the tide charts — tracing the lines, counting something — and lies, he suspected, would not hold up under that kind of reading.

"My aunt has summoned me to the family reunion," he said. "To discuss, and I quote, *the future of the property*. Which means she wants to sell my shop, and she wants to do it in front of the entire family, so that objecting will make me look difficult."

"Ah," said Bram. "A public execution."

"With sandwiches."

"Naturally. There's always sandwiches at executions. My grant committee served them when they cut my funding request by forty percent." She sipped her tea. "When is it?"

"Saturday fortnight."

"Hm." She looked at the window, where the drizzle was turning the harbor lights soft and gold, and then back at him, and Orsen had the distinct and unsettling feeling of being read. "And what happens at these reunions, besides executions? Interrogations?"

"Worse. Questions." He leaned against the doorframe. "Cordelia will ask about the shop's finances. Felix will ask why I'm still in Saltmere when I could be anywhere. And then someone — it varies, but it's always someone — will ask about my personal life, in that sympathetic voice people use, as though I'm a room in the house that hasn't been redecorated."

"And what do you say?"

"What I always say. That I'm busy. That Wren keeps me occupied. That I'm fine." He heard how it sounded — the small, worn-out word — and was annoyed at himself for letting it out where anyone could hear it.

Bram was quiet for a moment. She turned her cup slowly on its saucer, around and around, and Orsen noticed she had ink on her fingers and a smear of pencil graphite along her jaw, and that noticing these things felt dangerously like the beginning of curiosity, and curiosity, in his experience, was how trouble started.

"Say yes anyway," she said.

"To what?"

"The reunion. Go. Take the interrogation." She shrugged, casual as anything, as though she hadn't just said anything remarkable. "You've got a fortnight. That's plenty of time to prepare your answers."

"Prepare them how? I can't exactly invent a personal life between now and then. Cordelia checks facts. Cordelia probably has fact-checkers. Somewhere in Gullwing House there's a drawer full of my failures, alphabetized."

Bram set down her cup. She was smiling — not the mild-amusement smile, but something quicker, brighter, a little reckless, like weather coming in off the sea.

"Well," she said, "it's a funny thing, Mr. Thackeray. Because I happen to be free that Saturday. And I do own one smart dress. And I've always wondered what a Thackeray interrogation looks like from the inside."

Orsen stared at her. The rain ticked against the window. Downstairs, faintly, Wren was singing to herself while she folded her paper boat, and the harbor lights burned gold through the gray, and somewhere out past the breakwater the tide was turning, though no one in the shop yet knew toward what.

"Bram," he said carefully, "what exactly are you suggesting?"

* * *

Later — much later, after the fresh pot of tea and the negotiation conducted over burnt toast, after terms had been agreed upon with the gravity of a treaty and the giddiness of two people who had both, secretly, wanted to laugh — Orsen lay awake in the flat above the shop and listened to the sea.

It was absurd. It was completely absurd. She was a stranger who had lived in his back room for six weeks. She organized shelves disturbingly logically and put too much — no, he put too much sugar in her tea, that was the arrangement — and she had offered, over burnt toast, to spend a Saturday pretending to be in love with him in front of the most dangerous woman in the family.

*Hand-holding allowed,* she had said, ticking items off on her ink-stained fingers. *Feelings forbidden. We keep it simple. We keep it believable. And afterward, we never speak of it again.*

*Never speak of it again,* Orsen repeated to the ceiling, and found, to his considerable annoyance, that some small traitorous part of him was already hoping the pretending would go badly — hoping, perhaps, that they would be terrible at it.

Out past the breakwater, the tide crept in over the pools, filling the houses of all those small creatures who had learned to hold on.

In the morning, he thought, he would fix the third stair.

He fell asleep before he could decide whether he meant it.
