## 6. Dessert

I have testified in courtrooms where a man's entire net worth hung on my next sentence, and I have never been as nervous as I was passing the green beans to Quentin Nakamura's grandmother.

This is not an exaggeration. In court there are rules. There is procedure, precedent, a script you can hide inside like a shell. At the Nakamura reunion table there were no rules—only nineteen relatives, two tureens of something fragrant enough to make me reconsider every meal I'd eaten on the mainland, and Hana Nakamura at the head of it all, watching me with eyes that had clearly catalogued more in my first ten minutes than I'd managed to catalogue in thirty years.

"Edmund," she said now, in the tone of a woman who has decided to pronounce my name correctly forever and expects me to be grateful, "you take rice?"

"I would love rice."

"You would love rice." She repeated it as though filing it away for later cross-examination. "Quentin's last one, Daniel, he did not eat rice. He said carbohydrates." She ladled a portion that could have fed a fishing crew onto my plate. "Daniel is very thin now. Stress, I think."

Across the table, Quentin made a sound like a teakettle deciding against boiling.

I had been Edmund Voss, boyfriend of three months, for approximately four hours, and already I understood that this family communicated primarily through weaponized hospitality. It was, I'll admit, a language I found myself fluent in faster than expected. My own family had communicated through silence and the occasional forwarded article about tax law. Here, love arrived in bowls. You were never allowed to leave hungry or unobserved.

"So," said a man down the table—I'd been introduced to him as Uncle Theo, though he looked less like an uncle than like a man who owned several boats and had opinions about all of them—"how did you two meet? Quentin never tells us anything."

"She doesn't," agreed a cousin whose name I'd lost somewhere between the pickled vegetables and the grilled fish. "We had to find out about her *job* from a magazine."

"That was one time," Quentin said.

"It was a *good* magazine," said the cousin, with the air of a woman who had clipped the article and laminated it.

Everyone looked at me. This was the moment we had rehearsed—or rather, the moment Quentin had insisted we not rehearse, because "if it sounds rehearsed, Aunt Mariko will know, and Aunt Mariko knows everything, including things that haven't happened yet."

So I improvised. "The ferry dock," I said. "She was arguing with the schedule board."

"I was *reading* the schedule board," Quentin said.

"You were arguing with it. It was losing."

There was a pause, and then Uncle Theo laughed—a big, unguarded bark—and someone else laughed, and Hana smiled the small smile of a woman adding a point to a column only she could see. Quentin kicked me under the table. I chose to interpret it as applause.

It was true, what I'd said. That was the trouble with this whole arrangement: the true parts kept leaking in. We had built our story out of plausible fictions—the meet-cute at the ferry terminal, the slow courtship conducted mostly over text because Quentin was "shy about her work"—and then we'd salted it with real details, because lies go down easier with truth as seasoning. But the seasoning kept taking over the dish. Every time I reached for an invented detail, my hand came back holding a real one.

*You were arguing with it. It was losing.* I hadn't planned to say that. I'd said it because it was the first thing I'd thought of her, standing on the dock with my firm's file heavy in my bag, watching a woman in a linen shirt lose a fight with a laminated timetable and win anyway, somehow, by sheer force of posture. I'd thought: *there is a person who believes schedules should behave.* I'd thought it with a warmth that had no professional explanation.

Dessert arrived before I could finish being alarmed by myself.

Hana produced it from the kitchen herself: a plum cake, dark and dense, carried out on a plate that was visibly older than everyone present except possibly Hana. A hush went around the table. Even the children stopped negotiating over who got the window seat.

"My husband's mother's recipe," Hana announced, cutting it with the gravity of a notary. "She made it every reunion. Forty years." She set the first slice in front of Quentin. "Family gets it first."

I noticed the phrasing. So, apparently, did Quentin, because her hand paused halfway to her fork.

"Edmund is family-adjacent," offered the cousin with the laminated magazine.

"He is sitting at my table eating my rice," Hana said, which settled the matter the way a gavel settles it.

The cake was extraordinary. I don't have a sophisticated palate—I have eaten lunch over case files more times than I'd care to enter into evidence—but even I could tell this cake was carrying forty years of reunions in its crumb. Around me the family ate and talked and argued about whether the lighthouse needed repainting this year or next (a debate that, I noted with a cold twist in my stomach, had financial implications I was professionally positioned to understand better than any of them), and I ate my plum cake and performed the role of a man in love, and discovered that the performance required almost nothing from me.

That was the problem. Good acting is effortful. This wasn't effortful. When Quentin laughed at something her uncle said, I felt my own mouth do something unscripted. When she leaned into my shoulder during the argument about paint colors—she was firmly team *this year*, delivered with the same posture that had defeated the ferry schedule—I let her lean, and my body accepted the weight like it had been waiting for instructions.

Under the table, my knee was touching hers. It had been touching hers for some time. Neither of us had moved it.

And then—between the cake and the tea, in the lull where conversation dips and people check their phones and the children are sent outside—her hand found mine.

Not part of the script.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only discipline I have left. Our agreement covered hand-holding. It was item four on the list she'd typed up at her kitchen table with the brisk efficiency of a woman drafting a contract she hoped never to enforce: *Minimal public touch. Hand-holding acceptable. Nothing that requires choreography.* We had held hands walking up from the parking area, and it had been fine, pleasant, a business transaction conducted in skin.

This was different. This was her fingers sliding between mine under the tablecloth while she was mid-sentence, listening to her aunt describe a neighbor's divorce, her face composed, her voice steady—and her thumb moving once, slowly, across my knuckle. An absent gesture. The kind of gesture you make when you've forgotten your hand belongs to a performance.

Or the kind you make when you haven't forgotten anything at all.

I sat very still. Somewhere in my chest, a very careful lawyer began reading a document he had not authorized anyone to draft. *Clause one,* it said. *This is not in the terms. Clause two. She is doing it anyway. Clause three. You are not letting go.*

I did not let go.

I looked at her. She didn't look back. She kept her face turned toward her aunt, nodding along to a story about a man named Gerald and a boat named after his ex-wife, and if her ears had gone slightly pink, well, the kitchen was warm, anyone could see that, the kitchen was warm and the cake was rich and there was absolutely no other explanation for the color climbing her neck.

Her thumb crossed my knuckles again.

I am going to remember that thumb movement for the rest of my life, whatever length that turns out to be, and I say that as a man who has spent years training himself not to remember things. Memory is liability. Attachment is exposure. I learned early that the safest way to move through other people's lives is to keep your hands in your pockets and your exits mapped. And here I sat, exit unmapped, hand thoroughly out of my pocket, held.

"Edmund." Hana's voice, across the table, gentle as a trap. "You are quiet."

"He's always quiet," Quentin said, squeezing my hand once beneath the cloth—defending me, covering me, claiming me, some combination I couldn't parse. "He listens. It's his whole personality."

"A good personality," Hana said. "Listening men are either the best ones or the worst ones. Time will tell." She refilled my tea without asking. "More cake?"

"No, thank you. It was—" I searched for the right word and found, annoyingly, the true one. "It tasted like this house."

Hana looked at me for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she folded it away somewhere. "Yes," she said. "It does."

* * *

The walk home was not in the script either.

The script said: Quentin makes an early exit citing morning obligations, Edmund drives, they debrief briefly at the cottage, sleep in separate rooms, reconvene tomorrow for day two. Clean. Containable. The kind of operation I'd run a hundred times in other contexts—depositions, negotiations, the careful theater of settlement talks.

Instead, when the goodbyes finally unwound themselves—Hana pressing leftovers on us with the insistence of a customs official, Uncle Theo shaking my hand and saying "you'll do," which I gathered was the highest honor he conferred—we ended up on foot, because Quentin said the night was too nice to drive and I said okay before she finished the sentence.

The cliff path ran along the edge of the point, the cottage lights falling behind us, the sea below doing its enormous patient work. The air smelled of salt and cut grass and, faintly, of plum cake, because Quentin was carrying leftovers in a tin under her arm like contraband.

"So," she said. "Debrief."

"We're walking."

"We can walk and debrief. Multitasking. It's in the terms."

"It is not in the terms."

"I'm amending them." She hopped a rut in the path without breaking stride. "Item twelve: debriefs may occur ambulatory."

"Noted," I said. "Objection: the amendment process requires both parties."

"Overruled. I'm the client."

"You're the client," I agreed, and heard how it landed in my own mouth, wrong-shaped, and pushed past it quickly. "Fine. Debriefing. Your grandmother likes me."

"My grandmother likes everyone she intends to interrogate further. That's not affection, that's case preparation." She glanced sideways at me. "You were good tonight."

"I was adequate."

"You were—" She stopped walking. Below us the water hit the rocks with a sound like slow applause. "You told the ferry dock story. The part about the schedule board losing. I never told you that version. I told you I was *waiting* for the ferry."

I had known this moment might come. I had prepared, in the vague way one prepares for weather, a deflection: *you mentioned it, you must have, it was in one of our planning sessions.* A clean lie, low-risk, the kind I filed daily.

What I said instead was: "You didn't. I watched you argue with it before I ever met you. First thing I saw on this island."

She stared at me. The tin of cake shifted under her arm. "Why would you say that?"

"Because it's true, and your aunt has a radar for invented details, and—" I gestured vaguely at the ocean, at the evening, at the whole collapsing architecture of the weekend. "Because it was a better story."

"It was a better story," she repeated, softly, like she was testing the weight of it.

The path curved toward the point, and the point, I knew—even knowing what I knew, even carrying what I carried—was where the lighthouse stood. We couldn't see it yet; it was behind the rise, but its presence was structural, the way a parent's presence is structural in a house even when they're in another room. Quentin walked toward it the way people walk toward things they've walked toward ten thousand times: without looking, certain of the ground.

"Your family asks good questions," I said, to fill the space.

"They're lawyers' questions without the law degree. Hana especially. She used to run the guest registers at the keeper's cottage—back when it took guests—she says you can tell everything about a person by what they write in the margins." Quentin's mouth curved. "She's going to ask you back. Did you notice? 'Time will tell.' That's a summons."

"I noticed."

"And you stayed for seconds on the cake. Fatal. She'll have you painting the lighthouse by August."

"I'd paint the lighthouse," I said, and then heard myself, and shut up, because that sentence had come out wearing a ring I hadn't bought.

She didn't seem to notice. Or she noticed and filed it in the same drawer where she'd filed the thumb-across-the-knuckles, the drawer marked *later, not now, the weekend isn't over.* We walked. The grass whispered. Somewhere below, a buoy clanked its one note.

Here is what I did not say, walking that path with her hand still loosely in mine: *I am here to take this from you.* The file sat in my rented room eleven miles south, in a leather folder with a firm's stamp on it, containing a deed, a survey, a developer's timeline, and a signature—her grandfather's signature, allegedly given in 1974 to a man whose company now wanted the point for condos and a marina. I had skimmed the summary memo on the ferry over. I had not read the exhibits. I had told myself this was thoroughness deferred, diligence scheduled, professionalism pacing itself.

Walking beside her, feeling her pulse in her fingertips where they rested against my wrist, I understood that I had not read the file for the same reason a man doesn't open a letter he already knows is bad. Not caution. Cowardice, wearing caution's coat.

"Stop," Quentin said suddenly.

I stopped. "What?"

"The light." She nodded up the rise. "Watch."

For a moment there was nothing—just the dark shoulder of the point against the darker sky. Then the beam came around, sweeping across the cliff path like a slow white hand, catching the grass, the rocks, her face—lit for one full second, eyes bright, expression open in a way it hadn't been all evening, open the way people are open in photographs taken before they knew the camera was there—and then it passed, and the dark came back, and we stood in it together.

"It does that every seven and a half seconds," she said. "Grandpa set the interval. He said seven and a half was the rhythm of a calm heart. He measured it off Grandma's wrist." She laughed a little, embarrassed. "Sorry. I do this. I narrate the light. Everyone in my family does it. It's insufferable."

"It's not insufferable."

"It's completely insufferable. Ask anyone." She was facing me now. The tin of cake had migrated to the ground at some point; I couldn't say when. Her hand was still in mine, and neither of us had engineered it, and the fiction we'd built had quietly gone bankrupt sometime around dessert and neither of us had declared it.

Seven and a half seconds. I counted them. I was aware, distantly, of having counted many things in my life—billable hours, exits, the distance between myself and other people—and that this was the first count that felt like prayer instead of arithmetic.

"Edmund," she said.

"Yes."

"You're looking at me like—"

"Like what?" My voice came out lower than intended.

"Like the terms don't apply."

The beam swept again. Light, then dark. In the light her eyes were steady and terrified in the specific way of a person who has decided to stop being terrified. In the dark, everything was permitted, which is the danger of dark places on cliff paths: they feel like loopholes. They are not loopholes. Everything done in the dark is still entered into the record.

I leaned in. Or she leaned in. The geometry dissolved; there was just the closing distance, the salt air, her breath, the exact warmth of a moment arriving ahead of any schedule—

The beam swept past, and in its light I saw behind her, up the rise, the lighthouse itself—white tower, black lantern room, turning its patient heart—and with it, unbidden, the image arrived: the same tower rendered in a developer's rendering, glass and timber, *Voss Point Estates*, the file's mock-up I'd glimpsed on the ferry, and my stomach dropped through the cliff into the sea.

I stopped. Half an inch short. Maybe less. Close enough that she felt it.

"Edmund?"

"I—" Every word available to me was a lie or a confession, and I stood there at the intersection, a professional, trained for exactly this, discovering that training fails at half an inch. "Cold," I said, which was cowardice in a trench coat. "Sea air. We should—Quentin, we should get you back."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly governed, filed under *later*. "Right," she said. "Of course. Cold."

She picked up the cake tin. She did not take my hand again on the walk back, and the absence of it was louder than the sea.

At the cottage door she paused, key in the lock, and said, without turning around: "Tomorrow's the family brunch. Ten o'clock. Hana's making pancakes, which means it's mandatory and also a test." A beat. "Goodnight, Edmund."

"Goodnight."

The door closed. I stood on the step in the dark while the beam swept past overhead, seven and a half seconds, a calm heart, and I thought about the letter in my bag, the one I hadn't opened, the one I was running out of reasons not to.

Back in my rented room, I set the folder on the desk. I did not open it. Not yet. I sat with my hands flat on the leather cover like a man taking a pulse, and outside the window the light turned, and turned, keeping faith with a dead man's promise, while I broke mine in advance.

Tomorrow, I told myself. Tomorrow I'd read the whole file.

I already knew what it would say. I think, on some level, I'd known since the ferry dock—since the first thing I saw on this island was a woman winning an argument with a schedule, and the second thing was the lighthouse, and the third thing, God help me, was her face lit up by its beam for exactly one second, long enough to ruin me.

The file could wait one more night. Lies always can. That's their best feature, and their worst.
