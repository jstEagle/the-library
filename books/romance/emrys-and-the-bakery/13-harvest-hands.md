## 13. Harvest Hands

The forecast came in at four in the afternoon, the way bad news always does — casually, on a screen I wasn't even looking at, sandwiched between a coupon for tire rotation and a reminder that my flour order would arrive Thursday. *Severe weather advisory. Hail possible. Winds to forty.* The kind of forecast that means nothing in a town with sidewalks and everything in a town with six hundred acres of fruit hanging by a stem.

I stood in my kitchen with a dish towel over my shoulder and did the math I didn't want to do. First pick was scheduled for Friday. The storm was coming Thursday night. Grapes that survive hail don't become wine; they become compost with ambitions. And Jonah Sterling, who rationed her heart like it was the last bottle of a bad vintage, was going to lose the harvest that was supposed to save her.

I want to be clear about something: I had been avoiding this woman for three weeks. Professionally. Architecturally. I had rebuilt my entire counter routine around the assumption of her absence — moved the register, retrained my hands, taught myself to stop looking up when the door chimed. When she came in anyway, because she still bought bread like a person who eats, I gave her brisk politeness and exact change and nothing else. It was working. It was miserable, but it was working, which is more than you can say for most survival strategies.

And now the sky was about to do something cruel to her, and every instinct I had spent years sharpening said: *not your problem. You pulled back for a reason. You are a woman who once owned an oven and a ring and a future, and you learned what happens when you let yourself need things.*

But there's another thing about me, one I like less: I have never once in my life watched work go undone that I knew how to do.

I called my staff into the kitchen at closing. Dev, who'd been with me since the food truck days; Priya, who ran the counter and half the town's gossip network; Marcus Okafor — no relation to anyone important — who washed dishes and listened to opera on his earbuds like a man with secrets.

"So," I said. "Hypothetically. Who wants to spend tomorrow night picking grapes in a vineyard until their hands bleed, right before a storm hits?"

Priya didn't even blink. "Is this about the wine lady?"

"This is about grapes."

"The wine lady whose bread you've been sulking about for three weeks."

"I don't sulk. I bake."

"You bake *aggressively*," Dev said. "Last Tuesday you made forty croissants. We're a small shop. Forty."

"They were a technical exercise."

"They were a cry for help," Priya said, "and yes, obviously we'll come. What time?"

I looked at them — these people who had followed me from a failed business in another county to a counter in a town too small to appear on most maps — and felt the familiar ache of owing people everything and being unable to say so.

"Six," I said. "Bring gloves. The good ones."

* * *

Jonah met us at the gate with a headlamp already on and an expression I couldn't read in the dark — part suspicion, part something softer she was trying very hard not to be caught feeling.

"What is this," she said. Not a question. A demand for justification.

"Labor," I said. "You've heard of it. It's what happens before wine."

"Emrys." My name in her mouth did something to the air between us, and I hated that I noticed. "I can't pay—"

"You can pay in grapes. I'm told they ferment."

"That's not—" She stopped. Recalibrated. I could see her doing it, the way she always did, reaching for logistics the way other people reach for a hand. "There are eleven rows ready. Pinot noir, the east slope. If we get through those before the front arrives—"

"Then tell us where to start," I said, "and stop arguing with free help. It's rude."

Behind me, Priya snorted. Jonah looked past my shoulder at the five people standing in her vineyard at dusk holding pruning shears and thermoses, and something in her face cracked — just a hairline fracture, the kind you only see if you've spent weeks memorizing someone's tells. Which I had. Which was the problem.

"East slope," she said quietly. "Follow me."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about picking grapes: it is not romantic. It is crouching and clipping and hauling, over and over, until your lower back files a formal complaint and your fingers go numb in a way that outlasts the numbness. The clusters hide under leaves like they don't want to be found. Your knees learn new languages of protest.

It is also, and I say this as a woman who has spent ten years perfecting the alchemy of turning grief into bread — it is also the most honest work there is. You can't fake a picked row. You can't negotiate with a grape. Either the fruit comes off the vine before the hail comes down, or it doesn't. There's no spreadsheet for it, no contract, no fine print. Just hands and time and weather.

We started at six-thirty. By eight we had a rhythm — Dev and Priya working a row together like they'd done it all their lives, Marcus Okafor narrating his opera in a low baritone while he clipped, Jonah moving up and down the rows checking our work, correcting grips, showing Dev how to cradle a cluster so it didn't split.

And me. I ended up beside her somewhere around nine o'clock, in the fourth row, because the universe has a sense of humor and I have stopped pretending otherwise.

"You didn't have to do this," she said. Her headlamp caught the side of her face, the line of her jaw, the grape stain already purpling her knuckles.

"No," I agreed. "I really didn't."

"Why, then?"

I clipped a cluster, dropped it in the bin, reached for the next. "Because I know what it is to build something with your whole body and watch the sky take it."

She went still. In the dark, among the vines, with the wind starting to pick up its pace, I had handed her something true, and we both knew it.

"The fire wasn't your fault," she said.

"Neither is this storm." I cut another cluster, harder than necessary. "Doesn't mean it doesn't feel personal."

The wind gusted then, hard enough to set the vines swaying, and the first drops came — fat, cold, theatrical. Someone down the row whooped. Jonah laughed — actually laughed, head back, rain on her face — and the sound undid something in me that three weeks of careful distance had been holding together with twine.

"It's raining," she said, delighted, insane. "Emrys, it's actually raining, we might beat it—"

"We might," I said, and found I was grinning like a fool, soaked through, hands stinging, and happier than I had any right to be.

* * *

By midnight we were a legend in our own minds. The east slope was done — eleven rows stripped clean, bins loaded, the truck making runs down to the crush pad with headlights cutting through the drizzle. The storm proper hadn't arrived yet; instead we got this steady, generous rain, warm-ish, almost apologetic, like the sky was giving us a head start out of pity or respect.

We took a break in the third row, sitting on overturned crates under the widest trellis, passing around a thermos of coffee I'd brought in a quantity that could only be described as prophetic. Priya and Dev were arguing about whether grapes counted as fruit or candy. Marcus Okafor was asleep sitting up, which takes talent.

Jonah sat next to me. Close enough that our shoulders touched when either of us shifted. Neither of us moved away.

"My father used to say harvest nights were the only honest hours," she said, watching the dark rows. "Everything else is negotiation. This is just — you and the vine and whatever you're willing to give."

"He sounds like he'd have liked tonight."

"He'd have loved it." Her voice caught slightly. "He'd have put everyone to work and fed them at dawn and told terrible jokes about fermentation."

I turned the thermos cup in my hands. Rain dripped off the trellis wire, ticking against the crate. "Jonah."

"Don't." Soft. Immediate. "Don't say something true right now. I'll believe it, and then tomorrow you'll go back to charging me exact change, and I can't—" She exhaled. "I can't afford it. Whatever this costs."

And here's the thing. Every rational part of me — the part that kept the insurance settlement locked in a back room, the part that learned the lesson of the fire and the bankruptcy and Dana walking out with the last of her patience — every part that had ever protected me said: *she's right. Say nothing. Finish the harvest. Go home.*

But my hands were bleeding through my gloves, and her shoulder was warm against mine, and the rain was falling on six hundred acres of saved grapes, and I thought: *this. This is what belonging feels like. Not the having. The working. Standing in the wet dark with people who showed up, doing something that matters, needing them and being needed and not dying of it.*

I didn't say anything true. But I didn't move away either, and when her hand found mine in the dark — grape-sticky, nicked, shaking slightly — I let it stay.

"Row twelve tomorrow," I said instead. "If the storm holds off."

"If it holds off," she echoed.

Down the row, Priya yelled that the coffee was gone and someone had eaten the last of the almond croissants, and Dev blamed Marcus Okafor, who woke up to defend himself, and the argument dissolved into laughter that carried across the wet vines like something planted.

I sat there in the rain with bleeding hands and a full heart and understood, finally, what my grandmother meant when she said some things rise whether you want them to or not.

Because at dawn, when the storm broke apart over the western hills without ever landing its blow, and we stood in the mud surveying eleven rows of rescued fruit, Jonah turned to thank me — and stopped. Behind her, pulling up the drive in a car too clean for a vineyard, was her brother.

Marcus Sterling got out slowly, looked at the crew, the bins, the two of us standing hand-in-hand in the mud — and held up his phone.

"We need to talk," he said. "About what you've been posting, Jonah. Or rather — what you hired."
