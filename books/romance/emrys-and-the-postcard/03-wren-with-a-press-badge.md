## 3. Wren, With a Press Badge

Wren Okafor had a theory about small towns: they were not actually small. They were large places folded up small, like maps, and every crease hid something someone had worked hard to forget. Harborlight folded particularly well. Ten years of festival programs, four editors at the *Gull & Lantern*, one brother gone to water, and the whole town creased itself neatly around the absence and went on selling fudge.

She had come back in June with a press badge she no longer technically needed—the *Coastal Standard* had let her go in the spring, politely, the way you let go of a kite string when the wind turns—and a job title that sounded harmless: Festival Organizer. It was not harmless. It was a crowbar. A festival was the one time a year Harborlight opened all its drawers at once, and Wren intended to go through every one of them.

The bookshop was on Pell Street, third from the corner, with a green awning faded to the color of old sea glass and a hand-lettered sign: *HALF MOON BOOKS — New, Used, and Slightly Weathered*. Wren had passed it a dozen times since arriving. She had noted, in the way she noted everything now, professionally, reflexively, that the blinds were always half-drawn. Not broken—adjusted. Someone in that shop had made a study of exactly how much window the town was allowed.

She pushed the door. The bell above it gave a single flat note, like a dropped coin.

Inside, the shop smelled of paper and cedar and, faintly, of the sea, which was odd, because the sea was two streets away and this building had probably never been closer to salt water than its own foundation. It was the smell of a place that had absorbed its town. Shelves ran deep, deeper than the storefront suggested, and the light came down in long amber planes through those half-drawn blinds, striping the floor like the slats of a pier.

A girl of about twelve sat behind the register on an upturned milk crate, reading a paperback with the cover bent all the way back on itself, and a man stood on a rolling ladder in the biography section, reshelving with the unhurried precision of someone who had done it ten thousand times.

"Welcome to Half Moon," the girl said, without looking up. "If you break it, it's half price."

"Ivy," the man said mildly, from the top of the ladder.

"It's a joke, Da."

"It's a liability."

Wren smiled despite herself and let the door close behind her. "I'm actually here on official business," she said. "Mostly. Wren Okafor. I'm running the Autumn Harborlight Festival this year."

The man came down the ladder. He came down it carefully, rung by rung, and Wren had time to look at him properly before he turned: mid-forties, maybe, tall in the way that made him seem to fold himself smaller around the shop's low beams, dark hair going gray at the temples in a way that looked less like age than like weather. He wore a canvas apron over a flannel shirt with the sleeves pushed up, and his forearms had the fine white tracery of old scars—not one dramatic mark, but a scatter of them, like a map of small accidents.

He turned, and Wren watched something happen to his face.

It was not much. That was the thing she would remember later, turning it over at night like a stone with something underneath: how *little* it was. A man hearing the name of the town he lived in should feel nothing at all. But this man, hearing *Harborlight*—hearing it attached to *festival*, to *celebration*, to the one word in the language that should have been safe—went very slightly still. His hand, which had been reaching for the apron string, stopped. Not froze, exactly. *Paused*, the way a deer pauses when the forest goes quiet in the wrong way. Half a second, no more. Then his hands moved again, the apron string tied, and he smiled a smile that was warm and correct and arrived a beat late, like a train making up time.

"Welcome," he said. "Dominic Kestrel. This is my shop. My daughter, who is supposed to be shelving."

"I *am* shelving," Ivy said, holding up her book, which was clearly not a shelf.

"Dominic Kestrel," Wren repeated, and offered her hand. "And you're the owner? I've been meaning to come by. Every festival needs a bookstall, and honestly, every festival needs a bookshop more."

His handshake was brief, dry, careful. "We've done the stall before," he said. "Two tables out front. Ivy runs the register and overcharges tourists for bookmarks."

"I do *not*—"

"She donates the proceeds to the library," Dominic said, "which is the only reason I allow it." He said it lightly, but Wren caught the architecture underneath: the way he'd turned the sentence toward his daughter, the way his body had angled, almost imperceptibly, so that Ivy was slightly behind his shoulder. Not shielding. *Positioning.* She had seen that geometry before, in interviews with people who had something to protect. It was the posture of a man who had already decided where the exits were.

"Well," Wren said, and got out her folder, because folders made her harmless, folders made her a person asking for raffle prizes instead of a person asking questions. "Here's the pitch. The festival committee is putting together the program for the last weekend of September. Lantern walk on Saturday, closing ceremony Sunday night, the whole usual. We're asking local businesses for donations—goods, gift certificates, sponsorships if anyone's feeling flush. In exchange, your name in the program, your awning on the map, and my personal guarantee that I will personally carry your bookstall tables up Pell Street myself if my volunteer crew doesn't show."

"That last part I'd pay to see," Ivy said.

"Ivy."

"I'd *donate* to see it."

Dominic almost laughed. Wren saw it start and get caught, like a fish released back into the water. "Let me look at the form," he said, taking the folder. And then, because he was clearly a man who believed in finishing transactions cleanly, he opened it right there at the counter, reading with the focus of someone who read contracts for a living.

Which gave Wren the counter, the register, the girl, and thirty unguarded seconds.

"You've got the *Tideglass Trilogy*," Ivy said, appearing at her elbow with the bent paperback. "Book two's out of print. Da's been saving it for someone who'd actually read it. Are you visiting?"

"Living here now. Above the old chandlery on Water Street."

"Nobody lives on Water Street."

"I do. It's very dramatic. The fog comes right up to the windows like it's trying to get in."

Ivy considered this with the gravity of a twelve-year-old deciding whether an adult was worth the trouble. "That's the best thing anyone's said about Water Street. Mostly people say it smells."

"It does a little."

"See, *honest*." Ivy grinned, and the grin was so open, so unguarded, so entirely unlike her father's careful half-smile, that Wren felt something turn over in her chest. She knew that grin. She had owned that grin once, at that age, before her brother went into the water and the whole family's face learned to close like a shop at closing time.

"Your dad's been here long?" Wren asked, keeping it light, keeping it nothing, keeping it the kind of question you ask while waiting for change.

"Eleven years," Ivy said instantly, and then, with the flicker of a girl who has just heard her own answer land in a room where it wasn't supposed to, "I mean. A while. Before me being able to remember, mostly. He doesn't talk about before. There wasn't really a before. There was just here."

"Everyone has a before."

"Not Da." Ivy said it simply, without drama, the way children say the truest things, and then the bell over the door jingled and Mrs. Adeyemi from the bakery came in asking after a cookbook, and the moment folded itself away like a letter going back into its envelope.

* * *

Wren stayed forty minutes. She bought the *Tideglass Trilogy*, book two included—Dominic wrapped it in brown paper and string without being asked, an old-fashioned gesture that sat strangely on him, like a coat borrowed from a bigger man—and she watched him the way she had learned to watch people in a decade of chasing stories: sideways, through the ordinary.

He was good at ordinary. That was the remarkable thing. He greeted Mrs. Adeyemi by name and her late husband's reading tastes by heart. He told a tourist the truth about a local history book ("it's sentimental, but the maps are good"). He moved through his shop like a man moves through a house he has built with his own hands, and if Wren had met him anywhere else, in any other life, she would have filed him under *decent* and thought no more about it.

But she had watched him hear the word *Harborlight*.

And there was the other thing, the thing she was trying not to look at directly, the way you try not to look directly at the sun or at a person on a train you find beautiful: that when he looked up from the donation form and their eyes met, something in her chest went off like a struck match. He had gray eyes, she noticed. Gray-green, tide-pool colored, the exact color of the water over the rocks at the point, and they were the eyes of a man who had spent years practicing how to look at people without being looked at, and he was bad at it now, with her, and he knew it, and she knew he knew it, and neither of them did anything about it except that the air between the counter and the register got very slightly warmer, the way it does before weather.

"So," he said, signing the form—*D. Kestrel* in a hand that was quick, slanted, economical. "Two tables. Ivy at the register. What time do you need us set up?"

"Friday morning. The twenty-sixth." She took the form back and their fingers didn't touch, which she noticed, which annoyed her. "And—look, I'm new. To organizing, I mean. I did events in the city, but a town festival is its own animal. If you've done this eleven years, you probably know where all the bodies are buried. Figuratively," she added, and watched the word land.

It landed. Not hard—but it landed, a small stone dropped into still water, and the ripples went out through his face in that same half-second pause, that same wrong-quiet stillness, before he said, "Figuratively," in a voice that was perfectly level and perfectly empty, a voice with the volume turned all the way down on something.

"The committee meets Thursdays," Wren said. "Seven o'clock, the harbor master's office. You should come. Not just to donate. We need people who actually know the town. I've been here two months and I still get lost behind the cannery."

"I could draw you a map," he said.

"I'd rather you come tell me where I'm wrong."

Something moved through his eyes then—amusement, yes, but under it, briefly, something warier, something that looked at her the way you look at a door you thought was locked and just saw the handle turn. "Thursdays," he said. "I'll try. The shop—"

"The shop will survive you for two hours. Ivy can hold the fort."

"Da can't be spared," Ivy called from the shelves. "He gets lost in the biography section and comes out having bought three books about lighthouse keepers."

"That was *once*," Dominic said, and there it was again, the almost-laugh, the warmth he kept on a short leash, and Wren thought: *who taught you to keep it that short? Who taught you that warmth was something you ration?*

She knew the answer to that question, in outline. She had spent ten years learning the shape of answers like it.

* * *

She walked back up Pell Street in the long gold light of late afternoon, the brown-paper parcel under her arm, and made herself do what she always did after an interview, even an interview the subject didn't know had happened: she played it back.

*Dominic Kestrel. Half Moon Books. Eleven years in town—her words, and the girl had corrected herself around the number like it was hot. Half-drawn blinds, adjusted not broken. The flinch at Harborlight—no, not a flinch, a stillness, a held breath. The positioning around the daughter. The scars on the forearms, old, scattered, unexplained. The signature: D. Kestrel, slanted, fast, the handwriting of someone who had signed a different name so many times that this one was just the newest costume.*

And underneath it all, the thing she was most reluctant to write down even in her own head, because writing it down made it real and made her accountable to it: *he looked at me like I was a risk he was willing to take anyway.*

She stopped at the corner by the cannery wall, where the harbor opened out below her in its wide gray sheet, and she made herself say the other thing, the journalist's thing, the cold thing, because she had promised herself she would never again let the warm thing do the thinking.

Daniel Okafor had disappeared from these docks eleven years ago. Not ten. Not twelve. *Eleven.* She had the clipping in a box in her apartment, the *Gull & Lantern* from the second week of October, a photograph of a nineteen-year-old boy with their mother's smile, and the word *presumed drowned* doing its terrible tidy work. Eleven years. And a man who had been in town eleven years had gone still as a held breath when she said the name of the town.

It proved nothing. She knew it proved nothing. People flinched at words for a thousand reasons—grief, shyness, indigestion. She had built whole careers, whole *ruins*, out of coincidences that fell apart under the first honest question, and she had sworn, after the Standard, after the lawsuit, after the editor who told her she saw conspiracies the way other people saw faces, that she would never again mistake a pattern for a person.

But her brother had worked the docks that summer. Her brother had come home once with a split lip and a story about a crate that "wasn't oysters." Her brother had been afraid of something in the weeks before the water took him—or before whatever took him—and the town had folded itself neatly around the absence, crease by crease, and gone back to selling fudge.

And now there was a man on Pell Street who kept his blinds at exactly half, and a daughter who said *there wasn't really a before*, and a stillness in him shaped exactly like a secret.

Wren stood at the rail above the harbor until the light went long and copper, and then she took out her notebook—the real one, the paper one, the one that lived in her inside pocket like a second heart—and wrote, in her fast slanted hand, three lines:

*Half Moon Books. Kestrel, D. Eleven years. Ask about before.*

She looked at the lines for a while. Then, because she was honest with herself in the ways that mattered and dishonest in all the ways that were more fun, she added a fourth:

*Thursdays. Committee. Go early.*

* * *

That night she unpacked the brown-paper parcel in her apartment above the chandlery, where the fog did indeed press at the windows like something trying to get in, and found that Dominic Kestrel had tucked a bookmark into the first volume without charging her for it. It was a plain card, heavy stock, and on it, in that same quick slanted hand, he had written:

*The tide takes more than it gives. But the tide pools keep what the sea lets go. — D.*

She read it three times. It was probably just something booksellers said, something he'd written in a dozen bookmarks for a dozen customers, a nice line, a shopkeeper's grace note.

It was probably nothing at all.

She put the bookmark in the book, and the book on the nightstand, and the notebook in her inside pocket where it lived, and she lay in the dark above Water Street listening to the foghorn out at the point, counting the seconds between the blast and the echo the way she had done as a child, and she thought about gray-green eyes and a held breath and a girl who said *everyone has a before, not Da*.

Eleven years, she thought. What happened eleven years ago that a man would build a whole life on top of it, like a shop built over a cellar, with the blinds set at exactly half?

Outside, the fog came up Water Street and put its hands against the windows, patient as mail, waiting to be let in.
