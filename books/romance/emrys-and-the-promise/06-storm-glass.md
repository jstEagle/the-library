## 6. Storm Glass

The barometer on the research station wall had been falling all afternoon, and by four o'clock it had given up entirely, sliding into the red zone like a man sliding off a barstool. Freya tapped the glass twice, the way her mother used to tap a thermometer, as though the instrument might reconsider.

"Don't bother," she told it. "You're always right and I hate you."

Outside, the sky had turned the color of an old bruise — green at the edges, which every islander knew meant business. The gulls had gone quiet, which meant more business. And the ferry had made its last crossing at noon, which meant that whatever the storm wanted to do tonight, it would be doing it to a reduced audience.

Freya worked quickly, battening down the station: instruments stowed, samples labeled, the generator tested and coaxed into a grudging cough. The station sat on the bluff above the nesting beach — a low, stubborn building of gray timber that had survived four hurricanes and one very determined raccoon — and she had ridden out storms here before, alone, with tea and a paperback and the satisfying feeling of a woman who had made excellent life choices.

She was halfway through locking the equipment locker when headlights swung up the gravel drive.

She knew the truck now. She'd known it for three weeks — knew the particular way it idled too high, knew the surveying equipment strapped in the bed, knew the silhouette of the man who stepped out of it with a city coat that had no business on this island and a folder of papers he always seemed to be clutching like a man clutching a secret.

Callum Reeve. The new neighbor. The architect. The man who asked too many careful questions about the harbor and looked at her, sometimes, like she was a sentence he was trying to finish.

She went out onto the porch. The wind took her hair immediately, an enthusiastic assault.

"You're lost," she called. "The harbor office is the other direction."

"I'm not lost." He came up the steps two at a time, coat snapping. "I was driving the coastal road and the radio said the causeway floods at high tide. I figured I'd better get off it before it did."

"So you thought you'd drive toward the water."

"I panicked," he admitted, and the honesty of it caught her off guard, the way it kept doing. "You're the only light on this end of the island. I saw the station and thought — I don't know. I thought you might know whether the road back to town goes under."

Freya looked past him at the horizon, where the sea had gone the flat, oily gray of a held breath. "It goes under. In about an hour. And it stays under until morning."

His face did something complicated. "Ah."

"Ah," she agreed.

"I could wait in the truck."

"You could," she said. "Or you could come inside before the rain arrives and help me carry the generator fuel inside, and in exchange I'll allow you to sit at my table and drink my terrible tea while the sky tries to kill us."

He considered this with the gravity of a man weighing a contract. "The tea is really that bad?"

"It's the instant kind. I stock it for emergencies."

"This is an emergency."

"Then it's perfect." She stepped aside and let him in, and told herself the warmth in her chest was relief that she wouldn't spend the storm alone, and nothing else, and certainly not the way his shoulders dropped when the door shut behind him, like a man coming home.

* * *

The storm arrived at seven, the way storms do on this island — not gradually but all at once, as though it had been waiting politely at the door and finally lost patience. Rain hit the windows sideways. The lights flickered, steadied, flickered again, and Callum was already on his feet with a flashlight before Freya could move, which annoyed her, because it was helpful, and she was trying very hard not to find him helpful.

"Generator shed's around back," she said. "If it dies, the fridge goes, and the fridge has my sediment cores in it, and I will cry."

"I'm on it." He shrugged the coat back on and went out into the deluge, and Freya watched through the window as he fought his way to the shed, drenched in four seconds flat, and got the generator running with the competence of a man who had clearly grown up somewhere storms were a fact of life rather than an event.

He came back soaked through, hair plastered to his forehead, grinning like an idiot.

"You look pleased with yourself," she said, throwing him a towel.

"I fixed something. With my hands. Do you know how long it's been since I fixed something with my hands?" He scrubbed at his hair. "I draw buildings, Freya. Other people fix things. I just make very expensive drawings and hope."

"Modest, too."

"I contain multitudes." He sat down at her worktable, which was covered in turtle data sheets, and — she noticed — carefully moved a stack of them so they wouldn't get wet, aligning the edges. A man who aligned other people's data sheets without being asked. She looked away from that quickly, the way you look away from the sun.

She made the terrible tea. He drank it without complaint, which she decided was his single most redeeming quality, ahead of the data sheets and the generator and the shoulders, in an order she refused to examine.

"So," she said, settling across from him with her own mug. "Trapped together by an act of God. We should play a game to pass the time. Truth or truth."

"That's not a game, that's an interrogation with extra steps."

"Welcome to the island. Everything here is an interrogation with extra steps. Marisol at the bookshop once got my entire dating history out of me over the course of a single purchase of a bookmark."

"Did it take long?"

"I went in for a bookmark and left forty minutes later having confessed to a broken engagement I've never told my sister about."

Callum went very still, mug halfway to his mouth. "Broken engagement," he repeated, carefully neutral, and Freya realized with a small internal wince that she'd just handed him that, unprompted, because the storm had loosened something in her and because — annoyingly — he was easy to talk to, which was the whole problem with him.

"Long story," she said. "Short version: I was going to move to Boston. Marine biology postdoc, a man who owned more than one blazer, the whole future. And then my mother got sick, and I came home, and the man with the blazers discovered he'd fallen in love with someone who didn't have a dying parent."

"That's —" He set the mug down. "That's a terrible thing."

"It was eight years ago. He wasn't evil. He was just — a fair-weather person. And I learned something useful, which is that I'd rather be alone than be someone's fair-weather person." She turned her mug in a slow circle. "So now I have turtles and a station and a bookshop and a pen pal I've never met, and people keep telling me that's not enough, and I keep not believing them."

"Pen pal," he said. His voice had changed. She couldn't have said how.

"Through the bookshop. There's this program Marisol runs — ran, whatever — 'Dear Emrys.' You write a letter, you leave it in a drawer, someone anonymous writes back. It's been going for years." She laughed a little. "It's ridiculous. I know it's ridiculous. I started writing the year my mother was diagnosed, because I couldn't say any of it out loud to anyone on this island — everyone here knew her, everyone was grieving too — and some stranger started writing back. Ten years now. He's the only person who's ever known the whole of me."

The rain hammered the windows. Callum was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read at all — something raw and cornered and almost frightened, and she thought, absurdly, *I've said too much, I've made him uncomfortable, city people don't do pen pals* — and then he said, quietly:

"Why Emrys?"

"Marisol's idea. She says everyone needs an Emrys — someone who listens without a face." Freya shrugged. "Why? Do you think it's pathetic? You can say it. Everyone thinks it."

"No," he said, too fast. "No. I think it's — " He stopped. He looked at the window, at the storm, at his own hands, and Freya watched a man visibly wrestle with something and pin it to the mat. "I think everyone should be so lucky."

"Your turn," she said, to save him from whatever that was. "Fair trade. Something you've never told anyone."

He laughed, but it didn't land as a laugh. "You first-class interrogator, you."

"I told you about the blazer man. Pay up."

He was quiet for a long moment. Outside, something loose and metallic banged against the station's north wall, and neither of them flinched.

"I didn't grow up anywhere," he said finally. "That's the short version. Group homes, mostly, after my mother died. A rotation of houses where nobody wanted you to get comfortable, because comfortable meant you'd be hard to place." He said it flatly, like reading someone else's file. "When I was sixteen, a man took me in. An old architect, retired, lived alone up the coast. Emrys Vane."

The name went through Freya like cold water.

She must have made a sound, because he glanced up. "What?"

"Nothing," she said, too quickly. "It's — it's a common name. Go on."

It was not a common name. It was a name that existed, as far as she knew, in exactly one place in the world: the return address of a hundred letters sitting in a drawer at Marisol's bookshop. *Emrys.* The name she had written at the top of every letter for ten years. The name she had signed her heart to, over and over, to a stranger.

And here was this man — this man she had been carefully not thinking about for three weeks — saying it like it was his own history.

*It's a coincidence,* she told herself. *It has to be.* Emrys Vane had been Marisol's invention, surely, or some old island name she'd dusted off. Not a real man. Not a real man who took in boys.

But Marisol had never quite said where the name came from. Freya had asked, once, years ago, and Marisol had smiled and said, *Names find their people,* which had seemed mystical and charming at the time and now seemed like a door quietly closed.

"Freya?" Callum was watching her. "You've gone somewhere."

"I'm here." She made herself smile. "You were saying. The old architect."

"He taught me to draw. Taught me that buildings were promises — that you're telling people *you'll be warm here, you'll be safe here*, and you'd better mean it." Callum's mouth curved, but his eyes had gone somewhere far away. "He died about ten years back. Left me everything — the training, the license money, the start. On one condition."

"Which was?"

He was quiet so long she thought he wouldn't answer. The generator hummed. The storm threw rain against the glass like a handful of gravel.

"He made me promise to answer the letters that matter," he said. "His words. I was twenty-two and I didn't know what he meant. I still don't, most days. I've been trying to build a life worth — " He stopped himself. Shook his head. "It doesn't matter. It's an old man's poetry. I took a job I shouldn't have taken, and that's the real story, and I'm not telling you that one tonight."

"Callum —"

"Freya." He said her name like a man setting something down very carefully. "Can I tell you the thing I've never told anyone? The actual thing?"

Her heart was doing something unprofessional. "Yes."

"I've spent ten years writing to someone I've never met." The words came out low, almost drowned by the storm. "Not — it's not — " And then he closed his mouth, visibly, like a man catching a fish and throwing it back. He dragged a hand through his hair. "Forget it. That came out wrong. I meant — I've spent ten years building a career for someone who isn't here to see it, which is its own kind of correspondence, if you squint."

"That's not what you were going to say."

"No," he agreed. "But it's what you're getting tonight."

Freya studied him across the table — this soaked, guarded, generous man who fixed generators and aligned data sheets and carried secrets the way other men carried briefcases — and felt the ground shift under a suspicion she hadn't known she was standing on. *Emrys Vane. Ten years. Letters that matter.* The pieces were all there, loose on the table, refusing to assemble into anything she could look at directly.

Because if she looked at it directly, then either the world was a small, strange, impossible machine — or she was sitting across from the only person who had ever known the whole of her, and he was lying to her about the harbor, and both of those things could be true at once.

"More tea?" she said.

"God, yes," he said, relieved, and she poured it, and they talked for hours after that — about everything and nothing, about her turtles and his first drawing, about whether gulls sleep during storms (they do, badly, in the lee of things), and every hour the suspicion sat quietly in her chest like a swallowed stone, and every hour the warmth sat beside it, and the two of them did not cancel out. That was the terrible thing. They did not cancel out.

* * *

Near midnight the storm eased to a long, steady rain, and Freya spread blankets on the station's two cots, on opposite sides of the main room, with the diplomatic precision of a treaty negotiator.

"North cot's yours," she said. "It's nearer the window, so if the roof goes, you're the canary."

"Chivalrous."

"I contain multitudes."

He laughed in the dark, and she heard him settle, and for a while there was only rain and the generator's heartbeat.

"Freya," he said, when she was nearly asleep. "The letters. Yours. The ones you write to Emrys."

"Mm."

"What do you write about? When it's not — grief. What do you tell him?"

She lay in the dark and thought about it. About the letters describing the first hatchlings of every season, the smell of rain on the flats, the way loneliness could be a kind of weather you learned to dress for. About the letter she'd written last month, before she'd known his name: *Someone new moved in next door. He has too many books and a coat that's wrong for this island, and I think I'm in trouble.*

"Everything," she said. "I tell him everything."

A long silence. When Callum spoke again, his voice had a fracture in it that the storm almost covered.

"I hope he knows," he said, "how lucky he is."

She fell asleep before she could ask what he meant, and dreamed of a drawer full of letters, and woke at dawn to a washed-clean sky and a man already outside on the porch, looking out at the flooded causeway, holding a piece of paper he folded quickly away when he heard the door.

"Road'll be passable by noon," she said.

"Right." He turned, and he was wearing that careful face again, the one she was starting to recognize as the face he wore when he was deciding what not to say. "Thank you. For the tea. For the — all of it."

"Any time the sky tries to kill us," she said lightly. "I'm here all season."

He drove off down the coastal road, and Freya stood on the porch a long time, watching the truck shrink, and then she went inside and sat down at the worktable and pulled out a fresh sheet of paper.

*Dear Emrys,* she wrote. *Last night a man I think I'm falling for told me a story about someone named Emrys Vane, and I need you to tell me it's a coincidence, because you're the only one who's ever told me the truth about anything.*

*Except now I'm not sure you have.*

She stopped, pen hovering, and looked at the words, and did not cross them out.

She folded the letter in half and did not seal it, and set it on the table where she could see it, and went to check on her sediment cores with her heart going like the rain had gone — all at once, and sideways, and with no intention of stopping.

Because there was one more thing she hadn't said last night, one thing she'd kept back even from herself: the note Callum had left on her door her first week on the island, the one about the borrowed ladder, in handwriting she had looked at for exactly as long as it takes to read *back by five — C.R.*

And this morning, in the gray light, she had looked at the folded letter on her table and thought, with a clarity that felt like cold water: *I know that handwriting.*

She just needed to prove it.
