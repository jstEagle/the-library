## 7. What Emrys Knew

The letter sat in Callum's breast pocket the way it always did on bad days — a small square of weight against his heart, like a stone he'd chosen to carry.

He was standing in the site office at half past six in the morning, before anyone else arrived, with the redevelopment plans spread across the folding table and the smell of burnt coffee from a machine that had never once made good coffee. Outside the window, the harbor lay silver and patient under a sky the color of oyster shell. Somewhere past the headland, out of sight but not out of mind, was a beach where turtles nested. He had walked it at low tide with Freya Thornbury three days ago, and he had not slept properly since.

But that wasn't why he'd taken the letter out.

He took it out because it was Tuesday, and for eleven years Tuesdays had been letter days, and some habits go deeper than sense.

*Dear Freya,* the old letter began, in handwriting that wasn't his. *You asked me last week whether I believe people can change. I've been thinking about my answer, and I find I want to give you a better one than the first one I gave you...*

Emrys Vane's handwriting. Emrys Vane's words. Callum had copied them out himself, years ago, in his own hand, because a dead man cannot keep writing letters no matter how much the living need him to.

Callum set the page down on top of the blueprints and looked at the two documents side by side — the letter and the plans — and felt something in his chest turn over like a boat taking water.

Because here was the thing Callum Reeve had never told anyone, not Marisol, not the fishermen at the Anchor, certainly not Freya: the land those blueprints would bury belonged, by every promise that mattered, to a dead man. And the career that had carried him off this island and made him someone worth being — that too.

It all came back to Emrys. It always had.

* * *

Callum was fifteen the winter the state finally gave up on his mother, though nobody used those words. What they said was *placement*, said it in fluorescent rooms by men with laminated badges, and what it meant was that Callum would be moving again — fourth time in two years — to another stranger's house with rules taped to the refrigerator.

Except this time the stranger was different.

Emrys Vane was seventy-one years old, a retired marine cartographer with hands like driftwood and a house on the island's north shore that smelled of pipe smoke, cedar, and the particular dustiness of ten thousand maps. He had known Callum's grandfather back when the harbor still worked fishing boats instead of tourists, and when the caseworker called him — the last name on a list nobody expected to reach — he'd said only, "Send the boy. I'll need help with the boats anyway."

There were no boats. There hadn't been boats in twenty years. But Callum learned quickly that Emrys Vane lied the way other men breathed — casually, constantly, and almost always kindly.

"You'll be wanting breakfast," Emrys said the first morning, and produced eggs, toast, marmalade, and tea strong enough to strip paint. "I don't cook for myself anymore. Can't see the point. But a growing boy needs feeding, so I suppose I'll have to manage."

He cooked for Callum every single day for four years.

The house had a room at the back that Emrys called the chart room, though it held more books than charts — walls of them, sagging shelves, stacks on the floor in teetering cities. In the corner stood a desk with a green glass lamp, and on the desk, every week without fail, appeared a stack of letters.

They were extraordinary letters. Callum found them by accident, snooping the way fifteen-year-olds do, expecting nothing and finding instead a correspondence that spanned decades. A lighthouse keeper in Scotland. A nun in Portugal. A boy in Mumbai who wrote about cricket and monsoons. A woman in Nova Scotia who had lost her son to the sea and written to Emrys about it every month for nine years, and been answered every month for nine years, patiently, tenderly, until the tenth year when her letter said *thank you, I think I'm going to be alright now,* and Emrys had read it aloud to Callum with tears running freely down his seamed face and said, "That one took a decade, lad. Worth every stamp."

"What is all this?" Callum asked.

Emrys lit his pipe, which was against the rules and which they both knew it. "People write to strangers," he said, "when the truth is too heavy for the people who know them. Did you know that? You can tell your mother you're fine. You can tell your best friend you're fine. But a stranger — a stranger gets the truth, because a stranger has nothing to lose by hearing it." He tapped ash into a saucer shaped like a fish. "So there are folk out there carrying things too heavy, and most of them have nowhere to put them down. I put an advertisement in a magazine forty years ago. *Will answer letters.* That's all it said. They haven't stopped coming since."

"Answered letters," Callum repeated. "That's it? That's what you do?"

"That," said Emrys, "is everything. Everything else is weather."

* * *

It took Callum two years to understand what Emrys actually meant by that.

He understood it the night he came home at seventeen, furious, having punched Danny Kellerman behind the chandlery for saying something about Callum's mother that no one should say about anyone's mother. His knuckles were split. His future, as far as he could tell, was split too — another placement, another stranger, another refrigerator with rules taped to it.

Emrys didn't shout. Emrys sat him down at the kitchen table, wrapped his hand in a dish towel, and poured two glasses of the terrible sherry he kept for occasions.

"Do you know why I take in strays?" Emrys asked.

"Because you need help with the boats."

A dry snort of smoke. "Because when I was younger than you, I did something unforgivable, and I've spent fifty years trying to make the arithmetic come out even." He turned his glass slowly. "I was an engineer, young. Before the maps. I designed things. Big things — harbors, seawalls, a marina complex up the coast that I was very proud of. Proud enough not to look too closely at what it sat on top of. There was a tidal marsh there. Birds, fish, a whole quiet nation of small lives. The surveys were adjusted. Mine were. I signed them." He looked up. "The marsh died within three years. So did the fishing that fed four families I knew by name. One of them was your grandfather's family, if you want the whole of it."

Callum stared at him.

"I built a beautiful thing on a lie," Emrys said, "and the lie is what lasted. The building will fall down someday; lies are more durable than concrete. So." He refilled Callum's glass, unasked. "Here is what I know, boy, and you may take it or leave it. Every person is answering a letter whether they mean to or not. Life writes to you — trouble writes to you, grief, opportunity, all of it, letters arriving daily — and what you do next is your reply. Most people answer carelessly. Some people answer beautifully. And a rare few—" he pointed the pipe stem at Callum's bandaged hand— "answer the same letter twice, because the first answer was wrong, and they spend their whole lives trying to send a better one."

"I'm not sorry I hit him," Callum said mutinously.

"No. And I'm not asking you to be. I'm telling you that you get to choose what kind of man answers the next letter." Emrys rose, joints cracking like ship's rigging, and fetched the first-aid kit. "Danny Kellerman's father drank, you know. And his father before him. The boy's angry at a world that taught him anger is inherited. Doesn't excuse him. Explains him, though, and explanation is the beginning of mercy, and mercy is the beginning of everything worth building."

He taped Callum's knuckles with the competence of a man who had patched up sixty years of fishermen, then said, lightly, as if remarking on the tide:

"There's a girl on this island who writes letters to me through Marisol's shop. Been writing since she was small. Her mother's ill, you know — cancer, the long kind. She signs herself F., and she writes about turtles mostly, but between the lines she writes about being afraid, and being alone in a house with a dying woman, and not having anyone to say so to." He struck a match for his pipe. "I answer her. Have done for years. And I'll tell you something else, since we're being honest tonight: I won't be able to forever. Seventy-four next spring, and the eyes are going. Some mornings the pen wanders off the line like a drunk."

Callum said nothing. His heart was doing something strange.

"So here's my proposition," Emrys said. "Not today. Not this year, maybe. But one day — when I'm gone, or near enough — I'd have you answer the letters that matter. Not all of them. Just the ones that matter." He looked at Callum over the pipe smoke, and his eyes, watery as they were, missed nothing. "You've got a good heart under all that armor, boy. I've seen it. It's the one inheritance I've got to leave you, and it's better than land."

* * *

Land, though, there was also.

Callum learned about the land the following autumn, when Emrys took him walking the north shore with a surveyor's compass and a thermos of soup, pointing out boundaries with the delight of a man revealing a magic trick.

"Everything from the high-water mark to the old fence line," Emrys said, sweeping an arm across forty acres of dune grass, salt scrub, and wind-bent pines. "Mine. Bought it in 1962 with map money, when land here cost nothing because everyone was leaving. Everyone said I was a fool." He grinned. "Everyone was right, but I enjoyed it."

"Why'd you buy it?"

"To keep it safe." Emrys said it simply, the way he said true things. "This island eats its own shoreline, lad. Every generation somebody comes along with money and vision and a plan, and the plan is always the same — pave it, wall it, make it useful. And the island lets them, because islands are polite that way, right up until they aren't." He crouched and pressed his palm flat to the sand. "Under this beach, this time of year, are turtle nests. Have been since before my grandfather. The turtles don't know about deeds and fences. All they know is that this beach has always been here, and they come back to it the way the faithful come back to church." He stood, knees popping. "When I die, this land goes to whoever I trust to keep the faith. At present the shortlist is one name long."

"And if that person sells?"

"Then I shall haunt him," said Emrys placidly. "I've had practice. I intend to be very good at it."

* * *

He was not good at it, in the end, because he was not given time.

The stroke came in March, four days after Callum's nineteenth birthday, and it was efficient the way the sea is efficient. Emrys lingered eleven days in the county hospital, lucid in patches, and in one of the clear ones he gripped Callum's wrist with a strength that surprised them both.

"Papers are with Halloway the solicitor," he said. "House, land, the lot. Don't argue. Arguing wastes the time." His breath whistled. "The letters. Marisol knows the arrangement — she'll forward them. Answer the ones that matter, Callum. You'll know which ones. Your heart will know, even when your head's making excuses." His grip tightened. "And the land. Keep the land. Promise me the land."

"I promise," Callum said, and meant it with everything in him.

Emrys smiled — the same crooked, conspiratorial smile he'd worn over every kind lie he'd ever told — and said, "Good lad. Now fetch the nurse. And Callum—" the grip again, weaker— "don't waste your life paying debts. Pay them fast, and then live. That's the whole point of arithmetic."

He died on a Tuesday. Letter day.

* * *

For a while afterward, Callum answered the letters badly. Grief made his pen clumsy; he wrote stiff, dutiful replies that sounded like a schoolmaster, and he knew it, and hated it. Then one evening a letter arrived from F. — the turtle girl, grown now, seventeen maybe eighteen — describing the night her mother died. Not the death itself. The hours after. How she'd walked down to the beach at three in the morning because she couldn't be in the house, and how the tide had come in over her feet, cold as a fact, and how she had stood there thinking: *the sea doesn't stop for anything, and somehow that's the only comfort left.*

Callum read it five times. Then he sat at the desk under the green lamp, in the chair that still smelled faintly of pipe smoke, and wrote back the first honest letter of his life.

*I stood on that same beach after my mother left,* he wrote. *Different year, same water. You're right that it doesn't stop. But here's what I've learned since: neither do the turtles. Every summer they come back to that beach and dig and lay and trust it entirely, and the world gives them no reason to, and they do it anyway. That's not foolishness. That's faith with calluses. You have it too. I've read it in every letter you've sent.*

He signed it *Emrys,* because the letters came addressed to Emrys, because the fiction was the vessel and the truth was the cargo, and because — he told himself, and mostly believed — a promise is a promise, and the dead deserve continuity.

Then he folded the letter, sealed it, and thought: *I need to become someone worth these words.* Because the guilt was already there, quiet and corrosive — the guilt of a boy signing a dead man's name, borrowing a goodness he hadn't earned. He wanted, desperately, to deserve the desk he sat at. To build something real. To stand in front of Emrys's grave someday and say: *look. Look what your letters made.*

Architecture school took the money the land couldn't earn sitting empty — he leased the far field the first year, hating himself, promising it was temporary. Scholarships took the rest. He was good, then he was very good, then he was the kind of good that firms fight over, and every award, every commission, every glossy rendering went into the invisible ledger he kept against Emrys Vane's name. *This one's for the letters. This one's for the land. This one's for the marsh you couldn't save.*

Eleven years. He paid and paid and the debt never seemed to shrink, because he kept spending the payments on becoming someone new, someone city-made, someone whose coat was too warm for islands — and the person he'd been trying to pay kept getting further away.

* * *

Now Callum stood in the site office at half past seven, the letter in one hand and the blueprints under the other, and the arithmetic finally, brutally, refused to balance.

The nesting beach fell inside parcel four. Parcel four was load-bearing: the investors' financing required the full footprint, the marina apron, the resort terraces stepping down to a beach that would be renourished with dredged sand — dead sand, imported sand, sand no turtle would ever choose. He'd known this for months, abstractly, the way you know a storm is forecast. It had taken Freya Thornbury walking him across the tidal flats at dawn, her voice low and certain, her hand briefly steadying him over the slick rocks, to make it concrete. To make it *her*.

And here was the trap, sprung shut and elegant: the land was Emrys's gift, held in trust for turtles and faith. The career was Emrys's debt, paid in buildings. And the development — the career's crowning commission, the one that would finally clear every ledger, fund the office he dreamed of, the life worth describing in letters — the development would bulldoze the trust to pay the debt.

Pay them fast, and then live, Emrys had said. But what if the paying destroyed the thing you were supposed to be living for?

Callum slid the letter back into his breast pocket. Through the window, the tide was turning, the flats emerging inch by inch, glistening, patient. Somewhere out there Freya was counting nests, writing data into her logs with the same steady hand that wrote him letters every Tuesday — letters he answered as a ghost, wearing a dead man's name, while living next door to her as a man she was beginning, God help him, to trust.

His phone buzzed. A message from his project lead: *Investors call moved up to Thursday. They want confirmation the schedule holds. Especially parcel four.*

Callum looked at the message for a long moment. Then he typed: *Understood. Reviewing options.*

Three words. A door left open, barely, in a wall he'd built himself.

Outside, the sun cleared the headland and lit the harbor gold, and somewhere beyond it a beach waited, faithful and doomed, holding its buried hundreds of small futures in the dark beneath the sand — and Callum Reeve picked up his coffee, cold and terrible, and understood that whatever he decided by Thursday, he was going to break something irreplaceable.

The only question left was which promise.
