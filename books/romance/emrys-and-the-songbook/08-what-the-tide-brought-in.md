## 8. What the Tide Brought In

Wren woke to gulls and the smell of someone else's coffee, which was a strange combination of things to be grateful for, but she was.

The inn had a particular quality of silence at six a.m.—the kind that came from old buildings that had learned to hold their breath. She'd slept better than she had any right to, in a bed that wasn't hers, in a house full of strangers who believed she was dating a man she'd been lying to for four days now. Five. She'd lost count, which was its own kind of confession.

She found the coffee in the kitchen, made by nobody, left in a pot with a note that said *help yourself* in looping handwriting that could only belong to a woman who ran an inn for thirty years. Marisol Vasquez. The mother. Wren had watched her last night at dinner, cataloguing the way you'd catalogue an engine: what was worn, what was holding, what was about to give. Marisol had been warm to Wren in a way that felt like an apology she couldn't deliver to her son directly. Every kindness aimed at Wren had landed sideways on Emrys, and he'd sat there taking it like a man used to shrapnel.

Wren poured a cup and took it out the back door, because the tide was out and she wanted to see it.

The beach behind the inn was gray-gold in the early light, strewn with the night's deliveries: kelp in ropes, a crab carapace, a piece of blue plastic that had once been something useful. The water was doing that thing she loved, pulling back to show you what it usually kept hidden—sandbars, old pilings, the bones of a dock that had died before she was born.

She was twenty feet down the beach, boots sinking, when she heard the screen door.

Emrys came out in a sweater that had been expensive once and was now just soft, a mug in his hands, hair doing something unguarded. He hadn't seen her yet. She had maybe three seconds to decide whether to announce herself or watch him a moment longer, and she chose the watching, because that was the problem with her, wasn't it—she was always choosing to watch him from a distance, from the front row, from across a mechanic's bay, and never once had she chosen to be seen.

He saw her. He didn't startle. That was the thing about Emrys Vasquez—everything about him was quiet, even his surprise. He lifted the mug a half inch in salute and came down the steps and across the sand like a man approaching a horse he didn't want to spook.

"Couldn't sleep?" he asked.

"Slept fine. The bed's better than mine. Mine has a spring that's trying to communicate with me."

He almost smiled. She was keeping score now, his almost-smiles, and this was number eleven.

They walked. The beach didn't require conversation, which was the best thing about it, and for a while neither of them offered any. The tide line glittered. Somewhere behind them the inn's weathervane creaked its one note.

"I used to walk this beach," he said finally. "Every morning in the summer. My mother had a rule—if you slept in her house, you saw the ocean before you saw your phone."

"Sound rule."

"I broke it yesterday. Checked my phone in the car before I'd even parked."

"Rebel."

"Ten years of rebellion," he said, and there it was, the flatness that came into his voice when the past got too close, the tone she'd learned to hear the way other people learned accents. "That's what my sister calls it. My decade of rebellion. Not coming home. Not answering. Not—"

He stopped. He turned a shell over with his toe, a scallop, the color of a fingernail.

"You don't have to tell me," Wren said.

"That's the strange thing." He looked at her then, fully, and she felt it in her sternum, the specific weight of Emrys's attention, which was not a small thing to receive. "I want to. I keep wanting to tell you things. I don't know what to do with that."

The tide sucked at the sand between them. Wren held her coffee and thought: *I know exactly what you're doing. You're doing the thing where you practice on someone safe. I'm the rehearsal room. And the cruelest part is that I'm not safe at all.*

"Tell me about the concert," she said.

She hadn't planned it. The words came out the way water comes under a door—finds the gap, takes it.

Emrys went still. Not dramatically. Just a man on a beach who stopped walking.

"Which concert?" he said, and his voice was so carefully level that it was louder than shouting.

"Your sister mentioned—" *Careful. Careful.* "Last night. She said you used to play. That you were good."

"I was good." He said it like a fact about someone else. "I was—" He laughed, one note, no humor in it. "You know what the reviews said? After? They said *a promising career interrupted.* Interrupted. Like a phone call. Like I'd stepped out of the room and might come back."

"Do you want to come back?"

He was quiet for so long she counted the waves. Seven. Eight.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the honest answer. Everyone wants me to want it. My mother, my sister, the ghost of every teacher I ever had. And I can't even get my hands to—" He looked down at them, wrapped around the mug, long fingers she'd watched a hundred times without letting herself know why. "They're fine. That's the joke. Physically fine. Nothing wrong with them. I've had every test. It's just that somewhere between my brain and the strings there's a door, and it closed, and I can't find the handle."

"What do you think's behind the door?"

He looked at her like she'd asked the question nobody had asked in ten years. Everyone else had asked *why* or *when are you coming back* or *have you tried*. Nobody had asked what was behind it.

"Maybe nothing," he said. "Maybe that's what scares me. That I open it and there's just an empty room."

"I don't think it's empty," Wren said. "Rooms that are empty don't scare people. It's the rooms with something in them."

He stared at her. The gulls argued over something down the beach. And Wren stood there with the whole truth sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone—*I was there, I was in the front row, I saw your hands stop, I wrote the letter, I ended your engagement, I have been carrying you for ten years without your permission*—and what came out of her mouth instead was:

"My family's dead."

His face changed. "Wren—"

"Not all of them. Technically. My dad's alive somewhere. Last I heard, Reno. But the family—the thing that makes a family a family instead of a list of relatives—that's gone. My mom when I was nineteen. Car accident, if you want the whole inventory. Then my grandmother two years later, and my dad decided grief was a place he'd rather live alone, and that was that." She heard her own voice go brisk, the mechanic's voice, the one that turned everything into a problem with a fix or a write-off. "So when your sister asked me last night about my people, my traditions, my *history*—I did the thing where I smile and say 'we weren't really a holiday family.' Which is technically true. We were a family the way a boat is a boat. It floated for a while."

Emrys had stopped walking. He was looking at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere, and she realized with a lurch that it was the expression she'd seen on his face in old footage, the one from interviews, when someone asked about the music. Like she'd said something true and he hadn't been braced for it.

"Nobody ever told me that," he said.

"You didn't ask."

"I should have asked." He said it like a fault in his own hull. "I've been so busy managing my own wreckage I didn't—Wren, I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. It's not a wound, it's just—" She gestured at the beach, the wrack line, the delivered debris of the night. "It's just what the tide brought in. You get up in the morning and you walk the line and you see what you have to deal with. That's all grief is. Beachcombing."

"That's the bleakest metaphor I've ever heard."

"I'm a mechanic. We're not a poetic trade."

"You're the most poetic mechanic I've ever met."

"I'm the only mechanic you've ever met."

"That's not true," he said, and then stopped, and she watched him decide whether to finish the sentence, and he finished it: "I met a lot of people in ten years. You're the first one who talks about grief like it's a work order. It's the first time anyone's made it sound survivable."

The tide came in around their boots, cold through the leather, and neither of them moved.

"Here's what I keep thinking," Emrys said, slowly, like a man testing ice. "You said your family floated like a boat. And mine's an inn that's being sold. And we're both standing on a beach at low tide looking at what's left." He turned to face her fully. "So here's a thought. A bad one, probably. A transactional thought, since that's our language."

"Those are my favorite kind."

"The contract says the weekend. But the contract doesn't say—" He stopped, restarted. "My family is loud and intrusive and they will feed you until you beg for mercy, and my sister will interrogate you, and my mother will love you in that terrifying way she loves people, and none of it is real, I know it's not real, but it's *available*. It's right there. And you don't have any. And I—" His jaw worked. "I don't have anyone who looks at me the way you looked at me just now. Like I'm a person and not a tragedy with a name."

Wren's heart was doing something she refused to name.

"Are you offering me your family?" she said. "As part of the fake-dating package?"

"I'm offering you breakfast," he said. "And my sister's interrogation, and my mother's terrifying love, and a place at a table where people will remember what you like. For the weekend. And maybe—" He looked out at the water. "Maybe after. If you want it. I know it's not the same as the real thing. I know it's secondhand. But it's—"

"It's not secondhand," Wren heard herself say. "A family that takes you in isn't secondhand. It's just a family."

He looked at her like she'd handed him something heavy and he hadn't known he was carrying the other end.

"Okay," he said quietly.

"Okay," she said.

They stood there in the cold water and the early light and Wren thought: *this is the moment. This is the moment to tell him. He's offering you everything and you're standing here holding the letter in your pocket like a stone you've carried so long you've forgotten it's not part of your body.*

"I have to tell you something," she started.

Emrys's phone buzzed. He glanced at it, and his face did the thing—the careful flattening, the door closing.

"It's my sister. She wants to know if we're coming to breakfast or if we've eloped." He tried for light and mostly made it. "We should go in. She'll come looking, and she's a lawyer, she'll consider it a deposition."

"Right," Wren said. "Breakfast."

They walked back up the beach, side by side, not touching, and the gap between their hands was the width of everything she hadn't said.

At the top of the steps Emrys paused, one hand on the rail, and looked back at the water.

"You asked what's behind the door," he said. "I think it might be this. This exact morning. I think the room isn't empty. I think it's full of mornings I didn't have."

He went inside. Wren stood on the steps with her cold coffee and her stone of a secret, and behind her the tide was already coming in, patient and merciless, covering the footprints they'd left side by side in the sand—covering everything, the way it always did, until the next storm dredged it all back up.

Inside, she could hear his sister's voice, bright and probing, and under it, faintly, from the kitchen, the sound of Emrys tapping a rhythm on the counter—restless, anxious, four beats and a rest, four beats and a rest.

She knew that rhythm.

She'd been listening to it, in one way or another, for ten years.

* * *

Breakfast was eggs and ambush.

"So, Wren." Delia—Emrys's sister, the lawyer, the one with eyes like a customs officer—poured coffee she didn't drink and sat down across from Wren with the pleasant menace of someone opening a folder. "Ferry mechanic. That's a specific line of work for someone dating a musician."

"Ex-musician," Emrys said, too fast.

"Right." Delia's eyes didn't move off Wren. "And how did you two meet, exactly? Because Emrys said the garage, but he's vague on details, and Emrys is never vague on details. He's vague on *feelings*. Details he memorizes."

"Delia," their mother said from the stove, in a voice that had clearly ended a thousand arguments, but gently, the way you'd end them in a house with guests.

"It's a fair question, Mom. We're all just curious." She smiled at Wren. "It's just—your face is familiar. I keep thinking I've seen you somewhere. Ferry schedule? Local paper? You're not in politics, are you?"

"Delia." Emrys's voice had gone quiet, which Wren was learning was worse than loud.

"I'm just making conversation." Delia spread jam with terrible calm. "It's a small state, Wren. Small islands. You'd be amazed what turns up."

Wren smiled the smile she'd practiced in truck mirrors her whole life, and under the table, her knee pressed against Emrys's—solid, warm, a lie they were both holding up—and she thought: *not yet. Not this morning. But the tide's coming in, and it always brings everything back.*

Delia raised her coffee in a small toast, eyes bright with something Wren couldn't yet name.

"To new friends," she said. "And old ones."

The words sat on the table like something the tide had left.
