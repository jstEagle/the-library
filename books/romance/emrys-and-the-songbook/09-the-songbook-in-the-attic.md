## 9. The Songbook in the Attic

The attic of the Merrow Island Inn smelled like cedar and forty years of salt air, and it was full of everything my family had ever been too sentimental to throw away.

Wren had claimed the far corner by the dormer window, where the light came in gray and forgiving, wrapping teacups in newspaper with the efficiency of someone who'd packed a life before. She'd rolled her sleeves up. There was a smudge of dust on her forearm that I kept noticing instead of working, the way you keep touching a bruise.

"You're staring," she said, not looking up.

"I'm supervising."

"Supervising." She set a cup down and looked at me over the rim of her glasses—reading glasses, drugstore glasses, glasses that made her look like a librarian who could also rebuild an engine, which was accurate. "You've wrapped one lamp in two hours."

"The lamp required emotional processing."

"It's a lamp."

"It's a lamp from the dining room," I said. "It hung over table six. Table six is where Dad always sat because he said he could see the whole room from there, and Mom said that was nonsense, he just liked being near the kitchen door so he could steal food on his way past." I turned the lamp base in my hands. The brass was dull, the shade yellowed. "Everything here requires emotional processing. That's the whole problem with this island."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then she got up, crossed the attic, took the lamp out of my hands, wrapped it in four efficient turns of newspaper, and boxed it.

"There," she said. "Processed. Next."

I loved her a little, right then. I noted it the way you note weather coming in off the water—distantly, with a kind of dread, knowing there was nothing to be done about it now except watch it arrive.

* * *

We'd been packing for three days. My mother had made lists. My sister Priya had made spreadsheets, which was somehow worse, and had assigned us all zones like we were invading a small, dusty country. Wren had been folded into the operation without discussion—Priya handing her tape, my mother calling her *dear* and asking if she'd eaten—and every time it happened, Wren glanced at me like she couldn't quite believe it either. Like she was waiting for someone to check her credentials at the door.

Nobody did. That was the thing about my family: they were terrible at talking to each other and magnificent at absorbing strangers. We were a tide pool. Things washed in and stayed.

By the third afternoon we'd cleared the guest rooms, the linen closets, the office with its decades of ledgers in my father's cramped handwriting, and all that remained was the attic—the place where the inn kept its memory, the way a body keeps a scar.

It was Priya who found the trunk.

"Emrys." Her voice came from behind a wall of boxes near the north gable. "Come look at this."

I knew what it was before I saw it. I knew by the way she said my name—not *Emrys, come help me lift this*, but *Emrys* with the vowel held a half-beat too long, the way you say something when you've recognized it and aren't sure whether to show it or bury it.

The trunk was cedar, dented at one corner where it had taken a fall down the back stairs sometime in the nineties—I remembered the sound of it, the crack, my father swearing cheerfully. E.V. was stenciled on the lid in white paint gone amber with age. My initials. My childhood initials, painted by my father's hand, back when I was the only E.V. in the house.

"That's mine," I said, stupidly.

"Yeah," Priya said gently. "I figured."

My mother appeared at the top of the stairs with a box of picture frames balanced on her hip, took one look at the trunk, and went very still.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, I wondered where that went."

"Mom. What is it?"

She set the frames down. She looked at me with an expression I couldn't read—something between apology and permission. "Your father packed it when you left for conservatory. He said—" She stopped. Started again. "He said a musician should have his beginnings somewhere safe. In case he ever wanted to find them again."

I knelt in front of the trunk. The hasps were brass, green with tarnish. My hands—my hands, which had their own history, which had their own weather—hovered over them.

"Do you want us to go?" Priya asked.

"No," I said. Then, honestly: "Yes. No. Stay."

Wren had come over quietly from the window. She didn't ask anything. She just sat down on the floor beside me, cross-legged, close enough that our shoulders almost touched, and said, "Whatever it is, it's just a box."

It wasn't just a box. But I opened it anyway.

* * *

Inside: rosin tins. A metronome with a cracked face. My first bow, half-size, the frog worn smooth by a child's grip. And beneath all of it, wrapped in a wool blanket that still smelled faintly of my father's closet—

A songbook.

Not a method book. Not the Suzuki volumes my teachers assigned, with their tidy graded pieces. This was a composition notebook, spiral-bound, the cover soft with handling, my name written across the front in pencil: *EMRYS VASQUEZ — ORIGINAL WORKS — DO NOT TOUCH (THIS MEANS YOU, PRIYA)*.

Priya laughed, one startled note. "He wrote DO NOT TOUCH."

"He wrote it because you touched everything."

"I was eight."

"You were a menace."

I lifted the book out and the room went quiet around me. Even the rain on the roof seemed to lean in. I hadn't seen this notebook in fifteen years. I hadn't thought about it in ten, and even then only the way you think about a door you've decided never to open again.

But my hands remembered it. My hands, traitors and historians both, were already reaching for the cover.

I opened it.

The first pages were childish—melodies in wobbly notation, lyrics about lighthouse keepers and dogs, time signatures that defied mathematics. My father had written little comments in the margins in his blocky carpenter's print: *Nice one, kid.* *This one sounds like your grandmother.* *Play this for me Sunday?*

I turned the pages and the handwriting matured. The melodies grew spines. By the middle of the book I was fourteen, fifteen, writing with the terrifying fluency of a teenager who doesn't yet know that talent can leave—that it can stand up in the middle of your life and walk out of the room while everyone watches.

And then, three-quarters through, a page that stopped my breath.

The title, underlined twice, in ink:

***For the Girl in the Front Row***

I stared at it. I had no memory of writing it. Or rather—I had a memory of the *kind* of day it must have been, the kind of afternoon when a sixteen-year-old writes a piece with a title like that: a summer day, the dining room empty between services, sun coming through the west windows, some girl I'd noticed at a concert or on the ferry or on the beach, some stranger whose face I'd already forgotten but whose existence had apparently moved me enough to fill four pages of staff paper.

"For the Girl in the Front Row," Wren read softly, over my shoulder.

Her voice did something strange on the words. It caught, or thinned, or dropped half a register—I couldn't tell which. I felt her go still beside me, a stillness different from attention. Different from interest. Closer to the stillness of an animal that has heard something in the brush.

"It's nothing," I said. "I wrote a hundred of these. Half of them are titled after people I invented. There's one called 'For the Lighthouse Keeper's Cat.' I never met the cat."

"What's it sound like?" Priya asked.

"I don't know. I never played it. I never played any of them—for anyone." I ran my thumb down the page. The notes swam up at me, familiar and foreign, like faces in a yearbook. "That was the rule I made myself. Compositions were—mine. Everything else I ever did was for judges and juries and audiences and my teachers' approval. This book was the only place I wasn't performing."

"And now the inn's selling," my mother said quietly, "and the book needs somewhere to live."

"Now the book needs somewhere to live," I agreed.

I should have closed it then. I should have wrapped it in newspaper, boxed it, labeled it, processed it, the way Wren would have. Instead I kept turning pages, and the attic light went on getting grayer, and nobody spoke, and the rain kept up its patient applause against the roof.

* * *

Later—much later, after Priya and my mother had carried load after load down the narrow stairs, after the attic had emptied into boxes and the boxes into the truck bed—Wren was still there. Sitting on the floor by the dormer with the last unsealed carton, watching me pack the songbook with more care than I'd shown anything else in this house.

"You could play it," she said. "Someday. If you wanted."

"It's been ten years, Wren."

"I know how long it's been." Something in her voice made me look up. She was pale. Not tired-pale, not attic-dust-pale. Pale the way water goes before it freezes. "I just mean—it's a good piece. From what I can read." She nodded at the open page in my lap. "The one you're holding. It reads like it means something."

I looked down at *For the Girl in the Front Row*. At the ink, faded brown now. At the sixteen-year-old who'd written it for a stranger.

"Wren? You okay?"

She stood too fast. Knocked the carton with her knee. "Fine. Just—attics. Dust." She smiled, and it was the smile she used at dinner tables, the performance smile, flawless and wrong. "We should finish the north wall before dark."

She picked up a box and carried it toward the stairs, and I watched her go, and the strangeness of her walked out of the room with her like a draft.

I looked back down at the songbook. At the title page. *For the Girl in the Front Row.*

Somewhere below me, a box settled. Somewhere outside, the wind changed direction, and the first fat drops of a real storm began to hit the roof.

I sat alone in the emptying attic, holding a piece of music I'd written for a girl I'd never met, and for the first time in ten years, I wondered—
