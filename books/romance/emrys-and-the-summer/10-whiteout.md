## 10. Whiteout

The storm did not arrive so much as it annexed the coast.

Emrys woke on the second morning to a silence so complete it felt like pressure — the particular hush of a world buried under a foot and a half of snow, with more coming down in fat, wind-driven sheets that erased the fence line, the vine rows, the very concept of horizon. The cottage windows had gone opaque at the corners, frost feathering inward like something growing. He lay in the narrow bed in Ansel's grandfather's old room and listened to the house creak and settle around him, and he thought, with a strange calm: *I am not going anywhere.*

The ridge road had washed out sometime before dawn. They'd heard it happen — or rather, they'd heard the absence of it, a long subterranean groan beneath the wind that Ansel, half-asleep on the couch, had identified without opening his eyes. "Culvert's gone," he'd said. "Happens every twenty years or so. Granddad called it the road paying rent."

Now Emrys stood at the kitchen window with a mug of coffee going cold in his hands and watched Julian pace the length of the front room for what had to be the fortieth time since breakfast.

"There has to be another way out," Julian said. He was still dressed for the city — wool trousers, a cashmere sweater Emrys suspected cost more than a season's worth of vineyard labor — though by now the polish had gone soft at the edges. His hair, usually sculpted into something architectural, had surrendered to the humidity of a wood-heated cottage and curled slightly at his temples. It made him look younger. It also made him look, Emrys noted with an uncomfortable flicker of sympathy, stranded.

"The coast road," Ansel said from the doorway, not unkindly. "Also closed. The highway, closed. The ferry stopped running yesterday when the harbor iced over. There's a man in town — Dev Okafor — who plows with a tractor when it gets bad, but the ridge is out until the culvert's fixed, and the county won't send anyone up until the wind drops below forty." He paused. "So. Tuesday. Maybe Wednesday."

"Tuesday," Julian repeated, as if tasting something spoiled.

"It's Thursday," Emrys said gently.

Julian turned to the window and looked out at the white violence of it, and Emrys watched him do the arithmetic — three days of contract signing lost, three days of meetings in the city evaporating, three days trapped in a two-bedroom cottage with a lighthouse keeper's grandson who kept looking at Emrys the way people look at fires they're trying not to warm their hands on.

"I'm going to try the ridge anyway," Julian announced. "On foot. I have boots."

"You don't have boots," Emrys said. "You have loafers and one pair of trail runners you bought at the airport."

"They're waterproof."

"They're *aesthetic*," Ansel said, and then had the grace to look mildly apologetic about it, which was, Emrys was learning, the closest thing to remorse Ansel Ishikawa carried in his body.

* * *

By noon the storm had graduated from severe to biblical. The wind came off the water in long screaming gusts that rattled the shutters and drove the snow sideways, so that it didn't fall so much as travel, horizontally, in white rivers. The lighthouse itself — dark these ten years, its lamp removed, its gallery a rusted crown against the sky — vanished entirely behind the weather, and Emrys found he kept glancing toward where it should be, the way you touch your tongue to a missing tooth.

They had developed a system, the three of them, in the way that hostages develop systems. Julian claimed the table by the window for his phone calls, climbing the ridge behind the cottage at intervals when the signal flickered alive, shouting into the void about force majeure clauses and rescheduling and, once, in a voice cracked raw with frustration, "No, I understand the *concept* of a blizzard, Marcus, I am *in* one—"

Ansel kept the fire fed and cooked things. This, Emrys had forgotten about him — or perhaps had never let himself remember — the way Ansel moved in a kitchen without hurry, making food out of almost nothing. There were eggs from a neighbor's cold box on the porch, bread going stale in the cupboard, a jar of last summer's tomatoes Ansel's grandmother had left "for emergencies," which apparently included entertaining a stranded investor. Ansel turned these into something with rosemary and olive oil that made Julian stop pacing mid-stride and stare at the stove with open suspicion, as though the food might be a trick.

"What is it?" Julian demanded.

"Frittata."

"It smells—" He stopped himself, visibly recalibrating. "Fine. Thank you."

"You're welcome," Ansel said, and slid the biggest piece onto Julian's plate, and Emrys caught himself watching this small transaction with an emotion he couldn't immediately name and decided not to examine.

Emrys himself spent the morning doing something he could not explain to either of them: he put on his coat and went out into it.

Not far. Just to the edge of the vines, where the snow lay waist-deep between the rows and the wind tried its best to take him apart. The old vines — his father's vines, his grandfather's before that, some of them older than the town itself — stood bent under the weight, their bare canes whip-cracked and groaning, and Emrys moved down the row nearest the cottage shaking them free, one by one, his gloves soaked through in minutes, doing absolutely nothing that any viticulturist would call useful and everything that his hands, apparently, wanted to do.

He was on the fourth row when the cottage door banged open behind him and Ansel came out into the white chaos, coatless, furious.

"Are you *insane?*"

"It's my vineyard!"

"It's a *whiteout!* You can't see the cottage from the well! I couldn't see *you!*" Ansel grabbed his arm — hard, no politeness in it — and hauled him back toward the door, and Emrys went, because there was a strength in Ansel's grip he'd forgotten and because arguing felt suddenly beside the point. They fell through the doorway together into the sudden golden hush of the kitchen, snow melting off them in puddles, both of them breathing hard, standing closer than the argument required.

Ansel did not let go of his arm right away. Emrys did not step back.

"You'd have found me," Emrys said finally, and heard how it sounded, and watched Ansel hear it too.

"Yeah," Ansel said quietly. "I would've."

From the front room, Julian's voice floated in, phone pressed to his ear: "*No, the property is secure, it's just — hold on.*" A pause. Then, louder, dry as chalk: "Should I tell Marcus the heir apparent nearly died defending four acres of frozen grapes?"

"He wasn't defending the grapes," Ansel said, releasing Emrys's arm at last. "He was defending the vines. Grapes are the fruit. Vines are the—" He stopped. Shook his head. "Never mind."

But Emrys had gone still, because Ansel was right, and Emrys hadn't known the difference ten days ago, and now he did, and the knowing sat in his chest like a coal.

* * *

It curdled that evening. Emrys could pinpoint the hour.

Julian came down from the ridge at dusk with his coat plastered white and his phone dead in his pocket, having climbed it four times that day for a total of eleven minutes of signal, and something in him had passed beyond frustration into a colder country. He stood dripping in the entryway and looked around the cottage — at the low beams, the woodstove, the shelves of Ansel's grandfather's books, the faded photograph of two lighthouse keepers arm in arm on the gallery — and Emrys saw him see it all as an obstacle. As weather of a different kind.

"This place," Julian said, peeling off his gloves finger by finger, "is actively costing me money by the hour."

"Julian—"

"No, let me finish, because I've been polite for two days and politeness is getting us nowhere." He hung his coat with a precision that was somehow worse than throwing it. "Do you know what my partners are saying? That Vale is a vanity project. That the heir is a sentimental amateur playing farmer in his dead father's mud. That I've lost my mind proposing to sink capital into a *hobby*." He laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "And the worst part, Emrys, the genuinely humiliating part, is that I keep defending you, and then I come back to this — this *dollhouse* — and I can't imagine why."

The room went very quiet. The fire popped. Outside, the wind screamed past the gallery of the invisible lighthouse like something with a voice.

"Dollhouse," Emrys repeated.

"It's a figure of speech."

"It isn't, though. That's the thing about you, Julian — you're always precise underneath. You mean it exactly." Emrys heard his own voice come out level, and was distantly surprised, because inside he was anything but. "My father rebuilt those vines after the blight year when everyone told him to sell. He worked them eighteen hours a day for six years. Marisol — the foreman, seventy-one years old, she prunes those rows in February because her father did, and his father before that. And you're standing in my grandfather's kitchen calling it a dollhouse because the wifi is bad."

"I'm saying it's *failing*, Emrys. Which it is. The ledgers don't lie."

"The ledgers are a language," Emrys said, "and you're the only one here fluent in it, so we all just nod. But it's not the only language this land speaks." He stopped, startled by himself. He hadn't planned the sentence. It had simply arrived, fully formed, wearing his voice.

Julian stared at him. Even Ansel, by the stove, had gone motionless, a dish towel forgotten in his hands.

"That," Julian said slowly, "doesn't sound like a man who intends to sign on Monday."

Emrys opened his mouth to say something diplomatic — something about the terms, the timeline, the harvest clause, duty — and found that the machinery of diplomacy had seized. What came out instead was:

"My father used to say the vines keep time better than clocks. That you don't get to decide when they're ready. You just have to be there when they are." He looked at his own hands, red and chapped from the morning's work, and did not recognize them, and did not want the old ones back. "I used to think that was sentiment. I'm starting to think it was the whole point."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the walls the house ticked and settled, keeping its own time.

"Well," Julian said at last, into the quiet, with the terrible gentleness of a man laying down a card he's been holding all evening. "I suppose I should show you the rest of the sale terms, then. The ones my lawyers flagged. Before you fall any further in love with the mud."

He reached into his bag.

Across the room, Ansel set down the dish towel and said, very quietly, to no one in particular: "Here we go."
