## 20. Label: E & A

The cellar smelled the way it always had in September — dust and oak and the green ghost of crushed fruit, a smell Emrys had spent thirty years trying to name and had finally, this week, stopped trying, because some things were not for naming but for breathing.

Forty-two bottles stood on the long bench under the arched window, their shoulders catching the late light that slanted down the stone stairwell. The reserve. His father's hidden row, pulled from the far corner of the cellar where Marisol had kept them wrapped in burlap like sleeping children. Ten bottles had already gone to the cooperative's first buyers — the ones who had answered Ansel's grandmother's phone tree with the speed of people who had been waiting years for permission to believe in the Vale vineyard again. The remaining thirty-two waited now for their labels, and Emrys waited with them, because it had somehow become his job, and because he had discovered that he did not mind.

"You're holding it wrong," Ansel said, from the other end of the bench, where he was affixing labels to bottles with the concentration of a man defusing something.

"I'm holding it perfectly."

"You're holding it like a parking ticket. Like it's something you got and want to be rid of." Ansel came down the bench, took the bottle from Emrys's hands, and demonstrated: one hand cradling the base, the other smoothing the label from the center outward, pressing the air out in slow strokes. "Like this. Like you're glad it exists."

"I am glad it exists," Emrys said. "I'm also terrified of it existing. These can coexist. I've checked."

"They can," Ansel agreed, and handed the bottle back, and their fingers overlapped on the glass for a moment longer than the task required, which was, Emrys had learned, Ansel's entire method of communication. Words were the rehearsal. Hands were the performance.

The label itself had been Ansel's idea, or rather Ansel's grandfather's idea, recovered from a drawer in the cottage where the old man had kept his drawings: a simple block of cream paper, a border of vine leaves inked in green gone brown at the edges, and in the center, in a hand that trembled only slightly, the two letters that had started all of this.

*E & A.*

Emrys had stared at the drawing for a long time when Ansel first showed it to him. He had thought, absurdly, of Julian's branding consultants and their mood boards, of the resort developer's renderings with their infinity pools superimposed on the hillside like stickers on a photograph of somewhere else. This was the opposite of that. This was two letters, drawn by a lighthouse keeper who had loved his neighbor's son in the quiet, stubborn way that lighthouse keepers apparently did everything, and who had labeled a wine for a future he would not live to see.

"Your grandfather drew this," Emrys said. It was not the first time he'd said it. It might have been the tenth. Ansel never seemed to tire of it.

"He drew a lot of things. Mostly boats. This is the only label." Ansel smoothed another label down. "He told my grandmother once that he was saving it for when the vineyard made something worth putting it on. She said he was being romantic. He said he was being accurate."

"Those are the same thing in this valley."

"In this valley," Ansel said, "they have never once been different things."

* * *

They worked through the afternoon, and the light moved across the cellar floor in a long gold bar, and the town went on outside — he could hear the trucks on the gravel road, Marisol's voice raised in instruction to the pickers who were still bringing in the last rows, the distant percussion of the press house. The harvest was in, nearly all of it, brought in early and frantically and gloriously by forty neighbors who had shown up at dawn with gloves and crates and, in the case of the Delgado twins, a pickup truck stereo playing something with an accordion in it, which Ansel's grandmother had declared "the correct music for grapes" and no one had dared dispute.

The cooperative paperwork sat in a folder on the bench, signed by thirty-one families. The pre-orders for the first vintage — this vintage, the one in these bottles — had covered the vineyard's most crushing debt, the one that had a bank attached to it. Not all of it. Not comfortably. But enough that when Julian had driven away three days ago with his lawyers and his moving truck of ambition, he had left behind a handshake that Emrys still found himself turning over in his memory like a stone, looking for the trick in it and finding, to his ongoing astonishment, none.

*"You'll fail,"* Julian had said, not cruelly. Almost fondly. *"Statistically, you'll fail. But I've watched you for two years, Emrys, and I've never once seen you fight for anything the way you fought for that hillside this week. So. Fail beautifully."*

It was the closest thing to a blessing the man had ever given him, and Emrys had stood in the road and watched the taillights go and felt — grief, yes, and guilt, and the specific hollow ache of a door closing that had once seemed like the only door — but underneath all of it, unmistakably, relief. The relief of a man setting down luggage he had forgotten he was carrying.

The engagement was over. It had ended not with a fight but with a conversation on the cottage porch, the two of them wrapped in blankets against the lingering cold, speaking with the careful honesty of people who had finally stopped performing. Julian had wanted a partner for an empire. Emrys had wanted, it turned out, a hillside and a lighthouse and a man who held bottles like they were glad they existed. Neither of them had been wrong to want what they wanted. They had simply been offering it to the wrong person.

"You've gone somewhere," Ansel said now, not looking up from his labels. "Your hands stopped."

Emrys looked down. His hands had, in fact, stopped, the bottle resting forgotten in his grip. "I was thinking about Julian."

"Ah." A beat. Ansel was very good, these days, at not flinching. "Good thoughts or bad thoughts?"

"Grateful ones. Which surprised me."

"He did walk away without burning anything down."

"He walked away," Emrys said, "the way people walk away from a fire they realize was never theirs to put out." He turned the bottle in his hands, cradled now, the way Ansel had shown him. The cream label caught the light. *E & A.* "He said I should fail beautifully. I intend to disappoint him."

Ansel laughed — the real laugh, the one from the storm days, the one that had taken ten years and one freak June snowstorm and one devastating manuscript revelation to get back. "That's the most romantic thing you've ever said."

"That's a low bar. You've heard my romantic history."

"I've *read* your romantic history. It's in my manuscript."

"Your *unpublished* manuscript."

"My *about-to-be-finished* manuscript." Ansel set down his brush and looked at him across the bench, and his expression did the thing it did sometimes — went quiet and young, seventeen again, the boy on the lighthouse gallery who had once rehearsed a sentence for a decade. "Chapter twenty-one. I'm writing it tonight. The ending changed."

"The ending changed," Emrys repeated. His chest did something complicated. "To what?"

"To the truth." Ansel picked his brush back up, casual again, infuriating, wonderful. "You'll hear it when it's done. I'm reading it aloud. In the cottage. Where we were stranded. It's a whole thing. I've planned it."

"You've planned it."

"I rehearse things," Ansel said. "It's kind of my signature move."

* * *

It was Marisol who found the letter.

Not found, exactly — she had known where it was for thirty years, which in Marisol's taxonomy was a different thing entirely. She came down the cellar stairs at dusk with her keys jangling and her reading glasses pushed up into her gray hair, carrying a flat tin box that Emrys recognized from his childhood: it had lived on the top shelf of the office, behind the ledgers, and he had been told at age nine that it was "none of your business," a phrase Marisol had deployed with such finality that he had never once, in all the years since, touched it.

"Your father asked me to give you this," she said, "when you were ready."

"I'm not sure I know what ready means," Emrys said.

"Neither did he." Marisol set the box on the bench, between the bottles and the folder of cooperative paperwork, and looked at him over her glasses with the expression she had used on him since he was a boy — the one that said she loved him and would also be grading him. "That's why he wrote it down. So ready wouldn't have to be a feeling. It could just be a day. This is a day." She tapped the tin once, turned, and climbed back up the stairs, and her footsteps faded into the sounds of the harvest, and Emrys was left standing in the gold light with a box that was, at last, his business.

Ansel came to stand beside him. He didn't touch the box. He didn't touch Emrys, either, though Emrys felt him there the way you feel a wall at your back in a high wind.

Inside the tin: a bundle of letters tied with twine, a photograph Emrys had never seen — his father young, laughing, arm slung around the shoulders of Ansel's grandfather in front of the lighthouse, both of them squinting into sun — and at the bottom, alone, an envelope with Emrys's name on it in his father's handwriting, the ink gone the brown of old wine.

He opened it with hands that were not entirely steady.

"Read it out loud," Ansel said softly. "If you want. Some things are for breathing."

Emrys almost laughed. Almost. Then he unfolded the pages, and the cellar went very quiet, and he read.

*My Emrys,*

*If you are reading this, then Marisol has decided you are ready, and Marisol is never wrong, though she has been known to be early. Forgive her. She learned it from me.*

*You are holding this letter, which means you have survived my dying, which I am sorry for, and the first harvest, which I am not sorry for at all, because the first harvest is the best one and I am sorry only that you have to do it without me complaining about your trellising.*

*You never wanted this vineyard. I have known that since you were twelve and told me, with tremendous dignity, that grapes were "a lifestyle, not a career." I have never wanted you to want it out of duty. Duty is a bad reason to hold anything. Hold it like you're glad it exists, or set it down.*

*(I am told this is also how one should hold people. I was not good at it. Your mother was. Ask Marisol for the stories.)*

*So here is the only inheritance I have to give you, and it is not the land. The land is just dirt and weather and the stubbornness of forty families who never stopped believing in it even when the ledgers went bad, which, by the way, they never did — ask Marisol. The inheritance is this:*

*You are allowed.*

*You are allowed to love the valley or leave it. You are allowed to sell it or keep it. You are allowed to love whomever you love, and I will tell you now what I was too much a coward to tell my own father: it will not be the thing that ruins the vineyard. Fear will. Pride will. The refusal to say the true sentence out loud will. I watched a good man rehearse one sentence for ten years once, and I watched the silence between our two houses grow so tall that we both forgot there was never anything to forgive.*

*If you have found someone to love in this valley — and if Marisol has given you this letter, I suspect you have, because Marisol has the timing of a woman who has been reading weather her whole life — then bring him to the cellar. There is a row of bottles in the far corner with a label my friend drew, two letters, E and A. We made them the summer we were thirty, the summer I finally understood that the vineyard was never the point. The point was having someone to make the wine with.*

*Drink one. It is either very good or very bad and I honestly no longer remember which, and it does not matter, because by the time you drink it, it will be the best wine in the world, because it will be yours.*

*The label is your permission. Use it.*

*With all the love I was bad at saying and good at growing,*

*Your father*

The cellar was silent. The gold light had gone rose at the edges, then violet. Somewhere above them the last trucks were pulling out, and the valley was settling into that deep September hush that Emrys had spent his childhood fleeing and his adulthood mourning without knowing its name.

Ansel was crying. Not dramatically — Ansel did almost nothing dramatically, which was its own kind of drama — just standing there with tears running quietly down his face, looking at the letter in Emrys's hands like it was a lighthouse.

"Ten years," Ansel said. His voice was wrecked. "I rehearsed one sentence for ten years, and your father knew. He *knew*, and he wrote it down, and he — he put it in a tin box and he waited."

"He was good at waiting," Emrys said. His own voice was not much better. "It ran in the family. Both families, apparently. We're a valley of people who rehearse things."

"He called you *my Emrys*."

"He did."

"And the label — Emrys. The label was never a label. It was — " Ansel pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "It was a *permission slip*. He wrote you a permission slip and hid it in a wine cellar for thirty years."

"That's very on brand for this valley."

"Emrys." Ansel lowered his hand. His eyes were red and fierce and certain, and Emrys thought: *oh, there it is, there's the boy from the gallery, he never actually left, he just waited, we all just waited.* "Say the sentence."

Emrys looked at him. At the man he had loved across a broken fence line and a decade of silence and one impossible snowstorm. At the man who had shown up with frost candles at two in the morning without being asked, who had organized a town, who had changed the ending of a book to match the truth.

"I have loved you," Emrys said, "since the summer we were seventeen."

Ansel made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "That's my sentence. I've been saving it. I had a whole plan — the gallery, the dusk — "

"You can still do the gallery. I'll act surprised."

"You're terrible at acting surprised."

"I'm told I'm getting better at holding things," Emrys said, and reached out, and cradled Ansel's face in both hands the way Ansel had taught him to hold the bottle — glad that it existed — and kissed him in his father's cellar, under the label his father had kept for him, in the smell of dust and oak and the green ghost of the year's first crush.

When they broke apart, Ansel was laughing and crying at once, which Emrys had learned was his natural state, and which Emrys had decided he could listen to for the rest of his life and never once tire of.

"Okay," Ansel said. "Okay. One bottle. He said to drink one. The far corner."

They went together, past the bench, past the sleeping rows, to the far corner where the burlap had been pulled back. One bottle remained of the original reserve — the true reserve, the first one, the one made by two friends in a summer thirty years gone. Emrys worked the cork with a competence that surprised them both, and poured into two glasses that had been sitting on the bench since afternoon, and they drank standing up, in the violet light, holding the glasses like they were glad they existed.

It was very good.

It was, Emrys thought, watching Ansel close his eyes over the taste — watching him mouth the word *oh* like a prayer — the best wine in the world. Because it was theirs.

Above them, faintly, through the stone, came the sound of the valley settling into night, and somewhere far off, the first pale thread of the lighthouse beam sweeping its old arc over the water, lit again, keeping.

"Chapter twenty-one," Ansel said against Emrys's shoulder. "Tomorrow. The cottage. I'm reading you the new ending."

"I'll be there," Emrys said. "I rehearsed."
