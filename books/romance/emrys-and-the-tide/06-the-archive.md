## 6. The Archive

The storm had taken a shingle from the cottage roof and given Emrys a reason to knock on Orsen Vane's door.

He told himself that was all it was. A shingle, a bucket, a borrowed ladder. But his hands were not quite steady as he climbed the porch steps, and he knew the tremor had nothing to do with weather. Ten years of letters lived in boxes under his bed, and for ten years he had wondered about the hand that wrote them, and this morning, drying his coffee cup in the grey light, he had looked at a grocery list Orsen had left on the shared fence post—*sulfur, twine, bread, more twine*—and felt the floor tilt beneath him.

The *a* was open at the top. The *t* crossed with a single impatient stroke that drifted left, like a man writing while looking over his shoulder.

Emrys had spent a decade studying the Vane Letters. He knew that hand the way he knew his own heartbeat.

*No,* he told himself, climbing the steps. *Coincidence. Wishful thinking. You've been in this valley three weeks and you're seeing ghosts in grocery lists.*

Orsen opened the door before he knocked, which was its own small strangeness, as though he'd been standing on the other side of it, listening.

"You're dripping," Orsen said.

"It's raining."

"I noticed." But he stepped back, and the doorway swallowed Emrys into warmth—woodsmoke and dust and the green, mineral smell of wine that had soaked into the very bones of the house. The study was to the left; Emrys had seen it only in glimpses, a room Orsen seemed to guard the way other men guarded their wallets.

"Ladder's in the barn," Orsen said. "Bucket's—somewhere. I'll find it."

"I can look."

"Don't." The word came out too fast, too sharp, and Orsen caught it, softened it with an effort that was almost painful to watch. "The dog sleeps in there. She bites."

"There's no dog."

Orsen's jaw worked. "She's shy."

Emrys smiled and did not push. That was the dance they had learned these three weeks, the fence between them mended and re-mended, the tools shared and returned, the conversations that Orsen ended just as they were becoming something. Emrys had learned to read him the way he read manuscripts—by what was *not* on the page.

"I'll take the bucket," Emrys said, and walked toward the study before Orsen could stop him.

* * *

The door was ajar. That was the thing Emrys would return to, later, in the dark hours when he could not sleep: the door was ajar, and Orsen, who locked his truck, who locked his wine cellar, who locked the gate to the north field, had left it open by exactly the width of a hand.

Inside, the room smelled of ink and old paper and, beneath it, the faint sweetness of a candle long burned down. A desk under the window, its blotter worn pale in the center. Shelves heavy with ledgers—vineyard accounts, Emrys assumed, decades of them, the arithmetic of a dying farm. A lamp with a green glass shade. And on the desk, face down, a single sheet of paper.

Emrys knew he should not turn it over.

He turned it over.

The handwriting hit him like cold water. He knew it. Of course he knew it—he had spent ten years reading it, tracing it, pressing his thumb into the indentations the pen had left on the page beneath, as if the letters' author might be reached through the paper itself, through touch, through some communion of pressure and ink. The tall ascenders. The *y* that descended in a long, deliberate sweep, like a signature of patience. The way whole words seemed to lean slightly rightward, as though reaching toward the reader.

*Draft,* the page said, in that hand. *For the anniversary letter—*

And then a line crossed out. And then another. And then, uncrossed, unhidden, half-finished:

*You asked me once whether love is a thing that happens to us or a thing we choose. I have been choosing you for—*

The rest was blank. The pen had been lifted. The sentence hung there, unfinished, and Emrys stood in a stranger's study with his heart going like a trapped bird and the rain loud on the roof and the terrible, electric knowledge settling into him:

*It's him. It's been him all along.*

He heard Orsen's boots in the hall and turned the paper face down with fingers that barely obeyed him.

* * *

Orsen came in carrying a bucket and an expression like a man arriving at the scene of an accident he'd somehow caused. His eyes went to the desk. To the paper. To Emrys standing beside it. Something moved through his face—fear, Emrys thought, or its close cousin—and then it was gone, shuttered, and Orsen was only a gruff neighbor with a bucket.

"Found one," Orsen said.

"Thank you." Emrys took it. Their fingers did not touch, and the not-touching was louder than a shout.

For a moment neither of them moved. Rain ticked against the window. The green lamp threw its light across the desk, across the hidden page, across the space between them that seemed, suddenly, very small and very charged.

"You've read them," Emrys said. "The Vane Letters."

It was not the question he'd meant to ask. Or maybe it was.

"Some," Orsen said. His voice was flat as a field in winter. "My mother liked them. She'd read them aloud in the kitchen." A pause, and then, quieter, an offering or a deflection: "She died before they stopped coming."

"I'm sorry."

"She isn't the one who needs the sympathy." Orsen looked at the window, at the rain. "The person who wrote them. Ten years of carrying other people's griefs. That's a weight."

"You sound like you know him."

"I sound like a man guessing." But his hand had drifted, without his seeming to notice, to the edge of the desk. To the paper. His fingertips rested on its corner the way a man rests his hand on the shoulder of someone sleeping.

Emrys watched that hand and made his decision.

* * *

He tested him at dinner.

Not dinner, exactly—Orsen had produced bread and a bottle of the vineyard's red, which was rougher than it should have been and better than it had any right to be, given the state of the vines. They ate at the kitchen table because the study lamp had burned out, Orsen said, and would not meet Emrys's eyes when he said it.

Emrys turned his glass slowly by its stem. "I've been reading the letters again," he said. "The early ones. There's one I've never been able to place. The one about the lighthouse."

Orsen's knife stopped against the bread.

It was the smallest pause. A half-second, maybe less. But Emrys had spent ten years learning the pauses in the letters—the places where the author set down his pen to choose the true word instead of the easy one—and he knew this pause. It was the pause before *choosing* in the draft on the desk. It was the pause of a man deciding what to be honest about.

"Lighthouse," Orsen said.

"The fourth letter. The one to the woman in Maine whose son drowned. He wrote—" Emrys closed his eyes, let the words come up from memory, where they lived like scripture. "*Your son is not in the sea. He is in the kitchen at Christmas, in the smell of oranges, in the argument about the car. Grief is not where they went. Grief is where they stayed.*"

"That's a good line," Orsen said quietly.

"It's the best thing anyone ever wrote to me." Emrys opened his eyes. "Except he didn't write it. Not like that. The published version says *kitchen at Christmas*—but in the letter I have, the original, the author wrote *kitchen in winter* and then crossed it out and wrote *Christmas* above it. The archive copy has the correction. But everyone quotes the published version."

He had fabricated none of it except the ending. The letter was real; the crossing-out was real; he had held the manuscript in white gloves at the Bodleian and wept. But the *archive copy*—that was his invention, a small trap stitched from truth.

"An archive copy," Orsen said. His voice was careful, each word set down like a stone in a stream.

"Yale. The publisher donated the manuscripts when the collection went to print. Didn't you know?"

The silence stretched. Rain on the roof. The candle between them bending its small flame toward Orsen, as if even the fire leaned to hear him.

"No," Orsen said. "I didn't know."

And then he said, almost gently, almost to his wine: "The author would have hated that. His drafts being handled. He crossed things out for a reason. The crossed-out words were the true ones—he just couldn't afford them."

Emrys's heart turned over.

*He just couldn't afford them.*

There was no way to know that. No scholar had written it; no interview existed; the author had never spoken publicly, never given a name, never once stepped out of the ink. It was a thing only the author himself could know—the private arithmetic of every revision, the economy of a man who gave away tenderness in print because he could not afford it in the room.

Emrys set down his glass. His hand was steady. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it too.

"You talk about him," Emrys said, "like you're quoting him."

Orsen looked up. And for one unguarded second—the candlelight catching the grey at his temple, the exhaustion of a man who had been carrying two lives in one body for ten years—Orsen Vane looked at Emrys Hale with an expression of naked, terrible recognition. As if to say: *You know. I know you know. God help us both.*

"More wine?" Orsen said.

"Yes," Emrys said. "Please."

* * *

He stayed too long. They both knew it and neither said it. The rain had softened to a mist, and the walk between the kitchen and the front door was twenty feet that took a year to cross, because Orsen walked slowly and Emrys matched him, and their shoulders came close without touching, and the whole house seemed to hold its breath around them.

At the door, Orsen handed him the bucket. "For the roof," he said. "Bring it back whenever."

"Whenever," Emrys agreed.

Orsen's hand was still on the bucket. So was Emrys's. Two hands on a tin pail in a doorway, and neither letting go, and the mist drifting in past them, beading on the wood.

"You came here to unmask him," Orsen said. It was not a question. "The author. That's why you moved next door to a stranger's failing vineyard with your boxes of letters."

"I came here because of the letters," Emrys said carefully. "What I do with what I find—that's mine to decide."

"And if what you find ruins him?"

"Then I'll decide what finding means." Emrys held his gaze. "I've loved those letters for ten years, Orsen. Whoever wrote them gave me—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "He answered a letter I wrote when I was at my lowest. He wrote back in three days. Three days, and he made me feel like someone on the other side of the world had been listening the whole time. I'm not here to hurt him. I'm here because I need to know him."

Something in Orsen's face broke and reformed, quick as weather.

"Careful," Orsen said, very low. "Wanting to know a man like that. It's how people get hurt."

"Is that experience talking?"

"It's the only kind of talking I know how to do."

Emrys laughed—couldn't help it—and the sound loosened something in the doorway, in the night, in the space between their hands. Orsen almost smiled. It was a near thing, a ghost of a thing, but Emrys had spent a decade reading between lines and he knew a hidden smile when he stood in front of one.

"Goodnight, Orsen."

"Goodnight, Emrys."

He was halfway down the steps when Orsen said his name. Just his name. Emrys turned, and Orsen stood in the doorway with the lamplight behind him, and for a moment he looked like a man on the edge of a confession—lips parted, hand gripping the frame, the whole of him leaning forward into the dark as if the truth were a rope thrown across a gorge and he was deciding whether to jump.

"Your roof," Orsen said finally. "The shingle. Get it fixed before the next storm. I mean it."

"I will."

He walked home through the mist with the bucket swinging at his side and the draft letter burning in his memory, every word of it, every crossed-out line. *I have been choosing you for—*

Choosing. Not *loving*. Choosing. As if love were a labor, a row of vines, a thing done daily with the hands.

Emrys stood a long time on his own porch, looking back at the lit window of the study, at the silhouette that moved behind it and then went still, as though the man inside had sat down at his desk and taken up his pen.

The publisher's deadline was in six weeks. Emrys had come to the valley to give the Vane Letters an author's name.

He was beginning to fear he already knew it—and that when he spoke it aloud, he would lose the only man whose silences had ever sounded like home.
