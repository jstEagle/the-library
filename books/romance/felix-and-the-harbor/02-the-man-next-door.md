## 2. The Man Next Door

The boxes had labels in three different hands, which was the first lie of the day. *Personal. Kitchen. Misc.* As if a man's life could be sorted that neatly, as if the thing he'd actually carried across four hundred miles of coast could be filed under anything but *evidence*.

Felix Marsh cut the tape on the seventh box and there they were: the case files he'd sworn to burn.

He'd sworn it twice, actually. Once on the courthouse steps in the rain, with his resignation letter still warm in his jacket pocket and a reporter's microphone held up like a knife. Once more at a motel outside Santa Rosa, lighter in hand, box balanced on the hood of his car. He'd gotten as far as scorching one corner of the top folder — Marsh v. Calloway Holdings, or rather *the State of California v. everyone except Calloway Holdings* — before his hands had done what his hands always did when he thought about fire.

They'd stopped.

Now the files sat in a cardboard box in a rented house that smelled of other people's cooking, and Felix sat on the floor beside them with a beer he wasn't drinking, and through the window, across a fence made of gray cedar slats, the bookshop was turning its lights on.

It did this at 6:40 every evening. He knew because he'd been here four days and had watched it four times, and he was not proud of that arithmetic. A single lamp behind the front window, then the string of small bulbs along the awning — someone had strung them crooked, lovingly, so they sagged like a garland at a party that refused to end. Then, last, the light in the upstairs window, where a woman moved past the glass carrying something that might have been laundry or might have been a child's blanket, and was gone.

Freya Okonkwo.

He said the name the way you'd test a floorboard. Quietly. Ready for it to give.

There was a photograph in his wallet that had no business surviving as long as it had. Ten years folded into eighths, creased soft as cloth, taken by an investigator who'd been paid to follow Emeka Okonkwo and had instead, for reasons never explained, photographed the man's wife. She was standing outside a bookstore in a photograph that predated this bookstore, holding a paperback against her chest like a shield, laughing at someone out of frame. The investigator had written on the back: *Wife. Not relevant.*

Felix had kept her anyway. Not relevant, and kept. For ten years she'd lived in his wallet behind his driver's license, and when the license expired and got replaced, she stayed, migrating from plastic to plastic like a tenant who'd outlasted every landlord. He had looked at her face more often than he'd looked at his own reflection, which told him something about himself he preferred not to examine.

And now she was forty feet away, hanging string lights over a bookshop in a town called Harbor's End, and she didn't know him from the tide.

That was the design. That had been the whole point of choosing a town small enough to disappear into and far enough away that nobody asked questions with their eyes. He'd found Harbor's End on a map the way you find a place to hide a body — methodically, without sentiment. Good distance. Bad cell service. One road in. And then he'd pulled the file on the dockworker whose conviction his own office had built on paper so rotten it should have composted itself, and he'd read the name of the widow, and the map had stopped being a map.

Harbor's End. Where Freya Okonkwo had gone to be invisible too.

He hadn't known she'd come here. He wanted that on the record, in some courtroom that would never convene: he had not followed her. The universe had simply turned out to be smaller than his guilt, and had folded them into the same zip code like two letters into the same drawer.

The drawer. He thought about the drawer the way other men thought about a woman they'd wronged.

For six years, before everything, he had written letters to a stranger. It had started as nothing — a note left in the lending drawer of a bookshop in a city he no longer lived in, a joke about a novel neither of them had finished, signed with nothing. And then a reply had appeared, and then another, and somewhere in the third year Felix had understood that he was telling this unknown woman things he had never told anyone alive. His father's silence. His failures in the courtroom. The specific loneliness of a man who wins cases and loses nights. She wrote back in a voice like low light — funny, exact, unafraid of the dark parts — and he loved her the way you love a room you've never entered but can describe perfectly.

Then the Calloway case broke over his career like cold water, and he'd walked away from everything, including the drawer, including her, and he had told himself the letters were a closed file.

Except he'd kept one draft. In a donated book, because he was a coward even in his sentimentality — hiding a letter inside a novel he'd never mailed, inside a box he'd never burned, inside a life he'd never started. Somewhere in these boxes was a copy of *The Shipping News*, and pressed between its pages was a letter addressed to no one, beginning *You quoted Rilke at me once and got it slightly wrong, and I have thought about you every day since—*

He should have burned that too.

Felix stood, joints reporting in like tired witnesses, and went to the window. Across the fence, the bookshop glowed. A hand-lettered sign hung above the door — *Okonkwo's, Open 'Til Late* — and beneath the awning, a girl of maybe eleven was chalking something on the sidewalk with the fierce concentration of a muralist. Amara. He knew the name from the file, from the obituary he'd read forty times, from the photograph of a funeral he had not attended because attending would have meant explaining why a prosecutor felt entitled to grieve at the trial of a man he'd helped convict.

Emeka Okonkwo. Dockworker. Father. The man whose name Felix's office had handed to the Calloways like a coat check ticket — *hold this for us, we'll collect later* — and then never collected, because collecting would have meant indicting the family that owned half the valley and funded half the campaigns. Felix had been young and ambitious and had signed the paperwork that made a scapegoat out of a good man, and it had taken him nine years and one insomniac audit of his own conscience to admit what the paperwork had always said if you read it honestly: *we knew.*

The girl finished her chalk drawing — a whale, enormous and lopsided and grinning — and looked up, directly at his window.

Felix stepped back out of the light too fast, hip catching the corner of a box. Files slid. The scorched folder fanned open on the floor, and Emeka Okonkwo's face looked up at him from a ten-year-old booking photo, and Felix stood in his empty living room being seen, simultaneously, by everyone he'd ever failed.

"Great," he said aloud, to no one. "Great start."

* * *

He unpacked until midnight because sleeping felt like an activity for innocent men. Books first — he always unpacked books first, the way other people unpacked photographs. Then dishes. Then, last, reluctantly, the box labeled MISC., which contained the photograph of his mother, the lighter that had failed to burn anything, and the wallet.

He took out the photograph and unfolded it under the kitchen light.

Ten years had not been kind to the paper but had been kind to her. Freya Okonkwo, laughing at someone out of frame, book held to her chest. He had constructed entire conversations with this woman during the worst years of his life — not romantic conversations, exactly, though he wasn't going to pretend they were innocent either. Conversations like the ones they'd had in the drawer, before he knew her name, before the name arrived in his life wearing handcuffs in a courtroom transcript. After that, the letters had become impossible. Every sentence he wrote to her was a sentence written to the wife of a man he'd destroyed, and he had put down the pen the way you put down a weapon you've realized is pointed at yourself.

But she was here. Out of frame no longer. Forty feet of cedar fence away, running a bookshop with crooked fairy lights and a daughter who drew whales on sidewalks, and Felix Marsh — disgraced, unemployed, technically a person who had promised himself a fresh start — understood with total clarity that he had not moved to Harbor's End despite her.

He had moved here because of the case, and the case was her, and he had let himself believe those were different things.

The kitchen light buzzed. Outside, rain had started, the fine sideways drizzle the harbor specialized in, and through it the bookshop's bulbs blurred into gold coins strung across the dark. Upstairs, the second-floor light was still on. He imagined her reading. He imagined her the way he'd imagined her for six years of letters — a voice attached eventually to a face, a face attached now to a grief he had authored.

"I'm sorry," he told the window, quietly, uselessly. An apology with no addressee. A letter with no drawer.

He put the photograph back in the wallet. He did not put the wallet back in the box.

Tomorrow, he told himself, he would introduce himself like a normal human being. Tomorrow he would walk over with some plausible errand — borrowing a ladder, asking where people bought coffee — and he would shake her hand and say *Felix Marsh, next door, just moved in,* and none of the words underneath would surface. Guilt, he was learning, wore the mask of a fresh start very well. It unpacked neatly. It hung fairy-light-adjacent. It waved at neighbors.

What it did not do was burn.

He fell asleep on the couch with the lamp on, and dreamed of a lending drawer opening and closing, opening and closing, and woke at dawn to the smell of someone baking bread, and stood at the window with unshaved jaw watching Freya Okonkwo unlock her own front door at 7:15 in the morning like a woman who had never hidden from anything in her life.

She paused on the threshold. Looked across the fence. Looked directly at his window — or near enough that his spine went cold — and lifted one hand, not quite a wave. A question mark, maybe. *Do I know you? Should I?*

Felix did not wave back. He stood very still in the half-dark of his new house, holding a coffee mug he hadn't washed, and let the moment pass the way he'd let a decade pass — one unmade decision at a time.

Tomorrow, he told himself again.

But tomorrow, when he finally pushed open the little brass bell door of Okonkwo's Books, the first thing he saw, spine-out on the staff picks shelf, was a battered copy of *The Shipping News* — and tucked into it, unmistakable even from the doorway, a slip of paper in handwriting he would have known blindfolded, after ten years, in the dark, underwater.

His own.
