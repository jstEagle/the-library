## 1. The Muscle Theory

The contract arrived on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were for beginnings—Wren had always believed that, had built a small superstition around it, scheduling her first drafts and her dental cleanings and her difficult phone calls on the second day of the week so that the rest would follow like obedient sentences after a strong opening clause. But this Tuesday the contract sat on her kitchen table like something dead she'd been asked to revive, and she could not make herself believe in it.

*Ashcombe, Marguerite. Working title: untitled memoir. Deliverable: 85,000 words. Ghostwriter to remain unnamed on all materials, per Clause 14.*

Clause 14. She'd read it four times. It was a good clause, a generous one, drafted by lawyers who understood that some people pay handsomely for the privilege of disappearing. Wren had built a decade of work inside clauses like it. She was very good at not being there.

She made tea she didn't drink. The kettle's whistle went on too long, climbing into a register that reminded her of something she refused to name—a kitchen, ten years ago, a kettle screaming while two women stood on opposite sides of a flour-dusted counter and neither of them said what they'd come to say. She turned the burner off with more force than necessary. The silence afterward rang.

Her pen was already in her hand. That was the terrible part, the part she would examine later at three in the morning when the ceiling offered no answers: her hand had signed before her mind finished arguing. *Wren Thornbury*, in the tidy, anonymous cursive she used for contracts, nothing like the slanted, urgent script she used for letters nobody would ever read. Two signatures for two lives. She had gotten so practiced at the switch that she sometimes wondered which one was the forgery.

There was a theory she kept, folded small and carried everywhere, the way other people carried prayer cards or lucky coins. She called it the muscle theory. It went like this: the heart is not a mysterious organ. It is a muscle, and muscles obey training. You can teach yourself anything if you repeat it enough times under enough resistance—grief, absence, the specific weight of watching someone you love laugh at someone else's joke across a crowded room. Reps. Sets. Failure is just part of the program; you come back tomorrow and lift again. Ten years ago she had walked out of a bakery kitchen with her heart torn open and unspoken, and every day since she had done her exercises. She did not think about Meg Ashcombe. She did not say the sentence. She did not drive past the bakery on Delancey Street even though it added eleven minutes to every errand, eleven minutes she told herself were about traffic.

Ten years of reps. And still, holding this contract, her hands shook like a beginner's.

* * *

The meeting had been three days earlier, in the publisher's conference room on the eleventh floor, where the windows looked out on a city pretending to be smaller than it was. Her editor, Priya Chandra, had slid the folder across the table with the particular gentleness people use when they know they're handing you something dangerous.

"You'll want to say no," Priya said.

"I never say no."

"That's what worries me." Priya took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose, a gesture Wren had catalogued over six years of working together: it meant *I'm about to ask for something I'd refuse myself.* "It's Meg Ashcombe. Ashcombe's Bakery, Delancey Street. The sourdough cult, the mother-daughter thing, the—" she waved a hand, searching for the word the marketing department would use, "—the *story*. Her lease is failing, the advance is real money, and she wants a book. What she doesn't want, apparently, is to meet her ghostwriter. Ever. She was very clear."

"Anonymous clients are my specialty."

"I know." Priya's voice softened in a way that made Wren's skin prickle. "That's what worries me, Wren. I've watched you disappear into twelve books. I've watched you give other people your best sentences and walk away whistling. But this one—" She tapped the folder. "You knew her, didn't you? Before. I saw your face when I said the name."

The conference room hummed its fluorescent hum. Somewhere below, a delivery truck beeped its patient reverse through an alley, and Wren thought absurdly of the beep of an oven timer, of the little electronic chirp that had scored the entire soundtrack of her twenties.

"I apprenticed at a bakery," she said. "A long time ago. Not that one specifically."

It was the smoothest lie she'd told in years, and it tasted like burnt sugar.

"Not that one specifically," Priya repeated, unconvinced, and let it go, because editors learn early which doors stay closed and which ones open only if you pretend not to be looking at them.

* * *

That night—the same night as the signing, the tea gone cold, the contract zipped into its folder like a body bag—Wren dreamed of the kitchen.

Not dreamed. *Returned.* There was a difference, and the difference was that dreams released you at dawn and memories held you hostage until you paid.

The kitchen at Ashcombe's, ten years ago: steam on the windows so thick the street outside became a watercolor of itself. The smell that no other building on earth has managed to replicate since—yeast and caramelizing sugar and the mineral breath of the old stone hearth, the oven Marguerite Senior had built with her own hands from salvaged brick, the oven everyone in the neighborhood called *the Mother* because that's what it was, warm-bellied and demanding, feeding everyone who came near it. Wren had been twenty-four and hungry in every way a person can be hungry, and she had stood at that counter at midnight learning to shape boules while Meg Ashcombe—twenty-six, flour to her elbows, laughing at Wren's lopsided loaves with a cruelty that was actually tenderness wearing a disguise—stood close enough that their shoulders touched when they leaned over the dough.

"You're fighting it," Meg had said, guiding Wren's hands with her own, both of them white to the wrist. "The dough knows when you're scared of it. It just gets denser. Like people."

"Is that what I am? Dense?"

"You're the densest person I know, Wren Thornbury. Brilliant, and dense as a day-old rye." Meg had grinned, and there had been flour on her cheekbone, a perfect white crescent like a moon in orbit, and Wren had thought: *now. Say it now. Say the sentence.*

The sentence had lived in her chest for weeks by then. She had rehearsed it against her pillow, in the shower, on the bus, in the walk-in cooler where the cold made her brave for exactly as long as the cold lasted. It was not a complicated sentence. It was not even an original one. It was just true, and truth, Wren had learned by then, was the one ingredient she could never get right. She could ghostwrite anyone's heart but her own.

She hadn't said it. She had smiled instead, and wiped the flour from Meg's cheek with her thumb—an intimacy she'd disguised as housekeeping—and the moment had passed through the kitchen like a draft, closing the door behind it.

Three weeks later, Marguerite Senior died in her sleep, and the oven went cold for the first time in thirty years, and Wren received two envelopes in the same week's mail: one from the bakery, black-edged, asking her to come, to help, to *stay*; and one from a culinary writing fellowship in another city, offering her everything she'd ever said she wanted. A scholarship. A byline. A door out.

She chose the envelope with her name printed on it. She told herself it was practicality. She told herself Meg had her family, her grief, her ovens—she didn't need a ghost hovering in the corners of her mourning. She told herself she would write. She did not write. Letters require sentences, and Wren had proven, catastrophically, that she could not produce the one that mattered.

The fellowship had made her a career. The career had made her invisible. And invisibility, it turned out, was a muscle too, and she had trained it until she could vanish inside a single paragraph.

* * *

On Wednesday morning she returned the signed contract to Priya, who studied her for a long moment across the desk.

"First interview is Monday," Priya said. "Eleven o'clock. At the bakery—her terms. She wouldn't come here. She says her stories don't travel well."

"Most true things don't."

"Wren." Priya leaned forward. "One piece of advice, free of charge, worth exactly what you're paying for it. Whatever history you're carrying into that room—leave it at the door. Write the book she needs, not the apology you owe. Those are different books."

"They're the same book," Wren said quietly. "They've always been the same book."

Priya didn't answer, and Wren gathered her coat and her dread and took the elevator down eleven floors into the ordinary noise of the street, where she stood for a moment among the pedestrians and let herself do the math she'd been avoiding: eighty-five thousand words, months of interviews, hours in that kitchen, in that smell, beside that woman, transcribing a life she had helped break and was now being paid to make beautiful.

The muscle theory said she could train through anything. Resistance builds strength. Show up, do the reps, survive the burn.

But standing there on the sidewalk with the autumn wind carrying, faintly, impossibly, the smell of baking bread from somewhere across the city, Wren understood the flaw in her theory—the flaw she'd always known and trained around anyway. Muscles only grow if you tear them first. And hers had never healed. Ten years of exercises, and underneath all that scar tissue she was still twenty-four years old, still standing at a flour-dusted counter, still holding a sentence in her mouth like a stone she couldn't swallow or spit out.

Monday was coming. Eleven o'clock was coming. Meg Ashcombe was coming, with her mother's recipes and her failing lease and her fury at the anonymous stranger who would soon be asking her to open her life like a notebook.

And Wren Thornbury, professional ghost, champion of disappearance, walked home the long way—eleven extra minutes, purely for the traffic—and began, in the privacy of her own skull, to rehearse.
