## 27. Reading the Draft

The manuscript arrived on a Tuesday, which was the day the bakery made rye, and the smell of caraway had soaked into Meg's coat by the time she found the parcel waiting at her editor's office.

Not her editor. Wren's editor. That distinction mattered now; she held it like a stone in her pocket. Priya Nair had been careful on the phone—careful the way people are when they know a building is on fire and are deciding whether to mention it. *Come by whenever you like,* she'd said. *There's something I think you should see before we go any further.* And then, after a pause that lasted one breath too long: *Meg, it's good. Whatever else is true, it's good.*

Whatever else is true. As if truth were a list and this were only one item on it.

She almost didn't go. She had spent three weeks in the country of her own anger, a country with excellent borders and no exports, and she had gotten comfortable there. Anger was a kind of company. It kept the rooms from echoing. But the lease notice sat on her kitchen table like a guest who refused to leave, ninety days circled twice in red, and the rival chain's letter sat beneath it, all friendly fonts and predatory patience, and Meg had learned in the last month that grief does not pay arrears.

So she went. She took the bus across town with the parcel unopened on her lap because opening it felt like a door she couldn't close behind her, and she carried it into the first café she saw—a place called The Marginalia, which she would have found precious under any other circumstances—and ordered a coffee she didn't want and sat by the window where strangers could see her, because whatever happened next, she did not want to do it alone in a room.

The manuscript was heavy. Four hundred pages, give or take, printed single-sided, bound with a binder clip that had rusted slightly at the hinge. On the title page, typed: *Ashcombe: A Memoir. Working draft. Not for circulation.*

And beneath it, smaller, as if the typist had hesitated: *For M., who taught me everything about bread and nothing about leaving.*

Meg's hand shook so badly she had to set the page down.

That line wasn't hers. She had never said anything like it. She had never given an interview sentence shaped like that, never told Wren—or whoever Wren had been during those long afternoons of questions—that her mother had taught her anything about leaving. Her mother had died suddenly, stupidly, a brain aneurysm on a Tuesday much like this one, between the second proof and the shaping, and there had been nothing taught about it at all. Leaving had been an ambush. Leaving had been the ovens still warm when Meg got to the hospital and the dough still rising when she came home and nobody, in all the years since, had explained how a person could be mid-morning one moment and memory the next.

But someone had written it anyway. Someone had looked at the shape of her life from the outside and named the thing she couldn't name herself.

She turned the page.

*My mother believed yeast was a living argument. You didn't command it; you persuaded it. You gave it warmth and time and a little sugar, and you waited for it to agree with you. Everything I know about love I learned wrong from her, because she made it look like patience when really it was stubbornness wearing an apron.*

Meg read that paragraph four times.

It was her voice. Not imitated—*inhabited*. Whoever wrote this hadn't dressed up in her sentences the way ghostwriters do, hadn't borrowed her cadences like a coat. They had gone down under the floorboards of her speech and found the joists. *Stubbornness wearing an apron.* Her mother had said things exactly like that, or rather, Meg had thought things exactly like that and never said them aloud, and here they were on paper, in print, in a draft written by a stranger, and the stranger had known.

The café noise receded. The espresso machine hissed somewhere far away. A man at the next table laughed at something on his phone and the sound passed over her like weather.

She read about the kitchen on Fenwick Street. About the blue enamel bowl with the chip on the rim that her mother had refused to replace for thirty-one years. About the way her mother hummed show tunes while kneading but only the verses, never the choruses, because—this was in the book, this was *in the book*—"she said the chorus was where songs went to show off, and showing off was for croissants."

Meg had never told anyone about the choruses.

She would have remembered telling. She would have remembered the moment of saying it, the small vulnerability of it, the way you remember handing someone a fragile thing. There was no such memory. Which meant—

Which meant either she had said it without knowing, poured out across weeks of interviews until her whole life lay on the transcript like spilled flour, or—

Or the writer had known already. Had known before the interviews. Had known ten years ago, standing in this same kitchen, watching her mother hum.

Wren had worked at Ashcombe's for eleven months. Eleven months of early mornings and flour fights and the summer the plum jam boiled over, and Meg had spent those months in a state of furious, wordless attention that she had told herself was rivalry. Wren had been twenty-three and brilliant and insufferably precise, and Meg had hated her the way you hate a mirror. And Wren's hands had been in that kitchen. Wren's ears.

*The choruses were for croissants.*

Meg put her palm flat against the page, as if she could hold the words still, as if they might otherwise escape into the café air and be witnessed. Her coffee went cold beside her. She didn't notice.

* * *

The middle chapters were about her mother's illness—not the death, the illness, the six days between the ambulance and the end, six days Meg had never described to anyone because describing them felt like killing her mother twice. But here they were, rendered in the second person, tender and unbearable:

*You sat in the corridor outside the ICU and counted the tiles because counting was the only prayer you'd ever been taught. Forty-seven tiles to the vending machine, forty-seven back. Your mother was behind the glass doing the thing she always did when machines took over her work: supervising. Even unconscious, even intubated, her hands twitched at the rhythm of kneading, folding, turning. The nurse said it was reflex. You knew better. You knew your mother was proofing. She was giving herself one last rise, and she was going to come out of this oven golden, because Edith Ashcombe did not collapse, she transformed.*

Meg cried the way she did everything—privately, furiously, in public.

She hadn't meant to. She had promised herself, walking in, that she would read this like a professional, like a woman auditing a forgery, marking the false notes. And for the first fifty pages she had managed it, circling adjectives, bristling at liberties. But somewhere around the chapter about the funeral—the chapter where the writer described how Meg had baked through the entire wake because her hands needed work or they were going to break something, how relatives had murmured about her *coping so well* while she stood in her mother's kitchen at two in the morning shaping loaves she would never eat, how grief is just love with nowhere to go so you put it in dough and let it rise until it becomes something you can feed people—somewhere in there, the audit collapsed.

A tear hit the page and bloomed through the ink, and Meg watched it spread and thought, absurdly, *I'm ruining the manuscript,* and then thought, *no, I'm annotating it,* and then stopped thinking entirely and let it happen.

The man at the next table glanced over, embarrassed for her, and looked away. The barista turned the music down. Nobody came over, and she was grateful, because what was happening in her was not sadness exactly. Sadness would have been simpler. This was recognition. This was the experience, entirely new to her at thirty-four, of reading her own grief described by someone else and finding it *accurate*. Not softened. Not dramatized. Accurate, down to the tiles, down to the twitching hands, down to the specific loneliness of being the competent one in a crisis, the one everyone admires and no one holds.

Someone had paid attention. Someone had paid attention for years, quietly, the way you pay attention to a fire you're not allowed to warm your hands at.

* * *

By late afternoon the café had emptied and refilled around her. She had drunk three coffees and eaten nothing and read two hundred pages, and her face felt stiff and scoured, and she had reached the chapter titled simply "The Apprentice."

Her stomach dropped before she read a word. She knew whose chapter this was. Of course she knew.

*There was a girl in my mother's kitchen the year before she died. I have avoided writing about her for two hundred pages, which my editor says is itself a kind of writing about her, and she's right, editors are always right in ways you want to throw things at.*

Meg's breath caught.

*I resented her. That's the honest word, though I dressed it up as rivalry for a decade because rivalry has dignity and resentment doesn't. She was better than me at the technical work—her lamination was flawless, her crumb structure was a reproach—and she was worse than me at everything that mattered.
