## 1. The Muscle That Learns

There is a muscle you build when you plan weddings for a living, and it is not the one in your back, though God knows that one goes too. It's the muscle that lets you stand at the edge of the most emotionally catastrophic day of two people's lives and feel nothing but logistics. I'd been training it for eleven years. Two hundred weddings. You could hang a medal on it.

Wedding number two hundred took place on a Saturday in May at a converted granary in Bermondsey, and it was, by every metric I keep — and I keep them all, in colour-coded spreadsheets, because feelings are unreliable but rain contingency plans are forever — flawless.

The bride had wanted "effortless wildflower meadow" inside a venue with no windows and underfloor heating, which is like asking for a thunderstorm in a lift, but I gave it to her. Four hundred stems of cow parsley air-freighted from a grower who owed me a favour. A string quartet that learned her grandmother's favourite hymn from a phone video with wind noise on it. The groom's mother, who hated the bride with the quiet, patient hatred of a woman who has been rehearsing speeches for months, was seated eleven seats away from the microphone and given a glass of something excellent at the exact moment her face began its campaign.

I did all of this without my pulse rising above resting rate. That's the muscle. That's what it's for.

At 9:47 p.m., after the first dance (a choreographed waltz to a song about never letting go; the couple had been together fourteen months), I stood by the bar doing my final sweep — candles burning safely, gift table unlooted, no guest weeping in a stairwell — and felt the particular satisfaction of a machine confirming it still runs. Somewhere behind me someone was crying happy tears into a napkin. Somewhere else, a father was telling his new son-in-law he was getting a good one. The whole room hummed with belief. Two hundred times now I had built the temple and never once been asked to worship in it.

My phone buzzed against my hip. Then again. Then a third time, which broke protocol, because the only people who called three times were clients in crisis or my sister, and my sister knew better than to interrupt a wedding unless someone had died or gotten engaged.

I stepped into the service corridor, where the music went woolly, and answered.

"Mirabel." My sister's voice, flat in the way she flattens things when she doesn't want them to wobble. "Aunt Verity died."

* * *

Here is what I will say about Aunt Verity: I hadn't thought of her in years, which is its own kind of confession, because Verity Galloway was the only adult in my childhood who ever seemed to be paying attention. She lived alone outside Aldercombe, kept bees badly, drove a car held together by rust and opinion, and once told me, aged nine, that I had "an old soul, which is a polite way of saying you flinch." She was right. I've built an entire career on the flinch.

She'd been ill, apparently, since February. Hadn't wanted anyone told. Very her. My sister handled the funeral logistics — I offered, she declined, we both understood that my offers are professional-grade and therefore suspect — and then, three weeks later, a solicitor's letter arrived on cream paper so heavy it made a sound when you set it down, like a door closing somewhere expensive.

I opened it at my kitchen table on a Tuesday night, with the precision I bring to everything. Letter opener. Good light. The flat around me was the kind of place estate agents call "a calm space": grey, ordered, nothing on the walls that required feeling. I had designed it the way I design seating charts — to prevent incidents.

The letter said, in summary: Verity Anne Galloway, deceased, had bequeathed to me a one-third share in certain freehold property comprising some forty acres of orchard land near the village of Aldercombe, Somerset. Enclosed was a copy of the deed for my records.

I read the word *orchard* and felt a cold, specific dread, the kind you feel before you've let yourself know why.

Then I turned the page and saw the other names on the deed.

*Lindqvist.*

And beneath it, in ink that had faded fifteen years and somehow not at all:

*Freya Lindqvist.*

I want to describe what happened next accurately, because I've spent my whole life describing things inaccurately — beautifully, smoothly, in ways that make people feel attended to while telling them nothing. What happened next was this: nothing. I sat very still. The letter stayed in my hands. Outside, London did its enormous indifferent breathing, buses and sirens and someone laughing too loudly below my window, and I sat in my calm grey space holding a piece of paper that had just reached through eleven years of trained composure and taken hold of something I'd assumed was dead tissue.

Freya Lindqvist.

You can bury a name. I know this professionally. I have buried names for clients — exes struck from invitations, fathers uninvited, entire branches of family trees pruned for the sake of a peaceful ceremony. There's a technique to it. You don't delete the name; deletion leaves a hole people notice. You replace it. You seat someone else in its chair. You fill the silence where it used to be with so much competent noise that even you stop listening for it.

I had done that with Freya Lindqvist at seventeen years old, thoroughly, and I had maintained the work for fifteen years. Two hundred weddings' worth of maintenance. And here she was, co-signed on a deed, in a dead woman's handwriting-adjacent legal document, arriving through my letterbox like a bill.

Because here is the thing the letter didn't say but the deed made plain: Verity hadn't left me an orchard. She'd left me *into* one. A third share, held jointly — which meant that somewhere out there, Freya Lindqvist was about to learn that a stranger owned two-thirds of the ground under her feet, and that stranger was me.

I set the letter down. Picked it up. Read it again, as though the words might reorganize themselves into something less catastrophic, the way I ask florists to do with arrangements that have gone wrong.

Forty acres. I tried to picture it and got the old pictures instead, unbidden, unprompted, treacherous: long grass going silver at dusk. Apple blossom coming down like weather. A tree with our initials in it — hers cut deep, mine shallow, because I'd always been afraid of marking things permanently, even then, even at twelve, even before I had any idea what I'd eventually do. Freya halfway up that tree, calling down to me with bark dust on her palms, absolutely certain I would follow.

I always followed. That was the arrangement. She climbed; I followed; the world made sense.

Until the summer I didn't.

I stood up, walked to the window, looked at nothing. This is a technique. You'd be amazed how much of my life is techniques.

The solicitor's covering note mentioned that the co-owner had been notified of the change in ownership and that, given the joint nature of the tenancy, "the parties may wish to open discussions regarding the future of the asset." The future of the asset. Forty acres of trees that had known me before I learned to disappear, reduced to an asset with a future requiring discussion. Somewhere a developer was probably already circling, the way they do — I know the type, I've seated them at top tables, I've heard them talk about "maximizing the footprint" over the salmon.

And Freya — Freya, who had stayed. Who had grown up and stayed and presumably buried *me*, though clearly not as thoroughly, since the deed found her name intact, living, attached to the land the way I'd never been attached to anything except a schedule.

I thought about calling my sister. I thought about calling the solicitor. I thought about the four weddings I had booked between June and September, each one meticulously planned, each one a small cathedral of other people's certainty, and how none of them required me to feel a single thing.

What I actually did was this: I poured a drink I didn't want, sat back down, and read the deed a third time, slowly, the way you'd reread a message from someone you wronged, looking for the exit that isn't there.

Her name at the bottom. Mine above it. Verity's signature witnessing both, dated the year before she died — meaning she'd done it deliberately, recently, knowing exactly what she was doing. Knowing me. *An old soul, which is a polite way of saying you flinch.* She'd flinched-proofed me straight into a corner. The only way out of joint tenancy is through the other person on the deed.

Through Aldercombe. Through the orchard. Through her.

I put the letter down and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes until I saw blossom-coloured ghosts, and I understood, with the clarity I usually reserve for other people's crises, that the muscle had failed. Eleven years of training, two hundred flawless executions, and one name on one piece of paper had dropped the whole structure like a felled tree.

The train timetable was on my laptop. I didn't look at it. I looked at it. There was one direct service to Aldercombe the following morning, 7:42, changing at Taunton.

I closed the laptop. Opened it again. Booked the ticket before the sensible part of me could file an objection, because I have learned that about myself: if I give the sensible part time to speak, it wins, and it has been winning for fifteen years.

Then I did what any professional would do. I opened my calendar, rescheduled nothing, colour-coded my panic, and went to bed in my calm grey space, where I lay staring at the ceiling until three in the morning composing, in my head, the opening line of the conversation I would need to have with a woman I abandoned at seventeen, who now legally shared dirt with me.

Every draft began with *Dear Freya.* Every draft was terrible.

Outside, London carried on believing in nothing in particular, efficiently, all night long.
