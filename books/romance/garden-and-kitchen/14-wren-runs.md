## 14. Wren Runs

The fight started before breakfast, which was the worst part, because Wren had been counting on pancakes.

She'd heard them through the floor of the flat above the bookshop, their voices rising like water in a cistern — her dad's low and controlled, the way it got when he was angriest, and Elodie's sharper, quicker, cutting across him. She couldn't make out the words, only the shape of them, the way you can tell a storm is coming from the color of the sky without knowing how many millimeters of rain will fall.

By the time she came downstairs, they'd moved into the shop itself, standing between the poetry shelves like two people who'd forgotten they were in a room full of fragile things. Her father had his arms crossed. Elodie had her coat half on, one sleeve empty, as if she'd started to leave mid-sentence.

"—didn't tell me," Elodie was saying. "That's the whole point, Sebastian. You *decided* for me. Again."

"I was trying to protect—"

"You don't get to decide what protecting looks like. Not for me. Not anymore."

Wren stood on the bottom stair with her school bag still on her shoulder and watched them both realize she was there. It happened in the same instant, the way their faces changed — her father's going carefully blank, Elodie's flushing pink, the empty sleeve suddenly very interesting to her.

"Wren." Her dad's voice did the thing it always did, dropping into that warm, reasonable register he used when he wanted to pretend nothing was wrong. "You're going to be late."

"It's fine. Mrs. Okafor does registers at nine."

"Still." He crossed to her and kissed the top of her head, and she smelled the sawdust-and-paper smell of him, the smell she'd known her whole life, and something in her chest went tight and hot. "Toast's on the counter. I'll walk you up the hill after."

"Don't bother," she said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because his face did something complicated. "I mean — I've got the project. I'll go straight there."

Elodie was buttoning her sleeve now, not looking at either of them. "I'll be at the orchard," she said to no one in particular. "There's frost damage on the east rows I want to check."

The door chimed behind her. Her father stood in the middle of his own shop looking like a man who'd lost his place in a book he'd read a hundred times.

"Dad," Wren said. "Is she leaving? Like, actually leaving?"

"No." He said it too fast. Then, slower: "No. We're just — we're working some things out."

"You're always working things out." She hadn't meant to say that either. The words kept coming out of her like sap from a cut branch, running clear and then bitter. "You work things out at the orchard, and you work things out here, and nobody ever actually *works anything out*, you just keep having the same fight in different rooms."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw something flicker across his face that she'd never seen there before — not anger, not the tired patience she knew so well, but something older and more frightened. Guilt, maybe. Or recognition.

"That's not fair," he said quietly.

"I know," she said, because it wasn't, and because being twelve meant your unfairness got forgiven automatically, which somehow made it worse. She shouldered her bag. "I'm going to school."

* * *

She didn't go to school.

She got as far as the corner by the bakery, where Mrs. Fenwick was hosing down the pavement and called out something cheerful about blossom season coming early this year, and Wren waved and smiled and turned left instead of right, toward the orchard road, and kept walking.

It wasn't planned. That was what she'd tell herself later, walking the long way round past the stone walls with her bag bumping her hip — that it just happened, the way weather happens. But some part of her, the part that had spent three weeks making a wall chart with fourteen boxes and a countdown, the part that had photographed a demolition notice pinned to a gate and felt, for one bright moment, *useful* — that part knew exactly what it was doing.

Because here was the thing nobody said out loud: the orchard was hers too. Not legally — legally it belonged to her dad and Elodie, fifty-fifty, two names on one deed, which sounded romantic until you heard them fighting about it. But Wren had spent more hours under those trees this spring than she'd spent anywhere except her own bed. She'd chalked drawings on the shed wall — a sun with too many rays, a bee the size of a dinner plate, all three of them holding hands under a tree she'd drawn with suspiciously human faces. She'd helped carry smudge pots during the frost vigil until her hands smelled of paraffin for two days. She had a graft log with her name in it, in her own handwriting, dated entries about the Newton Wonder and the Lane's Prince Albert and the one they'd grafted together and named for her.

If Calloway won, he didn't just take the trees. He took the chalk bee off the shed wall. He took the loft where she'd once found an owl pellet and dissected it on a feed sack like a proper scientist. He took the version of her life where she had a place that wasn't the bookshop or school or her dad's worried face.

And now her dad and Elodie were ruining it, not with bulldozers but with each other, with their voices through the floor at seven in the morning, with *you decided for me again* and *I was trying to protect you*, and Wren couldn't fix that with a photograph or a chart or a festival. You couldn't testify against people you loved.

The gate was unlocked. It usually was now; the padlock had come off in March and never gone back on, because what was the point of locking a place you were trying to prove you loved?

Inside, the orchard was doing its morning thing, indifferent and beautiful. Blossom everywhere, drifts of it, petals coming loose in the breeze and settling on the grass like the aftermath of a celebration nobody remembered having. The bees were already at work in the east rows. Somewhere a wood pigeon said its five stupid notes over and over.

Wren walked down the central row to the barn, pushed open the big door, and climbed the ladder to the loft.

The loft smelled of hay and old apples and dust warmed by the light coming through the vent slats. There was the feed sack where she'd done the owl pellet. There was a crate she'd dragged over weeks ago to use as a table, still holding her tin of pencils and a jar with a snail shell in it. Someone — Elodie, probably — had left a folded blanket on the hay bales, and Wren pulled it around her shoulders like a cape and sat down with her back against the wall where she could see out through the gap in the boards.

From up here she could see the whole orchard laid out in rows, and beyond it the lane, and beyond that the roofs of town. She could see everything and nobody could see her. It was, she thought, the best feeling in the world, and also the loneliest.

She took out her school notebook, because even running away, she was still Wren Yarrow, and opened it to the orchard project page. At the top, in careful letters, she'd written: *GARDEN AND KITCHEN — A HISTORY OF HOLLOW ORCHARD, BY WREN YARROW, YEAR 7.*

Underneath, she'd written facts. *Planted 1911. 43 acres. 200+ trees. Grandmother Ivy Marsh ran it for 40 years.*

But projects weren't supposed to have feelings in them, and the feelings were the actual project, if anyone had bothered to ask. So she turned to a fresh page and wrote, in smaller letters:

*Question: why do grown-ups ruin everything they love?*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she wrote underneath:

*Method: observe subjects in natural habitat. Subjects: Dad (49), Elodie (44). Hypothesis: they love each other AND the orchard AND possibly me, and this is the problem, because loving things makes you scared of losing them and scared people do stupid things like keep secrets and sell land and shout through floors.*

She chewed the end of her pencil. Added:

*Conclusion: pending.*

Then she put the notebook away, wrapped the blanket tighter, and waited to see how long it would take anyone to notice she was gone.

* * *

They noticed at ten past nine.

Wren knew this because she heard the car come up the lane — her dad's ancient estate car, the one that sounded like a kettle deciding whether to boil — and then stop, and then nothing, and then his voice calling her name in a way that started normal and ended not-normal.

"Wren! WREN!"

She pressed her face to the gap in the boards. Below, her dad was standing by the gate, phone against his ear, turning in a circle. Even from the loft she could see the shape of panic on him, the way his shoulders had come up around his ears.

"No, she's not here—" A pause. "Well, when did you last— no, I understand, I'm not blaming—" Another pause, longer. "Yes. Yes, I'll call you the second—"

He hung up and stood very still in the middle of the lane, and Wren's stomach did a slow, sickening roll, because she had imagined this moment — had imagined it as triumphant, everyone realizing how much they'd missed her — and it didn't feel triumphant at all. It felt like watching someone drop a glass and waiting for it to break.

Her dad pulled out his phone again. This time, when he spoke, his voice cracked straight down the middle.

"Elodie. Is she with you?" Pause. "She's not at school. They called. Nobody's seen her since the corner by the bakery — Mrs. Fenwick says she turned toward the orchard road, but she's not—" His voice did the crack again. "I don't know. I don't *know*. Can you just—"

He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't have to. Thirty seconds later a second car came up the lane fast enough to throw gravel, and Elodie got out of it still wearing her orchard jacket, secateurs still in her pocket, hair full of blossom petals, and Wren watched the two of them meet in the middle of the lane like two people arriving at the same accident.

"When did you last see her?" Elodie said.

"This morning. Before—" He stopped. Started again. "We argued. She heard us arguing. She said 'don't bother' and left for school and I let her go because I thought—"

"You thought she was fine."

"I thought she was *angry*, which is different."

"It's not, though, is it." Elodie was already moving, scanning the hedgerows, the ditch, the gate. "Not for her. She hears everything, Sebastian. She's been hearing everything for weeks."

"I know that."

"Do you? Because from where I stand—"

"*Don't.*" His voice came out harder than Wren had ever heard it, and then immediately softer, breaking apart. "Please. Not now. Just help me find her."

Something passed between them then that Wren couldn't see properly from her angle — Elodie's face changing, the argument dying not because it was resolved but because it had been outranked by something bigger. She nodded once, sharply, and said, "Barn first. She likes the barn. And the loft — she found the owl pellet up there in February, she thinks nobody knows."

"You know about that?"

"I know everything about that kid. I've been feeding her stew since March, Sebastian. You notice things."

They split up. Wren watched them move through her orchard — *her* orchard, theirs, whatever it was — calling her name in voices that got less controlled and more frightened with every row. Her dad checked the pond and the far hedge. Elodie went straight to the barn, and Wren held her breath as the door scraped open below, as footsteps crossed the floor, as Elodie's voice came up through the boards, gentle and terrible:

"Wren? It's Elodie. I know you're probably not up there. But if you are, that's okay. Nobody's cross."

Wren said nothing. Her heart was going so hard she could hear it in her ears.

Footsteps retreated. The door scraped shut. And Wren sat in the hay-smelling dark and discovered that hiding worked exactly as well as she'd hoped and felt exactly as bad.

* * *

The afternoon went long and gold and awful.

Wren stayed in the loft because coming down had become impossible — every hour that passed made it worse, made it a Bigger Thing, and by two o'clock she understood with terrible clarity that she had built herself a trap out of stubbornness, the exact same material, she realized, that her dad built his silences out of, and that Elodie built her leaving out of. It was apparently a family trait, whatever family was.

Below, the search grew. Mr. Trelawney from the farm next door walked the boundary with a stick. Mrs. Fenwick arrived in her bakery van and handed out tea from a thermos like it was a siege. Somebody called the police, and a constable came and stood at the gate writing things in a notebook, and Wren watched her dad talk to him with his hands doing the explaining-for-him thing they did when words failed.

At some point Elodie stood alone in the middle of the central row, and Wren watched her press both hands flat against the trunk of the oldest tree — her grandmother's tree — and put her forehead against the bark, and stay there a long time. Wren had never seen an adult do that before. It looked like praying. It looked like asking permission.

By four o'clock, her dad's voice had gone rough as uncut timber. *"Wren! Love, please!"* — and that word, *love*, said raw into the empty rows, made her eyes sting worse than anything all day.

She almost came down then. She honestly almost did.

But dusk was coming, and dusk was when it would matter most, and some final piece of childish logic told her that if she stayed hidden until dark, they would understand — really understand — what it felt like to spend a whole day not knowing. What it felt like when people you loved kept things from you. When they decided things for you. When they fought over an orchard like it was a prize instead of asking the person who'd drawn the chalk bee whether she wanted to keep it.

So she stayed. And the light went amber, then rose, then grey, and the searchers thinned, and the constable's car left, and her dad and Elodie ended up standing together at the gate in the last light, and even from the loft Wren could see that the fight was gone out of them. Whatever was left was worse and better: two exhausted people leaning toward each other without meaning to.

"We should check the loft again," Elodie said. "Properly this time. With a torch."

"She's not—"

"With a torch, Sebastian."

They came into the barn together. The torch beam swung up and caught dust motes, the rafters, the folded edge of blanket —

—and Wren, before she could stop herself, sneezed.

The beam snapped to her like a hawk. For one second nobody moved. Then her dad dropped the torch — it hit the floorboards and rolled, throwing crazy shadows — and came up the ladder faster than she'd ever seen him climb anything, and then his arms were around her and he was saying her name over and over into her hair, and he was shaking, actually shaking, and Wren, who had planned to be dignified, burst into tears instead.

"I'm sorry," she sobbed. "I'm sorry, I was right here the whole time, I just wanted—"

"Shh. Shh. You're all right. You're all right."

Elodie came up more slowly and sat down on the hay bale beside them, close enough that her shoulder touched Wren's back, and didn't say anything at all, which was somehow the best thing anyone had done all day.

They stayed like that while the barn went dark around them and the first bats started up in the rafters. Finally Wren wiped her face on the blanket and asked the question she'd been carrying since morning, the one she'd written in her notebook, the one that had made her climb up here in the first place.

"Why do you keep ruining everything you love?"

Her dad went still. "Wren—"

"No, I want to know." Her voice wobbled but held. "You love the orchard and you fight about it. You love each other — don't say you don't, I've *seen* you — and you fight about that too. You love me and you don't tell me anything. Grandma Marsh loved this place her whole life and it nearly got sold anyway. Why does loving stuff make everyone do the worst things? Why can't anybody just *say* things?"

The silence went on so long that a bat swooped through the vent slats and out again.

Then her dad said, quietly, "Because we're frightened. All of us. Frightened people hoard the truth like it'll keep, and it doesn't. It rots." He took a breath. "I've been frightened for a very long time, Wren. Longer than you've been alive. And I've told myself it was protection, keeping quiet, deciding things for people — but Elodie's right. It isn't. It's just fear wearing a helpful coat."

Wren looked at Elodie. Elodie's eyes were wet in the torchlight, but her voice, when it came, was steady.

"And I ran away once, because staying felt like shrinking. And I told myself it was brave. Some of it was. Some of it was just running." She reached over and tucked a strand of hay out of Wren's hair. "You asked why adults ruin things. Here's my answer: we don't ruin them on purpose. We ruin them slowly, by not saying the true thing, one day at a time. And then somebody has to be brave enough to say it out loud — even if it's late. Even if it costs."

"Like at the hearing," Wren said. "Like you'll have to do."

Neither of them answered right away. Above them the bats stitched the dark. Down the rows, the blossom stood white in the last grey light, holding on.

"Fourteen days," Wren said into the quiet. "That's what's on my chart. Fourteen days, and you two are up here crying in a barn." She sniffed hard, and something in her chest shifted — the same feeling she'd had photographing the notice on the gate, the useful feeling, the one that mattered. "So here's what's going to happen. Tomorrow, we're all going to sit at the kitchen table, and you're going to tell me *everything* — the taxes, the offers, the night Mum died, all of it, because I'm twelve, not stupid. And then we're going to win this thing. Together. Because it's not just your orchard."

Her dad made a sound that was half laugh, half something else entirely.

"It's mine too," Wren said. "I did a project on it."

And below them, faint through the floorboards, her father began, at last, to laugh — a broken, grateful sound — while Elodie pulled them both closer, and somewhere out in the dark orchard the oldest tree stood over its rows like a grandmother who intended to see this through.

What none of them saw, from up in the loft, was the figure at the gate — Mr. Calloway, come to walk the boundary at dusk, pausing with his phone raised, photographing the lit barn window where three silhouettes leaned together.

Proof, he'd call it later, of anything he needed it to prove.
