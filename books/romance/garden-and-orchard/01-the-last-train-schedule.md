## 1. The Last Train Schedule

The ledger didn't lie, but Oscar had learned to make it hold its tongue.

He sat at the kitchen table with the lamp turned low — electricity was a line item now, one he'd begun to think of in hours rather than kilowatts — and worked through the winter audit the way other men said their prayers: nightly, methodically, without much hope of an answer. Outside, the first hard frost had silvered the vine rows, and the valley below was already going dark. Somewhere down there, the last train of the evening would be pulling through Kestrel Hollow at 9:47, right on schedule, carrying people toward lives that still had margins.

Oscar Eriksen did not have margins. He had columns.

*Cellar stock,* he wrote. *1,140 bottles, mixed vintages. Wholesale value at distress pricing: 11,400.* He knew the number the way you know your own pulse. The wine was good — his father's last vintages were better than good, they were the kind of wine that made sommeliers go quiet mid-sentence — but good wine sold slowly, and slow was the one currency the bank refused to accept.

*South slope, 4.2 hectares.* His pen hovered over that line longer than it needed to. The south slope caught the morning sun first and held it longest; it was where his father had planted the old Riesling, the vines that made the wine people drove up from the city for. Selling it would be like selling the east wall of a house and calling it renovation.

*Tractor, 1998, repairs exceeding value.* *Pickup, same arithmetic.* *Equipment, depreciated to sentiment.*

And then the last line, the one he wrote every night and crossed out every morning, as if the crossing-out could keep it theoretical:

*The house.*

Two hundred years of Eriken hands had raised those beams. His grandfather had cut them from trees on the north ridge. His mother had hung curtains in every window twice, once for each marriage, and both times she'd stood back in the yard to check the light through them, because light, she said, was the only thing you couldn't buy secondhand.

Oscar set down the pen and pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes until the dark behind them bloomed red.

The auction notice had come three weeks ago. *Public sale of mortgaged property, Eriksen Vineyard & Orchard, pursuant to default.* Ninety days. Sixty-seven remaining. He'd pinned it to the corkboard above the desk in the office, not out of self-punishment but out of discipline — the way a boxer tapes a photo of his opponent above his bed. You looked at what could take you from you until looking no longer hurt, and then you used the anger.

Except anger hadn't come. Only arithmetic.

He'd gotten very good at arithmetic these past two years. It had replaced other skills he'd once possessed — tenderness, mostly, which he thought of now the way you think of a language you studied in school: useful once, forgotten on purpose, embarrassing to attempt. There had been a woman who spoke it fluently. There had been a summer when he'd rehearsed saying *I love you* against the headboard of her rented room, practicing the weight of it, and then there had been a train platform in September and ten years of silence, and somewhere in between he'd decided that love was a liability. A debt you took on with no collateral and no fixed term. He'd trained himself out of it the way you train yourself out of any expensive habit.

It had worked. That was the terrible part. It had actually worked.

The kettle ticked on the stove. He got up to pour the last of the tea — the good coffee was gone by November, another line item — and while he stood at the window waiting, he saw the headlights coming up the valley road.

That was unusual. The road past Eriksen Farm went nowhere except to Eriksen Farm and, beyond it, the forest service track. Deliveries came in daylight. Neighbors called ahead. But these lights were moving fast and confident, the way city people drive, two sets of them, one following the other, and Oscar felt something in his chest tighten before he could name it — some animal instinct left over from a life he no longer lived, telling him that tonight, of all nights, the world was about to stop being arithmetic.

Then the snow started.

Not drifting, not flurrying — *starting*, the way mountain snow does, all at once, fat flakes dropping out of a sky that had been clear five minutes earlier. The forecast had said tomorrow. The forecast had been wrong about a great many things this year, including the price of grapes and the mercy of banks.

The headlights slowed. One pair swung wide, hesitated, kept climbing toward the pass. The other pair turned onto his drive, then off it, then sideways — the particular, sickening geometry of a car losing its argument with ice — and slid to a stop half-in the ditch at the bottom of his lane.

Oscar stood at the window with his cooling tea and watched the driver's door open. Even at this distance, even in the falling snow, he knew the silhouette. Ten years is a long time. It is also, apparently, no time at all.

He put down the cup. He took his coat off its hook, and his boots, and he went out into the cold to meet the two things he had spent a decade arranging his entire life to avoid: a stranger's trouble, and Maren Kowalczyk.

Behind him, on the kitchen table, the ledger lay open under the lamp, the last column unfinished, the house unsold for one more night.

* * *

Later — though he couldn't yet know how much later, or how everything that followed would begin here — he would remember the exact sound the storm made as it closed the roads behind her: not a roar, but a hush, like a hand laid gently over the whole valley's mouth.

The bank's letter had arrived that afternoon. He hadn't opened it yet. It sat on the hall table where he'd left it, white and patient as snowfall, and it would sit there all night, unopened, while three histories waited out the weather under one roof.

Some letters keep. Some storms don't.
