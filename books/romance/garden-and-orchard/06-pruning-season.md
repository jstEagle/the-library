## 6. Pruning Season

The storm held for three days, and on the fourth it didn't lift so much as settle — the sky flattening into a low white ceiling, the valley muffled, the roads still buried under drifts the plows wouldn't reach until the county ran out of excuses. Oscar woke before dawn out of habit, dressed in the dark, and stood at the kitchen window with his coffee going cold, looking at a vineyard that looked less like a business than like a sentence he hadn't finished serving.

Behind him, the farmhouse had changed its sound. For ten years it had been one man's silence: floorboards, pipes, the tick of the stove. Now there were two other sets of footsteps learning the house's grammar. Ingrid rose early too — earlier, maybe; he'd heard her in the guest room at five, moving with the crisp economy of a woman who treated even captivity as an itinerary. And Maren slept late, or pretended to, her door staying shut until the smell of coffee made its argument.

He should have resented it. He told himself he did resent it. Two rivals for his land, sleeping under his roof, eating his bread, drinking the wine he could not afford to open. But resentment required a clean surface to grow on, and his was crowded with older things: the way Maren said *good morning* like a question she already knew the answer to, the way she'd found the good cast-iron pan without being told, the way she stood at the window some evenings watching the snow come down over the south slope with an expression he refused, on principle, to name.

On the fourth morning he took down the pruning shears.

It wasn't strategy. It was survival. A vineyard left unpruned through a hard winter would wake up confused in spring, spending its strength on wood instead of fruit, and he could not afford confusion. He could not afford anything. That was the whole of it, the whole arithmetic of his life now, and work was the only ledger that balanced.

He was oiling the blades when Maren came down, hair loose, wearing one of his flannel shirts over her own clothes because her suitcase had drowned in Ingrid's ditch along with most of Ingrid's dignity.

"You're going out," she said. Not a question either.

"Vines don't wait for plows."

"Your father used to say that." She said it carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "He also used to say the vines wait for no one but forgive everyone."

"He said a lot of things." Oscar snapped the blade shut. "Most of them are still true."

She looked at him across the kitchen, steam rising off her mug, and he watched her decide something — he'd always been able to watch her decide things, that was the cruelty of knowing someone once, the knowledge doesn't leave, it just goes dormant like the vines. Then she set down the mug.

"Teach me."

"You remember."

"I remember watching." She pulled the flannel straight. "There's a difference. You used to say that too."

He hadn't. She had. He let her have it anyway.

* * *

The cold out on the slope was the honest kind, dry and total, the kind that made everything simple. Snow came to mid-shin between the rows. The vines stood bare and black against the white, knuckled and patient, each one a small decision waiting to be made about what lived and what went.

Oscar worked the first row alone while they watched, because demonstration was easier than conversation. The shears made their small wet sounds. Cane by cane he showed them the year's architecture — keep this spur, drop that arm, the vine wants to spend itself everywhere and your job is to be cruel in the right places.

"You prune for the wine you want," he said, "not the plant you have."

"And if you can't afford the wine you want?" Ingrid asked. She'd come out in a city coat worth more than his tractor, gloves folded neatly in her pocket, refusing help over the drifts with a look that dared anyone to offer it twice.

"Then you prune for the wine you can sell," he said, "and hate yourself in March."

Ingrid almost smiled. It was the closest thing to peace any of them had managed since the ditch.

Maren worked beside him. Of course she did; Ingrid drifted to the far row with her phone held aloft, hunting signal like a woman panning for gold, and the row between them became a border no one had agreed to. Maren's hands remembered faster than her pride allowed. She cut where he would have cut, three times out of four, and the fourth time she'd pause, frowning at the cane, and he'd reach past her — closer than necessary, closer than safe — and show her the bud hidden under the bark's gray skin.

"There," he said. "Feel it? Alive wood is green underneath. Dead is brown all the way through. Everything else is just weather."

Her thumb moved over the bud. Her shoulder touched his arm and stayed there a half-second longer than physics required.

"The Kowalczyk grafts," she said quietly. "My father's. Are any still in?"

"Rows eleven through fourteen. South side." He kept cutting. "I regrafted six of them myself after the frost year. They're stubborn. They take after him."

"They take after me," she said, and laughed — short, surprised, like the sound had escaped custody.

And there it was. Laughter, back on his land after ten years, trespassing, and God help him, he wanted to hear it again the way a thirsty man wants a well he knows is poisoned.

* * *

They fell into it over the days that followed, the rhythm of the rows. Ingrid worked too — nobody asked her and nobody thanked her, she simply appeared each morning in better boots than yesterday, having apparently ordered them telepathically, and pruned with the ruthless efficiency of a woman who had never loved a plant in her life and therefore cut exactly right. By the second day she and Oscar had stopped speaking except in numbers: yields, tons per acre, the price of French oak versus Hungarian. It was, obscenely, the best negotiation he'd had in years.

But it was Maren who learned the grafts.

He took her to row twelve on the third afternoon, when the light went long and blue, and showed her where his father's cuts met her father's — two dead men's handwriting joined in living wood, a scar tissue seam you could read with your eyes closed if you'd grown up reading it.

"Splice graft," he said. "His stock, your scion. Your father brought the cuttings the summer we were twenty-two. Said Eriksen rootstock was the only thing in this valley stubborn enough to hold a Kowalczyk top."

"He said the same about me," Maren murmured, running her hand along the seam. "About needing stubborn ground."

Oscar's shears stopped.

The wind moved down the row, lifting fine snow off the trellis wires, and for a moment neither of them breathed in it. He could feel the old sentence assembling itself behind his teeth — *I never stopped, I never wrote because writing would have meant meaning it, I sold my twenties to this dirt and it still isn't enough* — and he swallowed it, because hope was a liability, hope was the one debt he'd sworn off, and a man who couldn't pay his notes shouldn't be taking on new ones.

"Your father would have hated what this place has become," he said instead. "Half of it's mortgaged twice."

"Your father would have hated what you've become," she said, not cruelly. "A man who talks about his land like it's a debt instead of a garden."

"It is a debt."

"It was a garden." She turned to face him, snow in her dark hair, cheeks burned red with cold, eyes bright with something he refused to diagnose. "You used to say the difference between a garden and a field is whether anyone loves it enough to lie to the bank about it."

"I never said that."

"No," she agreed. "I did. You just laughed."

He laughed now — one breath, involuntary, gone as fast as it came — and hated himself for it, and wanted it back, and stood there in row twelve holding pruning shears like a man holding a knife at his own throat.

"We should finish the row," he said.

"Yes," she said. "We should."

Neither of them moved for exactly long enough to matter. Then they did.

* * *

That night he caught himself doing it — standing at the cellar door with a candle, listening to Maren's footsteps overhead, doing arithmetic he'd forbidden himself. Not debts this time. Futures. The south slope planted to her father's clones. The tasting room rebuilt. Her name on the deed next to his, which was insane, which was impossible, which was a fantasy for a man whose bank letter sat upstairs in a drawer like a loaded gun.

He blew out the candle. He went to bed. He lay awake rehearsing the sentence that would end it cleanly: *when the roads open, you should go.* Three words of mercy, self-administered, like pulling a tooth before the rot spreads.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow I'll say it.

In the morning, Ingrid was standing at the kitchen table with the bank letter open in front of her, and her face had the terrible calm of a chess player four moves from mate.

"Sit down, Oscar," she said. "We need to talk about what you owe."
