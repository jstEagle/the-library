## 5. Intermission

The storm came in the way storms do on this coast — without apology, without warning, and with the specific intention of ruining someone's day. Mine, as it happened.

I'd gone down to the boathouse at four o'clock to check on the rowboat my grandmother apparently kept, because the estate agent had mentioned it in the tone of voice people use for liabilities. The sky had been the color of an old pewter spoon. By five, it was the color of a bruise. By half past, the wind was tearing strips off the water and hurling them at the windows, and the road back up to the house had turned into a shallow river with ambitions of becoming a deep one.

Theo arrived at six, banging on the boathouse door like a man with a grudge against doors.

"You're still here." He was soaked through, which did nothing for his charm and somehow did everything for it. "The ferry's tied down. Road's flooded at the bend. Nobody's going anywhere."

"Then we're trapped."

"We're *stuck*. Trapped implies I didn't see it coming." He shook water off his jacket with the economy of a man who had done this many times and found it boring. "There's blankets in the locker. And a stove. And, if we're lucky, nothing else."

* * *

The boathouse, it turned out, was less a boathouse than a small stubborn building that had once known better days and had decided, with dignity, to keep them. There was a wood stove that smoked before it surrendered, two canvas chairs that smelled of mildew and decades, and a shelf holding a tin of biscuits so stale they could have been used as shims. Theo got the stove going. I got the blankets. Neither of us spoke much, which was its own kind of conversation.

The storm hit the roof in waves. The water slapped the hull of the rowboat, which rocked in its slings like something dreaming. Outside, the world had been reduced to noise and dark; inside, there was a circle of stove-light about six feet across, and we were both in it, and there was no polite way to leave.

"I should have checked the forecast," I said.

"Everyone should check the forecast. Nobody does. It's a human failing, like littering."

"You're very comforting."

"I'm not paid to comfort you." He fed another split of wood to the stove and watched the flames take it. "I'm not paid at all for this. I came because your car's parked at the top of the lane and it'll be underwater by morning if I don't move it."

"You came to move my car."

"I came to move the car." A pause, the length of a held breath. "And to check you weren't doing anything stupid with the letter."

I laughed — a short, involuntary sound that surprised us both. "The letter's in my coat pocket. It's not going anywhere. Neither am I, apparently."

"Good," he said, and then looked annoyed at himself for saying it.

* * *

We ate the stale biscuits because there was nothing else, and they were terrible in the way that food eaten during storms is never terrible — it was, in fact, the best thing I'd eaten in months, which said less about the biscuits than about the state of my life. Theo sat with his boots off, his feet steaming gently by the stove, and told me nothing about himself for a long time, and then, in the way of people trapped together, told me everything sideways.

His grandfather had worked the ferry for thirty years. Engine room, then deck, then the little office with the cracked window where he kept the logbooks in handwriting so beautiful it looked printed. He had a garden he tended like a second conscience. He never married. He never explained.

"He stopped writing letters in 2013," Theo said, to the stove rather than to me. "Before that, there were some. To a woman in the city, I think. He kept them in a cigar box. After — nothing. He'd still write to the ferry company, to the suppliers, Christmas cards to cousins. But not that. One day the box was empty and he never said why, and if you asked him he'd look at you like you'd asked about a debt."

"What happened in 2013?"

"I was fourteen. I asked him once, that winter. He was fixing the winch and he said, 'Some letters you write and some letters you answer, and the trouble is knowing which is which.' Then he sent me for parts and that was the end of it." Theo's jaw worked. "He wasn't a bitter man. That's what I keep coming back to. He was quiet. There's a difference. Bitter men talk. Quiet men just... stop."

The storm pressed against the walls. I found I was holding my coat pocket, over the letter, like a woman guarding a wound.

"May I tell you something?" I said. "And you can't do the thing where you're kind about it. I'd rather you weren't kind."

"That's the first sensible thing you've said."

"I didn't hurt my hand." The words came out flat, rehearsed by silence, worn smooth by two years of not saying them. "That's the official version. Tendonitis, overwork, the cello finally billing me for all those hours. It's a good story. Doctors confirmed it, more or less. Agents repeated it. It has the advantage of being nobody's fault."

Theo said nothing. The stove ticked.

"It was the third movement," I went on, because now that the door was open the whole house was coming through it. "The Elgar. I'd played it a hundred times. I could play it in my sleep, and some nights I think I did. And I was halfway through the second theme — the part where the cello has to sing, really sing, where there's nowhere to hide behind technique — and I looked up. At the hall. Two thousand people. And they were all *looking at me*."

"And?"

"And I understood, all at once, with total clarity, that every one of them could see me. Not the cellist. Me. The actual person, with the actual face, doing the actual thing I'd spent my whole life doing so that no one would ever have to look at me directly." My hands had gone cold, and I pressed them against my knees. "And I froze. Not my fingers — my fingers were fine, they were waiting for instructions. *I* froze. The bow hair snapped on a downbow because I was pressing too hard without knowing it, and the sound it made — that little dry crack — was the most honest sound in the whole concert."

Theo was watching me now, fully, and I discovered that being seen in a boathouse by a grease-stained mechanic during a storm was a different order of experience than being seen by two thousand strangers. Smaller. Worse. Better.

"Two years," I said. "I've let everyone believe it was my hand. My mother believes it. My agent believes it. I've had two surgeries' worth of sympathy and I never needed one. I came here to sell this house because it's the last thing of hers and I thought — if I close it all out, the house, the letter, all of it — I could go back to not being seen. Professionally. Permanently."

"That's a stupid plan."

"Yes," I said, and to my horror my eyes were burning. "It's a very stupid plan. It's the plan of a woman hiding in her dead grandmother's attic, reading letters that were never sent, and thinking she's the only one in the world who ever chose silence."

The wind screamed against the seaward wall. Theo got up, put another split of wood on the fire, and sat back down — closer, this time, though the boathouse had not shrunk.

"My grandfather," he said slowly, "wrote letters for years and got one back. One. And then nothing. And he decided it meant she didn't — that he'd been a fool, an old man's summer romance, whatever he told himself. He carried that for the rest of his life. He died still carrying it." His voice had gone rough. "And now you're sitting here with her letter, the one she wrote and never sent, and I'm supposed to believe the silence was hers alone. That he just stopped."

"Maybe he did."

"Maybe. Or maybe somebody answered and the answer never reached him. Or maybe he wrote and never sent it. I don't know. That's the thing that's been eating me since you walked onto my ramp with that envelope." He looked at me, and there was something raw under the bluntness, something that had been raw for a long time. "I watched that silence kill something in him by inches, over twenty years, and everyone called it dignity. And now there's a letter. Eleven years late. And I don't know whether it's going to heal something or just prove it was never worth healing."

"That's not your decision to carry alone."

"Isn't it? Whose else is going to carry it? You didn't know him. I did."

We sat with that. The storm threw itself against the boathouse and the boathouse, being older and more stubborn than the storm, held.

* * *

Sometime past midnight the wind dropped to a long exhausted moan, and the rain went soft, and the stove burned down to a red contemplative eye. We had dragged our chairs close without discussing it. There was a blanket over both our knees that one of us had arranged and neither of us acknowledged. His shoulder was a warm fact against mine, and I found I didn't move away, and that not moving away was its own small revolution.

"You froze," he said quietly. "On stage."

"Yes."

"And you've never told anyone."

"You're the first."

"Why me?"

I considered the honest answers and rejected them all as too honest. "Because you don't do kindness," I said finally. "And because you knew my grandmother's silence from the inside. And because if I can't say it to a stranger in a boathouse, I'll never say it to anyone, and I'll be the woman with the hand injury for the rest of my life."

He was quiet a moment. "He never talked about her either," Theo said. "Not once. But he kept the garden. Thirty years. He'd be out there at six in the morning in the rain, doing things to roses that I now realize were not really about roses." A short, unsteady breath that might have been a laugh. "I thought it was just what old men did."

"And now?"

"Now I think he was answering a letter the only way he knew how. Every year. In the dirt." He turned his head, and we were close enough that I could see the stove-light in his eyes. "Your grandmother wrote and didn't send. He wrote and — maybe didn't send. Two people, forty feet apart in every way that matters, both choosing the same silence." His voice dropped. "And you froze on a stage rather than be seen, and I've spent my whole life fixing engines because engines don't look at you."

"Engines are honest," I managed.

"Engines are honest," he agreed, and something passed between us then that had no name I was willing to give it — not tenderness, not yet, something before tenderness, the way a key is before a door.

The fire settled. The rowboat knocked gently against its slings. And just before sleep took me, in a boathouse that smelled of mildew and woodsmoke and other people's courage, Theo said, almost to himself:

"There's a place in the ferry office. A drawer he locked. I've had the key since he died. I never opened it."

He looked at the dark window, at the drowned road, at me.

"Maybe tomorrow I will."
