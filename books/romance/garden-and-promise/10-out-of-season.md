## 10. Out of Season

The post office on Harbor Road smells like old glue and colder weather, and the woman behind the counter has been weighing my life in increments all week.

"Two letters today," she says, not a question. She's seen me come in every morning for two weeks with one envelope at a time, like I was rationing courage. "You're almost done."

"I'm mailing them together," I say. "It seemed cowardly to keep splitting them up."

She weighs them anyway — grandmother's letter, unsent for eleven years, and Elias's reply, hidden in a drawer of the ferry office for just as long, each one an answer the other never received. Two people writing across a silence that someone else had built and maintained like a seawall. The postage comes to less than four dollars. It seems obscene that eleven years of damage could be shipped so cheaply.

"They'll go to Theo's family archive," I tell her, because she asks, because everyone in this town asks, because that's what a town is: a place where your business becomes communal property whether or not you consent. "His aunt keeps it. Boxes of letters going back to the 1950s. Ferry logs, photographs, correspondence." I pause. "Love letters."

"Good," she says, stamping them with a thump that sounds like a verdict. "Letters shouldn't die in attics."

I watch her drop them into the bin behind her, and something in my chest unclenches that has been clenched since August. My grandmother chose respectability over Elias and let him believe he'd been ignored. Elias wrote back anyway and hid the proof where no one would find it until his grandson went looking for a spark plug diagram and found a confession instead. There was no villain in it, finally. Just two frightened people and one lie of omission, tended carefully for a decade like a bitter little garden.

The letters are going home together now. Whatever happens after that — whatever Theo's aunt thinks, whatever the family archive makes of it — they will at least be in the same box. It's more than they got in life.

* * *

The developer's name was Craig, and he smelled like cologne applied by someone else, and he called the house "a tear-down with good bones," which is a sentence that should be illegal.

"The lot's the value," he explained, tapping the window that looked out on the garden — my grandmother's garden, the one Elias helped plant the summer everything ended, the one gone half-wild and then, under Theo's hands this fall, half-rescued. "Rip out the shrubs, put up a modern build, six bedrooms, water views from every angle. You'd walk away with—"

"No," I said.

He blinked. "The offer is quite—"

"It's not about the number." And here's the strange thing: it genuinely wasn't, and I knew it the way you know a note is flat, instantly and in your body. Three months ago I would have taken the money. Three months ago I was a woman closing doors — the house, the career, the whole corridor of my life — and a check is a very efficient door-closer. But I had spent September reading my grandmother's handwriting and October learning what hiding costs, and I found I could no longer be enthusiastic about demolition.

"Who else would want it?" Craig asked, with the sincerity of a man who genuinely couldn't imagine an alternative to himself.

"A musician," I said. "A young one. Someone who needs a room with morning light and doesn't mind that the floors creak in D minor."

He left. I sold the house three weeks later to a cellist named Priya who is twenty-six, plays Prokofiev like she's arguing with it, and cried when she saw the attic — not because of the letter, which I did not mention, but because of the light. "You could rehearse up here," she said, turning in a circle with dust motes swirling around her like an audience. "You could *live* up here."

"You could," I agreed. "I'm staying through winter anyway. To transition things."

"To transition things," Theo repeated later that night, in the tone of a man quoting a suspicious document. We were in the boathouse, which had become, without either of us announcing it, the place where we went to say true things. He was repairing a heater that had no business being repairable. "That's what we're calling it."

"That's what the paperwork calls it."

"And what do you call it?"

I watched him work. His hands were stained the permanent color of engines, and there was sawdust in his hair, and when he glanced up at me he had the particular patience of a man who has already decided to wait as long as it takes. Elias's grandson down to the bone, according to the photographs in the ferry office. The same set of the shoulders. The same refusal to look away first.

"I call it," I said, "staying."

He set down the wrench. "Say it again."

"Staying. Through winter. Past winter, probably. Indefinitely, if we're being precise, and I'm told precision matters to musicians." My heart was doing something embarrassing. "I spent years perfecting the art of leaving rooms before anyone could see me clearly. I'm trying a new discipline. It's harder. Nobody warned me it would be harder."

"Nobody ever tells you that," Theo said. "They just hand you the brochure with the sunset on it."

* * *

Winter came the way it does here — not arriving so much as settling, like silt. The ferry went into layup and Theo commuted to the yard in Rockport instead of the ramp, and I learned what the house sounded like with its radiators ticking, and Priya moved in upstairs and played Bach through my ceiling at hours that would have been unforgivable if the playing weren't so good.

I didn't play. Not at first. The battered ferry-office cello stayed in the boathouse where I'd played the last crossing, and I walked past it every day the way you walk past a dog that bit you once and keeps looking at you hopefully.

Then, in February, Priya knocked on my door holding sheet music. "D minor," she said. "You mentioned. Once. At length. While pretending you weren't mentioning it." She held out the part. "There's a recital at the library in March. Terrible acoustics, folding chairs, coffee from a urn. It's perfect. Nobody important will be there."

"Nobody important," I repeated.

"Well." She shrugged, younger than me and infinitely braver. "One person. Maybe. If he can get off shift."

I took the music. My hands did not shake, which surprised me; then they shook, which surprised me more; then I sat down at the kitchen table and read through the first page slowly, note by note, like a letter I was finally allowing myself to answer.
