## 13. Tide Pools at Low Water

The tide pools sat below the old quarry road, past the rusted gate that had been padlocked since before either of them was born. You reached them by climbing down a seam in the bluff where someone—Ada Quill, probably, decades ago—had driven rebar footholds into the rock, and you timed your descent to the moon or you didn't go at all.

Paloma checked her watch against the tide chart folded in her jacket pocket. *Minus 0.8 at 6:14 p.m.* She'd memorized it the way she used to memorize everything: as a hedge against chaos, a small arithmetic of certainty.

"You're sure this is legal," Theo said behind her, one hand on the bluff face, his boots finding the rebar with the easy confidence of a man who'd grown up on boats he wasn't supposed to be on.

"Nothing about this place has ever been legal," she said. "That's why it's still here."

The pools opened below them like scattered coins—dozens of them, basins of granite worn smooth by ten thousand years of patient water, each one holding its own small civilization. Anemones with their green-tipped tentacles furled like closed fists. A forest of feather-boa kelp lying spent and gleaming in the drained shallows. In the deepest pool, near the seaward edge, something orange moved—a garibaldi, stranded temporarily in its glass cathedral, furious about it.

Paloma crouched at the rim of the nearest pool and did what she always did when the world got too loud: she started counting.

"Twelve aggregating anemones," she murmured, half to herself. "Three ochre stars. One—that's odd—one chiton." She touched the water's surface film lightly, breaking it, watching the tension heal. "They'll refill in four hours. Everything you see dying right now is just waiting."

Theo lowered himself onto a flat shelf of rock beside her, close enough that his knee nearly brushed hers, not quite. He'd brought two thermoses of coffee in a canvas bag, which he set down between them without comment, and for a while neither of them spoke. The ocean worked at the edge of the exposed reef, a long rhythmic exhale. Gulls argued over something on the far rocks. The light was going amber and long, the kind of light that made even the burned-out marine center visible up the coast look almost gentle, its blackened ribs softened by distance into something like driftwood.

She had asked him here without explaining why. He had come without asking. That was the shape of them so far—two people circling the same wreck from opposite sides of the water, each pretending they weren't looking for survivors.

"Theo." She said his name the way you test ice before putting weight on it.

He turned. His eyes were brown going gold in the low sun, and there was nothing guarded in them, which was somehow worse than if there had been. Guardedness she could work with. Openness required things of her.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and I need you to not do the thing where you get quiet and start building a case in your head."

"I don't build cases. I build spreadsheets."

"Same thing with worse formatting."

A ghost of a smile. "Okay," he said. "Tell me."

* * *

She told him about the night itself first, because she had learned—if you could call fifteen years of avoidance *learning*—that the story came out cleanest in chronological order, like a dissection, like a log entry. Facts in sequence. No interpretation until the end.

"It was October. I was thirteen. Mateo was seventeen." Her voice came out level, and she was grateful for that, the way you're grateful for a handrail in the dark. "We weren't supposed to be out. There was a meteor shower—the Perseids, late that year—and Mateo wanted to watch it from the marine center roof. Ada let us. She kept a cot up there in summer; she said the best science happened after midnight. We were on the roof for maybe an hour. Then we heard a truck."

Theo had gone very still beside her. Not investigator-still. Listener-still. She could feel the difference, and it undid something in her chest that she hadn't known was knotted.

"I looked down through the skylight—we were right above Ada's office—and I saw Ruben Calloway carrying two metal cans across the loading dock. Red cans. The kind kerosene comes in. My brother's best friend, who'd eaten dinner at our table maybe forty times, walking into Ada's building at eleven o'clock at night with enough fuel to burn it twice over."

The garibaldi flicked through its pool, a live coal in the darkening water.

"Mateo saw him too. And Mateo grabbed my arm and said—" She stopped. Fifteen years, and the sentence still arrived in Mateo's exact voice, seventeen years old and terrified, the voice of a boy who understood something about loyalty that his little sister hadn't learned yet. "He said, *'Don't. Paloma. Don't you dare.'*"

"And you didn't," Theo said quietly.

"And I didn't." She picked up a fragment of mussel shell from the rock and turned it over. Blue-black on one side, mother-of-pearl on the other. "Forty minutes later the building was burning. Ada didn't make it out. And I stood in the crowd in my pajamas holding my brother's hand, and everyone in town was crying, and Ruben Calloway was crying harder than anyone, and I watched him do it and thought—that's what it looks like. That's what getting away with it looks like. It looks like grief. Better grief than anyone else's."

The tide was making its first tentative return, thin sheets of water sliding back across the reef, filling the outer pools first. Somewhere above them the quarry gate creaked in the wind.

"Why?" Theo asked. Not *why did Ruben do it*—she noticed that, noticed he'd saved that question, banked it. He asked instead: "Why did you stay silent?"

And there it was. The question under all the other questions, the one she'd been driving toward for three thousand miles and eleven days and fifteen years.

"Because Mateo begged me." She said it flatly, refusing to dress it up. "Because he was seventeen and Ruben was the only person who'd ever stood between him and trouble. You know Mateo got arrested at sixteen? Shoplifting, joyriding, the whole stupid catalog. Every time, Ruben took the blame or paid the fine or lied to the cops with a straight face. Mateo owed him his whole adolescence. So when Mateo grabbed my arm on that roof, he wasn't asking me to lie. He was asking me to help him pay a debt. And I was thirteen, and I loved my brother more than I loved a woman who was already dead, and I did the math the way children do math—which is to say, wrong."

She turned the mussel shell over again. Pearl side up now.

"And then the math got worse every year. Because the longer I waited, the more silence cost to break. First it would've been hard. Then it would've been cruel—to Mateo, to my mother, who died believing the fire was an accident. Then it would've been unbelievable. Who comes forward fifteen years later? What witness is worth anything after fifteen years? So I made a career out of the only thing silence was good for." She gestured at the pools, the reef, the gray-green immensity beyond them. "Counting. Turtles, transects, population densities. Numbers don't require testimony. You can spend your whole life counting things and never once have to say a true sentence out loud."

Theo was silent for a long moment. When he finally spoke, his voice was careful in a new way—not the carefulness of a man collecting evidence, but of a man handling something that might still be alive.

"The file," he said. "The one my predecessor left. It never named a suspect. But there was a statement in it—from Ada. Dated three days before the fire. She reported seeing 'one of the boys' with fuel cans near the center. The statement was withdrawn the next morning. She called the station and withdrew it herself."

Paloma's breath caught. "She withdrew it?"

"Signed withdrawal. Which means either she changed her mind about what she saw—" He shook his head. "Or someone convinced her to. Or she was protecting someone the same way your brother was protecting someone. This town runs on protection, Paloma. Half the docks are mortgaged to people covering for other people."

"She knew." Paloma stared out at the water, at the last band of copper light dissolving off the swells. "She knew and she tried to say something and then she took it back, and three days later she was dead in her own office." Her hands had gone cold despite the warmth of the evening. "I've spent fifteen years thinking I was the only coward in this story."

"You were a child."

"So was Ruben. Seventeen, same as Mateo. Nobody's using that word for him."

"No," Theo agreed. "Nobody is."

* * *

The coffee had gone lukewarm but she drank it anyway, wrapping both hands around the thermos cup, needing something to hold. The stars were arriving overhead, shy at first, then insistent. Below the reef line, the returning tide had begun its real work, water threading silver through the channels, and the pools that had been separate worlds all afternoon began, pool by pool, to reach each other.

"My colleague in San Simeon invited me to dinner once," she heard herself say. "Dr. Okafor. Nice man. Persistent." She laughed, short and self-aware. "I told him I was married to my dataset. He actually wrote that down. I think he thought it was charming. It was just true. I have organized my entire adult life so that no one ever gets close enough to ask me a question I can't answer with a number."

"And I'm asking you questions you can't answer with a number."

"You haven't asked me the one yet."

He turned to look at her fully then, and the starlight was enough to see his face by, barely—the line of his jaw, the attention in his eyes like a held breath. "Which one is that?"

"*Why did Ruben burn it down.*" She said it aloud for the first time in her life, and the words went out over the water and did not come back. "Everyone assumes faulty wiring. Ruben toasted to faulty wiring in front of the whole town last week. But Ada saw the cans. I saw the cans. Someone wanted that building gone, and I have spent fifteen years not letting myself wonder why, because wondering is the first step toward saying, and saying is—" 

"Saying is the sentence," Theo finished softly.

She looked at him sharply. "What did you say?"

"Nothing. Go on."

But he'd said it, and they both knew he'd said it, and the space between them changed temperature. Because she *had* a sentence. She had carried it for fifteen years, rehearsed it in motel bathrooms and rental cars and the tiled silence of a hundred field stations, a sentence polished smooth as sea glass from handling: *I saw Ruben Calloway carry the accelerant into the marine center the night Ada Quill died.*

She had never once said it to another living person.

"Theo." Her voice came out strange to her own ears. Thin. Thirteen years old. "If I tell you the rest of it—all of it—you can't un-know it. And you can't write it down. Not yet. Not until I decide when."

He set his cup down on the rock with deliberate care, and she watched him choose, visibly choose, between the man with the ledger and the man sitting on cold granite next to a woman coming apart at the seams.

"Not yet," he agreed. "When you decide."

The tide had reached them now, lapping at the base of their shelf, and the first pool—the one with the furious garibaldi—was open to the sea again, its prisoner free to leave and, being a fish, entirely unaware that it had ever been anywhere at all.

Paloma opened her mouth.

And that was when the flashlight beam swept down from the quarry road above them—white, deliberate, swinging once across the bluff face and once across the water where they sat—held there for three full seconds, and went dark.

Neither of them moved.

"Somebody knows this spot," Theo said quietly, already rising, his body shifting between her and the dark above. "Somebody who's been here before."

Paloma stayed crouched at the pool's edge, heart hammering against fifteen years of counted numbers, and watched the place where the light had been.
