## 19. The Night Before the Festival

Theo Vasquez had learned early that warrants were won on paper before they were won in courtrooms, and that paper was won on patience. He sat in the county courthouse in Port Alder at nine in the morning with a manila folder squared on the judge's clerk's counter, and inside that folder he had built, over eleven days, a small cathedral of evidence: photocopies of Ada Quill's letters, Marge Okonkwo's signed statement notarized at the bait shop counter between racks of squid jigs, the fire marshal's original report with its suspiciously tidy conclusion of faulty wiring, and the gap in Ruben Calloway's alibi that Ada's own statement—dated four days before her death—had papered over.

"Judge Voss doesn't like reopening old wounds," the clerk said, not unkindly, as she thumbed through the pages.

"Judge Voss doesn't have to like it," Theo said. "She just has to read it."

The clerk looked up at him over her glasses, and something in his face must have told her he wasn't going to be charming about it, because she carried the folder back without another word.

He waited forty minutes on a bench beneath a portrait of some dead supervisor, his knee bouncing in a rhythm he couldn't still. Through the tall windows he could see the harbor in his mind even though the courthouse faced inland—could see the *Pelican Rose* at her slip, could see the festival crews stringing lantern wire along the pier pilings, five hundred paper globes waiting in crates like sleeping birds. Tomorrow night they would all rise at once. Tomorrow night Paloma Dravenmoor would either speak or lose her chance forever, because Ruben Calloway was not a man who planned to be in the room when the room turned against him.

The clerk returned. "Judge will see you."

Judge Voss was seventy, sharp as a filleting knife, and she read the entire file in silence while Theo stood at parade rest. When she finally looked up, she tapped Ada's second letter—the one from the tide-chart drawer—with one lacquered nail.

"A dead woman's handwriting."

"Corroborated by a living witness, Your Honor. Marge Okonkwo received the confiding directly, eleven years ago, and can testify to the timeline."

"And the accelerant cans. Your fire marshal never tested for residue?"

"The original marshal was Ruben Calloway's uncle. The current one has agreed to re-test if we can secure samples from the site. That requires the warrant."

Voss leaned back. "You know what this town is going to do to itself when this comes out."

"I do, Your Honor."

"And the Dravenmoor girl. She's prepared to give a statement?"

Theo thought of Paloma on the tide pools at low water, her voice steady and her hands not. "She's been prepared for fifteen years. She just hasn't been asked."

The judge signed. The stamp came down like a gull striking water.

* * *

Ruben Calloway felt the change in the town the way he felt weather in his bad knee—before the clouds arrived, before anyone else admitted it was coming.

It was small things. Marge Okonkwo wouldn't meet his eye at the oyster bar, and Marge had been meeting his eye for thirty years, through two divorces and one funeral apiece. The harbormaster's office had been closed all morning with the lights on. And Danny Pruitt, who pumped fuel at the dock and had never in his life kept a secret longer than a tide cycle, had gone quiet when Ruben clapped him on the shoulder and asked if the *Calloway Girl* was fueled and ready for a run up the coast after the festival.

"Ready as she'll ever be, Chief," Danny had said, and looked at his shoes.

Ready as she'll ever be. Ruben stood in the boathouse behind his house on Cypress Lane and packed by lantern light, because he'd decided against switching on the overheads. A man packing for a fishing trip packed in daylight. A man packing for something else packed in the dark.

He worked methodically, the way he'd been trained, the way he'd trained three generations of volunteers. Waterproof bag. Cash from the safe—not all of it, just enough to look like nothing. The photograph of him and Mateo Dravenmoor at sixteen, arm in arm on this very dock, he wrapped in a shirt and left in the drawer. Some things you didn't take with you because taking them meant admitting they'd become evidence.

His phone buzzed. A text from a number with no name: *Judge signed at 10:40. Service tomorrow morning.*

He'd known it was coming. He'd known since the files vanished from Vasquez's office—no, before that, since Paloma Dravenmoor walked back into Pelican Hollow with her turtle-counting eyes and her fifteen years of silence wearing thin. He'd known since the night of the fire itself, standing in the eucalyptus grove with the cans sweating in his hands, watching the center bloom orange, watching little Paloma's face at the edge of the light.

He'd known, and he'd stayed anyway, because staying was the whole trick. Fifteen years of pancake breakfasts and toy drives and pulling kids out of rip currents. Fifteen years of being the town's best story about itself. You didn't outrun a thing like that. You buried it deeper every year, and you let the town do the shoveling, because the town would always dig faster and deeper than you ever could.

But the letters had changed the arithmetic. Ada's letters. He'd never known about the letters. Ada Quill, with her tide charts and her shanties and her soft voice, had been building a case against him in a desk drawer while she taught him knots.

He zipped the bag. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; that was the thing nobody understood about him, the thing Mateo had never understood even when he'd begged Ruben to take the blame for the juvenile record, even when Ruben had said *sure, hermano* and worn the stain like a favor. Steady hands weren't calm. Steady hands were just hands that had decided.

The festival was tomorrow. The lantern release was at sunset. And Ruben Calloway, volunteer fire chief, pillar of Pelican Hollow, would be thirty miles past the county line by the time the first paper globe left the water.

He looked at the photograph's drawer one last time and did not open it.

* * *

Paloma found Marge on the bait shop porch at dusk, shelling fava beans into a bowl with a violence that suggested the beans had personally wronged her.

"You've heard," Paloma said.

"I heard a judge signed something, and I heard Ruben Calloway bought two hundred gallons of diesel yesterday, which is a lot of diesel for a man who told everyone he's staying for the festival." Marge's hands didn't stop. "Eleven years I sat on those letters, girl. Eleven years I told myself I was waiting for you to be strong enough, and the truth is I was just a coward with good stationery. I will not sit on this too."

"The warrant's for the site. Samples, records. It doesn't stop him leaving."

"Vasquez knows?"

"Vasquez thinks he has until morning."

Marge set down the bowl. In the last light her face was all planes and history. "Then listen to me, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to finish these beans like a woman with a clear conscience. That ceremony tomorrow—five hundred lanterns, the whole town on the seawall, the press crew from the county paper filming the release. It's the only room in Pelican Hollow that Ruben Calloway can't control and can't leave early, because leaving early is the one thing this town would never forgive its fire chief." She picked the bowl back up. "If you're going to say your sentence, you say it there. Before the first lantern goes up. Not after. After, he's smoke."

Paloma stood on the porch a long moment after Marge went inside, the harbor lights coming on one by one across the water like a sentence being spelled out.

She had rehearsed it for fifteen years in bathrooms and rental cars and the white noise of research vessels. She had whispered it to Ada's ruin. She had almost said it to Ruben's charming face over oysters and failed. But she had never once said it to five hundred people, and tomorrow the tide of the whole town would be in one place at one time, and Ruben would be standing in it, smiling, waiting for the dark that would carry him away.

She went up to her room above the shop and took out the notebook she'd kept since San Simeon and wrote the sentence at the top of a fresh page, in full, for the first time. Her hand shook on the last word and she let it.

Then she called Theo.

* * *

Theo was at his desk at nine at night, re-reading the warrant's inventory list, when the phone rang. The office was still a crime scene in miniature—drawer pulled out, papers fanned, the ghost of the ransacking two weeks ago lingering in the way he now locked everything twice.

"Paloma."

"Marge thinks he's leaving. Diesel, a bag packed, a text about the warrant. She has sources I don't want to know about."

Theo sat forward. "I can have the sheriff's office hold his boat at dawn. Impound it for the site search—he's the owner of record on the property, I can justify—"

"No." Her voice was quiet and absolutely level, the voice she used for counting. "Listen to me. You impound his boat at dawn, and what happens? His lawyer raises hell, the town hears *harbormaster seizes fire chief's vessel* by breakfast, and by noon half of Pelican Hollow has decided you're the villain and Ruben's the victim, and he sails out of here tomorrow anyway with a sympathy escort. Or he doesn't sail, and he's standing in the crowd at the ceremony with nothing to lose and everything to discredit." She paused. "I've been thinking about this the way I think about turtle nesting. You don't dig up the nest to protect it. You wait for the night they emerge, and you make sure every light on the beach is the right kind of light."

Theo laughed once, despite everything. "You're telling me to let him run."

"I'm telling you he's going to run either way. The only question is whether the whole town watches him go, or whether it's just you and me and a dock camera." She breathed. "Tomorrow, before the lantern release. I'm going to say it. All of it. And I need you to not save me. Whatever happens on that stage—whatever he does, whatever the crowd does—you let me finish the sentence. Can you do that?"

He looked at the warrant on his desk, the stamp still sharp, the cathedral of paper he'd spent eleven days building. Everything in him that was a harbormaster and an investigator and a man who was falling in love with her wanted to control the variables, to intercept at the docks, to keep her off that stage and out of the fire.

"Paloma—"

"Can you?"

The harbor lights moved on the water outside his window. Somewhere down the coast, a man was packing a boat in the dark, and five hundred lanterns waited in their crates, and a town's founding story had less than twenty-four hours left to live.

"Yes," Theo said. "I'll be at the seawall. And I'll have the sheriff's deputy with me—unofficially, off the schedule, just a man watching fireworks. When you finish, we move."

"Then we move," she said.

After she hung up, Theo sat for a while in the lamplight, then took the warrant and the file and put them in a waterproof bag, because tomorrow the whole case would be standing on open water, and in Pelican Hollow, everything important eventually got wet.

* * *

Paloma didn't sleep. She walked the seawall at three in the morning, when the festival crews had gone home and the lantern crates sat in rows under tarps, breathing their paper-and-bamboo smell into the fog. At the end of the pier, the *Calloway Girl* rode at her slip, fueled, provisioned, her running lights dark, patient as a held breath.

Paloma stood at the rail and looked at the boat and felt, strangely, no fear—only the enormous, tidal patience of a thing that had waited fifteen years and would not have to wait past sunset.

She said the sentence once, out loud, to the empty water, to Ada, to the dark.

Then she went to wait for the morning.
