## 6. The Woman Who Runs the Tide

The tide chart on the wall of the festival office was wrong, and Marisol Reyes had been staring at it for four minutes without fixing it because the alternative was the budget spreadsheet, which was worse.

"Rosa," she called through the open door, not looking up. "If the king tide comes in at seven-forty like this says, we lose the north end of the boardwalk by eight. The lantern line has to move to the pilings."

"It's been wrong since March," Rosa said, appearing in the doorway with a coffee can full of nails and the unbothered expression of a woman who had been pulling crab pots out of that water for forty years. "You've been meaning to fix it since March."

"I've been meaning to fix it since March." Marisol took the red pen from behind her ear—the pen lived there the way other women wore engagement rings—and drew a clean line through the erroneous column. "There. Fixed. Tell Dev his stage can't go where he wants it."

"He's going to argue."

"Dev always argues. He argues, then he does what I say, then he takes credit for my idea at the volunteer dinner. It's our liturgy."

Rosa laughed, a sound like gravel poured into a bucket, and went back out into the salt-bright morning. Through the office window—formerly a bait shop window, formerly a cannery foreman's window, glass so old it went faintly green at the edges—Marisol could see the harbor doing what it did every September: shedding its quiet skin. Boats coming in heavy with the last of the season. The fish market stringing lights early. Kids already testing the strength of the barricades along the pier with the scientific method of ten-year-olds everywhere, which was to lean on them until something broke.

Eleven years she had been here. Eleven years since she'd stepped off a bus with two suitcases, a fake smile for the driver, and a bank envelope containing everything her father had been able to scrape together before the licenses were pulled. She'd meant to stay a week. Maybe a month. Long enough for the heat to die down in the valley, long enough for whatever Kestrel Kavanagh's father had set in motion to settle into the past where it belonged.

She'd stayed because Rosa had found her crying on the seawall the third night and hadn't asked why. Because old Tomas had given her a job counting ticket stubs and paid her in cash and fish stew and never once made her feel like charity. Because Dev had shown up with a broken PA system and a grin and said, *you look like someone who knows how to run things,* and she had been so starved for someone to believe that about her that she'd said yes before she knew what she was agreeing to.

Now the harbor festival was hers—not on paper, not officially, but in the way that mattered. When the lanterns went up, people looked for her. When the tide came in wrong, they came to her window. When Corliss's men had come around last spring with their glossy brochures about *revitalization* and *waterfront opportunity*, it was Marisol they'd asked to sign first, because everyone knew if Marisol Reyes signed, the town would follow.

She hadn't signed.

She picked up the budget spreadsheet instead, because cowardice was easier to face in columns.

* * *

By noon the office smelled like coffee and sunscreen and the diesel-and-brine smell that got into everything from June to October. The volunteers drifted through in shifts: Priya with the vendor contracts, arguing cheerfully with no one; Marcus and his crew of teenagers hauling folding tables that weighed more than they did; Dev, who had indeed argued about the stage and had indeed moved it, though he would deny both facts until his dying day.

Marisol stood at the big table with the harbor map spread across it and moved paper boats around like a general who had learned humility from the sea.

"Lantern line here," she said, tapping the pilings. "Food row back from the waterline—if the tide chart is right, and it wasn't, but now it is—we keep six feet of margin. Wine vendors along the cannery side, under cover, because if it rains I am not listening to Priya complain about soggy labels for a year."

"We have wine vendors?" Priya looked up from her stack of contracts. "Since when? You told me in July we weren't doing wine this year because the permit—"

"The permit came through Tuesday." Marisol kept her eyes on the map. "Tomas worked it out with the council. We needed one more anchor vendor for the west row or the whole layout collapses, and the council wouldn't budge on the noise variance unless we filled it."

"So who—" Priya flipped through her folder, frowning. "I don't have a contract for—"

"It's being handled."

It was not entirely being handled. It was being handled in the sense that Tomas had made a phone call, and the call had resulted in a name, and the name had been sitting in Marisol's inbox for three days like a stone she kept deciding not to pick up.

*Kestrel Kavanagh. Kavanagh Vineyards. Family operation, small lot, decent press a few years back. Good faith gesture, mija. The town needs the revenue.*

She had read the email eleven times, which felt thematically excessive even to her. Then she had typed three replies and deleted all of them, and finally written back: *Fine. Business only. They stay on their side of the row.*

Business only. Two words that cost her nothing to type and everything to mean.

Because here was the thing Marisol had learned about armor, the thing nobody told you when you were twenty-two and building your first set: it works. That was the terrible secret. It actually works. You put it on piece by piece—the competent voice, the clipboard, the title everyone respects, the laugh you deploy at exactly the right moments—and one day you realize you haven't taken it off in years, and you also realize you don't remember how, and worse, you're not sure there's anything underneath it anymore except the girl on the bus with the two suitcases, and she was tired, and she cried too easily, and she loved someone so much it had been used as a weapon against her entire family.

So you leave the armor on. You run the tide. You become the woman the town leans on, because leaning-on is a form of love that doesn't require you to be seen.

"You okay?" Dev asked, materializing beside her with the particular silence of a large man who has spent decades around sound equipment and knows how to move quietly. "You've redrawn the lantern line four times."

"The tide chart was wrong."

"The tide chart has been wrong since March."

"Then I'm making up for lost time."

He studied her with those patient eyes, and she gave him the smile—the real one, the one that was somehow also a closed door—and he let it go, because that was the covenant of their friendship: he noticed everything and said almost none of it.

At one-fifteen, Rosa stuck her head in. "Council rep's here early. Wants to walk the grounds before the two o'clock meeting."

"Tell him I'll meet him at the pier gate."

"And Marisol." Rosa paused. Her weathered face did something complicated. "There's a car parked down by the fish market. Been there an hour. Rental plates."

Marisol didn't look up from the map. "Tourists get lost. It happens."

"Rental cars don't sit an hour facing the harbor with nobody in them," Rosa said. "And the man walking up your pier right now doesn't walk like a tourist. Tourists stroll. This one walks like he's going somewhere he doesn't want to be."

Marisol's pen stopped moving.

She told herself, later, that she had known. That some part of her—the part that still kept a shoebox of unsent letters at the bottom of a closet, the part that flinched every time a car from the valley came over the headland road—had known the moment Rosa opened her mouth. But in the moment itself there was only the ordinary machinery of the day, and she finished drawing the lantern line, capped the pen, put it behind her ear, and walked out into the light.

* * *

The pier at midday was all glare and gull-noise, planks bleached silver, the water below holding that deep green-gold color it only got in September. She saw him from thirty yards and her body knew before her mind did—a cold drop, like stepping off a stair that wasn't there.

Kestrel Kavanagh.

Eleven years had done things. Had broadened the shoulders, grayed the temples early, carved lines around the mouth that hadn't been earned by laughing. The expensive coat was new but the way it hung was old—slightly wrong, slightly rumpled, the way Kestrel had always worn good clothes, as if apologizing for them. Standing at the rail looking out at the water like a person trying to gather courage from the horizon.

For one unguarded second—one—Marisol felt something other than fury. Something that looked, from a distance, like grief. The boy who used to wait for her behind the packing sheds. Who had kissed her for the first time with flour on his hands from helping his mother bake. Who had sat with her on the seawall and said, *when I take over the vineyard, I'm going to do it differently than my father,* and she had believed him, God help her, she had believed him the way you believe in weather, completely and without evidence.

Then the second passed, and the ledger opened, and eleven years of arithmetic came due.

Her father's licenses revoked on a technicality so thin it was practically theoretical. The fine that ate the boat fund. The letter from the Kavanagh family lawyer that her father had hidden in the tackle box and that she'd found only after he'd had his first heart attack, his hands shaking as he tried to explain, ashamed in a way she had never seen him ashamed of anything the sea ever did to him. *They said if you didn't go, mija. If you didn't go and stay gone, they'd bury us. Your father Kavanagh—he does not shout, this one. He just closes doors.*

And Kestrel had never come. Never written. Never once, in eleven years, climbed into a car and driven ninety minutes to ask a single question. Had graduated, had taken over the vineyard eventually, had lived a whole visible life—she'd checked, in the early years, hatefully, hopelessly, checking—and not one word.

She crossed the remaining distance with her spine straight and her face arranged into the polite mask she wore for difficult vendors and councilmen with agendas.

"Can I help you?"

Kestrel turned.

Up close the years were kinder and crueler at once. The eyes were the same dark, watchful brown, and they went through an entire catastrophe in the space of a breath—recognition, disbelief, something raw and hopeful that Marisol wanted to strike off the face of the earth with her open hand.

"Marisol." Her name in that voice. Eleven years and it still fit in her chest like a splinter.

"Festival grounds are closed to vendors until setup week," she said. "If you're here about the wine contract, paperwork goes through Priya Okafor, the office is—" she pointed, brisk, professional, a woman pointing a stranger toward a door—"that way. I'm on a schedule."

"Marisol, please. I need to talk to you."

"No, you don't." The words came out level, almost gentle, which frightened her a little because she could hear how much pressure it took to keep them level. "You need to turn around, get back in your rental car—it's the gray one by the fish market, in case you forgot where you left it—and drive home. Whatever this is, whatever you think you're owed, you waited eleven years to come collect it, and I don't have anything left to give this town's creditors."

Something moved across Kestrel's face—confusion first, genuine confusion, and then a dawning horror that didn't match the script Marisol had rehearsed in her head for a decade. In the rehearsal, Kestrel was smug. Guilty and comfortable in it. In the rehearsal, she could hate cleanly.

"I don't understand," Kestrel said. "I—there's a letter. There's something I have to tell you, something about that summer, about why you—"

"Don't." One word, and it cracked down the middle despite everything. Heads were turning along the pier now; Rosa had appeared at the far end with her arms folded; a knot of Marcus's teenagers had gone conveniently, curiously still. "Don't stand on my pier, in my town, in front of my people, and talk to me about that summer. You don't get to say that word. You spent it. You spent it and every one since."

"I never—" Kestrel took a step forward and Marisol stepped back, and the motion between them was so old, so practiced, that it hurt worse than shouting. "Marisol, I got a letter yesterday. Postmarked eleven years ago. From you. It was in the wall of the farmhouse, and it says you wanted to meet me the night before you left, and I never got it, I never knew, and I have spent one day knowing that and I already can't breathe—"

The world tilted.

Marisol heard the words and understood each of them individually and could not make them assemble into anything. *A letter. In the wall. From me.* Her own handwriting, the page she'd written at the kitchen table at two in the morning while her father slept, the page begging Kestrel to meet her at the platform before the last train, the page she'd slipped under the farmhouse door at dawn with her heart hammering, the page that had gotten no answer, no answer, no answer—

"That's not possible," she said.

"It's in my pocket."

"Don't." Her hand came up. "Don't you dare take that out here. Not here."

"Then where? Marisol, please, I have been driving since dawn and I have read that letter maybe fifty times and every version of the last eleven years I believed is wrong, and I think—I think my father—"

"My father had a heart attack," Marisol said, and her voice came out low and shaking and absolutely clear, carrying further than she intended, because the pier had gone quiet in that way crowds go quiet when they smell blood in the water. "Did yours mention that? Did the great Aldous Kavanagh ever mention what happened to the Reyes family the month after I left? Because I have spent eleven years believing you knew. Believing you stood behind that door and listened to him threaten us and chose the vineyard. Chose the money. Chose to let me disappear rather than make one phone call."

"I didn't know anything," Kestrel said, and there was such naked anguish in it that for one dangerous moment Marisol's armor—her beautiful, load-bearing armor—groaned on its rivets. "Marisol, I thought *you* left *me*. I built my whole life on that. And now there's a letter in my pocket that says otherwise and I don't know what's true anymore except that I have to hear it from you."

"Then you should have come eleven years ago."

She said it and immediately wished she could unsay it, because it was true and it was cruel and it was the wound talking, and Kestrel flinched like she'd been struck, and some distant part of Marisol noted that the crowd had grown, that Rosa was moving closer, that Dev had appeared at the top of the ramp with his arms crossed and his weight forward.

"This isn't over," Kestrel said quietly. "I'm not leaving without answers. I'll be at the cannery steps at six. If you don't come, I'll find you tomorrow, and the day after. I waited eleven years without knowing there was anything to wait for. Now I know."

And Marisol, who ran the tide, who fixed the charts, who held a whole town together with a red pen and sheer will, stood on her own pier with her heart going like a hammer on hull plating and could not produce a single word.

Kestrel nodded once—something old and formal in it, something from the kids they'd been—and walked past her down the planks, close enough that Marisol caught the smell of dust and gasoline and, beneath it, faintly, impossibly, the same soap from the farmhouse summers, and was gone.

Rosa reached her first. "Mija. What was that?"

Marisol looked out at the green-gold water, at the lantern poles waiting in their stacks, at the harbor that had saved her life and now, apparently, intended to finish dismantling it.

"Get Priya," she said. "And cancel my evening."

She had six hours to decide whether to hear him out—or to burn the bridge before it burned her.
