## 7. Blight

The first sign was the smell.

Sebastian noticed it on a Tuesday morning in late July, before he'd even reached the orchard gate—a sweetness in the air that was wrong somehow, like fruit rotting on the branch instead of ripening toward it. He told himself it was nothing. He had gotten good at telling himself things were nothing: the bank's third letter was nothing, the tractor that wouldn't start was nothing, the way Maren still said *Mr. Dravenmoor* when she was angry and *Sebastian* only when she forgot to be—none of it was anything he couldn't carry.

Then he walked the east rows and saw the leaves.

They were curling inward, browning at the edges as though someone had held a match beneath them. The apples beneath those leaves had soft, sunken patches, dark as bruises on pale skin. He picked one, turned it over in his hand, and felt his stomach drop through the soles of his boots.

By noon, Maren had identified it without hesitation. She crouched at the base of a Gravenstein, snapped off a twig, and split it with her thumbnail. The wood underneath was streaked rust-brown.

"Fire blight," she said. Her voice was flat, clinical, which he'd learned meant she was frightened. "It's been here a while. Weeks, maybe. We just didn't know what we were looking at."

"How bad?"

She stood, wiped her hands on her jeans, and looked down the row with the particular expression of someone counting casualties. "Bad. It moves fast when it's warm and we've had nothing but warm. If it's in half these trees—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "We cut it out. That's all there is. You prune past the infection, sterilize the shears between every cut, burn everything you take off. It's ugly work."

"Will it save them?"

"It might save some." She met his eyes then, and he saw she wasn't going to lie to him, even now, even about this. "It might not be enough."

* * *

The ugly work took four days.

They hired two of the harvest hands early—old Tomas Reyes and his nephew, who worked for less than they were worth because Tomas had known Maren's mother and seemed to regard the orchard's survival as a family obligation. The three of them moved down the rows like a triage unit, and by the second day Sebastian stopped flinching at the sound of the saw.

There was something almost meditative about it, cutting away the sick wood. Every tree they saved felt like an argument won against the season itself. Maren worked beside him without speaking most of the time, but her silences had changed over the weeks—they weren't walls anymore so much as rooms he was slowly being allowed into. On the third afternoon she handed him the tar pot without being asked, their fingers brushing, and neither of them pulled back quickly enough to pretend it hadn't happened.

On the fourth evening, they stood at the edge of the burn pile watching six weeks of diseased wood go up in smoke. Ash drifted over the rows like gray snow.

"Half," Maren said quietly. "Maybe more than half the east block is gone. We'll get a crop off the west side if the rest holds, but it won't be the yield the bank wants to see."

Sebastian watched the fire and did the arithmetic he'd been doing since the day he found her name on the deed. Crop revenue against loan payments against the interest accruing like water rising in a cellar. He'd been raised on this arithmetic; his father had taught it to him at this same kitchen table, with the same patient condescension. *Numbers don't care how you feel about them, boy.*

He didn't say any of it aloud. But Maren looked at him sideways, ash in her hair, and he had the uncomfortable sense that she'd done the same math and arrived at the same figure.

* * *

Corvane came on Friday.

Sebastian found him standing at the gate in a linen shirt too clean for Hollowmere, flanked by a younger man carrying a leather portfolio, looking at the orchard the way a man looks at a horse he intends to buy at a distressed price.

"Dravenmoor." Corvane extended a hand. His grip was dry and brief. "I heard about your blight problem. Terrible luck. These old blocks are fragile—one bad season and the whole thing comes apart. I thought I'd come by while the situation was fresh."

"The situation is being handled."

"I'm sure it is." Corvane smiled with excellent teeth. "Handled costs money, though, doesn't it? Fungicide, replacement stock, labor. And your bank isn't famous for its patience." He nodded toward the west rows, where Maren was visible among the trees, moving with an ease that suggested she'd been born between them—which, in a sense, she had. "I understand you have a partner now. Ms. Gideon. Interesting history there."

"We're not discussing her."

"No, of course not." Corvane opened the portfolio himself, unhurried, and produced a single sheet. "Just a courtesy call. A letter of intent. My number has gone up since we last spoke—considerably. Cash at close, debt cleared, both of you walk away whole. Frankly, Dravenmoor, given what's happening to those trees, this may be the last time anyone offers you whole."

Sebastian took the page and read the number twice.

It was, objectively, generous. It was generous enough to clear the loans, pay off the creditors whose letters filled the drawer in his study, and leave each of them with money in hand—the first real money either of them had seen in years, if Maren's worn coat and mended gloves were any indication. It was generous enough that refusing it would look, to any reasonable observer, like pride or madness.

"Take your time," Corvane said pleasantly. "Though not too much. Blight doesn't negotiate."

* * *

That night Sebastian sat at the kitchen table with the letter of intent under the lamp, and he was honest with himself, because no one else was going to do it for him.

He wanted to say yes.

Not because he wanted to sell—not anymore, and the realization of *not anymore* startled him more than the number had. But because saying yes would end the slow bleed. Because every morning he woke to the possibility that today was the day the bank called the note, and he was tired of carrying that weight alone, tired of hiding the worst of the ledger from Maren, tired of lying by omission to the one person who had more right than anyone to hate him for it.

He could give her the money and be done. She could go wherever people went when they finally stopped running. He could stop pretending he knew how to save an orchard, stop pretending the ghost of his father didn't sit at this table with him every night.

The house was quiet. Somewhere upstairs, the pipes ticked as they cooled. He poured a finger of the cooking sherry because it was all they had, grimaced, and read the number a third time, as if repetition might make the temptation smaller.

It didn't.

* * *

In the morning, he found Maren in the kitchen, and on impulse—or cowardice, he couldn't tell which—he set the letter in front of her.

"Corvane came yesterday. This is his offer."

She was buttering toast. She finished buttering it first, which he was learning was very Maren: finish the task in front of you, then face the crisis. She set down the knife, wiped her fingers on a dish towel, and picked up the page.

He watched her face.

He had spent eleven years telling himself he remembered her face from the trial—the set of her jaw on the witness stand, the way she'd looked at his father without blinking while she destroyed him. He'd built a whole architecture of grievance around that memory. But he had forgotten, apparently, how legible she was when she thought no one was reading. He watched the number land. Watched her eyes flick to the total, then back, checking it. Watched something move behind them—not greed, not relief, but a quick, involuntary calculation of what that money would mean for a woman who owned one suitcase and a mother's pruning knife.

And then her gaze dropped to the middle of the page, to the legal language, and everything in her face went still.

"What is it?" he asked.

She read it again, slower. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way it got when she was handling something sharp. "Paragraph nine. 'Buyer acknowledges no representations regarding the condition of the property, including but not limited to agricultural viability.'" She looked up. "He knew."

"He guessed. Everyone can see the blight."

"No." She laid the letter flat on the table and tapped the paragraph once, precisely. "This isn't boilerplate, Sebastian. Boilerplate covers *everything*. This clause names agricultural viability specifically. He wrote this knowing exactly what condition the trees are in, and he offered full price anyway." Her eyes narrowed. "Nobody pays full price for a dying orchard unless they're not buying the orchard."

"The land," Sebastian said slowly. "The parcel borders the county road. Water rights."

"Water rights," Maren agreed. "And zoning that changes the moment a developer owns it. He's not offering us a lifeline. He's offering us a buyout and calling it mercy." She pushed the letter back across the table, and her hand, he noticed, was not quite steady. "My mother worked those trees for twenty-two years. They paid her in wages and a pension they tried to take away. And this man wants to write *no representations regarding agricultural viability* over her grave and hand us a check."

She stopped. Looked down at the table. When she continued, her voice had dropped, gone rougher. "I came back here to sign my share away. First day. I had the pen in my bag. And I keep—I keep finding reasons not to." She laughed, short and humorless. "I told myself it was stubbornness. Now I think it's the only honest thing I've done in eleven years."

Sebastian looked at the letter—at the number that had kept him awake half the night, the number that solved everything—and felt it shrink in his hands until it was just paper with a stranger's arithmetic on it.

He tore it in half. Then in quarters, mostly because Maren's expression made him want to, and partly because it felt good.

"You could have just thrown it out," she said.

"Wastebasket's full of better intentions than mine." He swept the pieces onto a saucer. "For the record: I was tempted. Last night, before you saw it, I sat here wanting to say yes. I want you to know that, because you're going to find out anyway—you always do—and I'd rather you hear it from me."

Maren studied him for a long moment. Something shifted in her face—not forgiveness, not yet, but a kind of recalibration, the way she sometimes looked at a tree before deciding where to cut.

"I know," she said simply. "You've been tempted since the day I got here. So have I. Temptation's not the sin, Sebastian. It's what you do at the gate."

She picked up her toast, took a bite, and pointed the remainder at him with the air of a woman resuming normal operations. "Now. If we're staying, we're staying properly. Call your banker friend and ask for a meeting after the west block comes in—we need ten days, no more. And find out what Corvane knows about our water rights, because a man doesn't write a clause like that without homework, and I want to know who did his homework for him."

Something cold slid down Sebastian's spine, unrelated to the sherry.

Because there was only one person outside the two of them who knew the true state of the ledgers, the terms of the loan, the exact depth of Hollowmere's distress. One person who had been in the study, unhurried, the week before—with a key of his own and thirty years of the family's trust.

"Actually," Sebastian said, reaching for his coat, "I think I know exactly where to start asking."

Behind him, Maren set down her toast. "Sebastian. What did you just realize?"

He paused at the door, the morning light sharp on the frost-killed east rows beyond the window, and heard himself say the words that would change everything:

"I think someone inside this house has been selling us by the pound."
