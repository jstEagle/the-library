## 5. A Reasonable Arrangement

Rosalind rehearsed the proposal three times on the walk to the archive, and all three versions sounded like a hostage statement.

*It's a professional arrangement. Bounded. Transactional. Nothing you'd have to explain to anyone.*

The problem was that Maren Okafor had a way of looking at him—archival, he'd started calling it in his head—the way she looked at water-damaged letters, like she could read what was written between the lines and under them, in the pressure of the pen.

She was at the reference desk when he came in, reshelving a box of 1970s municipal correspondence with the tenderness other people reserved for infants. She glanced up.

"Back again," she said. "Third time this month. At some point I'm going to have to start charging you rent on that chair."

"I'm thorough."

"You're avoiding something." She said it lightly, sliding the last folder home, but her eyes stayed on him a beat too long. "Publishing records from 2004 don't require three visits. I checked your request slips. You already found what you were looking for the first time."

He had, more or less. He'd also come back twice for no reason he was prepared to name, and both times she'd pulled up a second chair without being asked, and they'd talked about nothing—paper conservation, the ethics of digitization, whether anyone actually read the acknowledgments page—and both times he'd left feeling lighter than he had any right to feel.

"Maren," he said. "I have a proposition."

Her eyebrows went up. "That sounds ominous."

"It's not. It's—" He stopped. Started over. "My family has a reunion in six weeks. My cousin's wedding is attached to it. And my grandmother has reached an age where she considers my unmarried state a personal failure of the entire Galloway line, and my sister has threatened to set me up with someone if I arrive alone. Someone awful. Her words. She has a candidate picked out. He does CrossFit and calls wine 'juice.'"

Maren pressed her lips together. He could see the laugh fighting its way out.

"So you need a date," she said.

"A fake one. A—a decoy. Someone charming enough to satisfy my grandmother and unmemorable enough that no one asks follow-up questions next Christmas."

"Unmemorable." She put a hand flat on her chest. "You know how to flatter a woman."

"That's not—I didn't mean—"

"Rosalind." She was smiling now, fully, and it did something unfair to his ability to construct sentences. "I'm teasing. Mostly. Why me?"

Because you're warm and perceptive and my grandmother will adore you and I've been thinking about you for two weeks in a way that is frankly inconvenient. Because every conversation with you feels like the first easy thing I've had in twenty years.

"Because you're good with people," he said. "And because it's a transaction. Clean terms. One weekend, expenses paid, and afterward we go back to being—whatever we are now."

"Which is?"

"Two people who share a reference desk occasionally."

"Right." Something moved through her expression, quick as a fish under ice, gone before he could identify it. She turned back to her cart of folders. "Let me think about it."

* * *

Maren thought about it for exactly four hours, which was four hours longer than she wanted to admit.

The honest answer—the answer she kept folding up and putting in a drawer—was that she'd been waiting for him to ask since the second visit, when he'd spent ten minutes defending the literary value of acknowledgments pages with such earnest absurdity that she'd caught herself laughing in a way she hadn't laughed in front of anyone in months.

The practical answer was Devon's engagement party.

Devon. Three years of being the reasonable one, the low-maintenance one, the woman who never made demands and therefore never got considered. Two years since he'd ended it with the sentence *I just think we want different things*, which she had later realized meant *you let me stop trying*. And now an engagement party—his engagement, to a woman named Priya who did pottery and had probably never once been described as convenient—an invitation that said *and guest* in that cheerful font that felt like a dare.

She was not going to that party alone. She was not going to that party alone, and she was not going to stand by the shrimp table while Devon gave her the pitying look he'd perfected, the one that said *see, aren't you doing great though*.

So when Rosalind appeared at the archive closing time the next day, hovering by the door with the posture of a man awaiting sentencing, she already knew her counteroffer.

"Coffee," she said, before he could speak. "There's a place around the corner that's terrible. We should negotiate there. Neutral ground."

* * *

The coffee was, as promised, terrible—over-extracted, served in cups that had survived some earlier civilization—but the corner table by the window was theirs within minutes, rain starting against the glass like static.

"Terms," Maren said, producing an actual notebook, because if they were going to do this she was going to do it properly, and because writing things down was the only way she knew to keep feelings out of negotiations where they didn't belong. "One: this is strictly platonic. Hand-holding permitted for audience purposes only. No kissing."

"No kissing," Rosalind repeated. His voice was very even. She noted, in the same corner of her mind where she catalogued everything, that he looked briefly like a man reading a contract with a clause he hadn't expected.

"Two: we build our story together. Real details only. If my grandmother asks how we met, I want an answer we've both agreed on, not improvisation. Improvisation is how lies collapse."

"Agreed. Three?"

"I get to add a term." She clicked her pen. "My ex is having an engagement party in five weeks. I need a plus-one. Same arrangement. You charm, I survive, we're even."

Something flickered across his face—not reluctance, she was almost sure, though she'd learned to distrust her own readings of him. "Your ex," he said carefully. "That won't be strange? Watching someone else's engagement?"

"It'll be fine. It's fine." She wrote *Devon's party — Rosalind attends* in the notebook with slightly more force than necessary. "Besides, you owe me. You're asking me to face down your entire extended family. What's one room of Devon's friends?"

"Fair." He wrapped both hands around his cup like it was something warm, which it wasn't. "Four: expenses split. I don't want this to be—"

"—transactional?" She smiled. "Rosalind, it's entirely transactional. That's the whole point. That's what makes it safe."

He looked at her then, a long look, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense that he could see straight through the notebook, through the pen, through the word *safe* to whatever she was actually doing here, which was spending money she didn't have on bad coffee with a man she'd known three weeks so she wouldn't have to spend one evening being visible alone.

"Five," he said quietly. "If either of us wants out, we say so. No ghosting. No letting it curdle. We end it cleanly."

"Like professionals."

"Like professionals."

They shook on it. His hand was dry and warm and he held on maybe half a second past protocol, or she imagined he did, which amounted to the same thing in the ledger she wasn't keeping.

"Now," she said, pulling the notebook back. "Our story. How did we meet?"

"We met here," he said. "At the archive. That part's true, we keep true things wherever possible. Lies hold better when they're load-bearing on facts."

She blinked at him. "That's a surprisingly sophisticated philosophy of deception."

"I've done a lot of reading." Something crossed his face when he said it, some old weather, and she decided not to press. Not yet. "So: I came in researching publishing records. You helped me. We kept talking. Is that plausible?"

"It's true. The only question is the timeline. How long have we been together?"

They looked at each other.

"Three months," he said. "Long enough to be serious, short enough that nobody expects a ring. Your grandmother—"

"My grandmother will ask if you're *the one* before the appetizers arrive. She asked my cousin Marcus that about a woman he'd known for nine days."

"Then three months it is. First date?"

"You tell me. You asked me out, presumably. Where would Rosalind Galloway take a woman on a first date?"

He was quiet for a moment, turning his cup. "There's a bookshop on Halsey Street with a cat that hates everyone. There's a bench outside it. I'd take her there, and we'd stay until closing, arguing about novels, and the cat would watch us through the window like a tiny disapproving landlord."

Maren wrote it down. Her pen slowed halfway through, because the detail was too specific, too lived-in—he'd clearly had that date, or a version of it, with someone, sometime, or he'd imagined it so many times it had worn grooves—and she felt the small cold weight of the thing she was already refusing to examine: that she wanted to be the woman on that bench, and that wanting it under cover of a contract was either the smartest thing she'd ever done or the most cowardly.

"What?" Rosalind said.

"Nothing. Good detail. Keep going. Who said 'I love you' first?"

"Nobody yet. Three months is ambitious but we're not reckless."

"Wrong. My grandmother will ask. We need an answer."

He sighed. "Fine. I said it first. On the fire escape of your building, during a rainstorm, after a book event, because I couldn't help myself."

"That's oddly specific."

"It's a good story. Stories need weather." He said it lightly, but his ears had gone faintly pink, and Maren looked down at her notebook and wrote *fire escape, rain* in her careful archivist's hand, and told herself the warmth climbing her own neck was the coffee, which was, again, terrible.

* * *

They rehearsed for two hours. By the end of it they had a shared history with the structural integrity of a well-built lie: the archive meeting, the bookshop cat, the fire escape confession, a running joke about her hatred of cilantro that Maren invented on the spot and Rosalind adopted so quickly it startled her, as if he'd been waiting for material.

"You're good at this," she said, capping her pen. "Alarmingly good."

"I'm a writer. Lying adjacent." He said it with a smile that didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, and then, as if hearing it himself, he added, "Ghostwriting, mostly. Other people's stories. I'm—good at disappearing into them."

"Doesn't that bother you? Writing all those books and nobody knowing they're yours?"

The rain thickened against the window. For a moment she thought he wouldn't answer.

"It used to," he said finally. "Now it's just—quiet. Quiet is underrated."

Maren looked at him across the terrible coffee and thought: *that is the saddest sentence I have ever heard a person say cheerfully.* She thought: *there is a whole locked room in this man and I am holding a fake key to a fake door and pretending that's enough.*

"Well," she said aloud, snapping the notebook shut. "We're ready. Grandmothers, exes, wedding guests—none of them stand a chance."

"None at all," Rosalind agreed.

They gathered their coats. At the door, under the awning, the rain came down in ropes, and they stood shoulder to shoulder in the small dry space, neither one moving to leave, the negotiated distance between them suddenly the only thing in the world with any weight.

"Six weeks," he said. "I'll email you the reunion itinerary. My sister made a spreadsheet. There's a color-coded seating chart."

"A spreadsheet. Rosalind. Your family sounds terrifying."

"They are. But my grandmother makes a lemon cake that justifies the entire enterprise."

"Then I suppose I'll endure it." She hesitated, then held out her hand, formal, contractual. "Pleasure doing business with you, Mr. Galloway."

"Pleasure's all mine, Ms. Okafor."

But neither of them let go right away, and the rain kept falling, and they stood there in the doorway of a failing café, holding hands under cover of a handshake, each privately certain the other felt nothing—which was, of course, the largest lie in the notebook, and the only one neither of them had written down.

Two days later, Rosalind's agent called with news that turned his blood to ice water: Julian Voss had accepted the memoir offer personally. Requested him by name.

"He asked for you specifically," she said, delighted. "Isn't that something? Says he's heard you're the best in the business."

Rosalind stood in his kitchen, phone against his ear, watching the rain that had followed him home streak the windows, and understood that the universe had just handed him a bill for every year of silence.

"Yeah," he said. "Something like that."
