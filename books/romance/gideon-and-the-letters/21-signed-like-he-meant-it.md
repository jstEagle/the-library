## 21. Signed Like He Meant It

**Rosalind**

The envelope had been sitting on my desk for three days, and I still hadn't sealed it.

Inside was a single sheet—my new first chapter, printed on good paper, the kind I used to buy when I was twenty-three and foolish enough to believe paper mattered. At the bottom, in ink that hadn't smudged, I'd written: *For Edith Galloway, who kept every letter. — Rosalind Nathaniel Galloway.*

My full name. All of it. The middle name I'd abandoned at nineteen because it sounded like someone's accountant uncle. The surname I'd spent two decades signing away as *for* other people, never *as* myself.

I picked up the envelope, put it down, picked it up again.

"You're going to be late," Maren said from the doorway. She was dressed for the party in a green dress she'd bought weeks ago, back when we were still pretending this was pretend. "Your grandmother is turning ninety. She has waited longer than anyone alive."

"What if she hates it?"

"She won't hate it." She crossed the room and took the envelope out of my hands, sealing it with one efficient motion. "She might cry. You might cry. But nobody will hate it."

"What if it's too much? Bringing my own chapter to her birthday like some kind of—"

"Like some kind of gift?" She handed it back. "Rosalind. She told me you used to sign your name like you meant it. Do you know what that sentence did to me? I've thought about it every day since. Give her the proof."

I looked at the envelope. My name on the front, written by my own hand, addressed to a woman who had watched me disappear in slow motion and never once said so out loud.

"All right," I said.

* * *

Edith Galloway held court in the sunroom of my cousin Denise's house, ninety years old and sharp as a letter opener, wearing a lavender cardigan and an expression that suggested she had already heard everything anyone would say today and found most of it wanting.

"There he is," she announced when we came through the door. "And there she is. Sit down before someone asks you about real estate."

We sat. The party flowed around us—cousins, their children, the particular chaos of a family that loved each other mostly through teasing. Maren fit into it the way water fits into a glass. She'd learned everyone's names after one Christmas gathering; she remembered that Denise's son was applying to conservatory, that Aunt Priya's hip was better, that the twins hated being called the twins.

"Grandma," I said, when there was a lull. "I brought you something."

"If it's another scarf—"

"It's not a scarf."

She took the envelope. Opened it with the deliberation of a woman who believed unwrapping things quickly was a form of disrespect. Read the first line. Then the second.

The sunroom noise receded. Or maybe I just stopped hearing it.

She read the whole chapter—four pages, the opening of the memoir I'd submitted to Marsh & Lyle six weeks ago, the one about a boy who wrote a novel and lost it and spent twenty years learning the difference between surviving and living. Her lips moved slightly. Her thumb traced the margin the way she used to trace the margins of letters my grandfather sent from the war.

When she finished, she didn't look up right away.

"You signed it," she said finally.

"I signed it."

"With your whole name."

"Every part of it."

She set the pages down on her lap and pressed both hands flat over them, as if keeping them from blowing away. Her eyes were wet, but her voice was dry as ever.

"Nathaniel," she said. "I always liked Nathaniel. Your grandfather's name. Everyone said it was too old-fashioned for a baby born in 1981, and I said, he'll grow into it." She looked at me. "Took you forty-odd years. Fine. Some things ripen late."

Maren made a small sound beside me—the sound of someone swallowing a laugh and a sob in the same throat.

"And you," Edith said, turning to Maren with terrifying directness. "Are you going to keep him? Because I'm not getting any younger, and I'd like to see this settled while I can still enjoy it."

"Grandma—"

"Hush. I'm asking her."

Maren didn't flinch. That was the thing about Maren; she never flinched. She leaned forward instead, elbows on her knees, meeting my grandmother's gaze with the same steadiness she'd once used to confront me in the archive stacks.

"He hasn't asked me anything yet," Maren said. "But I'm not going anywhere."

Edith looked between us for a long moment. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and tucked the chapter into her handbag with the care of a woman filing a legal document.

"Well," she said. "Then somebody get me cake, and you two stop looking so serious. It's a birthday, not a funeral."

* * *

**Maren**

He found me later, on the back porch, where I'd escaped to watch the light go gold over Denise's garden. Inside, someone had started music. Someone else was arguing about wine.

"So," Rosalind said, leaning on the railing next to me. "My grandmother just put your declaration in her handbag. For safekeeping, presumably. Evidence."

"She seems like a woman who keeps evidence."

"She keeps everything. Letters, receipts, grudges, love." He was quiet a moment. "You said 'he hasn't asked me anything yet.' In front of her. In front of everyone."

"I did."

"That was—" He stopped. Started again. "That was brave. Or reckless. With us, I've stopped being able to tell the difference."

"Neither can I," I admitted. "I've been trying to figure out which one I was being since about week three."

The gold light caught his face. He looked tired in a way I recognized—not the tiredness of deadlines but of a man who had been carrying something heavy for a long time and had recently, finally, set it down, and didn't yet know what to do with his empty hands.

"Maren." He turned to face me fully. "Can we renegotiate?"

A laugh came out of me, unexpected. "Renegotiate. Of course. You'd frame it contractually."

"It's the only language I trusted for twenty years." His mouth curved, but his eyes stayed serious. "So. Terms. I propose we delete all of them."

"All of them?"

"The plus-one clause. The engagement-party reciprocity. The no-real-feelings provision that we both violated so thoroughly and so early that it's embarrassing to look at." He took a breath. "One term remains. Honesty. That's it. That's the whole document. We tell each other true things, even when they're ugly, even when they're scary, especially then. Everything else we make up as we go."

I looked at him—at this man who had spent two decades making himself invisible, who had ghosted himself out of his own life, standing in his cousin's garden asking me for a relationship with a single clause.

"You know what happens to contracts with only one term?" I said.

"They're simpler to keep."

"They're impossible to break quietly. One breach and the whole thing collapses."

"Yes," he said. "That's rather the point. I don't want anywhere to hide anymore."

The music inside shifted to something slow. Through the window I could see Edith holding court, the chapter in her handbag, her hand resting on it like a hand on a shoulder.

"Counteroffer," I said. "Same single term. But I want it in writing. Not because I doubt you—because I'm an archivist. I believe in documents. I believe in what survives."

His eyes went bright. "What survives," he repeated softly.

"I'll draft it tonight. One sentence. We'll both sign it. Full names."

"Full names," he agreed. "Signed like we mean it."

We stood there while the light went from gold to copper, and neither of us moved toward the door, and neither of us needed to say anything else, because the terms were already in effect, and the first true thing under them was the silence itself—chosen, shared, and ours.

Inside, Edith called out that the cake was being cut, and that if we missed it, she was giving our slices to the twins.

"We should go in," Rosalind said.

"In a minute," I said, and took his hand, and he let me, and that was its own signature—unwritten, witnessed only by the garden, binding anyway.

* * *

Later, much later, driving home with the windows down and the last of the birthday candles' smoke still somehow in our clothes, Rosalind's phone buzzed against the console. He glanced at it, and I watched his whole body change.

"What is it?" I asked.

He turned the screen toward me at a red light. An email. From Marsh & Lyle.

*Subject line: Re: Your memoir — we need to talk tomorrow. Something's happened with Voss.*

The light turned green. Neither of us moved.
