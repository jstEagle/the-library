## 14. What the Photograph Showed

The photograph, when I finally saw it, was innocent. That's the part that still gets me — the part I keep returning to like a tongue probing a broken tooth. It was innocent. A woman named Marjorie Hollis, third cousin twice removed and self-appointed historian of every Ravensworth-adjacent gathering since roughly the Mesozoic era, posted forty-one photos from the reunion to some family group page with a caption about *golden autumn light* and *so wonderful to have everyone together*.

Forty-one photos. I was in nine of them. In six of those nine, I was looking at Cyrus.

I didn't know that yet, of course. What I knew, on the Tuesday morning it all came apart, was that my phone had buzzed eleven times before eight a.m., which meant either the board had an emergency or someone had died. Neither, as it turned out. Something worse. Something small.

The first message was from my cousin Priya: *ok WHO is she and why did nobody tell me Theo has a girlfriend who makes pastry like a religious experience*

The second was from an aunt whose name I'd have to look up: *Marjorie posted the reunion album! You two look so happy, Theodore. Your grandmother is beside herself.*

Beside herself. Grandmother had been beside herself for weeks now, ever since the dinner where Cyrus described a tarte tatin the way other people describe first love, and I had sat there watching her hands move while she talked and thinking, with the calm certainty of a man walking into a glass door he can see coming, *oh no.*

The third message was from Vivienne.

Vivienne didn't send messages at 7:40 in the morning. Vivienne sent messages at 11:02 p.m. on weeknights, precisely worded, punctuated like a legal document. So when my screen lit up with her name before breakfast, I felt the particular cold that precedes weather you can't outrun.

*You should see what people are saying under Marjorie's post. Before your grandmother does.*

That was it. No explanation. Just that — a door held open onto a room she wanted me to walk into alone.

I told myself, scrolling through the album on the train into town, that it would be nothing. A stray comment. Someone asking if Cyrus catered. The photos themselves were fine — better than fine, they were damning in a way I hadn't let myself notice in real time, because in real time I'd been too busy being happy. There was one of us by the cider house, taken from across the yard without our knowledge, Cyrus mid-laugh with flour still somehow on her jaw even though she'd been dressed for dinner for hours, and me looking at her with an expression I recognized only from the outside. It was the expression of a man who had stopped acting and said nothing about it. Chapter seven of my own private disaster, captured at 200 dpi.

The comments started benignly enough. *Who is the baker?* *Theo, you didn't tell us!* *She's lovely — how did you two meet?*

And then someone — I never found out who; I've had theories, all of them featuring Vivienne's arithmetic — replied:

*Didn't she post an ad? I swear I saw something months ago. Some kind of listing. Hold on.*

Hold on. Two words that should come with a warning label, a hazard symbol, a little icon of a man falling off a cliff in slow motion.

There was a link.

I want to say I hesitated. I want to tell you there was a moment — a breath, a heartbeat, a single rational voice saying *you know this woman, you've watched her graft apple trees wrong and laugh about it, you've eaten burnt espresso and liked it* — some part of me that understood what links do, what comment threads do, what the past does when you dig it up and hold it to the light without context.

But curiosity is not a gentleman. Curiosity kicked the door in.

The post was dated eight months ago. Eight months — before the café, before the burnt espresso, before the handshake over the counter where two liars shook hands and I thought, God help me, that I was the one doing the hiring. It was on one of those community boards, the kind where people sell used pianos and advertise lost dogs. The title read:

**WANTED: Fake boyfriend for family reunion weekend (one-time engagement). Will pay in pastries/goods/services. Must be convincing. Details in person. Serious inquiries only.**

I read it four times. I read it the way you reread a letter from the bank, hoping the numbers will rearrange themselves into something survivable. The words didn't change. *Will pay.* *Must be convincing.* *Serious inquiries only.*

Serious inquiries only. As though I were a job posting. As though the weekend — the cider house, the true things we traded in the dark, her father's death laid out between us like an offering, the almost-kiss interrupted by Aunt Carol — as though any of it had been a deliverable.

Here is what I did next, and I'm not proud of it, but honesty is apparently the theme of the season: I went looking for more. I scrolled her old posts. I found the thread where someone asked if the position was still open and she'd replied, three days before she hired me, *Still looking! The right candidate will be charming, tall-ish, and good with judgmental relatives.* Tall-ish. Good with judgmental relatives. I had been vetted against a rubric. Every joke she'd ever made about surviving the reunion reorganized itself in my memory into project management.

I sat on the train with my phone in my hand and let the rewrite happen. That's what betrayal does, I learned — it doesn't bring new information so much as it re-edits the old footage. Same scenes, new score. The night she stayed late teaching me to fold croissant dough became billable hours. The way she'd looked at me across the farmers' market, sunlight through the peach crates, became a performance review conducted in real time. The kiss at her door — off-script, I'd thought, off-script, the one thing that couldn't be faked because there was no audience, no grandmother to charm, no contract clause — even that curdled. Maybe she'd been maintaining the asset. Maybe consistency was part of the service.

My grandmother's founding letter was folded in my breast pocket. I'd been carrying it for a week, waiting for the right moment, rehearsing sentences about legacy and planting what you love. Legacy means planting what you love, not preserving what you fear. I'd read it so many times the creases had gone soft. And now here I stood — figuratively, and also literally, wedged against the train doors with commuters sighing around me — holding permission I no longer knew how to use.
