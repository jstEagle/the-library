## 2. The Man at Table Four

There is a particular species of cowardice that books itself a window seat.

I'd like to tell you I arrived in Hartwell's Point the way heroes arrive in the novels my grandmother devours — at golden hour, wind in the hair, some triumphant music swelling over the harbor. What actually happened was that my rental car's GPS gave up two miles out of town, the way all technology eventually surrenders to Hartwell's Point, and I found the place the way you find everything that matters here: by smell. Woodsmoke, salt, and underneath it, faint but insistent, someone baking lemon tarts.

Nine years, and the town still smelled like my childhood. That felt less like a homecoming and more like an ambush.

I parked outside what used to be Delacroix's Bakery, except the sign had changed. **BUTTER & SALT**, said the hand-painted letters, and beneath them, smaller, in a script that was trying very hard to be cheerful: *Est. by X. Hartwell. Yes, we're open.*

The *yes* was doing a lot of work in that sentence. You don't paint *yes* on a sign unless someone has asked the question enough times.

I sat in the car for eleven minutes. I know because I watched the clock on the dash and told myself each minute was the last one. This is a thing I do. I have done it in airport bars in Singapore and hotel lobbies in Zurich and once, memorably, in the back of a taxi in Lisbon while a woman I'd promised things to waited upstairs. I am, by every professional metric, a decisive man. I close deals other people flinch from. I have renegotiated contracts with men who own small countries' worth of shipping lanes. And I sat in a rented sedan outside a bakery, negotiating with myself like the two parties had irreconcilable terms.

The problem, you understand, was not the town. The town was fine. The town had forgiven me before I'd even left; towns are generous that way, they forget you the moment your taillights disappear. The problem was a woman behind a counter who had every right to remember.

* * *

The bell over the door did that thing bells do in small towns — announced you like a court summons. Four heads turned. A man at the corner table with a paperback western, a woman with a stroller, a teenager glued to a laptop, and an old man in a fishing cap who looked at me exactly as long as it takes to decide you don't know someone, then a half-second longer, just in case.

They didn't know me. Nine years had sanded off whatever edges had made me legible. I'd left at twenty-four, all elbows and unfinished sentences. I'd come back thirty-three, in a coat that cost more than the fishing cap man's boat, and to this room I was nobody. There was a freedom in that I hadn't anticipated. There was also, underneath it, an insult I hadn't earned the right to feel.

The counter was longer than it used to be, rebuilt in pale wood, and behind it — no. Not behind it. She was on a stepladder, of all things, changing the chalkboard menu, and she was arguing with the chalk.

"You can't just say *seasonal*," she was telling the board. "People hate *seasonal*. *Seasonal* means *we ran out of the thing you wanted*."

She was up on her toes, arm extended, and the board was a half-inch beyond her reach, and she was going to stretch until she won or fell, and both options appeared equally acceptable to her.

Xanthe Hartwell. Twenty-nine years old and still built entirely out of stubbornness and flour.

I should describe her properly, I suppose, because the truth is I'd prepared myself for a stranger. Nine years is long enough. I'd told myself she'd be someone I'd have to squint at, someone the memory would have to be adjusted to fit. Instead the memory fit her like her own apron. Same dark hair escaping a bun with the lawless energy of something that had never once obeyed her. Same flour smudge on her jaw that she'd never notice and that I — apparently, traitorously — still wanted to brush away. She'd gotten thinner, or maybe just more concentrated, the way a sauce reduces. There was a pencil behind her ear and chalk dust on her forearm and she was muttering to a menu board, and something in my chest did a thing I refused to name in a public establishment.

I took table four. I don't know why I remember that it was table four. Perhaps because when we were nineteen and this was still her mother's bakery, table four was the one with the wobbly leg, and Gideon Marsh, age nineteen, had folded a sugar packet under it with the gravity of a man defusing ordnance, and Xanthe had said, "You fixed it wrong and now it leans toward the window like it's eavesdropping," and we had laughed until her mother came out and told us some customers were trying to eat.

I sat down at table four and it did not wobble. She'd fixed it properly at some point in the intervening years, or replaced it, or the whole café had simply decided to be sturdier than the people in it. There was a lesson in that, probably. I ordered coffee from a teenager at the register and did not look at the stepladder, which took genuine effort.

* * *

Here is what I had not prepared for, in all my eleven minutes of vehicular cowardice: the sound.

She came down off the ladder, gave the board one last look of betrayal, and said to the teenager — whose name tag said DEV — "If anyone asks what's seasonal, tell them it's lemon tart and it's always lemon tart, and if they complain, tell them the chef will come out." She said *the chef* about herself the way other people say *the authorities*, and Dev nodded with the weary respect of a soldier who has seen this general win battles.

And then she laughed. At her own joke, alone, quietly, the small private laugh of a person who has learned to be her own audience because the house was empty too often.

I want to be honest with you, since we've come this far: I have spent nine years constructing a version of that laugh I could live with. The version where it had turned brittle. The version where she'd married a dentist in Bangor and laughed at his jokes now, at dinner parties, politely. The version where she'd forgotten me so thoroughly that my name, if it surfaced at all, surfaced as a trivia answer. *Whatever happened to that Marsh boy? Oh, him. He left.*

The laugh was none of those versions. It was warm and a little tired and entirely hers, and it made the constructed versions look like what they were — the architecture of a guilty man.

Because here is the thing I carried across nine years and four time zones, the thing I did not put in any of the speeches I'd rehearsed on the plane: I did not leave because of a job. Everyone in town believed I left because of the job — the London firm, the opportunity, the bright future, all the vocabulary adults use to make departure sound like ambition. And I did take the job. But jobs don't make you stand in a boathouse at seventeen — no. Not the boathouse. That was later, that was the other thing, the thing underneath. At twenty-four it was simpler and worse: a girl had asked me to stay, or nearly asked, or I had decided she'd asked, the distinction mattered enormously to me at the time and not at all now, and I had looked at her — at Xanthe, flour on her jaw even then, some things are constitutional — and I had understood that if I stayed, I would stay forever, and I was twenty-four and terrified of forever the way only people who secretly want it are terrified.

So I left. And I let her believe — I never said it, I was never brave enough to say it, but I let her believe — that she hadn't been enough to keep me.

There are sins of commission and sins of omission, and then there is the sin of letting someone write the wrong ending and not correcting the manuscript. I had nine years of material for my memoir of cowardice. I could have filled a shelf.

* * *

The coffee arrived. It was bad. I mean that with enormous affection — it was bad the way the coffee had been bad when her mother ran the place, burnt at the edges and over-strong in the middle, a coffee with a personality disorder, and I drank it and felt my shoulders drop a quarter inch for the first time in a transatlantic flight and a rental car and eleven minutes in a sedan.

I watched her work. I'll admit that freely; I was there to do it. She moved through the kitchen pass like someone conducting an orchestra only she could hear — pulling a tray of tarts, checking them against some internal standard, frowning at two, setting them aside with the gentle ruthlessness of a perfectionist. The rejected tarts went onto a plate by the register marked, in Dev's handwriting, **STAFF. DO NOT TOUCH. X.**

She'd kept the lemon tarts. Her mother's recipe — I knew it was her mother's because I'd watched Mrs. Delacroix make them a hundred times in this very kitchen, back when Xanthe and I were allowed in on the condition that we stayed on our side of the counter, a condition whose lifespan was roughly four minutes. Zest and juice and that short pastry that shattered like a secret. Mrs. Delacroix had been dead six years — I'd sent flowers from Singapore, which is the coward's condolence, flowers with a card signed in a lawyer's neat hand because even my grief went through channels.

And the café was struggling. I could see it the way I could see a failing property from a moving car — it's not a skill I'm proud of, it's just what I do, I look at a room and see its balance sheet. The good china had been replaced by mismatched. The second espresso machine was gone, its outline still visible in the dust on the counter. The chalkboard menu had exactly nine items on it, and three of them were coffee. The *yes, we're open* on the sign outside. The tart plate marked STAFF, because staff was her and a teenager and apparently the tarts were a benefit package.

She'd built something beautiful out of almost nothing, and almost nothing was running out.

I did the arithmetic I would later pretend I hadn't done. The building — she owned it, or her mother had and it had passed to her, I knew that much from the flowers-and-lawyer channel. The block, really — the café and the two storefronts flanking it, a little row of Hartwell's Point history that developers had been circling for a decade like gulls around a chip. I knew because my aunt Rosalind had mentioned it at Christmas, in her way, which is to say she'd slid a folder across the dinner table and said, "Hartwell's Point is *ripe*, Gideon, someone should tell the family before outsiders get ideas," and I had said I wasn't interested in the family business anymore, and she had smiled the smile of a woman filing something away for later.

Rosalind always files things away for later. Remember that. It becomes important.

* * *

She saw me on her third pass. Not me — the table. The empty cup, the plate with one tart's worth of crumbs — yes, I'd ordered a tart, and I'll defend that decision to my grave; you don't come home after nine years and not verify the tarts. She came over with the coffee pot and the professional smile, the one for strangers, and she said, "Refill?" and then she actually looked at me.

I watched it happen. I want to report that I behaved with dignity, that I said something gracious, that nine years of boardrooms had taught me to manage a moment like this. What I actually did was hold the cup slightly too tightly and say, "Please," in a voice I did not recognize.

Her eyes went through the same process the fishing cap man's had — the not-knowing, the half-second longer — and then something moved behind them like weather. Recognition is a strange thing to watch from the inside of the person being recognized. Her face did three things in one second: the flicker of *do I know you*, the flicker of *oh*, and then — and this is the one I'll be paying for — the third thing, the shutter. Not anger. Worse. Politeness. The smile stayed exactly where it was, but everything behind it stepped back into another room.

"Gideon Marsh," she said. Not a question. She said it the way you read a name off a document.

"Hello, Xanthe."

"Nine years." She refilled my cup without asking whether I wanted it, which was either hospitality or a small violence, and I honestly couldn't tell which, and with Xanthe it was often both. "You look —" She considered. She had always considered before she spoke, except when she didn't, and the times she didn't were always the true ones. "Expensive," she finished.

"I get that a lot."

"I bet you do." She set the pot down. "Lemon tart okay?"

"It was perfect."

Something crossed her face at that — fast, gone — and she said, "It's my mother's recipe," in a tone that dared me to make something of it, and I didn't, because rule one of returning from the dead is you don't haunt the kitchen on your first day.

"Welcome back to Hartwell's Point," she said, and it was the most professional sentence ever assembled, and she picked up the pot and turned to the next table, and I understood that I had just been served. Not the coffee. The verdict. *Welcome back* — as in, you may return. As in, the door is technically open. Nothing about being let all the way in.

* * *

I stayed an hour. I drank coffee that went cold because I kept not drinking it, watching her work, cataloguing my regrets like a man doing inventory before an audit. The teenager, Dev, cleared my plate and gave me a look of profound teenage suspicion, as though I were a health inspector or a first love, and honestly both were accurate.

At some point the fishing cap man left, and the woman with the stroller left, and the afternoon light came in long and gold across the floorboards, and Xanthe flipped the sign on the door — not to *closed*, but to a little wooden square that said **BACK IN 20**, and she disappeared into the back, and I heard water running, and I sat there at my table that didn't wobble anymore, in a town that remembered everything, holding a cold cup of coffee like evidence.

Here's what I'd come home to do. My grandmother — sharp as a gull and twice as hungry — had summoned me with a letter that contained the word *reunion* and the phrase *no excuses, Gideon* and, in a postscript that had kept me up for two nights over the Atlantic, a line about the family trust and what it required of me now that I was, in her words, *of an age*. I'd come home because the letter had the gravitational pull of a dying woman's wish, and because I was thirty-three and tired, and because some part of me that I'd been starving for nine years had smelled lemon tarts through the GPS's failure and started pulling like a tide.

I had not come home to see her. I want that on the record. I had come home *around* seeing her, the way you navigate around a reef.

And then the little wooden square flipped back, and the bell didn't ring because she'd come through the kitchen door instead, and she was standing there in her flour-dusted apron with her arms crossed, looking at me with an expression I remembered from the summer we were seventeen — the one she used right before she asked a question she already knew the answer to.

"You're staying at the Cliffmere house," she said.

It wasn't a question either. Small towns. Everyone already knew; I'd probably been reported before I'd finished parking.

"I am."

"Family reunion."

"That's the rumor."

She nodded slowly, and looked at me, and there was a whole unsaid paragraph in her face, nine years of it, and then she said — lightly, too lightly, the way you set down something heavy so it doesn't make a sound — "Well. Enjoy the tarts while you're in town, Gideon. They're the only thing around here that's stayed the same."

And she turned back to her counter, and I sat at table four with my cold coffee and my older regrets, and I understood with terrible clarity that I had exactly one thing I was good at in this world, and it wasn't honesty, and it wasn't staying.

It was making deals.

I watched her argue with the chalkboard again — she'd lost her battle with the word *seasonal*, it was still there, underlined twice — and I thought: she needs something. I could see it, the way I could see a failing balance sheet. And I needed something too, something my grandmother's letter wanted and my aunt Rosalind was already scheming toward, and the shape of the thing I was about to propose assembled itself in my mind with the horrible smoothness of a contract that writes itself.

I finished the cold coffee. It was still bad. I still loved it.

I stood up, and I walked toward the counter, and Xanthe Hartwell looked up at me with flour on her jaw and suspicion in her eyes, and I opened my mouth to say the first lie of the summer.
