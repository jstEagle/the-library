## 1. The Count at Low Tide

The tide was out by a full hundred yards when Freya Villalobos knelt in the wet sand and pressed two fingers to the edge of the nest cavity, feeling for the warmth of incubation the way other people checked a pulse.

"Fourteen," she said aloud, because the numbers liked to be said aloud, liked to be given weight and air. "Nest fourteen, sixty-one days, no depression yet."

The clipboard was old, its laminate peeling at the corners, and she'd been carrying it for five years — long enough that the sand had worked itself permanently into the spiral binding, long enough that her handwriting in the earliest entries looked like a stranger's. Tighter, back then. Angrier. She'd loosened over the seasons, the way driftwood loosens, the way a person loosens when they finally stop gripping.

Dawn came up gray-gold over Pelican Sound, the kind of light that made everything look forgiven. Freya moved down the beach with the unhurried economy of someone who had done this four hundred mornings in a row and intended to do it four hundred more. Stake, flag, GPS ping, note. Stake, flag, ping, note. The ritual was the point. The ritual was the whole architecture of her life, and she had built it deliberately, brick by small brick, after the year everything collapsed — a life made entirely of steps small enough that none of them could break under her.

She crouched at nest seventeen and found what she was looking for: a dimple in the sand, the telltale sink that meant hatchlings were tunneling up toward the surface, gathering beneath it like held breath. Three days, maybe four.

"Hello, little ones," she murmured. "Stay put. The world's not ready, and neither are you."

Her phone buzzed in her jacket pocket. She ignored it, the way she ignored most things that buzzed. It buzzed again. Then again — the triple-strike of someone who actually needed her, which narrowed the field to perhaps four people on the planet.

She checked it with sandy fingers. *Mr. Okafor's office. Please call at your earliest convenience. It's important.*

Freya stood very still. Mr. Okafor was not the kind of man who used the words *it's important* lightly. He was the kind of man who sent a reminder text about annual will reviews and then apologized for the intrusion.

The wind came off the water and moved through the sea oats with a sound like whispering, like the beach itself had opinions. Freya looked down at nest seventeen, at the sand holding its secret, and felt the first hairline crack run through the morning.

She didn't call. Not yet. She finished the survey first — nests eighteen through twenty-two, all quiet, all accounted for — because that was the other thing about her life: the counts always got finished. The world could be ending, but the count got finished, and then you could face whatever it was with clean hands and a complete column of numbers.

It was the only kind of control she had ever managed to keep.

* * *

Mr. Okafor's office sat two blocks off the water in a building that used to be a cannery, all exposed brick and windows that rattled when the shrimp boats came in. His receptionist, a young woman with kind eyes and a cardigan in July, took one look at Freya's salt-stiff hair and survey clothes and said, "He's expecting you. Can I get you coffee?"

"No, thank you." Freya's voice came out level. She'd had five years of practice making it level.

Okafor was at his desk with a folder in front of him, closed, both hands resting on it like a man keeping a lid on something. He was in his sixties, precise, silver-bearded, the only person in Pelican Sound who had known her grandmother's legal affairs, and he looked at Freya now the way you look at someone you're about to hand bad news to — except his eyes weren't sorry. They were something more complicated than sorry.

"Freya. Thank you for coming so quickly."

"The count ran long." She sat, spine straight, hands folded. "Is this about the estate? Because I thought everything was settled years ago. Abuela Rosa left me—"

"This isn't about the estate." He paused. "Or rather. It is. But not the way you think."

The crack in the morning widened.

"Eleven years ago," Okafor said, "your grandmother wrote a letter. A formal one, notarized, with a copy filed in her records. It was entrusted to a courier service out of Tampa — the kind of operation that handles legal documents, one courier, one delivery. The courier delivered it to the wrong address. A house on Sandpiper Lane. The resident was an elderly man named Hollis Peck, who was, it turns out, in the early stages of dementia, and who put the envelope in a drawer with his tax papers and never opened it. Never opened any of his mail, really, for the last decade of his life. His daughter found the drawer last month while clearing the house. She brought it here yesterday."

Freya's hands had gone still in her lap. Stillness was her other skill.

"My grandmother died eight years ago," Freya said. "Whatever the letter says—"

"The letter is still legally operative. That's the extraordinary part. Your grandmother drafted it with a condition precedent — a claim window. She was very specific about it, Freya. She was specific about everything." He opened the folder at last and turned it toward her, and there was the envelope, yellowed at the edges, postmarked in faded blue ink. A date eleven years old. Her grandmother's handwriting on the front — that looping, imperious script Freya would have known anywhere, the handwriting that had written *Freya, mija, come eat* across a thousand kitchen notes.

The room tilted, very slightly, the way a boat tilts.

"Before I give you this," Okafor said gently, "I want you to hear the terms. Because they're unusual, and I'd rather you hear them from me than from the paper. The letter names you as heir to the Villalobos Inn. And it names a second heir. The claim is only valid if both heirs claim it together, in person, within thirty days of the letter's discovery. The discovery was Tuesday. The clock is already running."

"Who," Freya said. Her voice was still level. She was amazed at how level it was. "Who is the second heir?"

Okafor looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him reach for the folder instead of answering.

"Read it," he said. "She wrote it to you. Both of you. But mostly to you, I think."

* * *

Freya did not open the envelope in the office. She carried it out past the receptionist with the kind eyes, past the rattling windows and the shrimp boats, and she walked — because driving felt suddenly impossible, because her body wanted motion the way it wanted air — all the way to the north end of the beach, past nest fourteen, past nest seventeen with its buried, waiting hatchlings, to the flat stretch of sand where the dunes gave way to sea grapes and there was no one, at eight in the morning, for a mile in either direction.

She sat down. She held the envelope on her knees.

Eleven years. The letter had been sitting in a dead man's drawer for eleven years, while her grandmother got sick and died, while Freya built her small safe life one counted turtle at a time, while — and her mind touched this thought and flinched away from it, touched it again, flinched again —

While *he* was —

She opened it.

The handwriting was Rosa's, unmistakable, the penmanship of a woman who had run an inn for forty years and signed every register herself. Freya read it once at speed, the words blurring, and then forced herself to go back to the beginning and read it slowly, the way you'd eat a last meal, tasting everything.

*Mija. If you are reading this, then the letter found you late, and I am sorry for that. I trusted the wrong courier and I have no one to blame but my own stubbornness — I would not use the post office because I did not trust the post office, which tells you everything about me.*

*The inn is yours. It was always meant to be yours, and I am sorry I never said it plainly while I could. But I am an old woman and I have watched you my whole life, and I know that you will not come for it unless you are made to. You run, mija. You run beautifully. You have been running since you were small, and I loved you too much to chase you, and now I am going to make the running stop.*

*The inn goes to you and to Gideon Marsh together. Not to one, not to the other. Together. You will have to stand in the same room as him and claim what is yours, and I know what I am asking. I know what you did. I knew then, too, though you thought I didn't.*

*Thirty days from the day this letter is found. That is all I can give you. It is not enough time to fix what you broke and it is not enough time to ruin what remains, and that is exactly the point. I am giving you exactly enough rope to hang yourself or to climb, and I will be watching from wherever old women go, with my arms crossed, waiting to see which.*

Freya stopped reading. Her eyes had filled without her permission, and a drop fell on the page and bloomed in the old ink, and she wiped it with her sleeve, furious at herself, furious at Rosa, furious at a dead courier and a dead man's drawer and the whole patient machinery of a past that had finally, after five years of her careful small steps, caught up with her.

*Gideon.*

The name sat on the page in her grandmother's handwriting and would not be un-seen. Gideon Marsh, who had been nineteen and asleep in the boathouse the last time she saw him, who had trusted her with everything, who had — no. She didn't think about that morning. She had a system for not thinking about that morning. It had taken five years to build the system and it worked, it had *worked*, and now a dead woman's letter had kicked the door in.

She read the last lines.

*Claim it together, mija. The inn, and everything else you left in it. Thirty days. I never liked giving people second chances — I liked giving them one true chance, once, and watching what they did with it. This is yours.*

*Con todo mi amor, y toda mi terquedad,*
*Abuela Rosa*

The tide was coming back in. Freya sat on the sand with the letter in her hands and watched the water erase, inch by inch, the long clean line of her morning's work — the stakes and flags and numbered nests all still standing, but the beach around them smoothing itself back to blank, as if the survey had never happened, as if nothing could be counted on to last.

Nest seventeen was behind her, its hatchlings days from surfacing, and she thought: *they wait under the sand until the temperature is right, and then they all rise at once, and they run for the water, and half of them don't make it.*

Her phone buzzed. Okafor's office, following up.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she looked north, up the beach, toward the crooked silhouette of the Villalobos Inn — the inn she had not let herself look at in five years, its sagging porch just visible past the sea grapes, and a thin thread of woodsmoke rising from its chimney, which was wrong, which was impossible, because the inn had been empty for years.

Except it hadn't been empty. Someone had been keeping the chimney clear. Someone had been there all along, all eleven years, all five years, and she knew — she knew with a sick drop of certainty, the way you know a voice on the phone — exactly who.

The letter trembled in her hands.

Twenty-nine days.
