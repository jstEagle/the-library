## 12. Between Rows

The third week of September came in hot and mean, the kind of heat that made the vines droop by noon and made tempers shorter than the shadows. Marisol had been up since four, checking sugar levels in the east block, and by six she was standing at the crush pad with a clipboard she wasn't reading, watching Astrid Ashgrove's truck rattle down the access road with a flatbed of empty bins behind it.

They had agreed to this. That was the strange part. Somewhere between the boundary dispute and the letter — the letter that sat now like a stone in Marisol's chest, unopened in her desk drawer because she could not make herself read what her own hands had carried out of this valley eleven years ago — they had agreed to meet weekly about the road, the fence line, the water rights, and somehow weekly had become daily, and daily had become this: Astrid pulling up at dawn with coffee in a dented thermos and dirt already under her fingernails from whatever she'd been doing in the orchard before sunrise.

"You're late," Marisol said, though she wasn't.

"Your road is full of potholes." Astrid climbed down from the cab, thermos in hand, hair tied back with what appeared to be a strip of blue bandana. She looked, Marisol thought, nothing like the woman who used to wear black to rehearsal and complain about rosin dust on her sleeves. She looked like someone who belonged to soil. "I nearly lost a bin back at the bend."

"That bend has been there since 1987."

"And you've never once thought to fill it?"

"I've thought about it the way I think about a lot of things." Marisol handed her a pair of gloves from the bed of the truck. "I think about it, and then I don't do it."

Astrid laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if it had escaped custody — and pulled on the gloves. And that was how the day began.

* * *

The hardest stretch of harvest was the Pinot Noir on the south slope, where the rows ran steep and the clusters hung tight and every one of them had to be cut by hand. Marisol's crew was small — six people, seven with Astrid, who had announced two days ago that she was done talking about easements and wanted to learn to pick.

"You'll hate it by noon," Marisol had warned her.

"I've spent three years teaching eleven-year-olds to hold a bow without strangling it," Astrid said. "I have reserves of patience you cannot imagine."

By ten-thirty she was cursing in two languages over a cluster that had hidden itself under a leaf canopy like something ashamed to be seen, and Marisol was laughing so hard she had to lean on her shears.

"It's not funny," Astrid said, wrestling the vine aside. "This thing is mocking me. I can feel it."

"Grapes don't mock."

"This one does. It has a personality. It's smug."

"Then leave it for the birds."

"Absolutely not." She got her fingers around the stem and cut it clean, holding it up like a trophy. "There. Tell your smug grape its reign is over."

Marisol took it from her, weighed it in her palm. "Good color," she said, and meant it, and watched something flicker across Astrid's face at the plainness of the praise — something pleased and startled, like a person who'd forgotten praise existed.

They worked. The sun climbed. The slope made conversation economical at first — *pass the bin, watch the drip line, mind the hornet* — but somewhere in the second row the economy loosened, and words started coming for free.

"Trellising," Astrid said, apropos of nothing, wiping sweat off her brow with her wrist. "I've been meaning to tell you your trellising is wrong."

Marisol stopped mid-cut. "My trellising is wrong."

"The vertical shoot positioning. You're tying too tight. The shoots can't move in the wind, so they thicken wrong and shade their own fruit."

"My father tied those vines for thirty years."

"And my grandmother pruned her apples the same way for forty, and half of them split in the storm two winters ago because nobody ever told her no." Astrid met her eyes, chin lifted, and there it was — not the careful woman from the crush pad negotiations, not the ghost from eleven years ago, but someone with an argument in her and nowhere to put it until now. "I'm just saying. A little slack isn't weakness."

Marisol looked down the row at the taut green shoots, lashed to their wires, holding themselves rigid against a wind they couldn't control. Something moved through her chest that had nothing to do with viticulture.

"I'll loosen the ties," she said. "On the new blocks."

Astrid blinked, visibly braced for a fight she didn't get. "Oh," she said. "Okay."

"Don't look so victorious. I'm doing it because you're right, not because you bullied me."

"I would never bully anyone about agriculture."

"You bullied me about a pothole at six in the morning."

"That pothole is a hazard."

* * *

Lunch happened at noon because Marisol's crew refused to let it happen any other way, and it happened under the big oak at the top of the slope where somebody had dragged a table sometime in the last century. Rosa, the forewoman, produced tamales from a cooler with the gravity of a priest revealing relics. Someone had brought a radio; someone else turned it off when Astrid sat down, politely, and Astrid said, "No — leave it," and then went quiet when it played something orchestral, and Marisol watched her hand move against her thigh, fingers finding a phrase inside the music, tracing it, letting it go.

She doesn't play anymore, Marisol reminded herself. Except she'd heard her. Two weeks ago, at dusk, alone among the rows — the cello singing low and private into the dark like a confession nobody was supposed to hear. Marisol had stood behind the equipment shed with her heart going like a threshing machine and hadn't moved until the last note faded, and she had never mentioned it, and she did not mention it now.

"Astrid," said young Tomás, mouth full, "Rosa says you teach kids cello."

"Beginners, mostly."

"My sister wants to learn. She's nine. She's very dramatic about it."

"Nine-year-olds are the best students," Astrid said. "They haven't learned to be embarrassed yet. They'll make the same mistake forty times in a row and still be excited about the forty-first."

"And adults?"

"Adults apologize." Astrid smiled into her tamale. "Every time they miss a note, they say sorry. To me. Like the cello is going to hold a grudge."

"Do you ever miss notes?" Tomás asked, with the tactlessness of the young.

"Constantly," Astrid said, easy and light, and Marisol heard the floorboard under the lightness, the place where the weight was stored. "That's the whole secret. Music is just missing notes beautifully."

Rosa snorted. "You should tell that to Marisol. She replays every mistake she's ever made like a film reel."

"Rosa."

"What? It's true. Ask her about 2019, the frost—"

"We're not asking about the frost." But Marisol was laughing, actually laughing, helpless against it, and Astrid was looking at her with an expression Marisol couldn't name — warm and wondering, like she was seeing something she'd been told no longer existed.

* * *

The afternoon turned. The heat broke around three and a marine layer came sliding over the ridge, gray and cool, smelling of salt and coming rain. They worked faster in it, lighter, the grapes cool in their hands. By five the south block was done, bins stacked and tarped, and instead of leaving — Marisol noticed this, catalogued it, tried not to hope too loudly about it — Astrid stayed to help wash the picking crates, standing hip-deep in the task beside her at the spigot, water running cold over both their hands.

"Your crew sings," Astrid said. "Did you know that? When they think nobody's listening. Rosa was humming earlier. Whole harmonies."

"They've always done that. Harvest makes people hum."

"I used to hum," Astrid said, scrubbing a crate with more attention than it required. "In the practice room. My grandmother said she could always tell which piece I was working on from the kitchen, even through two floors. Said the whole house knew my moods by what I hummed." She paused. "I don't hum anymore. I noticed that recently. I just — stopped, at some point, and never picked it back up."

The water ran. Marisol turned off the spigot, and in the sudden quiet she said, before she could stop herself: "Start with something small. Humming is like that. It comes back before you decide to let it."

Astrid looked at her. The light was going gold and gray at once, storm-light, the kind that made everything look remembered. "Is that what happened with the vineyard?" she asked. "It came back before you decided?"

Marisol's throat tightened. Eleven years ago this land had been her father's dream and her exile; she'd come home to bury him and stay to prove something, and somewhere in the proving the proof had become love. "Something like that," she said. "You come back to fix a fence and one morning you realize you know every vine by name."

"Do you? Know them by name?"

"The difficult ones."

Astrid grinned. "Like the smug grape."

"The smug grape is named Gerald."

Astrid laughed so hard she had to grip the edge of the crate-washing table, and Marisol felt the laugh land in her own body like warmth after cold — absurd, disproportionate, dangerous. When had this become easy again? When had the guarded courtesy cracked open into this — arguments and tamales and a grape named Gerald? She should be more careful. There was a letter in her desk drawer that could end all of this with one sentence, a letter she'd carried unopened for weeks because opening it meant learning whether the girl she'd loved had truly sent her away or whether—

Whether what? Whether some other hand had done it. And what would she do with that knowledge either way?

"Marisol?" Astrid was watching her. "You went somewhere."

"Just counting bins," Marisol said, and reached for another crate.

* * *

The rain held off but the wind didn't. By dusk it was pushing hard from the southwest, rattling the nets, and Marisol walked the perimeter out of habit while Astrid loaded her truck. At the gate — the old gate, the disputed one, the whole reason any of this had started — Astrid paused with her hand on the latch.

"Same time tomorrow?" she said. "Rosa says the Chardonnay block won't pick itself, and apparently I've been conscripted."

"You could say no."

"I could," Astrid agreed. The wind pulled at the blue bandana in her hair. For a moment she looked younger than her years and older than her grief, both at once, and Marisol's chest ached with the effort of not saying any of the ten thousand things stacked up behind her teeth. "But I find I don't want to. Is that strange? Three weeks ago I drove up here to drop off a lawyer's letter and flee, and now I'm volunteering for manual labor at dawn."

"Not strange," Marisol said quietly.

"No?"

"No. The land does that. It gets its hooks in."

Astrid held her gaze a beat longer than the sentence required. "Right," she said. "The land." And there was something in her voice — a question folded inside a statement, or a statement folded inside a question — that Marisol chose, deliberately, not to unfold.

"Drive safe," Marisol said. "Mind the pothole."

"Fill the pothole."

"Goodnight, Astrid."

She watched the taillights go down the access road, slow over the bend, gone. Then she stood a while in the wind among her named vines, arms crossed against the chill, feeling the shape of the evening settle into her like sediment.

* * *

That night she almost opened the letter.

She had it out of the drawer, envelope yellowed soft at the corners, her grandmother's handwriting — no, Astrid's grandmother's handwriting, Elena Ashgrove's looping script — facing up on the desk under the lamp. *For Marisol Vega,* it said, and below it, in smaller ink, *to be given only if.* Only if what? The rest of the instruction was on the back, and Marisol had read the back a hundred times and understood it none.

Her own grandmother had mentioned a letter too, once, near the end — *there are things Elena wrote, mija, things you should know someday* — and then died before saying more, and now two dead women had left two sealed sentences sitting between two living ones like a tripwire across the only road forward.

Tomorrow, Marisol thought. Tomorrow I'll open it.

She had thought that yesterday, too. And the day before. And each day Astrid arrived at dawn with coffee and an argument about drainage, and each day the present grew heavier than the past, and each day Marisol chose the rows, the work, the laughter that kept ambushing her — chose the living thing in front of her over the buried thing behind her, and called it patience, and knew it was fear.

Because here was the truth she circled nightly and never landed on: if the letter proved Elena Ashgrove had lied to her — had manufactured the goodbye Marisol had swallowed whole for eleven years — then Astrid had never chosen to let her go. And if Astrid had never chosen it, then everything Marisol had built her silence on — *she wanted me gone, I honored it, the guilt is mine to carry* — collapsed, and she would have to face what remained: that she had abandoned the person she loved most on the strength of a stranger's envelope, and that the woman now laughing over tamales under the oak had spent those same years believing herself abandoned too.

And there was something else. Something Marisol had never told anyone, the image she carried like a stone in her boot: a concert hall, three years ago, a stage, a cellist mid-phrase who suddenly stopped — and the terrible silence after, and Marisol sitting frozen in the twelfth row with her heart breaking open, having come secretly, having told no one, having slipped out before the lights came up and never once found the courage to say *I was there.*

Tomorrow, she promised the letter. Tomorrow I'll tell her everything.

Outside, the wind rose, and the first drops of rain began ticking against the tin roof, and somewhere down the dark hill, in the orchard across the water, Marisol imagined — hoped — that Astrid was humming.
