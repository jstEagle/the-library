## 7. The Dessert That Undid Them

There is a particular kind of panic that sets in around nine p.m. at a family party, when the cake has been cut and the old people have started their second wind and you realize you have agreed to spend the rest of your life with a woman you've known for six days, and the only person who doesn't know it's fake is everyone.

I was standing in the corner of Maren Amberdale's living room holding a plate of lemon cake I had no intention of eating, performing the role of Devoted Boyfriend, which mostly meant nodding at intervals and letting Elodie's cousin's husband explain boat engines to me. The cake was a prop. Everything was a prop. The ring on Elodie's finger was a prop—her grandmother's, borrowed, which was either very sweet or very efficient, I hadn't decided. My hand on the small of her back whenever we passed through a doorway: prop. The way I'd learned the exact pressure that made her lean into me instead of stiffen: that one had stopped feeling like a prop somewhere around the rehearsal dinner, and I was trying very hard not to think about that.

"—so the impeller's basically the heart of the whole system," the cousin's husband was saying. "You'd know that, being a journalist. You people love a good metaphor."

"I love a good nap," I said. "It's nine-forty."

He laughed like I'd said something charming and moved off toward the whiskey, and I exhaled and scanned the room for Elodie.

She was across the room in a green dress, laughing at something her aunt said, and I want to be honest about what that did to me, because honesty is supposedly my religion and I've been lying to everyone including myself all week. She laughed with her whole face. She laughed like someone who'd rationed laughter for years and had recently discovered a surplus. And every time it happened, some traitorous part of me catalogued it—*that one's mine, I did that*—which is a dangerous way for a man in my position to think.

Her position, too. Let's be fair. She'd caught me watching her twice tonight and hadn't looked away either time. We were two liars holding hands inside the lie, and the terrible thing was how well it fit.

She found me by the window a few minutes later.

"Rescue me," she said. "Aunt Petra wants to know our wedding colors and I panicked and said *oyster*."

"Oyster isn't a color, it's an animal."

"So is the wedding, at this point. Come on." She took my wrist. "I need to check on dessert. Come be decorative in the kitchen."

The lighthouse kitchen—because yes, Maren Amberdale lived in a decommissioned lighthouse, because of course she did, because this whole town was a novel that had gotten too big for its genre—was blessedly empty, warm, and smelled like butter and lemon zest. Elodie shut the door behind us and leaned against it and let out a breath that seemed to come from her shoes.

"Oyster," I said.

"I panicked. There were follow-up questions. There was a *Pinterest board*."

"Tragic. I would have gone with barnacle."

"You're no help at all." But she was smiling, that tired real smile, the one that only came out when the door was shut. She crossed to the counter where the second cake sat under a dome, the reserve cake, the one nobody was supposed to know about, and lifted the dome and looked at it with an expression I recognized from the mirror. The expression of a woman deciding whether cake counts as a meal.

"It's her birthday," I said. "Calories are ceremonial."

"That's the most romantic thing you've said all week." She cut two slices with the confidence of a woman who'd been sneaking cake in this kitchen since she was six, and handed me a fork, and we stood there at the counter eating birthday cake in the dark like a couple of teenagers, and I thought: *this is the problem. This is exactly the problem.* The fake parts kept being the best parts.

"You're good at this," she said.

"At cake?"

"At this." She gestured with her fork at the whole evening, the whole week, the whole fiction. "The hand on my back. The oyster acceptance. You've got the boyfriend thing down cold."

"Years of research," I said. "I read a lot of novels."

"Bad ones?"

"Only the bad ones. The good ones make you want things."

She looked at me then, a beat too long, lemon cake on her fork halfway to her mouth, and the kitchen got very quiet in the way kitchens do when two people notice the same silence at the same time. Outside, the party hummed. The sea did its sea thing against the rocks. And I stood there doing arithmetic I didn't like: *six days, one lie, zero exits.*

"You've got frosting," she said, "right—" and reached up, and instead of wiping it away she just touched the corner of my mouth with her thumb, barely, and then didn't move her hand, and neither did I, and the silence went from quiet to loud.

I should tell you what I was thinking, since I'm the narrator and honesty is the bit. I was thinking: *this is a bad idea.* I was also thinking: *her grandmother's ring is on her finger and it's fake and none of it is real and I have never in my life wanted anything to be real this badly.* Those two thoughts were having a knife fight, and I was losing.

"Jonah," she said. Not a question. A warning label.

"Yeah," I said, which was not a warning label, it was a surrender.

She kissed me first. Or I kissed her first. The historical record is disputed and both parties are unreliable. What I can confirm is that the fork went down on the counter with a clink that sounded, in the dark, like a starting gun, and that her hand came up to my jaw and my hand found the counter behind her because my knees had filed a formal complaint, and that the kiss was not a rehearsal-dinner kiss, not a perform-for-the-aunts kiss. It was the kind of kiss that has an argument in it. Six days of almosts and doorways and hands-on-backs, all of it cashed in at once, plus interest.

When we broke apart she was breathing like she'd run the cliff path and looking at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere.

"Well," she said.

"Well," I agreed.

"That was—" She stopped. I watched her do it, watched her reach for the safe word the way I'd watched her reach for it a dozen times this week, watched her build the little past-tense box to put the thing in so it couldn't hurt her. I knew the trick. I'd invented half of it.

"Part of the act," I said, giving her the line, because I was a coward and also because I wanted to see if she'd take it.

"Obviously," she said. "Method acting. Very committed. You'll get a good review."

"Five stars. *Convincing chemistry between leads.*"

"*Suspiciously* convincing," she said, and laughed, and the laugh shook a little at the edges, and then she pushed off the counter and busied herself with the cake dome and the forks and the whole small bureaucracy of not-talking-about-it, and I let her, because I am a coward, I believe I mentioned that.

We went back out to the party separately, two minutes apart, like professionals.

* * *

The guest room Maren had put me in used to belong to Elodie's father. I knew this because everything in this house had a provenance and Maren delivered it like museum placards. *That was David's bed. That was David's desk. David used to leave his boots on the stairs and I never once got angry about it, and now I'd pay money to trip over them.* I had been sleeping under a dead man's ceiling for a week and trying not to think about what it meant that the family had handed their ghost's room to a stranger because he was playing boyfriend to their daughter, and what it meant that it had felt, horribly, like being *handed something.*

I sat on the bed with my notebook—the real one, the one that lived in the inside pocket of my jacket, the one Elodie had never seen—and read back through the week's notes by the light of my phone.

Most of it was garbage, honestly. *E. laughs with whole face. E. hates being watched eating cake. E. said "past tense" like a life sentence.* Notes like a man keeping a diary and calling it journalism. I flipped past them, past the ferry schedules and the harbor authority org chart I'd sketched from the website, and there it was, the way it always was, patient as a tide:
