## 14. The Middle of It

The morning after Jonah walked out of the boatyard office without looking back, Hazel woke at four a.m. to the sound of gulls arguing over the harbor and lay in the rented room above the chandlery with her eyes open, watching the ceiling turn from black to gray to the pale, apologetic white of a coastal dawn.

She had cried. She would admit that much to herself, privately, the way one admits to small crimes — she had cried in the shower where the water could take credit for it, and she had cried once more into the flat pillow that smelled of someone else's detergent, and then she had gotten up and made coffee in a kitchenette that rattled like a boat engine and sat down at the little table by the window with her laptop open and nothing, absolutely nothing, to say.

This was the thing no one told you about grief's second act: it did not arrive with the same operatic force as the first. Eleven years ago, leaving Harbor Bay, she had been drowning — full-bodied, cinematic sorrow, the kind that soundtracks well. This was quieter and worse. This was the sorrow of a woman who had finally handed over the whole truth of herself and been met with a closed door. Not slammed. Jonah Reyes didn't slam doors. He simply closed this one the way he closed everything — carefully, deliberately, with the terrible gentleness of a man who had learned that anger was a luxury he couldn't afford, not with a brother on the inside and a case to fight.

*You should have told me eleven years ago,* he'd said. And then, worse, softer: *I don't know which version of you I've been working beside.*

She had stood in the doorway of his office while he turned back to his desk, back to her, shoulders squared against her like weather, and she had understood that there was nothing she could say that wouldn't sound like an application for a pardon. So she'd left. She'd walked down the dock past boats with names like second chances — *Second Wind*, *Grace Note*, *Done Runnin'* — and each one had felt like a joke at her expense.

Now, in the gray light, she opened her laptop anyway. Not to write. To work.

Because here was what the night had given her instead of sleep: a thought, arriving with the cold clarity of a fish hook through water. The letter had not found her by accident.

Eleven years ago she had written that letter and sealed it and carried it around for three days like a tumor, and then she had destroyed it. She was nearly certain. She remembered the match, the sink in her father's house in Westfield, the way the paper curled and went orange at the edges before she let it drop into the water. She remembered being surprised by how little ceremony it required. A life's great renunciation, accomplished in ninety seconds between dinner and dishes.

Except it hadn't been destroyed. It had surfaced — intact, unsent, postmarked nowhere because it had never traveled — in the estate of a dead man named Albert Voss, retired harbormaster of Harbor Bay, a man she had never met, whose lawyer had tracked her down through a return address she barely remembered using. Someone had kept that letter for eleven years. Someone had kept it safe through moves and decades and the slow erosion of everything else, and then, precisely now — now, when Margot Ashford's memoir was gathering steam, when Tomás Reyes's appeal window was narrowing, when Hazel Montrose had finally, after more than a decade, set foot back in this town — someone had arranged for it to land in her hands like a stone thrown through a window.

You didn't keep a letter for eleven years by accident. And you didn't release it at exactly this moment by chance.

Hazel set down her coffee and looked out at the harbor, at the water going silver and pink, and felt something shift in her chest that was not quite hope and not quite fear but had the structure of both: the feeling of a hunted animal realizing it has teeth.

* * *

By nine she was at the Harbor Bay public library, which had been renovated since her summer here — new glass front, banks of computers, a coffee kiosk where the microfiche cabinets used to be — but still smelled, underneath the fresh paint, of paper and dust and the particular salt-damp rot that no amount of funding ever fully cured. The librarian, a young woman with a nose ring who looked about nineteen, pointed her toward the local archives without asking why, which Hazel appreciated. In a town like this, questions were currency, and she was suddenly very conscious of how hers might be spent.

Estate records were public. Probate filings were public. She had ghostwritten enough legal memoirs to know exactly how boring and how revealing those documents could be.

Albert Voss. Retired harbormaster, thirty-one years of service. Died in February, age eighty-four, of congestive heart failure, at the Seaside Manor care facility. Estate modest: a house on Larkspur Lane, a fishing boat sold years ago, savings, some property in the hills. No spouse listed; a daughter, predeceased. The estate's executor was not family but a law firm — Whitcomb & Associates, of Rockport — retained, according to the filing, "at the direction of the decedent, per written instructions dated 2019."

Written instructions. Five years before his death, Voss had gone to a lawyer and put his affairs in order with unusual specificity. Hazel read the probate summary twice, then photographed every page with her phone, then sat back and stared at the screen of her laptop until it dimmed and she had to touch it to wake it up.

Here was what bothered her, what scratched at her like a splinter under the skin: the letter had been found, per the estate lawyer's phone call, "among the decedent's personal papers." Not in a safe-deposit box. Not in a sealed envelope marked with instructions. Among papers — loose, ordinary, filed or unfiled, the kind of thing an executor sorts through in cardboard boxes. Which meant either Voss had kept it casually for eleven years, mixed in with tide tables and fishing licenses, or someone had placed it there.

And if someone had placed it there — when? Before his death, with his knowledge? After it, without?

She thought about the phone call from the estate lawyer, Mr. Pell of Whitcomb & Associates. He had been courteous, faintly puzzled, professionally neutral. *We found correspondence bearing your name and handwriting among the personal effects. Given the sensitive nature of some of the other materials in the collection, we felt it prudent to contact you directly.* Sensitive nature. She had been too flooded with dread to hear it then. Now the phrase rang like a bell.

What else was in that collection?

She called Pell from the bench outside the library, in the sun, because sunlight made lies easier and she intended to lie. "Ms. Montrose, thank you for coming up so quickly," Pell said. He sounded tired, the tiredness of a small-town lawyer handling an estate bigger than his usual fare. "I assume you've had time to review the letter."

"I have." Her voice held. Eleven years of practice holding her voice. "Mr. Pell, I want to ask you something delicate, and I'd ask you to be equally delicate in answering. When you said the materials were sensitive — what did you mean, specifically?"

A pause. Papers shuffling. "The harbormaster's office maintained extensive records, as you can imagine. Logbooks, incident reports, correspondence with the state marine patrol, the D.A.'s office over the years. Mr. Voss appears to have kept copies of a good deal of it after his retirement. Some of it touches on matters that were... litigated." Another pause, longer. "Matters I understand you may have some familiarity with."

"Who else has asked about the collection?"

"Ah." The shuffle stopped. "That's an unusual question."

"It's an unusual situation. My handwriting is sitting in a dead man's boxes, Mr. Pell. I'd like to know who else has been reading my mail."

Another silence, this one calculating. She could almost hear him deciding how much a stranger was owed. "There have been two inquiries," he said finally. "One from a researcher — a woman, claimed to be doing historical work on the harbor for a publication. She provided credentials I'm still verifying." His tone cooled a degree. "And one gentleman who declined to give a name at all. He telephoned twice. I did not provide access to either party, for the record. The materials are pending cataloguing, and frankly, some of what's in there makes me want my own counsel present before anyone sees it."

"Describe the woman."

"Forties, maybe. Well-dressed. Very polished accent — New York, I'd guess. Dark hair, striking. She knew the harbormaster's service dates without being told." A beat. "Why does that matter?"

"Because Albert Voss retired in 2014," Hazel said slowly, "and the only people who memorize a dead harbormaster's service dates are people who needed to know exactly what years he was on duty." Exactly the year Tomás Reyes was arrested. Exactly the summer Hazel had spent here, and left, and buried.

"Ms. Montrose," Pell said, and now his voice had changed entirely, the professional neutrality dropped like a coat, "may I ask you plainly what's going on? Because I've practiced law in Rockport for twenty-two years, and I know when a file is radioactive. This file is radioactive. And you're the third person in two weeks to come sniffing at it."

Hazel looked out at the street, at the tourists drifting past the bait shop, at a child chasing gulls across the parking lot, and made a decision that felt less like courage than like falling.

"Can we meet?" she said. "Not on the phone. Today. And Mr. Pell — don't catalogue anything else. Don't let anyone else see anything. If someone comes back, call me before you call anyone."

"Why would I do that?"

"Because whoever sent me that letter wanted me here," she said. "And I intend to find out who, and why, and what they think I'm going to do about it. You can either be the man who helped, or the man who got used. Your choice."

Another silence. Then, quietly: "Four o'clock. My office. Come alone."

* * *

She had three hours to kill and a town she was trying not to feel, so she did what she always did with feelings too large to hold: she worked. Back in her room, she spread her notes across the bed like a general's maps — the timeline of the letter, the probate documents, the two inquiries, the dates. And she forced herself to do the thing she had been avoiding since the letter arrived, the thing that had been sitting in her peripheral vision like a figure in a dark window.

She made a list of everyone who could have known about the letter. Everyone who could have kept it. Everyone who could have wanted her in Harbor Bay, now, specifically.

Her father: possible, but wrong. Edmund Montrose did not pull threads; he cut them. If her father had wanted her back here, he'd have summoned her to Westfield for Sunday dinner and dismantled her resolve over roast chicken. He would never risk a piece of paper existing, let alone circulate one.

Jonah: no. He hadn't known the letter existed until she told him, and his face at that moment — she replayed it now, the furrow between his brows, the genuine bafflement — had not been performance. Jonah lied badly. It was one of the things she had loved about him and one of the things that had made lying to him feel like a special category of sin.

Tomás: couldn't have. Eleven years inside, no access to a retired harbormaster's private papers, and no motive to summon Hazel rather than simply write to her — he wrote to Jonah regularly; she'd seen the letters stacked in the boatyard office.

Margot Ashford.

Hazel sat with the name the way you sit with a diagnosis. Because it fit. God help her, it fit beautifully. Margot collected stories — Hazel had known that from the first week of working for her, had watched her hoard anecdotes the way other women hoarded jewelry, had heard her say, over wine in the Connecticut garden, *People are just material that hasn't learned what it is yet.* Margot had read every word of the memoir draft Hazel had written, including the chapters Hazel believed she'd scrubbed clean — and Margot Ashford did not become Margot Ashford by missing things. She had recognized Harbor Bay in Hazel's prose; Hazel had seen it happen, that flicker in her eyes over the manuscript pages, the stillness of a hunter hearing a twig snap. And Margot had arrived in town within days of Hazel, announcing coastal research, charming the diner and the chamber of commerce, cornering Hazel with velvet threats about scandal and sales.

But the letter itself — that was the problem. Margot couldn't have had the letter. Unless.

Unless the woman at Pell's office wasn't Margot but someone working for her. Unless Margot had found Voss first — months ago, during her research, a novelist sniffing around a harbormaster's archive for texture — and found the letter, and recognized the handwriting, because of course she would, she'd read Hazel's hand on a thousand manuscript pages. And what would Margot Ashford do with a love letter written by her own ghostwriter, a letter proving that the anonymous woman shaping her memoir had a secret history with a wrongful conviction?

She'd use it. Not crudely — Margot was never crude — but precisely. Send it home. Watch what the ghostwriter did. Because a story with living stakes was worth ten times a story from files, and Margot needed this book to be enormous. Her last novel had underperformed; her publisher was nervous; her advance had been structured around bestseller numbers. A memoir was already a gamble. A memoir with a buried crime in its margins, with a real convicted man and a real cover-up and a ghostwriter with a guilty conscience — that was not a gamble. That was a machine.

And the businessman. She turned that card over too, reluctantly. Charles Whitmore — no relation to the lawyers, the coincidence of names had struck her even in passing — the respected local developer whose money was quietly bankrolling the memoir's promotional apparatus, whose family name appeared, she now realized with a chill that started in her scalp, in the margins of her own research notes about the old arrest. Whitmore Marine Supply. Whitmore's father had been on the harbor commission the summer Tomás Reyes went to prison. She had noted it weeks ago and filed it away as background color, the way she filed away everything, the way she had built a whole career on noticing things and refusing to be noticed by them.

The pieces weren't arranged yet. But they were all on the table, and for the first time in eleven years, Hazel Montrose was not the piece being moved.

She showered, dressed in the armor of a good blazer, and looked at herself in the mirror over the sink — a woman with red-rimmed eyes and a set jaw — and said aloud, to the stranger there, "Whoever you are, you made a mistake. You didn't bring me back to be used. You brought me back to finish it."

* * *

Pell's office was above the hardware store in Rockport, up a staircase that smelled of varnish, in a suite with wood paneling gone amber with age and a view of nothing but a parking lot and, beyond it, a sliver of gray sea. Pell himself was sixty-ish, rumpled, kind-eyed behind bifocals, with the handshake of a man who still meant it. He cleared his desk of everything but a single cardboard box and a legal pad, and he did not offer her coffee, which she took as a sign of seriousness.

"Before we start," he said, "I need to know if I'm about to hear something that requires me to stop being your friend and start being an officer of the court. Because I like you, Ms. Montrose, and I'd rather stay your friend."

"Then let me tell you the part that doesn't require anything of you," Hazel said, and she told him — not everything, not her father, not the confession she'd made to Jonah with her heart in her hands — but the shape of it. That the letter was hers. That she had written it the summer a local man named Tomás Reyes was convicted of a crime she had reason to believe he didn't commit. That her presence in this town was not sentimental tourism. That someone had engineered her return, and she wanted to know who.

Pell listened without interrupting, which she suspected was rare for him, and when she finished he removed his glasses and rubbed the bridge of his nose for a long moment.

"You know what the strangest thing is?" he said. "Albert Voss told me almost none of this himself. He gave me instructions — detailed ones, five years ago — and he made me promise that certain materials would go to 'the person who asks the right question.'" He looked at her over the box. "I asked him what question. He said, 'They'll know.'"

Hazel's mouth went dry. "He expected someone."

"He expected *you*, I think. Or someone like you. He said — I wrote it down afterward, I still have my notes — he said, 'There's a girl who'll come back someday. They always come back. Harbors see to it.'" Pell slid the box an inch toward her, then stopped it with his palm. "But here's what I can't reconcile, and why I called you instead of just mailing you the damn letter like a normal executor. The letter was on top. Deliberately, neatly, on top — folded once, face-up, as if placed for finding. Everything beneath it is chaos. Forty years of a careful man's records, dumped in like laundry. Except for one folder, which is missing."

"Missing."

"Gone. There's a gap in the sequence — I can show you the inventory Voss himself prepared, item forty-one through forty-six, logbook excerpts from June through September of the relevant year. Not in the box. Not in his house. Not in the storage unit. I've looked everywhere twice." Pell's eyes, behind the bifocals, were sharp and troubled. "Either Albert Voss removed them himself and hid them somewhere only he knew — and he died without telling a soul — or somebody got to them between his death and mine. And Ms. Montrose, the woman who came asking? She asked about exactly that date range. Nothing else. Just the summer."

Outside, the gulls wheeled over the parking lot, screaming their one note. Hazel thought of the torn logbook in the storage locker she and Jonah had opened — pages cut cleanly away, the wound of it — and felt the two facts click together like bones setting.

Someone had the pages. Someone had had them long enough to cut them free. And someone — perhaps the same someone, perhaps another — had wanted Hazel here, holding the letter, asking the right question, standing in Albert Voss's harbor eleven years late.

"They always come back," she repeated softly. "Harbors see to it."

Pell pushed the box toward her, all the way this time. "Item forty-one through forty-six," he said. "Start telling me what I'm missing. And then, Ms. Montrose, you're going to tell me who else knows you're here — because if half of what you've said is true, the next person through my door won't be asking politely."

Hazel reached into the box, lifted out the top layer of a dead man's careful chaos, and began.
