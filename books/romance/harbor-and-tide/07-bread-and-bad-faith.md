## 7. Bread and Bad Faith

The last loaf of the day came out of the oven at four-fifteen, and by five I was already turning the sign to CLOSED, which was a lie, because nobody had come in since two. The harbor fog had rolled in thick and gray, pressing against the windows like something that wanted in but couldn't remember how doors worked. I wiped down the counter for the third time. It wasn't dirty. Wiping things that aren't dirty is what you do when your hands need an excuse not to think.

I'd been thinking anyway. About Elias Vane's hands, how they moved when he talked about his wife — Elena, he'd said her name was, though Marisol called her Mamá and once, in a flash of temper outside the estate office, *my mother* with a weight that made the air change. About the way Elias's stories were too smooth, like river stones, every rough edge worn away by telling them too many times. And about the second notebook I'd started keeping, the one I told myself was just for "texture," just for "background," but which was really full of everything Elias would hate me writing down.

I was stacking chairs when the bell over the door rang — the bell I'd been meaning to oil because it shrieked like something dying, and which tonight sounded almost glad.

Marisol Vane stood in the doorway, rain-damp, city coat, arms crossed like she was defending a border.

"We're closed," I said.

"I know." She looked at the sign as if it had personally offended her. "I watched you flip it. I waited until you did."

"That's either very honest or very rude."

"Both, probably." She came in anyway, letting the door swing shut behind her, and the fog curled in after her and then seemed to reconsider. She looked around the bakery the way she'd looked at me the first time we met — through the estate office doorway, catching me elbow-deep in her father's filing cabinets — like she was cataloguing my weaknesses for later use. "You're still open enough. You have bread."

"Some bread. Mostly the ugly loaves nobody wanted."

"Those are the best ones." She said it with such certainty that I found myself believing it. "The pretty ones are for other people. The ugly ones are true."

I didn't have an answer for that, so I pulled the day's survivors from under the cloth: two sourdoughs with split tops, a rye gone dense at one end, a boule shaped like an argument. Marisol picked up the argument-shaped one and turned it over in her hands like she was assessing it for flaws she could name out loud.

"Do you always let strangers into your shop after hours?" she asked.

"You're not a stranger. You're a source."

Something flickered across her face — not quite hurt, not quite anger. Something more tired than either. "Right. Of course. Everything's material." She set the boule down harder than necessary. "Do you want to know why I'm here, ghostwriter? Or do you want to take notes first?"

I should have taken notes. That was the whole problem with me; I could feel it even then, the old reflex rising, the part of me that heard pain and thought *chapter six*. Instead I put the notebook back under the counter where it lived, and I saw her notice me doing it, and I saw her decide — provisionally, suspiciously — to allow it.

"No notes," I said. "Bread?"

* * *

We ate standing up, because I only had two stools and one of them wobbled in a way that felt like a metaphor I refused to examine. Marisol tore pieces off the sourdough instead of cutting slices, which struck me as the kind of habit you learn young and never unlearn, eating like someone might take the food away.

"My mother used to bake," she said, apropos of nothing, or maybe apropos of everything. "Not well. She burned things constantly. My father kept a running joke about the smoke detector being the family dinner bell." Her jaw tightened around the word *father* like it was a pit she'd bitten into. "He tells that story now, you know. In his little recorded voice, all warm and nostalgic. Like it's charming. Like she was a character in *his* memoir instead of a person who existed."

"He told me that story," I admitted. "Tuesday. He laughed when he got to the smoke detector part."

"Did he tell you what happened the last time she burned something?"

I tore my own piece of crust off, slowly. "No."

"She'd tried to make him an anniversary cake. His favorite — almond, orange zest, the whole production. She'd been planning it for weeks, hiding recipe clippings in her purse like contraband. And she burned it. Badly. Black on the bottom, raw in the middle, a disaster." Marisol's voice had gone flat and careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something heavy across ice. "And he didn't laugh. He looked at that cake and he said —" she stopped, breathed, started again — "he said, *I married a woman who can't do anything right.* In front of me. I was eleven. And my mother put down the cake knife and walked out to the orchard and didn't come back inside until dark, and something in our house broke that night that never got fixed. That's the real smoke detector story. But it doesn't rhyme, so he cut it."

The fog pressed against the glass. Somewhere out on the water a buoy groaned, low and patient, like the harbor itself clearing its throat.

"He's dying, you know," Marisol said, when I didn't say anything. "Did he tell you that? Not in so many words. He'll never say it plainly. But I've seen the pill bottles. I've done the arithmetic on his appointments. He's writing this memoir because he wants to get his version nailed down before—" she waved a hand, dismissive and grief-stricken at once— "before the orchard gets the last word instead of him."

"And you think that makes it worse," I said. "That he's editing history on a deadline."

"I think it makes it *him*." She brushed crumbs from her palms with sharp little strokes. "So tell me, Noor Amberdale. You've spent more hours with him than I have in ten years. You've heard the polished anecdotes, the greatest hits of Elias Vane, Loving Father and Devoted Husband." Her eyes came up and fixed on mine, and there was nothing soft in them at all. "Does he deserve redemption?"

It was the question I'd been circling for days without landing, the one that sat in my chest like a stone swallowed wrong. Because here was the thing I couldn't say out loud: I liked him. God help me, in the sessions, with the recorder running, I sometimes forgot to be a professional and just *listened*, and Elias Vane was easy to listen to — funny in a dry way, self-aware in flashes, capable of describing a frost-killed blossom with such tenderness that I'd caught myself wanting to forgive him things I hadn't yet confirmed he'd done.

"He's a man," I said finally, "who tells beautiful stories about terrible moments. And I think some part of him believes that if the story is beautiful enough, the moment stops counting."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have. Redemption isn't mine to give, Marisol. I write things down. I don't absolve anybody."

"But you're helping him do it." She leaned forward, elbows on the counter, close enough that I could smell rain and cold air and something underneath that was just her. "Every polished chapter, every smoothed-over anecdote — you're the one sanding the edges. You're the craftsman of his forgiveness. So don't stand there telling me you're neutral. Nobody who writes someone else's redemption is neutral. You're either building the cathedral or you're complicit in the fraud."

The words landed harder than she probably intended, or maybe exactly as hard as she intended, because Marisol Vane did not seem like a woman who missed her targets. I thought of the false-smooth chapters in my bag. I thought of the second notebook. I thought of the eviction notice still folded in my jacket pocket, soft at the creases from handling.

"You want me to sabotage him," I said. "Write it cruel. Make him the villain in his own book."

"I want you to write it *true*." For the first time her voice cracked, just slightly, a hairline fracture in all that armor. "There's a difference. Cruelty is easy. Truth is —" she gestured vaguely at the ruined loaves between us — "it's this. Ugly and dense and nobody buys it, but at least it's real bread."

I laughed before I could stop myself — a short, startled sound, the kind that escapes rather than gets released. Marisol blinked at me like I'd spoken a foreign language.

"What?"

"'At least it's real bread.' You sound like a fortune cookie raised by wolves."

"I sound like someone whose mother burned cakes and whose father wrote poems about it afterward instead of apologizing." But her mouth was doing something at the corner, fighting gravity, losing slowly. "Don't make me laugh. I came here to be angry at you."

"Why are you angry at me? I'm the innocent bystander in this family war."

"You went through my mother's filing cabinets."

"I was looking for the deed clause—"

"In my *dead mother's* cabinets." She picked up a chunk of rye and gestured with it like a prosecutor. "Day one. Before you'd even said hello to me properly. What kind of person does that?"

"The desperate kind," I said, and the honesty surprised us both. I hadn't meant to say it out loud. "I'm behind on my lease. This bakery —" I looked around at it, at the fog-silvered windows, the flour-dusted shelves, the wobbling stool, all of it suddenly visible as what it was: a small, failing thing I loved — "this bakery is the only thing I've ever built that has my actual name on it. Everything else I've ever made belongs to somebody else. Their names on the covers, their stories in their mouths, my hands invisible. So yes. I went through the cabinets. I'd go through worse. Desperation makes people honest in ways politeness never will."

Marisol was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the buoy groaned again, and the fog shifted, and for a second the harbor light caught the window and threw a pale gold stripe across the counter, across the broken bread, across her hands.

"My landlord gave me three months," I added, quieter. "Your father's deadline and my lease end the same week. Isn't that a funny coincidence."

"It's not a coincidence," Marisol said. "Nothing around my father ever is."

* * *

We ate the rest of the rye in something that wasn't quite silence — the comfortable kind of quiet that happens when two people have stopped performing at each other and started simply existing in the same room. She told me, without meaning to, that she'd studied architecture and abandoned it. I told her, without meaning to, that I'd written a novel once, years ago, that lived in a drawer and had never seen daylight. Neither of us asked follow-up questions. There's a courtesy in that, a kind of mutual disarmament: *I showed you mine, I won't ask where you keep yours.*

When she reached for her coat, I expected the walls to come back up. They started to — I watched it happen, the shoulders squaring, the face reassembling itself into its public architecture — and then she paused at the door, hand on the frame, fog swirling behind her like a curtain half-drawn.

"The cake," she said. "The burned anniversary cake. After my mother died, I found the recipe clippings in her purse. Still there. She'd never gotten to use them again." Her thumb moved along the doorframe, back and forth, a small restless motion. "If you're going to write about my family, Noor — and you are, whatever you call yourself while you do it — then write about the clippings. Write about the woman who kept hoping long after hope was stupid. Don't write about the smoke detector."

Then she was gone, and the bell shrieked behind her, and the fog closed over the doorway like water over a stone.

I stood in my empty bakery holding a heel of rye, feeling like I'd been handed something fragile and told not to look at it directly. Warmth, I thought. That was warmth. And warmth, in my experience, was the most dangerous thing a source could offer you, because it made you want to protect them instead of use them, and protecting people didn't pay leases.

I distrusted it immediately and completely. I distrusted the way I was already replaying her voice. I distrusted the second notebook sitting under the counter, which had just become something else entirely — not ammunition anymore, but evidence, and I couldn't yet say evidence of what, or against whom.

I washed the boards. I banked the ovens. And when I finally climbed the stairs to my apartment above the shop, I took out the manuscript pages — Elias's smooth, beautiful, lying-adjacent chapters — and read the smoke detector anecdote again, and this time I heard what was underneath it, and my pen hovered over the margin for a long time before I wrote a single word:

*Ask him about the cake.*

Outside, the tide was coming in, patient and inevitable, dragging the past back up the harbor whether anyone had invited it or not.
