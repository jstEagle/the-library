## 11. Pruning Season

The saws came out of the barn on a morning so clear it felt accusatory.

Elias had woken before dawn, as he always did now, and stood at the kitchen window watching the light come up over the lower field like a slow confession. The frost had come early this year — mid-October, weeks ahead of itself — and it lay on the orchard in a white skin that would burn off by nine and leave everything looking briefly, cruelly alive. From the window he could see which trees were dead and which were only pretending. Forty years had taught him the difference, though lately he wondered if he'd ever known anything at all, or if he'd simply been very good at naming things after himself.

Marisol's car was already in the drive.

She had arrived at seven, an hour earlier than their agreed time, and she was standing at the gate when he came out with the first saw over his shoulder, wearing work gloves that were clearly new — tags still on them, he could see the little white loops of plastic at her wrists — and an expression he recognized from photographs of her mother: the look of a woman preparing to be right about something.

"You're early," he said.

"So are you."

"The deed says joint decision." He set the saw down on the tailgate of the truck, next to hers, and the two tools lay side by side like arguments waiting their turn. "I'm honoring it."

"You're *performing* it," she said, but without heat, and he thought — not for the first time since the letters, since the gate, since the morning he had broken open like a dropped jar and told her the truth about why he was writing the damn book — that she sounded less like a woman fighting him than like a woman tired of fighting him, which was worse, or better, he couldn't decide.

They walked into the rows together.

* * *

The orchard in frost was a country Elias had been governing badly for a decade. Three hundred trees, or what remained of three hundred; fire blight had taken the east block whole, canker had hollowed the oldest MacIntoshes from the inside out, and the ones still standing along the harbor fence had gone wild and tall, reaching for light they no longer knew how to use. A working orchard was supposed to be kept short and open, pruned into humility, every branch answerable to the sun. His had become a forest of grievances. He walked it the way he imagined a man walks through a house where his marriage ended — knowing exactly which room to avoid, avoiding it anyway, every day.

"Start here," Marisol said, stopping at the third row.

It was the row her mother had planted. Elena had put those trees in herself the spring Marisol turned six — twenty Jonagolds, bought at a discount because the nursery had mislabeled half of them, and Elena had laughed about that for years, said they were like her: sold under the wrong name but bearing good fruit anyway. Elias had not walked that row deliberately in eleven years. His feet took him there now because his daughter's feet did, and he understood that this was how the day would go: her leading, him following, the deed giving her the right to drag him through every room he'd boarded shut.

"This one's dead," she said, laying her gloved hand against the trunk of the nearest tree. Beneath the frost its bark had gone gray and loose, slipping off the wood beneath like old wallpaper. She scraped it with her thumbnail and the wood underneath was dry, brown, silent. "And this one." She moved down the row, touching each tree in turn, quick and clinical, a doctor doing rounds. "Dead. Alive — barely. Dead. This one I don't know."

"That one you don't know is a Jonagold your mother grafted herself," Elias heard himself say. "Off the rootstock at the far end. She used wax from the kitchen candles. It took, against all odds. Your grandmother said it wouldn't, and Elena did it anyway to prove her wrong."

Marisol went still.

He hadn't meant to say it. The information had lived in him for thirty years like a coin in a couch, and it fell out of his mouth now with no ceremony at all, and he watched his daughter stand in front of a scraggly half-dead tree and see, apparently for the first time, her mother's hands on it.

"She never told me that," Marisol said quietly.

"There's a lot she never told you." He meant it kindly. It did not land kindly. Nothing landed kindly between them yet; kindness was a language they were both relearning with accents.

"No," Marisol said. "There's a lot *you* never told me. About her. About any of it." She pulled the tag off her glove, finally, and stuffed it in her pocket. "So we're going to do this properly. Every tree. You tell me what you know, I decide what comes down, and you don't get to edit."

"I'm the one who taught you how to prune."

"You're the one who left the orchard to die." She picked up her saw. "Both things are true. Let's start."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the frost burned off, and the orchard gave up its dead one by one.

It was brutal work and neither of them pretended otherwise. The dead trees came down in stages — first the limbs, cut back to the trunk so the fall wouldn't tear open the neighbors' roots, then the trunk itself, notched and felled with a groan that Elias felt in his teeth every single time, three hundred times a season for forty years and it had never once stopped sounding to him like an execution. The stumps they'd grind later, or dig, or leave to rot honestly in the ground, which was what he'd done for ten years, letting the orchard keep its dead above ground where he could visit them.

Marisol worked like someone making up for lost time. She was forty-one and had spent twenty of those years in cities, and it showed in her hands, which blistered inside an hour, and not at all in her judgment, which was — he hated to admit it, admitted it anyway, silently, into the smell of cut wood — impeccable. She read a tree faster than he did. She saw the blight in the leaf scars before he saw it in the bark. When she wasn't sure, she asked, and when she asked, she actually listened to the answer, which was more than he had ever managed with anyone.

By noon they had cleared eleven trees from Elena's row, leaving four standing, including the candle-wax graft, which Marisol refused on sight.

"It stays," she said. "Whatever else happens."

"It's half cankered. Look at the crotch of the lower limb —"

"*It stays.*"

He looked at her face and shut his mouth and marked the tree with surveyor's tape instead, blue ribbon snapping in the wind off the water, and thought: *she has your stubbornness, Elena, and my hands, and God help everyone.*

They ate lunch sitting on the tailgate, backs against the felled trunks, sharing bread Marisol had brought from town — sourdough, dense and dark-crusted, from the bakery below the harbor, the ghostwriter's bakery. Elias chewed and said nothing about the fact that he knew where it came from, and Marisol said nothing about the fact that she knew he knew, and the bread sat between them like a third person who understood more than either of them.

"The woman who writes," Marisol said eventually. "Noor. She's been coming to the sessions."

"She has."

"What do you tell her?"

"Everything," Elias said. "That was the deal, after the letters. After the gate. The book gets rewritten or it doesn't come out. She types what I actually say."

"And do you actually say it?" Marisol turned the bread crust over in her fingers. "Or do you perform? Because I've watched you perform my whole life, Dad. You have a voice for boardrooms and a voice for funerals and a voice for me, and none of them are your real one. I didn't know you had a real one until I read her letters and realized she'd been getting it all along, in writing, for years, while the rest of us got the broadcast."

Elias looked down the row they'd cleared. Eleven stumps, pale and raw as bone, catching the afternoon light. It looked like a wound. It looked, also, for the first time in a decade, like a beginning.

"She used to say I narrated instead of lived," he said. "Your mother. She said I'd rather tell the story of a thing than be in it, because in the story I got to decide what happened." He wiped his hands on his thighs. "I told myself the memoir was different. That this time I was telling the truth. But I started it the same way I've started everything — by deciding the ending first and working backward."

"And what ending did you decide?"

"That I was a hard man who loved difficultly, and that the people around me should have tried harder to understand that." He said it flatly, without self-pity, because self-pity was the one performance even he had grown sick of. "Noor looked at me across that desk last month and said, *Mr. Vane, that's not a memoir, that's a press release with a body count,* and she was right, and I fired her for an afternoon and rehired her the next morning because she was the first person in twenty years who'd told me the truth to my face and then shown up again anyway."

Marisol made a sound that might have been a laugh. "She showed up at my door too. First week I was here. Snooping through the estate office like a burglar with a tote bag."

"I know. She told me."

"She *told* you?"

"We've reached an arrangement about honesty. It's new. We're all bad at it." He broke off a piece of crust. "She's better at it than either of us, if it comes to that. Which is a strange thing to say about a woman whose entire profession is putting words in other people's mouths."

Something eased between them then. He felt it happen physically, like a knot in his shoulder letting go — the two of them sitting in the wreckage of Elena's row, eating a stranger's bread, talking about a stranger who had somehow become the only person either of them spoke to honestly. Marisol described the flood, the boat, the box of letters surfacing out of the drowned field like the past refusing to stay buried. Elias described the recorder, the dictation, the terrible discipline of saying sentences aloud that he'd spent eleven years not saying. They compared notes like survivors of the same shipwreck who'd washed up on opposite shores.

And then, because ease is the most dangerous weather there is, because tenderness is the thing his hands had always ruined first, Elias opened his mouth and destroyed it.

* * *

It happened over the last tree of the day.

They had saved it — the big Baldwin at the head of the row, the largest tree on the property, planted by his grandfather, dead these five years but too massive to admit it, half its crown still throwing leaves every spring out of pure institutional habit. It would take chainsaws and a rigging rope and probably a professional. Marisol circled it twice, hand on bark, and said, "This one hurts you. I can tell. Why keep it standing so long?"

"Because your mother used to sit under it," Elias said. "Every September. She'd bring a book and a thermos and she'd sit under this tree for hours and I'd watch her from the house and think, *there, that's the whole point of everything, right there, the woman under the tree.*" He heard his own voice go strange. "I couldn't cut down the place where the point of everything used to sit."

Marisol's face changed. Softened. For one unguarded moment she looked six years old, and she stepped toward him — actually toward him, one step, her boot crunching on dead fruit — and said, "Dad—"

"She'd have hated this," he said, and heard the wrongness of his own tone a half-second too late, the old broadcast voice sliding back in, the narrator taking over from the man. "The state of it. The rot. She'd have walked these rows and wept, and then she'd have gone back to the city, Marisol. That's what she did. That's what she always did. She'd stand at the edge of a thing until it got hard, and then she'd leave, and leave me holding it — the orchard, the books, *you* — and call it freedom, and everyone called it freedom, and I was the only one who ever paid for it—"

He stopped.

Too late. The words were out, hanging in the cold air between them, and he watched them land on his daughter's face one at a time like blows.

Marisol stepped back. The softness left her eyes the way tide leaves a flat — fast, total, leaving everything exposed and stranded. "*Leave you holding me,*" she repeated. Her voice was very quiet. "Is that what you think she did. Left you holding me."

"Marisol—"

"Because here's what I remember." She was shaking, he could see it, her gloved hands trembling at her sides, and she did not stop. "I remember being eight years old and asking her why we didn't live at the orchard anymore, and her crying in the front seat of the car and telling me it was complicated, and it took me reading her *letters* — her private letters, the ones you kept in a box in a flooded field for eleven years — to learn that it was complicated because *you* told her that if she left, she left alone. That you'd fight her for custody. That you'd tell the town whatever version of her you needed to. She stayed in a marriage that was killing her for three extra years because you made her choose between her own life and her child, and you are standing in her orchard, cutting down her trees, telling me she *left us*—"

"Elena chose the city—"

"Elena chose *breathing!*" It tore out of her, raw, and somewhere down the row a crow lifted off a dead limb in alarm. "You want to know why I came to sell? Not the money. I came to sell because this place is the shape of everything you did to her, and I wanted it gone off the map. And then I read the letters, and I thought maybe — *maybe* — the man in them, the one she wrote about in the first years, the one who danced in the cider house and named a tree after our dog — maybe he was still in there somewhere under the version of you that won. And today I thought—" Her breath caught. She pressed the heel of her glove against her eyes for one second, one, and then her hand came down and her face was stone. "Today I thought we were getting somewhere. That's the cruelest thing you do, Dad. Not the cruelty itself. The almost."

"Marisol, please—"

"Don't." She picked up her saw, her gloves, the blue roll of surveyor's tape, moving with terrible efficiency, packing the day away like evidence. "The Baldwin comes down Monday. I'll hire the crew. You'll sign the work order because the deed says you have to, and that's the only reason you'll have left." She slung her bag over her shoulder and walked toward the truck, and stopped, and did not turn around.

"For the record," she said, "she sat under that tree because it was the only place on this property where she couldn't see the house."

She drove away. The taillights went down the lane and out the gate and the orchard stood around Elias in the failing light, twelve stumps raw as bone, one blue ribbon snapping on a dead tree, and the silence where his daughter had been.

He stood there a long time. Then he took out his phone to call Noor — to cancel the session, to postpone, to say nothing, just to hear a voice that belonged to neither the story nor the silence — and found a message waiting, timestamped an hour ago, from a number he didn't recognize.

*Mr. Vane — journalist, Coastal Review. I've received several chapters of your forthcoming memoir. I'd like your comment on the material concerning your late wife before we publish. Call me.*

He read it twice. The cold came up through his boots, off the ground, out of the graves of the trees.

Someone had the draft. Someone had the false chapter. And the first person his mind turned to, helplessly, horribly, was his daughter.
