## 14. Reading the Letters Aloud

The letters had been in my bag for three days before I could make myself carry them to the orchard.

I'd read them so many times the paper had gone soft at the folds, the ink blooming faintly where my thumb kept returning — *mi amor*, over and over, my mother's handwriting looping like something that wanted to fly and couldn't quite get off the ground. I knew them now the way you know a song you hated as a child and can't stop humming as an adult. I knew which letter was written in rain (the paper warped, the words bled), which one was written furious (the pen had torn through in four places), and which one — the last one, dated eleven days before she died — was written so calmly it frightened me more than all the others combined.

*Elias. You will tell this story your way whether I am here or not. So I am writing it down where you cannot reach it.*

She had known. That was the thing I couldn't stop circling. She had known he would build a monument out of his own version of her, and she had left these letters in a boat hull like a message against the tide, trusting the water to keep them better than any living person would have.

Three days of reading them in my apartment with the blinds down. Three days of Noor's voice on the phone saying, gently, *you don't owe him anything, Mari, but if you want to do this, do it while you still have the anger to hold the pages steady.* And then this morning, the anger had arrived on schedule, punctual as grief, and I put the letters in a canvas bag and drove out past the harbor, past the bakery where the lights were off because Noor was already up at the orchard doing whatever she did now — sitting with my father for hours, typing his fictions into permanence.

The gate was where it always was, iron gone soft with rust, hinges that sang the same two-note complaint they'd sung when I was nine years old and sneaking out to the creek. I stood in front of it longer than I meant to. The orchard rose behind it in its November shabbiness, rows of bare trees going gray at the tips, the blight having taken everything green weeks ago. It looked less like an orchard than like a congregation waiting for a verdict.

He was already there. Of course he was. Elias Vane walked those rows every morning like a man inspecting troops he no longer trusted, and today he was standing at the third row with his hands in his coat pockets, watching me come up the drive with the expression of a man who has been expecting bad weather and is trying to decide whether to be annoyed by it.

"You're not supposed to be here," he said. "Noor's session isn't until Thursday."

"I'm not here for a session."

"Then what—"

"I need you to listen." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Not talk. Not correct. Not perform. Listen. Can you do that? For once in your life, can you just stand there and let someone else finish?"

Something moved behind his face — the old flinch, the one that preceded every argument we'd ever had, the reflex that turned every conversation into a negotiation he intended to win. I watched him almost do it. Watched his mouth open around whatever deflection lived closest to his teeth.

And then he closed it.

Maybe it was the bag in my hand. Maybe it was the way I didn't come through the gate, just stood on the gravel side of it like a visitor, like someone who could still leave. Maybe it was that he was seventy-three years old and tired in a way that sleep doesn't touch. Whatever it was, he nodded once, small, and stepped back from the row, and folded his hands in front of him like a man at a funeral who isn't sure he's welcome.

I came through the gate. The hinges sang their two notes. The wind moved down the rows and set the bare branches whispering, and somewhere far off in the lower field a crow said something unkind and flew away.

I took out the first letter.

"This one is from March," I said. "Nineteen years ago. The spring after I left for college. She wrote it and never sent it. None of them were sent. Did you know that? She kept them in the blue boat, under the tarp, in the box with the rotted lid. The storm brought it up last week. The tide decided you'd hidden them long enough."

His jaw tightened. "Marisol—"

"*Listen.*" I held up one finger, and to my lasting astonishment, he went quiet again.

I unfolded the page. My mother's handwriting swam up at me, familiar as my own pulse.

*"Elias. You told the neighbors the trees failed because of the soil. You told them the same thing you tell yourself. But I was there the year you stopped pruning. I was there when you stopped grafting, when you let the suckers take over, and I know the difference between a tree that dies and a tree that is abandoned. You are doing to this orchard what you did to us — calling your absence a kind of grief, so that no one will call it what it is."*

I read it aloud slowly, letting each sentence land in the cold air between us. He stood very still. His hands, folded in front of him, had gone white at the knuckles.

"She wrote that?" he said finally, barely above the wind.

"She wrote forty-one of them. Over nine years. I'm going to read you all of them, or as many as you can stand, and you are going to stand there and hear them." I took out the next. "This one's from the summer the well went dry."

*"You sat at the kitchen table last night and told Marisol the orchard was dying of natural causes. She believed you. Children believe their fathers; it is the cruelest advantage you have. I am writing this down so that someday, if she ever asks me, I will not have to lie to her the way you lie to everyone, including yourself. The orchard is not dying of natural causes, Elias. Nothing dies of natural causes when a man stops watering it."*

A sound came out of him then — not a word, something smaller and older, the sound a house makes when it settles. His shoulders had begun to curve inward, the proud architecture of him giving way the way old barns give way, slowly and then all at once.

"The memoir," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word, which infuriated me, so I pushed through it. "The book you're building with Noor. I've heard the chapters. I know what you're putting in it. You're putting her in it as the reason. As the coldness. As the woman who 'withdrew,' who 'became distant,' who 'stopped believing in the orchard.' I've sat in that office and listened to you dictate her into a villain with perfect posture."

"She was—" He stopped himself. Actually stopped himself, teeth closing on the sentence like a trap on his own tongue. He breathed. "Go on," he said hoarsely. "Keep reading."

So I did. I read him the letter about the night of the harvest festival, when he'd gotten drunk on his own cider and told half the town his wife didn't understand what the land meant to him. I read him the letter written in rain, the ink bleeding, where she said: *"I used to think your silences were deep. Now I know they are only empty, and I have been pouring myself into them for twenty years like water into a cracked jar."* I read him the one where she described, in devastating detail, the afternoon she found the first draft of his autobiography in the estate office — pages and pages in which she appeared exactly twice, both times as an obstacle — and how she had sat on the floor of that office and laughed until she cried, because it was somehow funnier than it was sad.

"That's why she hid them in the boat," I said. "Do you understand? She knew you'd write your version. She knew you'd spend your last years sanding yourself smooth and varnishing the story. So she wrote hers down and gave it to the water, because the water was the only thing on this property you never managed to control."

The wind came hard down the rows then, bending the bare branches, and for a moment the whole orchard sounded like the sea, and my father stood in the middle of it with his head bowed like a man in a storm he'd spent seventy years pretending wasn't there.

Then his legs went.

It wasn't dramatic. There was no cry, no collapse. He simply sat down, right there in the gravel between the second and third rows, lowering himself onto the cold ground with the careful clumsiness of an old man whose body had made the decision before his pride could object. He sat with his back against the trunk of a blighted Gravenstein — the same row, I realized, where my mother used to stand during harvest, sorting fruit into crates — and he put his face in his hands.

I stood over him with the letters shaking slightly in my grip, and I discovered I didn't feel triumphant. I had expected to. I had carried these pages for three days like ammunition, and now that I'd fired them, there was just the smoke and the ringing silence and my father on the ground, crying the way men of his generation cry — silently, furiously, as though each tear were being extracted under protest.

"Dad." The word came out before I authorized it. I hadn't called him that in eleven years.

"Don't," he said into his hands. "Don't be kind to me right now. I can't — I'll come apart entirely, and I need—" He lowered his hands. His face was wrecked, the skin blotched, the eyes red-rimmed and astonished, like a man who has looked in a mirror and found a stranger serving his sentence. "I need to say it before I lose the nerve. Will you let me say it?"

I should have said no. Every angry cell in my body voted no. But I folded the letters closed, and I sat down across from him in the gravel, because some part of me — the part that was nine years old and sneaking out to the creek, the part that had loved him before I knew what he was — needed to hear it too.

"Say it."

He looked down the rows instead of at me. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough, stripped of the broadcast cadence he used for interviews and dictation and every other performance of his life.

"The memoir isn't a memoir," he said. "You're right. It's a defense. I hired that girl to build me a wall out of paper before I die, because I knew — I've always known — that if I left the record open, the truth would get in." His breath hitched. "Your mother saw it before anyone. Nineteen years ago, sitting on the floor of that office, laughing. She knew I'd spend my last breath curating myself. And God help me, she was right. I sat in that chair week after week and I lied about her. To her face, essentially. While her daughter sat ten feet away hearing every word." He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "I told myself it was legacy. Legacy. As if a legacy is something you can type. As if it isn't just — what people carry of you after the ground closes. And what she carries of me is a man who let the trees die and called it fate."

"Why?" It came out of me raw. "Why her? Why make her the villain? What did she ever do to you except love you badly enough to stay?"

He was quiet for a long time. A crow returned to the fence post and regarded us with professional indifference.

"Because she was right," he said finally, "and I couldn't forgive her for it. That's the whole rotten truth, Mari. She saw me clearly, and being seen was the one thing I couldn't survive. So I made her the villain, because a villain is a shape you can point at. A mirror has no edges. You can't defend yourself against a mirror." He looked at me then, directly, and there was something in his face I had never seen there — not charm, not authority, none of the equipment he'd used to run the town and the orchard and our family. Just an old man, terrified, out of arguments. "I'm going to fix it. The book. I don't know how yet, but I can't let it go out into the world the way it is. I won't bury her twice."

The wind dropped. In the sudden hush I could hear the tide, faint and patient, working the harbor a mile away.

"There's a girl inside that office," I said, nodding toward the house, "who's been typing your lies for months. She knows. Some of it, anyway. She keeps two sets of notes — don't look surprised, Dad, I've seen her bag. If you mean what you just said, it goes through her. Whatever we do next, it's the three of us or it's nothing."

He stared at me. Then, slowly, he began to laugh — a broken, disbelieving sound, half sob, aimed mostly at himself.

"Of course it is," he said. "Of course the ghostwriter is the honest one. It would be. It always is." He struggled upright against the tree, joints cracking, and stood looking down the long gray rows as if seeing them for the first time — not as a monument, not as a failure, but as work. Unfinished work. "Thursday," he said quietly. "Her session is Thursday. Bring the letters. All of them. And bring that girl." He turned to me, and his voice, when it came, was the steadiest thing about him. "Tell her the book is dead. Tell her I want to write the true one — and tell her I know she may not believe I deserve her help. Because I don't. But ask her anyway."

I walked back down the drive with the letters heavy against my hip, the gate singing behind me, and my hands wouldn't stop shaking — not from anger anymore, I realized, but from the strange vertigo of getting exactly what you asked for and finding it heavier than the fight ever was.

Noor's car was still parked outside the estate office. Through the window I could see her bent over her laptop, lit gold by the desk lamp, guarding a manuscript that no longer existed for anyone but the man who'd commissioned it.

I knocked on the glass.

* * *

She opened the door with the wary look of someone who has been caught before and expects to be caught again, and I understood, in one cold drop, that she already knew why I was standing there.

"We need to talk," I said. "All three of us. And Noor — before you say anything — I need you to tell me the truth faster than you've been telling it. Because I just watched my father break open on the ground, and I don't have the patience left for anyone else's careful versions."

Behind her, on the desk, the manuscript sat in a neat stack — and on top of it, in fresh ink, a single page I hadn't seen before, with a heading in her handwriting that stopped my heart:

*Chapter One. The true version.*
