## 6. Cider and Cross-Examination

The community center smelled like cinnamon and old paint, and June Okafor had learned in prison that the best place to hide a knife was inside a compliment.

"You've done this before," Coral Halloran said, watching her fold a paper garland into perfect pleats. There was something almost grudging in the admiration, as if Coral resented finding competence where she'd expected clumsiness.

"Ten years of craft hour," June said, not looking up. "You learn to make a lot out of very little."

It was true, and it wasn't, and that was the particular skill she'd refined behind wire and cinderblock: saying things that were honest on their surface and loaded underneath. *A lot out of very little.* Let Coral hear the craft hour. Let her also hear the ten years.

Coral moved down the folding table with her clipboard held like a shield, checking items off in her small, upright handwriting. *Cider press — confirmed. Hay bales — 40, arriving Thursday. String lights — vendor delayed, backup sourced.* Everything about her was ordered, contained, immaculate. Her hair pinned back so tightly it looked architectural. Her sleeves buttoned to the wrist even though the room was warm.

June had spent four months studying this woman through paper — through the careful, tender, evasive letters signed only with a first initial — and now she was studying her in person, at a distance of six feet, over a table of construction paper and twine, and the woman was nothing like what the letters promised. The letters had been warm. The woman was armor.

"Can I ask you something?" June said, and kept her voice light, curious, harmless. The voice of a helpful volunteer. The voice of a neighbor.

"Of course." Coral didn't look up from her clipboard.

"The festival. You do all these weddings, all these events — but this one seems different for you. You get this look when you talk about it."

"What look?"

"Like you're reading from a script someone else wrote."

Coral's pen stopped moving. Just for a second. A hitch, barely a heartbeat, before it resumed its steady progress down the checklist. "I've coordinated this festival three years running," she said. "It's a lot of moving parts. Scripts are how you keep them straight."

"Mm," June said, and folded another pleat.

* * *

She'd prepared for this. That was the thing Coral couldn't know — that every question June asked had been drafted, revised, and memorized the way other people memorized prayers. In her apartment next door, in a binder with a cracked spine, June kept the map of the night everything burned: floor plans drawn from memory, timelines reconstructed from trial transcripts she'd read so many times the pages had gone soft at the edges, and Coral's own words, highlighted in yellow, cross-referenced against the red-inked annotations in the margins of ten years of letters.

The fire had started at 9:47 p.m. on the second Saturday of October. Everyone agreed on that much. The old grange hall — the same building the town now dressed up in bunting every fall, rebuilt twice and patched a dozen times since — had gone up fast, faster than a structure that age should have, which meant accelerant, which meant intent, which meant someone.

The state said it was June. The state said a lot of things.

What June knew was this: she had left the hall at 9:15 to take a phone call in the parking lot. When she came back, the west wall was breathing smoke, and Coral Halloran was standing in the middle of the street in a white dress, soot-striped, shaking, pointing at her.

*That one,* Coral had said. Or something like it. Ten years of lawyers had sanded the exact words smooth, but the pointing finger had never blurred. It lived behind June's eyelids.

And what June believed — what the anonymous letter received in her third year of incarceration had planted and watered until it grew into the whole architecture of her parole plan — was that Coral Halloran had seen something else that night. Someone else. And had named June instead, whether from fear, from confusion, or from something worse.

So here was the strategy, simple as a shiv: ask questions that sounded like small talk. Get Coral talking about the fire the way she talked about everything else — smoothly, professionally, rehearsed. Because rehearsed testimony has seams. Real memory is ragged. Real memory hesitates, contradicts itself, doubles back. Rehearsed memory is a speech.

June needed to find the seam.

* * *

The afternoon wore on. They strung garlands along the windows. They argued amiably about cider booth placement — Coral wanted it near the entrance for traffic flow; June wanted it deep in the hall because she remembered, from a lifetime ago, how the smell of hot cider used to pull people all the way through the building, past the craft tables nobody visited. Coral yielded, and seemed surprised to have yielded, and covered the surprise by writing *cider — interior* on her clipboard with unnecessary force.

It was almost pleasant. That was the trap of it, June thought. That was the danger. Coral Halloran was easy to be around in the way a well-run event is easy — everything anticipated, nothing left rough. She refilled June's coffee without being asked. She laughed at a joke about the mayor's bunting obsession, a real laugh, quick and low, gone as soon as it arrived.

June felt the treacherous warmth of it and pressed it flat, the way you'd press a spider under a glass.

Then, casually, turning a sprig of dried wheat in her fingers, she set the hook.

"I heard the hall caught fire once," she said. "Years back. Is that why they rebuilt the east wing? The masonry looks newer."

Coral's hands did not stop moving. But they changed. It was subtle — a fractional slowing, the way a record wobbles just before the needle catches. "There was a fire," Coral said. "About ten years ago. During the festival, actually."

"No. Really?" June let her eyes go wide, let her voice carry the right note of neighborly astonishment. "During it? What happened?"

"It was late. Near closing." Coral smoothed a length of twine between finger and thumb, over and over, a motion with no purpose except to keep her hands busy. "There was a candle display near the stage. Harvest-themed. Someone knocked one into the curtains — the old curtains were dry as tinder. It spread along the west wall before anyone realized."

"That's terrifying." June kept her tone soft, awed, drawing her out. "Were you there?"

"I was there." Two words, delivered evenly, each one placed like a stone in a wall.

"You must have been so scared."

For the first time all afternoon, Coral looked up. Her eyes were pale and level and completely dry, and June understood suddenly that she was looking at a woman who had answered this question many times before, in rooms with bad lighting, from witnesses stands and therapists' chairs and the passenger seats of well-meaning friends' cars. *Were you scared.* Coral had an answer ready. June could see it waiting behind her teeth like a coin held in the mouth.

"I don't remember being scared," Coral said. "I remember being cold. Which sounds strange, but the doors were propped open for the crowd, and October air was coming through, and then afterward — everyone says shock makes you feel warm, but I was freezing. I stood outside in my dress and I couldn't stop shivering."

Every detail plausible. Every detail textured. The cold, the dress, the open doors — sensory specifics that no liar would think to invent and no innocent person could produce on demand.

Except a liar who'd told the story a hundred times could. Except a woman who'd built a life on the foundation of that night could recite it like a wedding toast.

"And you got out through—" June frowned delicately, as if consulting her own faulty memory of gossip. "The front? Where the doors were open?"

"The front, yes." Smooth. Instant. Too instant. "Everyone funneled toward the main doors. I went with the crowd."

"But the crowd was already outside by then, wasn't it? If the air was coming in the open doors, the crowd must have thinned early. Did you leave with the first group, or—"

"I left when I left." A hairline crack in the evenness. Coral's thumb pressed hard against the twine. "It was crowded and confusing. People were going both directions. I remember the doors being open because I remember the cold air meeting the heat — you could feel the two of them fighting in the doorway. Like the building couldn't decide whether to breathe in or out."

*The doors being open.*

June turned the phrase over gently, the way you'd turn a key you suspected fit a lock. Because here was the thing. Here was the thread. At trial, Coral Halloran had testified — June had read the transcript forty times, had underlined it, had fallen asleep with the pages on her chest — that she exited through the rear service corridor. That the back door was open. That she saw June Okafor near the back hallway minutes before the alarms, and that the back door being unsecured was how June would have moved the accelerant can in and out unnoticed.

The back door. Not the front. The state's whole theory of movement, of access, of opportunity, hung on that door.

But maybe Coral misremembered now. Maybe ten years had worn the detail smooth. People confused exits; trauma scrambled geography; June knew that better than anyone. So she didn't pounce. Pouncing was amateur work. She smiled and picked up another garland.

"It's strange," she said, "the details we keep. Cold air. A doorway. I bet you remember the strangest little things."

"I remember everything," Coral said quietly. "That's the problem."

* * *

They worked until the light through the windows went amber, then gray. The garlands were done. The seating chart for the opening ceremony was drafted and redrafted. And somewhere in the last hour, without either of them announcing it, the interrogation had become something closer to conversation, which was its own kind of danger, and June pulled back, checked herself, remembered what the letters were for.

Because here was what the letters were for: they were proof that Coral Halloran had known, all along, exactly who June Okafor was and what June believed had happened. Month after month, page after page, Coral had written to her — kind, gentle, careful, never explaining, never signing more than a first name — and you did not write tender monthly letters to a stranger. You wrote them to a wound you were tending. Or a crime you were monitoring. Either way, guilt or surveillance, the letters meant Coral had never once forgotten her. And a woman who never forgot is a woman who remembers everything, including which door she walked through.

June packed up slowly, watching Coral across the room. Coral stood at the window adjusting a garland that was already straight, making small precise corrections to something no one would ever notice, and the evening light caught the fine tremor in her hands.

"One more question," June said, shrugging into her coat, keeping it feather-light. "That night. The fire. When you got out — which way did you go? Front or back?"

It was phrased perfectly. Casual. A callback, a natural curiosity, the kind of thing a new acquaintance asks when a story snags her imagination. No accusation in it anywhere.

Coral went still.

Not frozen — June had seen frozen, had caused frozen, and this was different. This was the stillness of a computer encountering an error, a fraction-of-a-second stall while two files compete for the same slot. Her mouth opened. Closed. And then the professional smile arrived, deployed like a tablecloth snapped over stains.

"Front," Coral said. "I told you. With everyone else."

"You did," June agreed warmly. "Funny, though. For some reason I always pictured the back."

"Why would you picture the back?"

"I don't know." June laughed, shook her head, shouldered her bag. "Old buildings. Back doors are where the stories come out, my grandmother used to say. Anyway — same time Thursday?"

"Thursday," Coral said. Her voice had recovered its polish, but her hand, gathering her clipboard, missed the edge of the table on the first try.

* * *

Outside, the night had gone cold and clean, stars sharp over the rooftops. June walked the thirty feet to her own door and did not go inside. She stood on the small porch instead, breath fogging, and looked back at the community center, where a single light still burned above the door. Coral was still in there. Straightening garlands, probably. Making the crooked things straight.

June took out her phone and opened the note she kept pinned at the top of everything, the one she'd typed the week after her release:

*Her story: front door, open, cold air.*
*Trial transcript, p. 214: back corridor, rear service door.*
*Both cannot be true.*

She stared at it for a long time. Then she added a line beneath, her thumbs slow in the cold:

*Today she said front. Twice. Smoothly. Like a speech.*

*Which means either she's forgotten the truth —*
*or she's finally practicing the lie.*

Inside her bag, zipped into the inner pocket where she kept the things that mattered, was a photocopy of page 214, and beneath the photocopy, folded into quarters, the anonymous letter that had started all of this, its postmark a decade old, its message still burning in her chest like a coal that refused to cool:

*Ask Coral Halloran which door she really used. Ask her who was holding it shut.*

June read it again by the light spilling from the community center window, and for the first time since her release, a doubt slid cold under her certainty, thin as a blade: because if Coral had lied about the door, she'd lied smoothly, for ten years, to police and prosecutors and juries.

But if Coral had just told the truth today — if the front door was the real memory, surfacing now, contradicting her own sworn testimony —

then someone had made sure the back-door version was the one that reached the courtroom.

And that someone had put June Okafor in prison for it.

Through the window, the light above the community center door clicked off. Somewhere inside, Coral Halloran was gathering her things in the dark, and June turned toward home carrying the one thing she hadn't planned for: a question with no comfortable answer, and a name — *who was holding it shut* — that she suddenly, urgently, needed Coral to hear.
