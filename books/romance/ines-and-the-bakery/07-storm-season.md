## 7. Storm Season

**Casper**

The barometer had been falling for two days, and Casper Ashgrove trusted it more than he trusted most people.

He'd spent the morning at the research station nailing storm shutters over the windows of the old boathouse that served as his lab, while the harbor turned the color of gunmetal and the gulls went quiet in a way he'd learned to read like a warning. Saltmere storms came off the water sideways and mean. The town would batten down; the town always did. He'd been through three of them since arriving, each one endured alone with a thermos of coffee and a clipboard of readings, which was exactly how he preferred it.

His phone buzzed against the workbench. *Nadia.* He let it ring out.

She'd been calling every Sunday for a month now, ever since their mother's birthday, when he'd admitted — too honestly, after one glass of wine — that he hadn't spoken to anyone outside the station in weeks except to order bread. Nadia had a theory about him. She thought the turtles were a symptom. She thought small was another word for hiding.

The trouble with sisters was that they were usually right.

By four o'clock the wind had teeth. He secured the turtle tanks' backup generator, checked the seals on the incubation room where forty-two loggerhead eggs sat in their sand-filled trays — nests relocated from a beach that eroded every winter — and told himself the tightness in his chest was just weather. Numbers didn't scare him. Forty-two eggs. One generator. Three hours of fuel if the power failed.

Then the lights flickered, steadied, and died, and the rain came down like the sea had decided to reclaim the whole peninsula at once.

* * *

**Ines**

Ines Marchetti heard the cellar flood before she saw it — a wrongness in the sound of the rain, a gurgle under the floorboards like the building swallowing something it couldn't keep down.

"No," she said, to the bakery, to the storm, to the universe generally. "No, no, no."

She took the stairs two at a time with her phone flashlight cutting a shaking cone ahead of her. The cellar was where Nonna's whole life lived: the proofing racks, the sacks of flour stacked on pallets, the wine her grandfather had made the year Ines was born, the chest freezer full of dough she'd shaped for the harvest festival — the festival that was supposed to save them, the festival that was eleven days away. Water was already ankle-deep and rising, black and fast-moving, carrying a drowned cardboard box past her ankles like a little boat.

She waded in anyway, because the freezer was on blocks and the blocks were low, and because panic makes people stupid.

She got her hands under the freezer's edge and pushed. It didn't move. It weighed what grief weighs. The water climbed her shins, then her knees, and somewhere above her the wind peeled a shutter off the front of the building with a sound like a scream, and she understood, distantly, that standing in a flooding basement during a hurricane was not the inheritance Nonna had intended.

She stayed anyway. That was also an inheritance.

* * *

**Casper**

The sandbags lasted until nine. At 9:14 the sea came up Harbor Lane like it owned it, which historically it did, and Casper stood in the doorway of the station watching brown water sheet across the road toward the bakery two doors down — Marchetti's, dark now, its windows boarded but its back door, he could see even in the strobing dark, sitting open to the storm.

He should have stayed put. Every protocol he'd written himself said stay put. But the bakery's cellar flooded every storm; the whole town knew it; and the whole town was currently behind sandbags and shutters, which meant nobody else was going to see that door swinging like an invitation to disaster.

"Idiot," he told himself, and pulled on his rain gear, and went out into a world made entirely of noise.

The thirty meters between the station and the bakery took him five minutes. The wind hit him like a hand. By the time he shouldered the back door shut behind him, he couldn't feel his fingers and his heart was going hard enough that he could pretend it was all exertion.

"Bakery!" he shouted into the dark. "Anyone in here?"

A light appeared at the top of the cellar stairs — her flashlight, her face above it, pale and furious even before she knew who he was.

"What are you doing in my bakery?"

"Your back door was open and your cellar is flooding." He was already moving toward her, water sloshing somewhere below. "You can't be down there."

"It's my cellar."

"And you're five foot four and that freezer weighs four hundred pounds. Move."

"I don't need—"

The power surge chose that moment to die completely, taking even the streetlights, and in the sudden total dark her flashlight beam swung wildly and caught him full in the face. He watched her recognize him. Watched the fury reorganize itself around a new object.

"You," she said.

"Me," he agreed. "Can we do this later?"

* * *

**Ines**

Later, she would think: of everyone the storm could have sent me, it sent him.

But in the moment there was only water climbing and a freezer full of festival dough and a man she despised holding out his hands in the dark like he had any right to be useful.

"The freezer has to go up," she said. "Or onto something higher. There are crates by the wall — the wooden ones."

They found them by touch and sound, shouting coordinates to each other over the wind. He was stronger than he looked, or maybe he looked strong and she'd refused to notice. They got two crates under the freezer's edge, then a third wedged as a ramp, and then it was physics and swearing and her shoulder against cold steel beside his shoulder, both of them pushing on three, and the freezer groaned up onto the stack just as the water reached the motor housing.

Ines sat down on the stairs, shaking, laughing a little, hating the laugh.

"That's the festival," she said. "That's everything for the festival."

"It's dry," he said. "It's up. It'll hold."

"You don't know that."

"I know freezers." A pause. "I know water."

She aimed the flashlight at him. He was soaked through despite the rain gear, hair plastered down, a cut on his knuckle she hadn't noticed him favoring. He looked, infuriatingly, like someone who had come out into a hurricane for no reason anyone sensible could name.

"Why are you here?" she asked. "Really."

He looked at the water instead of at her. "Your door was open."

"People leave doors open in storms. The latch sticks. That's not a reason."

"Maybe I don't have better ones," he said, and something in how he said it — flat, final, a door of his own closing — stopped her from asking again.

* * *

**Casper**

They couldn't get back out. That became clear within the hour. The lane between the buildings was waist-deep and running hard, and when Casper cracked the back door to check, the wind drove a sheet of spray clear across the kitchen and Ines slammed it shut with her whole body and said, "Absolutely not, we wait," in a voice that ended discussion.

So they waited. In her grandmother's kitchen. Which was, he realized slowly, a museum.

Flour-dusted photographs on the walls. A rosary on a nail by the stove. Copper pans hanging in ranks like soldiers, polished by decades of hands. And on the shelf above the sink, a tin — blue, dented, older than either of them — sitting among the everyday jars like something kept close on purpose.

Ines caught him looking and moved slightly, unconsciously, so her body blocked the tin.

"Coffee?" she said, too briskly. "I have a gas burner. Storms knock the power out; the stove doesn't care."

"Sure," he said, and let it go, and hated himself a little for noticing what she'd hidden.

They made coffee by flashlight, and the storm screamed against the shutters, and neither of them mentioned the letters. That was the strange thing, he thought, watching her measure grounds with the same unerring precision she brought to everything. Somewhere out there, a stranger knew that he counted turtle nests to fall asleep, knew about the engagement that dissolved, knew he hadn't gone home in six years. This woman knew none of it and hated him for reasons equally opaque, and between those two versions of himself he felt stretched thin enough to see through.

"The generator at the station," he said. "If the power stays out, the egg incubators run on backup. Three hours of fuel."

"So?"

"So if it's still out at dawn, I have to get back. Forty-two eggs don't forgive."

Something crossed her face — reluctant arithmetic. "Loggerheads," she said.

He blinked. "You know turtles?"

"My nonna volunteered on nest watches, years ago. Before." She handed him a chipped mug. "She liked them. She said they come back to the beach they were born on, no matter how far they swim." Her mouth twisted. "Sentimental nonsense."

"It's true, though," he said. "They really do."

"I know it's true. That's what made it nonsense."

* * *

**Ines**

Around midnight the storm changed key — the deep roar dropping away into something worse, a sucking quiet, and Casper went very still with his mug halfway to his mouth.

"Eye," he said. "Maybe twenty minutes. Maybe less. If we're going to move anything, move it now."

They worked like they'd done it for years. That was the humiliating part, Ines thought later. No negotiation, no wasted words. He lifted; she braced. She pointed; he saw. They hauled the last of the flour up to the kitchen in the dead calm, stars visible through a ragged tear in the clouds, the whole town holding its breath, and when the far edge of the eye arrived they were both inside with the door barred and the flour safe and their hearts going like trapped birds.

"We make a decent crew," he said, before he could stop himself.

"Don't get comfortable."

"I'm not. I'm getting coffee."

But she'd seen it — the almost-smile, quickly banked, and worse, she'd felt her own face trying to answer it, and she turned to the window and watched the rain come back like a curtain falling on the wrong act.

Her mother's voice lived in her head like a tenant who paid no rent: *The Ashgroves take and take, and they never once say sorry.* Twenty years of that voice. Twenty years of a story with no loose ends, and here was its loose end standing in her kitchen making coffee with a cut on his knuckle he hadn't mentioned, and she found herself getting the first-aid kit without deciding to.

"Give me your hand," she said.

"It's nothing."

"Give me your hand, Ashgrove."

He gave it. His skin was cold from the storm and the cut was shallow and she cleaned it and taped it with a competence that surprised her, and the whole time he watched her with an expression she refused to examine — something open and startled, like a man being handed something he'd stopped expecting.

"You're good at this," he said quietly.

"I'm good at fixing things that are broken." She tore the tape. "Don't flatter yourself; it barely counts."

* * *

**Casper**

Dawn came gray and dripping. The storm had spent itself against the coast and moved on north, leaving Saltmere strewn with shingles and kelp and stunned silence. The power was still out. Casper stood at the bakery's back door looking at the flooded lane and did the math he'd been dreading: the station, the generator, three hours of fuel already burned through and counting.

"The eggs," Ines said behind him. Not a question. She'd remembered.

"If I can't get the generator refueled in the next couple of hours, the incubation room drops below threshold." He said it evenly, but his hands weren't. "Forty-two nests relocated because their beach washed away. If they cool now, that's it. There's no second season for this cohort."

Ines looked at the water, at the sky, at him. He watched her weigh it — watched her actually stand there and choose, visibly, between the easy thing and the other thing.

"There's a rowboat chained behind the fish market," she said. "Nonna's. Nobody's used it since the marina closed, but the chain's rusted through. If we go along the alley behind Cannery Row, the water's shallower. We could pole it."

"You want to help me."

"I want your turtles alive so you stop looking like that." She was already pulling on her boots. "And because they come back to the beach they were born on, and somebody in this town ought to believe in something."

They went together through the drowned morning, poling Nonna's leaky rowboat down what had been a street, past houses with their shutters hanging like broken wings. She poled; he bailed with a flour bucket. Neither of them spoke much. There wasn't any need. When the boat snagged on a submerged fence and listed, she shifted her weight without being asked and he leaned without thinking, and the boat righted, and they went on.

At the station he refueled the generator with shaking hands while she held the flashlight steady, and when the incubator's indicator light came back green — forty-two eggs, warm, saved — he made a sound he didn't plan, half laugh, half something rawer, and Ines Marchetti looked at him across the sand trays and the smell of salt and straw, and for one unguarded second her face did the thing he'd been watching for since the night began.

It softened.

"There," she said, gruff, turning away. "Now we're even. Don't make me regret it."

"Ines." He said her name for the first time. It landed between them like a dropped glass.

She stopped in the doorway, backlit by the washed-out dawn, and he saw the exact moment she remembered herself — saw the wall go back up brick by brick, her jaw set, her eyes going cold and careful.

"This changes nothing," she said. "You helped me, I helped you. We're square. Go home, Ashgrove."

She walked out into the wet light, and Casper stood alone among his saved eggs, listening to the water drip, thinking: *square.* As if anything between them had ever been square. As if a debt that size could be cleared in one night.

That evening, soaked and exhausted, he sat down at his desk and pulled out paper, because there was someone he needed to tell about the storm — someone who knew about counting things to stay sane, someone who might understand why a man would pole a rowboat through a drowned town for eggs that weren't even his.

*Dear Stranger,* he wrote. *Last night I learned that the baker hates me, and I still don't know why. But I learned something else too, and I don't know what to do with it...*

Two miles across town, Ines sat at her grandmother's kitchen table under the blue tin, staring at a blank page, trying to find words for a stranger about a night she couldn't stop replaying — and finding, to her horror, that every sentence she wrote was about him.
