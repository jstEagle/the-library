## 8. Wren's Map

The assignment was due in three weeks, and Wren Lockridge had already ruined four sheets of graph paper.

She sat at the back table of the bookshop, the one by the window where the radiator ticked and the light came in gray and gold through salt-fogged glass, and she drew the harbor again. The harbor was not hard to draw. The harbor was a crooked smile of water between two stone arms, and everything that mattered sat along it like teeth: the ferry dock, the chapel with its leaning spire, Marta's cottage with the blue door, the bakery, the bookshop. She could draw it with her eyes shut. She had been drawing it, in one form or another, since she was six.

What was hard was what Miss Okafor had asked for. *Map your community's dependencies,* she'd said, tapping the whiteboard. *Not streets — dependencies. What does what depend on? Draw the lines that hold you up.* And then she'd looked directly at Wren, because Miss Okafor had that way of looking at you, like she knew exactly which question would keep you up at night, and she'd added, *I expect yours will be interesting.*

Wren chewed the end of her pencil and thought about lines.

Her father said the heart was a muscle, and muscles were for containing things. He hadn't said it lately — he'd stopped saying a lot of things lately, ever since Ines Vidal moved into the flat beside them with her boxes that smelled of cinnamon even before they were opened — but Wren remembered. He used to say it when she asked about Mum. He would tap his own chest, twice, like knocking on a door he didn't intend to open, and say, *This is a muscle like any other, love. You train it. You teach it to hold still.*

Wren had been trying to draw the harbor without the bakery in it, to see if she could, and she couldn't.

That was the thing nobody said out loud. You could take the bakery off the map — she'd tried, on sheet number three, drawing over it with the pale blue of the sea — and the whole picture went wrong. The ferry crews came off the dock at six in the morning and walked straight up the lane, and if there was no bakery, where did they go? They stood in the cold holding nothing. Marta's blue door opened onto an empty street. The chapel bell rang for the early service and nobody came out afterward into the smell of warm bread; they just went home. Even the gulls looked wrong, wheeling over a lane that smelled of nothing.

She drew the bakery back in. Then she drew lines. A line from the bakery to the ferry dock, thick as a rope. A line to Marta's door. A line to the chapel steps. A line to the school, because half her class arrived sticky-fingered and calm on mornings when the morning buns came out right, and arrived feral and snappish on mornings when the ovens failed, which happened maybe twice a year and was treated by everyone, including the teachers, as a civic emergency.

Then she drew the line from the bakery to the bookshop — her line, their line, the short one across the party wall — and hesitated, and drew it thickest of all.

* * *

Ines came in at four, the way she did now, most days, carrying a paper bag that she set down on the counter without ceremony, the way you'd set down something you brought every day and didn't want made a fuss of. Today the bag held the ends of a almond loaf, the burnt-bottom piece her father used to call the cook's tax, and a handful of custard tarts that had cracked in the oven and therefore could not be sold, only loved.

"Cracked ones," Ines said, sliding the plate toward Wren. "Nobody wants them. It's a tragedy."

"They're the best ones."

"I know." Ines pulled out the chair opposite and sat, and for a while neither of them talked, because that was another thing about Ines: she didn't fill silence the way adults were supposed to, all the time, with weather and questions. She let it sit there like dough rising.

Wren worked on her map. Ines drank tea from the chipped mug that lived at the back table now, the one with the lighthouse on it, which had migrated from the bakery to the bookshop sometime in November and never gone home. Wren watched her out of the corner of her eye the way she watched everything now — carefully, hungrily, the way you watch weather coming.

"You've drawn the bakery four times," Ines said eventually.

"It keeps being important."

"Does it?"

Wren turned the graph paper around so Ines could see the whole web of it, the lines going out from the little square marked BAKERY like spokes, or roots, or something that was both. "Miss Okafor says we have to map dependencies. What depends on what." She tapped the bakery square. "Everything does. That's the problem."

"What problem?"

"The landlord." Wren said it flatly, the way kids say the true terrible things that adults are still saying gently to each other behind doors. She'd heard her father on the phone twice this week, his voice doing the low careful thing it did when he thought she was asleep. *Double the rent. Community support, they want proof of. As if the whole town isn't standing right here.* "If the bakery goes, my map is just a picture of everything falling down."

Ines looked at the map for a long time. Her finger came out and traced the line from the bakery to the ferry dock, and Wren saw her mouth do something complicated, like she was swallowing words.

"Ferry crews," Ines said quietly. "They come in at six. Tomás — the big one, with the beard — he doesn't order anything anymore. I just put his roll on the counter and he puts coins in the dish. His mother used to do that for him, before. Before she died, I mean. He told me once he can't make change in the mornings. His hands don't work right until he's eaten."

"And Marta comes at seven for her rye bread, and she stays forty minutes, and if the bakery closed she'd just be alone in her house with the blue door."

"Yes."

"And the chapel—"

"The chapel buys the altar bread from us," Ines said. "Has for sixty years. My grandmother's recipe. Father Brendan says God has opinions about substitutions." She smiled, but it was the tired kind of smile, the kind that was doing work underneath. "You've been paying attention."

"I always pay attention," Wren said. "Nobody notices because I'm twelve."

"I notice."

And there it was again, that feeling, the one Wren had been trying not to name because naming it might scare it off. It had started the second week, when Ines had found her crying in the bookshop's back room over a maths test and hadn't done any of the wrong things — hadn't said *there, there*, hadn't offered to call her dad, hadn't looked frightened. She'd just sat down on the floor next to her, among the boxes of unsold remainder books, and said, "Fractions are a lie invented to punish children. Tell me which part is lying to you specifically." And they'd worked through it on the back of a flour sack, and Wren had stopped crying somewhere in the middle without noticing, and Ines had never mentioned it since.

Mum had been dead eight years. Dad said the heart was a muscle and you trained it to hold still, and Wren had trained hers too, the same way, small and contained and quiet, a muscle that lifted nothing heavy. But sitting here with custard and graph paper and a woman who noticed, Wren felt her heart doing something unauthorized. Growing. Reaching. Being greedy.

She hated how much she wanted it. She wanted it the way you want the sun in winter: completely, embarrassingly, knowing it leaves every day at four.

* * *

On Sunday the rain came sideways off the sea and the harbor disappeared entirely, erased like someone had taken an eraser to the map, and Wren spent the afternoon finishing her project at the back table while her father restocked shelves and pretended not to glance at the bakery door every ninety seconds, which fooled absolutely no one, least of all Wren, who had a theory forming about her father and Ines Vidal that she kept in the same drawer as her other dangerous theories, the ones about Mum, the ones she took out only at night.

Ines came in soaked at five, laughing, shaking rain off her coat like a dog. "The awning leaked," she announced. "Directly onto the proving racks. I have spent the day moving dough around my kitchen like it was furniture in a flood." She dropped into her chair. "Your turn to make tea. I'm claiming refugee status."

Wren made the tea. She'd learned how Ines liked it — strong enough to stand a spoon in, milk first, no sugar, which her father said was a crime against tea and Ines said was a crime against nothing because tea should taste like tea. She carried both mugs over and set them down and then, instead of going back to her seat, she stood there, looking at her nearly finished map pinned under the teapot lid, and felt the question come up her throat like a tide.

It wasn't the question she'd planned. She'd planned to ask about the lease, actually, whether Ines would sign it, whether Lisbon was real, because she'd heard that word too, Lisbon, floating through the party wall some nights when voices dropped low, and she'd looked it up, and Lisbon was very far away, farther than the moon practically, or close to it.

But what came out was:

"I wish you were my mother."

The rain filled the silence after. Somewhere in the shop her father's hands stopped moving; she heard the absence of page-turning the way you hear a clock stop.

Ines went very still. Not the bad kind of still — not flinching, not recoiling — but the stillness of someone receiving something breakable with both hands. She set down her mug. She looked at Wren for a long moment, and her eyes were bright and wet and steady all at once, and she didn't say *oh, sweetheart* or *you don't mean that* or any of the things that would have been lies.

"I can't be that," she said softly. "I wish I could give you that. But listen—" and she reached across the table and turned Wren's map toward herself, and her finger found the bakery square, and moved outward, line by line. "Look what you made. Ferry crews. Marta. The chapel. Your dad. You. This town holds itself up, Wren. It's been doing it for hundreds of years, with or without me. I'm one square on your map. But—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she steadied it, "—if I get to choose a square to be on, I'd choose this one. This exact one. With the cracked custard tarts and the lighthouse mug and the girl who draws dependencies better than the council does."

"That's not the same as a mother."

"No," Ines said. "It's not. But it's not nothing. Some things are better than the shape you wanted them in. Some things arrive in the wrong container and they're still full."

Wren looked at her map. All those lines, holding each other up. She thought about how her father said the heart was a muscle, and how muscles got stronger the more they carried, and how maybe he'd been training his to carry nothing for eight years and had forgotten what it was for.

"Okay," she said finally, because she was twelve and honest and it was the truest word available. "Okay. But I'm keeping the map."

"Keep the map," Ines said. "Maps are how we find our way back."

From the shelves, very quietly, her father began turning pages again, and Wren understood suddenly, completely, the way children understand things in floods — that he had heard every word, and that something in him had come loose, and that whatever happened next with Lisbon and leases and landlords, the map on the table had already changed. There was a new line on it now, one she hadn't drawn yet, running from the bakery to the bookshop and back again, thicker than rope, and it wasn't going anywhere.

She picked up her pencil and drew it in.
