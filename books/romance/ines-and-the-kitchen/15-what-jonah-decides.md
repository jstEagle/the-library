## 15. What Jonah Decides

The manuscript sat on the kitchen table like something that had washed up from a wreck.

Jonah had carried it home from the cider press without opening it, the way you carry a dead bird—carefully, at arm's length, not sure whether the weight is grief or blame. He'd set it down between the salt dish and the lamp, and then he'd gone out to the orchard and worked until his hands ached too much to hold anything else, because there was a part of him that believed if he never turned the first page, none of it would be true. That Ada Fairweather would still be the girl who'd left. That her silence would still be a wall he could hate, clean and simple, instead of whatever this was.

But the light went down over Marrow Bay the way it always did, and the cottage got dark around him, and eventually a man has to either read or lie awake listening to himself not read.

He made coffee he didn't want. He sat down in his father's chair, which creaked under him with the same complaint it had made for thirty years, and he looked at the title page for a long time.

*Ines and the Kitchen.* No—that was the novel she'd published last month, the one with her own name on the spine that half the town had already read and the other half pretended they hadn't. This was different. This was typescript, double-spaced, bound with a binder clip gone rusty at the edges. The title page said only: *Marrow Bay: A Memoir.* Below that, in smaller type, as if the words themselves were trying to hide: *Ada Thackeray.*

Not Fairweather. Not the pen name. Her real name, the one nobody in town had known until the deed dragged it into the open like a root torn from wet ground.

He turned the page.

*My grandfather kept the lighthouse for forty-one years,* it began, *and he taught me two things: how to trim a wick so the flame burns steady, and how to keep a secret so well that even you forget you're holding it.*

Jonah read the first chapter standing up. He read the second sitting down. By the third, the coffee had gone cold in its mug and he didn't notice, because Ada's voice was doing something to him that no voice had done in ten years—it was telling the truth, and the truth had his family's name in it.

She wrote about 1948 the way an x-ray shows bone. His grandfather Elias Vane, young and proud, coming back from the war with orchard dirt still under his fingernails and a handshake deal with old Thackeray about the boundary line along the creek. She wrote about the filing—the clerk's office, the survey, the way her grandfather had waited three weeks after Elias signed before submitting papers that quietly redrew the line, folding six acres of bearing apple trees into the lighthouse parcel. Six acres. The heart of the orchard. The rows Jonah's father had spent his whole life farming on land the deeds said wasn't his, paying taxes on a ghost, fighting a claim no lawyer would take because the paper trail was airtight and the man who built it wore the town's respect like a medal.

She didn't soften it. That was the first thing that undid him. He'd braced for malice—for the memoir his editor wanted, the tell-all with the town's secrets strung up like lanterns. He'd imagined himself reading his family's ruin written by a woman who needed it to sell.

Instead she wrote:

*I have asked myself a thousand times why he did it, and I always come back to the same answer, which is that my grandfather loved this town the way some men love a fire—they cannot bear for anything to burn that isn't theirs. Elias Vane came home from the war with more than his share of everything: youth, luck, land that bore fruit in its second year when everyone said it wouldn't. And my grandfather, who had lost years and light and the use of his left hand to the sea, looked at that abundance and could not forgive it. So he took six acres. It took him twenty minutes at a clerk's desk and cost him nothing but the friendship of a man who died believing he'd been cheated by weather and bad luck, never knowing it was arithmetic.*

Jonah put the manuscript down.

The lamp hissed. Outside, the wind moved through the late rows, and somewhere in the dark an apple let go of its branch and landed with the soft thud he'd heard ten thousand times in his life and would hear until he died.

His grandfather Elias had died not knowing. His father had died not knowing—had died with the bank's letters on the kitchen table and the developer's offer in his inbox and the word *failure* hanging over the orchard like frost, and all of it, all of it, traced back to a desk in 1948 and a man with a burned hand who couldn't stand to see someone else's trees bear fruit.

And Ada had known. Ada had known since she found the ledger, and she'd come to the cider press in the rain and handed him the truth like a live coal and asked him what she should do with it.

He picked the manuscript back up.

* * *

He read through the night.

That was the thing he couldn't have explained to anyone—not to the town, not to his brother in Portland, not to the mirror. He should have stopped. A reasonable man would have stopped after the chapter about the deed, closed the binder clip, and driven to her door to say *I believe you, now burn it.* But the chapters kept going, and each one was a door he'd stood outside of for a decade, and here she was opening every one of them and turning on the lights.

She wrote about the letters. About finding them in her grandfather's records—forty-one letters over four years, his handwriting, addressed to her at the lighthouse, each one marked *RETURN TO SENDER* in the postal office's flat blue stamp, though she had never returned any of them because she had never received any of them. She wrote about the day she understood that her grandfather had intercepted them, that the man who taught her to trim wicks had also trimmed her life down to the size of a lamp room, and that she had stayed at the light for ten years partly out of love and partly out of a wound she'd mistaken for duty.

She wrote about herself. That was the part that kept him turning pages when he should have slept. She wrote about being Ada Fairweather in public and Ada Thackeray in private, about building a career out of invented men because the real one had gone silent, about writing eleven novels in which a woman waits and a man returns and the waiting turns out to mean something—because she had needed, desperately, for it to mean something, even if only on paper.

*Every book I ever wrote was a letter to him,* she wrote, in the chapter she must have written at three in the morning, because it had that three-in-the-morning honesty, the kind that can't be revised. *I told myself they were romances. They were arguments. Every hero who came back was me begging the world to prove that he could have.*

Jonah read that sentence four times.

Then he got up and walked out into the orchard in the dark, in his shirtsleeves, because the cottage felt too small to hold what he was feeling. The grass was cold and wet through his socks. He stood in the row where his father used to let him ride the ladder wagon, and he looked up at the sky doing its slow wheel over Marrow Bay, and he thought: *she waited too.*

All those years he'd spent sharpening his resentment like a grafting knife—she'd left, she'd chosen the city, she'd forgotten him—and the whole time she'd been fifty yards up the hill behind a wall of her grandfather's making, reading silence as rejection, exactly the way he had. Two people standing on opposite sides of the same locked door, each certain the other had walked away.

He laughed, once, and it came out wrong, closer to something else, and he pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes and stood in the wet grass until the cold climbed his legs and made him go inside.

* * *

Near dawn, he reached the final chapter.

It was short. Four pages. In it, she laid out the choice she'd given him at the press: publish or don't; the contract, the advance, the editor named Ruth Calloway who smelled money the way sharks smell blood; the deadline that would not move. She wrote that she would do whatever he decided, that she meant it, that she knew the words were cheap after ten years of silence and she intended to spend the rest of her life making them expensive.

And then the last paragraph:

*If you are reading this, Jonah, then you know everything, and I am either free or finished. I have been both before and could not always tell the difference. What I know is this: my grandfather stole your family's land to feed a hunger he never named, and I inherited the theft along with the lighthouse, and loving you does not unsteal it. Nothing unsteals it. But I can stop pretending the ledger doesn't exist. I can stand in front of this town and say the names out loud. Whether that is repair or just another kind of damage, I honestly don't know. You're the one who grew up in those rows. Tell me what the land needs. I'll do it.*

Jonah closed the manuscript.

The window over the sink had gone gray, then pale gold at the edges. The coffee he'd never drunk had a skin on it. His eyes felt scoured. He sat in his father's chair while the light came up over the orchard, and he thought about what the land needed, and the answer arrived with the terrible simplicity of things that have been true for a long time and are only now being said.

The land didn't need a memoir.

The land needed the ledger. The original deed, the 1948 filing, the confession—all of it laid out in the grange hall, on a folding table, under fluorescent lights, where the people of Marrow Bay could walk past it and see it with their own eyes. The town deserved to learn what had been done to the Vanes from the town itself, in the town's own voice, at its own pace—not from a hardcover shipped in from New York with a barcode and a marketing plan, not from a stranger's blurb calling their history "searing" and "unputdownable." Their grandparents had shaken hands over that boundary line. Their parents had farmed beside each other, shopped beside each other, buried each other. If the truth was going to cut—and it would cut, deep, in both directions—then it should be cut by hands that would still be here next season to help close the wound.

A publisher's book would make the town a story. The town was not a story. The town was sixty families and a failing orchard and a lighthouse that had lied to all of them for seventy years.

And Ada—Ada deserved better than to be the woman who sold her grandfather's sin to save her career, even if she gave every cent away, even if she renounced everything. Some doors, once opened publicly, never close again. He'd watched it happen to other towns. He wasn't going to watch it happen to this one.

But the publisher had a copy. That was the stone in the shoe of the whole beautiful morning. Ruth Calloway had a typescript in a Manhattan office, and a contract with dates in it, and a company that did not spend seven figures on advances it planned to shelve. He turned that fact over and over as the sun cleared the ridge and lit the rows gold, and he could find no version of it that ended cleanly. Whatever he decided, the decision had a shadow attached.

Still. There was a difference between a shadow and a surrender.

He showered, dressed, and drove up the coast road with the manuscript on the passenger seat, its pages fluttering at the window like something eager to be done with itself.

* * *

She was on the lighthouse gallery when he arrived, and she saw the truck coming and came down the spiral stairs fast enough that she was breathing hard when she opened the door. She looked like she hadn't slept either. She looked, he thought, like a woman who had spent the night standing in a doorway of her own.

"You read it," she said.

"I read it."

"And?"

He held up the manuscript. Then, before she could speak, before either of them could make this harder or easier with words, he walked to the iron fire pit at the base of the tower—the one her grandfather had used to burn driftwood and, Jonah suspected, other things—and he dropped the manuscript into it. He struck a match off the box in his jacket pocket, the way his father had taught him, one motion, and touched it to the corner of the title page.

The paper caught. Ada made a sound—not a word, just a sound, small and involuntary, the sound of a door closing on a hand.

"The town gets its own telling," Jonah said. He watched the flames take the first chapter, the second, the page with his grandfather's name on it, the ink lifting off the burning paper in little ghosts. "At the grange hall. On a table. From the ledger, from the deed, from your mouth if you're willing to stand up and do it. Not from a warehouse in New Jersey. Not with a barcode."

"Jonah—" Her voice cracked on the second syllable. "Ruth has a copy. The deadline—"

"I know." He watched the fire eat the last paragraph, the one about being free or finished. "I know exactly what she has. But listen to me." He turned to face her fully, and the morning wind off the water pushed between them and he stepped through it, closing the distance. "Last night I read my family's ruin written by the granddaughter of the man who caused it, and it was the gentlest thing anyone has ever said to me. Do you understand what that means? It means I'm not angry anymore. It means the anger was a coat I wore for ten years and it never once fit and I'm finally cold enough to admit it."

Her eyes were shining and terrified and fixed on his face like he might still catch fire instead of the paper.

"So we do it our way," he said. "You and me. The ledger goes public before her book does. We call the meeting ourselves—we get ahead of it. Because I promise you, Ada, that woman is not going to sit on a manuscript she paid for. The moment she figures out we're moving, she'll move faster. She'll leak it, serialize it, publish it unauthorized if she has to. We have days. Maybe less."

The fire collapsed inward with a soft rush. Ash lifted on the wind, gray flakes drifting out over the water, and Ada watched them go with an expression he couldn't fully read—grief and relief tangled together, the look of someone setting down a weight and discovering the shape of it printed on her shoulders.

"You burned it," she said quietly. "Nobody's ever burned something for me before."

"I didn't burn it for you." He almost smiled. "I burned it for the town. For Elias. For my dad, who died thinking he was a bad farmer when he was actually a robbed one." He looked out at the water, at the light sweeping its slow arc above them, blind and faithful. "For me, maybe. A little."

She reached out and took his hand. Her fingers were cold and ink-stained and shaking slightly, and he held on.

"Days," she repeated. "Then we start today. The grange hall, the select board, whoever controls the hall keys—"

"Mrs. Okafor," they said together, and despite everything, despite the ash and the deadline and the whole machinery of New York beginning to grind toward them, something passed between them that was almost laughter—the first in ten years that neither of them had to fake.

They stood a moment longer, watching the last of the manuscript curl and blacken, two people planning a war in the ashes of a book.

Neither of them knew yet that Ruth Calloway's email had already gone out at 6:04 that morning—to a literary editor at a national magazine, three lines long, offering an exclusive excerpt of a "devastating true account of fraud and betrayal in a small coastal town," with a photograph of the original deed attached.

The deadline hadn't moved.

It had detonated.
