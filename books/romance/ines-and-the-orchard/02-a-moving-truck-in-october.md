## 2. A Moving Truck in October

The truck came at half past seven in the morning, which was, in Mirabel's opinion, a rude hour for anything to begin.

She was on the second terrace with the Pinot Noir rows, a picking crate balanced on her hip and secateurs in her back pocket, when she heard it — the low diesel grumble of an engine taking the hill road too slowly. She straightened, pressed her wrist to her brow to shade her eyes, and watched it come: a white box truck, unmarked except for a phone number stenciled on the side, crawling past her gate with the particular caution of a driver who has been told *just follow the road until you can't go any further*.

It did not stop at her gate. It continued, rattling over the cattle grid, past the dry stone wall where she'd spent three Aprils coaxing back the moss, up toward the Marchetti place.

Mirabel stood very still among the vines.

The Marchetti orchard had been abandoned for eleven years. Everyone said so; everyone had stopped saying so around year six, because after that long a thing stops being news and becomes landscape. The orchard sat on the ridge north of the Delacroix vineyard like a sleeping animal — forty hectares of apple and pear trees gone leggy and wild, the farmhouse behind them with its shutters hanging by one hinge each, the well cover rotted through. Birds nested in the eaves. Tomas swore a fox had raised two litters under the packing shed. The grass between the rows grew chest-high by June and died back gold by September, and every autumn the windfall apples fermented on the ground and made the whole ridge smell faintly of cider gone wrong, a sweet-sour ghost of the farm it used to be.

Mirabel had grown up with that smell. She had also, for ten years, written letters to someone who signed themselves only as a friend of the Marchetti family, and received answers from someone who called herself M., and neither fact had anything to do with the other, officially speaking, and she was not going to think about either fact before nine in the morning.

But the truck kept going up the hill. And then a second truck followed it. And then a small silver car, driven with more confidence than the trucks, took the bend fast enough to make Mirabel wince on behalf of her fence posts.

"Tomas," she said, when her foreman appeared at the end of the row twenty minutes later with his thermos and his air of a man who has already formed several opinions, "did you see—"

"The moving trucks." He poured coffee into the cap, offered it to her. She shook her head; he drank it himself. "Half the village saw them. Madame Roux saw them first, naturally. She was out at six with her dog."

"Madame Roux is always out at six with her dog."

"That is why she sees everything worth seeing." Tomas leaned against the end post, unhurried. He was sixty-one and had worked the Delacroix land since before Mirabel was born, and he had mastered the art of delivering gossip as though it were field intelligence, grave and measured. "Three trucks. Two men from the agency in town. And the buyer came separately, in the silver car."

"Who?"

"Ah." He drank again, savoring. "That is the question."

"Tomas."

"Madame Roux says the notary's clerk let something slip at the bakery yesterday. He bought his morning bread and he was pleased about something, and Madame Roux asked him directly, because Madame Roux has never once in her life failed to ask directly, and he said—" Tomas paused, purely for effect, and Mirabel resisted the urge to shake him "—he said the sale closed last week. The whole property. Orchard, house, the lot. Cash."

"Cash?" Mirabel frowned. Nobody bought eleven years of neglected orchard for cash. The land alone might be worth something if you flattened the trees and replanted, but that was five years of patience before the first real harvest, and nobody with cash was patient. "Who buys a dead orchard with cash?"

"A romantic," said Tomas. "Or a fool. In my experience the two categories overlap considerably."

He went off down the row whistling, having delivered exactly enough information to ruin her concentration, and Mirabel turned back to her vines and pruned nothing for a full minute, secateurs idle in her hand, watching the ridge.

By ten o'clock there were men on the Marchetti land. She could see them from the upper terraces — small figures moving along the fence line, one of them walking the boundary with what looked like a surveyor's wheel. By noon the shutters were coming off the farmhouse, or rather being reattached; she could hear, faintly on the wind, the sound of a drill. Someone was fixing the shutters. On day one. Before unpacking a single box, apparently, this person had stood in the yard of a derelict farm and decided the shutters mattered.

Mirabel told herself she didn't care. She had a harvest running late, a bank statement she hadn't opened in nine days, and a cellar full of wine that needed racking. She had no time for the private dramas of strangers who bought dead orchards with cash and cared about shutters.

She borrowed the binoculars anyway.

They had belonged to her father. They lived in the drawer of the kitchen dresser, beneath the batteries and the string, and they were older than she was, and using them felt like putting her hands inside his — the leather worn smooth exactly where his thumbs had rested. She stood at the kitchen window, which had a clean line of sight up through the bare autumn poplars to the Marchetti yard, and she told herself this was due diligence. A new neighbor was a practical matter. Fences, water rights, shared roads. It was responsible to know who had moved in forty meters from her property line.

Through the lenses, the yard resolved: the trucks parked nose-to-tail, their backs open, movers carrying furniture with the resigned competence of men being paid by the hour. A woman directed them.

Mirabel found her without meaning to — the eye goes to whoever is giving orders — and held on her longer than due diligence strictly required. Tall, dark-haired, hair tied back with what looked like a strip of cloth rather than an actual tie. She moved through the yard the way Mirabel moved through her own vineyard: knowing where everything was supposed to go, correcting people gently, doing the heavy lifting herself when the movers hesitated. At one point she picked up a crate marked in handwriting too far away to read, carried it into the house, and came out again wiping her hands on her jeans, and she stood for a moment looking south — looking, Mirabel realized with an odd jolt, directly at the Delacroix property, at the roofline, perhaps even at the window where Mirabel stood.

Mirabel stepped back from the glass, heart tapping oddly against her ribs, feeling absurd. The woman couldn't have seen her. Windows reflect; everyone knows windows reflect.

She put the binoculars away and opened the bank statement instead, which was worse.

* * *

The name arrived at four o'clock, riding in on Tomas's shoulder like a delivery he'd been saving all day.

He found her in the cellar, checking barrels she'd checked that morning, and he didn't announce himself, just stood at the foot of the stairs holding his cap until she gave up pretending and said, "Out with it, then."

"You'll want to be sitting down."

"I'm standing in my own cellar, Tomas. Say it."

He came down two steps, which he almost never did; Tomas treated her cellar with the reverence of a church. "Madame Roux struck again. The notary's clerk had lunch at the café. She got the name out of him over the cheese course."

"And?"

"And the buyer of the Marchetti orchard," he said, "is a Marchetti."

The world did a small, slow tilt.

Mirabel reached for the barrel beside her — an automatic gesture, steadying herself on something solid — and her hand closed instead on the tasting glass she'd left on the bung hole, and it went over. It didn't shatter; cellar floors are forgiving that way. It rolled, rang once against stone, and spun to a stop against her boot, and Mirabel stared at it as though it had spoken.

"Ines," said Tomas, into the silence. "Ines Marchetti. Granddaughter of old Elena Marchetti, who owned the place before it went to ruin. The family lost it — debts, the father's death, the usual sad story — and the girl was sent away to relatives somewhere north. Madame Roux remembers her. Used to spend summers here as a child, climbing the apple trees, stealing plums. Then nothing for fifteen years. And now—" he spread his hands "—she comes back. Buys it all back. Every hectare, cash."

Mirabel crouched and picked up the glass. Her fingers weren't entirely steady, and she was grateful Tomas was looking elsewhere, at the barrels, anywhere else.

Ten years of letters. Ten years of Tuesday evenings at the kitchen table with the good lamp on, writing to a stranger who understood things no one else in her life understood — who wrote back about grafting and grief and the strange loneliness of loving land that doesn't love you back, who signed herself M. and never once asked for more than Mirabel offered. Letters that began, a decade ago, with a note found folded inside a bottle washed up against the culvert after the spring flood — a message in a bottle, an absurdity, a child's game — and had become, without either of them ever deciding it, the truest relationship of Mirabel's life.

And the return address on those letters — the address Mirabel had mailed her replies to, season after season, an address in a northern city she'd never visited — had been given to her by a correspondent who wrote about orchards. About apples. About a grandmother's grafting knives.

"Oh," Mirabel said. Just that. A small syllable, dropped into the cellar like the glass.

"You've gone pale," Tomas observed. "Sit down before you follow the glass."

"It's nothing. It's—" She laughed, and it came out wrong, high and thin. "It's a coincidence, that's all. Common name. Half of Italy is named Marchetti."

"Mm," said Tomas, who had known her since she was six and did not believe her for a second, but had the grace to pretend otherwise. "Well. Coincidence or not, you'll have a neighbor by nightfall. I'd say bring over a welcome basket, but we haven't got anything welcome. We've got wine and debt."

"We have wine," Mirabel said automatically. "The debt is separate."

He climbed the stairs, pausing at the top. "For what it's worth," he said, not turning around, "old Elena Marchetti was a good woman. Your father thought the world of her. If the granddaughter's anything like her, the ridge will be better with her on it than empty."

Then he was gone, and Mirabel was alone with the barrels and the rolling glass and the sound of her own pulse.

* * *

That evening she cooked without tasting any of it. Polenta, because it required stirring and stirring required nothing of her mind. Through the window over the sink, the Marchetti farmhouse showed lights now — warm squares in the dark, upstairs and down, the first light that property had held in eleven years. A figure crossed one window, silhouetted, carrying something. Unpacking. Settling in. Forty meters and one fence line away, someone was making a home out of the ruin.

Mirabel dried her hands and went to the dresser and took out the shoebox.

It lived behind the winter linens, which fooled no one, least of all herself. Inside: ten years of letters, tied in bundles by year with butcher's twine, each bundle soft-edged from handling. She untied the most recent — last spring's, only a handful, because the replies had slowed lately, arriving later, growing shorter, and Mirabel had lain awake more than once wondering what she'd done wrong, whether M. was ill, whether M. had finally found someone whose letters didn't have to travel through the post.

She read the last one again, though she knew it by heart.

*Mireille — forgive the delay. We are selling the house. My grandmother's house, or what remains of it, and I find I cannot write about it lightly, so this letter will be short. There is a place I may go soon. South. Near vines, near hills. If it works out, I will tell you where, and you may tell me it's a foolish idea, and I will ignore you, as is our custom.*

South. Near vines, near hills.

Mirabel set the letter down on the counter, next to the cooling polenta, and looked out the window at the lit windows on the ridge, and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do all day. A correspondent who wrote about orchards. A grandmother with grafting knives. A sale, a departure, a move south. An orchard bought back, in cash, by a Marchetti who had spent childhood summers stealing plums.

It couldn't be. The odds were absurd. The world was large; the post was slow; coincidences were Tomas's department, along with Madame Roux and the notary's clerk. There were thousands of orchardists, hundreds of grandmothers, dozens of towns with vines and hills. The letters would continue as they always had — arriving from that northern address, or wherever M. landed next — and the woman in the silver car would prove to be a stranger, and Mirabel would bring her a bottle of wine across the fence like a normal neighbor, and that would be the end of it.

Except.

Except that M.'s letters had always smelled faintly, impossibly, of apples — Mirabel had decided years ago this was her imagination, paper absorbing some orchard air from the sender's hands. Except that M. had once described, in a letter three winters ago, a scar on her left palm from a grafting knife, earned at age nine, and Mirabel had read that sentence standing in her own kitchen and felt it land somewhere below her ribs and stay there. Except that when Mirabel tried, just now, to imagine the woman from the binoculars writing those letters — the sure hands, the way she'd carried the crate, the strip of cloth in her hair — the imagining fit so perfectly it frightened her.

The polenta went cold. The lights across the fence went out one by one, upstairs last, and Mirabel stood at her own dark window for a long time afterward, holding a letter she suddenly could not put down.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow I'll walk up the hill with a bottle and settle it. Neighbors meet neighbors. It's what anyone would do.

She rehearsed it as she washed up, the way she rehearsed everything: *Welcome to the valley. I'm Mirabel Delacroix, from the vineyard just south.* Simple. Innocent. A sentence with nothing hidden in it.

She only hoped her voice wouldn't give her away — and she only admitted, somewhere past midnight, lying awake listening to the wind come down off the ridge through eleven empty years of apple trees, how much it would cost her if the woman in the farmhouse turned out to be exactly who Mirabel was afraid she was.

Because if she was — if the impossible arithmetic came out right — then for ten years Mirabel had told a stranger everything. And the stranger had moved in forty meters away. And sooner or later, one of them was going to say something true.

The bottle for the welcome basket, she decided, would be the good one.
