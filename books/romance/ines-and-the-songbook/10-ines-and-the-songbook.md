## 10. Ines and the Songbook

The book arrived on a Tuesday, in a box of forty advance copies that smelled of glue and new paper and something faintly like the inside of a printing press—hot metal memory, ink still settling into its bones. I sat on the farmhouse steps with the box between my knees for a long time before I opened it.

On the cover: an orchard at dusk, rendered in watercolor so loose it might have been painted from memory rather than life. A single tree in the foreground, its trunk split by a seam of gold. Three names were worked into the bark in letters small enough to miss unless you knew to look.

I knew to look. I had asked for them myself.

*Ines Dravenmoor*, said the title page. Not Vesper Lark. Not the false name I'd worn like a coat against weather for ten years. My own name, printed twice—once as author, once, in the dedication, as granddaughter.

*For the three names in the tree,* it read. *May they all be spoken now.*

My hands shook. Not from fear this time. From the strange vertigo of standing in open ground after a decade spent indoors.

The hearing was four months behind us. The fraud was public record now—the clerical sleight of hand by which Aldous Merrick, the orchard's previous owner, had folded a fourth family out of their own land in 1961 and called it paperwork. The Reyes family's descendants had their name back on the deed. Marisol Reyes came up from the valley most weekends now, her sons scrambling over the fence lines my grandmother had once measured, learning the trees the way you learn a language: slowly, by immersion, by getting your hands sticky.

And Silas—

Silas was in the orchard.

I could see him from the steps, moving down the rows with a crate on his hip, checking the late apples for windfall. He moved through the trees the way he always had, even when we were young and the orchard belonged to neither of us: like a man greeting old friends who owed him nothing and gave him everything anyway. His grandfather had worked this land and been cheated of it. Now his grandson walked it freely, and some mornings I caught him just standing among the rows with his face tipped up to the light, as if listening for something.

For a songbook, maybe. That was what we'd come to call it—the orchard's own music, the way the wind moved through the branches in October and made every tree hum a half-tone apart. His grandfather had written about it in a journal we found in the courthouse archives, pages of it, the old man transcribing what he heard like a hymn he wasn't allowed to sing aloud.

*They took the land but they cannot take the song of it,* he'd written. *A man can be erased from a deed. Not from the roots.*

I had stolen that line. It was in the book now, page 212, in the mouth of a character named after no one and everyone.

I set the box down, took one copy, and carried it out to him.

* * *

He was at the oldest tree—the one with the initials, E.D., S.M., and R.R. carved into the heartwood where the lightning scar had split the bark decades ago. Our three families, cut into wood by hands we would never meet, waiting all this time for someone to read them correctly.

"Early," he said, without turning around. He always knew my footsteps now. "Harvest's not till Friday."

"I'm not here for harvest." I held the book out. "It came."

He wiped his hands on his jeans—a habit, since the crate held nothing dirty—and took it with both hands, the way you take something breakable or holy. He turned it over once. Read the cover. Read the dedication. When he reached the title page and saw my name there, plain and unhidden, something moved through his face like weather crossing a field.

"You used it," he said. "Your real name."

"All of it." I sat down against the trunk, in the crook where the roots rose out of the earth like the shoulders of something sleeping. "Publisher thought I was insane. Ten years of brand recognition, they said. Ten years of readers who know the other name. I told them the other name was a locked room and I was done living in it."

"And they said?"

"They said, *yes, Ms. Dravenmoor.*" I smiled. "Turns out being right about a land fraud in open court buys you a certain authority."

He laughed—that low, surprised laugh I had spent ten years writing into villains because I couldn't bear to write it into anyone I loved. Then he sat beside me, our shoulders touching, and opened the book.

"Read it to me," I said. "Out loud. Here."

"In the orchard?"

"It's an orchard book. Where else would it live?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Dusk was coming down through the rows, laying long blue shadows across the grass, and the first of the evening birds were starting their arguments in the hedgerow. Somewhere beyond the fence line, Marisol's truck rattled up the lane, and her boys' voices carried, bright and unbroken.

Then Silas Vane, who had not read aloud to anyone since he was a boy, cleared his throat and began.

* * *

He read the way he pruned: carefully, without hurry, giving each sentence the space it needed to bear weight. The opening chapter was set in a lighthouse cottage—I'd stolen that too, shamelessly—and by the second page he was doing voices, a gruff keeper, a girl who wrote secrets into the margins of tide tables.

"That's you," he said, tapping the page. "That's exactly you. You did this at fourteen. I found your notebook in the churchyard once. You'd written a whole novel about a smuggler with my exact jawline."

"He was a villain."

"He was a villain with excellent bone structure." He turned the page. "You've improved him since."

"Have I?"

"Immeasurably." But he said it quietly, and his thumb rested on the margin, and I understood that he was not talking about the smuggler anymore.

The light went from gold to copper to that deep bruised violet that only happens over orchards in autumn, when the air itself seems to remember summer fondly but has already agreed to let it go. He read through the middle chapters—the false names, the locked rooms, the heroine who hid behind a pen name because visibility had once cost her everything. He read the courtroom scene without stopping, though his voice roughened at the line where she stood up and said her name, all of it, into the record.

When he reached the sentence—the rehearsed one, the one I had carried for a decade like a stone in my shoe and finally spoke on the courthouse steps with cameras rolling—he stopped reading and looked at me.

"You wrote it differently," he said.

"A little."

"On the steps you said it plainer. Simpler. Like it was just a fact." He closed the book gently on his finger. "*I loved him then and I love him still.* Four words each side. No ornament."

"Ornament is for people who are afraid the truth won't hold on its own."

"And now?"

"Now I know it holds." I took the book from him, found my place, and read the last line aloud myself, there under the oldest tree with the three names in its heart:

*Love, she understood at last, was not a thing you survived. It was a thing you tended—pruned in winter, trusted in spring, pressed sweet and tart and honest at the end of every growing season. It did not ask to be hidden. It asked only to be kept.*

Silas didn't say anything. He leaned over and kissed my temple, and then he did something better than saying anything: he picked the reading back up and finished the chapter, and started the next, because the dusk was warm and the story wasn't done and neither were we.

* * *

Marisol found us there an hour later, full dark, the book lit by the lantern Silas had fetched from the shed. Her boys were already asleep in the truck cab, folded against each other like commas.

"There's cider from the first pressing," she said, holding up two jars. "And I heard my grandmother's maiden name in chapter nine. R.R., page 212. Don't think I missed it."

"You were meant to find it," I said. "All three of you were. That's why it's dedicated that way."

She sat down on the grass with us, passed the jars around, and for a while none of us talked. The orchard hummed its October chord overhead. The stars came out between the rows like fruit too high to pick.

"Next season," Marisol said eventually, "we should plant something new. Something that's ours from the start. All four deeds' worth of ours."

"Pears," Silas said. "My grandfather wanted pears. There's a page about it."

"There's a page about everything," I said, "if you dig deep enough. That's the whole trick of it. That's the whole book."

Marisol laughed and clinked her jar against ours, and the sound rang out flat and clear across the rows, one note added to the orchard's song.

Later, walking back to the cottage with the lantern between us, Silas took my hand—not the careful hand he'd offered at the start of the season, all terms and coexistence, but the easy one, the settled one, the hand that had learned where mine lived.

"The epilogue," he said. "Of the book. You never let me read it."

"It's short."

"How short?"

"One line." We reached the porch step where the whole thing had begun, the deed and the dread and the almost-burning, and I stopped there and looked up at him under the lighthouse beam sweeping its slow white arm across the sky. "It says: *and she stayed.*"

He smiled, and the beam swept past us, and the dark that followed wasn't dark at all.

"Read it again," he said.

So I did.
