## 7. Marren's Last Clear Day

*Marren*

The fog in her head had lifted overnight, the way it sometimes did, and Marren woke on the first of March knowing exactly who she was, where she was, and — this was the rare part — how long she might have. She lay still a moment, listening to the house. Wind in the chimney. The sea working at the rocks below like an old woman kneading dough. And voices downstairs, low and careful, the way people talk when they think they're being quiet about something.

She knew that carefulness. She'd invented half its dialects herself over eighty-one years.

She got up, dressed without help — a small victory she intended to enjoy privately — and went down to find her granddaughter and that Marsh boy sitting at opposite ends of the kitchen table like two chess players pretending they weren't playing chess. There was a pot of tea between them, untouched, going cold. There was a manuscript face-down beside Ivo's elbow, as if paper could be embarrassed.

"Well," Marren said. "One of you is going to have to tell me what year it is, because I've lost track again."

Ivo was up instantly, hands fluttering. "Gran. It's March. 2024. Do you want toast? Theo's here, he got stranded in the—"

"I know who he is." She looked at him properly. Young man, good bones, tired around the eyes in a way that wasn't from lack of sleep but from carrying something too long. "You're the one who writes the sentences with all the weather in them."

Theo blinked. "I — thank you? I think?"

"It's a compliment. Mostly." She lowered herself into her chair at the head of the table, the one with the cushion embroidered with gulls that Ivo had done at fourteen, all beaks and fury. "Now. I'm going to be clear today, and I don't know for how long, so I'd like everyone to stop performing and pour me some tea while it's still warm enough to drink."

The kettle went back on. The tea got poured. And Marren watched them do it — watched Ivo hand him the cup he liked, the big chipped blue one, without thinking; watched Theo take it the same way, without thinking either — and thought: *Ah. There it is. Still there. Forty years and one broken decade later, and there it is.*

She had spent a lifetime reading water. This was easier.

* * *

*Ivo*

There was a version of her grandmother that only appeared on clear days, and Ivo had learned to recognize the signs the way sailors learn weather: the straight back, the sharp eye, the way Marren said *performing* like a woman who had never once done it herself.

They sat through breakfast under her scrutiny. Theo ate his eggs like a man being graded. Ivo kept almost telling him about the press statement she'd found folded in his coat pocket two nights ago — *the Whitmore shortlist, one winner, bury Underhill's book gently* — and kept not telling him, because Gran was lucid and lucid mornings were finite and Ivo refused to spend this one bleeding.

But Marren had never once let anyone else set the agenda.

"Right," she said, setting down her cup. "Ivo. Take me up to the lamp room after breakfast. Both of you. And before you make faces — yes, both. I haven't got the strength I had and if I fall on those stairs I'd rather it were witnessed by someone who can lift more than a grudge."

"Gran," Ivo said carefully.

"Don't *Gran* me. I'm having a clear day and I intend to spend it like money."

* * *

*Theo*

The lamp room smelled of brass polish and cold glass and, underneath it, salt so old it had become part of the walls. Marren climbed the last steps ahead of them, refusing an arm, one hand on the rail, breathing hard but arriving.

From up here Gullwrack was laid out like a diagram: the town huddled gray and gold behind the seawall, the causeway a dark seam just showing above the water, the flats beyond it shining where the tide had gone out and left the sand bare as a confession.

"Forty years ago," Marren said, without preamble, "a man walked out onto those flats at low water and didn't walk back."

Theo went very still.

"His name was Aloys. He was my husband for six years and my sweetheart for nine before that, and he was the kind of fool who believed the tide ran on a schedule he could argue with." She said it evenly, the way you'd read a shipping forecast. "They found his boat. They never found him. And I stood at that window—" she pointed at the gallery below, the narrow room where she'd slept for fifty years "—and I waited. Every evening. For forty years, I waited for a silhouette on the causeway."

Ivo made a small sound, and Marren raised a hand without looking at her.

"I'm not finished. Waiting felt like love. That's the trick of it. It feels like loyalty, like devotion, like the one decent thing left to do. But waiting is a choice, girl. It's a choice you make every single morning, and I made mine for forty years, and it did not bring him back and it did not make me happy and it did not even make me interesting at parties." She turned then, and her eyes — Marren's eyes, quick and bright and merciless — went from Ivo to Theo and back. "It just made me old in a particular shape."

"Gran," Ivo whispered, "what are you saying?"

"I'm saying I've watched the two of you circle each other for a week like moths who've read too much about flames. I'm saying whatever happened between you — and don't tell me, I don't want the inventory, I want the outcome — you are both standing at that window. One of you is waiting to be forgiven and won't ask. One of you is waiting to forgive and won't say so. And meanwhile the tide," and here her voice did something none of them expected, something almost gentle, "the tide does not care. The tide took Aloys. It will take me, probably within the year, and it will take everything else eventually too, and the only thing it cannot take is a thing you choose out loud."

The wind pressed at the glass. Below them the flats gleamed, patient, enormous.

"So choose," Marren said. "Out loud. While your mouths still work and your heads are still your own. That's the whole of my wisdom. Forty years compressed into four words. You're welcome."

* * *

*Ivo*

Afterward, in Marren's room, her grandmother opened the bottom drawer of the dresser — the stuck one, the one Ivo had been told since childhood never to force — and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone soft as cloth.

The logbooks. Dozens of them. Her grandfather's handwriting for the early decades, neat as print: *Wind NW, moderate. Lamp lit 6:14. Marren made pie.* Then, somewhere in the seventies, the handwriting changed, and Ivo realized with a jolt that these were Marren's own — that after Aloys was taken she had kept the log herself, and had kept it for forty years, long past any legal requirement, long past any reason.

"What's in them?" Ivo asked, though her hands were already untying the string.

"Weather," Marren said. "Mostly weather. Some of it isn't." She sat down heavily on the bed, suddenly looking every one of her eighty-one years, the clear morning spending itself down to its last coins. "A book needs a spine, Ivo. Yours has been all nerve and no bone. His too — don't look surprised, I read, I get the *Gullwrack Gazette*, I know what the Whitmore is." She waved off Ivo's start. "Those logs are forty years of a woman talking to a man who couldn't answer. If that isn't a novel, nothing is. Take them. Write it together or don't write it at all, but for God's sake stop writing *apart*. Apart is how you both got so tedious."

Ivo laughed, and it came out wet, and Marren patted her knee twice, briskly, which was how she said *there, now* and had said it since Ivo was six.

"And the other thing?" Ivo asked quietly. "The thing you actually brought us up the stairs for?"

Marren looked at her for a long moment. Outside, the light was changing — thickening, the bright hard morning going soft and strange at the edges, the way it did before weather.

"The barometer's falling faster than I've seen since '78," she said. "And I can feel a fog coming back into my head, sitting out there like a bank of it, waiting. So whatever you two are going to say to each other — say it today. Not tomorrow. Not when the papers are signed and the ferry runs and it's convenient." Her grip tightened on Ivo's hand, sudden and strong. "Clear days run out, child. That's not a warning. It's just the tide."

Then she lay back against her pillows, closed her eyes, and when she opened them again a minute later she smiled pleasantly at Ivo and asked, in a voice gone vague and sweet, whether the young man staying with them was married, because he seemed like such a nice boy and she hated to see nice boys go to waste.

* * *

*Theo*

He found Ivo in the kitchen, the oilcloth bundle open on the table, her hands flat on the topmost logbook as if holding down something alive.

"You heard all that," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Every word." He came around the table slowly. "Ivo — the press statement, the shortlist, I never sent it, I called my editor and told her—"

"I know. I saw the email. Two days ago." She looked up at him, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "I've been waiting for you to tell me yourself."

The words landed like cold water. *Waiting,* he thought. *At the window.*

Outside, the light had gone the color of pewter, and far out past the causeway the sea had begun, very quietly, to stand up.
