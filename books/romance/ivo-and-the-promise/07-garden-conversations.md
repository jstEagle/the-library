## 7. Garden Conversations

**Edmund**

The fence between our properties was six feet of weathered cedar, and I had spent three weeks treating it like a border checkpoint.

I knew the exact hours he came outside—mid-morning for coffee, late afternoon when the light went gold and the heat let go of the day. I timed my watering to avoid his. When his gate creaked, I became very interested in my tomatoes, which were dying, because I had never learned to garden and had bought them only as a prop. A woman with a vegetable bed reads as rooted. As local. As someone whose life has a shape you could sketch from memory.

Then one Tuesday in June, he caught me at it.

"You're drowning those," Ivo said over the fence.

I looked down at the tomato plants, which lay against their stakes like passengers asleep on a long flight. "They like water."

"Nobody's tomatoes like that much water." He was leaning on the fence with both forearms, and up close I could see what the cane and the distance had hidden: he was thinner than a man should be, the bones of his wrists prominent, his skin carrying that faint translucence of someone who'd spent too many months under fluorescent lights. But his eyes were quick. Amused. The eyes of someone who had decided something about me already and found it entertaining.

"I'll take it under advisement," I said.

"Take this instead." He held out a tomato of his own, absurdly perfect, deep red, still warm from the sun. "Beefsteak. My nonna's method. She'd have disowned me if she saw what you're doing to yours."

I took it because refusing would have required a story about why, and stories are currency I hoard. The tomato was warm in my palm like something alive.

"Edmund," I said, because we hadn't formally done this, and names are the smallest possible gift.

"Ivo." He didn't offer a surname, and neither did I, and there was a strange relief in that—a mutual agreement to conduct ourselves at the level of first names only, two people who had apparently materialized out of nothing, gardening badly and well respectively on either side of a cedar border.

That was how it began. Not with suspicion, which I could have managed, but with ease, which I could not.

* * *

**Ivo**

Here is what I told her about myself, in pieces, across the first weeks of summer:

That I'd been a structural engineer in Chicago until my body staged its mutiny. That bridges were the only argument I'd ever cared about winning—the math either holds or it doesn't, no committee required. That I'd come out here because rent was cheap and the light reminded me of somewhere I couldn't afford to live anymore.

All true. None of it complete.

I did not tell her about the letters.

This was not strategy, exactly. Or maybe it was. There is a version of telling her—Ivo Marchetti moves to a town because a stranger's letters carried a postmark, and spends his mornings hoping the woman next door will say something that cracks her open—that sounds less like romance and more like surveillance. I wanted to be a neighbor first. I wanted whatever grew between us to grow without the letters underneath it, propping it up, weighting every word with expectation.

So I omitted them, and discovered that omission is its own kind of intimacy. You build a person a smaller house than the truth requires, and then you both live in it, and it fits so well you start to believe the house is everything.

She never asked what brought me here. I noticed that. Most people ask within the first conversation—it's the question you hand strangers like a ticket stub. Edmund never did. She talked about soil pH and the ethics of grocery store flowers and whether the town's one bookstore should stock more poetry (it should; she'd checked). She asked questions the way some people give gifts—generously, and without expecting change back.

And when I asked her anything real—where she was from, what she'd been before this—she answered smoothly, completely, and left me holding nothing. She'd say she was from "back east," that she wrote, that writing was quiet work and suited her. Her voice never wavered. Her hands, though. Her hands would find something to do. A weed to pull. A tag on a tomato cage to straighten. As if the truth lived in her fingers and she had to keep them busy so it wouldn't climb out.

I started watching her hands. I'm an engineer. We're trained to look where the load is bearing.

* * *

**Edmund**

He told me about bridges. He told me about his sister, Chiara, who called every Sunday and had strong opinions about his diet. He told me about Chicago winters and a dog named Bruno who'd died at fourteen and was, Ivo maintained, the finest creature ever to walk the earth.

He did not mention the letters. Not once. And every day he didn't mention them, I understood something new about the architecture of my cowardice: I had assumed that being near him would mean waiting for discovery, braced like a woman under a falling chandelier. Instead he simply... talked to me. About mulch. About the Cubs. About whether the town needed a second traffic light (it did not; he'd run the numbers).

The relief was worse than fear. Fear I knew how to carry. Relief made me greedy.

By the third week I was timing my watering *for* him instead of against him. By the fourth, I'd stopped rehearsing excuses before going outside. I caught myself standing at the fence forty minutes over a conversation that had, strictly speaking, been about compost, and walking back inside feeling lit from somewhere internal, some generator I'd thought was broken.

This is what frightened me. Not that he might discover me—but that I might, in these small unguarded minutes, become visible to myself again. That I might remember what it felt like to be a person who could be known, and then have to go back to being a ghost in my own kitchen, eating dinner alone, answering emails under a name that wasn't mine, being no one with great professionalism.

My editor called while I was deadheading the marigolds. Julian Ashe's memoir, chapter four, due Friday. I took the call inside, but I could see the fence through the window, and Ivo moving along it, and I thought: I am lying to two men at once now. One pays me. One grows tomatoes.

Only one of them makes me hate it.

* * *

**Ivo**

The afternoon I almost asked her, she was telling me about her brother.

It came out sideways, the way real things do. We'd been talking about the bookstore, and she mentioned a novel she'd loved as a girl, and then she said, "My brother gave it to me," and then—stopped. Like a hiker stepping to the edge of a drop and deciding against the view.

"He was a reader?" I asked.

"He was a writer." Her hands went to the tomato cage immediately, straightening a tag that did not need straightening. "Good one. Better than—" She smiled, and it was the most effortful smile I'd ever seen, beautiful and load-bearing. "Anyway. He'd have liked you. He liked people who built things."

"Was?" I said gently. Past tense does a lot of work in a sentence like that.

"A long time ago," she said. "Chicago, actually. That's partly why I ended up out here. Distance."

And there it was—Chicago, my city, offered up like a coin she didn't know she was spending—and I felt the net tighten around my own chest, tenderly, the way it had been tightening all summer. Because I knew things about Chicago too. I knew a hospital room on the eleventh floor, and a nurse named Dolores who read mail aloud, and ten years of letters from someone who signed herself only *E.* and knew things about the city no tourist would know—the way the el groans at night like a ship, the bakery on Western that fries its doughnuts in view of the street, the exact blue the lake turns in November, a blue you have to have grieved beside to recognize.

Whoever wrote those letters had grieved beside that lake. Had stood where I stood. Had lost someone the way I was losing myself.

"My sister thinks I moved here to die," I heard myself say. It surprised us both. "Chiara. She thinks the town is a hospice with better landscaping."

"And is it?"

"No." I looked at her over the fence, at the dying tomatoes and the careful face and the hands finally still. "I moved here because of a letter."

Her face did something extraordinary. Nothing moved—no muscle flinched, no breath caught—and yet everything changed, the way a room changes when a door opens somewhere else in the house. A draft. A pressure shift. She heard it. I watched her hear it.

"What kind of letter?" she said, and her voice was perfectly level, and I suddenly understood that this woman could lie to federal investigators without raising her pulse, and that I loved her anyway, or perhaps because the lie was so clearly costing her everything she had.

"The kind you write back to," I said.

* * *

**Edmund**

*The kind you write back to.*

I stood at the fence holding a pair of pruning shears I had no idea how to use and felt the whole summer tilt beneath me. He was testing me. Or confessing. Or simply telling the truth into the space between us to see what it would strike—there was no way to know, and his face gave nothing away except patience.

I had three exits. I could play dumb—*people still write letters?* I could redirect—he'd taught me that trick myself, in a sense; I'd written it into a politician's memoir once, the artful swerve. Or I could stand there and let the silence answer for me, and watch whatever we'd built collapse under the weight of ten years of paper.

What I did instead was worse than any of them. I got curious.

"What did the letter say?" I asked. "The one that brought you here?"

Something moved behind his eyes—not triumph, exactly. Something more tired. As if he'd hoped I'd make it easy and wasn't surprised that I hadn't.

"It said," he began, and then stopped, and looked past me at the tomatoes, at the ruined, drowned, faithful tomatoes, "that November on the lake looks like grief. Who says that? Who knows that? You'd have to have stood there. You'd have to have lost something at that water's edge and gone back anyway."

"I've seen the lake in November," I said quietly. Which was true. Which was the most dangerous sentence I'd spoken in twelve years, because it was true, and because I watched him understand it was true, and because neither of us did anything with that knowledge. We just held it between us over the fence, hot and fragile as the tomato he'd given me weeks ago.

For a moment—one full rotation of the earth, it felt like—we were both perfectly honest. Two people who had each confessed something without saying it. His illness. My brother. The lake. The letters, unnamed, hanging in the air like the scent after rain.

Then his phone rang inside the house—Chiara, probably, Sunday-adjacent, checking his diet—and the moment broke the way moments do, apologetically, irreversibly.

"I should get that," he said.

"Yes," I said.

He paused at his door, cane in hand, and looked back at me with an expression I couldn't file anywhere. "Edmund. Whoever wrote those letters—she saved my life. Not metaphorically. There were nights the letters were the only reason—" He shook his head. "Anyway. If she's out there, I hope she knows that."

He went inside. I stood in my garden among the casualties of my neglect, holding pruning shears against my chest like a talisman, and understood that the summer was over—not the season, the fiction. He knew. Maybe not my name, maybe not my face, but he knew the shape of me the way you know a melody through a wall.

That night I sat at my desk with Julian Ashe's manuscript open to chapter four and could not type a word, because every sentence sounded like a lie told by a woman who had just been given a way out and hadn't taken it.

In the morning, there was an envelope in my mailbox. No stamp. Hand-delivered. Inside, a single index card in Ivo's careful engineer's print:

*Test question. The doughnut place on Western—what color was the awning?*

Below it, smaller:

*No pressure. But I've been looking for someone for ten years, and you're the first person who's ever looked back.*

I sat down on my front steps with the card in my shaking hands, and for the first time since I'd died twelve years ago, I had no idea what to write back.
