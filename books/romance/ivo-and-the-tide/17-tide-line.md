## 17. Tide Line

The tide does not care that you are early. That is the first thing I relearn, standing at the water's edge at six in the evening with my shoes in my hand and my heart doing something arrhythmic and undignified in my chest. The sea comes in on its own schedule, indifferent to appointments, to letters, to eleven years of accumulated grievance. It simply arrives, folds itself over the sand, and withdraws, taking a little of everything with it each time.

I had told myself I wouldn't come. I had told myself this all through the confrontation at the estate — that strange, suspended hour when Beatrix held out her letter like evidence at a trial and I produced mine, the forged reply, and we stood there in the orangery like two archaeologists who had dug up the same skeleton from opposite ends. For one hour we had been twenty-three again. Then my grandmother had appeared in the doorway with her cane and her smile, and we had both retreated so fast into duty you could hear the armor clicking shut.

But I couldn't sleep after that. I lay in my room above the east wing listening to the wind worry the shutters, thinking about the letter she'd shown me — *meet me at the tide line, midnight, the night before your father's party* — and thinking about how I had spent that particular midnight eleven years ago pacing my father's study, waiting for a reply that never came, while she had been standing somewhere in this same dark, on this same sand, waiting for me.

Two people, one beach, no meeting. You could not invent it. Well — apparently someone did. My grandmother's secretary, more precisely, but the effect was the same: fiction where our lives should have been.

So at half past five I found myself walking down the cliff path with my shoes off, because some part of me — the part that remembered being twenty-three and stupidly, gloriously certain — insisted that if you are going to keep an appointment eleven years late, you should at least feel the sand under your feet.

She came at seven.

I heard her before I saw her — the crunch of her steps on the shingle above the tide line, unhurried, deliberate, the walk of a woman who has decided not to run even though every nerve is telling her to. She was wearing a grey coat over whatever she wore to plan weddings, and her hair was down, which I had almost forgotten it ever was. She stopped about ten feet away, at the exact border where the wet sand gleamed like hammered pewter, and looked at me the way you look at a house you once lived in and can't decide whether to enter.

"You came," she said.

"I came." I turned the letter over in my hand — her letter, or rather my letter to her, the one she'd finally opened. "Eleven years late. I'm told punctuality is a virtue."

"Only in weddings," she said. "In love it's mostly a rumor."

We stood there while the water came in and went out. The light was doing that thing coastal light does in October, turning everything the color of old silver, and I thought absurdly that she would have opinions about it — that she would know what flowers matched this hour, what fabric, what music. Eleven years of building a career on beauty, and here she was standing in the most beautiful light of the season looking like a woman at a funeral.

"I've been trying to work out what to say," I admitted. "I had a whole speech. There were bullet points."

"There were *bullet points*?"

"Three of them. Very organized. Accusation, reconciliation, forward planning."

"That sounds like one of my timelines."

"It was meant as a compliment."

She laughed — a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody — and then pressed her lips together, and I understood that we were both terrified, and that naming it would only make it worse. So instead I did the thing I had decided to do on the cliff path, the thing that took more courage than any speech: I started at the beginning, and I didn't stop, and I didn't accuse anyone.

* * *

"My father called me into his study the morning after you left," I said. "You remember the study — the ugly desk, the stuffed trout. He sat there with your letter in front of him — the one you wrote him, the goodbye — and he let me read it. And then he handed me the reply. Typed. Cold. 'Miss Winterbourne acknowledges the impossibility of the situation and requests no further contact.' Signed with your name but not by your hand — I know that now, I didn't know it then. I was twenty-three and my father was telling me the woman I loved had weighed me against her future and chosen the future. What was I supposed to do? Argue with paper?"

"No," she said quietly. "You were supposed to do exactly what you did. Believe it. That was the design."

"The design." I tasted the word. "My grandmother designed us apart. Do you understand what that means? Not fate, not ambition, not incompatibility. A woman in her eighties with a fountain pen and a grudge against anything she couldn't control. She decided I would marry well and stay local and be useful, and you were — what did she call you?"

"She didn't call me anything to my face," Beatrix said. "That was the elegance of it. She never insulted me. She just made arrangements." She hugged the coat closer around herself. "She summoned me two days before your father's party. Tea in the yellow drawing room. She told me the family had been generous letting the relationship go on as long as it had, that you had a future that required — her word was *stability* — and that girls like me eventually wanted things boys like you couldn't give them. She said it kindly, Ivo. That's what I can't get over. Every cruelty she did to me was wrapped in kindness, in concern, in *sensible advice*. And then she said that if I left quietly, there would be no scene, no scandal, and your father's health — which was already failing — would be spared the strain. If I didn't leave quietly, she would make sure I was ruined before I was twenty-four. She knew people. She had reach. I believed her. I still believe her, honestly. Look at what she's doing to Allegra's wedding."

"And instead of telling me any of this—"

"Instead of telling you, I wrote you a letter explaining everything, sealed it, and then — this is the part I've been ashamed of for eleven years — I didn't send it. I couldn't. Because sending it meant asking you to choose, and she'd made it clear what choosing me would cost you. Your inheritance. Your father's peace. Possibly your father himself, given his heart. So I mailed a different letter — a short one, a coward's letter, a goodbye with no reasons in it — and I kept the true one in a drawer for a decade until my mother died and I found yours underneath my old things, unopened, postmarked the week I disappeared." Her voice caught and steadied. "And your letter asked me to meet you here. Midnight. The night before the party. I never knew. She intercepted my mail too — I confirmed it with her secretary last week, God help me, I actually interviewed the woman, she cried. Your letter sat in a box in the Contessa's writing room for eleven years until your father died and she lost track of which papers mattered."

The tide slid up and touched our feet, cold as truth, and withdrew.

"So there it is," I said. "End to end. She intercepted your mail. She dictated a forgery. She threatened you into silence and threatened me into obedience, and then she watched us both suffer for a decade and called it family stewardship." I shook my head. "Do you know what the worst part is? Not the losing. The arithmetic. I have spent eleven years doing sums — if she hadn't done X, if you hadn't done Y — and it turns out the entire equation was falsified. There was nothing to forgive in you. There may be nothing to forgive in me. We were two people standing on a beach waiting for each other, and a third person locked the doors."

Beatrix was crying now, quietly, without drama, the way she seemed to do everything — as if even grief had been put through quality control. "I built a company on that night," she said. "Did I tell you that? Everything Winterbourne & Co. is — the checklists, the contingencies, the motto about beauty over chaos — it's all scaffolding around the shape of that night. I decided that if joy could be taken away by other people's arrangements, then I would become the person who made the arrangements. Control disguised as service. I planned four hundred weddings, Ivo, and I never once believed in a single one of them, and I told myself that was professionalism." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, an unguarded gesture that undid me more than the tears. "And now your grandmother has offered me a partnership. Contingent on silence. Again. The same deal, eleven years later, with better terms. And some tired, frightened part of me wants to sign it, because the company saved my life — my mother's debts, the bankruptcy, all of it — and I don't know how to be a person without the scaffolding."

"Don't sign it," I said.

"I haven't signed it."

"But you're going to think about signing it."

"Of course I'm going to think about it. I'm a planner. Thinking is the whole job." She looked at me then, really looked, and something in her face shifted from testimony to question. "What about you? Where do you stand tomorrow, when she makes her move?"

I looked down the beach toward the house, lit gold against the dusk, all that beautiful architecture resting on a foundation of forged letters. "I signed the venue contract yesterday," I said. "With a shaking pen. Allegra accused me of caring more about you than her happiness, and she wasn't wrong, and my grandmother reminded me exactly whose estate this is. I am, as of this week, the obedient executor of a lie." I laughed, and it came out bitter. "I used to think loyalty was a virtue. Now I think it's a cage with good lighting."

"And what do we do about cages?"

"We—" I stopped. The honest answer was forming itself somewhere below language, in the place where I was still twenty-three and the tide was coming in and a girl with sand on her ankles was laughing at my terrible swimming. "I don't know yet," I said. "But I know this. Tomorrow night there's a rehearsal dinner, and the day after there's a wedding, and neither of them is real. Allegra doesn't love that man — she told you, she told me. My grandmother knows it and doesn't care. And if we stand up at that reception and say nothing — if we let another Marchetti wedding happen inside the lie — then she wins twice. She wins our past and our present. And I refuse. I have been reasonable for eleven years, Beatrix. Reasonable got me a framed photograph of a life I didn't choose."

The water came in further this time, swirling cold around our ankles, and she didn't step back, and neither did I.

"If I speak," she said slowly, "I lose the company. You understand that. Not risk it — lose it. She'll pull funding from every vendor I work with. Twenty years of work, gone in an afternoon."

"Yes."

"And if I don't speak, I keep the company and lose—" she gestured between us, at the beach, at the letter in my hand, at the whole drowned decade "—this. Whatever this is. Which I have only just gotten back and cannot bear to lose again on principle."

"That's the choice," I said gently. "I won't pretend it isn't terrible. But I'll tell you what I told myself on the cliff path tonight: I would rather stand in the wreckage of the truth with you than in the finished, flawless, catered lie without you. That's not a bullet point. That's just the whole thing."

For a long moment she said nothing. The lighthouse at the point came on, sweeping its slow white arm across the water, and in its light her face was open in a way I had never seen it in any conference room — unarmored, unfinished, young.

"Ivo," she said. "If I do this — if I burn it all down tomorrow night — will you be standing next to me when it falls?"

"Front row," I said. "Center aisle. Best seat in the house."

She laughed again, properly this time, wet and helpless, and stepped forward, and I met her halfway, and we stood at the tide line holding each other like people surfacing from deep water — not kissing, not yet, just breathing, her forehead against mine, the sea erasing our footprints behind us as if the beach were already making room for whatever came next.

"Tomorrow," she whispered.

"Tomorrow," I agreed.

And far up the lawn, in a lit window of the house, a curtain moved — and neither of us saw it.
