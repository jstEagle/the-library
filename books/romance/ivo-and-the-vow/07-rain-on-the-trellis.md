## 7. Rain on the Trellis

The storm announced itself the way Marguerite's mother used to announce dinner guests: with a great deal of fuss and no consideration for anyone's plans.

She had been halfway through the equipment inventory when the sky over the valley turned the color of a bruise, and by the time she reached her rental car, the first fat drops were hammering the gravel like an impatient audience. She got the door open, got one hand on the wheel—and then the sky simply opened, all of it, at once, as if someone had tipped the entire Pacific over the hills.

Through the sheeting rain she could see Felix Eastwick sprinting across the yard, waving his arms at her in a gesture that was either *come inside* or *the building is collapsing, run*. With Felix it was genuinely difficult to tell.

"Voss!" He reached the car and shouted through the glass, water streaming off his jaw. "The road to town floods at the creek crossing. You're not driving anywhere tonight."

"I have a hotel reservation."

"You have a swimming pool with a bed in it, more like." He jerked his head toward the winery. "Come on. Cellar's dry, and I make a passable coffee."

She should have refused. She catalogued the reasons—propriety, professionalism, the audit, the forty-seven reasons her supervisor would invent to be uncomfortable with it—and then the thunder cracked directly overhead, close enough to feel in her teeth, and she got out of the car.

The cellar swallowed them both in cool darkness and the smell of oak and damp earth. Felix lit a lamp, then a second one, and Marguerite took inventory of her situation: stone walls, barrel rows receding into shadow, one man wringing out his shirt with the total absence of shame that seemed to be his native condition.

"You can take the office cot," he said. "I'll take the floor. And before you say anything—yes, I know how this looks. It looks like a storm, Marguerite. Even I'm not arrogant enough to engineer one."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were absolutely going to say something. You have a look. It's the same look you give my fertilizer invoices."

Despite herself—despite everything—she laughed. It escaped before she could catch it, and she saw him register the sound with an expression she couldn't read, something startled and pleased, like a man finding a coin in the street.

* * *

The rain was a living thing on the roof, relentless, and after an hour of strained small talk about drainage and barrel rotation, it did what rain does in cellars: it made the world small and the silences honest.

"Why wine?" Felix asked eventually, from his side of the cellar, a barrel between them like a border. "You could do anything with that mind of yours. You could run a country."

"My family had a farm," she said, before she could decide not to. "Next valley over. Stone farmhouse, blue shutters. My father grew grapes for the co-op, never his own label. He worked for other people's names his whole life and died tired." She turned her glass—Felix had produced a bottle of his own reserve, which was, annoyingly, very good. "I decided I'd rather be the one holding the pen than the one waiting to be written about."

"That's not an answer to why wine."

"It's an answer to why I left it."

The lamp flame bent in some small draft. Felix was quiet a long moment, and when he spoke, his voice had lost its usual armor of jokes.

"My father died believing this place would kill me," he said. "Not the vineyard. The *debt*. He signed loans in his last year that I didn't find out about until the will. I used to come down here at three in the morning and read the old labels. 'Eastwick & Sons.' There was never a sons. There was never even a plan for sons. I think he just liked how it sounded." He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "I keep the name because throwing it away felt like burying him twice."

Marguerite found that her throat had gone tight. She thought of her father's hands, cracked and stained, holding a glass of the co-op's thin red and saying, *someday, little one*, and she thought about how she had made a career out of other people's someday.

"I wanted to make wine," she said. The words came out of her like something unstitched. "Years ago. I was accepted to a program in Beaune. Full ride. I gave it up for a consulting track at Meridian because the salary had a number with two commas in it, and my mother's medical bills didn't care about my soul." She had never said this aloud. Not to anyone. She heard herself continue, helpless against the honesty the rain was pulling out of her. "I tell myself I chose stability. But some nights I think I just chose fear with better furniture."

Felix looked at her across the barrel, and there was nothing in his face of the daylight man, the one who bristled and blustered and defended every receipt like a wounded animal. What was there instead made her heart behave badly.

"For what it's worth," he said, "your canopy advice was the best thing anyone's told me in three years. I hated you for it. Still used it."

"I know. I could see it from the road. You did it badly and then did it again properly."

"I contain multitudes."

The storm raged. They talked. Somewhere past midnight the conversation stopped having sections and became a single unbroken thread—her mother's long illness, his mother's long absence, the way ambition curdles into obligation if you don't watch it, the specific loneliness of being the person everyone depends on and no one asks. At some point she was sitting on the floor beside his barrel instead of across from it, shoulder almost touching his shoulder, and neither of them had moved there so much as discovered it.

At one point he said, "You know what's strange? I feel like I've told you all this before."

She knew exactly what he meant. She felt it too, that eerie familiarity, like a door opening into a room she'd been furnishing for weeks in the dark. She thought of her letters—the ones she wrote to a nameless stranger, the ones where she had confessed Beaune—and a cold thread of *almost* ran through her. But the storm was loud and the lamp was warm and she let the thought drift away like a leaf on floodwater.

"Eastwick," she said, near dawn, when the rain had softened to a whisper on the trellis above, "if you were anyone else, I think we'd be friends."

Something crossed his face—quick, complicated, gone. "If I were anyone else," he said quietly, "I think I'd tell you something."

"What?"

He looked at her for a long moment. The lamp guttered. "Nothing. Forget it. Storm talk."

* * *

Morning came in gray and businesslike, as if embarrassed by the night.

Marguerite woke on the cot to find Felix already up, already armored, already handing her coffee with a briskness that could have cut glass. The man from the cellar—the one who had talked about burying his father twice—was nowhere to be found. In his place stood the vineyard owner with something to hide.

"The creek's down," he said. "Road's passable. You've got your nine o'clock."

"Yes." She straightened her blazer like putting on a coat of paint. "Thank you for the cot."

"Standard hospitality. Don't mention it in the report."

"I never mention anything in the report, Felix. I *write* it."

It came out sharper than she intended, and she saw it land, saw him flinch and cover it, and she wanted—absurdly, furiously—to take it back, to say *last night was real, I'm still here, the woman who told you about Beaune didn't leave with the rain.* But the sun was up and the audit was waiting and the daylight world had rules, and so she walked to her car through the dripping vines with her spine straight and her heart in ruins.

She made it half a mile before she had to pull over.

*That night*, she thought. *We talked until dawn and I told him things I've never told anyone, and this morning he looked at me like a stranger.* And the terrible thing, the thing she couldn't stop turning over on the drive to town, was that she had done exactly the same thing. Retreat was a language they both spoke fluently.

That evening she sat down at her desk and opened the festival portal. The cursor blinked in the empty letter field, patient as the rain had been.

*Dear friend*, she typed. *Something happened to me last night that I can't explain—*

She stopped. Stared. Deleted it.

Because the truth was pressing against the back of her teeth with a specificity that frightened her: the stone farmhouse with blue shutters. The father who worked for other people's names. She had written those things to her anonymous correspondent weeks ago, word for word, and last night she had said them to Felix Eastwick as though they were new.

She told herself it was coincidence. The valley was small; childhoods rhymed. She told herself that the way her chest had ached in the cellar had nothing to do with the fact that Felix's voice, when he stopped performing, had a cadence she almost recognized.

She told herself all of it, and then she wrote a careful, guarded letter about nothing in particular, and hit send, and lay awake listening to the last of the storm drip off the trellis of the house across the street, wondering why the silence felt exactly like a door closing.

Three valleys away, in a stone farmhouse with blue shutters, a man was doing precisely the same thing.
