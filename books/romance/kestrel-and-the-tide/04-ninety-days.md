## 4. Ninety Days

### Ilse

The lease on my mother's bakery had eighty-one days left when I started sleeping badly, and by the time it hit sixty I'd stopped pretending the insomnia was about anything else.

I kept a countdown in three places: a grease-pencil tally on the walk-in door, a note taped inside my cash register, and — this was the one I never admitted to anyone — a running calculation in my head that updated itself like a stock ticker. Sixty days. Fifty-nine. Each morning I woke up already doing arithmetic.

The strange part wasn't the deadline. The strange part was how good the bakery had gotten.

Word travels fast in a neighborhood that's been waiting for something to open. By week two there was a line at seven a.m. By week four, a woman from the historical society came in and stood very still in front of the display case for a long time before saying, "My mother used to buy the almond twists here," in a voice like she was reporting a death. I gave her one free. She cried into her coat sleeve and bought six more.

The almond twist was Elena Marchetti's recipe. My mother's. Everything in that case was hers — the cardamom buns, the rye loaves with the cross-hatched tops, the thing she called *kestrel bread* because she claimed the shape of it, folded over twice, reminded her of a bird holding its wings against the wind. She invented that story or she stole it from somewhere; either way, I told it to customers now, and they loved it, and every time I said it out loud I felt like I was keeping a lamp lit in a window for someone who wasn't coming home.

The landlord, a man named Drescher who had the personal warmth of an unpaid invoice, had stopped returning my calls in week three. That was when I noticed the other things. A surveyor's chalk marks on the corner building. A "For Sale" sign on the laundromat that hadn't been there in October. And a business card, left on my counter by a polite young man in a nice coat, from a company called Meridian Holdings — which, when I looked it up, turned out to be a subsidiary of a subsidiary of a company whose name I knew the way you know the name of weather.

Vasse Media Group.

Corin Vasse owned the publishing house that had published my mother's single novel, let it die in six weeks, and then — according to a letter I found after her funeral, addressed to me but never sent — taken the manuscript she'd been working on when she got sick and filed it away as "an asset." Her words. *He collects what he cannot kill.*

So when Meridian Holdings started buying my block, I understood exactly what kind of weather was coming.

What I did not expect, in the middle of all that, was to start looking forward to eleven o'clock at night.

### Bram

Here is what nobody tells you about ghostwriting a memoir: you spend months inside another man's head, and eventually his voice starts living in yours rent-free. I'd catch myself thinking in Corin Vasse's cadences — the little rhetorical pauses he inserted before his own wisdom, the way he said "the truth is" as though the truth were a shy animal he alone could coax out.

The truth, in my case, was that I owed forty thousand dollars to a credit union and my entire professional existence to a man whose archive contained sentences I had written to a stranger ten years ago, during the year I don't talk about at parties.

I worked nights because Corin worked nights. He believed genius kept nocturnal hours, which mostly meant he liked having an audience after dinner. So I'd take the elevator down to my apartment around midnight, stand in my kitchen eating cereal over the sink like a raccoon with a book deal, and hear — through the floor, faintly — the sounds of someone else also awake below me.

Flour being worked. An oven door. Once, unmistakably, humming.

The bakery had reopened in February under a woman named Ilse Marchetti, and I had been buying my coffee there every morning for a month while maintaining the emotional transparency of a filing cabinet. She was sharp-eyed and unhurried and she made the best cardamom bun I have ever eaten, and if she recognized anything about me beyond "tired man, large coffee, tips too much," she gave no sign.

But here's the thing about guilt: it makes you paranoid in inverse proportion to your actual crimes. I hadn't done anything to her. I'd written letters. Anonymous ones, to a stranger, back when writing to a stranger was the only honest thing I did all day. If she somehow connected those letters to me — and why would she, they were unsigned, unmailed even, slipped into a library book like messages into bottles — well. Still nothing to feel guilty about.

Except that I now suspected the letters had ended up in Corin Vasse's archive, mined for material, braided into a memoir I was being paid to make convincing. Which meant my most private year had become someone else's product, and I was the craftsman assembling it.

So yes. Guilt. Paranoia. And yet, at eleven p.m., when the smell of proofing dough rose through my floorboards like an apology from the universe, I went downstairs.

* * *

### Ilse

He came down on a Tuesday, ostensibly because the shop lights were still on.

"Shouldn't you be asleep?" I asked, and he said, "Shouldn't you?" and that was the whole negotiation. I put an apron on him — he looked like a man being fitted for a costume — and taught him to fold kestrel bread without telling him why my hands slowed when I demonstrated the second fold.

"You do that like it means something," he said.

"It does."

He didn't push. That was the first thing I noticed about Bram Oyelaran up close, flour on his forearms, lamplight on his face: he didn't push. Ten years of letters from a man who never demanded answers had apparently produced a person who extended the same courtesy in person. Or maybe he was just tired. People mistake exhaustion for depth all the time; God knows I've benefited from the confusion myself.

We fell into a rhythm that should have alarmed me more than it did. He'd come down after midnight, we'd shape tomorrow's loaves, and we'd talk — or rather, we'd trade. He told me about the indignities of ghostwriting without naming his client. I told him about the lease without naming the vulture circling it. We were both fluent in a dialect of almost-truth, and each of us spoke it so naturally that it felt like intimacy.

"I grew up above a bakery," he said one night, pressing his thumb into a seam of dough. "Different city. My mother worked for someone else, though. Never her own place." A pause. "She wanted one."

"What happened?"

"The usual. Money. Then less money." He smoothed the seam flat. "I write other people's stories now. It pays better than honesty ever did."

I laughed, and he looked pleased, and I thought: *you have no idea how literally I could take that sentence.*

Because here was my secret, the one I carried around like a stone in my apron pocket: I had read his letters. All of them. Forty-some pages of a stranger's worst year, tucked into a copy of my mother's novel in the university library where I'd gone to grieve, written in a hand that slanted left like it was leaning away from its own words. I'd answered them — once, then again, then for years — sliding replies into the same book, never signing more than an initial. *I.* He'd never known who I was. I'd figured it out in February, the first morning he walked in and ordered a large coffee and said *thank you* in a voice I had memorized across a decade of paper.

And he didn't know I knew. And I didn't say.

Why? Because I wanted to see if he'd claim them. Because a man who wrote like that and wouldn't own it was either a coward or a survivor, and I needed to know which before I handed him the last intact piece of my heart. Also — I'll be honest, since honesty is the theme I keep failing — because the late nights were good. Because folding bread next to him felt like the letters had felt: safe, sideways, true in the way things are true when nobody's watching.

Mutual concealment, my mother would have called it. She had a phrase for everything. *Two people keeping warm off the same fire, each pretending not to notice the other's shadow.*

* * *

### Bram

By day fifty of her countdown — I'd started counting too, though I pretended I hadn't noticed the grease-pencil tallies — I knew things about Ilse Marchetti that I couldn't have learned from any dossier. I knew she hummed when the dough behaved and swore softly in French when it didn't. I knew she checked the door lock twice. I knew that when she talked about her mother, her voice did something complicated, like a key turning in a lock that might be rusted shut.

And she knew things about me. I could feel it — the way she watched my hands when I shaped the loaves, like she was comparing them to something. Like she'd seen these exact hands before, doing something else.

One night she asked, casually, terribly casually: "Did you always want to be a writer?"

"No," I said. "I wanted to be a person who told the truth and didn't lose everything. Couldn't find a market for it."

"And the letters?"

The word landed between us like a dropped pan.

I heard it correctly. I knew I did. *Letters.* Plural. Specific. And some ancient survival instinct — the one that had gotten me through the worst year of my life and then buried it — reached up and took the wheel.

"What letters?" I said.

Ilse looked at me for a long moment. The oven ticked. Somewhere outside, a delivery truck changed gears on the empty street.

"Never mind," she said, and smiled, and handed me the bench scraper. "Fold from the left. You keep folding right-handed like a coward."

I laughed. She laughed. We shaped bread until two in the morning, and it was the closest thing to happiness I'd felt in years, and every minute of it was built on a lie I'd just told with my own mouth.

Walking back upstairs, I caught my reflection in the dark window of the laundromat — the one with the For Sale sign, the one Meridian Holdings would own within the month — and the reflection looked like Corin Vasse. Not physically. Worse. It looked like a man who had learned that the safest version of himself was also the smallest, and had moved in permanently.

Fifty days left on her lease. Forty thousand dollars of my debt. One manuscript in a locked archive with my mother's— with *her* mother's sentences in it, and mine, braided together like something that belonged to both of us and had been sold to neither.

The next morning, Corin Vasse called and asked me to come in early.

"There's a clause I want to add to your contract," he said, in the voice he used when generosity was about to show its teeth. "You're going to like it."

I stood in the bakery doorway with my coffee going cold, and downstairs Ilse waved at me through the glass, and I waved back, and neither of us knew — or both of us knew — that the fire we'd been sharing was about to need somewhere new to burn.
