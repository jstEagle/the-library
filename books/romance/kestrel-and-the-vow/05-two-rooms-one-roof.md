## 5. Two Rooms, One Roof

The housekeeper—Mrs. Pell, a woman built like a walnut and about as forthcoming—showed me to my room on the second day of the storm, and I knew before she opened the door that something had gone wrong with the universe's seating chart.

"The east wing's got the better light," she said, in the tone of someone explaining why you'd been given the smaller portion. "Mr. Kestrel's next door."

"Next door" turned out to be an optimistic euphemism. Our rooms shared a wall, and that wall was apparently decorative. I could hear him unpacking. I could hear the particular *thunk* of Jonah Kestrel setting down his laptop bag the way he always did—too hard, as if the bag owed him money. I could hear him testing the radiator, muttering something at it when it clanked, and I stood there in the middle of my beautiful room with its four-poster bed and its view of white nothing, thinking: *ten years, and the universe still puts us back to back like children who can't be trusted at the same table.*

"Thank you, Mrs. Pell," I said. "Is there any chance of a room farther from... the plumbing?"

"The plumbing's fine." She gave me a look that suggested she knew exactly what I was asking and found it beneath her dignity to acknowledge. "Dinner's at seven. Cook does her best with what the storm lets her have."

Then she was gone, and I was alone with the wall, listening to Jonah Kestrel exist.

Here is what nobody tells you about proximity: it isn't dramatic. It doesn't announce itself. It's the slow accumulation of small sounds—the creak of a floorboard you learn by heart within hours, the rhythm of someone else's footsteps until your body starts keeping time with them without permission. By the first evening I knew that he paced when he thought, that he stopped pacing around eleven, and that he slept badly, because I heard him turn over at two in the morning and again at three-forty, and I lay there in my own excellent bed doing the math on how many nights of this I could survive.

The answer, I suspected, was all of them. That was the torture of it. You don't die of proximity. You just stop being able to remember what silence sounded like before it had a shape.

* * *

We established a system by accident, the way most systems between Jonah and me were established.

I went down to breakfast early—he was never an early riser in the old days, and apparently ten years hadn't cured him—and ate my eggs in the vast cold grandeur of the morning room while snow ticked against the windows. He came down at nine-thirty, saw the state of my plate, and said, "You're still a morning person. God. Some things really are permanent."

"You're still a person who considers nine-thirty a moral position."

"It's not a position, it's a lifestyle." He poured coffee with the economy of someone who'd learned to make do in cheap apartments, which surprised me—I'd assumed he'd married money or at least comfort. The ring finger said no marriage either, but I wasn't going to look directly at that information. I looked at it sideways, the way you look at the sun.

"Mrs. Pell says we're to stay out of each other's way," he said. "She used the word 'harmoniously,' which I think means she's had writers in the house before and one of them bit."

"I would never bite."

"No. You'd just vanish." He said it lightly. He meant it heavily. We both pretended otherwise, which was its own kind of fluency—we'd always been good at pretending, Jonah and I, so good that sometimes I wondered if we'd ever actually talked at all, or just performed two parallel monologues at each other for years until one of us fled the theater.

"The library," I said, because the alternative was answering him. "Vivian wants me to work from the library. She said the family papers are there."

Something moved behind his eyes—quick, guarded, gone. "The Kestrel papers are in the library too. My grandfather's correspondence. It's why they hired me here instead of letting me work from London."

"Convenient."

"Efficient," he corrected. "There's a difference. Convenient would be if we'd been assigned different wings."

"We were assigned adjacent rooms."

"Yes, well." He drank his coffee. "Mrs. Pell has a sense of humor. I checked. It's very small and mostly punitive."

* * *

The library deserved better than both of us.

It occupied the whole south face of the ground floor—a long gallery with windows that should have shown mountains and instead showed a white wall of weather, shelves rising two stories, a brass ladder on rails, ladders being, I will admit, one of my few reliable weaknesses. There were maybe eight thousand books, and Vivian Ashcombe wanted them catalogued—not read, catalogued—as part of the centenary project. "A house is its books," she'd told me in our first interview, her voice dry as old paper. "Everything else is furniture."

So we divided the territory with the careful diplomacy of nations sharing a disputed border. Jonah took the west end, where the Kestrel materials lived; I took the east, where the Ashcombe papers were shelved; and the middle—two hundred feet of neutral shelf, biography and poetry and a suspicious number of books on falconry—belonged to no one.

It lasted four hours.

The problem was the ladder. The problem was that the ladder ran the whole length of the gallery on its rail, and every time I needed a book from the upper shelves I had to roll it past him, and every time he needed a book he had to roll it past me, and by the third day we had developed a whole silent etiquette of ladder-sharing that felt more intimate than most conversations I'd had in a decade. He'd steady it with one hand while I climbed. I'd hold it while he reached. Neither of us spoke during these transactions. Speaking would have made them real.

On the fourth day he broke the silence.

"You catalogue like a surgeon," he said, not looking up from his notes. "Alphabetical, then chronological, then by how much dust was on them. I can hear you thinking from over there."

"You can hear me thinking?"

"I could always hear you thinking. It's one of the more annoying things about you. Your brain is loud."

"My brain is *organized*."

"Your brain is a filing cabinet with opinions." But he was almost smiling, and I caught myself almost smiling back, and I made myself turn to the shelf and pull the next book—*Ashcombe: A Family Record, 1924–1974*, water-stained, spine cracked—and pretend my hands were steady.

This was the torture, precisely calibrated. Not arguments. Not confrontations. *Small talk.* Ten years of material between us and what we traded across forty feet of gallery was weather reports and complaints about the radiators and Mrs. Pell's soup, which was, he maintained, "technically a broth, legally speaking a rumor." And underneath every exchange ran the other conversation, the real one, the one we'd rehearsed separately in our separate rooms at night—I could feel him drafting his version through the wall, and he must have felt mine, and neither of us sent it.

I ghostwrite confessions for a living. I know exactly how a sentence should be built to carry weight. I had mine load-bearing and ready. I just couldn't find the crane.

* * *

On the fifth evening the power flickered, held, and died for six minutes, and in those six minutes the library went dark except for the fire, and Jonah said my name.

Not "August" the way he'd been saying it all week—careful, armored, each syllable handled with tongs. Just my name, plain, the way he used to say it across a shared desk in a workshop neither of us could afford, and my whole body responded like a struck bell before my brain could intervene.

"The lights," I said stupidly.

"They'll come back." They did, thirty seconds later, and whatever had been in his face went back into storage. "Your candle count is low, by the way. In case it goes again. I noticed."

"You noticed my candles."

"I notice things. Occupational hazard." He gathered his notes. "Goodnight, August."

"Goodnight."

He paused at the door of the gallery, silhouetted against the corridor lamp, and for one suspended moment I thought—*this is it, this is where one of us finally says the sentence*—and then he said, "The soup really is a rumor," and was gone, and I stood alone among eight thousand books feeling like the only unfinished manuscript in the building.

That night I lay awake listening to him turn over at two, and at three-forty, and I thought about how we'd once promised each other honesty, once, one snowbound night that I was absolutely not going to think about, and how the promise had apparently survived better than we had—it was still there, intact, unkept, sitting between our rooms like a guest neither of us would acknowledge.

* * *

Saturday, Vivian summoned me.

Her sitting room was on the ground floor, kept warm enough to grow orchids, and she received me in a wingback chair with a blanket over her knees and eyes that missed nothing, which at ninety-one seemed less like luck and more like discipline.

"You sound tired," she said.

"I'm cataloguing."

"You're avoiding." She let that sit. "My grandson tells me Mr. Kestrel arrived before the road closed. How convenient for the publishers. Two writers, one roof."

"It's efficient," I said, and heard Jonah's word come out of my mouth, and Vivian's mouth did something at the corner that was not quite a smile.

"Mm. Efficient." She turned her rings—one old garnet, worn smooth. "August. When you write a life, whose voice do you use? Yours or theirs?"

"Theirs. Always theirs. That's the job."

"And does yours get loud? In the margins? While you're writing someone else's truth?" She watched me with terrible patience. "Because I've read your work, dear. Under other people's names. I know a buried voice when I hear one. It has a particular... pressure."

I said something diplomatic. I don't even remember what. She waved me off with instructions to begin her dictations Monday—"the true history, not the one Marcus will want"—and as I left, she added, without looking up:

"And tell Mr. Kestrel the library ladder needs oiling. I'm told you two have been sharing it. Sharing is fine. Squeaking is not."

I walked back down the corridor with my ears burning, past the door of his room, through which came the soft irregular sound of typing, and I thought: *she knows. Whatever else is happening in this house, that woman knows exactly what she's done, putting us both under one roof, and I have no idea yet whether she's matchmaking or setting a trap.*

Probably both. The best ones always are.

That night, through the wall, I heard him stop pacing at eleven. Then, unusually, I heard him laugh once—short, private, at something on his screen or in his head—and the sound went through the plaster and into my chest and stayed there like a splinter I had no intention of removing.

Monday, Vivian would begin to talk. I fell asleep dreading it and wanting it in equal measure, which is, I've since decided, the exact recipe for a story that changes everything.
