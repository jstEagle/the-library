## 12. Thaw Lines

### August

The lights died at 9:47 p.m., and I want it noted for the record that I did not scream. I said a word I'd deny under oath, dropped my pen, and sat very still in the dark of my room like a woman practicing for the nineteenth century.

The generator had been coughing all evening—a wet, asthmatic sound from somewhere beneath the east wing—and when it finally gave up the ghost entirely, Ashcombe went dark in stages, like a theater dimming between acts. First the corridor sconces. Then the kitchen wing. Then, with a theatrical little flicker, everything.

I found a candle in my desk drawer—Vivian's housekeeper, Mrs. Pell, had issued them to every room that morning with the grim efficiency of a woman who'd survived four decades of mountain winters and considered electricity a temporary arrangement anyway—and made my way downstairs, one hand on the wall, feeling like the protagonist of a gothic novel who should know better by now.

The kitchen was already lit.

Not electrically lit. Candlelit. A dozen of Mrs. Pell's tallow sticks arranged across the long oak table with what could only be called intention, and at the center of this altar of wax and shadow stood Jonah Kestrel, sleeves rolled, holding a saucepan over a camp stove like a man performing surgery.

"You're making soup," I said.

"I'm attempting soup." He didn't look up. "There's a difference, and the difference is currently whether I find the salt."

"In the dark."

"In the dark, yes. It builds character." He glanced at me then, and the candlelight did something unfair to his face—softened the jaw I'd spent ten years remembering as sharper than it was, gilded the gray at his temples that hadn't existed the last time I'd seen him by daylight. "Mrs. Pell says the generator's dead until morning. Something about a fuel line and a man named Doug who is apparently forty minutes away even in good weather."

"Doug," I repeated. "Of course there's a Doug."

"There's always a Doug. Doug is the patron saint of rural infrastructure." He nodded at the table. "Sit. There's bread. Mrs. Pell left instructions that we were to feed ourselves and not burn the house down, in that order of priority."

I sat. This was, I would later reflect, the strategic error of the entire evening—not the storm, not the power failure, not Vivian's increasingly pointed questions about my personal life, but the simple act of sitting down at a candlelit table across from Jonah Kestrel while he stirred a pot with his back half-turned and the wind screamed against the windows like it had opinions about us.

Because here is the thing about proximity that no one tells you when you're young and stupid and making vows in boathouses: it doesn't fade with practice. You'd think ten years of professional distance would build an immunity. You'd think a person could learn to sit across a table from the great unfinished sentence of her life and feel nothing but mild indigestion. But no. Proximity is cumulative. Every hour in the same building added interest to a debt I'd been pretending not to owe, and tonight, in this kitchen, with the candles guttering and the snow erasing the world outside, the balance came due.

"So," Jonah said, ladling soup into two mismatched bowls. "Chess?"

"We played last night."

"And you lost."

"I was distracted."

"By what?" He set the bowl in front of me and sat down across from it, elbows on the table, chin on his laced fingers—the exact posture he'd had at twenty-four, at the library carrel, when he used to wait for me to admit I'd stolen his thesis argument. Some men age into their faces. Jonah had aged into his gestures, and it was unbearable.

"The chapter," I lied smoothly. "Vivian's war years are complicated."

"Mm." He tore a piece of bread. "She told me today that she once threw a champagne glass at a British colonel."

"She told me she *hit* a British colonel."

"With the glass?"

"With the glass."

He smiled—that reluctant, sideways smile that had gotten him out of trouble our whole acquaintance and had never once gotten me out of anything. "We should compare notes sometime. For accuracy."

"That would violate approximately six clauses of my contract."

"Would it? Or would it just violate your general policy of telling me nothing?" The smile stayed but the eyes changed, and there it was—the old wound, surfacing through the banter like a rock through river ice. "You know, August, most people, when trapped in a house with someone, manage to exchange basic biographical facts within the first week. Favorite foods. Childhood pets. Whether they've been married."

"Have you?" I asked, because deflection was also a craft, and I was very good at my craft.

"No." He said it simply, without performance, which was somehow worse. "You?"

"No."

"Huh." He looked at his soup. "Ten years, and we both end up alone in a freezing house eating camp-stove soup. If I wrote that in a memoir, my editor would call it implausible."

"If you wrote that in a memoir, your editor would be right. Real life has no structure. That's why people like me exist—to impose one retroactively."

"Is that what you do? Impose structure?"

"On other people's lives, yes. Never my own. My own I leave in first draft form, permanently, because every time I try to revise it I—" I stopped. The sentence had been heading somewhere dangerous, somewhere with words like *rehearsed* and *ten years* in it, and I steered hard away. "More soup?"

"You just got soup."

"I'm being hospitable. It's a defense mechanism. Didn't they cover this in your family's counter-history seminars?"

He laughed, and the laugh did what the candlelight had done to his face—made the whole evening suddenly, treacherously warm. And then, because I am weak, because the storm had stripped away every excuse I usually kept between us like furniture, I heard myself say, quietly:

"Jonah. That night. At the boathouse."

The wind filled the silence. He set down his spoon.

"What about it," he said, and his voice had gone careful, the way voices do around live wires.

"I—" And here is where I should tell you what I said. Here is where the rehearsed sentence, the one I have carried through a decade of other people's love stories, finally got spoken. Except that what actually happened was this: I opened my mouth, and every version of the sentence I'd ever drafted—all forty-something revisions, the angry ones, the forgiving ones, the one I'd written at three a.m. in a hotel in Lisbon that began *I loved you then*—every single one of them evacuated my head simultaneously, leaving behind only the blank page feeling I knew too well from the wrong side of the keyboard.

"I think the soup needs salt," I said.

"August."

"It really does. Camp stove cooking, no seasoning, it's basically punishment."

"August." He leaned forward. The candle between us bent in some draft I couldn't feel, and his face came close enough that I could see the exact moment he decided to be brave, the way I'd seen it once before, ten years ago, in a boathouse that smelled of lake water and gasoline. "I have been trying to say something to you since the night you walked into the great hall and pretended you didn't recognize me. And I know you've been doing the same thing, because I've watched you do it—you get to the edge of it and then you make a joke, or you mention the contract, or you ask about salt. And I'm tired of it. I'm tired of the edge. So I'm going to say—"

The door banged open.

Snow came in first, a white swirl of it, and then Marcus Ashcombe, in a shearling coat worth more than my advance, stamping his boots and radiating the specific energy of a man who believes himself to be the protagonist of every room he enters.

"Power's out on the whole ridge," he announced. "Doug says the fuel line froze solid. Also, hello, hello, don't get up." He shed the coat, dropped it over a chair, surveyed the candlelit tableau—two bowls, one candle bending between us, whatever expression had been on my face and Jonah's—and smiled with all the warmth of a tax audit. "Well. Isn't this cozy. Grandma's ghostwriter and the Kestrel boy, sharing a candlelight supper. She'll be thrilled. She loves a narrative."

Jonah closed his mouth around whatever he'd been about to say. I watched him do it—watched the sentence physically retreat, swallowed whole, filed back in whatever drawer he kept it in—and felt something in my chest crack along a fault line that had been forming for ten years.

"Marcus," I said. "You made it through the pass?"

"Barely. Snowcat's parked by the stable. Roads will be clear in two days if the plows run overnight." He helped himself to bread, uninvited, and bit into it. "Which means, Kestrel, that you and I should talk business while we've got the time. And you—" he pointed the remaining crust at me, cheerful as a headsman, "—should probably check on Grandma. She was asking for you before the lights went. Something about final pages."

Final pages. The words landed like ice water. Vivian's health had been sliding for a week, and we both knew what *final pages* meant, and Marcus knew we knew, and he said it anyway, lightly, watching us over the bread like a man moving pieces on a board only he could see.

I stood. I took my bowl. I thanked Jonah for the soup—it needed salt; I said so again, out of spite, out of self-preservation, out of the cowardice I keep polished and ready for exactly these moments—and I walked out of the warm kitchen into the cold dark corridor, carrying my candle, carrying my unsaid sentence, hating myself with a fluency that only comes from long practice.

Behind me, faintly, I heard Marcus say, "So. Business."

And the wind took the rest.

### Jonah

Here's what nobody warns you about almost saying it: the almost is worse than the silence. Silence you can live inside, furnish, call home. But the almost—the moment when the sentence is actually in your throat, when you can feel the shape of it against your teeth—I loved you then, I love you now, duty be damned, ten years, August, ten years and I never stopped—the almost leaves a bruise on the inside of your chest that aches every time you breathe deep.

Marcus Ashcombe arrived at the precise moment I'd committed. I want that on the record too. I had done the thing. I had leaned forward. I had watched her face in the candlelight and seen her stop deflecting—actually stop, for the first time in eleven days, the wit dropping away like a coat, and underneath it something frightened and open and so familiar it hurt to look at directly—and I had thought, *now, say it now, you coward, say it now or spend the rest of your life writing other people's endings.*

And then the door opened and the heir walked in on a gust of snow, and August stood up so fast her chair scraped, and the moment died on the table between the soup bowls like something small and warm caught outside in January.

I sat through forty minutes of Marcus's pitch with my hands flat on the table and my blood audibly in my ears.

He'd come, ostensibly, to check on his grandmother. Ostensibly. In practice, he'd come to corner me, and he did it the moment August's candlelight disappeared up the back stairs—he pulled a chair around to my side of the table, sat close, dropped his voice into the register men use when they think they're offering you a gift.

"My grandfather built half of what's standing in this valley," he said. "And your family built the other half, and then they spent sixty years pretending they hated each other because it was easier than admitting what actually happened. You know what happened, Kestrel?"

"I'm writing the book. You tell me."

"I'll tell you what my father tells me: that Vivian Ashcombe rewrote history to make herself the wounded party. That the feud started because the Kestrels cheated her, not the other way around. That's the story you're here to document. That's the story my family is paying for." He smiled. "But here's the thing. I've read the archive. And there are letters in that restricted collection that tell a different story entirely, and if those letters surface—if the wrong person finds them—my grandmother's version falls apart, the estate valuation collapses, and everyone loses. Everyone. Including, I'd guess, a certain ghostwriter whose contract forbids her from breathing a word of any of it."

My hands went cold. "What letters?"

"Oh, come on." He laughed. "Don't do the innocent face. You two have been cataloguing that archive for two weeks. You think I don't know what's in there?" He stood, clapped me on the shoulder with proprietary warmth. "Roads clear in two days. Think about whose side of the story pays better. Goodnight, Kestrel. Enjoy the candles."

Then he was gone, upstairs, whistling something tuneless, and I sat alone in the kitchen with the dying candles and the two bowls and the bruise in my chest, and I did what I always do when I can't speak.

I wrote.

Not the counter-history. Not the chapters Marcus wanted, not the rebuttal my father expected, not the careful, dutiful, poisoned prose I'd been producing for two weeks like a man milking a snake. I opened my notebook to a page near the back—one I'd been avoiding, one with a single line at the top dated ten years ago in handwriting younger and stupider than mine—and underneath it, by candlelight, while the wind tried the windows like a burglar, I drafted the sentence.

*I loved you then. I love you now. Duty be damned.*

It looked small on the page. Ten years of weight, and it looked like nothing—fourteen words, the kind of thing that gets printed on bookmarks. I crossed it out. Wrote it again. Crossed it out again. The problem wasn't the words. The problem was that every time I got near saying them aloud, something intervened—the contract, the feud, the door banging open, my own father's voice in my head saying *remember what the family expects*—and I was starting to suspect, sitting there in the guttering light, that the interventions weren't accidents. That I had been arranging them. That a man can build his own prison out of duty and call it honor, and serve a ten-year sentence in it, and thank the guards on the way to his cell.

August had asked about the boathouse. Tonight. Before Marcus. She'd said *that night, at the boathouse,* and her voice had done something I'd never heard it do in all her polished, professional, armored sentences—it had shaken.

She remembered. Of course she remembered. She'd been carrying it the same way I had, all this time, two writers hoarding the same unfinished manuscript, each waiting for the other to write the ending.

I put out the candles one by one. In the last flame's light I looked at the notebook, at the crossed-out sentence, and I made myself a promise that felt, for once, like the opposite of duty:

Tomorrow. Tomorrow I'd find her before Marcus did, before the archive did, before another storm or contract or dead generator gave me an exit. Tomorrow I'd stand in front of her with no chessboard and no soup and no audience, and I'd say it, and if she threw me out into the snow, at least I'd freeze honest.

I climbed the stairs in the dark. Her door was shut, the candle out, a line of gold light gone from under it. Through the wall—these old walls, thin as secrets—I could hear nothing at all, which meant she was awake. Sleeping people make noise. Awake people lying in the dark, holding a sentence they can't say, make none.

"Tomorrow," I said to the wall, very quietly.

From the other side, so faint I might have invented it, came the smallest sound—breath, or a laugh, or the beginning of a word.

And then, from the floor above, a crash. A voice calling. Mrs. Pell, sharp with fear, shouting for someone to fetch help—

Vivian's room.

I was moving before I'd finished deciding to, taking the stairs two at a time, and the last thing I registered, absurdly, in the middle of all that noise and dark, was the notebook still open on the kitchen table below, the sentence crossed out twice, waiting.
