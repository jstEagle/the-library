## 1. The Muscle That Learns

The valley announced itself the way all good liars do: gently, and with something you wanted to believe.

Silas Nystrom watched it unspool past the rental car's window—rows of vines marching up hillsides in disciplined green ranks, a church steeple, a water tower painted with a faded mural of grapes that someone had loved once and then stopped. He had been driving since dawn, and his hands ached from gripping the wheel, though the road had been smooth for hours. He recognized the ache for what it was. His body always knew before he did when he was about to become someone else.

He pulled over at an overlook where tourists presumably took photographs in autumn, when the valley turned gold and red. It was June now. Everything was still green, still promising, still pretending nothing would ever be cut down. Silas sat with the engine ticking and rehearsed.

*The heart is a muscle,* he told himself, the way he'd told himself for ten years, in hotel bathrooms and rented rooms and the dead air after signing contracts for other people's lives. *And a muscle learns. You train it like anything else. Repetition. Discipline. Containment.*

It was a good creed. It had kept him alive through the worst winter of his life, and it had kept him employed ever since, because no one wants a ghostwriter who feels things. They want a ghostwriter who can feel things *on command*, in the right register, at the right length—grief in twelve hundred words, redemption in a closing chapter, longing that never gets messy enough to require a second draft. Silas could do all of it. He was very good at not being anyone.

He checked his phone. A text from Marcus Delacroix's assistant, sent forty minutes ago: *Mr. Delacroix asks that you come straight to the house when you arrive. He'd like to begin this evening.*

Of course he would. Marcus Delacroix was seventy-one years old, three times divorced, the owner of one of the oldest vineyards in the valley, and a man who had reportedly never once waited for anything he wanted. The advance materials Silas had studied on the flight north were a masterclass in self-mythology: press clippings, a documentary transcript, a wine label with the man's own face on it, rendered in heroic profile. *Delacroix. Since 1911.* The kind of name that got printed in gold.

Silas put the car back in gear. *A muscle learns,* he thought again, and drove down into the valley like a man lowering himself into cold water.

* * *

The cottage was exactly what the contract had promised: small, whitewashed, tucked behind the estate's tasting room among a stand of olive trees gone slightly wild. Inside it smelled of dust and lavender and, faintly, of other summers—other tenants, maybe, or just the valley itself, which seemed like the sort of place that remembered people whether they wanted to be remembered or not.

Silas carried his bags in two trips, because that was all there was. Ten years of ghostwriting had taught him to travel light. You couldn't leave fingerprints if you didn't bring anything to fingerprint. His life fit into two suitcases and a laptop whose hard drive held the voices of eleven published memoirists who were not him. There was a twelfth book too, finished but unsold, written under a pen name so flimsy it embarrassed him—a novel about a man who teaches himself to stop wanting things. His agent called it "beautiful" and "unsellable" in the same sentence, which Silas had privately decided was the most honest review he'd ever received.

He unpacked the essentials: laptop, charger, the contract in its leather folder, a photograph he did not look at. Then he stood at the kitchen window with a glass of tap water and let the evening come down over the valley.

That was when he saw the neighbor.

The property line ran along a dry stone wall maybe fifty yards from the cottage, and beyond it the land changed character—not abandoned, exactly, but less curated. Wilder rows. Older vines, gnarled and low, their trunks twisted like arthritic hands. And moving through them, in the last copper light, a young man with pruning shears.

He worked the way surgeons work, or the way Silas imagined surgeons work—without hurry, without wasted motion, each cut decided before the shears closed. He was barefoot, which struck Silas as either madness or intimacy with the ground, and his shirt had come untucked and gone dark at the shoulders with sweat. Every few rows he'd stop, cup a cluster of grapes in his palm, and hold it up to the light like a jeweler checking for flaws. Then he'd make his cut, and the vine would bleed a little sap, and he'd move on.

Silas stood at the window longer than he meant to.

There was no reason to. The man was a stranger doing ordinary work. But something about the scene snagged on him—the tenderness of it, maybe, the way the man touched the fruit like it might bruise, like it mattered. Silas wrote tenderness for a living. He manufactured it by the paragraph, sold it wholesale, and had long ago stopped believing it described anything real. Watching this stranger handle grapes in the dying light felt like catching a glimpse behind a curtain he'd assumed was a wall.

*A muscle learns,* he reminded himself, and looked away, and looked back.

The man straightened suddenly, as if he'd felt it—that specific weight of being watched—and turned toward the cottage. Even at fifty yards, even in the failing light, Silas felt the look land. He stepped back from the window, heart going oddly fast, and told himself it was the drive, the altitude, the wine country air.

Through the wall, faintly, he heard the shears resume. Snip. Snip. Steady as a pulse.

* * *

Marcus Delacroix received him on a terrace overlooking his own kingdom, holding a glass of his own wine, wearing linen the color of heavy cream. Up close he was less a monument than a well-preserved ruin—all sharp angles softened by money, eyes quick and pale and appraising. He looked Silas over the way he might look over a horse.

"Nystrom." He pronounced it slowly, testing it. "That's Scandinavian."

"My grandfather's. I've never been."

"Honest. Good." Marcus gestured at a chair. "You'll want to know why I'm writing a book."

"I assume everyone your age is writing a book."

Marcus laughed—one bark, genuine, gone immediately. "Everyone my age is *dying*, boy. The book is just what we're doing about it." He settled across from Silas and swirled his glass. "I built half this valley. The other half I outlasted. And there are certain families"—he said the word *families* the way other men say *rats*—"who have spent eighty years trading on a story about 1911 that isn't true. My book will be the true version. The version where the founding vineyard belongs, morally and historically, to me."

Silas kept his face pleasant and professional. Later—he would tell himself later, many times, right up until he couldn't—he hadn't understood. He'd heard an old man's grievance, the standard fuel of every memoir, and filed it accordingly. "We'll need documents," he said. "Deeds, correspondence, whatever supports the claim."

"You'll have everything." Marcus smiled, and the smile had teeth in it somewhere. "I like that you don't flatter. Everyone else who works for me flatters. It's exhausting."

"I'm not working for you," Silas said. "I'm working for the book."

Something flickered in the old man's eyes—approval, or appetite; with men like Marcus they were the same organ. "Good answer. We start tomorrow morning. Six o'clock. I dictate best before the heat comes up." He raised his glass an inch. "To the truth."

"To the truth," Silas echoed, because that was what pens were for.

Walking back through the darkened grounds, he passed the tasting room, shuttered now, and saw the crates stacked by the loading door. Empty ones, waiting for harvest. And on the nearest crate, stenciled in faded black lettering, a name:

*Rafael.*

Just the first name, hand-lettered, the R looping high and open, the final *l* trailing off like a sentence the writer hadn't quite known how to end.

Silas stopped.

He didn't know why he stopped. It was only a name on a crate. But his chest did something strange and quiet, the way a house does when a door opens somewhere far away and you feel the draft before you hear the sound. He stood in the dark looking at that handwriting, at the confident, careless beauty of it, and felt—for the first time in ten years, in a valley he'd known for six hours—the creed stutter.

*The heart is a muscle.* Trainable. Containable.

Then why, he wondered, standing alone in the dark beside a stranger's name, did his hands want to shake?

Snip. Snip. Somewhere beyond the stone wall, faint on the night air, the pruning went on—steady as a pulse, patient as a letter waiting to be answered.
