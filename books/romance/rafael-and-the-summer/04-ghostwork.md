## 4. Ghostwork

Marcus Delacroix kept his study the way he kept his cellar: cold, orderly, and arranged so that everything in it looked older than it was. The desk was a reproduction of something eighteenth-century, the leather chairs were distressed on purpose, and the portrait above the fireplace—a stern man holding a glass of red wine like a verdict—was supposedly a Delacroix ancestor, though Silas had already learned that "supposedly" was doing heavy lifting in this house.

"Sit," Marcus said, not looking up from the papers spread before him. "We start with the founding."

Silas sat. He had been coming to the house for two weeks now, three mornings a week, and he had developed a system for these sessions: recorder on, notebook open, pen moving. His body did the work while his mind stayed somewhere neutral, somewhere without windows. It was the oldest trick he knew—be the instrument, not the hand.

"The founding," Silas said. "Nineteen forty-seven. Your grandfather bought the east parcel."

"My grandfather *staked* the east parcel." Marcus finally looked up. He had a face built for boardrooms, all planes and patience, and eyes that never quite finished a sentence when they looked at you. "There's a difference, and it's going to matter. Staking means vision. Buying means money. Anyone can have money."

"Anyone can have vision too, if you write it for them."

Something flickered across Marcus's face—not offense. Recognition. He liked that Silas understood the transaction. That was the trouble with ghostwriting for people like Marcus: they respected you precisely because you had no soul of your own to get in the way.

"Good," Marcus said. "You're learning. Now. The founding story has three parts. My grandfather's stake. The war years, when everyone else sold out or burned out and we held. And then—" he tapped the papers, "—the part where the valley forgot who actually built it."

Silas's pen paused. A half-second. He hoped it didn't register.

"Forget how?"

"There's a family up the road." Marcus said it the way other men said *there's a rat in the wall*. "The Reyeses. They've spent sixty years telling anyone who'll listen that their grandfather discovered this valley, planted the first vines, taught my grandfather everything he knew. There's even a plaque at the historical society. Did you see it?"

"I saw a plaque."

"It's a lie with a bronze frame." Marcus leaned back, lacing his fingers over his stomach, a man settling into a story he'd rehearsed his whole life. "My grandfather corresponded with a vintner in Sonoma—that's where the real techniques came from. The Reyes patriarch was a field hand who married well and bought a sliver of land adjacent to ours. Adjacent. But proximity breeds mythology, and mythology breeds claims. Their grandson—" a small, humorless smile, "—Rafael, I think his name is. Your neighbor. He's been making noise lately about the original parcel boundaries. Water rights. Old easements. He's hired a lawyer."

Silas wrote none of this down. That was its own kind of note.

"And what does that have to do with the memoir?" he asked.

"Everything." Marcus stood, crossed to the window. Beyond the glass, the vine rows ran downhill in long green sentences, and somewhere among them—though Silas couldn't see him from here—was Rafael. Silas knew this the way you know weather. "A memoir isn't a book, Nystrom. It's a weapon with a dust jacket. When mine comes out, every wine blogger, every trade paper, every sommelier with an Instagram account is going to repeat one story: the story of who founded this valley. Public opinion is a deed. You can't record it at the county office, but it holds better than ink."

"You want me to write the version that wins."

"I want you to write the *true* version." Marcus turned, and his voice took on the warm, reasonable tone of a man about to ask you to do something terrible. "The truth is on my side here. I have letters. Ledgers. My grandfather's own hand. The Reyeses have a plaque and a grievance. Which sounds more credible to you?"

*The grievance,* Silas thought. *Grievances are always true somewhere.* But he said nothing, because saying nothing was the job.

* * *

That afternoon he went through the archive.

Marcus kept it in a locked room off the library—acid-free boxes, labeled folders, seventy years of the Delacroix paper trail. It was, professionally speaking, a gift. Letters in faded fountain pen. Photographs of men in suspenders standing in dirt rows. Receipts, deeds, a 1951 contract with a distributor whose signature looked like a seismograph reading.

And it was, Silas realized around four o'clock, a prosecution file.

He found the first piece of it by accident: a letter dated 1949, from Marcus's grandfather to the Sonoma vintner, asking detailed questions about rootstock and canopy management—and then, in a postscript, a line that made Silas's skin prickle:

*The young man working my north block shows real instinct. I've begun letting him think some of it is his idea.*

The young man. Emilio Reyes. Rafael's grandfather.

Silas sat very still in the archive's hush, the letter flat under his hands, and read it again. Then he found the next one, and the next. A decade of correspondence in which Emilio Reyes's name appeared again and again—not as a partner, not as a student, but as a resource. *The boy has a palate.* *Ask the Reyes boy to check the brix.* *I let him believe the blend was his suggestion; it keeps him loyal.*

And then, in 1961, a single line that stopped Silas's breath:

*He's asked for a share of the east parcel. Told him no such arrangement exists. He signed nothing. Sentiment is not a contract.*

Sentiment is not a contract. Six words, and Silas could hear the whole rest of the story behind them—the argument, the betrayal, the family that walked away up the road and spent sixty years telling the truth to people who preferred the prettier lie.

Because that was the thing. Marcus hadn't lied to him, exactly. Marcus had simply handed him a curated truth and trusted him—trusted the pen—to make it the only truth there was.

Silas closed the box. His hands were steady. That was the worst part. Ten years of ghostwork had trained his hands to stay steady through anything, through other people's divorces and deaths and manufactured redemption arcs, and now they stayed steady while he held a family's stolen history in a folder marked FOUNDING — SUPPORTING DOCS.

*You're just a pen,* he told himself, walking back across the lawn in the gold late light. *Pens don't choose what they write.*

But pens don't read archives at four in the afternoon and feel their chest go tight. Pens don't look up the hill toward a cottage with a borrowed ladder still leaning against its fence and feel sick.

* * *

That evening he tried to work on Chapter Two—the war years, the noble holding-on—and got four hundred words into a paragraph about Marcus's grandfather's courage before he understood what he was actually doing. Every heroic sentence he polished became a brick in the wall between the Reyes family and their claim. Every elegant turn of phrase would be quoted, shared, repeated until *Delacroix* and *founding* fused in the public mind like a brand burned into hide.

He deleted the paragraph. Typed it again. Deleted it.

Through the open window came the smell of the vines warming down after the day, and underneath it, faintly, woodsmoke from Rafael's place. Silas thought about the crate label he'd seen weeks ago, the handwriting he'd know anywhere, the letters he'd stopped answering because answering meant becoming someone, and being someone meant being seen, and being seen meant—

He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes.

Here was the arithmetic he couldn't escape: if he wrote the book Marcus wanted, he would be doing to Rafael's family what he had once done to Rafael himself—taking something given freely, in trust, and using silence as the weapon. The letters had been a gift. The archive was a gift. Both times, the same man, taking.

His phone buzzed. An email from Marcus, subject line: *Chapter Two notes.* Silas opened it with numb fingers.

*Strong start. One structural thought: the founding material should come FIRST, not second. We lead with the claim. I want the Reyes question addressed head-on—call it "Setting the Record Straight." Draft it by Friday. Be thorough. Be devastating. That's what they pay you for.*

*Be devastating.*

Silas set the phone face-down on the table, as if it might bite. Outside, the last light was draining off the hills, and down the slope he could just make out a figure moving along the vine rows in the dusk—checking fruit, probably, or just walking, the way Rafael walked his rows every evening like a man taking attendance.

Ten years ago, an envelope arrived every week with handwriting like that figure's silhouette: sure, unhurried, beautiful. Silas had read them by streetlight in a basement apartment he couldn't afford, and they had kept him alive, and then he had stopped writing back, and the envelopes stopped coming, and he had told himself for a decade that this was mercy—for both of them.

Now he was being paid to erase the man's whole family.

He opened his laptop. The cursor blinked in the empty document, patient as a held breath. *Setting the Record Straight*, he typed, because Marcus wanted a title, and titles cost nothing.

Then, below it, he typed one more line—not for the book, not for Marcus, just to see what it felt like to put it in the world:

*Emilio Reyes planted the first vines in this valley, and everyone who says otherwise knows less than I do.*

He stared at it for a long time. Then he saved the document, closed the laptop, and sat in the dark listening to the vines breathe, telling himself he had plenty of time to decide which side of the page he lived on.

Friday was four days away.
