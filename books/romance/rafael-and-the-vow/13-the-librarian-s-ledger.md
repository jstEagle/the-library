## 13. The Librarian's Ledger

The Marrow Bay Public Library smells like pencil shavings and old carpet, and on the morning I walk in with a canvas tote full of contraband, it smells like opportunity.

Opportunity, that is, if you define it the way I've come to define most things lately: as a bad idea I'm going to have anyway.

"Morning, Coral." Odette Pringle looks up from behind the circulation desk, where she's been stamping due-date cards since roughly the Eisenhower administration. She's seventy-something, built like a fire hydrant, and runs the library with the gentle totalitarianism of a woman who has outlived every school board member who ever tried to tell her what to do. "You're not due back those Steinbecks until Thursday."

"I know." I set the tote on the counter. "These aren't returns."

She peers into the bag. Then she takes off her glasses. That's never a good sign. When Odette removes her glasses, it means she wants you to see her eyes while she judges you, and her eyes are very good at judging. Forty years of shushing has given them a kind of laser quality.

"Coral Lockridge," she says slowly, "are those first editions?"

"They might be."

"*Cannery Row* with the original dust jacket. *Tortilla Flat*. Is that—good Lord, is that a *Cup of Gold*?" She says the title the way other women say a lover's name. "Where did you get a *Cup of Gold*?"

"Estate sale in Port Angeles. The widow didn't know what she had." I keep my voice casual, which takes effort, because my heart is doing something embarrassing against my ribs. "I thought the library might want them."

"The library would want them the way a drowning man wants a raft." Her fingers hover over the books without touching, the way people hover over things they can't afford. "But we both know the library budget couldn't cover a fraction of what these are worth. So either you're feeling charitable, which we both know you're not, or you want something."

I look at her over the counter. She looks at me.

"What do you want, Coral?"

Here's the thing about small towns: everyone knows your business, but nobody knows your business better than the librarian. The library is where the town keeps its memory—the tax rolls, the old newspapers on microfilm, the minutes of every council meeting since 1924, and now, apparently, the sealed correspondence of forty-one citizens who signed up for a centennial pen-pal program because Wren told me I needed friends and Ada Voss apparently had a whim she couldn't explain.

I still don't know why I'm doing this. That's a lie. I know exactly why. Six weeks ago a stranger started writing to me under the name Lighthouse, and somewhere between her second letter and her ninth, she became the most honest conversation I have in a week. And then last week, right after the council meeting where Ada Voss stood at a podium and gutted my grant petition in front of God and everybody, Lighthouse went quiet. Not gone. Quiet. Her letters got shorter, careful, like someone walking across ice and listening for cracks.

And I need to know something. Anything. Whether the woman who writes to me at midnight could be the same woman who smiled politely while telling the council my bookshop was "a charming anachronism with no viable preservation case." Because if they're the same woman, I need to grieve that properly, alone, before I say something unforgivable to one of them by accident.

Or maybe I just need to know that the person who knows me best isn't wearing a power suit and a town attorney badge.

"You have the matching records," I say. "For the pen-pal program. You paired everyone."

Odette folds her hands on the counter. "Those records are confidential. It's the entire premise of the program. Anonymous correspondents. Sealed identities. There's a whole paragraph about it in the signup form. I wrote the paragraph myself."

"I know. I read it. Twice."

"And yet here you are, attempting bribery with stolen literature."

"Not stolen. Acquired at fair market value from a grieving widow who is presumably spending the proceeds somewhere warm." I slide the tote another inch toward her. "Look, Odette. I'm not asking you to tell me who she is. I'm not even asking you to confirm anything. I just—" I stop. Reorganize the sentence. Start again. This is what grief does to a man; it turns every honest impulse into a negotiation. "I write to someone. It matters to me. More than it should. And lately I've been wondering if I'm being made a fool of, and I'd rather know than wonder."

Something shifts in Odette's face. Not softness exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman who once waited three years for a man to come back from the fishing fleet and learned everything there is to know about wondering.

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said at this desk since the mill closed," she says. "Which is unfortunate, because honesty doesn't change the answer."

"Odette—"

"No." She holds up a hand. "But." And there it is, the word I came for, hanging in the air between us like a fishhook. "*But* I am a librarian, Coral, and librarians believe in a very specific thing: that information belongs to the person willing to do the work to find it. I won't show you the ledger. But I never said the ledger was the only record."

I stare at her. "What does that mean?"

"It means," she says, putting her glasses back on with a decisive little motion, "that when I set up the program, I kept two sets of records, because I am seventy-four years old and I don't trust my own filing system, let alone the town's. The official ledger is locked in my desk. The working copy—the one I used to actually make the pairings, with all my notes and cross-outs and second thoughts—is in a box in the archive room, misfiled under local history, 1974, because nobody has asked for a book about the herring industry since 1974." She sniffs. "If someone were to, hypothetically, volunteer to help reorganize the archive room—which is a disaster, and has been for years—that person might stumble across it. And I might be too busy at the front desk to notice what he did or didn't read."

"Odette Pringle. Are you corrupting me?"

"I'm bribing you with labor instead of taking your bribe in literature, which makes me the only honest person in this transaction." She pushes the tote back toward me. "Keep the books. Donate them after you find whatever you're looking for, if you still want to. And Coral?" She leans forward. "Whatever you find in that box—think hard about whether knowing is worth more than not knowing. Some doors only open one way."

"I know," I say. "That's what everybody keeps telling me."

"Everybody's usually right. That's why they keep saying it."

* * *

The archive room is in the basement, down a staircase that creaks in six different places like it's announcing each step to the building. It's a long low room lined with metal shelving, lit by humming fluorescents that flicker in a rhythm I suspect is less electrical fault than institutional mood. Boxes everywhere. Decades of boxes. Minutes of the Rotary Club from 1961. A taxidermied herring, inexplicably, staring at me from a shelf with the glassy conviction of a creature that died for nothing.

I find the 1974 herring-industry box on the third aisle, third shelf, exactly where she said. It weighs too much for a book about fish.

I carry it to the worktable under the light and stand there for a moment with my hands flat on the lid.

Here's the arithmetic of my life lately. On one side: a woman whose letters arrive like weather, changing the temperature of whole days. Who wrote me four pages about her father's habit of reading obituaries aloud at breakfast "so the dead could hear how they were remembered," and made me laugh until Wren asked what was funny and I said nothing and meant everything. On the other side: a woman in a gray blazer who looked me in the eye at the council meeting and said the word "denied" like she was closing a file.

Two women. One town. Statistically, the overlap should be impossible. Marrow Bay has maybe nine hundred residents left, and half of them are related to each other. But the letters are unsigned, the handwriting is typed—everyone submits through the library's ancient typewriters, Odette's rule, "so nobody recognizes the loops"—and the program has forty-one participants. Forty-one. I've been trying to hold that number in my head like a shield. Forty-one possible people. The odds protect me. The odds let me keep both women without choosing.

Except.

Except Lighthouse types her lowercase i's slightly off-center, drifting left, like the key sticks. Except she uses em-dashes wrong, too many of them, the way lawyers do in briefs. Except she knew, without my ever saying it, that the customer I complained about in my first letter—the one who buys children's books and never smiles—was a woman, and referred to her as "she" in her reply, and I never corrected her, because some part of me wanted to see if she'd flinch.

She didn't flinch. She wrote back: *Maybe she doesn't smile because no one's ever given her a reason that didn't cost her something.*

I open the box.

Inside, under a decoy layer of yellowed pamphlets about cannery safety, is a green ledger book, spiral-bound, the cover soft with handling. Odette's handwriting fills it in columns: participant numbers, pseudonyms assigned, dates of letters received and forwarded. And in the margins, in smaller script, her notes. Little observations. *No. 12 seems lonely.* *No. 17 dropped out, matched No. 3 with spare.* *No. 29 writes like she's being graded.*

My hands are sweating. I wipe them on my jeans like a teenager.

I don't look up names. That's the line I drew on the drive over, the line I repeated to myself in the parking lot like a prayer: *numbers only, numbers only.* I can know the shape of the thing without knowing the face. I can see whether my pairing is real, whether Lighthouse is who Odette thinks she is, whether there's some clerical error, some crossed-out second thought, that explains the silence. I will not put a name to her. Knowing the name would end it—one way or the other, it would end the version of this that lives outside the world, and I'm not ready for it to live in the world. In the world it can be taken away. Everything in the world gets taken away. Elena taught me that, though she'd hate me for saying so. Elena would say, *Coral, honey, that's not grief talking, that's just cowardice with a vocabulary,* and she'd be right, and I'd kiss her forehead, and we'd both pretend the sentence hadn't happened.

I find my number. Anchor, No. 14. My own entry stares up at me in Odette's tidy hand, and next to it, the pairing:

**No. 14 — ANCHOR — matched with No. 27 — LIGHTHOUSE**

Sealed. Real. One correspondent, not a committee, not a prank by Wren, not some library volunteer writing sermons to lonely shopkeepers. Someone sat down at one of these typewriters, week after week, and chose me.

In the margin beside No. 27, Odette has written: *Reluctant signup. Almost walked out twice. Asked if the program guaranteed the other person wouldn't know who she was. Told her yes. She seemed disappointed.*

Disappointed. I read that word five times. She seemed *disappointed* that anonymity was guaranteed. Which means somewhere in this town is a person who signed up to write to strangers and immediately wished it weren't anonymous—and then spent six weeks writing to me like I was the only solid ground in her week.

I close the ledger. Open it again. Close it. My hand rests on the cover like it's holding down something alive.

Don't. Don't don't don't. The names are in here—I can feel them, the way you can feel a splinter through a bandage. One column over. One glance. One flick of the page and I'd know whether the woman who wrote *you sound like a man keeping the tide out with his hands* is the same woman who denied my grant with a straight face, and either way I'd never un-know it, and either way I'd start performing, start editing myself, start writing to the name instead of the person—

The fluorescent light buzzes. The herring watches. Somewhere upstairs, a door opens and closes, footsteps crossing the floor above my head, and I realize I've been standing in a basement for forty minutes having a moral crisis over a spiral notebook, and that this is, objectively, the most dramatic thing I've done since the funeral.

I turn the page.

Not to the names. To the back section, where Odette tracked letter counts. No. 27's column: eleven letters sent, ten received. Mine: ten sent, eleven received. The math of us, balanced like a scale. And beneath the last entry, dated three days ago—a date I know, because that's the day my letter about the council meeting arrived and hers stopped mid-week—there's a note in fresh ink:

*No. 27 requested pause. Reason given: "personal conflict of interest discovered." Declined to elaborate. Advised her the program allows pauses, not disappearances. She said, quote, "I know. I'm not disappearing. I'm deciding."*

Deciding.

I sit down on the metal stool, which complains under me, and I laugh—one short, ugly syllable that echoes off the shelves. She's deciding. Somewhere out there, in a gray blazer or a cardigan or bare feet in a kitchen at midnight, a woman is sitting with my letters spread around her like evidence, weighing me, and I have spent six weeks thinking I was the one being toyed with.

The relief hits first, and it's shameful how big it is. She didn't leave because of me. She paused because of herself. Whatever war she's fighting, it started inside her own walls.

Then the fear arrives, right on schedule, because relief is always a down payment. *Personal conflict of interest.* Those are lawyer words. That's not the language of a librarian or a teacher or a waitress. That's the language of someone whose job involves interests, conflicts, disclosures. That's the language of the courthouse.

I think of Ada Voss buying picture books with exact change, never smiling. I think of her voice at the podium, precise and bloodless. I think of Lighthouse writing, *maybe she doesn't smile because no one's ever given her a reason that didn't cost her something,* and I feel the two sentences lock together like gears, and I slam the ledger shut so fast it knocks the herring box sideways.

No. No, I'm not doing this. I'm not going to build a case out of margins and ink smudges. I've watched this town convict people on less. I've been convicted on less.

I put the ledger back in the box, under the cannery pamphlets, square it to the shelf, and stand there breathing in the smell of dust and dead herring, waiting for my heart to behave.

It doesn't quite. Because here's what I know now, and can't unknow: she's real, she's singular, she chose me, and she's deciding something that scares her enough to go quiet. Three facts. I carried four questions into this basement and I'm leaving with three answers and a worse question, which is probably how knowledge works generally.

Upstairs, Odette looks up as I emerge, and reads my face the way she reads overdue lists.

"Well?" she says.

"I didn't look."

"I know. Your hands are shaking, but your eyes are clear. Guilt shakes the hands and clouds the eyes." She stamps a returned book with unnecessary force. "Find what you needed?"

"Some of it." I rest my forearms on the counter, suddenly tired in the marrow. "Odette, when she comes back—if she comes back—don't tell her I was down there."

"Wasn't planning to. Librarians keep secrets professionally." She studies me a moment. "But I'll tell you something for free, since you refused to pay for yours. In thirty years of running programs in this town, I've learned one thing about the ones who ask for pauses." She slides my canvas tote back across the counter—the books still in it, the bribe refused twice now. "They always come back. It's the ones who keep writing who you have to worry about."

I take the bag. At the door I stop, one hand on the frame, and look back at the long room with its lamps and its microfilm and its basement full of sealed doors.

"Odette. If a letter comes in for No. 14—from No. 27—call me at the shop. Any hour."

"Any hour," she agrees. "Now go home. You look like a man who needs to reread something."

I walk out into the afternoon, the tote heavy against my hip, and I'm halfway down Harbor Street before I admit to myself what I'll be doing tonight: pulling every letter out of the biscuit tin, laying them across the bed in order, and reading them like a man reading scripture—hunting for the verse that tells me who she is, terrified of finding it, more terrified of wanting to.

The shop bell rings as I unlock the door. Wren looks up from behind the register, doing homework among the paperbacks, and her face does the thing it does—brightens, then narrows, the twelve-year-old radar sweeping me.

"You went to the library," she says.

"I did."

"For a long time."

"Archives are slow."

Her eyes drop to the tote. "Did you get any books?"

I look down at the first editions I stole to give away, refused twice, and am now stuck with. "As a matter of fact," I say, "I got exactly what I deserved."

She goes back to her homework, satisfied, and I head for the back room and the biscuit tin, and that's when the phone rings—the old wall phone, the one almost nobody calls—and Odette's voice comes down the line before I finish saying hello, quick and low, like a woman passing contraband:

"Coral. She broke the pause. Letter came in twenty minutes ago for No. 14." A beat. "It's two pages. And Coral—it's not signed 'Lighthouse.' It's signed with a real initial. Just one letter of the alphabet. And I have a feeling you already know which one."
