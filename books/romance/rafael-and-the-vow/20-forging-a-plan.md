## 20. Forging a Plan

There is a particular sound a photocopier makes when you are committing career suicide on it. I had never noticed it before Tuesday night. It's a kind of arrhythmic wheeze, like an animal that knows it's being watched and has decided to be dramatic about it.

The town attorney's office after hours is not a glamorous place. It smells of toner and old carpet and the lemon cleaner Mrs. Pell uses on the conference table every Friday as though the table itself might one day testify. The fluorescents hum in a key I can only describe as bureaucratic minor. And I stood there at nine-forty in the evening, in my second-best blazer because the best one was at the dry cleaner along with my dignity, feeding pages of a seven-year-old appraisal into a machine like a woman feeding coins into a slot machine she already knew was rigged.

Page forty-one. The comparable sales. Page forty-two. The appraiser's certification, signed by a man named Dale Pruitt who, according to the state licensing database I'd checked twice from my kitchen table with a glass of wine I did not finish, had let his license lapse in 2019 and been formally disciplined for inflated valuations in two counties.

Page forty-three. My signature. Notarized. Dated. Certifying, in language drafted by someone paid considerably more than me, that the appraisal attached thereto was "true, complete, and correct to the best of my knowledge and belief."

I had been twenty-nine years old. I had four hundred files on my desk and a supervisor who said "initial here" the way other people say "breathe." I had initialed here approximately nine thousand times that year.

None of which is an excuse. I want to be very clear about that, because if this chapter of my life ever gets read aloud—and God help us all, some part of me suspects it will—I don't want anyone mistaking context for absolution. I certified a fraudulent appraisal. I helped take a house from a dead woman's family and hand it to a bank that had manufactured the number out of thin air and ambition. The fact that I didn't know doesn't make it not true. It just makes it the kind of true that lives in your chest and waits.

The copier wheezed. I fed it another page.

* * *

Let me back up, because even my own internal narration is getting ahead of itself, and Ada Voss does not get ahead of herself. Ada Voss makes lists. Ada Voss color-codes. Ada Voss once alphabetized a spice rack during a power outage by candlelight, which tells you everything and also nothing, depending on how charitable you're feeling.

It started, properly started, three weeks ago, with a folder.

The Meridian file room is in the basement of the county annex, which is the kind of architectural decision that tells you exactly how much a municipality values its own history. Fluorescent tubes, half of them dead. Boxes labeled in three different clerks' handwriting across thirty years. I'd gone down there originally to pull a right-of-way easement for the harbor path project—the thrilling stuff of small-town law, the bread and butter, the reason my mother still tells people her daughter "works with contracts"—and I'd come up the stairs forty minutes later with the easement in one hand and the Lockridge foreclosure file in the other, and no memory of having made the decision to take it.

That's not entirely honest either. I remember the decision. It felt less like a decision than like a door opening in a house I thought I knew the floor plan of.

I told myself it was due diligence. Centennial year, lots of old records surfacing, prudent to review. I am very good at telling myself things in complete sentences with legal structure. I once talked myself out of crying at my own father's funeral using the phrase "deferred emotional processing," and I believed it for almost six years.

But the truth—the truth I have only ever told to a stranger with a pen name—is that I went looking. After the council meeting, after Coral Lockridge stood up in front of the whole room with his petition for the preservation grant and his voice doing that thing where it stays level through sheer muscular effort, after I ruled against him citing procedural deficiencies I could have recited in my sleep because I'd helped draft the guidelines—after all that, I went home and I couldn't stop hearing his daughter's name. *Wren.* He'd said it once, when he gathered his papers, quiet, like a prayer or an apology: *Wren wanted that house on the application photo.*

So I pulled the file. And the file had a seam in it. Files like that always do, if you've spent eleven years learning to read them the way sailors read weather. The appraisal came in at $412,000 for a house that had sold eight years earlier for $189,000, in a market that had done nothing but sag. The comps were properties in Cedar Hollow, twelve miles inland, waterfront-adjacent in the way that a parking lot is ocean-adjacent. The loan-to-value math worked only if you believed the appraisal, and the appraisal worked only if you didn't look at it.

And my signature sat at the bottom of the certification page like a small, neat tombstone.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about discovering you were an instrument of injustice: the institution does not thank you. I want that stated plainly, because I spent most of my adult life believing the opposite—that systems are self-correcting, that the machinery grinds toward truth eventually if you just feed it enough paper. I believed it the way you believe in the tide. It was the last elegant thing I owned.

On Thursday, two days after the basement, I made what I would later recognize as my first real move: I requested the original loan underwriting package from Meridian Bank's records department, in writing, citing the centennial records audit. A fiction. A polite, notarized fiction. Meridian's records department took nine days to respond, which for a routine request is geological time, and when the response came it was a partial package with three pages missing—the three pages containing the appraisal review worksheet, the document where somebody at the bank was supposed to have asked, in writing, whether $412,000 for a sinking house in Marrow Bay was insane.

Somebody had removed them. Recently. The staple holes didn't match.

I sat in my office holding that response letter and I understood, with the calm clarity that I usually reserve for other people's catastrophes, that I was now in a story with villains in it. Real ones. The kind with institutional budgets and outside counsel and a government affairs office that sponsors the centennial festival's main stage. The kind who, when a small-town attorney starts pulling threads, do not write strongly worded letters. They make phone calls, and the phone calls become meetings, and the meetings become a quiet conversation in which a man named—well, we'll get to him—explains to you, smiling, what happens to towns like Marrow Bay when their attorney develops a reputation for being difficult.

I knew all this. I want it on the record that I knew all this, sitting there, holding the letter, and that my hands were not shaking. My hands were perfectly steady. It was everything else that was shaking—the floor of the profession I'd built my whole self on, the assumption that the rules were the refuge, the belief that if you just followed procedure, procedure would protect you.

Procedure had protected the bank. Procedure had taken Elena Lockridge's family home while her husband sat in a hospital waiting room learning the word *oncology*. Procedure had my signature on it.

* * *

Friday morning, my boss called me into his office.

Gordon Hale is not a bad man, which is the most dangerous thing about him. He has a photograph on his desk of his grandkids at the lake and a plaque that says SEMPER FI and a way of leaning back in his chair when he's about to disappoint you that suggests he's already grieved about it privately so you won't have to see him do it publicly.

"Ada." He laced his fingers over his stomach. "You request anything from Meridian lately?"

"Records audit," I said. "Centennial."

"Mm." He looked at me over his glasses for a long moment, and I watched him decide something. "Got a call yesterday. Friendly call. From a fellow I know at the bank's regional office." A pause with weight in it. "They're aware of your requests. They think maybe you've got a concern. And they'd like to resolve any concerns informally. Over lunch, maybe. Before anything gets—" he made a small gesture, like smoothing a tablecloth, "—formal."

"I don't have concerns," I said. "I have a records audit."

"Ada." He said my name the way you'd say it to a daughter, or a defendant. "Whatever you think you're doing. Don't. This town runs on that bank's goodwill. Half the mortgages, the business loans, the festival sponsorship. You start pulling threads on Meridian, you're not pulling threads on Meridian. You're pulling threads on Marrow Bay." He leaned forward then, and his voice dropped into something almost kind. "You're good at your job. Better than good. Whatever this is, it isn't worth your license. Stand down. Please."

I thanked him. I am proud of that, actually—of the thanking. Of walking out of that office with my spine straight and my face composed and my heart going like a trapped bird, and saying *thank you, Gordon, I appreciate the heads-up,* because whatever else I am, I am not a woman who gives people the satisfaction of watching her flinch.

Then I went back to my desk and I sat down and I thought: *stand down.*

Stand down. As though conscience were a military position you could abandon in good order. As though the signature weren't already on the page. As though Coral Lockridge hadn't stood in front of the council two weeks ago and called the system, in his quiet terrible way, exactly what it was—and as though I hadn't been the one holding the gavel-shaped piece of it.

I opened my bottom drawer, where I keep the things I'm not ready to look at, and I took out the copy of the Lockridge file I'd made in the basement, and I put it in my bag, and I drove home, and I did not sleep.

* * *

Which brings us, circuitously, the way all my worst decisions travel, to Tuesday night. To the copier. To the wheeze.

I want to describe the copying honestly, because in the movies this moment is always scored with strings and close-ups, and in life it is mostly toner smudges and the sudden paranoid certainty that every car passing the window is slowing down.

Three sets. That was the plan, and I am a creature of plans. One set for the state bar's ethics intake—they have a whistleblower channel, I'd found it at one a.m. reading the regulatory code in bed like a deviant. One set for a journalist. I had a name: Priya Raman at the *Coastal Register*, who'd done a series two years ago on appraisal fraud upstate and whose byline I trusted more than I currently trusted my own profession. And one set—the third set—for reasons I couldn't fully articulate even to myself, except that it was addressed, in my head, to a man who ran a bookshop and hated me, and who deserved to see the documents before anyone else decided what they meant. His wife's name was in that file. His grief was in that file. If the truth was coming out, it was going to reach him first, from me, not from a headline.

The copier jammed on set two, page nineteen. Of course it did. I knelt on the carpet in my second-best blazer and extracted a sheet mangled through the fuser roller, and I laughed—one short, unhinged syllable that echoed off the filing cabinets—and somewhere in that laugh was the full comprehension of what I was doing.

Because let's be precise about the stakes, since precision is my love language. Copying these documents without authorization: potential termination, certainly. Potential criminal exposure for unauthorized access to confidential records—my own bar card used against me, my own training turned inside out. Disbarment proceedings if the bar decided I'd tainted my own evidence. Blacklisting from every firm within commuting distance. The end of the only identity I had ever successfully built. Ada Voss, town attorney, keeper of contracts, alphabetizer of spice racks—gone, replaced by Ada Voss, *difficult*, the cautionary tale they tell new hires.

And against that: a house returned to a family that had it stolen. A girl named Wren getting the home her father promised her mother. A bank taught, publicly, expensively, that small towns can read numbers too.

I stood there with the warm copies in my arms and I did the arithmetic, and the arithmetic came out the way arithmetic does when one column is your entire career and the other column is a word like *justice*, which doesn't convert cleanly into any currency.

My father was a fisherman. He used to say the sea doesn't care about your plans, it cares about your knots—tie them right and you might live through what you can't control. I never understood the saying until that night, kneeling on municipal carpet, feeding a machine. Duty was the plan. Conscience was the knot. And I had finally, finally tied it right.

I finished set three at ten-fifteen. I shredded my working notes—not to hide, but because the notes were mine and sloppy and the documents speak for themselves. I locked the originals back in the cabinet, because destroying them would be obstruction and I refuse, absolutely refuse, to become the thing I'm reporting. There is a line between whistleblowing and vandalism, and I intend to die on the correct side of it, possibly literally.

Then I turned off the lights, and I stood in the dark of my own office for a moment, and I said goodbye to it. Not dramatically. Just—a nod, colleague to colleague. Eleven years of case files and form letters and the harbor easement and the dog-leash ordinance and the centennial permits. Eleven years of believing the rules were the refuge.

The rules were never the refuge. They were the weather. What you do in the weather—that's the self.

* * *

Wednesday morning I mailed two envelopes. Bar intake, certified mail, return receipt. *Coastal Register*, same. Standing at the post office counter, licking the envelope like some analog criminal, I felt lighter than I had in years, which is its own kind of terrifying, because lightness, in my experience, is what gravity feels like right before the drop.

The third set stayed in my bag. I carried it around for two days like a organ waiting for a transplant recipient. Wednesday night I drafted a cover letter to Coral four times. Thursday night, five times. Every draft collapsed under the same problem: how do you hand a man the proof of his own catastrophe and sign your name to it, when your name is already written all over the crime?

*Dear Mr. Lockridge. Enclosed please find the documents showing that I helped ruin your life. Coffee sometime?*

No.

*I have recently discovered—*

No. He knows what he lost. He's known for seven years. He doesn't need discovery; he needs restitution, and an apology, and neither of those fits in an envelope.

Friday afternoon I gave up on the letter and decided I would simply have to give him the papers in person, which meant finding him, which meant the bookshop, which meant walking into Harbor Street Books and facing the man whose hatred of me is the most honest relationship I have left in this town besides one anonymous stranger who may or may not hate me too.

And then, because the universe has a sense of humor that would get it fired anywhere else, my phone buzzed. Festival committee reminder. Centennial planning meeting, Monday evening, town square pavilion. Attendance expected of all board members.

All board members. Which included me. And, per the volunteer list I'd signed off on last month without reading it properly, also included one Coral Lockridge, representing the merchants' association, because of course it did. Because this town is small enough that you cannot swing a cat without hitting your own consequences.

Monday, then. The square. Him.

I put the third set of copies back in my bag, deep in the inner pocket, against my hip, where I could feel it like a heartbeat. And I thought about what Lighthouse would say—if Lighthouse existed, if Lighthouse were real, if somewhere in this town there was actually a person who wrote to me about bad coffee and Mary Oliver and the specific loneliness of doing the right thing with no witnesses. Lighthouse would say something infuriatingly gentle. Lighthouse would say: *the bravest letters are the ones you sign.*

Monday, then. I'd bring the papers. And God willing, I'd find the nerve.

The envelope arrived Saturday morning, cream-colored, my name handwritten in ink I almost recognized—careful, slightly pressed, the handwriting of a man who writes slowly on purpose. I stood on my porch steps holding it, the sea wind pulling at my hair, and I knew, before I opened it, the way you know weather, that whatever was inside was going to change the shape of everything.

I opened it anyway. I always open the letter.
