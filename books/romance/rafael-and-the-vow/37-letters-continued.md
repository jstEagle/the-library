## 37. Letters, Continued

*Anchor to Lighthouse — Tuesday, 7:14 a.m., written at the kitchen table while you were still asleep*

Dear Lighthouse,

I want it on the record that I am writing this letter three feet away from you. You are asleep with your cheek on a casebook and your hair doing something the laws of physics should be ashamed of, and I am writing you a letter anyway, because last night you said—"said," out loud, with your mouth—that you had something difficult to tell me and then you told me it was that you'd finished the crossword.

You did not finish the crossword. I watched you fill in "osprey" as "sparrowhawk" and then defend it for eleven minutes.

Some truths are easier said in ink. That's what we agreed. So here is mine: I have started sleeping better since you moved your toothbrush in, and I don't know what to do with that information except write it down where you can't interrupt me by kissing me, which is your usual method of ending conversations you find too sincere.

The coffee this morning is terrible. I made it. This is not an apology; it's a warning. Wren says we should buy the good beans but Wren also said we should adopt a second cat named Judge, so her counsel is under review.

Yours across the table,
Anchor

P.S. There is a letter in the mail slot downstairs from the pen-pal program. The librarian has launched Year Two. We'll discuss. Or rather, I'll discuss and you'll say "we're mentors now, Coral, act like it" in the voice you use when you're right, which is all of them.

* * *

*Lighthouse to Anchor — Tuesday, 8:02 a.m., written at the same kitchen table, which is now a contested jurisdiction*

Dear Anchor,

First: the osprey was correct in spirit. Second: I was not asleep, I was resting my eyes strategically, which is a legal term I just invented. Third: your coffee is not terrible, it is punitive, and I've drafted a formal complaint.

Here is my truth-in-ink for the morning, since we're doing this: I have lived alone for nine years and I keep forgetting that other people exist before eight a.m. Yesterday I walked into the bathroom and found Wren's shampoo sitting next to mine like it had always been there—some coconut thing shaped like a pineapple—and I stood there for a full minute feeling something I can only describe as *unlawfully content*. I checked. It's not against any statute. I looked.

I also want to note, for the record you seem determined to build, that you hum when you shelve books. You think you don't. You do. It's mostly sea shanties, which is the most on-brand thing about you, and I am telling you here instead of out loud because if I said it aloud you'd stop, and then the shop would lose its only soundtrack besides the radiator's death rattle.

The complaint re: coffee is attached. Remedy sought: one bag of the dark roast from the roaster on Pell Street. Damages: my dignity, re: crossword.

Lighthouse

P.S. I saw the envelope from the library too. I opened it. Don't make that face—I'm an officer of the court, opening other people's mail is basically my origin story. They want us to mentor the new pairs. Anonymously. Which means some poor soul in Marrow Bay is about to pour their heart out to a stranger who is actually two people sharing a kitchen table like a very slow, very literary horse costume.

* * *

*Anchor to Lighthouse — Wednesday, 6:40 a.m.*

Dear Lighthouse,

Regarding the mentorship: no.

That was going to be the whole letter but you'd call it "insufficiently reasoned," so here are my reasons. One: anonymity is dead to us. We killed it ourselves, on a stage, in front of four hundred people and a marching band. Two: whoever writes to "us" will figure it out in a week because you sign off with case citations and I sign off with grievances about the price of shipping. Three: I got into this program to complain about you. Now that I'm married to—forgive me, *partnered with*, I know how you flinch at the M-word like it's a subpoena—the letter-writing loses its edge.

Counterproposal: we mentor them openly. Names attached. Let them watch two people who spent a year loving each other through a mail slot and hating each other through a courtroom learn how to do it across a table. If that doesn't teach them anything, nothing will.

Also enclosed: the dark roast. I went to Pell Street at six. The roaster asked if "your wife takes sugar" and I said yes before my brain cleared customs. So. There's my truth in ink today. I called you my wife to a stranger and meant it and didn't correct it and don't intend to.

Anchor

P.S. Wren has begun charging admission to her room. She calls it "privacy tax." She learned that word from you and I want you to know I hold you personally responsible.

* * *

*Lighthouse to Anchor — Wednesday, 9:15 p.m., written in bed while you pretend to read*

Dear Anchor,

Re: your counterproposal. Accepted. Obviously. I accepted it around the time you wrote "married to" and then panicked mid-sentence like a man stepping on ice. For the record, I didn't flinch at the M-word. I flinched at the way you said it, which was the verbal equivalent of setting down something fragile and backing away. Do it again sometime and back away slower.

Now, my truth in ink, and this one took me all day to draft, so be gentle:

I used to think letters were a coward's medium. Ink as armor. You get to revise yourself before anyone sees you. Then I spent a year being known by a stranger who turned out to be the man whose house I helped take, and I learned that a letter isn't armor—it's a confession booth with a stamp. Everything true between us arrived on paper first. The kiss was just the paperwork catching up.

So here's what I can't say out loud yet, but can write three feet from you or thirty inches away, whichever: I'm afraid I'll ruin this. Not dramatically. Not with fraud or foreclosures. Just—slowly. With case citations at dinner. With the way I go quiet when I'm working a problem. With the fact that I have loved exactly one person before you and she left, and part of me keeps waiting for the pattern to repeat like it's precedent.

There. Filed.

Your wife-to-be-or-not-be,
Lighthouse

P.S. The privacy tax is extortion and I paid it. Four dollars. I want it noted that I negotiated her down from ten and she cited "market rates." She's going to be a terror. I've never been prouder of anyone.

* * *

*Anchor to Lighthouse — Thursday, 7:30 a.m.*

Dear Lighthouse,

I read your letter twice. Once at the table, once in the doorway of the bathroom while you brushed your teeth and pretended not to notice me reading it, which—you knew. You always know. It's unsettling and I've decided to find it charming.

Here's the reply I couldn't give you out loud either:

I'm not afraid you'll ruin this. I'm afraid I already did, once, to someone else, and that grief made me a man who kept receipts instead of keeping people. Elena used to say I loved in inventory—that I could tell you exactly how many copies of *Jane Eyre* we had but not what I was feeling on a Tuesday. She was right. She was usually right, which is probably why you two would have gotten along, or staged a coup together, fifty-fifty.

But here's what I know now, standing in doorways reading your handwriting: the vow I made at her grave wasn't "never love again." It was "Wren gets a home." And home turns out to be a word for wherever the pineapple shampoo sits next to mine and someone leaves case citations on the fridge like love notes, which, incidentally, *are* love notes, and I've kept every one, and yes I know that makes me a hypocrite about the receipts.

So. Precedent overturned. New ruling: nobody's leaving. Court adjourned until further notice, which is to say never.

Anchor

P.S. Tell Wren the privacy tax is unconstitutional. Tell her I said so. Watch her face when she realizes she has to argue with both of us now. That's not cruelty, that's civics.

* * *

*Lighthouse to Anchor — Thursday, 11:52 p.m., written on the back of a motion for summary judgment because we were out of paper and I refuse to go downstairs*

Dear Anchor,

Short one tonight, because you fell asleep on the couch with *Persuasion* open on your chest like a man staging his own cover art, and I wanted to write this while the watching-you-sleep tenderness was still fresh and hadn't curdled into "he snores."

Truth in ink: tonight Wren asked me to help her study for the spelling bee. Not you. Me. She said, and I quote, "You're stricter but you explain things better." I have been called many things in my career—ruthless, by-the-book, "that woman"—but never *better at explaining*, and I had to leave the room and stand in the pantry for a minute. The pantry, Coral. Where we keep the good beans. It's becoming a shrine to my own feelings and I need you to install a shelf in there or something so I have somewhere to put them.

We start practicing Saturday. If she wins, I'm taking partial credit. If she loses, I'm blaming your genes.

Lighthouse

P.S. You don't snore. You *murmur*. Last night you murmured "not the first editions" and rolled over. Even unconscious, you're insufferable about the inventory.

* * *

*Anchor to Lighthouse — Friday, 6:55 a.m.*

Dear Lighthouse,

Noted re: spelling bee. I'll be in the audience pretending to read while watching you coach her, and I will pretend not to be proud, and we will both see through it, and that's fine. That's the whole marriage, isn't it? Two people seeing through each other constantly and calling it love.

Which brings me to today's truth in ink, and I warn you it's practical, because even romantics have to fix gutters:

I found the box last night. The one from the old house—the Lockridge house, the one with the deed back in our name and Wren's curtains hanging in windows that used to belong to the bank's ledger. In it: Elena's letters to me, from before. Forty-one of them. I haven't opened the box since we moved it in. I stood there holding it like it might go off.

I'm not asking you what to do. I'm telling you what I'm going to do, which is read them, one a night, starting Sunday, and I'm telling you because for twelve years I would have done it alone in the shop after close like a man licking his wounds in the dark. Instead I'm going to do it at the kitchen table, with the lamp on, with you across from me pretending to work. Some truths are easier said in ink. But some are easier survived in company.

One a night. You're allowed to ask about them. I'm allowed to not answer. Those are the terms.

Anchor

P.S. The shelf in the pantry is happening this weekend. I measured. Your feelings will fit comfortably, with room to expand.

* * *

*Lighthouse to Anchor — Friday, 10:03 p.m.*

Dear Anchor,

Terms accepted. All of them. Especially the one where I'm allowed to ask and you're allowed to not answer, because I suspect the not-answering nights will tell me more than the answering ones, and I have learned to read your silences the way I used to read contracts—for what's missing.

My truth in ink tonight is small, but small things are load-bearing:

Today at the courthouse, clearing out my office, I found the original file. The Lockridge foreclosure. My signature on the certification page, twenty-nine years old and twenty-nine years wrong. The state bar sent a courtesy copy of the restitution order with it—Elena's family's name restored, the settlement routed to the preservation fund, your name listed as claimant. I stood in an empty office holding my own worst decision and felt—nothing I expected. Not dread. Not relief. Something quieter. Like a door closing gently after years of slamming.

I brought the file home. It's in the hall closet, behind the winter coats. I'm not hiding it. I'm *storing* it. There's a difference and I'm learning it. You taught me the difference without ever saying it, which is the most efficient teaching you've ever done.

Sunday, when you read Elena's first letter, I'll be across the table. I won't pretend to work. I'll just be there. Consider that my signature on your terms.

Lighthouse

P.S. Wren's word list includes "forgive." She asked me what it means. I said, "It's what happens when you stop making someone carry the whole weight forever." She said, "Like taking the backpack off?" Out of the mouths of babes, etc. Yes, Wren. Exactly like taking the backpack off.

* * *

*Anchor to Lighthouse — Saturday, 8:20 a.m., written fast, before you wake, because some things need to beat the sunrise*

Dear Lighthouse,

Last night you were asleep by ten and I sat up reading—not Elena's letters, those start Sunday—but ours. The whole year of them. Anchor to Lighthouse, the angry ones, the aching ones, the one where I told you about the foreclosure and named you and hated myself for the postage alone. I read them all in one sitting like a man rereading his own trial transcript.

And here's what I want to say, quickly, before the coffee's done and the day starts and we become people who say things out loud again:

Thank you for answering the first one. Whoever matched us—the librarian swears it was random, and I've decided to believe her, because the alternative is fate and fate has a lousy track record with this town. But somebody put my rage in front of your loneliness and let us do the rest, and the rest turned out to be everything.

Year Two starts Monday. Somewhere in Marrow Bay there's a pair of strangers writing their first letters—one of them angry, one of them careful—and they have no idea what's coming. On Monday we find out who they are. We sit across from them, names showing, scars showing, and we tell them the only thing we know for certain:

Write it down. Even when it hurts. *Especially* then. The truth keeps better in ink, and love—well. Love is just the day the letters stop needing envelopes.

See you in three feet.

Anchor

P.S. The coffee is good this morning. Pell Street dark roast. I made yours first. Some truths don't even need ink—they just need someone to get up early enough to prove them.
