## 11. Thaw, Briefly

The sound woke me at six-forty in the morning: a plow.

Not the town's little Kubota that Marisol's nephew drove, but a real one — the county's yellow beast with the steel blade, grinding up Harbor Road with the low, patient roar of something doing its job after being told to wait. I lay in my bed above the bakery and listened to it pass twice, once each way, and then heard the smaller miracle underneath it: tires. Actual tires, more than one set, moving at actual speed.

I got up and pressed my forehead to the cold glass. The storm had spent itself sometime before dawn, leaving the world scoured and blue-white, the sky so clean it looked rinsed. Down the hill, the harbor was a sheet of hammered pewter with ice fringing the moorings. The road out of town — the one road, the only road, the road that had been a rumor for four days — ran open and black between snowbanks taller than the mailbox at the general store.

Open. Which meant, among other things, that Elias Marsh could leave.

The thought arrived without drama, the way weather does. It simply was. He had a rental car parked under two feet of snow behind the inn, a firm in Boston waiting on his report about a building he'd come to acquire, a life with edges and appointments and a partner track that presumably did not pause because of coastal Maine weather. The storm had been an accident of timing. Timing was over now.

I dressed slowly, which was unlike me, and told myself it was because my socks were damp by the stove, which was true but not the reason.

* * *

By the time I came down the back stairs into the kitchen, Marisol was already there, flour to her elbows, and Elias was already there too, standing at her worktable in a borrowed apron that said KISS THE COOK in cracked lettering, looking like a man who had lost an argument he didn't know he'd entered.

"Tell her," Marisol said, without turning from the oven.

"Tell me what?" I said.

Elias held up his hands, palms out, as if surrendering to both of us at once. "Your car is buried," he said. "The inn has a shovel. I have been informed that the inn's shovel is broken."

"The inn's shovel is *broken*," Marisol confirmed, pulling a tray of rolls with the serenity of a woman delivering a verdict she'd reached days ago. "Rafael broke it last February prying ice off the dumpster. Nobody ordered a new one." She set the tray down and finally looked at him, and there was something in her face I hadn't seen before — not mischief exactly, but a kind of quiet, deliberate kindness, the look of someone arranging furniture in a room whose occupants don't yet know they're staying. "Roads are open till the next front comes in tomorrow night. You've got a window. But you're not driving anywhere until you help me finish this batch, because I promised the church supper forty loaves and my shoulder gave out on Tuesday."

"That's extortion," Elias said.

"That's *community*," Marisol said. "Extortion is when I also make you wash the proofing buckets."

He looked at me over the worktable. There was flour on his jaw, a smear of it high on one cheekbone like a bad attempt at war paint, and his hair was doing something unmanageable that ten years ago would have made me laugh out loud. Ten years ago I would have laughed. Now I made myself look at the dough instead.

"You could walk to the inn," I offered. "It's four hundred yards."

"It's four hundred yards uphill through knee-deep drift," he said. "And Rafael informed me this morning, very cheerfully, that the plow took out the footpath."

"Rafael," I said, "sounds like a busy man."

"Rafael," Marisol said, "is twelve and enjoys chaos. Wash your hands, both of you. We're making pain de campagne and we're making it properly, because if I'm going to feed forty Methodists, they're getting the good crust."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about bread: it is a clock you can touch.

Marisol had started her levain the night before, and it sat on the counter now, risen and domed and faintly sour-smelling, alive in the way that only flour and water and time can be alive. She divided it, fed it, talked while she worked — about hydration percentages, about why the church ladies preferred a softer crumb than God intended, about her daughter Lucia calling from Portland to say the roads were clear enough for a visit next week if the next storm behaved. And Elias listened the way he used to listen, which is to say completely, leaning in, asking the second question instead of the first.

"Why overnight?" he asked, when she explained the bulk ferment. "Why not just more yeast?"

"Because yeast is speed, and time is flavor," Marisol said. "You want fast bread, use yeast. You want bread worth eating, you let it sit in the cold and think about what it's done."

I snorted before I could stop myself. Both of them looked at me.

"Sorry," I said. "It just sounds like advice my grandmother would give."

"Your grandmother sounds wise," Marisol said.

"My grandmother sounded wise. Mostly she was talking about bread."

But Elias was still watching me, and there was something in his expression — not the recognition from the candlelit night of the storm, that stunned moment when my name had finally landed on him like a dropped plate, but something adjacent to it. Something that said: *you keep doing that. Saying things that sound like they cost you.*

"Shaping," Marisol announced, clapping flour off her hands. "Beatrix, show him. You've got good hands."

"I haven't done this since—"

"Since you were nineteen and ate half my mother's brioche dough raw in this kitchen," Marisol said. "Muscle memory doesn't expire. Show him."

So I showed him.

I turned my lump of dough out onto the floured wood and demonstrated the fold — how you stretch it up and over, quarter turns, gentle, never tearing, building tension in the surface without deflating the structure inside. "You're not kneading," I said. "Kneading is for sandwich bread. This is coaxing. You want it tight on the outside and loose in the middle."

"Tight outside, loose inside," Elias repeated, and began to fold his own, and was terrible at it. His dough tore. His tension collapsed. His loaf looked like something a tide had left.

"You're rushing," I said.

"I'm following instructions."

"You're following instructions *fast*. Slow down. Feel when it resists. When it resists, stop and wait, then fold again. It'll tell you."

"And if it doesn't talk to me?"

"Then you're not listening."

He stopped. Looked down at his hands in the dough, then up at me, and for a second the kitchen went very small around us — just the two of us and the smell of raw flour and the memory of another kitchen, another decade, when I'd stood behind him at a community-center counter correcting his grip on a knife and he'd gone still in exactly this way, listening harder to me than to the lesson.

"Okay," he said quietly. "Show me again."

I put my hands over his. I told myself it was instruction. His hands were warm and flour-dusted and slightly trembling, or maybe mine were; there was no way to tell whose tremor it was, and after a moment I stepped back because stepping back was the correct professional decision.

Marisol, at the oven, said nothing at all, which from Marisol was a shout.

* * *

We got the loaves into their bannetons and covered them, and Marisol declared a rest period — for the dough, she said pointedly, though she was looking at us — and went upstairs to call Lucia, leaving the two of us alone in her kitchen for the first time since the night he'd said my name and the whole hidden architecture of my life had come down around my ears.

I washed bannetons. He dried them. We worked down the line of them in silence, and the silence wasn't comfortable, but it wasn't hostile either. It was the silence of two people standing on either side of a thing too large to carry and neither willing to say *pick up your end*.

"So," he said finally. "The road's open."

"I heard."

"I should probably—" He set down the towel. "There's a process. When I get back. Reports, meetings, the whole machinery. They'll want photos of the building, comps on the lot, a timeline."

"Of course."

"And I keep thinking I should leave today. Beat the next front. Get ahead of it." He turned the towel over in his hands. "And I keep not leaving."

"Elias—"

"The loaf isn't done," he said. "That's the official reason. Marisol will tell you the official reason is the loaf. Forty Methodists are counting on me."

"Forty Methodists survived decades without you."

"They don't know that." He almost smiled, then didn't. "Beatrix. Can I say something that isn't about the loaf?"

My hands went still in the sink water. "You can say anything. That's apparently the theme of the week."

"When I realized who you were — that night, by the candles — I wanted to ask you a hundred questions. Why you left the way you left. Why you never answered the letters. Why you became—" he gestured vaguely, taking in me, the stairs, the lighthouse visible through the window above the harbor, "—this. A person who slips out the back door of a bakery rather than be recognized." He winced. "Marisol told me about that, by the way. The back door."

"Of course she did."

"But I'm not going to ask those questions. Not today. Because every time I start, I hear myself, and I sound like a man collecting evidence for a case, and I don't want to litigate you. I did enough of that at twenty-three, in my head, for ten years." He folded the towel into a precise rectangle, which was such an Elias gesture that my chest hurt. "So instead I'm going to stand here and finish a loaf of bread, and tomorrow, if the road's still open, maybe I'll leave. Or maybe I won't. And I'm not going to pretend I know which."

"That's not an answer," I said. "That's procrastination with flour on it."

"Yes," he agreed readily. "It absolutely is. I'm a man who closed a nine-figure hotel deal in eleven weeks, and I am currently hiding from a departure decision behind a church supper." He looked at me straight on, and there was no armor in it, which was worse and better than everything else. "I figure if I name the cowardice, it counts as progress. That's the system I'm running on right now. Low bar. Very achievable."

Despite everything — despite the ten years and the letters I'd burned and the rejection letter I hadn't, despite the fact that he was here to buy the building out from under the woman who'd become my family — I laughed. It escaped before I could vet it, short and helpless, and his face changed when he heard it, the way a room changes when a lamp comes on.

"There she is," he said softly.

"Don't," I said. "Don't do the thing where I'm a discovery."

"Sorry." He didn't sound sorry. "It's just — I read a book once where the narrator says people laugh in self-defense, and the ones worth loving laugh last, when they forget to defend."

The words went through me like a needle finding a vein. Because I knew the line. I had written the line, at two in the morning seven years ago, in a rented flat in Edinburgh, crying over a chapter I thought no one would ever read.

"*Tidewater*," I said, before I could stop myself, and then watched his whole face open.

"You've read it?" He said it the way people say the names of saints. "God, of course you have, everyone sensible has — that line wrecked me. I must have read that book five times. I bought copies for people. My sister. Two ex-girlfriends, which in retrospect was a mistake." He shook his head, laughing a little at himself. "I've always wanted to write to the author. Tell her what it did. I actually drafted a letter once, years ago, sitting in an airport. Never sent it. Felt too — I don't know. Too much. Like she'd think I was unhinged."

The sink water had gone cold around my wrists. Somewhere upstairs, Marisol's phone chimed. The levain sat on the counter, alive and patient, doing its slow work in the dark.

"You should send it," I managed. "Writers like that. Unhinged is fine. Unhinged sells books."

"Yeah?" He smiled, rueful. "Maybe I will. If I can find an address. She's famously private — no photo on the jacket, no interviews. My editor friend says she's a ghost." He picked the towel back up, resumed drying, casual as anything, and every word he'd just said sat in my chest like swallowed glass. "Anyway. Loaf. What's next, chef?"

"Bench rest," I said. "Twenty minutes. Then shaping."

"Then shaping." He nodded toward the window, where the black ribbon of the open road ran down toward the highway and the city and the life he'd built in the shape of everything I'd refused. "I really might go tomorrow, you know. I want you to hear me say it plainly, so it's not a trick later. The job is real. The offer on this building is real. I came here to do a thing and I haven't done it, and eventually I'll have to decide whether I'm the person who does it or the person who doesn't."

"I know," I said.

"And I need you to know that whichever way it goes, it isn't about the bread."

"No," I said. "It never is."

* * *

Marisol came back down as the afternoon light went long and gold across the snow, and we shaped the loaves — all forty, plus the twelve she called "the sinners" because they were for us — and scored them, and fired the oven, and by four o'clock the kitchen smelled like the inside of a promise. Elias's loaves were ugly. Two of them were genuinely structurally compromised. Marisol pronounced them "rustic" in the tone of a diplomat averting a war, and Elias held one up like a trophy and said, "Tight outside, loose inside," and I hated how warm the words felt in my ears.

At five, the county plow came through again, salting, and behind it Rafael's truck, and Rafael himself leaned on the horn twice in greeting, and the road stayed open, black and inviting, running south.

Elias stood at the window with a heel of the first test loaf in his hand, looking at it.

"You could make the inn by dark," Marisol said mildly, from the oven. "Get an early start in the morning. Beat the front by hours."

"I could," he said.

"Or," Marisol said, "there's soup."

He looked at the road a moment longer. Then he looked down at the loaf in his hand — his ugly, rustic, structurally compromised loaf, the first thing he'd finished in this town, the first thing in ten years, I suspected, that he'd finished with his hands — and something crossed his face that I recognized because I'd worn it myself for a decade: the look of a person deciding to call a delay a choice so they don't have to call it a fear.

"Soup," he said. "Obviously. The loaf needs to cool anyway."

"That's the spirit," Marisol said, and caught my eye over his shoulder, and winked.

Outside, far down the coast, past the point, the sky had already begun to stack itself with the next front — high gray clouds coming in off the water like a fleet under sail. The forecast on the radio, murmuring from the shelf by the flour bins, gave it eighteen hours, maybe twenty. Then the roads would close again, and whatever window was open now would shut, and everyone in this kitchen would have to live with what they'd done with the time.

Elias ate his ugly bread standing up. He said it needed salt. Marisol threw a dish towel at him. And I stood at the window with my cooling coffee, watching the road stay open a little longer, and understood — with the clarity that only arrives when you refuse to look at a thing directly — that I was doing the exact same arithmetic he was.

Neither of us was staying because of the loaf.

Both of us were going to let the loaf take the blame.
