## 35. Saying Yes Badly

The flour was everywhere.

That's the first thing I want you to understand about the most important moment of my life: I was covered in flour. Not a light dusting, not a charming chef's-kiss sprinkle — I mean everywhere. In my hair. Down my collar. On my glasses, which meant I'd been walking around half-blind for an hour and calling it focus. There was a white handprint on my thigh where I'd wiped my hands without thinking, so that from a distance I probably looked like a man who had grabbed his own leg for comfort, which, honestly, wasn't far from the truth.

Marisol had left twenty minutes earlier with a canvas bag of test loaves and a look on her face that I could only describe as *conspiratorial*. She'd paused at the door, looked at me, looked at Beatrix kneading at the back counter, looked at me again, and said, "I forgot — I have to go be somewhere that isn't here," with the subtlety of a woman shoving two people onto a stage and then striking the lights herself.

The bell over the door had barely finished ringing before the silence got heavy.

So I did what any reasonable man does when he's terrified. I baked.

I pulled out the rye starter Marisol kept in the walk-in like a sleeping animal, fed it, talked to it under my breath because Beatrix had once told me her grandfather talked to his boat engines and I'd never forgotten that she found it endearing. I scaled flour wrong twice. I over-salted the first batch of dough because my hands were shaking and the salt cellar has no business being that small. Beatrix watched all of this from the counter with her chin propped on her fist, saying nothing, wearing the particular expression of a woman attending a one-man show she already knows the ending to.

"You're going to kill that dough," she said finally, as I folded it for the fourth time in ninety seconds.

"It likes firm handling."

"It's rye, Elias. It wants to be left alone."

"Everything wants to be left alone," I said, and immediately heard how that sounded, and watched her hear how that sounded, and wished very sincerely for the floor of the bakery to open up and swallow me whole, flour and all.

She didn't laugh. Worse — she went soft. She came around the counter, took the dough out of my hands with a gentleness that felt like being disarmed, set it aside, covered it with a cloth, and turned back to face me. Up close she smelled like yeast and cold air and that cedar thing she always carried from the lighthouse, and I thought, *now*, and then I thought, *no, not now, you'll ruin it,* and then I opened my mouth anyway, because apparently ten years hadn't taught me a single thing about timing.

"Beatrix," I said. "I have something to say."

"I can see that." Her eyes were doing the thing where they crinkle before she smiles but she holds the smile in reserve, like a baker holding back proofing time. "You've got flour on your nose."

"I know." I didn't. I found out later there was flour on my nose.

I had planned this. That's the humiliating part. I'm a man who builds acquisition decks, who negotiates leases, who once talked a landlord down forty thousand dollars over a lunch he paid for. I had *planned* this confession. I had drafted it in my head during the drive back from the city, polished it in the shower at the inn, rehearsed it against the bathroom mirror until even the mirror looked embarrassed for me. The plan had structure. It had three movements. It began with an acknowledgment of everything I'd gotten wrong and ended with a question I'd been carrying since I was twenty-three years old and too proud to ask it.

What came out instead was:

"I tore up the memo."

Her eyebrows lifted. "I know. You told me."

"Right. Yes. But I want to tell you again, properly, because—" My hands were doing something independent of my brain, gesturing, flour drifting off them in little weather systems. "Because the memo wasn't just paper. It was eleven years of my life organized into bullet points. Market analysis. Comparable sales. A paragraph about 'synergies with our coastal portfolio' that I wrote at two in the morning and actually believed. And I stood in front of the inn yesterday with it in my hand, and I read your essay — I read it four times, standing in the cold like an idiot, and my hands were numb and I didn't care — and I understood that every word of that memo was true except for the only part that mattered, which is that the building isn't the point. The bread isn't even the point. The *person* is the—"

I stopped. Because somewhere around "the person is the" my brain caught up with my mouth and realized I was about to say something enormous in a bakery at four in the afternoon while looking like a ghost that had died mid-bake.

Beatrix waited. She was very good at waiting. She'd had practice — ten years of it, most of it my fault.

"The person," I tried again, more carefully, like crossing ice. "Is the point. For me. You're the point. Have been. Since—" I gestured vaguely at the last decade, which knocked more flour off my sleeve. "Since before the memo. Since before the storm. Since the night you corrected my fold technique at midnight and I lay awake in that terrible bed at the inn thinking, *oh no.* Oh no, oh no, because I knew what it meant, knowing that. It meant I'd never actually left. All those years in the city, all those restaurants, all that climbing — I told myself I was building something. I was just circling. Like a lighthouse beam, except useless, because I was the one going around in circles and you were the one standing still, and I—"

"Elias."

"—and I know I don't get to just arrive now and say all this, I know that, I know the arithmetic doesn't work in my favor, I did the math, believe me, I did it for ten years—"

"*Elias.*"

"—but I need you to know that I'm not asking you to forget anything. I'm asking if we can—"

And that's when she laughed.

Not cruelly. Not dismissively. She laughed the way water boils — suddenly, all over at once, unstoppable — and pressed both floury hands to her mouth, and her shoulders shook, and through her fingers she said, "Oh my God, you're *nervous.*"

"I am not nervous," I said, with the conviction of a man whose voice cracked on the second syllable.

"You are. You're babbling. You've been babbling for two full minutes. You used the phrase 'the arithmetic doesn't work in my favor.' Who says that? Who confesses love in *arithmetic?*"

"A man with a finance degree and no alternatives!"

"There were alternatives. There was a plan. I could see you reaching for it." She stepped closer. This close, I could see the flecks of gold in her eyes that I'd spent ten years failing to forget. "There was a whole speech. Three parts. I could see the scaffolding."

"There was a conclusion," I admitted. "It was good. It had a callback."

"To what?"

"To the first time I saw you. You were reading on the dock behind your grandfather's house and you had a pencil in your hair and you told me the ocean was showing off and I said—" My throat closed on it, the way it always closed on it. "I said I hoped it never stopped."

Something moved across her face. The laughter drained out of it slowly, the way tide leaves a beach, and what it left behind was bare and shining and terrifying.

"And now?" she asked quietly. "Do you still hope that?"

"No," I said.

Her expression flickered — hurt, quick as a fish — and I rushed forward, hands up, flour snowing off my sleeves.

"No — wait — that came out wrong, that's the opposite of what I—God, see, this is why I needed the speech, the speech would never have done this—" I dragged a breath in. "I don't hope it anymore. Hoping is for people standing on the shore. I'm not on the shore anymore, Beatrix. I resigned. I burned the boats. I am standing in your — in Marisol's — in *our* — bakery, covered in flour, having just compared myself unfavorably to a lighthouse, and I am telling you that I don't hope it never stops showing off. I'm telling you I'm staying to watch. Every morning. For as long as you'll let me. That's not hope. Hope is cheap. I'm offering the expensive thing. I'm offering the boring, daily, unglamorous—"

She kissed me.

Mid-sentence. Mid-flour-dust. Mid-word, technically — I will go to my grave not knowing whether the sentence ended in "unglamorous" or "unremarkable" or some other word beginning with U, because her mouth arrived and took the ending with it.

Her hands came up to my jaw and left two perfect white prints on either side of my face, and I felt her smiling against my mouth, laughing almost, still laughing, the laughter and the kiss braided together into one thing that I couldn't separate and didn't want to. And I understood, in the way you understand things with your body before your mind signs off on them, that this was the answer. Not yes, exactly. Better than yes. Yes performed badly, joyfully, in a room that smelled of rye and salt, by a woman who had spent her whole life choosing careful words and had just chosen none at all.

When she pulled back, we were both breathing hard, and there was flour on her mouth now too, transferred, evidence, and I wanted to tell her but I also never wanted to speak again in case speaking broke whatever spell had made this possible.

"So," I managed.

"So," she said.

"That was — I had a conclusion. To the speech."

"I know. I heard enough of the middle to reconstruct it." She was still close, her forehead nearly touching mine. "For the record, the arithmetic works fine. It always worked fine. We were both just bad at math."

"I'm bad at math," I agreed. "I'm apparently bad at everything. I'm bad at confessions. I'm bad at folding rye. I'm—"

"You're bad at being quiet," she said, "which, historically, has been my favorite flaw of yours," and kissed me again, shorter this time, a punctuation mark rather than a sentence, and then stepped back and looked at me — really looked, head tilted, taking in the full catastrophe of my appearance — and started laughing all over again.

"What?"

"You have a handprint on your face. Two handprints. You look like you've been arrested by ghosts."

"I feel like it."

She picked up a towel, wet it at the sink, and came back and cleaned my face herself, with the same unhurried attention she gave dough, and I stood there and let her, and thought: *this. This is the thing the memo couldn't price. This exact motion. Her hands. The ordinary miracle of being tended to.*

"Marisol planned this," I said.

"Obviously."

"She forgot she had to be somewhere that isn't here."

"She's a criminal mastermind," Beatrix said. "She's been leaving us alone together for two weeks. Yesterday she sent me to the walk-in for 'more vanilla' when we both know the vanilla shelf is fully stocked. I checked. I checked the vanilla, Elias. That's where I am emotionally."

I laughed, and the laugh turned into something else, something bigger, sitting in my chest like a second heart. "So this is real. You and me. This is — we're doing this?"

"We're doing this." She said it simply, the way she said everything that mattered. No flourish. No scaffolding. "Badly, probably. We'll do it badly for a while. I'll hide things. You'll over-plan. We'll fight about whose fault the twenties were."

"Yours," I said.

"Mostly yours," she said, "but we can litigate later," and she smiled, and outside the big front window the late light was coming off the water in long copper bars, and the dough sat under its cloth, rising in the dark the way things do when you finally leave them alone.

* * *

We closed up together, which felt like a rehearsal for a life neither of us named out loud. She showed me where the key lived — a hook behind the flour bins, painted over so many times it took her three tugs to free it — and I memorized it like scripture. Outside, the air had that iron-and-sea smell it gets before weather, and the lighthouse stood up the hill with its lamp just flickering on, right on schedule, keeping its promise to nobody in particular.

"Walk me home?" she said. "Or — walk me halfway. I go the rest alone. Tradition."

"Tradition," I said, and we walked, our shoulders bumping, and at the fork where the lane split toward the lighthouse road she stopped, looked up at the light sweeping overhead, and said, "Ask me tomorrow."

"Ask you what?"

"Whatever the conclusion of the speech was." Her eyes were bright and scared and certain, all three at once. "Tomorrow. Out loud. Somewhere public. I've spent ten years loving you in private, Elias. I'd like to try it the other way."

She kissed my cheek — leaving, I have no doubt, a third flour print, which I wore the entire walk back to the inn like a medal — and went up the hill toward the light.

I stood at the fork a long time, planning tomorrow's speech, knowing perfectly well I'd abandon the plan the moment I saw her, and hoping — no. Not hoping. Staying.

Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow, in front of everyone, I'd ask her badly, and she'd say yes worse, and it would be the best thing either of us ever did.

I just didn't know yet that the whole town would be watching — or that Marisol had already told them to be.
