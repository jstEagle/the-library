## 22. Rosalind and the Kitchen

The ovens wake before I do.

I learned that the first month, when I still thought I could outrun the schedule — that a kitchen has its own tide, its own dawn, and it does not wait for the woman who owns it to finish grieving or finish sleeping or finish standing in the pantry doorway with her hand pressed flat against the new drywall, listening for something she knows is not there. The ovens simply heat. The dough rises on its own stubborn clock. The coffee — Rosalind's blend, dark as harbor water, the recipe she surrendered to me the way a queen surrenders a border town, with a signed card and a look that dared me to ruin it — the coffee begins to smell like home at 5:40 exactly, and by 5:41 I am no longer a woman with a past. I am a woman with a morning.

I come down the stairs from the apartment above the café at a quarter to six, barefoot on the cold boards, and I do the thing that would have been unthinkable two years ago: I open the front door before I'm ready. Before the counters are wiped. Before my hair is pinned and my face arranged into the pleasant, impenetrable mask I wore for three years like a dive helmet. I open the door onto the dark pier and the gray water beyond it and the first gulls arguing over the tide line, and I let the whole cold salt morning in, unmeasured, unmanaged, and I stand in the doorway and take it.

This is what Callum taught me, though he'd laugh to hear it put that way, because he never taught anything so directly. He just lived his last year at full volume — the bad days and the luminous ones, the hospital corridors and the flour fights, the night we sat on the beach with a thermos and he said, *Li, stop counting the steps. Just walk on the sand.* He lived like a man spending money he knew was borrowed, and he spent it on all of us, and the interest has been paying out in this town ever since.

The hatchlings from that last nest — his nest, we all called it, though the mother loggerhead would object to the credit — the hatchlings went to sea in June, a year ago this week. Some of them are out there now, past the sandbar, past the shipping lane, riding the gyre in the dark water, the size of a silver dollar and swimming like they were born knowing something we spend our whole lives trying to learn. I think about them more than is professionally necessary. I think about how they don't hesitate at the waterline. How the ocean is enormous and full of teeth and they go anyway, flippers churning, because going is the only thing they know how to do with a body built for it.

I used to think I was built for retreat. It took a dying man and a small town and one unsent letter pulled out of a wall to teach me otherwise.

* * *

At six-fifteen the first regulars come, and I know their orders the way I know tide tables.

Marta from the bait shop, black coffee, no sugar, sits at the counter and reads the fishing reports like scripture. The Delgado brothers, who renovated the kitchen with me and still can't look at the new pantry wall without grinning like they buried treasure there, which, in a way, they did. Old Whit with his oatmeal and his opinions about the county council. And in the corner table by the window — the one with the view of the whole room and the whole pier, the seat of a woman who has earned the right to watch — Rosalind.

She comes at seven now, not six. Retired is a word she wears like a coat that almost fits. She still corrects my espresso pulls with a raised eyebrow, still reorganizes the pastry case when she thinks I'm not looking, still tells the story of the tin box to any tourist who'll listen, embellishing a little more each season. Last month the box was bricked up by her grandfather during the war. By August it will have survived a hurricane. I let her have it. The story belongs to her more than to me, though the letter in it had my handwriting, my grief, my name folded up small behind a pantry wall for eleven years while I built a life out of not-sending.

She waves me over this morning, which she does not usually do, and I wipe my hands and cross the floor — my floor, our floor, the boards Callum sanded on his good days and sat on on his bad ones, telling the Delgados lies about his surfing years — and I sit across from her in the light coming up gold off the water.

"You're doing it again," she says.

"Doing what?"

"The doorway thing." She wraps her hands around her cup. Her knuckles are knotted as driftwood, and I have come to love them the way you love a coastline, for what the years have carved. "Standing in the open door every morning, letting the wind in. Your predecessor — that is, me — I never did that. I kept that door shut against the draft for forty years and look what it got me. A wall full of unsaid things and a heart like a closed jar."

"You got a café," I say. "You got all of us."

"I got a café," she agrees, "because I finally opened the box. Forty years late." She looks out at the water, and for a moment she is not the sharp-eyed schemer who maneuvered two stubborn people into a gutted kitchen with a sawdust floor and one letter between them. She is a girl who lost someone to a word she never gave. "I wanted to tell you something before the season starts. Before it gets busy and there's no room for it."

I wait. In this town you learn to wait; the tide will tell you everything if you stop talking.

"The wall," she says. "The new one. I know what's in it."

I keep my face still, but my heart does the thing it does — the lurch, the old seawall trembling. Behind that drywall, sealed with the others, are three letters. Mine, the first one, the angry and aching one, eleven years late and read aloud in a gutted kitchen by a man whose voice shook on every line. His — the one he wrote in the last weeks, in handwriting that was already going soft at the edges, and asked Rosalind to brick in for whoever renovates next, and for me, someday. And mine again — the second one, the list, the one I wrote at two in the morning with flour still on my hands: *what I want if I were brave.* Callum. The café. A life not measured in retreats. I hid it in the wall the way you plant a seed in a season you're not sure you'll survive to see, and every item on that list has come true, which is either a miracle or a lesson or both.

"You don't have to tell me what they say," Rosalind says. "His especially. A letter bricked into a wall is a letter to the future, and the future isn't me." She turns her cup a quarter turn, the way she does when she's choosing her words like fruit at market. "But I'll tell you why I'm telling you this. Because someday — not soon, I hope, but someday — you're going to renovate this place again. Or your someone will. And they'll open that wall, and they'll find three letters, and they'll wonder. And I want you to have already decided what you'll do."

"What would I do?"

"You'll know," she says. "That's the thing nobody tells you about becoming the woman who keeps the kitchen. The day comes when you're not the one the letters were written about. You're the one who keeps them. And keeping them is its own kind of answer." She pats my hand, once, brisk, and the moment closes like a door that will open again later, in its own time. "Now. The sourdough starter. Have you been feeding it or has it been feeding on your neglect? Be honest. I can smell a starving starter from the pier."

* * *

The season starts the way seasons do here — all at once, like a door blown open.

By eight the café is full and loud and warm, and I move through it in the muscle-memory dance of the counter: pour, call, laugh, slide the plates, catch the story Marta is telling about the turtle that stole her bait, and answer it with the one about the hatchling I found stuck in a ghost crab hole two summers ago, the one I almost didn't help, the one whose rescue I now tell as the beginning of everything. The regulars know the story. They let me tell it anyway. That's what belonging is — a room full of people who let you tell the story again.

At nine I hang the CLOSED FOR SURVEY sign in the window — the sign Callum painted, crooked letters and a lopsided sea turtle, because I'd said we needed one and he'd said he was the man for the job and nobody had the heart to argue — and I take my kit down to the north beach.

The survey is mine still, three years running, five years total in Pelican Sound. The university offered me the grant I'd once been robbed of, the one with my name on it that another scientist stole and another man I loved stayed silent about. It took me a long time to accept it. Longer to stop flinching when people said my name and *research* in the same sentence. But the data is good and the work is honest and every morning I walk the tide line with my clipboard and my flagging tape and I count what the sea has given and taken, and it is enough. It is more than enough. It is mine, earned twice.

This morning the beach is writing its usual news in tracks: ghost crabs, a gull kill, and there — the wide, deliberate drag of a mother coming ashore to scout, though it's late in the season for it. I follow the track up past the wrack line and find the body pit, already abandoned, and beside it, half-buried in blown sand, a single egg the mother left behind. Unviable — I can tell by the weight when I lift it, the way the shell gives — but I carry it back to the pit anyway and tuck it in with the others, because that's what you do. You finish what the sea leaves unfinished. You send it home.

I stand there a while with my hand in the sand.

Callum's grave is on the bluff above the north beach, under the live oak, facing the water. The town gave him the plot the same week he died, before anyone asked anyone's permission, the way this town does its kindest things. I visit on Sundays, mostly, and talk to him about the café and the turtles and Rosalind's escalating embellishments, and sometimes I get angry at him all over again for hiding the diagnosis, for the lie of protection, and I tell him that too, because he always said he loved my fury, said it was the sound of a woman who hadn't given up. The grief doesn't get smaller. It just gets a shape I can carry. It walks beside me on surveys and sits in the corner booth and rides in the truck next to the turtle kit, and it is not the enemy. I learned that from the hatchlings, from the ones that don't make it. The ones that don't make it are not a reason to stop sending the ones that do.

* * *

In the afternoon I do the thing I do once a season, and I have never told anyone, though I suspect Rosalind knows, because Rosalind knows everything that happens within a mile of her kitchen.

I take down the panel behind the pantry.

Not the whole wall. Just the access panel the Delgados built in — discreet, painted to match, because Callum made them promise, the week before the collapse, that the letters would never be truly unreachable. *In case she needs to read them,* he'd said, and the Delgados, who adored him, had built it without being asked twice. I take down the panel and I sit on the kitchen floor with the three envelopes in my lap and I read.

Mine first. The angry one. It still stings, reading the woman I was at twenty-six, all that grief with nowhere to go, sharpened into accusations that were half true and wholly unfair. *You watched him take my life's work and you said nothing.* I don't regret a word of it. That's the strange grace of it. That letter was the truest thing I could say at the time, and it sat in a wall for eleven years waiting for me to become a woman who could hear the answer.

His second. I read it slower, the way you eat the last of something. It's short. His hands were bad by then. *Li — if you're reading this, then the wall opened, which means either the café changed hands or you got brave, and I'm betting on brave, because I've seen you at the waterline and you always, always go in. Don't count steps. Don't ration your heart. The ocean is enormous and full of teeth and I went anyway and it was worth every single day. Spend it all. — C.* There's a grease spot in one corner from a morning he read it over breakfast and laughed at his own handwriting, and I press my thumb there every time, and every time it's like touching him.

And then the third. My own. *What I want if I were brave.* I read the list and check it the way you check a nest count, item by item, and I have started, this season, doing something new at the bottom of the page. In pencil, in my own hand, I add lines. *A full house on Sundays.* *Rosalind's laugh from the corner table.* *The grant renewed.* *A garden out back, herbs, the ones he liked.* *Someone to teach the survey to.* And at the bottom, in the newest ink, still fresh enough to smudge: *A table that never gets smaller, no matter who it holds.*

I put the letters back. I fit the panel into its groove. The wall closes over them like water over a diver, and I stand up and dust off my knees and I am not sad. That's the part I could never have imagined, back when I thought safety was a thing you could build out of routine and distance. I am not sad. I am full. The letters are not ghosts in the wall. They are seeds.

* * *

Evening comes in gold, the way it does in the season's last weeks, and the café empties by degrees until it's just me and Rosalind and the smell of tomorrow's bread already starting in the proofing cabinet.

She's dozing in the corner booth, her crossword finished, her tea cold, and I don't wake her. I wipe down the counters. I stack the chairs. I stand at the window and watch the light go long and amber across the pier, across the water where the year's last hatchling nest is due to emerge any night now — the volunteers have flagged it, and I've been walking out at dawn for a week, waiting, the way you wait for anything in this town: patiently, together, with coffee.

And that's when I hear the truck.

It comes down the pier road slow, headlights off in the last light, and it stops at the end where the road meets the boards, and I know the truck — everyone in Pelican Sound knows every truck — but I don't know why my heart has gone strange and loud, why my hands have gone still on the counter, why Rosalind has opened one eye in the corner booth like a woman who has been expecting this since the season started.

The door of the truck opens. Someone steps out, backlit by the last of the sun, and stands there at the head of the pier looking at the café — at my kitchen, my lit windows, my name stenciled on the glass next to hers — and does not move.

I untie my apron. I leave it on the counter.

I go to the front door, and I open it, all the way, and let the whole warm evening in.
