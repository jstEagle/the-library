## 10. Come Home When You're Ready

The ring came off in the kitchen, over the sink, where Ilsa could wash her hands afterward as if that would absolve her of anything. It didn't. The gold band sat in her palm, warm from her finger, and Nadia stood at the table with her coat already on—she had known, Ilsa realized, before Ilsa said a word. Nadia had probably known for weeks.

"Say it plainly," Nadia said. "I'd rather have the whole wound than a polite half of one."

Ilsa set the ring on the counter between them, on the tile, where it made a small bright sound. "I love you," she said. "But I think I love not being alone. I think I've been holding your hand to keep from drowning, and that's not—you deserve to be someone's air, Nadia. Not their life raft."

Nadia was quiet for a long moment. Outside the window, the gulls argued over something on the breakwater, and the light came in the way it always did in Greywater Bay in late spring—thin and silver, like the sea was thinking about warming up but hadn't committed.

"You could have let me find out at the altar," Nadia said. "Most people would have. It would have been easier for you."

"It would have been easier for me to do a lot of things," Ilsa said. "I've had practice choosing the easier thing. Eleven years of it. I'm not doing it anymore."

Nadia picked up the ring, turned it once in her fingers, and set it back down. She didn't cry. She was too decent for drama and too honest for pretense, and Ilsa thought, not for the first time, that she was losing someone extraordinary, and that knowing it made it no less necessary.

"August Ravensworth," Nadia said. Not a question. Just the name, set down on the counter next to the ring.

"She walked away from me when we were twenty-four because she decided I'd be better off without her," Ilsa said. "She never asked. She never gave me the chance to argue. And I have spent eleven years being angry about that and eleven years waiting for her anyway, and I can't marry you with both of those things still true. It wouldn't be fair to you. It might be the cruelest thing I could do—be kind to you every day for the rest of your life while half of me is in a practice room across town."

Nadia nodded slowly. She picked up her bag. At the door she stopped, and Ilsa braced for the anger she deserved, but it didn't come.

"When my mother was dying," Nadia said, "my aunts decided things for her. What treatment, what to tell her, when to tell her. They thought they were protecting her. She told me once it was the loneliest year of her life—not the illness. The decisions made around her." She looked at Ilsa steadily. "Don't let anyone decide things around you again. Including her. Including yourself."

Then she was gone, and the door closed with a sound like a period at the end of a very long sentence, and Ilsa stood in her kitchen with her bare left hand and the gulls still screaming, and she felt—God help her—light. Guilty about feeling light. Light anyway.

* * *

Word traveled through Greywater Bay the way word always did, at the speed of gossip and roughly twice the accuracy. By the end of the week, Mrs. Halloran had brought a casserole to the shop "for the poor doctor," and old Tom Vess had come in to buy strings he didn't need and stood at the counter working up to saying something kind, and Ilsa had thanked him before he managed it, which saved them both.

Nadia, to her lasting credit, did not leave town, did not make Ilsa a villain at the clinic or the co-op. She was seen once, walking the seawall with a friend, laughing at something, and Ilsa watched her from the shop window and felt the specific ache of having done the right thing to someone who deserved better than the right thing done late.

"You gave her the grace nobody gave you," August said. She was at the workbench, fitting a new bridge to the old Klotz violin, her hands steady in the way Ilsa had learned meant she was concentrating and the way Ilsa had learned, more recently, meant she was feeling everything and refusing to let it reach her fingers.

"She deserved better than grace," Ilsa said. "She deserved the truth at twenty-six instead of thirty-eight."

August set down the knife. "You could have had the truth at twenty-four," she said quietly. "I could have given it to you. That's the thing I keep—" She stopped. Started again. "I keep thinking I stole eleven years from you. But you were here. You built a life. You loved someone. I didn't steal those years from you, Ilsa. I just wasn't in them."

"No," Ilsa said. "You weren't." She came around the workbench and stood beside her, close enough that she could see the sawdust in August's hair, the small scar on her knuckle from the night she'd slipped with the purfling cutter three weeks ago. "But the waiting was mine. I chose that. Nobody made me keep the shop, keep the town, keep the postcard in a drawer like a reliquary. I did that. So we're even, maybe. We were both cowards in different directions."

"We were both twenty-four," August said.

"We were both twenty-four," Ilsa agreed.

And then, because eleven years was long enough and life was short and the light through the shop window was doing something golden to August's face, Ilsa reached out and took her hand—the one with the scar—and August let her, and then turned her palm up and laced their fingers together like it was the most natural motion in the world, like her hand had been waiting eleven years in the same position.

"I'm not going to decide things around you," Ilsa said. "That's a promise. Whatever happens next, we decide it together or not at all."

August's breath went out of her slowly. "That's the most terrifying thing anyone's ever said to me."

"Good," Ilsa said. "Terror's the honest one. I learned that recently."

* * *

The sign went up on a Tuesday.

August had made it herself, in the back room, from a plank of the old shop counter that Ilsa's father had replaced in the nineties and never thrown away—because he never threw anything away, because the shop was less a business than a body, everything in it still living. She'd sanded it for two days until the grain came up golden, painted the letters in her mother's careful hand, the hand Ilsa recognized from a hundred concert programs.

MERROW & RAVENSWORTH
REPAIRS AND LESSONS

She stood on the step stool with the hammer, and Ilsa stood on the sidewalk holding the level, and the whole operation took twenty minutes because August kept stopping to check the alignment from different angles, and Ilsa kept telling her it was fine, and August kept saying "It's crooked, I can feel it's crooked," and Ilsa finally said, "August, if you don't nail that in the next ten seconds I'm going to kiss you in the middle of Main Street," and August nailed it in the next four.

Mrs. Halloran walked by and looked at the sign, and looked at the two of them, and said, "Well. Finally," and kept walking, and that was the whole ceremony.

The first lesson was booked before the paint was dry: the Delgado girl, nine years old, who had been renting a half-size cello from the shop for a year and whose bow arm, Ilsa had noticed, had real fire in it. August took her into the back room and put the small instrument into her hands and said, "First lesson, we don't play anything. First lesson, we learn how to listen to the room." And the girl had nodded solemnly, as if this were the wisest thing anyone had ever told her, and Ilsa had turned away to the counter and cried a little into an invoice for rosin.

* * *

The practice room had been locked for eleven years.

August opened it the week after the sign went up—really opened it, not just the door but the room itself, windows washed, the dust sheets taken off the piano, her mother's sheet music sorted and shelved in the cabinet Ilsa helped her carry up the stairs. The concerto score sat on the piano's music desk, open to the last page, the one August had never played for anyone. In the margin, in her mother's handwriting: *Let it ring.*

August sat down on the piano bench one evening with the old postcard in her hand. Eleven years old. *Come home when you're ready. I'll wait.* The ink had faded to the color of weak tea. She had read it so many times the creases were going soft.

She found a pen. She thought for a long time.

Then she turned the postcard over, to the blank side, and wrote:

*I'm ready. I was ready the day I left and too much of a coward to know it. Come to the shop tomorrow. I'll be the one pretending to work. — A.*

She addressed it to the shop—four blocks away, an address she could walk to in six minutes—and the next morning, on her way to open the store, she dropped it in the blue mailbox on Harbor Street, and felt ridiculous, and did not take it back out.

* * *

Ilsa was sorting a delivery of strings when the mail came.

She heard the slot, heard the fall of envelopes, and kept sorting, because the mail was the mail and most of it was invoices. But something in her—the part of her that had waited eleven years, the part that had kept a drawer like a reliquary—made her stop, and go to the door, and pick up the small rectangle of cardstock lying on the mat.

She turned it over. The handwriting was August's, and it was new ink, and it was eleven years late, and it had come the very next morning, the way mail does when the distance is nothing at all.

Ilsa read it standing in the doorway with the morning coming in behind her, the gulls, the smell of the sea, the sign above her head with both their names on it. She read it twice. Then she laughed—a wet, broken, delighted sound—and put the postcard in her apron pocket, over her heart, where it belonged.

She locked the shop door behind her. Four blocks. Six minutes, if she didn't run.

She ran.
