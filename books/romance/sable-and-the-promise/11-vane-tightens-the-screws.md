## 11. Vane Tightens the Screws

The envelope arrived the way bad news always did at Hartwell Vineyard — hand-delivered, cream-colored, and smelling faintly of money.

Jonah found it wedged in the barn office door when he came in from the morning rounds, his boots still crusted with clay from the east field. No stamp. No postmark. Just *Jonathan Hartwell* typed in a font that probably cost more per letter than he'd made last week selling eggs at the farmers market.

He knew before he opened it. You learned to know, after a while. Bad news had a weight to it, a density that ordinary mail didn't carry.

Inside: a single sheet on heavy stock, and beneath it, a second document that made his stomach drop through the floor and keep falling.

*Mr. Hartwell —*

*Given our recent conversations, I've taken the liberty of revising my offer. $1.4 million for the vineyard, fixtures, and inventory. This represents my final position. I'll be in the area Thursday and can have funds wired within seventy-two hours of signature.*

*Time is a asset neither of us can afford to waste.*

*Regards,*
*Marcus Vane*
*Vane Development Group*

The typo — *a asset* — was almost comforting. It meant a human being had typed it, which meant Vane could be rushed, and men who could be rushed could be beaten. Jonah clung to that thought for about four seconds before he looked at the second page and stopped clinging to anything at all.

It was a notice of assignment. Official county language, notarized, stamped. His eyes snagged on the middle paragraph and read it three times because the first two times his brain refused to process it.

*...the holder of record for the mortgage note secured by the residential property located at 4420 County Road 12, parcel 118-B, currently occupied by one Sable Reyes, has assigned said note to Vane Development Group, LLC, effective as of—*

He sat down on the stool by the workbench. Then he stood up, because sitting felt too much like surrender, then sat down again because standing made the room tilt.

Vane owned Sable's mortgage.

Not her bank. Not some faceless fund in Delaware. *Him.* The man circling Hartwell Vineyard like a hawk that had learned patience had quietly bought up the paper on the house next door — the house Sable had moved into in March, the house she was slowly, box by box, turning into something lived-in. Something loved.

Jonah read the notice again. The date of assignment was three weeks old. Three weeks. She'd been living under Vane's ownership since before the harvest, before the rain on the tin roof, before—

Before everything.

And she hadn't said a word.

He set the papers down carefully, the way you set down something that might go off, and walked out into the gray morning light. The rows stretched away toward the fence line, toward her property, toward the thin strip of trees where the old oak stood. Somewhere past that line, Sable was probably awake. Probably drinking coffee out of the chipped blue mug she liked, probably cursing at a box she couldn't find the tape gun for.

Probably not knowing that the ground under her feet had just changed hands.

Or knowing. That was the other possibility, and it sat in his chest like a stone.

* * *

He told himself he wasn't going to think about it. He told himself that all the way through the morning — while he patched the drip line in row fourteen, while he argued with the parts supplier on the phone about backordered emitters, while he ate half a sandwich standing over the sink at noon without tasting any of it.

Then he gave up telling himself things and drove into town.

The bank was the same bank that held most of his debt, which meant the same bank that sent him letters in red envelopes, which meant walking through its doors always felt a little like walking into a creditor's dream. The teller — Dana, mid-fifties, kind eyes that had seen every flavor of desperation this county produced — looked up as he approached and her smile tightened by a millimeter.

"Mr. Hartwell."

"Dana." He slid the assignment notice across the counter. "Can you tell me if this is real?"

She read it. He watched her face do the thing faces did when they were deciding how much they were allowed to say. "It's a public record," she said finally. "Assignments get filed at the courthouse. Anyone can look them up."

"I'm asking if it's true."

"It's filed." She lowered her voice. "Jonah, I can't discuss another customer's loan. You know that."

"Her mortgage was with First Cascade. Why would First Cascade sell to a developer? Banks sell notes to funds, to servicers. What does a developer want with a single residential note on forty acres outside town?"

Dana's silence was an answer in itself.

"He's not going to collect it," Jonah said. The thought arrived whole, fully formed, ugly. "He's going to call it. Foreclose. Is that right? If she misses a payment — or if he decides she's missed one —"

"I really can't."

"Is that *right*, Dana?"

She glanced toward the manager's office, then back at him, and something human won out over something institutional. "A new holder reviews the payment history of any note it acquires. If there's anything irregular — anything at all — they're entitled to act on it." She pushed the notice back across the counter. "I'd tell your friend to talk to an attorney. Quickly."

*Friend.* The word landed strangely. Six months ago Sable Reyes had been a stranger hauling boxes at dawn. Now she was the person whose laugh he heard in his head when the rows got quiet, the person whose coffee mug he washed without being asked, the person whose door he'd stood at last Tuesday for no reason he could defend except that he wanted to see her face.

And now she was a liability on a piece of paper Vane was holding like a knife.

He thanked Dana and walked out into the parking lot, where his truck sat baking in the October sun, and stood there with his keys in his fist and understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what Vane had done.

It wasn't two offers. It was one offer with a second blade hidden inside it. Buy the vineyard or watch your neighbor lose her house — and know, the whole time, that her losing it would be your fault, because you could have stopped it. Because you could have signed in September and been free, and instead you'd spent the autumn falling for the woman next door while a man in a good suit bought the ground out from under her.

*Pressure applied from both sides of the fence.* That was the phrase that came to him, and he hated how precisely it fit.

* * *

Thursday came faster than Thursdays should.

Vane arrived at eleven in a charcoal car that cost more than the harvest was going to gross, wearing a sweater that probably had a name Jonah couldn't pronounce. He didn't come to the house. He came to the barn, uninvited, and stood in the doorway of the office looking around at the pinned maps and the yellow legal pads and the twenty-year-old space heater with the expression of a man appraising a garage sale.

"Hartwell." He smiled the way sharks are said to smile, which is to say he showed teeth and nothing behind them moved. "You got my letter."

"I got it." Jonah stayed behind the desk. It was a small advantage and he intended to use it. "I also got the second page. Interesting reading."

"You flatter me. It's boilerplate."

"It's a foreclosure waiting to happen." Jonah kept his voice level. "You bought Sable Reyes's mortgage three weeks ago. A single residential note. From a bank that doesn't sell single notes to developers." He leaned forward. "So let's skip the part where we pretend this is a coincidence."

Vane's smile didn't falter, but something behind it recalibrated. "You've been doing homework."

"I've been doing arithmetic. There's a difference, but people confuse them around you a lot, I'd guess."

For a moment the polished surface cracked — just a hairline, just enough to show the machinery underneath. Then Vane stepped fully into the office, pulled the visitor's chair out without being offered it, and sat down like he owned the chair, the desk, the barn, and the dirt it stood on.

"Here's what I admire about you, Hartwell," he said. "Stubbornness. Real, old-fashioned, self-destructive stubbornness. Your father had it too. Cost him the '08 season, if I remember — though I suppose you remember better than I do."

"Don't talk about my father."

"I'm making a point about genetics." Vane folded his hands. "The offer is now one-sixty. And I want to be transparent with you, because I respect transparency: the Reyes note is in my portfolio. Her payment history has certain irregularities. Two late payments before acquisition. If I were inclined to enforce strictly — and enforcement is entirely my discretion now — the timeline could compress considerably. Foreclosure in this county runs ninety days if nobody contests. Faster if the borrower doesn't have counsel."

"She hasn't missed a payment since she moved in."

"No. She hasn't." Vane let that hang there, generous as a noose. "Which shows character. But character isn't collateral, and winter is coming, and wineries fail in clusters, Hartwell. When yours goes, the neighbors get nervous. Nervous borrowers miss payments. It's practically seasonal."

Jonah's hands were flat on the desk. He noticed distantly that they'd gone white at the knuckles. "What do you want."

"What I've always wanted. Signature, wire, done. You walk away with one-point-six million and zero debt, and Ms. Reyes keeps her house, because a man who owns both properties has no reason to squeeze either one." Vane spread his palms, a picture of reasonableness. "Everybody sleeps well. The valley gets a resort it can be proud of. You get a life."

"A life." Jonah laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You mean an exit."

"I mean an exit from a burning building. Yes." Vane stood, buttoned his jacket, and paused at the door on his way out. "I need an answer by Monday. And Hartwell — I'd think about what happens to her if you don't sign. Not because I'd enjoy it. Because you would."

He left. The space heater ticked in the silence. Outside, through the barn's open doors, the vineyard went on being beautiful in the way it always did in late October — gold light, long shadows, the rows running toward the mountains like lines on a page someone had started writing and never finished.

Jonah put his head in his hands.

* * *

That night he didn't drink. That surprised him. There was a bottle of the '19 in the pantry — the last decent vintage, the one he'd been rationing like a man rationing memories — and every instinct in his body said pour a glass, maybe the bottle, soften the edges of a day like this.

Instead he made coffee at nine p.m., which his grandfather would have called a cry for help, and carried it out to the porch, and sat in the dark looking at the fence line.

Her kitchen light was on. He could see it through the trees, a warm rectangle floating in the black. She was up late, then. Packing or unpacking; with Sable it was always one or the other, as if she couldn't quite commit to staying anywhere, as if some part of her kept a bag by the door.

He thought about calling her. Telling her. *Vane owns your mortgage. He's going to use it against me. Against us.* The words arranged themselves easily enough in his head.

But every version of the conversation ended the same way: with her asking the question he couldn't answer. *Why do you care this much, Jonah? We've known each other six months. Why would you sell your family's land — the land you've bled on for ten years — for my house?*

Because he didn't have an answer that wasn't enormous. Because the honest answer involved words like *always* and *home* and *you*, and those words required a foundation he hadn't poured yet, and building foundations took time, and time was the one thing Vane had just informed them both they didn't have.

There was also the other thing. The small cold splinter he'd been carrying since the bank, since before the bank, if he was honest — the way she'd gone still that night in the barn when he said she felt familiar. The stack of envelopes in her kitchen he'd glimpsed and she'd angled away from. The handwriting on the address labels that had nagged at him for weeks like a word on the tip of the tongue.

No. He shut that drawer. Tonight was not the night for that drawer.

He finished the coffee and went inside and sat down at the kitchen table with Vane's letter and the contract draft and a pen, and he told himself he was only reviewing terms. Reading the fine print. Being responsible.

The pen hovered over the signature line for a long time.

One-point-six million. Debt gone. The weight off his shoulders that he'd carried so long he'd forgotten it was weight and not just his skeleton. He could start over somewhere. He could sleep through nights. He could stop doing math at three a.m. about emitter costs and tank repairs and whether love of a thing was worth anything to a bank.

And Sable would keep her house. That was the seduction of it, the genius of it — Vane had built the trap so that saving her and saving himself were the same motion. Sign here and be the hero. Refuse and be the reason she loses everything.

Except.

Except that Vane didn't release notes out of gratitude. The moment Jonah signed, the moment the vineyard was Vane's, Sable's mortgage became just another asset in a portfolio — and assets got managed. Squeezed. Flipped. The promise of mercy from a man like Marcus Vane was a promissory note drawn on a bank that had never existed. Jonah would be gone, powerless, a hundred miles away with his money and his clean slate, and she'd be alone on that road with a landlord who'd already shown her exactly what he was.

Helping her meant sinking with her. That was the truth of it, stripped of romance. Either he sold and abandoned her to a softer-looking version of the same ruin, or he refused and they went down together — his vineyard, her house, both of them, into whatever Vane decided to do with people who inconvenienced him.

Those were the options. Nobody was coming. There was no third page to the letter.

Jonah looked at the signature line a long time. Then he looked up, through the window, at the warm rectangle of her kitchen light still burning in the dark.

He thought about her laughing at his worst joke in row nine, flour on her hands, saying *that's terrible* in a voice that meant *tell me another*. He thought about the rain hammering the tin roof and her hair dripping and the way she'd kissed him like she was apologizing for something. He thought about the oak tree he hadn't visited in years and couldn't explain why he avoided.

He didn't know why he was choosing what he was choosing. That was the honest truth of it, sitting alone at his father's table at midnight. He couldn't have defended it to a banker or a lawyer or Marcus Vane. All he knew was that the idea of her standing in that kitchen watching a foreclosure notice arrive — alone, because he'd traded her for his own exit — was unbearable in a way that had nothing to do with logic and everything to do with the marrow of him.

Some choices you make with your head. Some with your heart. This one he made with whatever part of a man decides, without evidence, that a person is worth the fall.

He picked up the pen.

And tore Vane's letter in half. Then in half again. Then he fed the pieces to the wood stove and watched them curl and go to ash, and wrote on the back of the contract draft, in block letters Vane couldn't misread:

*No. Stop sending offers. Stop buying my neighbors' debts. The answer is no until you're out of this valley.*

He'd deliver it Monday. Or sooner.

He was sealing the envelope when headlights swept across the window — slow, unfamiliar, stopping. Through the glass he saw a car he didn't recognize pull onto Sable's drive, and a figure get out, unsteady, catching itself on the car door.

A man. Standing in her yard at midnight, swaying, looking up at her kitchen window.

Jonah was out the door before he'd decided to move.
