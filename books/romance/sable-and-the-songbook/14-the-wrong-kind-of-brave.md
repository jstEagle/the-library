## 14. The Wrong Kind of Brave

The demolition schedule arrived by email at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, subject line: **FINAL — NO FURTHER EDITS.** Marguerite read it standing at the window of her rented room above the hardware store, watching the first light come up over Main Street the way it did every day, indifferent and golden, gilding the awning of Bellwether Books like something worth keeping.

Her father had attached the permits. All of them. Signed, stamped, filed. The Voss Group's legal team had moved through the town's bureaucracy the way water moves through paper—slowly at first, then all at once, and then there was nothing left to save. Phase One: the eastern block, the diner, the old pharmacy, the building that had been a hat shop for sixty years and was now a storage unit for someone's grief. Phase Two: the western block, the theater, the bakery. Phase Three—

Phase Three was Bellwether Books. November 3rd. The week after the Harvest Festival.

She had known this. She had signed off on this. She had, in fact, written the memo that made it possible, the one that reframed the bookshop from *heritage asset, low priority* to *structural liability, expedited review*—her own language, her own signature, her own particular gift for making erasure sound like maintenance.

But seeing it in a calendar, in bold, between *Festival vendor load-in* and *Site survey, theater parcel*—that was different. That was the difference between knowing a thing and being asked to hold it.

She sat down on the edge of the bed in her good suit and did not cry, because crying was not on the schedule either.

* * *

Her father arrived in town on Thursday, and Marguerite met him at the Hollow Creek Inn, where he'd taken the corner suite because corner suites were what Aldous Voss took, the way other men took breath.

He was smaller than she remembered, which happened every time and surprised her every time. In her memory he filled rooms; in person he merely occupied them, a neat silver-haired man in a navy blazer with the particular stillness of someone who had never once needed to raise his voice. He kissed her on both cheeks. He smelled of cedar and cold coffee.

"Margot." Only family used the name. He'd given it to her when she was six, and she had understood, even then, that it was less a nickname than a leash—something he could shorten at will. "You look tired."

"I've been working."

"I can see that." He gestured her into a chair by the window and poured coffee from the room's carafe without asking how she took it, because he had never asked, because asking implied the possibility of a no. "The town meeting went well."

"It went *terribly*," she said. "Half the room walked out. The other half stayed to shout."

"And yet the permits are filed." He sat opposite her, crossing one ankle over his knee. "Which means it went exactly as well as it needed to. Margot, I didn't come here to discuss the meeting."

The coffee was too hot in her hands. She held it anyway.

"I came because I've heard something I don't like." He said it mildly, the way he said everything, and that mildness was the most frightening thing about him. "A man named Galloway. The bookshop. I'm told you've been—visible. In his establishment. Repeatedly."

"I'm doing due diligence. The lease termination requires an inventory of—"

"Margot."

She stopped. The single word, her own name, laid across the table like a card he'd been saving.

"I have spent thirty years," he said, "building a firm that people trust with their towns. Their *main streets*, Margot. Do you know what a main street is? It's not buildings. It's a story people tell themselves about who they are. And stories are only valuable if someone owns the printing rights." He sipped his coffee. "I don't care who you drink tea with. I care about appearances. A developer consorting with the owner of the property she's terminating—before the termination is final—looks like a conflict. Conflicts invite scrutiny. Scrutiny invites *audits*." He smiled, and it reached his eyes the way frost reaches a window. "So. Keep your distance from Galloway until the paperwork clears. That's not a request."

Marguerite looked at her father—at the face she had loved her whole life, the face that had taught her chess and contracts and the difference between losing and appearing to lose—and felt something in her chest shift, a hairline crack in a load-bearing wall.

"No," she said.

The word sat in the room. Her father raised his eyebrows, genuinely interested, as though she were a specimen doing something unexpected.

"I'm sorry?" he said, though he wasn't.

"I'm delaying Phase Three," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "The bookshop. I'm moving the demolition to spring. There's a structural assessment I want done first, and the festival season is the worst possible window—contractors, traffic, the town's already—" She was talking too fast. She made herself stop. "It's a delay, Dad. Not a reversal. Two months."

"Two months." He set down his cup. "For what purpose?"

"For *decency*."

"There's no line item for decency." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse—cruelty would have meant he'd noticed, that the crack had registered. This was just arithmetic to him. "Margot, I've watched you build a career on knowing the difference between what you feel and what you do. It's the best thing about you. Don't unlearn it now over a bookshop and a sad man with a daughter."

"You know about the daughter."

"I know everything about this town. That's the job." He leaned forward, and for a moment—just a moment—the mildness dropped and she saw the machinery underneath, all of it, the whole cold engine of him. "Delay it if you like. I'll even sign off, because you'll do it my way or not at all. But understand what you're doing. You're not being brave, Margot. You're being *sentimental*, and sentiment is just debt with better marketing. Someone will collect." He stood, straightened his blazer, and was, apparently, finished. "Dinner tomorrow. Wear the grey suit. The bank people respond to you in grey."

He went into the bathroom and shut the door, and Marguerite sat alone in the corner suite of the Hollow Creek Inn with her cooling coffee and the sudden, vertiginous understanding that she had just won a battle she had already lost—that he had given her the delay the way you give a child a cookie to stop it asking for dinner, and that somewhere in a filing cabinet in the Voss Group's Hartford office, the November 3rd date still existed, patient as a knife.

* * *

She delayed it anyway. That was the thing about hairline cracks: once they started, they went where they went.

Friday morning she filed the amendment herself, at the county office, standing at a counter while a clerk with reading glasses on a chain stamped the paper and said, "Spring, huh? The Galloway girl will be pleased." Marguerite smiled and said nothing and walked out into a bright cold morning feeling, for approximately four minutes, like the kind of person who did things like this.

Then her father called.

She let it ring twice. Then, because she was forty-one years old and had never once let it ring three times, she answered.

"The structural assessment," he said, without greeting. "Interesting choice. I had the file pulled last night. You know, the original acquisition file. The one from the estate sale." A pause, measured out like an ingredient. "The Hollis estate. The composer."

Marguerite stopped walking. Main Street continued around her—Mrs. Okonkwo sweeping her bakery step, two kids arguing over a bike, the ordinary music of a town she had come, without permission, to love.

"What about it," she said.

"The songbook." Her father's voice was pleasant. Conversational. The voice of a man reading a menu. "Mother of yours. Unfinished. Sentimental value, I'm sure. And yet I notice the copyright was assigned to the Voss Group in March. Signed by an officer of the firm." Another pause, and now the pleasantness thinned, and underneath it was the thing she had spent her whole life pretending not to see. "Signed by *you*, Margot."

"I signed what I was told to sign. You put it in front of me at the quarterly—"

"You signed a document assigning the copyright of a dead woman's compositions to a development firm," he said, "as a *courtesy filing*, so that the estate sale could proceed cleanly. It was a legal fiction. It was always a legal fiction. The book belongs to you, or to her memory, or to whoever she meant it for—'my Margo, finish it for me,' I believe the inscription reads. But on paper, Margot, on paper it belongs to us. To me." She could hear him turning a page. He was reading her own signature as he spoke to her; she was certain of it. "And if a certain bookseller, or a certain town council, or a certain *journalist* ever became curious about how the Voss Group's demolition schedule came to be delayed—if anyone suggested that Marguerite Voss had gone soft, had a conflict of interest, had let personal feelings compromise a transaction—it would be my sad duty to point out that the officer in question has been trading on assets that don't legally belong to her. That she has been carrying that songbook around for two years, reading it, *showing it to people*, while the copyright sits in our name." He closed the page. "Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

She was standing in front of the bakery. Mrs. Okonkwo had gone inside. The street was empty, or it might as well have been; she couldn't have told you.

"You'd use it," Marguerite said. "Against me. Your own daughter."

"I'd use it to *protect* you," her father said. "From yourself. From the mess you're making. That's what I've always done, Margot, and you've always been grateful, and I see no reason this time should be different. Delay the demolition if you must—spring, fine, I've signed off. But the bookshop comes down. The schedule holds. And you will keep your distance from Galloway, and you will not make speeches at town meetings, and you will remember that everything you are, everything you've built, sits on paper I wrote." A breath, and then, almost gently, which was the cruelest register he had: "You're my best work, Margot. Don't make me file an amendment."

He hung up.

* * *

She walked for a long time. Past the theater with its marquee letters missing, past the diner, past the library where Ms. Okafor was taping something to the door—a flyer, bright orange, HARVEST FESTIVAL: PEN-PAL REVEAL CEREMONY, and beneath it, in cheerful marker: *Every letter finds its person eventually.*

Marguerite stood on the library steps and read that last line four times.

Every letter found its person. She had a letter in her bag right now—drafted at two in the morning, unsent, addressed to a stranger whose handwriting she knew better than her father's face. *I have to tell you who I am,* it began, and then it didn't, because telling who she was meant telling what she'd done, and what she'd done had just grown a second layer, a legal fiction with her signature on it, a copyright she'd signed away without reading, a dead woman's music filed under a development firm's name like a parcel in a warehouse.

Her mother had written *finish it for me* in a hand gone thin and careful, and Marguerite had carried the book for two years without finishing it, and now she understood why. Not grief—or not only grief. She hadn't finished it because finishing it would have meant reading it, and reading it would have meant claiming it, and claiming it would have meant admitting that the paper said it wasn't hers. Her father hadn't even needed the threat, not really. The trap had been built in March, at the quarterly meeting, with her own pen. He had simply waited for her to walk into it on her own.

And she had. She had walked into it the way she walked into everything—competently, in good shoes, trusting the hand that held the map.

She thought of Isandro Galloway's face across the counter, cold as a closed register. She thought of Juniper asking, *why does the clipboard lady look sad?* She thought of the letter she'd received last night, the anonymous one, the one that made her laugh and then, three lines later, made her sit very still on the edge of her bed: *I'm afraid of wanting things. I trained myself out of it. I'm starting to suspect that was not the same as being happy.*

She was the thing he was afraid of. She was the wanting, wearing a lanyard and carrying a demolition schedule.

Marguerite climbed the library steps, took down one of the orange flyers, folded it into her bag next to the unsent letter, and walked back up Main Street in her good shoes, past the bookshop with its lights on and its two silhouettes in the window—a man and a child, heads bent together over something—and she did not go in, and she did not look away, and she understood at last, completely, the shape of what she'd become.

Her father's power had never lived in his fists or his voice. It lived in paper. And he had taught her to write.

She was a liar twice over now: once in the letters she hadn't sent, and once in ink, dated March, filed in Hartford, bearing a name that used to mean something to her.

The festival was in nine days. The reveal ceremony was in nine days. Every letter finds its person eventually.

She only had to decide which person she'd be when hers did.
