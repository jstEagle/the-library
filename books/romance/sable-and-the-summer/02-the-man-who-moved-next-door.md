## 2. The Man Who Moved Next Door

The farmhouse at the edge of the north field had been dying for so long that Hazel had stopped seeing it the way you stop seeing a scar on your own hand. Its porch sagged like a tired shoulder. Ivy had climbed through a broken window on the second floor and come out again through the eaves, green and shameless, as though the house were being slowly swallowed by something patient. Her grandfather used to call it the Hollis place, back when Hollises still lived in it, and he used to say that one day it would fall down and take half her fence line with it.

It did not fall down. Instead, on a Tuesday in late April, a moving van backed up the gravel drive with the careful deliberation of an animal entering unfamiliar territory, and by Wednesday morning there was a man on the porch with a crowbar, prying boards off the windows like a surgeon removing old bandages.

Hazel watched him from the vine rows for the better part of an hour before she admitted to herself that she was watching. The vines needed pruning—there was always pruning—but not this row, and not this urgently, and certainly not while crouched low enough to peer between the canes like a spy in a bad novel. She was thirty-four years old and she was hiding in her own vineyard from a man carrying a crowbar. It occurred to her, distantly, that this was not the behavior of someone who had her life under control. But control had always been the thing she was best at pretending to have.

He worked all day. By late afternoon the boards were stacked, the shutters hung crooked but hung, and the man stood in the yard surveying his work with his hands on his hips, and then—this was the part that made her forget to breathe—he took a case from the porch, opened it, and lifted out a cello.

She knew instruments. Her grandfather had kept a radio in the equipment shed tuned to the classical station out of Ashford, and she had spent enough summers hauling crates within earshot of it to know a concerto from a quartet, though she could never have named what she heard. But she had never seen anyone play outdoors, alone, for no audience at all. The man set the cello between his knees the way another man might settle into a chair he'd been promised all day, drew the bow across the strings once—a long, low note that traveled across the field and through her chest like weather—and began to play.

It was not a song she knew. It didn't sound finished, either; there were places where he stopped, went back, tried again, as if he were arguing with the music about how it ought to go. But even unfinished, even quarrelsome, it filled the valley. The notes came across the fence line and settled over the vines like warmth after frost, and Hazel stood among the rows with dirt on her knees and her heart going strangely, and thought: *oh no.*

She couldn't have said what the *oh no* was about. Not yet.

* * *

He introduced himself two days later, and he did it with biscuits.

Hazel was mending the fence—the actual fence, the shared one, because a post had rotted through over winter and the wire sagged—and she heard footsteps on gravel behind her and turned to find a tall man holding a plate covered in a tea towel, standing on the far side of the gap with the expression of someone who had rehearsed this moment and was now improvising anyway.

"You must be Winterbourne," he said. "I'm Elias Marsh. I bought the Hollis place." He held out the plate. "I'm told these are called shortbread. I'm told that by the woman at the bakery in town, who also told me you buy flour from her, which is how I know you exist. I hope that's not alarming."

It should not have been alarming. It was, mildly, because Hazel had spent ten years cultivating a reputation for existing as little as possible, and here was a stranger who had traced her through flour.

"Hazel," she said, and took a biscuit because refusing would require conversation. "Winterbourne. Yes."

"The vineyard." He looked past her at the rows climbing the hillside, the new green catching the light, and something moved through his face—not hunger exactly, not greed, but a kind of recognition, the look of a man finding a room he'd been dreaming about. "My grandmother grew grapes. In Cornwall. Bad ones, she said, but she grew them." He said it lightly, but Hazel had spent a decade reading people through their letters, through the small hesitations between lines, and she heard the weight under the lightness. Everyone who came to this valley came carrying something.

"Bad grapes are still grapes," she said, before she could stop herself.

His smile arrived slowly, like dawn rather than lightning. "That's exactly what she'd have said."

They talked about the fence. He offered to help; she declined; he helped anyway, holding posts plumb while she tamped the earth, and he was better company than she wanted him to be—quiet when quiet was right, asking questions that weren't really questions about her, more like open doors he wasn't insisting she walk through. Where had she learned to set a post like that. Had she always been here. Did the frost really come down into the hollows the way the old-timers claimed. She answered in monosyllables and found herself, against every policy, adding syllables.

By the time the sun dropped behind the western ridge, the fence stood straighter than it had in years, and Elias Marsh wiped his hands on his jeans and said, almost as an afterthought, the thing that undid her.

"I should tell you I'm not entirely a stranger to the area. Or to you, I suppose, in a way." He laughed, self-conscious. "That sounds sinister. What I mean is—I write letters. To someone. For years now. Someone I've never met." He picked up his tools. "It's a strange thing to say out loud. You're the first person I've said it to."

Hazel's hands went still on the fence wire.

"There's a literary magazine," he went on, not looking at her, fiddling with the clasp of his toolbox as if the words were easier when his eyes had somewhere else to be. "In London. They run classified advertisements—people looking for correspondents, pen friends, that sort of thing. Ten years ago I put one in. Half as a joke. And one letter came back, and then another, and—" He shrugged, and the shrug contained entire seasons. "We've never exchanged names. Real ones. We use—well, she uses a name. I assume it's not hers. I use mine, or most of it. It's the strangest friendship of my life, and I've never once heard her voice." He glanced up, suddenly boyish, suddenly embarrassed. "You probably think that's mad."

The world tilted.

It was not a dramatic tilting. There was no rushing wind, no cinematic vertigo. It was smaller and worse than that: the ground simply became unreliable beneath her feet, the way ice does, the moment you hear the first crack. Because Hazel knew the advertisement. She had answered it ten years ago on a night so black she could hardly remember it now—the night after the bank's first letter, the night she'd sat at her grandfather's desk with his debts spread out around her like a diagnosis, writing to a stranger because she could not bear to write the truth to anyone who knew her face. She had signed it *Sable*, because sable was the color of the soil in the north field at dusk, and because a name that was a color could not betray anyone.

Ten years of letters. Ten years of Tuesday evenings and Sunday mornings, of telling a nameless man things she had never told Ruth, never told the bank, never told God. Ten years of a friendship built precisely on the fact that he did not know her—that no one knew her—and now here he stood on her fence line holding a toolbox, real as weather, saying *you're the first person I've said it to.*

And she said nothing.

"I don't think it's mad," she heard herself say. Her voice came out level. She was distantly impressed by her own voice. "People need somewhere to put things."

"Yes," he said, and looked at her with that slow attention again, as if she'd said something worth keeping. "Exactly that."

* * *

That night she sat at her grandfather's desk with a candle burning because the electricity in the east wing had been cut off in March and she hadn't yet admitted that to the electric company, let alone to anyone with a face. The desk drawer held the bundle—letters bound with twine, a decade thick, his handwriting marching across the pages in a loose, musical slant that she now recognized with horrible clarity as the same hand that had held the fence post steady that afternoon.

She read the last one. Dated three weeks ago, before he'd ever loaded a cello into a van:

*Sable — I've done something impulsive, which you'll say is unlike me, though I suspect you secretly enjoy my lapses. I've bought a house. A ruin, honestly, but it sits above a valley where they grow wine, and when I stood in the broken doorway I thought: here is a place where a man could be quiet without disappearing. You'll understand that distinction. You're the only one who does.*

*I may even meet a neighbor or two. If I do, I'll try to remember how to talk to people who aren't you. Wish me luck.*

*I remain, as ever, E.*

Hazel set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Luck, she thought. He'd asked for luck. And he'd gotten her instead—her fence line, her flour, her monosyllables thawing into sentences despite every defense she owned. Somewhere in London a typesetter had placed his advertisement beside hers, and some clerk of fate had routed his reply to a grieving girl at a vineyard desk, and now the whole architecture of her hidden life had walked across the valley carrying shortbread.

She could end it. That was the sane move, the controlled move: stop writing, tonight, before the distance collapsed entirely. Sign nothing more. Let *Sable* fade the way pen friends fade, gently, unremarkably, and keep Elias Marsh as what he appeared to be—a neighbor, a cellist, a man who held fence posts plumb. Two separate relationships, cleanly divided, neither contaminating the other.

Except that he was both. That was the whole unbearable truth of it. The man on the page and the man in the yard were the same man, and she had spent the afternoon liking them equally, and the letters had already begun to bleed through—when he'd said *exactly that,* she had heard the phrase before, had read it, had kept it.

Hazel blew out the candle and sat in the dark, and the farmhouse next door showed one lit window, and faintly, impossibly, she could almost believe she heard the cello again, though it was surely only wind in the wires.

One letter, she told herself. One more, and then I'll decide.

But she knew, even as she reached for the pen, that she was lying—that the decision had already been made somewhere down in her chest, in the place where loneliness made its arguments—and that she was about to do the most dangerous thing she had ever done, which was to get closer to the truth while making sure it never quite touched her.

She wrote: *Dear E — Tell me about the house.*
