## 3. Borrowed Umbrellas

The storm came in off the water like something with a grudge.

Rafael had watched it build all afternoon from behind the counter of the shop — a bruise-colored wall stacking itself over the harbor, gulls dropping low and silent before the wind changed its mind about them. By four o'clock the light had gone strange and green, the kind of light that made the spines of old books glow as if lit from within, and June had pressed her nose to the front window and announced, with the authority of an eight-year-old meteorologist, that the sky was "going to break."

It broke at ten past five, just as he flipped the sign to CLOSED.

He was locking the door when the first drop hit the awning above him like a thrown stone. Then a hundred more. Then the sky simply opened, and the street became a river running silver under the streetlamp, and Rafael stood in the dry pocket beneath the faded canvas awning his grandfather had hung forty years ago and thought, not for the first time, that the shop's roof leaked everywhere except over the one spot where people might actually stand.

That was when he saw her.

She came down Harbor Street at a half-run, coat pulled over her head, cardboard box clutched against her chest in a way that told him she valued whatever was inside more than her own dry skin. She reached the awning's edge and stopped there, hovering, caught between the deluge and the small territory of another person's shelter — and even soaked through, even breathless, she hesitated like someone who had spent years being careful about where she stood.

"It's not going to let up," Rafael said, because the silence seemed crueler than speaking.

"I know. I've been watching it chase me." She lowered the box, revealing a face he'd seen only across hedges and moving vans — the woman from Number Nine, Wren Sable, though he knew that now only because June had extracted her name through the fence last week with the ruthless efficiency of a customs officer. Her hair was plastered dark against her cheek. She pushed it back with her wrist, careful not to set down the box. "I'll just wait for a gap."

"There won't be a gap. This is the front edge. It'll rain like this until midnight."

"Of course it will." She said it with a small, tired laugh, as if the weather were one more thing that had recently conspired against her, and looked out at the drowning street rather than at him.

Rafael should have gone inside. The polite choreography of New England neighborliness offered him a clean exit — a nod, a wish of good luck, the warm dark of the shop. Instead he heard himself say, "There's room under here for both of you," and then, hearing the plural, corrected himself too late, "—for you and the box."

"The box thanks you." She stepped under the awning, keeping a careful foot of space between them, and for a moment they stood side by side watching the storm hammer the street, two strangers pretending this was ordinary.

Up close she smelled of rain and cardboard and something underneath it, faintly floral, like a garden remembered rather than grown. He resented noticing. He resented the way the space under the awning felt suddenly smaller and warmer, the way the storm outside made the dry circle around them feel like a room with the door shut. It had been a long time since Rafael had stood this close to anyone who wasn't handing him change or asking if he carried books on marine engines.

"You're the bookshop," she said.

"Sometimes I wonder."

"The one with the cat in the window? June told me. She also told me the cat is named Admiral and that he's 'mostly friendly but judgmental.'" A smile tugged at the corner of her mouth, quick and quickly controlled, like a match struck indoors. "She's quite the ambassador."

"She's quite the interrogator. You gave her your full name and address history in under four minutes. That takes talent to survive."

Wren laughed then — really laughed, a short surprised sound that seemed to escape before she could vet it — and Rafael felt the laugh land somewhere under his ribs and immediately wished it hadn't. He studied the street instead. Rain sheeted off the awning's edge in a beaded curtain, and beyond it the town was dissolving, lights smearing long and gold across the wet asphalt, and he thought: *this is what it looks like when the world goes soft.* He hadn't let himself think things like that in years. He filed the thought away the way he filed everything — deep, labeled, locked.

"So how are you finding Number Nine?" he asked, because silence had begun to feel dangerous.

"Loud. In a good way. The house creaks like a ship." She shifted the box to her other arm. "And the previous owner left about forty years of garden chaos, which I'm choosing to call character."

"Elena planted most of that," he said, before he could stop himself. "The roses along the south fence. They were her—" He stopped. The name sat in the air between them, unfamiliar in his own mouth after so long, and Wren went very still beside him, the way people do when they realize they've stepped onto ice they can't see the edges of.

"I'm sorry," she said quietly. "I didn't know."

"How would you?" He kept his voice level, easy, the voice he used for customers who lingered too long near the poetry section. "It was a long time ago."

But she was looking at him now, and her eyes in the lamplight were the color of strong tea, and there was something in them — attention, real attention, the kind that doesn't perform sympathy but simply pays it — that made him want to look away and made him unable to. The rain filled the silence for them, drumming on the canvas overhead, and Rafael understood with sudden clarity that he was standing closer to a feeling than he had stood in ten years, and that every instinct he owned was telling him to step back into the rain.

* * *

June did not believe in hesitation.

She came bursting out of the shop at that exact moment — because children have a radar for interesting adults the way sharks have a radar for blood — still holding the stub of pencil she'd been using to draw, hair staticky, socks mis-matched, and skidded to a stop in the doorway with the expression of someone who has just discovered treasure.

"You're the rose lady," she said.

"I'm— what?"

"The roses. At your house. Dad says Mom planted them but you get to keep them now." June delivered this with the breezy cruelty of childhood, entirely unaware of what she'd said, and Rafael felt the floor of the conversation tilt beneath all three of them. But Wren crouched down, box and all, setting herself at eye level with his daughter as naturally as if she'd been doing it her whole life.

"That's right," Wren said. "I get to keep them. Do you think they'll mind a new person watering them?"

"They'll mind if you do it wrong. Roses are dramatic." June peered into the box. "What's in there?"

"Books."

June's gasp was theatrical enough to power a small city. "Can I see?"

"June," Rafael started.

But Wren had already tilted the box toward her, and June was already reaching in, and Rafael watched his daughter — his guarded, motherless, careful-in-her-own-way daughter — fall upon a stranger's belongings with total trust, and watched the stranger let her. Inside the box, wrapped in dish towels, were perhaps a dozen books, their covers soft with age. June lifted one out with reverent hands: a battered copy of *A Wrinkle in Time*, its spine held together with yellowed tape.

"This is my favorite," June breathed.

"It's mine too," Wren said. "It was my mother's. Then mine. Now it's mostly tape."

"Mrs. Okafor read it to us but she stopped at chapter six because Tommy Kessler ruined it for everyone." June hugged the book to her chest, then seemed to remember property law, and held it out again. "You can have it back. But you should finish it. Chapter seven changes everything."

"I'll take that under advisement." Wren took the book, and her fingers brushed June's hair as she tucked it back into the box — a small unconscious gesture, maternal and unguarded, and Rafael looked away fast, out at the rain, jaw tight.

*This was a mistake,* he thought. Not the girl, not the kindness — the ease of it. The way three strangers under an awning had become, in the space of four minutes, something that looked unbearably like a family waiting to happen. He knew better than anyone that families were things that ended. He had watched one end in a single night on a wet road outside Millbrook, and he had spent ten years learning that the safest place to stand was exactly here: dry, alone, behind a counter, mailing letters to no one.

"Your dad doesn't like me," June observed, with devastating accuracy.

"June."

"What? You're doing your face."

"I don't have a face."

"You have a whole face." June turned to Wren, confiding. "It's the one he makes at the tax man."

Wren pressed her lips together, clearly fighting the laugh again, and lost. And Rafael — cornered by his own daughter, betrayed by his own face — found that despite everything, despite the alarm bells ringing somewhere in the old rooms of his heart, the corner of his mouth was doing something unauthorized.

"The tax man," he said, "is a serious matter."

"He is," Wren agreed solemnly. "I fled one myself. They follow you across state lines."

"Dad, she's funny," June reported, as though filing evidence. "Can she come in out of the rain? The awning is drippy on the left side. Look, she's getting wet."

She was. The old canvas sagged at its left corner and funneled a thin cold stream directly onto Wren's shoulder, and she bore it with the stoicism of someone accustomed to slightly worse circumstances, and Rafael heard himself sigh the sigh of a man surrendering to forces larger than himself.

"Come inside," he said. "Both of you. Before the awning finishes defecting."

* * *

Inside, the shop smelled the way it always did — paper and dust and the ghost of Elena's lavender oil, which Rafael had never cleaned out of the reading corner and told himself was because the wood needed conditioning. June towed Wren inside by the sleeve as if she'd known her for years, sat her down in the big worn armchair by the window without asking, and began pulling books off the shelf to show her, narrating each one with the breathless scholarship of a child who has been raised among shelves.

Admiral descended from his windowsill throne, inspected Wren once, twice, and then — traitor — climbed into her lap and began to purr.

"He never does that," June whispered, scandalized and delighted. "He judged Mrs. Pell for two weeks."

"Well. I'm honored." Wren's hand moved over the cat's fur, slow and automatic, and Rafael noticed — catalogued, resented, could not stop noticing — that she had the kind of hands that looked right holding things. Books. Cats. Possibly other things he refused to enumerate.

He made tea because his hands needed a job. The kettle's small roar covered the sound of June's chatter, and through the pass-through window to the back room Rafael watched them: his daughter perched on the arm of the chair, Wren cross-legged on the rug with the cat, the two of them bent together over a picture book June had selected as a test, apparently, because she was watching Wren's face while she read rather than the pages themselves.

Wren read aloud when June asked her to. Her voice was low and unhurried, and she did the voices — badly, June informed her, and she agreed cheerfully and did them worse — and Rafael stood in the back room with two mugs of tea going cold in his hands and felt something crack open in his chest that he had soldered shut a long time ago.

Because Elena used to sit exactly there. In that chair, with June exactly that size, doing voices badly on purpose, and the lamp made the same gold circle on the same rug, and Rafael had to set the mugs down on the counter very carefully, one at a time, because his hands were not steady.

*Ten years,* he thought. *Ten years of dry ground, and one storm, and look at you.*

When he brought the tea out, June had fallen asleep against Wren's shoulder — mid-sentence, mid-scheme, out like a snuffed candle, the way only children can fall asleep in the middle of their own enthusiasm. Wren sat perfectly still under the small weight of her, one hand cupped instinctively behind the girl's head, and looked up at Rafael with an expression of mild apology and absolute contentment, and said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't break the moment, and they both knew it.

"She does that," Rafael whispered. "Falls asleep on people she's known four hours. It's how she tests them. You passed."

"Did I get a grade?"

"Admiral likes you. That's the honors track."

Wren smiled down at June, and the lamplight caught the fine tired lines at the corners of her eyes, and she said, softly, almost to herself: "I haven't held anything this small in a long time."

And Rafael, standing there with cooling tea and a heart behaving like a struck bell, thought: *neither have I.*

He hated how much he wanted to stay in the gold circle of the lamp. He hated that the storm outside sounded less like a threat and more like permission. He hated — most of all, with a clean bright hatred — that some buried part of him, the part that still wrote letters to a stranger named Sable, the part that believed in second chances against all evidence, was whispering that this was not an accident, that storms and awnings and cats and sleeping children were how the world arranged things when it wanted something from you.

"Your box," he said abruptly, too loudly. "The books will get damp. I have bags. Plastic ones. For— damp."

Smooth, Thornbury. Truly the prose stylist of the county.

Wren blinked up at him, and something flickered across her face — amusement, he thought, or maybe recognition, the look of someone who has also built walls and also heard them creak. "Plastic bags," she repeated gravely. "For damp. Yes. Thank you."

He fetched them. Of course he fetched them. He wrapped each book with the fussy precision of a man handling first editions, and Wren watched him do it with her chin propped on her hand and June still asleep against her, and neither of them spoke, and the rain went on and on, patient as tide.

At the door, finally, with the box bagged and the cat evicted and June transferred, boneless and dead-weight, into her father's arms, Wren paused on the threshold. The storm had softened to a steady silver fall. She looked back at him over her shoulder, hair drying in waves, and said:

"Thank you. For the awning. And the tea. And—" she glanced down at June, asleep on his shoulder, "—the company."

"Any time," Rafael said, and meant: *never again,* and meant: *please, God, soon.*

She smiled — the full one this time, unguarded, gone in a second — and stepped out into the rain, and walked the twelve feet to her own door through the falling silver, and Rafael stood in the doorway of his shop with his sleeping daughter and watched her go, feeling the pull in his chest like a tide going out, dragging things with it he wasn't ready to lose.

Behind him, the shop clock struck nine. On the counter, forgotten, lay the letter he'd written that morning — number one hundred twenty-one, unsent, addressed as always to a stranger named Sable.

He stared at it for a long moment.

Then he put it in the drawer, and locked it, and did not let himself wonder why his hands were shaking.
