## 9. Letters, Read Aloud

The notice went up in the bookshop window on a Tuesday, between the display of discounted autumn paperbacks and June's crayon drawing of a whale that Rafael refused to take down even though it had faded to a ghost of itself in the sun.

*Thornbury & Daughters Books Presents: An Evening of Words. Friday, seven o'clock. All readers welcome. All stories honored.*

Wren saw it while carrying a box of Wren's Own Belongings — she was down to the last three boxes now, the ones she kept moving from corner to corner of the cottage because unpacking them meant admitting she was staying, and admitting she was staying meant admitting she'd fled — and she stood on the pavement in the cold gold light of late October reading the notice three times before her courage caught up with her ambition.

She had been volunteering at the shop for two weeks now. It had started with the reading corner — June, solemn and imperious in the way of eight-year-olds who have decided you are theirs, had appointed herself Wren's supervisor — and it had spread outward, the way kindness does, until Wren found herself shelving returns on Thursdays and arguing with Rafael about the correct alphabetical placement of books that had no business being alphabetized at all. He argued back. He argued the way some men dance: stiffly at first, then with a surprising and reluctant grace, and always, always as if he were apologizing for enjoying it.

She had learned his rhythms. The way he touched the spines of certain books — poetry, mostly, and one water-stained copy of Neruda that lived behind the counter like a talisman. The way he went quiet at four o'clock, when the light through the front windows went amber, as if the hour itself were a wound. The way he looked at her when he thought she wouldn't notice: not the way men usually looked at her, appraising, but the way people look at a letter they are afraid to open.

That was the thing about Rafael Thornbury. Everything about him was a letter he hadn't sent.

* * *

The literary night was Dev's idea, though Rafael would later claim it was a committee decision so no one would have to take the blame. Dev Iversen — no relation to anyone, he liked to say, though the town had long ago decided otherwise — ran the bakery on Harbor Street and had the build of a man who tested his own inventory with religious devotion. He had known Rafael since school. He had the particular authority of an oldest friend: the right to say things no one else could say.

"Everyone reads," Dev announced, arriving at the shop on Thursday with a tray of almond croissants as both bribe and argument. "Two minutes each. Poems, letters, passages, whatever. It's for the shop, Rafe. People come in, people buy books, people remember the shop exists before Hale flattens it into a parking structure for his ego."

"Hale isn't flattening anything yet," Rafael said, in the careful voice he used when the subject of Marcus Hale came up, a voice Wren had learned to hear the seams in.

"Yet." Dev set down the tray. His eyes went to Wren, and something passed across his face — a flicker, a hesitation, the look of a man carrying a secret that didn't belong to him. "You should read, Wren."

"I don't—"

"The letter," Dev said. "The one you read to June. The one about the lighthouse keeper's wife. You've got the voice for it."

Wren felt her face go warm. She hadn't known anyone had heard her reading to June in the corner by the window, June cross-legged on the floor with her chin on her knees, Wren holding the pages of a letter that was twenty years old and had never been meant for an audience.

"I couldn't," she said. "It's private. It's not mine, exactly. It was written to me, but it feels like—"

"It feels like it belongs to whoever needs it," Dev said. "That's the whole point of words, isn't it? That's why you keep a bookshop alive."

She looked at Rafael, expecting him to rescue her from the invitation, and found him watching her with an expression she couldn't read — something strung tight behind his ordinary face, a wire pulled to humming.

"Read whatever you like," he said quietly. "It's your voice. It should be your choice."

Later she would turn that sentence over and over, looking for what was hidden inside it. Later. But that Thursday she only heard kindness, and said yes, and went home to her cottage and stood before the three remaining boxes and finally, finally opened the one marked in her own faded handwriting: *SABLE — KEEP*.

* * *

She chose the letter on Friday afternoon, sitting on her floor with the whole decade spread around her like a fallen archive. A hundred and nineteen letters. She had counted them again last week, because counting them was a form of prayer. A hundred and nineteen letters from a stranger who knew her heart better than anyone she'd ever let touch it.

She had come to this town because of them. That was the truth she hadn't told anyone, not even Rafael — especially not Rafael. Ten years of a return address in a seaside town she'd never visited, and when her engagement collapsed and her career in the city turned to ash in her mouth, she had packed her car and driven toward the only place that had ever felt like it was expecting her. She had told herself it was a coincidence, moving in two doors down from the return address. She had told herself a lot of things.

The letter she chose was number sixty-one. It was about forgiveness — the writer's own, unwon. *You asked me once, in the way people ask the dark, whether a person can be forgiven for a thing they can never undo. I have thought about my answer for a long time, and here it is: I don't know if forgiveness is something you receive. I think it might be something you do, like digging a garden, like carrying water. I think you do it before you believe it, and the believing comes after, the way dawn comes after you've been awake all night watching for it. I am watching for it. I am not good at it. But I am watching.*

She read it aloud to the empty room twice, and her voice broke in the same place both times, and she decided that was all right. That was the letter's honesty, not her failure.

* * *

The shop on Friday night was fuller than Wren had ever seen it. Dev had brought trays of pastries and a urn of coffee that smelled like a rescue. The townsfolk came in waves — the Harlow sisters from the bakery's morning shift, old Mr. Petraglia who bought one Western a week and no other books, young families, teenagers hovering at the edges pretending they hadn't come. Rafael worked the room in his careful host's way, and Wren watched him and thought: *he doesn't know what he looks like when he's afraid. He thinks he's hiding it. He's holding the Neruda like a shield.*

The readings began. Mr. Petraglia read a passage from Louis L'Amour and got a standing ovation from the Harlow sisters, who had clearly planned it. A teenager read her own poem about the sea and pretended not to care that it was good. June, who had appointed herself mistress of ceremonies, read a passage from a book about a fox with such fierce seriousness that the room went soft as candle wax.

Then June said, into the microphone Dev had rigged from a karaoke machine, "And now my friend Wren is going to read a letter. It's a real letter. Somebody wrote it to her. It's the best thing in the world."

The room laughed, kindly. Wren walked to the front on legs that didn't feel entirely hers.

She had expected to be nervous. She hadn't expected what happened instead, which was that the moment she unfolded the pages and saw the handwriting — that handwriting, slanted and urgent and tender, the handwriting she had traced with her fingers a hundred times in a hundred lonely apartments — the nervousness burned away and left only the words.

She read.

She read about forgiveness as a garden dug before you believe in spring. She read about watching for dawn. And the room — she could feel it happening, the way you feel weather change — the room went still in that particular way a room goes still when a private grief has been recognized as a shared one. The Harlow sisters held hands. Mr. Petraglia took off his cap. Somewhere near the back a man she didn't know wept openly and without shame.

And Rafael—

She found him at the edge of her vision, standing by the poetry shelf with his arms folded like a man holding himself together at the seams. His face had gone the color of paper. He was looking at her the way you look at a door swinging open onto a room you thought was sealed forever. And she understood, in a way she couldn't yet articulate, that the letter was doing to him something different than it was doing to the room. The room was hearing a beautiful stranger's words. Rafael was hearing something else. She couldn't say what. But she saw the wire inside him pull tight enough to sing.

She finished. The silence afterward lasted three full seconds, which in a room full of people is a geological age, and then the applause came like a tide.

* * *

Afterward, the shop emptied slowly, the way good nights do, reluctant and warm. Dev pressed a croissant into Wren's hand and said, "Told you," with the smugness of a prophet. The Harlow sisters asked if the writer was someone local. Wren laughed and said she didn't know, and it was almost true.

June had fallen asleep in the reading corner, curled in the big chair with the fox book open on her chest. Rafael lifted her with the practiced tenderness of a man who had done it a thousand times, and Wren watched him and felt the thing in her chest that she had been not-naming for weeks turn over and show its face.

She loved him. That was the fact underneath all the other facts. She loved this guarded, grieving, infuriating man who alphabetized books wrong on purpose so she'd have something to argue about.

He came toward her with June in his arms, and for a moment they stood close in the lamplight of the emptying shop, and the air between them did the thing the air had been threatening to do for weeks.

"You were extraordinary," he said.

"They were your—" She stopped. "They were *somebody's* words. I just carried them."

"You carried them well." His voice was rough. "Whoever wrote that letter was lucky to have you on the other end."

"Was he?" She smiled, tired and true. "I used to think he was a coward. Ten years and never a name. But I don't anymore. I think he was someone who couldn't stop being honest, and couldn't bear to be known. Both at once." She looked up at him. "I'm going to find him someday. I've decided. I've moved across the country chasing a return address, Rafael. I'm not going to stop until I know who he is."

Something crossed his face then — she would remember it forever, that something, though she wouldn't understand it until much later. It was fear, but it was also, underneath the fear, something that looked almost like relief. Like a man standing at the edge of cold water who has just heard someone call his name.

"Maybe," Rafael said, very quietly, "you already know him."

And then he smiled, the careful host's smile, and said goodnight, and carried his sleeping daughter out into the dark, and Wren stood alone in the lamplight of the shop with a hundred and nineteen letters' worth of someone else's heart, and wondered — for the first time, but not the last — why the words *maybe you already know him* felt less like a riddle than like a door left deliberately, tremblingly, open.
