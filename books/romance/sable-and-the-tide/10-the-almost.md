## 10. The Almost

The festival lights came down at eleven, strung bulbs unclipped one by one from the eaves of Harbor Street until the whole town dimmed back into its ordinary self. Wren stood in the doorway of the bookshop long after the last folding chair had been stacked, watching Rafael coil extension cords with the careful, unhurried hands of a man who had learned to make small tasks hold him together.

"You didn't have to stay and help," he said, not looking up. "The reading was the hard part. Everything after is just cleanup."

"I like cleanup." She stepped out onto the sidewalk, where the night smelled of salt and cooling fryer oil and the faint sweetness of spilled cider. "It's honest work. You can see what you've done."

He glanced up then, and in the amber spill of the single bulb still burning over the door, she saw something flicker across his face — recognition, maybe, or the memory of her voice an hour ago, carrying his own sentences back to him from a stage he hadn't known she'd stand on. *Grief doesn't shrink. You just grow rooms around it.* The room had wept. She had wept, reading words she'd believed were written for her alone by a stranger who knew her better than anyone alive.

"June's asleep on the couch," Rafael said. "She fought it like a lawyer, but the festival won."

"She danced until her shoes came off."

"She did." A smile moved through his voice before it reached his face. "Elena used to do that. Dance until — " He stopped. The sentence hung there, unfinished, like a bridge to nowhere.

Wren waited. She was learning the shape of his silences, which ones were walls and which ones were doors left ajar out of habit. This one felt like a door.

But he turned back to the cords, winding them around his elbow, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"There's wine inside," he said. "The good bottle Dev brought. It seems wrong to let it go to waste on a night when nobody died."

* * *

Rafael poured two glasses because stopping himself would have required a reason, and he had no reason he could say aloud. The shop after hours was a different country — lamps low, the stacks throwing long shadows, June curled under a quilt on the old reading couch with one of her picture books open-faced on her chest like a fallen bird. The rain had started again, soft against the windows, the kind of rain that made the whole world feel indoors.

Wren ran her fingers along the spines as she walked, the way she always did, and he watched her the way he had been trying not to watch her for weeks: the tilt of her head, the loose knot of her hair coming undone, the wine-dark smudge of exhaustion under her eyes that she wore like she'd earned it honestly.

"To your correspondent," she said, raising her glass. "Whoever they are."

"To them." He drank too fast.

"Do you ever wonder about people like that?" She settled into the armchair across from him, tucking her feet beneath her, and the lamplight caught the rim of her glass. "Someone who writes letters for ten years and never signs them. I used to invent them. A lighthouse keeper, once. A woman who'd lost a sister. For a while I was convinced it was someone who'd died and arranged the letters before — " She laughed at herself, small and embarrassed. "That's morbid. Forget I said it."

"It's not morbid." His voice came out lower than he intended. "It's — hopeful, actually. Believing someone could love you enough to arrange for it to continue past the end of them."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and somewhere down the street a shutter knocked in the wind.

"Rafael," she said quietly. "Sometimes when you talk, I feel like you're answering questions I haven't asked yet."

He should have told her then. He felt the truth rise in him like tide water, pressing at every seam — the journal in Elena's handwriting, the return address given away ten years ago by a dying woman's hand, the hundred and twenty letters folded in a stranger's boxes next door. He opened his mouth, and what came out was:

"More wine?"

* * *

They ended up on the couch eventually — not touching, but close enough that Wren could feel the warmth radiating off his shoulder, could hear the slow tide-sound of his breathing beneath the rain. June slept between them without knowing she was a border, or perhaps knowing exactly, in the way children know things.

"This is my favorite hour," Wren admitted. "The one after something good. When everyone's gone home and the town goes quiet and you get to keep the good thing a little longer, privately."

"Elena called it the second sunset." The name came easier tonight; the wine had loosened whatever gate usually held it. "She said the best part of any day was the part nobody else saw."

"She sounds like someone I'd have loved."

"You would have." He said it simply, without flinching, and Wren felt the enormity of that — a man setting his grief on the table between them like a third guest, letting her look at it. "She would have liked you too. She liked people who read to children like it mattered."

"I do think it matters."

"No." He turned his head, and suddenly his face was very close, closer than conversation required, closer than safety allowed. "I mean you do it like it matters. Like June is a person and not a performance. Do you know how rare — "

The rest of the sentence dissolved. Because she had turned toward him too, and now there was no distance left to pretend about, only the space of a breath, and his eyes dropped to her mouth and stayed there, and Wren felt the whole evening tilt toward a point of no return.

*Rafael,* she thought. *Please.*

He leaned in. Or the room leaned in. The lamp hummed. Rain wrote its endless script on the window. His breath touched her cheek, warm and uncertain, and her eyes were closing, and —

A small voice split the dark.

"Papa?"

June sat up under the quilt, hair wild, face wet, her whole small body trembling. "Papa, I dreamed about the water again. Mama was in the water and she kept waving and I couldn't — "

Rafael was off the couch before Wren finished inhaling, crossing the room in three strides, gathering his daughter against his chest as though pulling her out of the sea itself. "Hey. Hey, I've got you. You're here. You're home. Mama's not in the water, sweetheart. Mama's — " His voice cracked and reassembled itself. "You're safe."

Wren stood, feeling suddenly enormous and intrusive, a piece of furniture that had learned to walk. She gathered her coat. When she touched June's damp hair on her way past, the little girl clung to her fingers for one desperate second, and Wren's heart broke cleanly in two.

"Goodnight, June-bird," she whispered.

At the door, she looked back. Rafael stood holding his daughter in the lamplight, and his eyes found hers over June's shoulder — full of something urgent and unfinished and terrified — and then he looked away first.

The door clicked shut behind her. The rain took her home the twelve steps to Number Nine.

* * *

Wren did not sleep. She sat by her own window with the cold teacup she'd forgotten to drink, watching the light in the bookshop burn until nearly two in the morning, waiting for a knock that never came. Twelve steps. He could have crossed them in the time it took to button a coat. She counted them the way you count the gaps in a fence you're deciding whether to jump.

By three she had constructed and demolished a dozen explanations. June's nightmare, obviously. A father's first duty. Any decent man would have done the same. But decent men knocked later, once the child was sleeping again. Decent men sent a note through the door in the morning. Decent men did not look at you over their daughter's shoulder like a man watching a lifeboat pull away from a wreck he wasn't sure he wanted to be rescued from.

And underneath all the reasonable explanations, colder and simpler: *he stopped.* At the last inch, he stopped. Whatever lived in that final distance between them, he had chosen it over her.

She thought of the letters then, absurdly — her anonymous writer, who never hesitated, who said the true thing at whatever cost, who had spent a decade telling her that love was a thing you walked toward even with wet matches in your pockets. *What would he do?* she wondered, and then, with a strange hollow laugh at herself: *whoever he is, at least he never almost.*

When the shop light finally went out, she went to bed and lay listening to the rain and rehearsing, for no reason she could defend, the opening line of a letter she would never send: *Dear Sable — I think I have ruined something tonight, and I don't know how to sign my way back to you.*

Across the twelve steps of wet pavement, Rafael sat on the edge of June's bed long after her breathing went deep and even, his wine untouched, Elena's photograph smiling from the dresser in the dark. He pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw stars.

*I almost kissed her,* he thought. *I almost — *

And beneath the thought, older and colder, the voice he had carried for ten years rose up like water in a hull: *You couldn't save the last woman you loved. What makes you think you deserve this one?*

In the drawer of his desk, unseen, letter 121 waited in its envelope, addressed to no one, saying everything.

He did not sleep either. And in the morning, when Wren passed the shop on her way to town, the CLOSED sign hung in the window, and Rafael did not come to the door.
