## 4. Sandpiper Point at Low Tide

The tide was out so far it looked like the ocean had changed its mind about being here.

Delia Voss stood at the top of the dune stairs with her hands jammed in the pockets of her brother's old jacket and let the wind have its way with her. Ten years of California sunshine had softened her, she'd thought. Ten years of driving past correctional facilities on her way to work, of sitting in visiting rooms that smelled like floor wax and despair, of writing letters to a woman who never wrote back — apparently none of it had prepared her for how much this beach could still hurt.

Sandpiper Point at low tide was a different country. The water pulled back half a mile, exposing sandbars and tide pools and the long black spine of rock that locals called the Serpent, all of it gleaming and dripping under a sky the color of an oyster's inside. When Delia was nine, Danny had told her the Serpent woke up at high tide and swam out to sea, and she'd believed him for two full years because Danny could sell anything. He'd sold their mother on the idea that the dent in the car door was a gift from a grateful seagull. He'd sold Mrs. Abernathy on the idea that her cat had run away instead of, as everyone in town knew, taken up residence in Danny's treehouse for three weeks.

And then one night in October, ten years ago, he hadn't been able to sell anyone anything, because the whole town decided overnight that he was a thief, and no amount of charm could buy that back.

Delia took the stairs down. Her boots sank into wet sand still printed with the night's gulls, stitching and stitching across the flats like someone practicing a signature they couldn't get right.

She knew exactly where she was walking before she started walking. Her feet remembered the way the way a tongue remembers a chipped tooth.

* * *

It had been a Thursday. She knew that because Danny had been supposed to pick her up from debate practice, and he'd been late, which was normal, except that he never showed at all, which wasn't. She'd walked home in the dark with her trophy — third place, regional finals, a hunk of plastic shaped like an eagle mid-scream — getting angrier by the block. By the time she hit their street she'd composed a speech about responsibility and reliability that would have won first place anywhere in the state.

Then she saw the police lights washing blue across their living room curtains, and every word she'd rehearsed fell out of her head like coins through a torn pocket.

Her mother was on the porch in her housecoat, arms wrapped around herself, and a policewoman was saying something in the low, careful voice people use when they're delivering weather reports about other people's lives. *Taken into custody. The marina. We'll know more after processing.*

"The marina," Delia had said, stupidly. "Danny doesn't even like boats."

Nobody laughed. That was the moment she understood, before anyone explained a single fact, that the world had tilted and wasn't going to tilt back.

Here's what the town said happened: the Harborview Gallery on Front Street was robbed of eleven thousand dollars' worth of inventory on a Thursday night in October. Here's what the town said next: Danny Voss did it. Here's why: Marcus Hale said so, and Marcus Hale was engaged to a sweet young thing who worked at the bakery, and Marcus Hale was the kind of man whose word arrived pre-notarized.

Here's what actually happened, as far as Delia could ever piece together: Danny had been at the marina that night, yes, but not robbing anything. He'd been meeting someone — he never said who, not even to her, not even in the end — about buying back their father's fishing boat, the *Molly B*, which had been sold out from under them after Dad died. Danny had cash saved from two summers of deckhand work. He had a plan. He had a receipt, probably, or a handshake, or something. And then the gallery alarm went off six blocks away, and Danny Voss, nineteen years old, standing in the dark near a boat he couldn't afford yet, became the easiest answer to a question nobody wanted to examine too closely.

Eleven thousand dollars. They found four hundred and sixty in his savings account and called it suspicious. They found his fingerprints nowhere and called that suspicious too. What they found instead was a witness. A young woman from the bakery, pretty and frightened, who told the grand jury she'd seen a man matching Danny's description near the gallery that night.

A man matching his description. Delia had read the transcript so many times the pages went soft at the corners. Half the men in Sandpiper Point matched Danny's description. It was a town built out of fishermen and carpenters; tall and dark-haired described the entire volunteer fire department.

But the witness had cried, Delia learned later. She'd cried on the stand, and juries forgive crying women everything, and Danny Voss went away for eight years with good behavior hanging over him like a carrot made of time.

Their mother lasted four years before her heart gave out, and Delia had always privately counted those years as stolen goods too, entered on the same ledger. Eleven thousand dollars, one boat, one mother, one brother's twenties, one sister's entire belief in the basic fairness of the universe. Itemized. Subtotaled. Carried forward.

* * *

The Serpent's rocks were slick under her boots now, and she sat down on the flat boulder where Danny used to eat his lunch during summer breaks, legs dangling, throwing mussels to the gulls and narrating their lives to her in voices. *That one's Gerald. Gerald has a gambling problem.* She was twenty-nine years old and she was sitting on a rock talking to a ghost, and she let herself do it for exactly one minute, because grief unattended gets ideas above its station.

"Hey, Danny," she said to the water.

The water said nothing, which was more honest than most people.

Ten years of letters. That was the part that still made her jaw tighten when she thought about it, and she thought about it constantly, the way you probe a sore tooth. She'd written to Orsen Thornbury — the witness, the baker, the fiancée of Marcus Hale — forty-one letters over ten years. Not hate mail. She'd wanted to write hate mail; she'd drafted some spectacular ones in the early days, back when her handwriting shook. But her mother had raised them better than that, or maybe just her, since Danny's moral compass had always pointed somewhere between *generous* and *gullible*. So instead Delia had written facts. Dates. Questions. The name of the security company whose logs didn't match the prosecution's timeline. The detail about the gallery's back door, which opened with a key only three people possessed, none of them named Voss.

Forty-one letters. Every single one returned unopened, stamped in cheerful red ink: REFUSED BY ADDRESSEE.

She'd kept them all. They lived in a box under her bed in Sacramento, and she'd brought the box with her, because she was done writing letters into a void. If Orsen Thornbury wouldn't read them, she could look at them. She could hold them up like a mirror.

Because here was the thing Delia had figured out somewhere around year six, sitting in a visiting room watching her brother age in increments the state charged him for: she hadn't come back for vengeance. Vengeance was a luxury. Vengeance assumed you had something left to spend. No — she'd come back because Danny's appeal hearing was coming, finally, after years of lawyers who took retainer checks and produced nothing, and the petition needed a public reckoning, and the person best positioned to give it was the woman whose testimony had put him away. And if that woman happened to be comfortable, and successful, and beloved by a town that had eaten her testimony like it was fresh bread — well. Comfortable people rarely moved unless the ground shifted underneath them.

Delia Voss had come home to shift the ground.

She looked down the beach toward the harbor, where the lights of Front Street were coming on one by one, gold and smug against the dusk. Somewhere up there was the bakery with its hand-painted sign — THORNBURY'S, in looping script that Delia recognized from the wedding cake boxes she'd seen at a hundred receptions she'd catered in another life. Thornbury's cakes were famous within fifty miles. Thornbury's owner was famous for making people cry happy tears at ceremonies, for believing — publicly, professionally, relentlessly — in love.

*Love,* Delia thought, and almost laughed. The woman had refused forty-one envelopes. Whatever she believed in, it wasn't the truth, and Delia intended to find out which one scared her more.

* * *

She stayed until the light went the color of weak tea, and then she climbed back up the dune stairs, and that was when she saw the cottage.

She'd signed the lease sight unseen from Sacramento, on the recommendation of a landlady who asked exactly zero questions, which should have been her first clue that small towns never stop being small towns. The cottage sat at the top of the bluff road, white clapboard gone gray at the corners, with a porch swing that hung slightly crooked and a garden that had clearly been beautiful once and was now mostly ambitions. It backed directly onto the property behind it.

The property behind it had a lit kitchen window, and through that window, moving between counter and oven, was a woman with flour on her forearms and her hair escaping its bun in soft brown wisps, humming to herself while she worked.

Orsen Thornbury. In the flesh. Close enough to wave to. Close enough to borrow a cup of sugar from, if either of them were the borrowing type.

Delia stood in the darkening yard and felt something complicated move through her chest — not quite satisfaction, not quite dread, something with the teeth of both. Of all the cottages in all of Sandpiper Point. She'd have called it fate, except she'd stopped believing in fate around the same time she stopped believing in the justice system, and for roughly the same reason.

But there was poetry in it, and Delia had always been helpless before poetry. The witness and the sister, separated by one fence and ten years of silence. The town would call it coincidence. Delia called it an opening.

She watched through the window as Orsen pulled a tray from the oven, tested something, frowned at it, and ate the evidence anyway. Something in the gesture — the small, private failure of it, the way the woman's shoulders dropped half an inch before she squared them again — snagged on something in Delia that she hadn't expected and didn't appreciate. It looked, for one unguarded second, less like a woman eating a failed pastry and more like a woman carrying something heavy and pretending the tray was the weight.

*No,* Delia told herself firmly. *Don't you dare start humanizing her. You know what she did.*

She knew what the transcripts said she did. She knew what the jury heard. She knew that a crying girl had been worth more to the State of Maine than a dock logbook, apparently, though God knew nobody had checked any dock logbooks.

Tomorrow, Delia decided. Tomorrow she'd carry a casserole next door like a normal neighbor, because nothing disarmed a person faster than kindness they hadn't earned, and she'd introduce herself with a name that meant nothing to Orsen Thornbury yet. *Voss. Delia Voss. Just moved in. Isn't that funny, we're neighbors.*

Let her smile and shake my hand, Delia thought, turning toward her own crooked porch. Let her be warm to me. I can wait. I've had ten years of practice.

Behind her, the tide was already turning, the ocean creeping back in over the flats, patient as anything, covering the place where Danny used to sit as if it had never been exposed at all.

Inside the cottage, Delia set the box of returned letters on the kitchen table, dead center, where she'd have to look at it every morning. Forty-one red stamps stared up at her through the cardboard.

"Soon," she told them.

And out on the water, the first buoy bell of evening rang once, low and clear, like a door opening somewhere far away.
