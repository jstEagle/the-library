## 5. You Knew My Name

The morning the stranger finally came into my bakery, I burned an entire tray of lemon tarts because I was watching her through the front window.

Not watching, exactly. Noticing. There's a difference, and I'd have sworn to it under oath — a phrase that would come back to haunt me before the day was out.

She walked past the shop every morning for two weeks. Same time, just after seven, when the light came off the water and turned everything gold and forgiving. She never came in. She stood at the edge of the boardwalk with her hands in the pockets of a canvas jacket gone soft at the elbows, and she looked at my window like she was reading something written on the glass. Then she'd walk on toward the cottage next door — *her* cottage now, though I still thought of it as Mrs. Pell's — and disappear behind the dune grass.

I told myself she was one of those people who move to small towns and spend a month deciding whether they like it. We get them every spring. They buy lavender soap and ask about the ferry schedule and are gone by June.

But there was nothing tentative about her. Tentative people look at bakeries the way you look at a puppy in a store window — wanting, weighing, moving on. This woman looked at my bakery the way a lawyer looks at a witness.

I know that look. I've been on both sides of it.

* * *

"Orsen." Priya, my assistant, materialized at my elbow holding the ruined tray of tarts like evidence of a crime. "These are charcoal."

"They're rustic."

"They're on fire. Slightly. Historically." She set them down and followed my gaze to the window, where the stranger had stopped again, today actually facing us, close enough that I could see she had her brother's face.

That thought arrived uninvited and I dismissed it immediately, the way you dismiss a word that's been sitting on the tip of your tongue for ten years. Danny Voss had a face like that — sharp jaw, dark brows, a mouth that looked like it was always about to say something funny and then didn't. I hadn't let myself think of that face in years. It lived in a room in my head I kept locked, and I was very good at keeping it locked. Practice makes perfect.

"She's been out there every morning," Priya said. "Should I go invite her in? We have a first-timer protocol. Free sample, charming banter, lifetime customer."

"We do not have a protocol."

"We absolutely do. You wrote it on a napkin in 2019."

The bell over the door rang, and the stranger walked in, and every clever thing I might have said evaporated.

Up close she was taller than I'd guessed, with sun-creased skin and gray coming in at her temples like frost on a windowpane. Her eyes did a slow inventory of the room — the cases, the chalkboard menu, me — and I watched her arrive somewhere and decide not to show it.

"Good morning," I said, in my bakery voice, the warm one, the one that has sold ten thousand wedding cakes to people I will never think about again. "Welcome to Sugar Sand. First time?"

"No," she said.

"Oh — you've been in before? I'm sorry, I—"

"I haven't been in before." She looked at the case. "What's good?"

"The lemon tarts," said Priya, with total sincerity, "are legendary."

The stranger's mouth twitched. "I'll take whatever isn't legendary, then."

"A blueberry scone," I said. "And coffee?"

"Black."

I got it myself, which I never do; Priya runs the counter and I run the ovens and we have a treaty older than some marriages. But I wanted my hands busy. There was something about being behind the counter with this woman looking at me that made me feel like I was the one on display.

She paid in cash. Counted out exact change from a worn leather wallet, coins stacked in little towers like someone who'd learned to make money last. When she took the scone she didn't leave. She sat at the corner table by the window, the one with the wobbly leg I'd never fixed because regulars said it added charm, and she ate slowly, and she watched the morning crowd come in: the Hendersons arguing happily about croissant quantities, old Tom Beale getting his usual and telling his usual joke, the whole gentle machinery of a town that believed itself to be exactly what it appeared to be.

"You're the wedding planner," she said, when the rush had thinned.

It wasn't quite a question, so I gave it the answer it deserved. "Guilty."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under water. "Are you."

"That was a joke," I said. "Occupational hazard. Everything sounds like a confession if you say it flat enough."

"Mm." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the saucer. Turned it back. "Do you believe in them? Weddings."

Priya, wiping the case three feet away, went very still, the way you do when you sense weather coming.

"I believe in cake," I said lightly. "Cake has never lied to me."

"And people?"

"People are why cake exists."

She laughed — one short breath of it, surprised out of her — and for a second the sharpness in her face softened into something almost kind, and I felt the strangest lurch, like missing a stair in the dark. Because I knew that laugh. I knew the shape of it. I'd heard it across a courtroom once, muffled behind a hand, when a defense attorney made a joke that fell dead in the air and only one person in the gallery found it funny.

I put the thought down carefully, like setting down something hot, and told myself I was imagining things. Grief does that. Guilt does that. Ten years of not-thinking-about-something will make you see its ghost in every stranger with a familiar jaw.

"Big festival coming up," she said. "The bicentennial. I hear there's going to be a wedding component."

"There's going to be a wedding *circus*," I said. "Twelve ceremonies in one weekend. The town council has lost its collective mind, and I have agreed to produce it, which proves I've lost mine too."

"Twelve couples. That's a lot of cake."

"It's a lot of everything. If you'll excuse the pun, it's a logistical marriage."

Another flicker of that laugh. Then it banked itself, and she looked at me straight on, and the temperature of the room changed.

"Ms. Marchetti," she said. "Can I ask you something personal?"

"Customers ask me personal things all the time. Usually it's whether the gluten-free option is also dairy-free."

"When you testified," she said, "did you ever wonder if you were wrong?"

The case lights hummed. Somewhere in the kitchen the timer on the sourdough went off, a small electronic scream, and nobody moved to stop it.

"I'm sorry," I said, and my voice came out steady, which I would later be proud of, "do I know you?"

"Not yet," she said.

"Then I don't discuss testimony with strangers. I don't discuss it with anyone, actually. It was a long time ago, and it isn't—" I stopped, because the sentence forming was *it isn't anyone's business*, and even as I shaped it I could hear how false it was, how it sounded like a door slamming on a room full of screaming.

"It was my brother's business," she said quietly.

The timer screamed on. Priya had backed up against the far wall with a dish towel clutched to her chest like a rosary.

"Your name," I said. "Please. Your name."

She reached into the canvas jacket and took out an envelope. Just one. Soft-cornered, postmark faded to gray, addressed in careful block letters that had clearly been written by someone with all the time in the world and no way to soften what the words would do.

She set it on the counter between us.

Then another. And another. From the same jacket, from a manila folder she'd carried in every morning for two weeks while she stood outside my window deciding — no, not deciding. Waiting. Gathering herself the way a wave gathers before it comes ashore.

Eleven envelopes. No — twelve. Thirteen. She laid them out in a row across my marble counter, over the pastry tongs and the tip jar and the little stand of business cards that read ORSEN MARCHETTI, WEDDINGS & EVENTS, SANDPIPER POINT, and each envelope landed with a sound too small for what it was, like snow falling on a roof before it collapses.

Postmarked Fairview Correctional Facility. One per year, roughly. The earliest dated eight months after the trial.

"I wrote you every year on the anniversary," she said. "Danny's arrest anniversary. I figured you'd remember the date better than I would." A pause. "I was right, wasn't I. You flinched when I said *testified*. You've known the date every year for ten years. You probably wake up on it."

I couldn't speak. My mouth had gone dry as flour.

"You never opened them," she said. "I know, because I checked the forwarding records after I got out. Return to sender, addressee moved, addressee unknown. Twelve letters, twelve trips back to Fairview. They gave them to me in a box when I was released, along with his other things." Her hand rested on the topmost envelope without picking it up. "So these are copies. I copied them all out by hand, in the cottage, the first week I was here. In case the originals weren't enough. In case you needed convincing."

"Convincing of what?" My voice cracked on the second word and I hated myself for it.

"That I'm not going away," she said. "And that you're going to hear what he had to say. Eventually. Today, ideally."

"Who—" But I already knew. God help me, I knew, and I said it anyway, because saying it made it real and some part of me had spent ten years making sure none of this was real. "Who are you?"

She stood. Up close, with the morning light behind her, she had Danny Voss's exact face, and I don't know how I'd pretended otherwise for even a day.

"My name," she said, "is Delia Voss. And you sent my brother to prison."

* * *

Afterward I couldn't tell you how long we stood there. Minutes. An hour. The sourdough timer had given up its screaming and settled into an occasional reproachful beep, like a conscience.

Priya had retreated to the kitchen doorway. I registered her there the way you register furniture — peripherally, uselessly. Later she would tell me she'd never seen anyone look the way I looked, and that she'd wanted to come stand next to me and hadn't dared, because whatever was happening at that counter was happening in a country where assistants couldn't follow.

"I don't—" I started, for maybe the fourth time. Every sentence I began collapsed under its own weight. *I don't know what you want me to say. I don't understand. I don't—*

"Sit down, Ms. Marchetti."

"I own this place."

"Then sit down in your own place." She pulled out the chair at the corner table, the wobbly one, and waited.

I sat. My legs made the decision for me.

Delia Voss remained standing, and I understood that this was deliberate, that she had spent ten years standing in visiting rooms and courtrooms and parole hearings while other people sat in judgment, and today the geometry was going to be different or she was going to die trying.

"Danny didn't do it," she said. "You know that, don't you? Some part of you has always known it."

"I testified to what I saw."

"You testified to what Marcus Hale told you you saw." She said the name the way you'd spit out a pit. "There's a difference, and I think you've known that difference for ten years, and I think it's been eating you alive, and I think that's why you plan weddings for people you can't look in the eye."

"That's not—" I pressed my palms flat against my thighs. "You don't know anything about me."

"I know everything about you," she said, and there was no cruelty in it, which somehow made it worse. Cruelty I could have fought. "I've had ten years to learn about you. I read every article. I sat in on the appeal hearings. I know you were twenty-four. I know you were engaged to the prosecution's star investigator. I know you identified my brother from a photo array where his picture was the only one taken at night. I know the defense attorney was a public defender with forty other cases who met Danny for the first time the week of trial." She leaned forward, hands on the table. "I know you were a witness, Orsen. And I know witnesses get made."

The bell over the door jangled. Mrs. Henderson, come back for the croissants she'd decided against, took one look at the room — the row of prison envelopes, the white-faced baker, the stranger standing like a drawn bow — and did the small-town thing, which was to say "Oh — I'll — later," and back out onto the boardwalk.

When she was gone, Delia spoke again, softer.

"I didn't come here to hate you. I want you to know that. I spent the first five years hating you, and it nearly killed me, because hate needs feeding and I didn't have anything left to feed it with. So I starved it. And what grew in its place was worse." She looked down at the envelopes. "Questions. I came here with questions. And every year I wrote them down and mailed them to you, and every year they came back, and I started to think maybe that was the answer. Maybe silence was the answer. Maybe you knew exactly what you'd done and you were fine with it."

"I'm not fine with it," I whispered.

Her head came up sharply. For the first time since she'd walked in, she looked off-balance — a woman whose script had run out.

"What did you say?"

"I said I'm not fine with it." My voice was shaking and I let it shake. Ten years of steadiness, of bakery-voice warmth, of *congratulations, of everything must be perfect, of smiling through other people's joy like a woman pressing her face against the glass of a lit house* — and here, in my own shop, in front of the sister of the man I'd helped bury, it all came apart at once. "Do you think I don't know? Do you think I sleep? I planned a wedding yesterday for a couple who bickered about napkin colors for forty minutes, and I stood there nodding, and the entire time I was thinking about a courtroom in 2015 and a boy in an orange jumpsuit who looked at me — did you know he looked at me? During my testimony. He didn't glare. He just *looked*, like he was trying to figure out how I'd gotten it so wrong, like he assumed I was mistaken instead of lying, because he was twenty-two and he still believed people were basically honest—"

I stopped. My eyes were burning and I refused, absolutely refused, to cry in front of her.

Delia Voss had gone very still. When she spoke, her voice had changed key entirely — lower, rougher, almost gentle.

"He talked about you, you know. In his letters to me. Early on. He said, *the girl on the stand looks like she's the one on trial.*" She exhaled slowly. "I hated him for that. For being generous to you. It would have been easier if he'd hated you back."

"Well," I said, and laughed, and it came out broken. "He was smarter than both of us."

For a moment — one strange, suspended moment — something passed between us that was not enmity. Recognition, maybe. Two women who had spent a decade carrying the same body between them, taking turns, neither able to set it down.

Then Delia straightened, and the moment closed like water over a stone, and she gathered the envelopes back into their folder with brisk, deliberate movements — all except one, which she left on the counter, square in front of me.

"This one stays," she said. "The first letter. Read it or don't. Burn it if you want. But it lives in your shop now, and you'll walk past it every morning, and we'll both find out what kind of person you are by what you do with it."

She shrugged into her jacket. At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and didn't turn around.

"One more thing, Ms. Marchetti. Since we're neighbors now." A beat. "You might want to look into who bought the building your lease is on. I hear the paperwork finalized last week."

The bell chimed. She was gone, up the boardwalk, into the gold light, leaving the smell of black coffee and the weight of thirteen years of letters sitting on my marble counter like a verdict.

Priya emerged from the kitchen doorway. "Ors," she said carefully. "Who owns the building?"

I picked up the envelope. My name on the front, in block letters, patient as erosion.

"Priya," I said, "go find my lease."
