## 7. Terms of Surrender

### Delia

Delia had rehearsed this conversation in the shower, on the ferry, and once — memorably — into the bathroom mirror of a rest stop outside Millbrook, where a woman washing her hands had given her a look of deep concern.

None of that rehearsal survived contact with Orsen Thornbury standing in her own bakery at seven in the morning, flour on her forearms, looking like she'd slept in her clothes and won the argument with herself about whether to bother changing them.

"You bought my building." Orsen said it flatly, the way you'd read a diagnosis off a chart. "Through a holding company. Voss Holdings. Very subtle."

"I wasn't trying to be subtle," Delia said. "I was trying to be legal."

"Congratulations. You're both." Orsen wiped her hands on her apron with the brisk violence of someone who wanted very badly to be wiping something else. "Say what you came to say."

Delia had imagined this moment for ten years. In some versions she was calm. In others she was magnificent. In one particularly satisfying draft she'd made Orsen cry, which had felt righteous at two in the morning and shameful by dawn.

What she actually did was set a folder on the counter between them, next to the display case where a single lemon tart sat under glass like an exhibit in a museum of things Delia used to love.

"Recant your testimony," she said. "Publicly. Before the bicentennial festival. On the record, before witnesses, or I don't renew your lease."

The bakery was quiet enough that Delia could hear the refrigerator hum and, faintly, the sea. Orsen didn't move. She had the stillness of a woman who had learned, somewhere along the way, that motion could be held against you.

"That's it?" Orsen said finally. "No preamble? No villain monologue?"

"I've had ten years to write the monologue. It got shorter every year."

Something flickered across Orsen's face — not quite a smile, but adjacent to one, the way a bruise is adjacent to skin. "And if I refuse?"

"Then in ninety days, Sandpiper Point loses its only bakery, and you lose everything you built here." Delia kept her voice level. It cost more than she let show. "I'm told the sourdough is famous. Regionally."

"Who told you that?"

"The woman at the hardware store. She also told me you catered her daughter's wedding and cried during the vows."

"I did not—"

"She said you cried so hard the best man asked if you were okay."

Orsen's jaw tightened. "That was allergies. The lilies."

"Sure," Delia said. "The lilies."

* * *

### Orsen

Later — much later, after Delia had left with her folder and her terrible calm and her face that looked like Danny's if you were cruel enough to notice — Orsen stood behind her counter and tried to do arithmetic.

Not money. She knew the money. Ninety days of revenue against a lease she couldn't renegotiate because the person holding the lease didn't want money. That math was simple and fatal.

It was the other arithmetic that kept snagging: ten years of letters, unopened, sitting in a box in her storage room like a debt she'd been pretending wasn't accruing interest. One woman's brother, inside. One woman's decade, spent writing to someone who never wrote back. And now that woman owned the ground under Orsen's ovens, and all she wanted — all she'd crossed the country to want — was four words.

*I was wrong. Recant.*

Orsen pulled out her phone and called Priya, her attorney, who answered on the second ring with the tone of a woman who billed by the minute and regretted nothing.

"He bought it through an LLC," Orsen said. "Is there anything — any angle, any clause, anything?"

A pause. Paper rustling. "You signed a five-year renewal option in 2019, which expired. The new owner has no obligation to renew. Unless—" another pause, longer, "—unless there's something he wants that isn't money. Is there something he wants that isn't money?"

Orsen looked at the storage room door.

"Yeah," she said. "There is."

"Then I can't help you with the lease. But Orsen? If someone's holding property over your head to make you say something under oath is false—" Priya's voice went careful, lawyer-careful, "—that's coercion. Documented properly, that cuts both ways. Whoever's doing the coercing should think hard about that."

Orsen hung up and stared at the lemon tart under its glass dome.

She thought about Marcus's hand on her shoulder outside the grand jury room, light as a bird landing, saying *you don't have to remember anything harder than what we talked about.* She thought about how young she'd been, how much in love, how thoroughly she'd mistaken obedience for devotion.

And she thought — with a shame so hot it felt like fever — that Delia Voss had no idea how right she was, and no idea how wrong.

Because recanting would mean admitting the testimony was false. And admitting it was false meant admitting whose hands had shaped it. And those hands belonged to a man who still sent her a Christmas card every year, cheerful and unbothered, signed *M.*

* * *

### Delia

Delia spent the afternoon on the beach below the cottage, skipping stones badly, which was a family trait. Danny had been worse. He once hit a pelican, and they'd run half a mile laughing while the bird expressed its opinion from the sky.

Her phone buzzed. Mrs. Okonkwo from the hardware store, who had appointed herself Delia's unofficial intelligence service within forty-eight hours of her arrival.

*She's still open. Made three wedding cakes today. Thought you'd want to know.*

Delia typed back: *Why would I want to know?*

*Because you walked past the window four times, honey. I count things. It's why I'm good at inventory.*

Delia put the phone face-down in the sand and sat there watching the tide come in over the flats where, twenty years ago, two kids had dug clams and argued about whether the moon caused tides or just liked them.

Danny had said the moon just liked them. He'd been wrong about that and right about everything else, and none of it had mattered in a courtroom where a frightened nineteen-year-old girl had pointed her finger at him because a man with a good jawline had told her which direction to point.

Delia hadn't come here for vengeance. She'd told herself that so many times it had almost worn grooves smooth. She'd come because Danny's appeals had run out of road, because their mother had died last spring still saying *he'll be home for Christmas*, and because the only living witness to the lie was baking wedding cakes sixty feet above the tide line, selling happiness by the slice.

But sitting there in the cold gold light, Delia admitted the thing underneath the thing: she didn't actually want Orsen ruined. She wanted Orsen *ruined and then rebuilt* — remade into someone who could stand up in front of a town and say Danny Voss's name without flinching. Ruin was just the demolition phase. Any contractor knew you had to gut a place before you could restore it.

She just hadn't expected the restoration project to have flour on her arms and a laugh like a struck bell that she'd heard exactly once, through the bakery window, and thought about more than was strictly strategic.

* * *

### Orsen

At six the next morning, Delia found the bakery door unlocked and Orsen inside, elbow-deep in dough, surrounded by the smell of cinnamon and the wreckage of a sleepless night.

"You're supposed to lock your door," Delia said. "You live above a woman who hates you."

"You don't hate me." Orsen didn't look up. "Hate requires engagement. What you feel is closer to litigation."

Despite herself — despite the entire architecture of the last ten years — Delia felt her mouth twitch. "Sit down, Thornbury. We're negotiating."

"We negotiated yesterday."

"Yesterday I made an offer. Today we negotiate." Delia pulled out a stool and sat across the counter from her, close enough to see the shadows under Orsen's eyes and the smear of flour on her cheekbone that she clearly didn't know about. "Here are my terms. One: you recant publicly, before the festival closes. Two: you name the circumstances of your original testimony — not necessarily names, but circumstances. Three: in exchange, I sign a ten-year lease at the same rate, and I tear up every copy of the eviction notice in existence."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I've had offers on the building from a developer who wants a frozen yogurt franchise, and I will take it, and I will move somewhere with better weather and worse bread, and Danny will stay in Fairview until he dies, and you will keep making cakes for people who mean their vows, and I genuinely do not know how you'll stand it, but that's not my problem anymore."

The words landed the way she'd intended — hard, clean, final. What she hadn't intended was the way Orsen set down the dough, peeled off her gloves finger by finger, and looked at her with something worse than anger.

Comprehension.

"You think I'm protecting myself," Orsen said quietly. "You think I recanted nothing because I'm comfortable, because my reputation matters more than your brother's freedom. That's the story you've written for ten years. Am I close?"

"It's the story the evidence supports."

"The evidence," Orsen said, "is incomplete." She untied her apron, folded it with maddening precision, and laid it on the counter between them like a flag of some kind — surrender or truce, Delia couldn't tell. "Come upstairs. I'm going to show you something, and then you're going to hate me in a completely different way, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide whether to take my building away."

Delia should have said no. Every instinct honed over a decade of disappointment said *no, don't follow her, don't give her the chance to manage you, she's a professional, managing people is literally her job.*

She followed her up the stairs anyway.

* * *

The apartment above the bakery was small and immaculate except for one long table covered in paper. Letters — no. Not letters. Documents. Grand jury transcripts, photocopied and annotated in three colors of ink. A timeline pinned together with red thread. A photograph of a man Delia recognized instantly from newspaper archives, older now but unmistakable: Marcus Hale, the prosecutor's star investigator, the case that had made his career.

"My testimony wasn't mine," Orsen said. Her voice had gone flat, the voice of someone reading a report about a stranger. "I identified Danny because Marcus spent eleven days making sure I would. I was nineteen. He was my fiancé. He told me what I'd seen, and I believed him, because believing him was easier than believing I'd loved a liar. By the time I understood what I'd done, the verdict was in, and he explained — very reasonably — that recanting would destroy us both. Perjury, obstruction, the whole catalog. So I stayed quiet. For ten years I stayed quiet, and I told myself it was shame, and it was, but it was also fear, and I have never once said that sentence out loud until right now."

The kitchen timer went off in the bakery below. Neither of them moved.

Delia looked at the table — at the years of evidence Orsen had assembled in secret, the annotations, the thread, the obsessive, terrified, hopeful architecture of a conscience that had never actually gone to sleep — and felt the ground shift under a decade of certainty.

"Why didn't you ever answer my letters?" she asked. It came out smaller than she meant it to.

"Because every letter was a mirror," Orsen said, "and I couldn't afford to look. And because—" she stopped, started again. "Because I was afraid that if I opened even one, I'd have to open all of them. And I wasn't ready. I'm still not ready. But I've been ready-adjacent for about eight years, and I am so tired, Delia. I am so tired of being the woman in that courtroom."

Delia's hands had curled into fists without her permission. She made herself open them.

"So here's my counteroffer," Orsen said. "I'll recant. Publicly, before the festival, all of it — including him. But not because you're holding my lease hostage. Because it's true, and because it's overdue, and because if I do it as a ransom payment it'll sound like what it isn't. Give me until the festival. Help me get it right. And when it's done, you can decide whether you still want to be my landlord."

The refrigerator hummed. Downstairs, the timer kept ringing, patient and absurd.

"And if I say no?" Delia asked. "If I want it now, on my terms?"

Orsen met her eyes, and there was no flinch in her at all, and Delia understood suddenly that this — this exact steadiness — was what courage looked like when it had been marinating in guilt for a decade.

"Then you'll get your recantation," Orsen said, "and you'll spend the rest of your life knowing you had to buy it. I don't think you're that woman. I've read your letters' return addresses a hundred times, and I don't think you're that woman."

Delia stared at her. "You read the envelopes."

"I read the envelopes. Ten years of envelopes. It took me until last week to earn the contents."

Outside, the gulls were arguing about something. Inside, Delia Voss stood in her enemy's kitchen and felt the strangest sensation — the sensation of a plan dissolving, and something less certain and far more dangerous taking its place.

"One condition," she said.

Orsen waited.

"When you name him — when you say it in front of the whole town — I want to be standing next to you." Delia heard her own voice go rough. "Ten years I've fought this fight alone. I'm done doing it alone."

Orsen nodded once. Then she went downstairs to rescue whatever she'd left in the oven, and Delia followed, and neither of them mentioned the lease again — which was how, three days later, the town council found them standing side by side at the planning meeting, volunteering — *volunteering*, God help them both — to co-produce the bicentennial wedding festival.

Mrs. Okonkwo, in the third row, counted the seconds they stood too close together, and added it to her inventory.
