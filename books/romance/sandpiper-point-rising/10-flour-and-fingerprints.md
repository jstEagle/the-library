## 10. Flour and Fingerprints

The tasting was in four days, and the festival committee had made one thing abundantly clear: if Orsen Thornbury and I couldn't produce a menu together, the bicentennial wedding festival would be catered by the gas station on Route 9.

Mayor Pruett said this at the council meeting with the serene confidence of a man who believed he was being funny. Nobody laughed. Orsen's jaw did a thing — a small, tight flex, like a drawbridge deciding whether to go up or down — and I found myself thinking, not for the first time that week, that she had a whole weather system living behind her face.

So there we were. Saturday morning, seven a.m., her kitchen.

I'd been inside Thornbury's bakery exactly once before revealing who I was, and that had been to buy a loaf of sourdough and study her hands while she sliced it. Now I stood in the back kitchen wearing an apron she'd thrust at me without ceremony — white, monogrammed *T*, which felt like being asked to wear the enemy's colors — and watched her line up ingredients on the steel counter with the precision of a surgeon prepping for amputation.

"Rules," she said. She didn't look at me. She cracked eggs into a bowl, one-handed, all six without a single shell fragment, and I hated how impressive it was. "One: you don't touch my oven settings. Two: you don't rearrange my station. Three: when I say fold, I mean *fold*, not stir, not beat, not whatever aggressive thing you did to that meringue last Tuesday."

"That meringue was fine."

"It was a war crime." She set the bowl down and finally looked at me. Her eyes were brown, nearly black in the low morning light, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "We are making three things. A lemon cake because the committee chair's wife loves lemon. A honey-almond torte because it photographs well. And your grandmother's plum galette, because you won't shut up about it."

"I mentioned it twice."

"You've mentioned it four times since Monday. Twice to me, once to the florist, once to a child." She picked up her whisk. "So we're making it, and then you can be quiet about plums for the rest of your natural life."

There it was again — that flicker under the frost. I was starting to collect them, these small betrayals of the armor. The almost-smile when I'd called the mayor's bow tie "aggressive." The way she'd said *sit down, you'll bleed on my floor* when I cut my thumb opening a box of festival programs, and then bandaged it herself, brisk and wordless, her fingers warmer than they had any right to be.

Danny used to say I held grudges like other people held babies: carefully, close to the chest, unwilling to put them down even when my arms got tired. Ten years of letters sat in a box in my cottage, every one of them returned unopened, and I told myself I was here for him. For the recantation. For the truth.

But some mornings, standing in flour dust and early light, watching Orsen Thornbury argue with a bag of powdered sugar like it owed her money, I forgot to be angry. And that frightened me more than she did.

* * *

The trouble started — or didn't start; that was the problem — around nine.

She was zesting lemons, and I was rolling out the galette dough, and we'd fallen into something I refused to name. Not conversation. Not peace. Something more like tide: she'd say a thing, I'd say a thing back, and between us the work moved forward without either of us steering.

"My grandmother made this every August," I heard myself say. My hands kept working the dough while my mouth went off-script. "Plums from the tree out back. She'd let me eat the bruised ones over the sink so nobody knew how many I'd stolen."

Orsen's zester paused for half a second. Half a second, then resumed. But I'd learned to read her pauses the way sailors read water.

"Mine sold pies," she said. "Out of a church basement in Harwick. Apple, mostly. She thought anything fancier than apple was showing off."

"Was she right?"

"She was right about everything and wrong about the things that mattered." A short exhale through her nose — the closest thing to a laugh I'd gotten out of her all week. "She'd have hated this festival. All those centerpieces. She'd have called it 'a lot of lace looking for a purpose.'"

This time I laughed. Out loud, unguarded, and the sound startled both of us. Orsen looked up from her lemons with an expression I can only describe as *betrayed by physics* — as though the laugh had escaped her own custody too and she was annoyed at it on my behalf.

"Don't get comfortable," she said.

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"I mean it, Voss. This is—" She gestured with the zester, a wet curl of lemon peel flying off and landing on my sleeve. Neither of us acknowledged it. "This is logistics. Two people stuck on a project. It doesn't mean anything."

"Of course," I said solemnly. "Purely logistical."

"And stop smiling like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you're *winning* something."

I bent back over my dough. "For the record," I said, "you're winning. You've made me wear your apron, follow your rules, and abandon my position on butter temperature. I'm a broken woman."

"The butter should be cold. That's not a position, that's *chemistry*."

"Mm."

"*Mm?*" She set down the zester and turned fully toward me, and there it was — the real face, the one under the wedding-planner polish, young and furious and alive. "Say what you mean, Voss. You've been doing this thing all week where you agree with me in this flat little voice and then do whatever you want anyway. If you have opinions about butter, have them."

I wiped my hands on the apron — her apron, God help me — and met her eyes.

"Fine. Your butter is too cold. You're laminating a galette, not croissants. Cold butter makes a tough crust, and your grandmother would agree with me, and she sounds like a woman who was right about everything."

Silence. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside a gull screamed its opinion about the harbor.

Then Orsen Thornbury — Orsen *Thornbury*, who hadn't smiled at me once in ten days except ironically, and even those had looked painful — laughed. Really laughed. Both hands still dusty with flour, head tipped back, a sound that filled the whole kitchen and knocked something loose in my chest that I'd spent a decade welding shut.

It lasted maybe three seconds. Then she caught herself, pressed her lips together, and turned back to her lemons with her ears going pink.

"That never happened," she said.

"Noted," I said, and my voice came out rougher than I intended.

* * *

We got the lemon cake into the oven by ten, and that's when the rhythm really took hold. I don't know how else to describe it. She'd reach for the vanilla and I'd already have it uncapped, passing it over without looking. I'd need the good rolling pin — the marble one, apparently sacred — and it would arrive at my elbow before I finished asking. We moved around each other in a kitchen built for one the way two people dance who've known the same song their whole lives.

At some point she started humming. Some old standard, half under her breath, and I matched it without meaning to, and when she noticed she stopped, and when I noticed she'd stopped I stopped, and we stood there in the flour-smelling silence like two people caught holding hands.

"Danny hummed," I said.

The words came out before I could stop them. That was the thing about her kitchen — it disarmed me. All that warmth and sugar and steam, and her sleeves rolled up, and a smear of flour on her cheekbone she didn't know about. It was very hard to remember to be a weapon in a place like that.

Orsen went still. Whisk suspended over the torte batter.

"He hummed when he worked the boats," I went on, quieter. "Same three songs, always. Drove the other deckhands crazy. He said a man who works with his hands needs music or he starts thinking, and thinking is how you lose a finger."

"Delia—"

"You don't have to say anything." I turned back to my galette, laying the plum slices in overlapping circles, my grandmother's pattern, my hands steadier than the rest of me. "I'm not doing the thing. I'm not asking you for anything. I just — he existed. Before all of it. He was a person who hummed, and stole plums, and once cried at a dog food commercial, and I need somebody in this town to know that besides me."

The whisk came down slowly against the side of the bowl. Once. Twice. I didn't look at her, but I heard everything in those small sounds — the war she was having with herself, fought entirely in stainless steel.

"What kind of dogs," she said finally.

I blinked. "What?"

"In the commercial. What kind of dogs."

"Golden retrievers. An old one and a puppy. The old one waits by the door."

Her breath went out of her in a long, careful stream. When I glanced over, she wasn't looking at me — she was looking at the window, at the gray light coming off the harbor, and her eyes were bright in a way she'd deny under oath.

"My father waited like that," she said. "After my mother left. Every night, dinner plate set for two, sitting in his chair until midnight. I used to think if I set the table fast enough he'd give up." She picked the whisk back up. "He never gave up. I don't know if that's love or stubbornness. I've never figured out the difference."

"No," I agreed softly. "Neither have I."

We didn't speak for a while after that. But it was a different silence than the ones we usually kept — not a wall but a room, one we were both standing in, and the torte went into the oven, and the galette came out golden and blistered at the edges exactly the way hers used to, and when I pulled it from the rack Orsen leaned in beside me, shoulder almost touching mine, and inhaled.

"Oh," she said. Small. Almost involuntary. "That smells like — okay. Fine. You were right about the plums."

"Say it again slower."

"Don't ruin it."

* * *

We ate lunch standing at the counter, straight from the pan, because neither of us wanted to break the spell by sitting down like civilized people. Galette still warm, sharp-sweet, the crust shattering in that way that means somebody's grandmother is smiling somewhere. Orsen ate with her eyes closed for the first bite, and I watched her do it and felt something in my ribs turn over like a boat coming about.

Here is what I understood, standing there in flour to my elbows: somewhere in the last week, without my permission, I had started seeing her. Not the witness. Not the woman whose signature lived at the bottom of the affidavit that broke my family open. Just — a person. Tired, funny, precise, lonely in a way that matched mine like a key matches a lock it was never cut for.

And here is what frightened me: I didn't know yet whether that made what I had to do easier or impossible.

Because the letters were still in the box. Because Danny was still on the inside. Because whatever this was — this flour-dusted truce, this accidental harmony — it had been built on top of a lie she'd told under oath, and I couldn't let it stand no matter how well she folded meringue, no matter how she laughed with her whole head thrown back, no matter how she looked at me across a counter like I was a question she actually wanted to answer.

"You've got flour," I said, "right there." I touched my own cheekbone to show her.

She scrubbed at the wrong cheek. I reached over before I could talk myself out of it and brushed it away with my thumb — a two-second thing, nothing, a crumb of contact — and she froze under my hand like a deer hearing a twig.

The kitchen got very quiet. The oven ticked. Outside, the gull screamed again, less helpfully this time.

"Delia." Her voice had gone careful. Careful the way ice is careful.

"You had flour," I said, and took my hand back, and picked up my fork, and pretended my pulse was behaving.

She looked at me a moment longer — searching for something, I think, though what she expected to find in my face I couldn't have said. Then she looked down at her plate, and exhaled, and said, in a voice smaller than any I'd heard from her:

"The tasting committee is going to lose their minds over this galette."

"They're going to lose their minds over all of it."

"We make a decent team." She said it to her plate, grudging, like paying a bill she disputed. "Don't let it go to your head."

"Too late."

And she almost smiled — really almost, the corner of her mouth giving up valuable ground — and for one whole minute I let myself believe the week could stay like this. Flour and plums and accidental music.

Then the bell over the front door rang, and Orsen went to see who it was, and I heard her greet whoever it was in her shopkeeper voice, warm and automatic — and then I heard the voice answer.

A man's voice. Smooth, amused, familiar from a decade of newspaper clippings and courtroom sketches.

"Well, if it isn't Sandpiper Point's finest. Orsen Thornbury. Marcus Hale — I hear you've been busy."

The fork slipped out of my hand and hit the floor.
