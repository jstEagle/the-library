## 11. The Festival Gains a Groom

The email arrived on a Tuesday morning, wedged between a cake-tasting confirmation and an invoice from the linen company, and it sat in my inbox for eleven minutes before I opened it because some part of me — the animal part, the part that remembers things the mind would rather file away — already knew what it said.

**Subject: Bicentennial Wedding Festival — Sponsorship Confirmation**

**From: Hollis & Marchetti Events Group**

*Ms. Thornbury,*

*It is our great pleasure to confirm that Hollis & Marchetti will serve as headline sponsor of the Sandpiper Point Bicentennial Wedding Festival. Our founder, Mr. Marcus Hollis, will arrive in town on Thursday to oversee preparations personally. He looks forward to working with you again.*

*Warm regards,*
*Patrice Okafor, Executive Assistant*

I read it three times. The word *again* did something unpleasant in my chest, like a splinter working its way toward the surface.

Marcus Hollis. Not Marchetti anymore — he'd dropped my surname somewhere around year four of his climb, the same year he'd dropped me, though the paperwork had come later and less gracefully. He'd built himself an events empire out of exactly the skills I'd once admired: the charm that made strangers feel chosen, the memory for names, the instinct for what people wanted before they knew they wanted it. I had loved him at twenty-four the way you love a lit match — dazzled, and too close.

And I had testified for him at twenty-five, because he'd asked me to, because his hand had been warm on my shoulder in the courthouse hallway, because I hadn't yet learned to ask why a man planning a robbery needed a witness so badly.

I closed the laptop. I washed my hands even though they were clean. Then I went out front and pulled the festival binder off the shelf and stared at the budget page where, in my own handwriting from six weeks ago, I had written: *Headline sponsor: TBD — pray for someone boring.*

God, apparently, has a sense of humor. It's just never been one I've enjoyed.

* * *

Delia found me at the counter an hour later, flour to my elbows, aggressively shaping sourdough loaves as if the dough had personally insulted my family.

"You're either making bread," she said, leaning against the doorframe, "or committing violence. Hard to tell."

"Both."

She came in properly then, tying on the spare apron without asking — she'd started doing that last week, after the tasting, after we'd stood shoulder to shoulder over a double boiler and discovered that we worked together the way oars work, which is to say better than either of us expected and neither of us was ready to name. She picked up a bench scraper and began dividing dough beside me, easy as anything.

"Council meeting notes," she said. "The banner vendor wants payment up front. Mrs. Alvarado thinks the string quartet should play 'Canon in D' and also that 'Canon in D' is overplayed, and she'd like both positions respected."

"Noted. Filed under impossible."

"And there's one more thing." Her scraper paused. "Patrice Okafor called the town office this morning. Very polite. Very thorough. Asking about venue logistics, security requirements, press access." A beat. "For her boss. Some events guy flying in Thursday. You know him?"

The oven ticked. Somewhere down the street a gull screamed its opinion about something.

"Marcus Hollis," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, like a recording of me. "He used to be Marcus Marchetti. We were engaged. Ten years ago."

Delia set the scraper down very carefully, the way you set down something when you suspect the person next to you might need to grab it.

"The one whose brother—" she started, and stopped, and I watched her do the arithmetic, watch her land on Danny Voss sitting in Fairview Correctional while the man who put him there built a brand on love stories.

"Yes," I said. "That one."

"Orsen." My name in her mouth had gotten gentler over the weeks, but just now it was careful, a hand extended across a table. "Are you okay?"

"I'm fine." I punched the loaf down harder than necessary. "It's fine. It's a business arrangement. He sponsors things. That's what he does. He'll show up Thursday, be devastatingly charming to the council, take credit for everything, and leave Sunday. I've survived him before."

"That's not what I asked."

I looked at her. She was still holding the bench scraper, flour on her cheekbone, watching me with those steady gray eyes that had spent ten years learning exactly how much a person can hide and how little it works.

"No," I admitted. "It isn't."

* * *

Thursday came the way bad weather comes — you can see it building all day, and it arrives anyway.

I was arranging the tasting tables in the community hall when I heard his voice before I saw him, because of course I did. Ten years and a rebrand hadn't changed it: warm, resonant, pitched to carry, the voice of a man who had never once entered a room quietly or doubted he belonged in it.

"—and I told Patrice, if we're doing this, we're doing it *right*. Two hundred years! This town deserves a festival people talk about for the next two hundred."

Applause. Council-member laughter, the bright obliging kind. I kept folding napkins. My hands were steady. I want that noted somewhere, by someone: my hands were completely steady.

"Orsen Marchetti."

Hollis, now. But he'd said Marchetti, deliberately, the old knife turned in the old wound, and when I turned around he was standing six feet away with his arms open slightly, as if the world owed him a hug and he was willing to wait for it.

He'd aged well, which felt unfair. Silver at the temples now, artfully arranged. The same smile — white, calibrated, reaching all the way to his eyes because he'd practiced until it did.

"It's Thornbury," I said. "You know that."

"Force of habit." He stepped in and kissed the air near my cheek, close enough that I caught his cologne, expensive and familiar, and hated that it was familiar. "Look at you. Flour dust and glory. I always said you were the real talent."

"You fired me."

"I *restructured*." He said it lightly, but his eyes didn't stay light; they flicked once, quick and assessing, toward the corner of the hall where Delia stood with a clipboard, and back to me. "Water under the bridge. We're colleagues now. Partners, even. I'm told you're running the whole production."

"The council hired me. And Delia Voss. Co-directors."

"Mm." He smiled wider. "The sister. Small world. She must be thrilled to have you at the helm."

There it was. Under the charm, quick as a fish under dock pilings: the thing underneath. I felt it the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm — not a thought yet, just the body knowing first.

"The council is delighted with the sponsorship," I said evenly. "The budget was in trouble."

"The budget is *always* in trouble. That's why they need me." He plucked a macaron off the nearest table and examined it. "Lavender honey. Ambitious. Is this yours?"

"Delia's recipe."

Something crossed his face — barely a flicker, gone before it landed. "Of course it is." He ate the macaron in two bites, chewed, nodded approval at no one. "Thursday through Sunday, I'm all yours, Orsen. Let's give them a festival to remember."

He said *remember* the way other men say *forget*, and then he was moving off toward the council president, hand extended, laughter arriving ahead of him like a herald, and I stood among my folded napkins and realized my hands were no longer steady at all.

* * *

Delia drove me home. I didn't ask her to; she simply appeared at my elbow after the hall emptied, took the box of napkins out of my arms, and said, "Come on," in a tone that permitted no negotiation, and I followed because following her had recently become a thing my body did without consulting me.

She didn't talk on the drive. She let the windows down and let the salt air do its work, and only when we were parked outside our two cottages — hers and mine, ten feet of grass between them, a distance that had been shrinking all summer — did she say:

"He said your old name. On purpose."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I looked out the windshield at the darkening water beyond the dunes. "Because he wanted to see if I'd flinch. It's how he checks his investments. He does it to everyone — finds the soft spot early, presses it gently, files away how you react. It's not cruelty, exactly. It's inventory."

"And what did he find?"

I laughed, short and hollow. "That I flinch."

Delia was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice had an edge I'd heard only twice before — once about the letters, once about Danny.

"My brother has spent ten years in a cell," she said, "because of testimony you gave. I came here to make you pay for that, and I won't pretend otherwise — you know why I'm here, you signed nothing away. But I've watched you for months now, Orsen. I've baked beside you. And I'm telling you, as someone who knows what fear looks like from the inside—" she turned in her seat to face me fully— "that man scares you more than prison ever scared Danny. What did he hold over you?"

The question sat in the car between us, patient as she was.

"Delia—"

"You don't have to answer tonight." She reached over and squeezed my hand, brief and warm, then let go before I could decide whether to pull away. "But he flew here for a reason. Sponsors don't fly in for festivals this size. Whatever he's checking on — whatever he's afraid you'll say or do — he came to stand in the room while he decides what to do about it."

She got out of the car. I sat there a long time after her porch light came on, watching the tide creep up the sand, thinking about a courthouse hallway ten years ago, a warm hand on my shoulder, and a voice saying, *You don't need to understand, sweetheart. You just need to tell them what you saw.*

I had seen almost nothing. I had said almost everything.

* * *

Friday morning, there was a gift basket waiting outside the bakery door when I unlocked it at five a.m. — cellophane and ribbon, imported chocolate, a bottle of prosecco, and a card in Marcus's slanted, confident hand:

*To new beginnings. So glad we're on the same team again. — M.*

Beneath it, smaller, added as an afterthought or a message, depending on how paranoid I was feeling, and I was feeling quite paranoid indeed:

*Some stories are better left as legends. Ask Delia about the letters sometime.*

I stood on the doorstep in the gray dawn light, holding a basket of goodwill that smelled like a threat, and behind me, from the direction of the cottage next door, I heard a screen door open and close.

Delia was standing on her porch in a robe, coffee mug in hand, watching me with an expression I couldn't read across the grass.

"Orsen," she called softly. "You'd better come see this. There's a letter on my porch too."
