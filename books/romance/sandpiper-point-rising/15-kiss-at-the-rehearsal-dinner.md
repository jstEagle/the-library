## 15. Kiss at the Rehearsal Dinner

The rehearsal dinner was supposed to be the easy part.

That's what I told the town council, what I told Delia, what I told myself every night for three weeks while I color-coded seating charts in my sleep. Rehearsal dinners are controlled chaos — a few toasts, a run-through of the processional, forty people pretending to care about which side of the aisle they sit on. After the tasting fiasco (the less said about the collapsed pavilion and the fire department, the better) and the invitation printer that had eaten an entire order of two hundred cards and spat out one card reading *BICENTENNIAL WEDDING FESTIVL*, I figured we were due for something simple.

The universe, as it turns out, keeps its own ledger.

* * *

It started raining at four o'clock. Not a polite coastal drizzle but a full-throated October gale that came off the water sideways and turned the harbor lawn — our venue, our *crown jewel* venue, the one I'd promised the council would showcase Sandpiper Point at its most romantic — into something resembling a car wash with seagulls.

By five, the tent was gone. Not damaged. *Gone.* I watched it cartwheel down the pier like a great white ghost with somewhere better to be, and I stood there in the rain holding a clipboard that was dissolving in my hands, and I thought: *this is it, this is how they'll remember me, the woman who lost a tent to the sea.*

"Orsen."

Delia appeared beside me, hood up, flour still dusting her sleeve from the emergency bread delivery she'd been hauling since noon. She looked at the empty stakes where the tent had been, then at me, and she didn't say anything comforting or useless. She just said:

"How many people?"

"Ninety-six confirmed. Maybe a dozen walk-ins."

"And the dinner?"

"Catered. Cold by now."

She nodded slowly, the way she did when she was turning a problem over, and then she looked past me, up the hill, at the bakery. At my bakery. Our bakery, if you believed the lease negotiations, which I tried not to.

"The ovens are hot," she said. "I've got sixty pounds of dough proofing for tomorrow. And the grange hall has tables."

"The grange hall has *linoleum* and a mural of the founding families that makes everyone look like they're being exorcised."

"It has a roof," Delia said. "Which is more than we've got." She was already walking. "Call your caterer. Tell them to bring everything to the bakery. We'll do it potluck style — my bread, whatever people bring, and if anyone complains about the mural, tell them the founding families died so we could eat lasagna indoors."

And that — that offhand joke, delivered while she marched uphill into the wind like a small furious general — was the moment I stopped being able to pretend I didn't feel what I felt.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about disaster: it strips away performance. For three weeks Delia and I had orbited each other with exquisite politeness, two women sharing a clipboard and a history neither of us named. She'd hand me a tasting spoon; I'd take it without touching her fingers. She'd make a joke about Marcus's sponsorship banner (he'd insisted on gold lettering, because of course he had); I'd laugh a half-second too late. We were careful. Careful the way people are careful around a stove they know is hot.

But there is no room for careful when ninety-six damp townspeople descend on a grange hall at six p.m. expecting a rehearsal dinner.

We worked like we'd done this forever. She ran the kitchen; I ran the room. When the caterer's truck got stuck on Harbor Road, she sent bread out the back door while I redirected guests through the front. When old Mrs. Abernathy announced loudly that the mural's depiction of Ezekiel Sandpiper was "an insult to his memory and also possibly a crime," Delia appeared at my elbow with a tray of rolls and said, "Ezekiel also once tried to tax the tide pools, so let's not canonize him," and Mrs. Abernathy laughed for the first time since 2019.

At some point around eight, during the toast from the mayor that was running long enough to qualify as a filibuster, I found myself standing next to Delia at the serving table, both of us stealing olives like teenagers, and she bumped my shoulder with hers — deliberately, I realized. Deliberately.

"Not bad," she said, surveying the room. Ninety-six people eating bread and catered lasagna under a mural of haunted founders, and somehow it looked — warm. Lit by the string lights I'd salvaged from the tent wreckage. Someone had started a playlist on a phone propped against the salt shaker.

"We should be panicking," I said. "There's a whole ceremony tomorrow and half our infrastructure is in the bay."

"There's always another disaster," Delia said. "This one turned out okay." She looked at me then, really looked, and the noise of the room went strange and distant, like I'd stepped underwater. "You did that. You held ninety-six people together with a clipboard and sheer stubbornness."

"You held the kitchen together with sixty pounds of dough."

"Team effort," she said softly, and the word *team* sat between us like something fragile and new.

I want to say I thought it through. I want to say I weighed it — the letters, the lease, the ten years of silence sitting between us like a third person at the table. But the truth is simpler and more humiliating: the mayor finally finished talking, someone put on a slow song, the string lights caught the flour still in Delia's hair, and I leaned in before my brain could file an objection.

She met me halfway.

Her mouth tasted like salt and the espresso she'd been drinking all evening, and her hand came up to my jaw with a gentleness that undid me more than the kiss itself, because Delia Voss had every reason to be hard with me and chose, in that moment, not to be. The room kept moving around us — somebody laughed, the phone skipped a song — and I kissed her back and felt, for about four seconds, like a person who deserved good things.

Then I remembered.

Not everything. Just enough. Marcus's hand on my shoulder outside the grand jury room. *Just tell them what we talked about, Orsen. That's all you have to do.* The sound of my own voice saying words I hadn't understood yet were a knife. And underneath all of it, the thing I couldn't outrun: the man sitting in Fairview Correctional because of me, and the woman kissing me now who had written him letters every month for ten years, letters I'd left in a box unopened because opening them would mean becoming someone who had to *do* something about it.

I pulled away.

It wasn't graceful. It was the kind of pull-away that has physics to it, a flinch, my hand coming up between us like a traffic barrier. Delia went still. Her eyes searched my face, and I watched her assemble the story from the pieces — not confused, never confused, she'd had a decade to learn to read people through walls.

"Orsen—"

"I can't." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "I'm sorry. I — not here. Not like this."

"Like what?" she asked quietly. "Like something real?"

And God, that was the wound of it, because yes. Like something real. Real things required truth, and truth required testimony, and testimony meant burning down the last shreds of the life I'd built on top of a lie — the reputation, the business, the carefully maintained fiction that Sebastian Marchetti's favorite wedding planner had simply *made a mistake once*, long ago, and moved on.

"I have to check on the dessert service," I said, which was the coward's line, the worst possible line, and I took it anyway.

I walked to the kitchen and stood in front of the oven's open glow like it could absolve me, and through the doorway I heard the slow song end and another begin, and I did not look back at where Delia was standing.

* * *

We closed the hall at eleven. The cleanup was quiet, efficient, wordless — we'd gotten good at wordless. She stacked chairs; I swept. Outside, the storm had blown itself out, and the sky over the harbor had that scrubbed-clean blackness that comes after weather, stars sharp as tacks.

When the last chair was stacked, Delia shrugged into her coat and paused at the door.

"For what it's worth," she said, not looking at me, "the kiss wasn't a strategy. I need you to know that. Whatever else is true between us — the letters, the lease, all of it — that wasn't part of any plan."

"I know," I said. That was the terrible part. I knew.

"And tomorrow," she said, "when you're deciding whether to keep running from that kitchen, maybe ask yourself what exactly you're afraid of. Because I've watched you face down collapsing tents and Mrs. Abernathy, and you don't scare easy, Orsen Thornbury. So it isn't the kiss." She opened the door onto the cold clean night. "It's what telling the truth would cost. And I think you already know it costs less than staying silent. You just haven't let yourself count it yet."

The door closed behind her, gentle as anything.

I stood alone in the grange hall under the exorcised founding families, and I reached into my bag for my keys, and my fingers found instead the corner of an envelope — Danny Voss's eleventh letter, the one I'd carried in my bag for two weeks now without opening, moving it from purse to coat to tote like a stone I couldn't set down and couldn't lift.

Tomorrow was the festival. Tomorrow, Marcus would be on that stage in his gold-lettered glory, shaking hands, playing the benefactor.

And tomorrow night, when the lanterns went out over Sandpiper Point, I was going to have to decide whose version of me got to survive.

I took out the letter. I didn't open it. Not yet.

But I stopped hiding it — and that, I thought, walking out into the cold, might be the first honest thing I'd done in ten years.
