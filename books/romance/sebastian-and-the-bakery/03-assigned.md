## 3. Assigned

The flyer had been pinned to the community board at the public library for so long that its corners had yellowed into little curls of toast. *WRITE TO A NEIGHBOR*, it said, in a font that suggested someone's nephew had designed it for free. *Bridgewell Bakery Community Outreach — Anonymous Pen-Pal Program. Combat isolation. Share your week. All letters routed through the bakery. No names required.*

Lior Halloran had read it three times before she admitted to herself that she was reading it on purpose.

She was supposed to be at her desk, forty blocks south, inside the glass-walled annex of Voss Hospitality Group where the air smelled like nothing at all—filtered, temperature-controlled, scrubbed of every human scent except the faint vanilla of the candles Marguerite liked burned in the conference room during dictation sessions. She was supposed to be drafting Chapter Seven: *The First Restaurant*, which meant she was actually standing in a library in a neighborhood she'd only ever seen through the window of a rideshare, holding a flyer about letter-writing like it was a clue.

It wasn't a clue. It was research texture. That was the phrase she'd used with herself, and she stuck to it the way she stuck to all her best rationalizations: firmly, and with excellent grammar.

Marguerite's memoir needed grounding. The woman narrated her own life the way she plated food—all height, all gloss, nothing you could actually grip—and Lior had spent six weeks trying to anchor the prose in something real. The problem was that Marguerite's idea of real was a tasting menu. When Lior asked about the neighborhood where the first restaurant still stood, Marguerite waved a hand lacquered in rings and said, "Oh, darling, write something atmospheric. Cobblestones. A sense of *arriving*." As if the street were a set piece that existed solely because Marguerite had once deigned to park a restaurant on it.

So: texture. The bakery sat four blocks from that first restaurant, close enough that Marguerite's book would inevitably brush against it, and if Lior spent an hour or two understanding what the block felt like from the ground—if she learned how the people who actually lived there talked about their days—then Chapter Seven might stop floating. That was all this was. An assignment she'd assigned herself.

She took a tab off the flyer, wrote her first name and a post office box she'd opened that morning under the name "L. Marlowe," and dropped it into the bakery's letter slot on her way out, feeling slightly ridiculous, like a woman mailing herself into a plot device.

* * *

The Bridgewell Bakery at seven in the morning was a different country from the Voss annex. Where the annex was silent and scented with nothing, the bakery roared. Not loudly—the roar was made of small things: the tick of the deck oven cooling, the wet slap of dough being turned out onto a floured bench, a radio playing something with horns, low, half-swallowed by steam. The whole room breathed warmth. It smelled the way the annex refused to smell: like yeast and caramelized sugar and scorched flour at the edges of the oven, like work.

Lior ordered a coffee she didn't want because she wanted a reason to stand there, and watched the counter woman—a broad-shouldered Black woman with flour dusted up one forearm like a tattoo gone shy—wrap a loaf in paper for an old man who clearly came every day, because neither of them said anything at all, just nodded, just smiled, the shorthand of years.

*This,* Lior thought, and then caught herself, because *this* was exactly the kind of sentence Marguerite paid her to produce. *This is what bread means.* She drank the coffee. It was very good, which annoyed her, because she'd been prepared to be professionally unimpressed.

The program itself, according to the woman at the counter—Deni, she said, no last name offered, though her name tag said DENI OKAFOR, so the last name was right there if you could read—worked like this: volunteers wrote once a week, letters dropped off or mailed to the shop; the staff sorted them to participants on a list kept in a binder behind the register; nobody got anybody's full name until both sides asked, and even then Deni played gatekeeper like a bouncer with a soft spot. "Mostly it's folks who can't get out much," she said, sliding a ruled index card across the counter. "Mr. Abernathy, he's eighty-nine and his hip's a liar. Mrs. Ferreira mostly wants somebody to complain about her son-in-law to. You'll get whoever's next on the list."

"Whoever's next," Lior repeated.

"We don't matchmake. It's not eHarmony, it's the mail." Deni looked at her a beat too long, the way people did when they were deciding whether Lior was the type who'd quit in three weeks. "You write back regular, that's the whole contract. One-sided pen pals are worse than none. You know what that is? That's just a stranger ignoring you with extra steps."

"I'll write back," Lior said, and meant it, which surprised her. She was good at meaning things quietly, at meaning them without letting them cost anything visible. It was practically her job description.

The card Deni gave her had a participant number—P-14—and nothing else. No age, no address, no story. Just a number and a stamp-sized note in pencil: *Prefers letters. Grumpy. Good writer though.*

"Grumpy," Lior said.

"He'll deny it," Deni said. "That's how you'll know."

* * *

That night, in the apartment she kept immaculate the way other people kept diaries—everything in its place so that nothing inside her had to be—Lior sat at her kitchen table with two documents open in front of her, and the contrast between them was almost funny.

On the left: the manuscript draft of Chapter Seven, printed single-spaced, Marguerite's voice rising off the page in its usual key. *I looked out at that scrappy little street and I knew—I *knew*—that I could make it sing.* On the right: a blank sheet of good paper she'd bought specifically for this, cream-colored, heavy stock, because if she was going to do a thing she was going to do it properly, and because stationery was the one luxury she'd never trained out of herself.

Dear P-14, she wrote. Then stopped.

The trouble was that she didn't know how to be casual on paper. Every letter she'd ever written had been load-bearing—an application, an apology, a pitch, a retraction. Her hand didn't know how to make marks that weren't trying to get her something. She thought about what Deni had said, *one-sided pen pals are worse than none*, and understood that the entire transaction here was the opposite of everything she did for a living. For a living, she took other people's lives and made them legible, polished, profitable. This required her to put down something unpolished and give it away.

She tried again.

*Dear P-14 —*

*I signed up for this partly as research and partly because my weeks have started to look identical to each other, which I'm told is a warning sign. I work with words. I can't say more than that yet, per the rules, but I'll tell you that today I spent nine hours making a sentence about a lemon sound important, and I would like, very much, to hear about a day that contained actual lemons.*

*What does your week look like?*

She read it over. It was honest in a way that startled her slightly—it had slipped past her internal editor wearing a disguise—and she decided not to examine that too closely. She sealed it, addressed it to the bakery, and walked it to the corner mailbox at eleven at night in the rain, because apparently she was now a person who did that.

* * *

Eight days later, a letter came back, forwarded in a plain envelope with BRIDGEWELL stamped in the corner and a note from Deni on a sticky flag: *Told you. Grumpy.*

The handwriting hit her before the words did. It was a strong hand, slanted forward like it was leaning into the page, ink pressed hard enough to emboss the paper's back. Someone who wrote the way some people cooked—with their whole arm, no apology.

*Dear L.,*

*First: I know what "research" means. My sister uses that word too, usually right before she signs me up for something. Second: actual lemons are overrated. They're a garnish with a publicist. If you want a fruit with integrity, grow a fig tree and then spend August defending it from birds. That's a day that contains something.*

*My week: I get up at 3:40. I feed a sourdough starter older than most marriages. I bake until my shoulders file a complaint, I sell what I baked, and around 4 p.m. I have a recurring argument with a man in a suit about a building. He always wins the argument and I always show up for the next round, which between us is the definition of stubbornness, and I've decided stubbornness counts as a personality.*

*You make sentences about lemons sound important. Fine. But here's my question, since we're being honest-ish: important for whom? Because I've noticed that when people with money describe a neighborhood, they don't describe it. They describe what they're going to do to it. There's a word they use—"vibrant"—and I've learned to flinch at it. Vibrant is what a place is called right before it stops being itself.*

*Anyway. Tell me about the lemon sentence. Was it true? That's the only test I've got.*

*S.*

Lior read it twice, sitting on the floor of her hallway because the mail had come while she was carrying groceries and she'd opened it standing up and then simply... descended. She was aware, distantly, that she was smiling at a piece of paper in an empty apartment, and she filed that information away for later examination and declined to act on it.

*Lemons are a garnish with a publicist.* God. She'd spent the afternoon writing Marguerite's line about how the neighborhood's "humble storefronts gave way to something luminous," and here was a stranger calling garnishes publicists, and both sentences were about the same four blocks, and only one of them was true.

She also noted—he would never know how precisely she noted—that he hadn't named the man in the suit, hadn't named the building, hadn't given her a single usable fact. Either he was naturally discreet or he suspected, in some general way, that words travel. She found she respected it. She also found, with a small cold prickle she chose not to name, that she hoped the building wasn't anywhere near Voss's first location. The city was large. The odds were long. She put the thought down like a hot pan and went to answer him.

*Dear S.,*

*The lemon sentence was not true. It was persuasive, which is a different thing, and I appreciate that you asked, because nobody in my working life asks. We are all in agreement about the persuading. It's like working in a country where everyone speaks the same language and no one says anything.*

*A starter older than most marriages—now that's a sentence worth keeping. How old? And don't say "older than yours," I can feel you preparing it.*

*Tell me about the argument with the man in the suit. You don't have to name names. Just tell me what he says, and what you say back. I collect arguments. It's a professional hazard.*

*—L.*

* * *

They fell into a rhythm the way water finds a drain—inevitably, and with a certain amount of noise.

His letters came roughly weekly, thick, sometimes written on the backs of order slips when paper ran short, and they were nothing like what she'd expected. She had expected—she could admit this now—a lonely retiree, gentle and rambling, grateful for any voice at all. What she got instead was a correspondent who wrote like a man arguing with himself and winning, funny in a dry, sideways way, prickly about developers the way other men were prickly about sports referees, capable of describing a proofing basket with more tenderness than most people managed for their children.

*"The starter is sixty-one years old,"* he wrote in the third exchange. *"It belonged to my teacher, who got it from hers. It has survived two moves, one divorce (mine), a rat incident I will describe only if you promise never to look at me the same way, and every winter since 1963. People ask me why I don't buy one of those instant yeasts and I ask them why they don't trade their grandmother for a nice new one."*

And: *"The man in the suit said today that our block is 'underperforming.' Underperforming. Like the block is a stock he owns. I told him the block isn't underperforming, it's just not performing for HIM, and there's a difference, and he wrote something on his clipboard, which is what cowards do instead of answering."*

And once, briefly, almost against his will: *"Some mornings the light comes through the front windows at 6 and hits the cases and for about four minutes the whole room looks like something in a painting, and I think, okay, fine, this is why. Then the light moves and it's just a room with flour in it again and I get back to work."*

Lior answered all of it. She told him about the lemon sentence and then, carefully, about other sentences—about a career spent making other people's voices sound like theirs but better, though she kept the specifics blurred, kept "work with words" doing all the lifting. She told him she'd once wanted to be a reporter, and stopped there, at the edge of the cliff she never showed anyone, and he didn't push, which was its own kind of courtesy. She told him about the rain, about the library, about a pigeon outside her window she'd named after a minor French philosopher. She caught herself, on a Thursday, composing a letter in her head during a dictation session while Marguerite described her "spiritual journey through citrus," and realized she was smiling into her stenographer's silence, and that Deni's binder now held more of her true sentences than her own desk did.

That was the part she kept turning over at night, the part she examined the way you probe a sore tooth: these letters were supposed to be research. Texture. Color for Chapter Seven. And instead she was telling a stranger numbered P-14 things she hadn't told her agent, her sister, or the mirror. There was no byline in it. There was nothing in it. That appeared to be precisely why it worked.

She should have felt guilty about the asymmetry—that he was giving her his block, his mornings, his war, and she was giving him a curated version of herself with the dangerous rooms locked. She did feel it, a little. But she told herself the lock was temporary. Everyone's was. She'd open the doors eventually, or she wouldn't, and either way the letters would survive, because letters were the one form of intimacy she trusted: slow, revisable, survivable. You could be brave on paper at your own pace.

On a gray Tuesday, Deni handed her the newest envelope with an expression Lior couldn't read. "He asked a question this time," Deni said. "A real one. Been saving it up, I think."

The letter was shorter than usual. The handwriting leaned harder into the page.

*Dear L.,*

*A question, since you collect them. The man in the suit came back with paperwork this time, which means the argument is entering the phase where it stops being an argument. So here's what I want to know, and answer honestly, because you're the only person I write to who deals in words for a living:*

*When people like you write about places like mine—when it ends up in print, glossy, with a quote from somebody important—does the place ever survive it? Or is the article just the paperwork arriving early?*

*I ask because something's coming. I can smell it the way I smell a loaf ten minutes before it burns. And I'd like to know, from someone on the other side of the page, whether words are weather or whether they're the storm.*

*S.*

Lior sat with it for a long time. Outside her window the city did its evening glitter, all those lit windows she'd never knocked on. Somewhere in a drawer she had a folder of drafts with a chapter in them about humble storefronts giving way to something luminous, and for the first time the sentence tasted like ash, and she didn't let herself ask why.

She pulled out the cream stationery, and began: *Dear S.—Honest answer? Both.*
