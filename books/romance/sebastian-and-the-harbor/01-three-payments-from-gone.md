## 1. Three Payments from Gone

The ledger did not care that the plums were perfect.

Ansel Zhou knew this the way she knew her own hands — flour in the knuckle creases, a burn scar shaped like a comma on her left wrist — and still she stood at the counter of Salt & Sugar at six in the morning, turning one of those plums over like it might change its mind about being beautiful. It was a Santa Rosa, deep as a bruise, dusted with that silvery bloom that meant it had been picked ripe and not picked early and shipped. The farmer at the Saturday market had let her taste one. Ansel had closed her eyes. She had said, *I'll take twelve pounds.*

She had not done the math until she was home.

Twelve pounds of Santa Rosa plums: forty-one dollars. Forty-one dollars was half a day's electricity. Was a third of what she owed the flour supplier, who had stopped calling her by name last month and started calling her "the account." Was one of the three payments standing between her and gone.

Three payments from gone. She'd started counting that way in June, when the bank's letter arrived with its cheerful sans-serif font, the kind of font designed to make foreclosure feel like a newsletter. July. August. September. If she could hold on through September — if the summer tourists came up the coast the way they always did, if the plum tarts sold, if the harbor stayed busy enough to fill the café with salt-sticky families wanting lemonade and something sweet — then October would arrive and she would still be here, wiping down this counter, burning this particular toast, knowing which floorboard near the register squeaked in B-flat.

Her grandmother had opened this café before Ansel was born. Her grandmother had fed half the fishermen on this stretch of harbor through two recessions and one hurricane, and she had done it with a rule Ansel had inherited along with the recipe box: *No one leaves hungry, and no one pays for what they can't.* The second half of the rule was currently killing her. Old Mr. Ibarra came in every Tuesday and ordered coffee he hadn't been able to afford since his pension went sideways, and Ansel charged him for a small and gave him a large, and the ledger absorbed the difference the way the sea absorbs rain — silently, endlessly, without ever filling up.

She set the plum down. She tied her apron. She got to work, because that was the only thing she'd ever been good at that didn't require anyone else's permission.

* * *

By ten, the tart was a problem.

Not a failure — Ansel did not fail things, she *revised* them — but a problem. The frangipane wanted more almond; the plums had given off so much juice that the pastry underneath had gone soft in the center, surrendering where it should have held. She pulled the second attempt out of the oven at 10:40 and stood over it while it cooled, arms crossed, glaring at it the way her ex-fiancé used to say she glared at people who disappointed her. *You look at croissants the way other women look at cheating husbands,* Daniel had told her once, laughing, back when his laughing still felt like a gift instead of an invoice.

Daniel. She put him away again, the way she did, in the drawer where she kept things she'd already paid too much for.

The third tart came out at noon and it was right. The plums had fanned themselves into overlapping rows, glossy and dark, their edges caramelized into little amber curls. The crust held. When she cut the test slice, the layers separated cleanly — butter, almond, fruit, butter — and the smell rose up around her like an argument she'd finally won.

"Okay," she told it quietly. "Okay. You're worth forty-one dollars."

"You talking to the tart or the plums?"

Ansel startled so badly she nearly dropped the knife. Theo from the bait shop was leaning on the pass-through window, grinning, a man whose entire personality was weather reports and opinions about bait.

"The tart," she said. "The plums know what they did."

"Whatever it is, I'll take two slices. Mei wants one after her shift."

He paid actual money, which felt like a small miracle, and left with a paper bag that Ansel watched all the way out the door, as if affection were visible and might leak out of it.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, running a place like this. You spent your whole life feeding people. You learned their orders, their kids' names, which ones took sugar and which ones took grief with their coffee. And they loved you for it — she knew they loved her, Theo and Mei and old Mr. Ibarra and the kayak instructors who came in sandy and loud — but love wasn't a currency the bank accepted, and somewhere along the way Ansel had gotten very good at giving and catastrophically bad at receiving. When Mrs. Okafor brought her a container of jollof rice last month, unasked, just because, Ansel had nearly cried into it and then spent twenty minutes insisting she'd pay her back, could pay her back, would bring her a whole tart tomorrow, no charge, please don't —

Mrs. Okafor had looked at her with such pity that Ansel had stopped mid-sentence.

*Let people feed you sometimes,* her grandmother used to say. *A kitchen is a circle, not a door.*

But her grandmother had also died owing nothing to anybody, and Ansel intended to do the same, and these two ambitions appeared to be at war.

* * *

At 4:50, ten minutes before close, the bell above the door rang, and Ansel felt the small, stupid lift in her chest that she refused to examine.

He came every day. That was the thing. Every single day since April, at ten minutes to closing, when the case was picked over and everything left was technically yesterday's stock — the morning's croissants gone soft, the canelés sweating slightly in their molds, the last two slices of whatever tart survived the afternoon. He never haggled. He never asked what was fresh. He bought the day-olds at the discount price without a flicker of embarrassment, thanked her in a voice low enough that she sometimes leaned forward to catch it, and left.

He was tall in a way that seemed apologetic about itself, like he'd been taught to take up less room than he did. Dark hair pushed back by hands that were clearly working hands — there was sawdust in the creases of them most days, and once, memorably, a smear of varnish along one forearm that he'd tried to scrub off in her bathroom sink and mostly failed. He wore flannel even in warm weather, sleeves rolled, forearms bare, and he moved carefully, the way big men do when they've decided to be gentle about it.

He worked at the boatyard two piers down. She'd learned that much from Theo, who learned everything from everyone. Restorer, Theo said. Not a shipwright exactly — he fixed up old boats, the wooden ones, the ones everyone else had given up on. Took the rotting hulls nobody wanted and made them seaworthy again. Theo said he was good. Theo said he didn't talk much. Theo said his name was Sebastian Hale, and then Theo had waggled his eyebrows and Ansel had thrown a brioche at him.

"Closing time," she said now, by way of greeting, though they both knew the ritual. "Everything's day-old."

"I know."

"It's not — " she gestured vaguely at the case, at the slightly deflated canelés, at the world. "It's not my best work."

Sebastian Hale looked at the case the way she'd seen him look at boats, she suspected. Like something worth restoring. Then he looked at her, briefly, and away.

"I'll take whatever's left," he said. "And a coffee, if it's not too much trouble."

The coffee was fresh. The coffee was always fresh, because she made a new pot at 4:45 every day and had been doing so since roughly the second week of April, and if either of them acknowledged this out loud she thought she might die of it.

She boxed up two croissants, a canelé, and the heel-end slice of the plum tart — the ugly slice, the test slice, the one with the slightly soft center from batch two that she'd kept back out of some stubbornness she couldn't name. She rang him up for the day-old price plus coffee. He put a five in the tip jar anyway, which he did every day, which drove her insane, because the tip jar was supposed to be for tourists.

"Thank you," he said.

"You're going to get scurvy," she heard herself say. "Eating day-old pastry every day. No vitamins. You need vegetables. Someone should feed you properly."

The words escaped before she could vet them, and then hung in the air between them, absurd, too warm, entirely unlike her usual closing-time script. Sebastian blinked. For a moment something moved across his face — surprise, maybe, or the expression of a man who has just been offered something he'd stopped letting himself want.

"Someone should," he agreed, quietly. He picked up his box. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and said, "The plum tart smells incredible. Whatever you're doing different today."

"Forty-one dollars of plums," Ansel said. "Don't tell my accountant."

"You have an accountant?"

"I have a ledger and a prayer. Same thing."

And there it was — the almost-smile. It happened rarely, maybe once a week, and it was small and crooked and it undid her more than she was prepared to admit, so she turned to the espresso machine and pretended to be fascinated by descaling it until the bell rang and he was gone.

* * *

After close, she sat on the back step with the ledger open on her knees and the harbor going gold and then violet in front of her, gulls arguing over something in the shallows. The numbers hadn't changed since morning. They weren't going to. Three payments. Sixty-some days. A number of plum tarts that would require her to sell pastry to every resident of the county twice over.

Her phone buzzed. Her sister.

Ansel stared at it. Calls from Vivian were never casual; Vivian texted in paragraphs and called only when there was news, and news in their family was usually someone else's good fortune arriving wrapped in Ansel's obligation to celebrate it.

She let it ring out. Then she listened to the voicemail, because she was a glutton for punishment, and Vivian's voice filled the little kitchen, bright and breathless:

*"Ans! Okay, don't freak out — reunion's confirmed for the twenty-third, Nai Nai's house, everyone's coming. Also — don't be mad — Daniel's bringing his fiancée. Yes, THAT Daniel. Auntie Ru already asked me if you're seeing anyone, and Mom's doing that face, so. Bring someone or prepare to be pitied, is all I'm saying. Love you! Call me back!"*

Ansel sat very still on the step. A gull screamed triumphantly about something. Down at the boatyard, one light was still on in the long shed, and she found herself looking at it without meaning to, the way you look at a lighthouse.

Bring someone, or be pitied.

She thought about Daniel's laugh. About Auntie Ru's questions delivered like scalpels. About her mother's face, that particular soft devastation, *my poor Ansel, alone with her ovens.* She thought about how she'd rather eat the ledger than walk into that house unaccompanied.

Then she thought about a man with sawdust on his hands who bought day-old croissants every night at closing, who tipped too much, who fixed broken boats for a living, and who had said, tonight, in that low careful voice: *someone should.*

Ansel looked at the ledger. She looked at the light in the boatyard shed.

"Oh no," she said aloud, to no one, to the gulls, to the harbor. "Oh, absolutely not."

She was already reaching for her keys.
