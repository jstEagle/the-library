## 1. Debts Instead of Grapes

The frost had come in the night, the first hard one, and Vesper Winterbourne walked the rows before sunrise because she couldn't sleep and because someone had to see what it had done.

She pulled her father's old canvas jacket tighter around her shoulders. It still smelled faintly of sulfur and pipe smoke, though he'd been dead six years now, and the jacket had been hers for five of those. The gravel crunched under her boots in the dark, and her breath came out in small ghosts that dissolved as fast as she made them.

Row twelve was the worst of it. The Chardonnay — her grandfather's pride, planted in 1974, the block that had won the medal that hung crooked in the tasting room — had turned the color of wet cardboard. The leaves hung on the vines like things that had given up but hadn't yet let go. She pressed her thumb into a cluster of grapes and the skin split too easily, the juice running thin and brownish over her knuckle. She wiped it on her jeans without looking at it. She'd stopped looking at the fruit in September. It was easier that way.

"Come on," she said quietly, to the vines, to the vineyard, to nobody. "Hold on one more season."

They wouldn't. She knew it the way you know a thing in your body before your mind agrees to carry it — the way you know a fever is coming, the way you know a phone call is bad news before you answer. The irrigation system had failed in July and she'd patched it with parts salvaged from a tractor that had died in 2011, and the patch had held, mostly, except where it hadn't, and the west blocks had gone three weeks without water in the worst heat of the decade. The vines hadn't died outright. They'd just stopped trying.

She walked the rest of the rows anyway. It felt like a duty, the way visiting someone in a hospital is a duty — not because it helps them, but because not going would be a kind of abandonment, and she had abandoned enough things already. Her brother, Daniel, had left in the spring. Sold his share of the vineyard to her for one dollar and a promise never to call him about it again, and then moved to Sacramento to sell insurance, and now he sent her a text on the first of every month that said *You sure?* and nothing else. She never answered. Answering would start a conversation, and conversations with Daniel ended the same way every time: with him being right.

The sky was going gray-blue over the eastern ridge when she came around the winery building, and that was when she saw the paper on the door.

She stopped. For a moment she just stood there, ten feet away, her hands in her pockets, looking at the white rectangle of it — a standard legal notice, printed on a standard printer, pinned to the oak door her great-grandfather had hung by hand — as if by not approaching it she could make it not be there. But it was there. It had been there at six o'clock last night, when she'd locked up, or it hadn't, and someone had come in the dark to put it there, which was its own kind of message.

She walked the last ten feet and read it.

She'd read foreclosure notices before. There had been two others over the years — one she'd bought her way out of with the insurance settlement from the fire that took the east barn, one she'd delayed through eighteen months of paperwork and a lawyer in Healdsburg who charged her four hundred dollars a month to keep the bank at bay and then moved to Arizona. This one was different. This one said the word *final* in it. This one had a date — December 14th — that was forty-one days away, and an amount at the bottom that she read three times because the first two times her eyes slid off it, the way a hand slides off a hot pan.

Two hundred and sixty-one thousand dollars. Plus penalties. Plus the second lien, which she'd almost forgotten existed, the one Daniel had co-signed before he left, the one that made his monthly text messages make a certain grim sense.

Vesper stood in the cold with the paper in front of her for a long time. A crow landed on the fence post by the equipment shed and watched her with the flat, patient interest of a creature that has seen this before, many times, in many places, and knows how it ends.

"You don't get to have an opinion," she told it.

It didn't leave. She went inside.

* * *

The tasting room smelled of dust and old oak and, underneath it, the ghost of ten thousand afternoons — wine and bread and her mother's lavender soap, though her mother had been gone even longer than her father. Vesper didn't turn on the lights. She knew the room by feel, by the creak of the third floorboard and the place where the bar's edge caught her hip if she cut the corner wrong, and she went behind the bar and knelt and opened the low cabinet where the good glasses used to live, before she'd sold most of them, and pulled out a shoebox.

Not a shoebox, exactly. It had once held a case of the '09 Merlot, in miniature, a promotional thing the distributor had sent, and for ten years it had held something worth considerably more to her than the wine ever had.

Letters. Forty-one of them. She knew the count because she'd counted last winter, on the anniversary, which was a thing she did and did not admit to doing. Ten years of letters, arriving roughly three or four times a year, always postmarked from cities she'd never been to — Sacramento, Portland, once from Chicago — always typed, because the handwriting, she'd learned early, was deliberately, carefully neutral, the kind of hand you develop writing court documents for thirty years, though she hadn't known that then. Always unsigned. Always signed, rather, with the same impossible name: *A Friend of the Vine.*

The first one had come eleven days after the trial ended.

She didn't need to reread that one. She knew it by heart, the way you know a scar — *I watched the proceedings and I want you to know that not everyone in that courtroom believed what they were instructed to believe. Your father's name will outlive the verdict. So will yours, though I suspect you cannot see that from where you're standing. Keep the vineyard if you can. Keep it even if you can't.* — and the strange, fierce comfort of it, arriving when the newspapers were still printing the words *fraud* and *Winterbourne* in the same sentence, when people she'd known her whole life crossed the street to avoid her, when she was twenty-six years old and had just learned that a father could be both the man who taught her to prune and the man who falsified the books.

She'd assumed, in the beginning, that it was someone from the trial. A juror, maybe. One of the ones who'd held out. Then the letters kept coming, year after year, through the fire and the drought and the year the bank first came knocking, and they stopped being about the trial and started being about her — *I trust the harvest was kind to you this year, though I have learned that kindness is not the harvest's chief virtue* — and she stopped wondering who it was, mostly, because the not-knowing had become part of the gift. Someone, somewhere, was paying attention. Someone thought the vineyard was worth keeping. On the bad nights, that was the whole load-bearing wall of her sanity.

Tonight was a bad night, so she took out the most recent one. It had come in August, postmarked Sacramento, which meant Daniel's city, which she'd noticed and tried not to read anything into.

*Vesper,*

*I read the county assessor's filings, which is a habit I have never managed to break, and I see the bank has moved to a new firm. The ones they use now are not patient people. I say this not to frighten you but because you have always struck me as someone who would rather be frightened with information than comforted without it.*

*If it comes to the end, and I confess I do not know how to write that sentence without my hand wanting to stop, I want you to understand something. A vineyard is not the land. I have watched men own land their whole lives and never once possess a vineyard. What you have — the thing the bank cannot put a number on, though God knows they will try — is that you learned from a flawed man how to do a true thing. The vines know the difference. The town knows it too, even the ones who stopped speaking to you. Especially them.*

*Keep the ledger. Not the one your father kept. The one you keep. It will matter, someday, in a way neither of us can predict.*

*I remain, as ever,*

*A Friend of the Vine*

She read it twice, sitting on the tasting room floor with her back against the bar and the shoebox open on her knees, the gray light strengthening slowly in the windows behind the dust-furred bottles.

*Keep the ledger.* She glanced up at the office door, where the current ledgers lived in a locked drawer — hers now, kept clean and boring and honest for six years, the most tedious virtue of her life. She'd always taken that line as encouragement. Tonight, in the flat cold light of the morning after the first frost, it sounded almost like a prediction. Like the writer knew something.

She was tired of not knowing things.

That was the thought that arrived, uninvited, as she folded the letter along its worn creases and put it back. Ten years of gratitude, and underneath the gratitude, all along, a thread of something she'd never let herself name, because naming it felt like ingratitude: *Who are you? Who spends a decade writing to a stranger? Who knows about assessor's filings and bank attorneys and ledgers, and signs it "a friend"?* Friends had names. Friends had faces. Friends, when the foreclosure notice went up on the door, showed up.

She looked at the notice through the window, white against the dark oak, and felt the despair come up in her the way groundwater comes up — not dramatic, not sudden, just steadily rising, filling the low places first. Forty-one days. Two hundred sixty-one thousand dollars. A vineyard full of dead Chardonnay and a brother who texted *You sure?* on the first of every month like a man checking on a patient he expected to lose.

And underneath it, quieter, the other thing. The thing she'd been carrying since August, since the letter, since she'd stood in this same room and realized that whoever wrote to her had watched her whole ruin from somewhere close enough to read the filings.

Weary, she thought. That was the word. She was not broken — she'd had ten years of practice at not being broken, she was very good at it, it was possibly her single most developed skill. But she was tired in the place where tired stops being about sleep.

Vesper closed the shoebox. She stood, put it back in the cabinet, and went out through the cellar door into the cold.

The sun was up now, fully, and it was doing that thing the light did here in late autumn — coming in low and gold across the valley and making even the dead rows look, for about twenty minutes, like something worth saving. She walked out to row twelve and stood among the brown vines and let the light have its twenty minutes with her.

Then, from the road, she heard a sound the valley hadn't made in years: an engine, a big one, downshifting.

She turned. A moving truck was crawling past the vineyard's front fence, slow, the driver reading the mailboxes. It passed her gate. It passed her mailbox, with its faded W. It slowed further, and put on its blinker, and turned in at the next drive — the overgrown one, the one she'd mowed twice a year out of neighborliness for a decade, the one that led up to the old Hollis bakery, shuttered since she was nineteen, its windows boarded, its chimney standing over the trees like a monument to something nobody remembered.

The truck crunched up the gravel and stopped, and a man got out of the cab.

He stood in the early light looking up at the boarded windows, and even at this distance — even with ten years of caution and forty-one days of deadline sitting on her chest — Vesper felt something turn over in her, slow and reluctant, like a vine waking.

He was looking at the bakery the way she'd been looking at her vines all morning.
