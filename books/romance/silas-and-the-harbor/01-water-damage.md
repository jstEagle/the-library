## 1. Water Damage

The chapel smelled like the inside of a drowned clock.

Freya Thackeray stood in the doorway with her clipboard pressed to her chest and let that thought settle, because it was true and she had learned not to fight true things. Salt water had gotten into the stone. The tide had come up through the harbor floor the way it always did in March, but this year it had kept coming, creeping past the seawall and into the decommissioned chapel of St. Brendan's, filling the nave like a bowl. Now, six weeks later, the water was gone and everything it had touched was swollen, stained, and quietly rotting.

Perfect for a wedding.

"Ms. Thackeray?" The client's sister hovered behind her, a small woman named Petra who wore anxiety like perfume. "The planner on the phone said the pews might need replacing."

"The pews will be fine." Freya said it with the calm she'd spent eleven years perfecting — the voice of a woman who could turn a flooded chapel into a venue, a budget overrun into a line item, a life into a series of manageable steps. "We'll dry them out, sand them down, re-stain. By June they'll look like they've been here two hundred years instead of one hundred and eighty."

Petra looked doubtful. Freya smiled her professional smile and walked the aisle, counting steps out of habit. Forty-two from door to altar. She'd have the aisle runner custom-dyed, oyster gray, nothing so obvious as white. Candles in hurricane glass because open flame plus old wood equaled an insurance claim. She wrote *check tide tables* at the top of her list even though the wedding wasn't until June and the tide tables were the least of anyone's problems.

This was what she did now. This was who she'd become: Freya Thackeray, of Thackeray Events, the woman who made other people's beginnings beautiful. She was good at it. She was, if she was honest, excellent at it, and excellence had been the whole point — build something no one could take, be someone no one could ruin, keep every feeling folded small enough to fit in a filing cabinet.

She knelt at the third pew from the front because the kneeler was warped and she wanted to see how bad.

Bad. The wood had buckled along the seam where the seat met the base, the grain fanned open like a book left in the rain. She ran her thumb along it, already composing the email to the carpenter, and then her thumb caught on something that wasn't wood.

Paper.

She should have pulled her hand back. Any sensible person would have. But the edge of it was soft and yellowed and wrong, wrong in a way that made her stomach drop before her mind caught up, and she hooked one finger under the seat's lip and pulled.

The pew resisted. Water-swollen oak does not give up its secrets easily. Freya set down the clipboard, braced both hands, and wrenched upward until the seam cracked and the seat came loose with a groan that echoed off the vaulted ceiling like a word spoken too loudly in church.

Inside the pew's hollow — wedged deep against the back panel, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark — was a letter.

Not just any letter. She knew before she touched it. Some part of her knew from the moment her thumb found paper instead of wood, the way you know a voice through a wall.

She unwrapped the oilcloth with hands that had stopped being hers. Inside, sodden but legible, ink blooming at the edges like bruises: an envelope. Postmarked. Dated eleven years ago this spring. Addressed in blue-black ink to **Mr. Silas Merrick, Merrick Vineyard, Harbor Road**.

And the handwriting on the envelope was hers.

*

Freya sat down on the broken pew she'd just dismantled, which felt appropriate, and read the address four times.

She remembered writing it. That was the vertigo of it — not amnesia, not mystery, but memory, full and sickening. She had been nineteen. She had written the letter at her kitchen table at two in the morning by the light over the stove, three drafts burned in the sink because her mother couldn't know, and then she had sealed it and walked to the post box on Harbor Road in the rain because she didn't trust herself to wait for morning.

*I never mailed a second copy.*

That sentence arrived in her head in her own voice, flat and factual, and beneath it the arithmetic began, the terrible clean arithmetic she was good at:

One letter written. One letter posted. One letter never received — because Silas Merrick had looked at her across the diner parking lot three days later with a face like a locked door and said, *You could have at least told me why,* and she had assumed he'd read it and hated her for it, and she had built her entire adult life on top of that assumption the way you build a house on a fault line.

But here was the letter. Here, in a chapel neither of them had entered in a decade, hidden in a pew, wrapped in oilcloth by someone who had wanted it found by no one.

Someone had taken it out of the post box. Someone had carried it here, through the rain, eleven years ago, and pried open a pew, and buried it.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them the way you notice weather happening to someone else.

"Ms. Thackeray? Is everything—" Petra's voice, from the doorway.

"Fine." Freya slid the letter into the inner pocket of her blazer, against her ribs, where her heartbeat made it feel alive. "Just found some old damage. I'll handle it."

She was aware, distantly, that she was lying in her professional voice, and that the letter against her chest was soaking cold through the silk of her blouse, and that none of it mattered, because the only real fact in the world was this: Silas Merrick had never known why she left. He had believed the worst. And someone had made sure of it.

*

She finished the walkthrough. That was the thing about her — the thing she'd mistaken for strength all these years. She finished the walkthrough. She measured the altar alcove, photographed the waterline stains for the insurance claim, discussed candle colors with Petra until Petra's face unclenched, and shook hands with the caretaker, and got in her car, and drove exactly four blocks before pulling over beside the shuttered fish market with her hazards blinking and the engine running and the letter open in her lap.

The ink had run where the water had gotten in, but she'd written it in pen, pressing hard, the way she did when she was afraid someone would tell her not to. Whole passages survived. She read them like a stranger reading evidence.

*You'll have heard by now what my father owes him. I won't write the number down, even here, even to you. I promised him I'd leave and never tell you why, and I'm breaking that promise the only way I can — on paper, where he can't hear me say it.*

*Aldous told me what happens to my father if I stay. He said it the way he says everything, like he's discussing the weather. I believe him. You should believe him too, and hate him for it, not me.*

*If you're reading this, come find me. I'll be the one who never stopped—*

The rest was drowned. A bloom of black water damage where the final lines had been, the ink dissolved into gray clouds, the sentence lost.

*Never stopped.* Never stopped what. She knew. Of course she knew; she'd written it. But seeing the gap where the words should be, the hole in the page like a missing tooth — that was different. That was her own handwriting testifying against the last eleven years of her life.

Because here was the other arithmetic, the one she'd been refusing to do since she knelt at the third pew:

Silas had never read this. Which meant that when he said *you could have at least told me why*, he hadn't meant it as a wound. He'd meant it literally. He genuinely did not know. He had spent ten years believing she'd abandoned him for no reason at all — or worse, for reasons he'd invented himself, each one uglier than the truth — while she had spent ten years believing he'd read her heart and thrown it away.

They had built their separate fortresses on the same false foundation, and someone had poured that foundation deliberately, brick by brick, and that someone's name was on the first line of her own letter.

Aldous.

Aldous, who had held her father's debt like a knife at the family throat. Aldous, who had *inherited* the vineyard when his brother died — conveniently, cleanly, with papers signed by the town clerk. Aldous, whose velvet voice she still heard sometimes in dreams, saying *families are fragile things, Miss Thackeray, handle yours carefully.*

The letter trembled in her hands. Outside the car window, the harbor lay flat and silver, indifferent, and beyond it the vineyard climbed the hill — Merrick land, Merrick vines, worked now by a man who had stayed when she left, tending grapes that weren't legally his, answering to an uncle who had stolen everything including the truth.

Freya put the letter back in its oilcloth. Her hands steadied as she wrapped it, and she understood why: this was a task. Tasks she could do. She had built a career on turning catastrophe into checklist.

*Find Silas. Give him the letter. Watch his face when he reads it.*

Then, underneath, in smaller handwriting, the thought she'd been avoiding for eleven years:

*And find out who else knew.*

She pulled back onto Harbor Road. The vineyard gate was two miles ahead, iron and green, half-hidden by cypress. She had driven past it a hundred times in ten years without stopping — a detour she'd mapped into every route out of town, the way you route around a wound.

Today she slowed. Today she signaled, absurdly, courteously, as if the empty road cared, and turned in.

The gravel crunched under her tires like something breaking open.
