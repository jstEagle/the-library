## 3. What the Letter Said

**Freya**

The diner on Harbor Road hadn't changed in eleven years, which felt like a small act of cruelty. Same cracked vinyl booths the color of old mustard. Same pie case fogged with condensation. Same waitress — Dot, older now, her name tag still crooked — who looked at Silas and then at me and then at the space between us and wisely said nothing except *coffee?*

We both said yes. It was the last thing we agreed on for a while.

Silas sat across from me with the letter between us on the laminate table, still flattened under a glass of water he'd ordered for it like a patient. The paper had dried to something brittle and buckled, my handwriting swimming where the water had gotten to it, but legible. Legible enough.

"You could read it yourself," I said. "You've had it since yesterday."

"I read it." His jaw worked. "I wanted to hear you say it."

"That's not what this is."

"Then what is it?"

I looked at him. He was thinner than the boy I'd loved, harder through the shoulders, with a new scar cutting through one eyebrow that I desperately wanted a story for and knew better than to ask. But his eyes were the same. That was the unbearable part. Eleven years, two lives, an ocean of silence — and his eyes were exactly the same.

"It's an accounting," I said. "That's all. You read your side. Now you get mine."

Dot brought the coffee. Neither of us touched it.

* * *

**Silas**

She started reading out loud because she said her voice would shake less than mine would. She was wrong about that, but I let her have it.

*"Silas — if you're reading this, then I failed at being brave twice, which feels about right for me."*

My hands were flat on the table. I remember noticing that. Remembering pressing them down so they wouldn't do anything embarrassing.

*"By the time you get this I'll be gone. My father owes Aldous Merrick eleven thousand dollars, and Aldous called tonight to collect. Not money. Me. He wants me gone from you before the will is read, and he's holding my father's debt over me like a knife. If I stay, he takes the house, the boat, everything. If I go, we keep our heads above water until Dad can rebuild."*

"Stop," I said.

She stopped. Her thumb stayed pinned to the page like she was afraid the words would move.

"He told you that?" My voice came out lower than I meant it to. "He told you he'd take your father's house?"

"He didn't have to raise his voice. He never did." Freya's laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "He sat in our kitchen drinking my mother's coffee and explained it like a weather report. Debt comes due. Storms come through. Your father's estate is complicated, Freya, and complications cost money. And then he looked at me and said, *the boy inherits nothing anyway, once the papers are settled.*"

"The papers." The word tasted like copper. "What papers?"

"I don't know. I was nineteen. I thought he was talking about your father's will." She finally lifted her eyes. "Silas, I thought you knew. I thought — God, I thought maybe you'd agreed to it. That you'd let him buy you out and chosen the vineyard over—"

"Chosen the—" I heard my own voice crack and hated it. "Freya. I stood at your door for three hours the night you left. Three hours. Your mother came out eventually and told me you'd gone to your aunt's in Rockport and that I should let it be. Let it *be.* Like you were a dog off a leash."

"My mother thought she was protecting me."

"She thought she was protecting *Aldous*," I said, and watched it land on her like a wave she'd been waiting eleven years to be hit by. "Because he must have told her the same thing he told you. That it was settled. That everyone had agreed."

The diner noise went on around us — the grill hissing, Dot refilling some trucker's cup, the bell over the door — and none of it touched our booth. We were underwater together, finally, after a decade of drowning separately.

* * *

**Freya**

Here is what I had rehearsed on the drive over: that I would be calm. That I would present the letter like evidence, like a wedding planner presenting a seating chart, everything in its place, nothing spilling. That I would not cry in a diner booth across from Silas Merrick, because I had done enough crying over this man to fill the harbor.

I made it four paragraphs.

*"I promised myself I'd send this and then come back for you when Dad was steady again. Six months, I told myself. A year at most. But I know how promises work when Aldous Merrick is the one holding the other end of them. So if I don't come back — if years go by — please know that I left because staying would have destroyed everything you were going to be. And I couldn't watch that happen and call it love."*

"You were coming back," he said. Not a question. Worse than a question — an accusation shaped like wonder.

"Six months. Maybe a year."

"A year." He leaned back against the vinyl and put both hands over his face, and for a moment he was nineteen again, standing in the vineyard rows in the dark, and I was nineteen again, watching him from a bus window, and we were both children being handled by a man who treated people like ledger entries. "I waited a year, Freya. I drove past your father's place every Sunday for a year. Then your dad got sick, and the house sold, and someone told me you'd moved inland with a man from the city, and I—" His hands dropped. "I stopped. I believed him. That's the part I can't get past. He told me you'd found someone else and I *believed him*, because believing him hurt less than believing you'd just — vanish."

"What did he tell you, exactly?"

"That you'd written. That there was a letter." Silas's mouth twisted. "He said you'd asked him to give it to me when I was ready. And then a month later he said you'd asked for it back. That you'd met someone. He showed me—"

He stopped.

"Showed you what?"

"Nothing. It doesn't matter."

"Silas."

"He showed me a page in your handwriting." Each word came out like a stone set down carefully on glass. "It said you were sorry for the trouble. It said you wished me well. Five lines. I stared at it so long I memorized it, and then he took it back and burned it in the study fireplace while I watched, and I thought — well. That's that, then. She's made her peace. Why shouldn't I?"

The pie case hummed. Somewhere behind the counter a radio played something tinny and cheerful.

"He forged a goodbye," I said.

"He forged a whole decade," Silas said.

And there it was — the thing we'd been circling, laid bare between the coffee cups. Not a misunderstanding. An architecture. Every wall between us built by the same mason, brick by brick, paid for out of the same account.

* * *

**Silas**

We sat with it awhile. I don't know how long. Long enough that Dot came by twice and retreated both times without asking.

Then Freya did the thing she used to do — squared the letter on the table, aligned its edges with the table edge, the way she straightened everything when she was turning panic into a plan.

"Tell me about the inheritance," she said. "Not the grief version. The facts."

So I gave her the facts. My father died in March, eleven years ago. The will was read in April. By May, Aldous held the vineyard outright — every acre, every barrel, the house, the name on the sign. The paperwork was clean. Immaculate. My father's signature, notarized, dated six weeks before he died, leaving everything to his brother, with a clause about providing for me "at Aldous's discretion."

"At his discretion," Freya repeated.

"I've worked the land since. No wages worth the name. Room in the bunkhouse. He calls it my inheritance-in-practice." I turned my cup a slow circle. "Three years ago he started borrowing against it. The harvests have been thin, the loans are heavy, and now there's a developer sniffing around who wants the waterfront parcel. Aldous doesn't care. He never loved the vines. He loved owning what my father loved."

"And the dates," Freya said quietly. "On the paperwork. Do you actually know when your father signed it?"

"I was told April. But my father was dying in February — the last time I saw him hold a pen, he couldn't manage his own name."

Freya went very still. I watched her arrive somewhere ahead of me.

"Silas. In the chapel basement yesterday, the flood damage — the town records from the clerk's office are stored down there. Boxes of them. Old filings, permits, correspondence." Her eyes had gone bright and hard. "If your father's signature was witnessed, or filed, or copied anywhere official, there might be a date. Something that contradicts the will."

"You want to dig through flooded town records."

"I want to find out what your uncle stole," she said. "All of it. The vineyard. The letter. Ten years." She reached across the table — hesitated — and then covered my hand with hers, brief as a struck match. "I owe you a decade, Silas. I intend to start paying it back with interest."

* * *

**Freya**

We walked out into the late light together, and the harbor smelled like salt and diesel and my entire lost youth.

At her car, Silas stopped. Close enough that I could see the gray starting at his temples, close enough that for one dangerous second the eleven years collapsed and we were just two kids on Harbor Road with everything still ahead of us.

"This doesn't mean—" he began.

"I know what it means," I said. "It means we investigate. Nothing more."

"Nothing more," he echoed, and neither of us believed it, and both of us pretended to.

He drove off toward the vineyard. I sat in my car a long minute, the letter in my lap, and that's when I saw it — the postmark, half dissolved, but readable if you tilted it against the light.

Mailed from the harbor. Dated three days after I wrote it.

Which meant it never left town. Which meant someone took it from my father's mailbox, kept it eleven years, and hid it in a pew of a chapel that Aldous Merrick helped deconsecrate.

Someone close. Someone careful.

And tomorrow morning, I had a walkthrough at the Merrick estate — a wedding job, booked weeks ago, before any of this. Before I knew I'd be walking straight into the lion's study with a clipboard and a smile.

I turned the key in the ignition.

Well, I thought. He did always say I could plan anything.
