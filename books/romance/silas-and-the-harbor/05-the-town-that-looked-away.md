## 5. The Town That Looked Away

The list lived in Silas's head for three days before he wrote it down.

He wrote it on the back of a pruning schedule, in the cab of the truck at the top of the vineyard rows, because there was no other paper in reach and because once the names started coming they would not stop. Old harbor families. The ones who had been there the night his father died, the night the will was read, the night everything Aldous touched turned to gold and everything Silas loved turned to ash. He wrote twenty-two names before his pen slowed.

Then he sat with it a long time, looking down at the vines, and asked himself the question he'd been avoiding since the diner: *What do you think you'll find?*

He already knew what he'd find. That was the worst of it. You don't grow up in a town like Pelham's Reach without learning its arithmetic. Every silence has a price, and Aldous Merrick had never once haggled.

* * *

The first door belonged to Ruth Calloway, who had taught half the town to read and the other half to fear her red pen. She was eighty-one now, tiny behind her screen door, and when Silas said his name her face did something complicated — grief and guilt wrestling in a small space.

"Silas Merrick," she said. "You have your mother's chin."

"Mrs. Calloway, I need to ask you about the night my father's will disappeared."

She didn't pretend not to know what he meant. That was something, at least. She looked past him at the street, at the gulls working the harbor light, and her hands knotted together on the doorframe.

"I was at the wake," she said quietly. "Everyone was at the wake. And Aldous came in late, and he wasn't wet even though it had rained, and he was smiling, and I remember thinking — I remember thinking, *why is that man smiling.*" Her voice thinned. "And then eleven years go by and I never said a word about it to anyone."

"Why not?"

She met his eyes then, and didn't flinch from what he'd see in hers. "Because in the spring after your father died, my roof got replaced. New boiler too. And I never asked who paid for it, Silas, because I knew, and knowing was bad enough. Asking would have made me complicit." A pause. "More complicit."

She pressed an envelope through the screen — old, yellowed, sealed. Inside, he could see, was a check stub. "I've kept this since the year it happened. I told myself I'd use it someday. Then someday became ten years and I stopped believing in it." Her chin came up, his mother's stubbornness living on in someone else's face. "Ask the others. Ask them all. But know what you're asking, boy. You're asking people to price their own shame."

* * *

They priced it differently, but they all priced it.

Garrick Voss, who ran the chandlery, took over his father-in-law's building lease the same season the will vanished — a lease Aldous owned. He talked to Silas for four minutes on his own doorstep and never opened the door wider than six inches, and at the end he said, "Your uncle was good to this town," in a voice like a man reciting lines he'd been given, and closed it.

The Harmons wouldn't come to the door at all. Through the window Silas watched Mrs. Harmon pull her husband back by the sleeve, and he thought: *that's not guilt. That's fear.* Different animal entirely.

The lawyer was worse than all of them.

Edmund Pike had drafted Thomas Merrick's will — everyone knew that, though no one said it above a whisper anymore. His office still sat above the pharmacy on Cordage Street, brass letters gone green, PIKE & SON though there had been no son in twenty years. Silas climbed the stairs on a Tuesday morning and found the old man exactly where the town's memory kept him: behind a desk stacked with deed boxes, drinking tea he clearly wasn't tasting.

Pike listened to the whole thing without interrupting. That was the first sign. Guilty men interrupt. Men with nothing left to lose let you finish.

"You want to know if the will I read aloud was the will your father signed," Pike said at last.

"I want to know what happened to the real one."

The old man set down his cup with great care, as if it might detonate. "I'll tell you a professional secret, Mr. Merrick. In forty years of practice, I have learned that documents don't disappear. Documents are *disappeared*. There is always a hand." He looked at his own hands, liver-spotted, folded. "There was a hand."

"Yours?"

"My job was to witness signatures. I witnessed two signatures on a document I had not drafted, dated eight months before the date on its face, and I read it aloud at a funeral gathering while the ink of my conscience was still wet." His voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Aldous paid me. Not in cash — he was smarter than cash. He forgave a debt. My practice was mortgaged to him, did you know that? Half this town was mortgaged to him. It's remarkable how much of a town one man can own without ever holding title."

"And Freya's father?" Silas heard himself ask it, though he'd sworn not to bring her into this room. "Thackeray. Was he—"

"Mortgaged," Pike said simply. "Deeply. Your uncle called in Thackeray's notes the same week the girl left town. Coincidence, I'm sure." His eyes were dry and terrible. "Nothing Aldous Merrick ever did was coincidence. That was his genius. He never pushed anyone. He just arranged the world so that pushing themselves was the only direction left."

Silas stood to go. At the door, Pike spoke again, quieter.

"There were witnesses that night who weren't bought. One or two. People Aldous couldn't reach because they had nothing he wanted." A pause. "Nell Okafor still walks the seawall every morning at six. If I were a young man trying to un-arrange the world, that's where I'd start."

* * *

He found her at six-oh-four, exactly as promised: a broad, weathered woman in a canvas coat, walking the seawall with the rolling gait of someone who'd spent forty years compensating for a deck that moved. She didn't stop when he fell into step beside her. She looked at him sideways and kept walking.

"You're Thomas's boy," she said. Not a question. "You have his walk. He used to come down here at dawn too, when the catch was bad, and stand right where you're standing, doing sums in his head."

"Nell Okafor."

"Retired fisherman, current insomniac. Go on, then. Ask."

So he asked. He told her about the letter, about Freya, about the ledgers in the cellar and the clerk and the dates that didn't match, and she listened without breaking stride, gulls wheeling around her like punctuation. When he finished she walked another fifty yards in silence, out past the breakwater's shadow, before she spoke.

"I was on the water that night," she said. "Came in late — engine trouble off the point. I tied up at the private dock below the big house, because mine was full, and I was sitting in the wheelhouse having a smoke before the climb up, and I saw a man come out the study window instead of the front door." Her voice flattened into the cadence of testimony, the rhythm of a woman who had rehearsed this alone for eleven years. "Carrying papers. Wrapped in oilcloth, because it had rained. And he stood on the dock a long minute, deciding, and then he walked down to the old incinerator by the boathouse, and I watched the fire light up his face."

"Aldous."

"No." She stopped walking and turned to face him fully, and her eyes were the gray of deep water. "That's the thing, Silas. It wasn't Aldous. It was the town clerk — Whitlock, the younger one, the father of the man who holds the office now. Aldous didn't burn the will himself. He never touched anything. He had hands for that, the way he had hands for everything." She resumed walking. "I told myself for years I'd seen wrong. Dark, rain, distance. But I know what a man looks like when he's burning something that isn't his. I've seen poachers. I've seen men sink gear that wasn't theirs. There's a set to the shoulders."

"Why didn't you come forward?"

"Because two weeks later my boat's mooring fee was paid five years ahead, anonymous, and my daughter's school was suddenly covered, and I understood the shape of the offer without anyone making it." She laughed, short and bitter, a sound like a hatch slamming. "I didn't take money, Silas. I took *quiet*, which is the same currency. This whole town took quiet. That's what your uncle built — not a fortune. A silence. Big enough to live in."

The sun was fully up now, laying gold across the harbor, and Silas stood in it feeling the weight of twenty-two names, of roofs and leases and forgiven debts, of a town that had sold itself in increments so small nobody had to call it betrayal.

"So that's it," he said. "Everyone's bought. Everyone's scared. There's no one left."

Nell stopped again. She reached into her coat and pulled out a tin — tobacco, once — and held it without opening it, turning it over in her broad fingers.

"I didn't say that," she said. "I said everyone was bought or scared. I didn't say everyone stayed that way." She looked out at the water a moment. "Eleven years ago I watched a man burn a dead friend's last words to his son, and I did nothing, and I have hated myself in a low-grade way ever since, the way you hate a toothache you won't get pulled. So yes. I'll testify. Courtroom, newspaper, church steps, wherever you want me. I'll say what I saw."

Silas felt something loosen in his chest that had been clenched so long he'd mistaken it for anatomy.

"But hear the rest," Nell said, and her voice dropped, and she stepped closer, and the gulls seemed to quiet with her. "Burning the original only works if there's no copy. And Tom Merrick was not a careless man. He was a careful man with a superstitious streak — he used to say a promise should live in more than one place." She tapped the tin against her palm, once, twice. "The night before he died, he came down to my boat, soaked through, carrying papers in oilcloth. Same oilcloth. He said, *Nell, if anything happens to me, and I mean anything, there's a true copy where the town keeps its oldest promises.* And I asked him what he meant, and he smiled — your father's smile, boy, you have that too — and he said, *stone remembers longer than paper.*"

The harbor noise came back all at once: water, rigging, the far horn of the ferry.

"Stone," Silas said.

"Stone," Nell agreed. "I've had eleven years to work out what he meant, and I keep coming back to one place in this town old enough to hold a promise. Built by the founding families. Every one of them put something in it when it went up." She fixed him with those deep-water eyes. "Your family helped build the harbor chapel, Silas. And I'd bet this boat — what's left of her — that Thomas Merrick put his will where the stones could keep it."

The chapel. The flooded, decommissioned, condemned chapel where Freya had found a letter sodden in a pew — where, in two weeks' time, she was due to stage a wedding for a client, locked doors and all.

Silas was already reaching for his phone.
