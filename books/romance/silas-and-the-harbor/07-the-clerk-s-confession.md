## 7. The Clerk's Confession

The town clerk's office smelled of toner and old coffee, the way municipal buildings everywhere smell when they've given up pretending to be anything other than what they are. Freya stood at the counter with a folder of wedding permits she didn't need, waiting for Wendell Pryce to look up from his computer.

He was a small man gone smaller with age — cardigan the color of oatmeal, reading glasses pushed up into thinning hair, hands that had spent forty years stamping things. She remembered him from her childhood as a fixture of harbor parades, waving from a convertible with a paper crown on his head. Keeper of the town's records. Keeper of its secrets, too, if Silas was right.

"Miss Thackeray." He said her name like it cost him something. "Permits for the chapel again? I thought we processed those Tuesday."

"We did." She set her palms flat on the counter and kept her voice pleasant, the voice she used with caterers who'd shown up drunk to a reception. "I have a different question today. About the Merrick inheritance papers."

Something moved behind his face — quick, submerged, like a fish turning under ice.

"I'm not sure that's an appropriate topic," he said. "Those are sealed records."

"They're public records, Mr. Pryce. Probate filings in this state are open to any resident. You know that better than anyone." She slid a photocopied page across the counter — the inheritance filing Aldous had produced eleven years ago, the one establishing him as sole heir to the vineyard. "What I can't figure out is the dates."

"Dates?"

"The notarization date is March third. But the witness signatures are dated February eleventh. And the filing stamp — your stamp, Mr. Pryce — says March ninth." She tapped each date in turn. "A will witnessed before it's drafted. Filed six days after it's notarized by a clerk who supposedly processes documents within forty-eight hours. I plan weddings for a living. I notice when paperwork doesn't line up. It's the whole job."

Wendell Pryce looked at the photocopy for a long time. His hand drifted toward it and stopped, hovering, as though the paper were hot.

"You should talk to a lawyer," he said quietly.

"I did. My lawyer says these discrepancies would be laughed out of any courtroom — dismissed as clerical error, unless someone testifies otherwise. Unless the man who stamped them says they weren't errors at all." She let that sit. "Nobody's asking you to perjure yourself, Mr. Pryce. I'm asking you to stop doing it."

His jaw worked. Behind him, through the open door to the records room, Freya could see the filing cabinets, the gray boxes of a town's entire memory, and she understood suddenly how easy it had been — one small man, one drawer, one stamp pressed down on a lie until it looked official.

"Close the door," he said.

* * *

They sat in the records room among the boxes, and Wendell Pryce told it the way men tell confessions — in pieces, staring at his hands, as if the story belonged to someone else and he was only relaying it.

"He came to me the week after the funeral," he said. "Aldous. Your father had just been buried, Miss Thackeray, and I want you to understand I liked your father. Everyone did. That's part of why—" He stopped, swallowed. "That's part of why it worked. Grief makes people pliable."

Freya kept still. Eleven years ago, her father dead, the family debt to Aldous Merrick crushing them, and she had run — taken the ferry to the mainland and a job and a life built carefully on top of the hole where Silas used to be. She had never known the debt was the lever. Now she was hearing how the rest of the machine fit together.

"He had papers," Wendell went on. "An inheritance filing naming him sole owner of the vineyard — Elias Merrick's estate passing entirely to his brother, no provision for the son. And it was wrong. I knew it was wrong because I'd seen Elias's will years earlier, when Elias updated his insurance designations. Elias left everything to Silas. Everything, with a trust managed by Aldous until the boy turned thirty. There was never any version where Aldous owned it outright."

"But you filed it anyway."

"He paid me." The words came out flat, stripped of excuse. "Eight thousand dollars. I had a daughter starting college and a wife with medical bills and eight thousand dollars might as well have been eight million. He put the envelope on my desk and he said, 'Wendell, families need looking after, and nobody looks after this town but me.' And I took it." He laughed, a terrible dry sound. "You know what the worst part is? Backdating wasn't even hard. I changed the notarization date, adjusted the filing stamp, initialed the corrections. Twenty minutes' work. I've thought about those twenty minutes every day for eleven years."

"Then testify," Freya said. "Say exactly that. In front of a judge."

"And lose my pension, my name, probably my freedom — for a case that takes two years to crawl through probate court while Aldous sells the vineyard out from under Silas?" He shook his head. "I'm sixty-seven years old, Miss Thackeray. I don't have two years. Neither does that boy."

"Then give me something faster. The original will. Where is it?"

And here his face did the thing she'd dreaded — collapsed inward, all the confession draining out of it and leaving only defeat.

"Gone. I destroyed it." He said it fast, wanting it over. "Aldous asked for it back after the filing went through, and I burned it in the incinerator behind the fire station. I watched it curl up. Elias Merrick's last wishes, and I watched them curl up." His eyes were wet now, furious at themselves. "There's nothing left. I'm sorry. God help me, there's nothing left to find."

* * *

She found Nell Okafor at the end of the breakwater, mending nets in the late light, her hands moving with the unconscious economy of sixty years of work. Freya sat down on the cold stone beside her without asking, the way you sit beside someone who has already decided to make room.

"Well?" Nell didn't look up. "Did the little stamp-man sing?"

"He confessed. Backdating, the bribe, all of it. He'll sign a statement — I got that much. But the original will is destroyed. Burned. There's no document, Nell. A statement about forged dates against Aldous's lawyers and the town's whole habit of forgetting — it's not enough. It'll take years, and Silas has until harvest."

Nell's hands kept moving. Pull, loop, tighten. For a long moment the only sound was water worrying the rocks below them.

"Elias Merrick wrote two copies," she said at last.

Freya turned. "What?"

"Two copies of the will. I know because I watched him seal the second one myself, summer before he died. He was a careful man, Elias. Sailors are — you learn redundancy or you learn drowning. One copy went to Wendell Pryce for the official file." Her hands stilled on the netting. "The other one, he gave to me. He said, 'Nell, if anything happens to me, and anything happens to the paper in town, I want what I wanted written down somewhere no clerk can burn it.'"

Freya's heart was going hard against her ribs. "Where?"

Nell finally looked at her, and there was something almost amused in the old woman's seamed face — the look of someone who had carried a secret so long it had become furniture.

"Where do you hide a thing on this island for eleven years? Not in a house — houses get sold, searched, cleared out. Not with a person — people get threatened. You saw what happened to everyone who tried to hold onto their piece of the truth." She jerked her chin back toward the shore, toward the white clapboard shape rising above the harbor road. "You hide it in the one building nobody opens except for weddings and funerals. The cornerstone of the harbor chapel, girl. Copper box, lead seal. Elias helped lay that stone himself when the chapel was rebuilt — insisted on it, made a little ceremony of it. Nobody thinks twice about a cornerstone. It's just part of the wall."

The chapel. The flooded, decommissioned chapel where Freya had pried open a pew and found her own drowned letter — where, by some arrangement of fate she was no longer willing to call coincidence, she was due to stand tomorrow afternoon and watch two strangers promise each other everything.

"The cornerstone's sealed," she said. "It's been sealed since the rebuild."

"Mortar cracks," Nell said. "Stone shifts. Especially in a building that's been taking seawater through its bones for a decade." She went back to her netting, pull, loop, tighten. "But you'd best go tonight, or first thing tomorrow before the guests arrive. Because I'll tell you something else for free: Aldous signed the sale papers with that resort developer yesterday. Contingent on clear title. Once that closes, even a real will turns into a lawsuit instead of a key."

* * *

She drove to the chapel at dusk and found it locked — chained doors, a padlock bright and new against the weathered wood, and a printed notice taped at eye level:

**HARBOR CHAPEL CLOSED FOR PRIVATE EVENT — REHEARSAL IN PROGRESS — REOPENS FOR CEREMONY 4 P.M. TOMORROW**

Through the windows she could see the florists moving among the pews, candlelight blooming, her own careful arrangements coming alive for someone else's joy. Tomorrow, harvest day, the whole town would be here — and half a mile up the hill, the first pickers would be walking into the vineyard rows under Aldous's orders, harvesting grapes from land that had never been his.

Freya pressed her palm flat against the cold door and felt the chain shift, solid, immovable. Four o'clock tomorrow. Guests arriving at three-thirty. Aldous signing at nine a.m., if Nell was right.

She took out her phone and called Silas, and when he answered she said only: "It's in the chapel. The will. We have until four o'clock tomorrow — and then I'm walking it down the aisle in front of everyone."

There was a silence on the line, long enough to hear wind moving through vines somewhere far away.

"Then we'd better get some sleep," Silas said. "Tomorrow we harvest everything."
