## 10. First Press

The press room smelled like the beginning of something.

Silas stood in the doorway with two coffee cups and watched the morning light come through the south windows, catching the steel of the new press, the gleam of it, the way the must ran pale gold into the collection pan. Eleven years he'd worked these rows under a man who treated the vineyard like a trophy torn from a dead brother's hands. Now the grapes were in. Now the first bottles would carry his name — *Merrick*, spelled correctly, on labels Freya had designed herself, the typeface clean and unafraid.

"You're going to let it overflow," Freya said from behind him, "standing there admiring your own destiny."

"It's not my destiny." He handed her a cup. "It's my inheritance. Destiny's what you do with it."

She took the coffee and leaned into his shoulder, and he felt the small weight of her settle against him the way she had at nineteen, before everything, before Aldous's ledger and the town's silence and a letter rotting in a flooded pew for a decade. Some things came back whole. He hadn't believed that until this spring.

The first bottle came off the line an hour later. Silas held it up to the light and turned it slowly, reading his own name like a man checking for forgery. There was none. Just dark glass, a wax seal the color of late harvest, and a date.

"Say something," Freya said. "This is a moment."

He thought about it. "It weighs about the same as a brick."

"That's the romance I fell for."

But her eyes were bright, and when he kissed her she tasted like coffee and cellar dust and the future, and he held on longer than a joke required.

* * *

The harbor chapel reopened on a Sunday in October.

They'd pumped out the basement in June, replaced the warped pews one by one — Silas milled the wood himself from a storm-felled oak on the vineyard's north slope, because some things you wanted to put your own hands into. The cornerstone they left sealed, but with a bronze plate now: *Here lies the truth, kept safe by Nell Okafor, 2014.* The congregation that filled the chapel that first Sunday was larger than any service in twenty years. Half the town came to look at the plate. Half came to be forgiven. The halves overlapped.

Pastor Ives said grace over a building that smelled of new lumber and old salt, and when the hymn started, Silas found himself standing beside Nell in the back row, her cane hooked over the pew rail, her voice surprisingly strong on the second verse.

"Your uncle's lawyer called me again," she said during the benediction. "Wants to know if I'll soften my testimony before the civil suit."

"What did you tell him?"

"I told him I'm eighty-one years old and I've buried a husband and two boats and every friend I started fishing with. I don't have time to lie badly." She looked up at him, eyes sharp as gull's beaks. "How's the case?"

"The clerk's cooperating fully now. The state's reviewing the probate records. Aldous's accounts are frozen pending investigation."

"And the resort developer?"

"Gone. Took his deposit and his renderings and went to ruin someone else's coastline."

Nell nodded once, satisfied, the way she might nod at a clean haul. "Good. Now walk me to the car, boy, and tell me about this wedding everyone's whispering about."

* * *

There was no wedding yet. That was the thing. Everyone in town assumed, because Freya Thackeray planned weddings the way tides planned shorelines, that hers would be an event — three hundred guests, imported peonies, a tent the size of a hangar on the vineyard lawn.

Instead she'd booked the chapel for a Saturday in November. Forty chairs. One florist, local. A reception in the press room among the barrels, because, as she put it, "I want to marry you where the wine is born, not where it gets dressed up."

Silas found her there one evening in late October, alone in the chapel, arranging and rearranging forty chairs into rows that only made sense to her. She was barefoot on the cold stone, hair pinned up with whatever had been nearest — a pencil, he saw, and a sprig of dried lavender from the vineyard.

"Most brides don't do their own setup four weeks early," he said.

"Most brides aren't me." She stepped back, squinting at the aisle. "Eleven years ago I measured my life in safe steps. Small moves. Nothing that could break. And it broke anyway, in ways I never planned for. So now—" she gestured at the forty chairs, the plain altar, the storm-milled pews— "small and unafraid. That's the brief. That's the whole brief."

He came down the aisle toward her, boots loud on stone that had heard a century of promises. "You know what I keep thinking about? The letter. The one that rotted down here."

"Don't," she said softly. "Not tonight."

"No — listen. It took water damage and a stolen decade and a forged will and a town full of cowards to get us here. And I'd do all of it again. Every year of it. If the road was the same." He stopped one step away. "That's not grief talking anymore. That's just arithmetic."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Then she pulled the pencil from her hair, wrote something on the back of her seating chart, and folded it into his breast pocket.

"For the vows?" he asked.

"For after. You'll see."

* * *

The night before the wedding, the town threw them a dinner at the fish house — uninvited, unorganized, tables dragged together under string lights by people who eleven months earlier wouldn't have met either of their eyes.

The lawyer who'd drafted the false papers came, sat near the door, ate little, and left an envelope with a check inside and no note. The creditor who'd helped squeeze Freya's father paid for the bar and left before the toasts. The clerk — thin now, gray, diminished — stayed the whole evening and shook Silas's hand with both of his, saying nothing, which was its own kind of sentence.

It wasn't absolution. Silas understood that. A town doesn't get absolution for a decade of looking away; it gets the long work of amends, done in checks and testimony and showing up, year after year, until the arithmetic balances or doesn't. But they showed up. That was the first payment.

Nell stood last, tapping her glass with a spoon until the room hushed.

"I've given a lot of toasts in this harbor," she said, "and most of them were lies dressed in nice words. This one isn't." She raised her glass toward the head table, where Freya sat with her hand laced through Silas's. "I watched a boy lose everything twice — once to a thief, once to silence. And I watched a woman carry a letter's worth of love for eleven years without letting it rot, even when the paper did. So here's to the Merricks — the real ones. To truth kept in stone. To love that files no extensions." She drank. "And to hell with Aldous Merrick, wherever they're keeping him."

The room laughed and drank, and somewhere in the laughter Silas felt something in his chest finally set down a weight it had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his skeleton.

* * *

The wedding day came in gray and mild, the harbor flat as hammered tin.

Forty guests. Pastor Ives. Freya in ivory, walking herself down the aisle because, she'd said, "I've spent my career giving brides away; I'd like to arrive at one under my own power." Silas stood at the altar in the chapel he'd rebuilt board by board and discovered his hands were shaking exactly the way they'd shaken reading her letter in the vineyard gate — except now the shaking meant the opposite of ruin.

They said the ordinary words. They were not ordinary. When the pastor pronounced them, the bell in the chapel tower rang — the old harvest bell, restored, rung by Nell with a rope she'd refused to let anyone else touch — and the sound rolled out over the water and up the coast road toward the vineyard, where the winter vines slept in their rows, waiting.

At the reception, in the press room, among barrels marked with his own name, Silas opened the folded seating chart Freya had slipped into his pocket weeks ago. In her handwriting, in pencil:

*I never stopped choosing you. — F.*

He looked up. She was watching him across the barrel room, candlelight in her eyes, waiting.

"You knew," he said.

"I've known since the chapel basement. I just wanted to say it once where nothing could steal it." She crossed the room to him. "So I'm saying it again. Out loud. In front of the wine."

"I never stopped choosing you either," he said. "Every season. Even the bad ones."

"Especially the bad ones," she said, and kissed him while the first bottle of Merrick vintage was passed hand to hand around the room, and Nell rang the bell one more time just because she could, and outside the harbor held still, keeping, at last, everything it was given.
