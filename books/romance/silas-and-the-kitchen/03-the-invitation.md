## 3. The Invitation

The phone rang at seven in the morning, which on the island meant the light was still gray and the birds were conducting their usual argument about who owned the cypress tree, and Oscar Ishikawa knew before she answered that it was her mother, because her mother was the only person alive who believed seven a.m. was a civilized hour for delivering news.

"Oscar." Her mother's voice came through crisp and salted with disapproval, the way it always did, as if she were calling from a boardroom rather than a condo in Vancouver with a view of a parking garage. "I have good news."

Oscar sat up in bed and pulled the quilt around her shoulders. Good news from her mother was a category of event that required bracing. "Good morning to you too."

"The dedication is set. The memorial garden. The third Saturday of next month—no, don't check your calendar, I've already checked yours, metaphorically speaking. Three weeks. Everyone's coming."

Oscar's stomach performed a slow, deliberate drop, like an elevator whose cable had begun to fray. "Everyone."

"Everyone. Theo and Priya are flying in Thursday before. Aunt Ren is taking the ferry—she says she won't fly, which you know is code for she wants to bring her own knives. Your cousins from the mainland. Even Uncle Dennis, and you know what a production it is getting Dennis onto a boat." A pause, the kind of pause that was never empty. "It will be good for you, Oscar. To be around family. You've been up there alone for five years."

"I'm not alone," Oscar said. "I have four hundred vines and a cat who despises me."

"A cat is not a support system."

"He's very opinionated. It's almost like conversation."

Her mother made a sound that had ended many arguments in Oscar's childhood—a small exhale through the nose, the verbal equivalent of a door closing gently but firmly. "There's something else. And I want you to hear me out before you do that thing where you go quiet and pretend the call dropped. The signal up there is bad, I know, but not that bad, and I know all your tricks because I invented most of them."

Oscar looked at the window, where the fog was doing its slow burn-off over the vineyard rows, and felt the particular dread of a person who knows exactly which of her tricks is about to be discussed.

"You told your aunt Mariko," her mother said, "at Christmas, that you'd met someone."

The fog kept burning. The birds kept arguing. Somewhere down the hill, the old hotel sat shuttered except for one kitchen window that had been lit at midnight, and Oscar thought about that light with a strange, disproportionate longing, the way a drowning woman might think about a piece of driftwood.

"I may have mentioned," Oscar said carefully, "that my social life was not entirely barren."

"You said—" and here her mother's voice took on the flat, quoting tone of a prosecutor reading from evidence, "'I've actually met someone. He's wonderful. It's serious.' Those were your words, Oscar. Aunt Mariko repeated them to everyone. Grandma's sister cried. Then you stopped answering questions, which we all took to mean you were protecting something precious, and honestly? We respected it. We gave you space. We have been so respectful, Oscar, for months."

"That sounds like a threat wrapped in a compliment."

"It's both. Because now everyone is coming to the island, to dedicate your grandmother's garden, and they are expecting to meet him. The wonderful, serious someone. Mariko is bringing a pie. Ren has already asked me twice what he cooks. Your grandmother's oldest friend asked if he was 'tall, because Ingrid always said Oscar would need someone tall to reach the top shelves,' which is a strange thing to fixate on, but grief makes people odd."

Oscar closed her eyes. Five years ago, she had come to this island to bury her grandmother and save her vineyard, and she had learned quickly that the two tasks required opposite skills: grief wanted honesty, and survival wanted lies, and she had gotten very good at choosing survival. But she had never expected the lies to develop legs. To walk back into her life wearing a pie.

"Mom," she said. "What exactly happens if there's no one to meet?"

"Then Mariko starts the blind dates again. She has a list. She showed it to me at Easter. There's a man named Gerald who does something with insurance and owns a boat he talks about more than he sails. There's a sommelier who described himself as 'passionate about terroir' for forty minutes. There's—" her mother paused, and when she resumed, her voice had shifted into something softer and infinitely more dangerous, "—there's the fact that you would rather invent a fictional boyfriend than let anyone help you, and we all see it, sweetheart, and some of us find it heartbreaking."

"I'm fine."

"You're fine the way the vineyard is fine. Technically producing. Visibly struggling."

Oscar laughed despite herself, a short, startled sound, and hated how much it cost her to give her mother even that. "That's a terrible thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say, which is worse. So here's what's going to happen. We arrive on the Friday ferry. The dedication is Saturday morning. Dinner Saturday night at the vineyard—I'll cater the sides, don't argue—and everyone will meet this man. This tall, wonderful, serious man who apparently exists."

"And if he doesn't exist?"

"Then you'll have to stand up in front of the people who loved your grandmother most and explain why you lied to them at Christmas while they were grieving, and then Mariko gets to start the campaign again, and Gerald gets your number." Her mother sighed, and for one unguarded moment the armor slipped and Oscar heard just her mother underneath—tired, worried, loving in the blunt way that had raised three children on criticism and casseroles. "Or you figure something out. You're resourceful, Oscar. You've kept that place alive for five years on stubbornness and arithmetic. Surely you can produce one boyfriend."

She hung up before Oscar could answer, which was also a trick she had invented.

* * *

Oscar stood in the kitchen for a long time after, holding a mug of coffee she had forgotten to drink, watching steam rise off it in the gray light. The ledger sat open on the table where she'd left it at midnight—the numbers still balanced, barely, the way they always were, a life held together with arithmetic and denial.

She did what she always did when panic arrived: she made lists.

Option one: tell the truth. She imagined the phone calls, the follow-up questions, Mariko's wounded voice, her mother's look—not angry, worse, vindicated—and the slow, public autopsy of why Oscar Ishikawa, thirty-one years old, successful enough to keep a vineyard solvent, could not manage the simple human task of letting someone love her. She crossed it out before the pen finished moving.

Option two: confess the lie preemptively. See option one, plus pie-based guilt.

Option three: produce a fiancé.

She wrote that one down and stared at it. The pen hovered. Outside, the fog had burned off entirely, and the vineyard lay exposed in the morning sun, row after row of vines that her grandmother had planted and that Oscar had spent five years keeping alive without ever quite learning to love them the way Ingrid had—or maybe she loved them exactly the way Ingrid had, quietly and desperately and without ever saying so, which was possibly the whole problem with this family.

"There's no one," she said aloud to the kitchen. The cat, draped across the windowsill, opened one eye to confirm this.

But that wasn't quite true either, and this was the thought that arrived, unwelcome, carrying its own luggage: there was a man. A new cook at the hotel, taciturn as a locked door, who had rescued a pot of sauce at midnight and looked at her like he recognized something. She had seen him exactly once, for eleven minutes, and he had spoken perhaps forty words, half of them about reduction temperatures. And yet.

"No," she said to herself firmly.

The cat blinked.

"It's insane. It's the plot of a movie. A bad one."

The cat began washing his paw, which Oscar chose to interpret as agreement.

She spent the day among the vines doing the work that didn't require thinking—pruning, checking irrigation lines, scraping moss off the trellis posts—and by evening she had constructed and demolished seventeen plans, each more elaborate than the last. She could say he worked offshore. She could say he was a merchant marine. She could say he was in a coma. Every plan collapsed under the same weight: three weeks was not enough time to build a fiction that could survive Aunt Ren, who had once identified a counterfeit antique by smelling it, or her mother, who could detect a lie the way sharks detected blood, or Theo, who was kind but observant in the way of people who had never needed to hide anything.

Which left only the truth-shaped lie: a real man, really present, really willing.

And there was exactly one man on this island she could imagine asking, mostly because he seemed like a man who understood transactions, who had looked at her across a ruined sauce and seen not a customer but a colleague—someone else working late, someone else hiding.

She hated the plan. She hated how well it fit.

* * *

At nine that night she stood outside the hotel kitchen door with a bottle of wine she'd brought as a bribe and rehearsed the speech six times. Through the window she could see him—Silas, the new cook, though she'd had to ask the ferry agent for his name—moving through the dark kitchen with a competence that looked almost angry, wiping down surfaces that were already clean.

She knocked. He opened the door, looked at her, looked at the wine, and said nothing, which she was learning was his native language.

"Hi," she said. "Again. Sorry. Do you have a minute? It's—it's a business question. Sort of. Not really. Yes. Business-adjacent."

He stepped aside to let her in, which she chose to take as encouragement.

Inside, the kitchen smelled of stock and scorched sugar—he'd been practicing, she realized, burning things on purpose until they behaved—and she set the wine on the steel counter between them like a peace offering or a hostage.

"So," she said. "Here's the situation. My family is coming to the island in three weeks. All of them. For my grandmother's memorial garden dedication. And there's a complication, and the complication is that I told them at Christmas that I had a boyfriend, and I did that specifically so they would stop setting me up with men named Gerald, and now they're all flying here expecting to meet him, and he doesn't exist, and I need—" she heard her own voice climbing toward hysteria and reeled it back down, "—I need a boyfriend. Temporarily. Professionally. For one weekend."

Silas set down his towel. He looked at her for a long moment with those unreadable eyes, and she watched him assemble the full shape of what she was asking, and she braced for the laugh, or worse, the pity.

"You're asking me," he said finally, "to pretend to be engaged to you."

"Fiancé, technically. I may have used the word 'serious.' Possibly 'wonderful.'" She winced. "The details got away from me."

"Why me?"

Because you looked at me like you knew what hiding was. Because you're here at midnight burning sugar in an empty kitchen, which means you're running from something too. Because you're the only person on this island who doesn't ask questions.

"Because you're available," she said instead. "And because I'll pay you."

Something moved behind his eyes—not offense, exactly, but its cousin. "You'll pay me."

"Competitively. Room and board at the vineyard for the weekend. And a fee. I haven't decided the fee yet, but it'll be fair. More than fair. I'm told I overpay when I'm panicking."

He picked up his towel again, folded it once, set it down. She had the distinct sense she was being measured against some internal standard she couldn't see.

"No," he said.

The word landed like a dropped pan. "No?"

"No." He turned back to his station, and she understood she was being dismissed, and something in her chest that had dared, briefly, to hope, folded itself back up and put itself away. "Find someone else."

"There is no someone else. There's Gerald, and he has a boat."

"Then take Gerald."

"Gerald describes himself as passionate about terroir, Silas. He'd be dead by Sunday. Yours truly included."

He almost smiled. Almost. It flickered at the corner of his mouth and was gone, and somehow that ghost of a smile was worse than refusal, because it meant he could be reached, and being reachable was a vulnerability, and clearly neither of them could afford one of those.

"I'll leave the offer," she said, retreating toward the door with whatever dignity remained, "on the table. Metaphorically. The wine can stay literally. Think about it. Sleep on it. Panic about it, ideally, so we're even."

She was nearly gone when he spoke again, quiet, not turning around.

"What's his name?"

She stopped. "Whose name?"

"The fiancé. The one I'd be playing. What did you tell them?"

Oscar stood in the doorway with the cold night air at her back and realized, with a lurch, that she had never gotten that far. She'd invented a boyfriend at Christmas in a moment of desperation and had never bothered to furnish him with so much as a first name, and now the omission yawned open like a hole in a hull.

"I'll have to get back to you on that," she said.

She walked home up the dark hill with the fog rolling back in off the water, and somewhere between the hotel gate and the first row of vines, the panic she'd been holding at bay all day finally caught her, and she stood in the middle of her grandmother's vineyard in the dark and laughed until it turned into something else, because she had just offered money to a stranger to play a man who didn't exist, and he had said no, and she had left the offer anyway, and worst of all—worst of all—some traitorous part of her had already started hoping he'd say yes.

His name, she thought. He needs a name.

And standing there among the sleeping vines, she invented him: tall, because of Ingrid and the top shelves. Quiet. Good with his hands. Someone who cooked.

Someone, she realized with a fresh wave of horror, suspiciously like the man who had just refused her.

She went inside, opened the ledger, and under tomorrow's column of numbers she wrote, in small careful handwriting, a single line item that belonged on no balance sheet anywhere:

*Boyfriend (temporary): pending.*

Then she blew out the lamp and lay awake listening to the wind move through the vines, cataloguing everything that could go wrong, and falling asleep, eventually, to the image of a dark kitchen and a man folding a towel, saying *no*, and meaning, possibly, something else entirely.
