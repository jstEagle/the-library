## 5. What Silas Is Running From

The stock pot had betrayed him.

Silas Vane stood over it at eleven o'clock the first night, staring into the murky depths of what was supposed to have been a veal stock and had instead become something closer to regret. He'd misjudged the burner. The old gas range in the hotel kitchen ran hot on the left side, cold on the right, and hot in the middle only when it felt like it, which apparently was never. He'd known that within ten minutes of arriving. He'd used the left burner anyway, out of some stubborn muscle memory from a kitchen where the equipment obeyed.

The stock had scorched. Not burned—scorched, which was worse, because scorched was a slow insult. It sat there at the bottom of the pot like a confession.

"Great," he said, to no one.

The hotel kitchen was his now, in the way that a rented room is yours. Six burners, one oven with a door that groaned like a ship, a walk-in that hummed a half-step flat, and a prep counter scarred by forty years of knives that hadn't been his. The Marrow Island Hotel had seen better decades. Its restaurant, the Lantern Room, served dinner to perhaps twelve people on a good night, most of them hotel guests who'd already paid for the meal in their room rate and ate it out of a kind of fiscal obligation.

It was perfect. That was the whole point.

Silas tipped the stock into the drain, ran hot water until the smell gave up, and started again. He could do this all night. He had, in fact, been doing this all night, and would do it tomorrow night, and the night after. Cooking for paychecks was a strange, hollow discipline—like practicing scales for an audience that never came—but his hands still knew what to do, and his hands were the only part of him he trusted anymore.

* * *

Three years ago, Silas Vane had been somebody.

Not famous—chefs are famous the way weather is famous, everyone knows you and no one remembers your face—but *somebody*. Sous chef at Auberon, in the city, which meant he was the right hand of Marcus Ellery, and Marcus Ellery was the kind of chef whose name appeared in sentences that also contained the word *genius* without anyone laughing.

Silas had given Auberon nine years. He'd started as a stagiaire peeling shallots until his fingertips went translucent, worked up through garde manger, through the fish station where a single overcooked piece of turbot could earn you a silence that lasted the whole service, and finally to sous, where he ran the line while Marcus stood at the pass and made food that made grown critics put down their pens and just breathe for a moment.

He had loved it the way you love a thing that is actively hurting you. Sixteen-hour days. Burns layered on burns. A language of shouted orders and grunted assent that outsiders would have mistaken for abuse and which was, in its way, a kind of tenderness—the tightest family he'd ever had, bound together by heat and exhaustion and the shared religion of the plate.

And then the review.

Silas could recite it. That was the sickness of it—he'd read it maybe two hundred times and it had worn grooves into him like water into stone. The critic had come on a Tuesday. Tuesdays were the night the sous chef ran the pass, because Marcus took Tuesdays to develop new dishes, and so the review—the review that ran under the headline *The Emperor's New Menu*—had been, in every way that mattered, about Silas's kitchen.

*The cooking, tonight, has the flavor of a kitchen running on memory,* the critic wrote. *One senses the maestro is elsewhere. What remains is competent, occasionally lovely, and utterly without a soul at the pass. Auberon was never competent. Auberon was a miracle. Miracles, apparently, take Tuesdays off.*

Marcus read it standing at the pass, in the empty restaurant, at two in the morning, the way he read everything. He didn't say anything. He folded the paper into quarters, set it down next to the salt cellar, and went into his office, and Silas understood, with the terrible clarity of the guilty, that he had just watched a man's life's work be folded into quarters.

The fallout took four months to kill the restaurant. Reservations dropped. Investors got nervous. Marcus got quieter, which was the worst part—Marcus had always been loud, had always filled the kitchen with his voice, and now the kitchen filled with its own hum instead, and everyone flinched at every silence.

Silas tried to fix it. He worked harder, arrived earlier, left later. He took the pass seven nights a week. He stood where Marcus stood and tried to be a miracle and knew, every single service, that he wasn't. You can't be someone else's genius. It's like trying to be someone else's height.

The night Auberon closed its doors for good, Marcus shook Silas's hand in the empty dining room and said, "Nothing you did, kid. It was time." And then, at the door, without turning around: "Although it's funny. I always took Tuesdays off because the food was best when you ran it. You cooked like you had something to prove. Now look at you."

Silas had thought about that sentence every day for three years, and every day he understood it less, or understood it more—he couldn't tell which. Either Marcus had meant it as absolution or as an indictment, and the not knowing had followed Silas out of the city like a dog he couldn't shake.

He'd cooked at four places since. Good places, respectable places, places where the paychecks cleared and nobody shouted and nobody cared. He cooked cleanly, efficiently, without mistakes and without joy, and every chef he worked for eventually pulled him aside and said some version of the same thing: *You're wasted here.* And Silas would nod and say he knew, and six months later he'd be gone, because being told he was wasted was at least a kind of attention.

Then he'd seen the listing. *Cook wanted. Marrow Island Hotel. Room included. Must be self-sufficient.*

Room included. Island. Self-sufficient.

It read like a witness protection ad. He'd applied within the hour.

* * *

The trouble with running away to a small island, Silas discovered, was that small islands have a way of finding you things to feel.

He'd been at the hotel nine days when the woman came in.

He heard her before he saw her—the front door of the restaurant opening at an hour when nothing opened, footsteps crossing the empty Lantern Room with the brisk economy of someone who did not waste steps, and then she was standing in the kitchen doorway with her arms crossed, looking at him like he was an item on an invoice she intended to dispute.

Oscar Ishikawa. He knew the name the way you know the names on a small island—half the ferry crew said it with respect, the other half with the particular pity islanders reserve for people who are drowning politely. She ran the vineyard up the hill. The vineyard that was, according to the dock gossip, one bad season from becoming a cautionary tale.

She wanted a bar job. Fridays and Saturdays, she said, cash if possible. She said it the way people ask for things when they've already rehearsed the asking and pre-suffered the refusal.

He should have just said no. He was a cook, not a hiring manager; the hotel's owner, a perpetually sunburned man named Dev who appeared roughly twice a week, handled employment. But something in the way she stood there—chin up, eyes flat, the whole posture of a woman who had decided in advance that dignity was the only thing she'd be leaving with—made him want to poke at it.

So he'd poked. He'd asked about the vineyard. She'd deflected. He'd asked again. She'd deflected better. And then he'd said something about people who work themselves to death to avoid thinking, and she'd looked at him with those flat gray eyes and said, "And what is it you're avoiding, exactly? Since we're being honest."

He hadn't had an answer. That was the problem. Three years of running and he still didn't have an answer, and she'd seen the gap where the answer should have been, and something had passed between them—not warmth, exactly. Recognition. The ugly, grateful recognition of one fugitive spotting another across a crowded room.

She'd left without the job. He'd stood in the kitchen holding a rescued pot of sauce and felt, absurdly, like he'd failed a test he hadn't known he was taking.

* * *

The next evening, he watched her walk down from the vineyard.

He was out back of the hotel, taking the ten minutes of evening he allowed himself, leaning against the loading dock with a coffee that had gone cold an hour ago. The road from the vineyard curved down through the cypress trees and spilled out onto the coast road, and she came down it with her jacket collar up against the wind, moving at that same unsentimental pace, a woman with a list of problems and a plan for each one.

She didn't see him. She passed within twenty feet, eyes fixed on the road ahead, and Silas found himself cataloguing her the way he catalogued everything now, automatically, professionally: the tiredness carried high in the shoulders, the hands—working hands, capable hands, hands with a cook's scars on them, which was interesting—a small crease between her brows that suggested the list of problems had recently gained a line item.

And underneath the cataloguing, uninvited and unwelcome, came the recognition again. Sharper this time.

He knew that walk. He'd worn that walk for three years. It was the walk of a person who had decided that if they just kept moving, kept working, kept the books balanced and the tasks completed, the thing they were actually running from would get tired and give up. It never did. It kept pace. It was very patient, the thing you were running from. It had nothing but time.

She's not running toward anything, he thought. She's running in circles and calling it a road.

The thought had no business being in his head. It was none of his business. He was here to cook for twelve people a night and be left alone, and she was here to—whatever she was here to do, and the correct amount of interest he should take in it was zero.

But he watched her until the road bent and took her, and he noticed that he'd gripped his cold coffee hard enough to dent the paper cup, and he thought: *fellow fugitive*, and the thought sat in his chest with an uncomfortable warmth, like a coal that had rolled out of the fire.

* * *

She came back the next night.

He was mid-service—or what passed for mid-service, six diners murmuring in the Lantern Room—when Dev appeared in the kitchen doorway with an expression Silas had learned to read as *incoming complication*.

"Vineyard woman's here again," Dev said. "Wants to talk to you."

"To me."

"You're the one who talked to her last time. I don't know what you said, but she's got a look."

"What kind of look?"

Dev considered. "The kind my wife gets before I agree to something."

Silas wiped his hands and went out. She was standing by the bar in the empty restaurant, and she was not alone—no, she was alone, but she had the energy of a delegation. She'd dressed with intent: a clean shirt, hair pulled back, the posture of someone arriving at a negotiation she intended to win or at least survive with honor.

"Mr. Vane," she said.

"Chef Vane," he said, before he could stop himself, and then wished he hadn't, because it sounded like a man defending a title that had been revoked. Her eyebrows did something small and devastating.

"Chef Vane," she repeated, without mockery, which was somehow worse. "I have a proposition."

"I don't want a bar job offer twice."

"It's not a bar job." She glanced at the kitchen door, then back at him, and he watched her assemble the words like someone laying out knives before service. "I need someone to be my boyfriend. For one weekend. Three weeks from now. Paid."

The restaurant went very quiet. Somewhere in the kitchen, the flat-humming walk-in held its note.

Silas looked at her. She looked back, chin up, ears faintly pink, a woman who had clearly rehearsed this in the mirror and was discovering that the mirror had been more merciful than reality.

"No," he said.

Her jaw tightened. "You haven't heard the—"

"I don't need to. No."

"It's three days of work. You're a *cook*. You stand around being paid to be present. This is the same job with better company."

"That's a matter of opinion."

"Name your price. I'm serious. Whatever the hotel pays you for a weekend, I'll double it."

And there it was—the thing he'd seen the first night, the thing under the flat eyes and the brisk walk. Desperation, wearing competence like a coat. She was offering him double because she couldn't afford to be refused, and she couldn't afford to be refused because whatever waited for her at that family reunion was worse than humiliation. He knew the arithmetic. He'd done it himself, three years ago, when he'd taken a job he hated because staying in a city where he'd failed had felt worse.

"No," he said again.

Something flickered across her face—not hurt, exactly. She was too well-defended for hurt. It was more like a door closing, one of many, and he had the sudden, irrational conviction that behind her doors was something that had been locked a long time and would not survive being looked at directly.

"Fine," she said. "Thank you for your time."

She turned. And here was the thing Silas would think about later, in the dark, in the rented room that smelled of other people's cooking: she turned the way people turn when they've been refused before. Efficiently. Without ceremony. A person who has learned that lingering is losing.

She was at the door when he heard himself speak.

"The pay's an insult."

She stopped. Didn't turn. "You said no."

"I said the pay's an insult. You offered to double a line cook's weekend wage for what you're actually asking. Do you know what you're asking? You're asking a stranger to lie to your entire family, in person, for days, under scrutiny from people who love you and therefore watch closely. That's not a weekend gig. That's a performance. And performers charge."

Now she turned. Her eyes had gone sharp and calculating, which he recognized as an improvement over flat. "Then name a number."

"Triple."

"Triple."

"Plus expenses. I'll need a decent jacket. And we'd need to rehearse—our story, our history, how we met, all of it. Families interrogate. If we walk in there with a fiction full of holes, you'll be exposed by the second glass of wine."

Something moved through her expression that might, in a less defended person, have been relief. "You're negotiating."

"I'm a professional. Professionals negotiate."

"You said no twice."

"I did." He crossed his arms. "And you should know I think this is a terrible idea. You're buying a person, and what you actually need isn't for sale. But it's your money, and it's your family, and I've got nothing but time and a hotel room I'm starting to hate. So. Triple, plus expenses, plus rehearsals. And one condition."

Her eyes narrowed. "Which is?"

"We do this my way, in the details. If I'm going to be your boyfriend, I'm going to be a *good* one. Convincing. No half-measures. Half-measures get people caught."

She studied him for a long moment, and he watched her decide—watched the exact moment the calculation tipped, the moment she chose to trust a stranger because the alternative was worse. He knew that moment intimately. He'd lived in it for three years.

"Deal," she said. "When can you start?"

"Tomorrow. Come by after dinner service. Bring everything I need to know—names, birthdays, the family landmines. Every family has landmines."

"Mine has a whole minefield."

"Then we'll map it." He stuck out his hand, and she took it, and her grip was firm and flourless and calloused in the places a cook's hands callous, and he thought, again, *interesting*, and filed it away.

She left. He stood in the empty restaurant, listening to her footsteps fade down the coast road, and asked himself the obvious question, the one Dev was going to ask, the one any sane person would ask.

*Why did you say yes?*

Not for the money. The money was real but it was a pretext, a respectable answer he could hand out to anyone who asked, himself included. The truth was uglier and simpler and had been sitting in his chest since the night she'd walked past the loading dock with her collar up: she was a fugitive, and he was a fugitive, and three years of running alone had taught him exactly one thing about it.

It doesn't work. You run, and the thing you're running from keeps pace, and the only thing worse than being caught is being caught with no one there.

He didn't name any of this, of course. He went back into the kitchen, checked his mise, and started a stock—on the right burner this time, the cold one, because he was learning the stove's moods the way you learn a difficult colleague's, patiently, without hope of affection.

Three weeks, he told himself. One weekend. Triple pay, plus expenses, plus a decent jacket.

It was a job. It was only a job.

The stock began, very slowly, to simmer, and Silas Vane stood over it in the humming kitchen and did not believe a word of it.
