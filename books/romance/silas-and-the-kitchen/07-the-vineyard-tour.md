## 7. The Vineyard Tour

Silas called at seven in the morning, which was the first sign that something had gone wrong with our arrangement.

Nobody who was pretending to be in love with me should have been awake at seven in the morning. Real boyfriends slept in and sent apologetic texts at noon. Fake ones, apparently, telephoned before the dew had burned off the vines and said, in that low gravel of a voice he used for everything short of shouting, "I want to see the vineyard today."

"You've seen it," I said, propping the phone against my shoulder while I stirred coffee I wasn't going to drink. "You drove past it on your way to the hotel every day this week."

"I've seen the road. That's not the same thing." A pause, the sound of a pan being set down harder than necessary. "If I'm going to stand in front of your family and tell them I love a woman who pours her whole life into forty acres of grapes, I'd like to know what I'm defending."

"It's thirty-eight acres."

"Then I'll defend those."

I should have said no. I had a list of reasons ready — the trellises were mid-repair, the east block needed spraying, there was paperwork — but the truth was simpler and more embarrassing: nobody had asked to see the vineyard in five years. Not as a place. People asked about it the way you ask about a hospital patient, all sympathy and lowered voices, and then they changed the subject to Theo's promotion or Aunt Ren's hip.

So I said, "Be here by nine. Wear shoes you don't like."

* * *

He arrived at nine exactly, which told me he'd either been waiting at the end of my drive since eight-thirty or had calculated the walk from the hotel to the minute. He wore boots that had already given up on being liked, a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, and an expression of professional neutrality, as though touring a dying vineyard were a shift he'd clocked into.

"You look terrible," I said, because he did, in the specific way of a man who had slept four hours and would deny it under torture.

"You look like someone who's been awake since five," he said.

"One of us is honest."

"One of us is a liar professionally this weekend," he said, and held out his hand for the keys to the truck. "Which one are we being today?"

I gave him the keys. It seemed safer than answering.

The truck was my grandmother's — a 1987 Toyota with a bench seat, a radio that only received one station, and a passenger door that required the finesse of a safecracker. Silas got in, studied the dashboard with the gravity of a man inspecting a kitchen he'd been warned about, and said nothing when the engine turned over on the third try. I decided this was a point in his favor. I was keeping a list. It was getting longer than I liked.

"The tour has three stops," I announced, pulling onto the gravel track between the rows. "Stop one: the vines, which are dying. Stop two: the cellar, which is haunted. Stop three: the garden, which is overgrown. Any questions?"

"What's haunting the cellar?"

"Raccoons. But 'haunted' keeps the insurance adjusters sympathetic."

The corner of his mouth moved. It was barely anything — a millimeter of concession — but I'd spent two weeks cataloguing his face the way you catalogue weather, and I knew a near-smile when I saw one. I looked back at the road quickly, before he could catch me noticing.

* * *

We started in the east block, because I believed in leading with the worst news.

The vines there were old — some older than me, planted by my grandmother's father — and they showed it. The trunks were gnarled and gray, split in places where winter had gotten into the wood. Half the trellis wires sagged. The fruit, what there was of it, came in small and stubborn, clusters like clenched fists.

"This is Pinot Noir," I said, running my thumb along a trunk. "Or it was. The clone's outdated, the rootstock's tired, and honestly, half of these should have been pulled and replanted a decade ago. That costs money I don't have, so instead I prune them like they're fine and hope they don't notice I'm lying."

"They know," Silas said.

"I know they know."

He walked the row slowly, hands behind his back like a man in a museum, and I watched him do the thing he apparently did with everything: look properly. Most people glanced at the vineyard and saw green. Silas looked at it and saw the arithmetic underneath — the missing posts, the rust bleeding down the wires, the way the ground cover had gone to weeds in the third row because I'd run out of hours in August.

"You're down two workers," he said.

"How could you possibly know that?"

"There's grass to here." He pointed at his knee. "Somebody mows these rows every week until they can't anymore, then gives up around row twelve and never comes back. You're the somebody."

I didn't say anything. The morning was warm and smelled of dust and ripening fruit and, faintly, of him — some kitchen smell that clung to his clothes no matter how far he got from a stove, rosemary and char and something clean underneath.

"Why the east block first?" he asked.

"Because if you see it first, the rest looks better."

"That's a restaurant trick."

"That's a life trick."

He huffed — almost a laugh — and crouched to examine a cluster of grapes, turning them toward the light with a cook's hands, careful even of things he intended to be ruthless about later. "These aren't dead," he said. "They're tired. There's a difference."

"Don't say encouraging things. It ruins the tone of the tour."

"Noted." He stood, brushed off his knees. "What's stop two?"

* * *

The cellar was not actually haunted, unless you counted the raccoons, the wasps' nest above the north door, and the ghost of my grandmother, which I did count, privately, always.

It was a stone room dug into the hillside behind the house, cool even in summer, smelling of oak and damp and decades of spilled must. Barrels lined the walls in ranks, most of them empty now, sold off one by one the way you sell off furniture after a death — quietly, painfully, telling yourself you didn't need them.

Silas went down the steps ahead of me and stopped in the middle of the floor, turning slowly. I watched him take inventory the way I'd once watched him take inventory of the hotel's failing pantry: fast, unsentimental, thorough.

"How many barrels left?" he asked.

"Nineteen. There were sixty when she died."

"And the wine in them?"

"Mostly hers. Last few vintages she made herself. I've bottled some, sold some at the farm stand. The rest..." I touched the nearest barrel, ran my hand along the staves the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "The rest I can't bring myself to open. Which is stupid, because wine doesn't improve through grief. It just turns."

"It's not stupid," Silas said.

"It's a little stupid."

"It's a little stupid," he conceded, "but it's not only stupid." He crossed to the far wall, where a workbench sat under a window too small to matter, cluttered with her things — the refractometer with the cracked lens, the tasting notebook with its spine broken soft, a coffee mug shaped like a bunch of grapes that I had washed exactly once in five years and then put back, because washing it twice felt like letting go and once felt like enough.

"She made wine here," he said. It wasn't a question.

"My grandmother. Mirabel Amberdale — I'm named for her, which she found hilarious and I find increasingly heavy." I sat on the edge of the workbench, because my legs had opinions about standing. "She bought this place in 1971 when everyone told her a woman alone couldn't run a vineyard. She ran it for forty years. Made wine the critics ignored and the island loved. She was—" I stopped. Started again. "She was the loudest person I've ever known. In a good way. She laughed like she was daring you not to join in."

Silas leaned against the opposite wall, arms folded, and waited. He was good at waiting. It was possibly his single most annoying skill, the way he could make silence into a question you eventually answered just to fill it.

"She taught me to cook in this room," I said. "Not the house kitchen — here. Said a kitchen without stone walls was just a room with appliances. We'd crush grapes in the fall and she'd let me taste the must off her finger and make a face like I'd betrayed her if I didn't love it." My throat did something inconvenient. I kept talking so it couldn't become anything else. "That's why I went to culinary school. Everyone thinks it was a rebellion, the granddaughter of a winemaker going off to cook. It wasn't a rebellion. It was the most obedient thing I ever did."

The words came out with more weight than I'd planned, and I heard them land in the cool air of the cellar and wished I could gather them back up. Silas was quiet a moment.

"And then you came back."

"And then I came back."

"Why?"

And there it was — the question, the one I'd been dodging for five years from family, from neighbors, from myself at three in the morning. Why does a woman with a degree and a city full of opportunities come back to a bankrupt vineyard on an island of nine hundred people?

I had answers prepared. I reached for the standard one, the one I served like a house wine.

"The debt," I said. "She left the place mortgaged to its teeth. Somebody had to stay and keep it from the bank. It's not sentimental, it's bookkeeping. I'm good at bookkeeping. I stay, I pay it down, eventually I sell or lease or whatever makes sense, and then I get on with my actual life."

I delivered it smoothly. I'd had practice.

Silas looked at me for a long moment. The light through the little window fell across half his face and left the other half in shadow, which felt unfair, because I wanted to see what he was thinking and he clearly had no such curiosity about making it easy.

"That's not why," he said.

My stomach dropped in a way I recognized from bad audits and worse phone calls. "Excuse me?"

"You said it like a line item. You talk about the vines like they're yours — 'my east block,' 'my rows.' You keep a dead woman's notebook on a bench you never use. Nobody stays five years for bookkeeping." He shrugged, unbothered by the fact that he'd just peeled a layer off me in a cold cellar before ten in the morning. "But fine. It's the debt."

"It is the debt."

"Sure."

"Silas—"

"I said sure." He pushed off the wall and headed for the stairs, and then paused beside me, close enough that I caught the rosemary-and-char smell again, closer than the terms of our arrangement strictly required. His voice dropped into something gentler than his words deserved. "For what it's worth — I've lied about why I stay, too. Everybody who ends up on an island has. So I'm not asking again. I'm just saying I know the difference between a reason and a story."

Then he climbed the stairs and left me sitting on my grandmother's workbench with my heart going like a crusher-destemmer.

* * *

Stop three was the garden, and I nearly skipped it.

I nearly skipped it every time, honestly. The garden sat behind the house where the vineyard sloped down toward the water, half an acre that had once been my grandmother's pride — raised beds in neat ranks, a greenhouse with real glass, tomato stakes she'd painted red because "the plants should have something to aspire to." Now the beds had gone to grass and bramble, the greenhouse glass was cracked in a spiderweb pattern, and the tomato stakes leaned at angles suggesting surrender.

But Silas stopped at the gate — an actual gate, arched, wooden, with a latch shaped like a grape cluster — and said, "This is it, isn't it? This is the part you didn't want to show me."

"I show you everything else, you'll want to see this too. Tour logic."

"Liar," he said, mildly, and went through the gate.

I followed because following was easier than explaining, and because some traitorous part of me wanted to watch him see it.

He walked the ruined beds slowly. He read the garden the way he'd read the cellar — not the ruin of it, but the intention underneath. Where the tomatoes had been. Where the herbs still fought on, thyme and sage gone woody and wild but alive, alive, filling the air when he brushed past them. He stopped at the far corner, where a fig tree grew against the south-facing wall, planted too optimistically for this climate and thriving anyway out of pure grandmotherly stubbornness.

"Fig tree," he said. "On an island. In this latitude."

"She babied it. Wrapped it every winter. Swore at it in two languages."

"It's producing." He reached up and turned a fruit, swelling and green, and something in his face shifted — softened — in a way I hadn't seen before. All week he'd been granite and economy, a man who communicated in nods and ingredient lists, and now he stood under a feral fig tree looking almost young.

"She'd have liked you," I said, before I could stop myself.

He glanced back at me. "Why?"

"Because you look at things properly. She hated people who didn't look." I wrapped my arms around myself, though the day wasn't cold. "Theo walks through this garden and sees a liability. My mother sees an embarrassment. You see a fig tree that shouldn't exist and you touch it like it matters."

Silas was quiet. A bee investigated the sage blossoms and moved on. Somewhere down the slope, water slapped the rocks below the bluff, patient as it had been for ten thousand years.

"Why do you stay?" he asked again.

"Silas."

"Different question this time. I'm not asking about the debt." He turned fully to face me, and the morning light was behind him, and I couldn't read his eyes, which was probably the point. "I'm asking why you stay in the garden. You could let it go. You let the barrels go, you let the workers go, you let everything go except this half acre of weeds. You mow around it. I checked — the grass stops at the gate. You've been cutting around a garden you claim is dead for five years."

I opened my mouth to say something — anything, the ledger answer, the practical answer, the lie with clean lines — and found that in this garden, of all places, the lie wouldn't come. It sat in my throat like a stone.

"Because it's hers," I said finally, and my voice came out smaller than I meant it to. "Because if I let the garden go, then it's really over, and then I have to admit that I—" I stopped. Pressed my lips together. Started over, quieter. "There's a version of this where I tell you the truth, and it's a long story, and it involves a phone call I didn't answer and a night I drove home too slow, and I'm not — I can't do that story today. Maybe not this weekend. Maybe not ever."

Silas nodded slowly. He didn't push. God help me, he didn't push, and somehow that was worse than pushing, because it meant he understood exactly how much weight was in the sentence I hadn't finished.

"Okay," he said. "Today's not the day."

"Just like that?"

"Just like that." He picked a fig — one fig, the ripest one, quick and unapologetic as a thief — and held it out to me across the ruined bed. "Rehearsal rule. When your aunt asks how we met, we say the hotel kitchen. When anyone asks why you stayed on this island, you give them whatever story you want. But you don't have to give it to me. I'm the one person this weekend you don't have to perform for."

I took the fig. Our fingers didn't brush, which I noticed, which I resented noticing.

"That's not in the contract," I managed.

"A lot of things aren't in the contract." He turned back toward the gate, then paused, his hand on the latch shaped like a grape cluster. "Oscar."

"Yes?"

"For the record — the garden isn't dead. Neither is the east block. Tired, both of them. There's a difference." He said it lightly, like a man commenting on the weather, and went through the gate, and left me standing among my grandmother's weeds holding a fig I suddenly couldn't eat and couldn't put down.

* * *

We drove back along the top road with the windows down and the one radio station playing something old and crackling, and neither of us talked, and it was — I searched for the word and didn't like any of the candidates — comfortable. That was the word. Comfortable. With a man I'd hired. With a man whose last name I'd learned from a payroll form.

At the end of my drive he handed back the keys and stood by the truck a moment, working up to something. I recognized the posture; I wore it myself whenever I had to call the bank.

"Saturday," he said. "Your mother arrives Friday, yes? I'll come Friday evening. Early. Before the others."

"Early?"

"If I arrive with you already there, we look rehearsed. If I arrive early, we look eager." He almost smiled. "Eager is believable. Rehearsed isn't."

"You've thought about this."

"I think about everything," he said, which was the truest thing he'd said all morning, and turned and walked back down the drive toward the hotel, boots crunching on gravel, not looking back.

I stood there holding the fig until he was out of sight. Then I went inside, set it on the counter next to the sink, and told myself I'd eat it with lunch.

I didn't eat it with lunch. I didn't eat it at dinner either. By midnight it sat there in the lamplight, green-gold and patient, and I caught myself looking at it the way I looked at Grandmother's letter I hadn't opened, the way I looked at the phone number of the buyer in Seattle I hadn't called — at all the small sweet things I couldn't quite bring myself to take a bite of.

Three days until my family arrived. Three days until the performance began.

And somewhere between the cellar and the garden, the performance had already started slipping — because I'd caught myself, walking the rows beside him, forgetting it was a rehearsal at all.
