## 8. Fitting the Ring

Silas had a rule about props: if you were going to lie to someone's family, you did it with good materials. Bad props got people caught. A wilted bouquet said "gas station guilt." A cheap bottle of wine said "he doesn't know her at all." And an engagement ring—God help you—an engagement ring that looked like it came out of a gumball machine would have Oscar's mother calling the mainland police before the appetizers landed.

Which was why he was standing in the parking lot of Marv's Pawn & Loan on the mainland, three days before the ferry brought the family, holding a ring box in his palm and arguing with himself about the receipt.

The problem wasn't the ring. The problem was the money.

Oscar had paid him half up front, cash in a plain envelope, exactly as agreed. Two hundred a day for the weekend, plus expenses—"expenses" meaning anything he could justify with a receipt, which she'd said with the flat efficiency of a woman who balanced books at midnight and trusted no one's arithmetic but her own. He'd taken the envelope because refusing it twice already felt like cruelty dressed up as principle. But the ring was different. The ring sat in some gray zone between expense and confession, and Silas Ishikawa—no relation to Oscar, though the shared name had made them both laugh the first week, a startled, surprised laugh neither of them had expected—did not intend to let her pay for the one piece of this production that would touch her actual skin.

He'd seen what happened to actors in bad costumes. They stopped believing their own scenes.

So: his money. Four hundred and ten dollars, which was most of what remained of the savings he'd sworn he wasn't touching, the fund that used to be a restaurant and was now just a number in an account he checked less and less often, the way you stop visiting a grave.

He looked down at the box again. Inside was a thin gold band with a small stone—not a diamond, because Marv didn't really deal in diamonds, but a white sapphire that caught the parking lot light and threw off a hard little spark. It was simple. It was the kind of ring a man bought when he meant it and couldn't afford much, which was, ironically, exactly the wrong message for a fake engagement to a woman whose family expected a fiancé with prospects.

It was also, Silas admitted, standing there in the salt-stink air between a bait shop and a laundromat, the kind of ring he would have bought for real. If he were the kind of man who bought rings for real. Which he was not. Which was the whole point of him now: a man who cooked beautifully and felt nothing, who took jobs and left them, who kept his knives sharp and his heart in a dry storage area with the door locked.

His phone buzzed. Oscar.

*Where are you? The 4 o'clock boat gets in at 4:40 and I need to rehearse the proposal story again, I keep mixing up whether we went to the lighthouse first or the harbor.*

He typed back: *Mainland. Errand. Back on the 6.*

*What errand?*

*Ring shopping.*

He watched the three dots appear, vanish, appear again. Then nothing. He pocketed the phone, grinning despite himself. He could picture her exact face—the small frown she made when a number didn't come out even, the way she pressed her lips together like she was physically holding a question behind them.

He got in the truck and drove to the ferry landing to wait, and spent the whole wait not thinking about how the ring had felt in his hand when Marv dropped it into his palm. Light. Almost nothing. And yet heavier than any pan he'd ever lifted.

* * *

She was waiting at the dock when the ferry slid in, arms crossed against the wind, wearing the gray sweater she wore when she was worried and pretending not to be. She fell into step beside him as he came up the ramp, and they walked the length of the pier in silence until the crowd thinned and the gulls took over the noise.

"You bought a ring," she said.

"I bought a ring."

"With your own money."

"With my own money."

"That's not—we agreed on expenses."

"This isn't an expense." He shifted the paper bag from Marv's from one hand to the other. "This is equipment."

"Equipment." She said it the way she said *nineteen percent interest*, flat and dangerous.

"Every production needs equipment. You wouldn't ask me to cook on a cracked pan. I'm not going to propose to your mother with a ring that screams 'bought it at the ferry terminal gift shop.'"

"My mother is going to look at that ring and know exactly what it cost," Oscar said. "She has a gift. She once told my cousin her honeymoon was 'generous' in a tone that made my aunt cry for a week."

"Then she'll know I'm broke," Silas said. "Which is true, and therefore easy to maintain under questioning. Lying is exhausting. I only do it in kitchens."

That got a breath of a laugh out of her, quickly suppressed, and he felt the small victory settle somewhere in his chest where victories had been scarce lately. She walked a few more steps, chewing on it. He could see her doing the math—she was always doing math, the woman ran numbers in her sleep—and he knew the moment she arrived at the answer because her stride hitched.

"How much?" she said.

"It doesn't matter."

"Silas."

"Four-ten."

She stopped walking. Right there in the middle of the dock, with the ferry crew coiling rope around them and a tourist dragging a cooler past her elbow, she stopped and turned and looked at him with an expression he hadn't catalogued yet—a new one, somewhere between fury and something softer that she was clearly trying to drown in the fury.

"Four hundred and ten dollars," she repeated. "Of your own money. For a fake ring. For a fake engagement. That ends on Sunday."

"Yes."

"Why?"

And there it was, the question he'd been dodging since Marv's counter, the one he'd answered for himself at two in the morning with a honesty he had no intention of sharing aloud: *because you deserve to wear something real, even if the reason is a lie; because when your family looks at your hand, I want them to see someone chose you; because I've been watching you feed everyone on this island and keep nothing for yourself, and it turns out that bothers me more than it should.*

"Because," he said instead, "if I use your money, then it's a transaction all the way through, and transactions show. Your Aunt Ren will look at me across the dinner table and see a hired hand. But if it's mine—" he shrugged, "—then at least the ring is honest. Everything else can be a lie. Let the ring tell the truth."

Oscar stared at him. The wind pushed her hair across her face and she didn't fix it, which for her was the equivalent of dropping something and leaving it broken on the floor.

"That's the stupidest thing anyone's ever said to me," she finally said, "and last month Theo tried to convince me that biodynamic farming was 'just astrology for grapes.'"

"Thank you."

"It's not a compliment!"

"It sounded like one."

She made a sound that was half growl, half laugh, and snatched the paper bag out of his hand, and before he could react she'd opened it, popped the little box, and was staring down at the ring with her jaw tight.

"It's too small a stone," she said. Her voice had gone strange. Careful. "Her ring—my grandmother's—was a garnet. Big, ugly thing. She wore it to knead dough and it caught flour in every setting. I used to think it was the ugliest ring on earth." She snapped the box shut. "This looks like something a person buys when they mean it."

"Yeah," Silas said quietly. "That was the idea."

* * *

They argued the whole way to the truck, and then in the truck, and then—because Oscar refused to continue a fight while stationary—they argued in the parking lot of the ferry landing, standing between his truck and a rusted-out Datsun with a bumper sticker that read *I'D RATHER BE OYSTERING*.

"Let me pay you back," she said. Fourth time.

"No."

"Then let me buy it from you after. After Sunday. I'll keep it as—I don't know, a prop for future emergencies."

"What future emergencies require an engagement ring, Oscar?"

"I don't know! Another reunion! My mother's seventieth! You never know when you'll need a fiancé!"

"And you want to keep me on retainer."

"I want to keep the *ring* on retainer. You're—you're a separate line item."

He leaned against the truck and crossed his arms and looked at her, really looked, the way he'd learned to look at a sauce that was about to break—one second from ruin, still saveable, if you knew what it actually needed. What she needed, he suspected, was for none of this to cost her anything. Not money. Not gratitude. Not the terrible vulnerability of owing someone something that couldn't be repaid in an envelope of cash. Five years of paying her grandmother's debts had taught her that every kindness was an invoice waiting to arrive.

"Here's what's going to happen," he said. "You're going to wear the ring on Saturday. Your family is going to believe it, because it's true that I bought it for you, with everything I had on hand, because I wanted to. That part isn't acting, Oscar. I need you to understand that, so you can relax into the rest of it."

The wind filled the silence. Somewhere behind them, the ferry horn blew its long, mournful note, and a line of gulls lifted off the water all at once like a sheet being shaken out.

"That's a dangerous way to talk," she said at last, very quietly. "For a job."

"I've had dangerous jobs."

"Silas." She looked down at the ring box in her hand, turning it over, over. "Why are you really doing this? The real why. Not the equipment speech."

He should have lied. He was good at lying; he'd been doing it professionally for years, the great performance of being fine, of having left the city by choice, of cooking without wanting anything. Instead he heard himself say:

"Because when you talked about your grandmother's garden on Tuesday, you sounded alive for the first time since I met you. And because your family is coming here expecting to meet the man who makes you happy, and I thought—if he doesn't exist, somebody should stand in for him. Somebody should show up and be evidence that you're allowed to be loved. Even if it's temporary. Even if it's me."

Oscar's eyes went bright and furious, the way eyes do when they're losing a fight against themselves.

"You can't just say things like that," she whispered.

"Why not?"

"Because—" She gestured helplessly at the ring, the dock, the entire island behind her. "Because then Sunday comes, and you get on the ferry, and I'm standing here holding a ring some man bought me because he *pities* me—"

"That is not what this is."

"Then what is it?"

And God help him, he almost said it. Standing in a parking lot that smelled of diesel and low tide, four days into a lie, he almost said the true thing, whatever the true thing was, the thing that had been growing in him since she'd stood in his wrecked kitchen and rescued his burnt sauce without a single word of pity. He felt it climb his throat like heat up a pan handle.

What came out instead was: "Try it on."

She blinked. "What?"

"The ring. Try it on. If we're going to sell this, it has to fit. It has to look like it lives on your hand." He held out his palm, steady, and after a long moment she set the box in it, and he opened it, and he took the ring out, and the world went very quiet and very simple, the way a kitchen goes quiet at the exact moment the dish comes together.

"Give me your hand," he said.

She gave it. Her fingers were cold from the wind, and there was a small burn scar on the side of her thumb he'd noticed the first week and never asked about. He slid the ring onto her fourth finger, and it fit—of course it fit; he'd traced the size off a flour-dusted measuring spoon she'd left in the hotel kitchen and guessed the rest—and Oscar looked down at her own hand as if it belonged to a stranger.

"Oh," she said.

Just that. One syllable, cracked down the middle.

"It fits," he said, unnecessarily.

"It fits," she agreed, unnecessarily.

They stood there in the parking lot, two liars holding hands over a truth neither of them would name, and Silas became aware that he was still holding her fingers, that he had not let go, that letting go was going to require a decision and he was in no hurry to make it. Oscar didn't pull away either. The gulls screamed. The Datsun's bumper sticker grinned its idiot grin. And the ring threw off its small hard spark, honest as a knife.

"We should rehearse the proposal," she said finally, not moving. "In case someone asks how you asked."

"Right," he said. "How did I ask?"

"You—" She swallowed. "You burned something. You burn things sometimes, when you're nervous, which you never admit. And I was fixing it, and you said, 'Marry me, because clearly this kitchen needs both of us.' Something stupid like that."

Silas looked at her. His chest did something unprofessional.

"That's not stupid," he said.

"It's completely stupid. Nobody proposes over a ruined sauce."

"Somebody should." His thumb moved, barely, across her knuckle, next to the ring. "Somebody should propose over every ruined sauce. That's the whole point of a kitchen. It's where people bring the things they've broken and find out who stays to help fix them."

Oscar looked up at him then, and her eyes were doing the thing he'd started cataloguing without permission—the flicker toward the door, the inventory of exits, the accountant tallying what this moment might cost her later. He watched her decide, deliberately, to stay anyway.

"Silas," she said.

"Ferry's boarding soon," he said, because his voice needed cover. "We should go. You can drive. I'll navigate badly and blame you."

A laugh escaped her—real, unguarded, startled out of her like a bird—and she didn't take her hand back, so they walked to the truck that way, fingers laced, the ring catching the low sun, and Silas understood with a clarity that frightened him more than any review ever had:

The fake was starting to cost real feelings.

And he was no longer sure who was conning whom.

* * *

On the crossing, Oscar fell asleep against the window, her ringed hand curled in her lap like something newly hatched. Silas watched the island grow out of the haze and made himself a promise he had no business making: that whatever happened Sunday, however the lie ended, she would keep the ring. Even if she had to steal it. Especially then.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. A number he didn't recognize, mainland area code. He almost ignored it. Then he answered.

"Silas? Marcus Hale, First Coastal Bank. We've been trying to reach Ms. Amberdale about the vineyard account. There's been a development on her loan, and frankly, given the timing, we'd prefer to discuss it in person. We'll be attending the family gathering this weekend."

The line hummed. Beside him, Oscar slept on, dreaming whatever accountants dream, her hand glittering in the gray light.

"What development," Silas said.

But the line had already gone dead, and the island was coming up fast to meet them.
