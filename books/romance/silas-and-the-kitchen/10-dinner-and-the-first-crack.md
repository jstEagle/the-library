## 10. Dinner and the First Crack

I had cooked for harder rooms than this one. I'd worked a Saturday service in a restaurant where a food critic sat at table nine with a notebook and a grudge, where the sous chef threw a sauté pan at my head because I'd salted the blanching water wrong. I'd cooked through a grease fire, a health inspection, and the night my mentor found out his business partner had emptied the accounts. Rooms full of people who could end you with a sentence.

None of it prepared me for the Ishikawa family dinner.

The long table on the hotel's veranda had been dressed within an inch of its life—linens the color of cream, candles in jars, a centerpiece of eucalyptus that someone, probably Aunt Ren, had cut that morning and arranged with the casual authority of a woman who'd been doing it for sixty years. Nine place settings. Oscar had counted them twice, I'd watched her do it, her lips moving slightly, like the numbers might rearrange themselves if she looked away.

I'd been cooking since six. The menu had been Oscar's idea, delivered in a rapid-fire list at seven that morning while she stood in the hotel kitchen in her pajamas with her hair doing something ambitious, and I'd written it down because it was easier than arguing: roast chicken with the island's lemons, her grandmother's potato gratin—Aunt Ren had talked her through it over the phone twice—salad from the garden Oscar pretended not to be proud of, and a lemon tart that I'd made three times this week so it would come out right on the night it mattered.

It was a good menu. It was also, I realized somewhere around the second course, a message. Everything on that table said *I belong here. I know this family. I know this island.* Oscar had built her fake boyfriend a fake history out of real ingredients, and the realness of it was starting to worry me.

"Silas." Her mother, Vivian, had a voice like a well-kept garden—beautiful, and clearly maintained at considerable expense. "Tell us how you two met. Oscar is famously vague about the details, and I refuse to believe she proposed to you at a bus stop, which is the only story she's ever given me."

"It was the hotel kitchen," I said. "Late. She came in asking about a job."

"That's not romantic," said Theo's wife, Priya, who had so far eaten everything I put in front of her with the honest enthusiasm of someone who actually liked food, which had already earned her my permanent loyalty.

"No," I agreed. "It wasn't romantic. She insulted my sauce and I let her think she'd won the argument."

Oscar made a sound into her wine glass that might have been a laugh or a cough.

"And the engagement?" Vivian again. She wasn't smiling, exactly. She was doing something with her mouth that was adjacent to smiling, the way a customs officer is adjacent to welcoming you to the country.

"Also not romantic," I said. "There was rain involved. And a ring I nearly lost in a drain."

"That part's true, at least," Oscar said quickly, and I felt the table turn toward her, and I understood that we had made a tactical error: the true details were the ones we'd never rehearsed, and they came out of her with a warmth she hadn't planned.

* * *

It was Theo who broke the evening open.

He waited until the plates were cleared from the main course, until the wine was poured and the candles had burned down enough that everyone's faces had gone soft and golden, and then he set down his glass with the careful click of a man beginning a speech he'd been drafting for weeks.

"The vineyard's looking rough this year," he said. "I walked the lower rows this afternoon. Half the trellising needs replacing."

"The lower rows are on the list," Oscar said. Her voice was level. I'd learned her level voice by then. It was the voice she used when she was holding something very heavy at arm's length and pretending it weighed nothing.

"The list." Theo smiled at the table, inviting them into it. "Oscar's famous list. Five years, Oscar. Five years of lists. And what's the vineyard worth now, versus what Gran left?"

"Theo," Aunt Ren said, in the tone of a woman who had said *Theo* many times before and expected it to work this time the way it had never once worked before.

"No, I think it's a fair question." He spread his hands, the picture of reasonableness. "We all pretend it's fine. Mom pretends, Aunt Ren pretends, and now—" he gestured at me with his wine glass, an easy, inclusive motion that managed to make me furniture, "—now Silas pretends too, because he's new and doesn't know better. But somebody at this table should say it out loud. The vineyard is failing, and Oscar is going down with it, and nobody's done the math out loud."

The veranda went quiet. Somewhere below us, water moved against the dock pilings, patient and indifferent.

I looked at Oscar. She wasn't looking at Theo. She was looking at her plate, at the ghost of the gratin she'd made from her grandmother's recipe, and her jaw was set in the particular way it set when she was deciding how much of herself to amputate in order to keep the peace.

I knew that look. I'd worn it for two years after the review came out, sitting across from my mentor in a restaurant that had stopped being a dream and started being a crime scene, doing the math on how much of myself it would cost to stay and deciding, in the end, that the price was everything and paying it anyway.

I had promised Oscar a performance. I had a contract, verbal, three pages of rehearsed biography, and nowhere in it did it say *defend her.* Nowhere did it say *tell the truth in front of her family.* But she had cooked her grandmother's potatoes tonight, and she'd called Aunt Ren twice to get them right, and she'd stood in that kitchen at midnight tasting the cream sauce with a spoon and saying, quietly, like she was confessing something, "She'd have hated it. It's not right yet."

Nobody defends that woman. That was the whole problem with her. Nobody had defended her in five years, and she'd gotten so good at not needing it that she'd forgotten it was an option.

"The numbers," I said.

Theo turned. "Sorry?"

"You asked if anyone's done the math out loud. I have. She showed me the ledgers in our first month—she shows everyone eventually, it's how she apologizes for being busy." I kept my voice even, conversational, the voice I used for expediting a slammed kitchen. "The lower trellising is on the list because it's the third priority, not the first. First is the drainage on the south slope, which she fixed in year two, which is why the vines there outproduced the north rows by thirty percent last harvest despite being younger. Second was the press, which she bought refurbished instead of new and which has paid for itself twice over. The trellising is third because it's the most expensive and she doesn't spend money she doesn't have, which, in my experience, is called competence, not failure."

Theo's smile had developed a crack in it. "You've known her four months."

"Five," Oscar said faintly.

"Five," I agreed. "And in five months I've watched her do the work of four people for the salary of none. I've watched her turn down a buyer because the buyer wanted to rip out the old vines, and I've watched her eat dinner standing up over the sink because the books took until midnight." I picked up my wine, unhurried. "So yes, the vineyard is struggling. Struggling is what happens when someone refuses to do the easy thing, which is to quit. The math says she's losing. The math also says she's the only person at this table who's been fighting for something instead of just buying things that already won."

The silence that followed had texture. Priya was looking at me with her eyebrows up and her mouth curved, like she was watching a match she'd bet on. Aunt Ren had gone very still, the stillness of a woman memorizing something. And Vivian—Vivian was looking at me the way I'd once seen a head chef look at a young cook who'd just done something either brilliant or fireable, and hadn't decided which.

"Silas," she said pleasantly, "that was quite a speech."

"It wasn't a speech. It was arithmetic."

"Mm." She turned to Oscar, and her voice softened by one degree, which from her seemed to mean something enormous. "You didn't tell me he was good with numbers."

"You didn't ask," Oscar said. Her voice came out strange. I glanced at her and found her looking at me—not with gratitude, exactly, or not only that. There was something startled in it, something that hadn't finished deciding what it was. Like I'd done a trick she hadn't taught me.

I raised my glass slightly, an apology to the table, and said, "The tart's better than the argument. I'd recommend the tart."

* * *

Later, when the family had drifted inside and the veranda held only the two of us and the candles burning down in their jars, Oscar stood at the rail with her arms crossed, looking out at the black water.

"You didn't have to do that," she said.

"No."

"Theo's going to be unbearable now. He'll spend the rest of the weekend trying to beat you at something."

"Probably."

"You memorized my ledgers." She turned. The candlelight did her no favors and every favor at once; it showed the tiredness under her eyes and the thing she was trying not to let her face do. "Silas. Why do you know my drainage numbers?"

Because you told them to me at two in the morning over burnt sauce, I didn't say. Because you talk about those vines the way I used to talk about food, before, and I listen to you the way nobody listens to anybody anymore. Because when Theo said *failing,* I heard every person who ever said it about the restaurant, and I wanted him to be wrong about you the way nobody was willing to be wrong for me.

"Occupational hazard," I said. "I remember everything that matters in a kitchen. You're in the kitchen."

She looked at me for a long moment. The water kept moving against the pilings. And then her mother's voice came through the open door behind us, light and carrying, addressed to Aunt Ren but pitched, I was almost certain, to carry:

*"Ren, darling, don't be ridiculous. Nobody falls in love over drainage statistics."*

A pause.

*"Well,"* said Aunt Ren, *"somebody should."*

Oscar went inside. I stayed at the rail a while longer, looking at the water, doing my own math—the kind I was no good at, the kind with no ledger, where the numbers kept coming out wrong in a way I was beginning to suspect wasn't an error at all.
