## 11. Kitchen Confessional

*Oscar*

The dinner had gone on for three hours, which was two hours longer than any meal should last when the people eating it were related to you by blood and obligation rather than choice.

By the end of it, Oscar had learned several things. She learned that Theo's wife, Priya, was pregnant again and would be announcing it properly at the gala, once the timing was right. She learned that her mother had opinions about the vineyard's drainage that were somehow both entirely uninformed and delivered with the confidence of a hydraulic engineer. And she learned that Silas Ishikawa—her fake boyfriend, her hired hand, her five-day employee—could make her aunt Ren laugh so hard the woman had to put down her fork.

That last one was the problem.

He'd done it with some deadpan comment about the ferry's coffee being "less a beverage than a threat," and Ren had cackled, and her mother had smiled the small real smile she almost never spent on strangers, and Oscar had sat there watching it happen with a feeling in her chest she refused to name. Jealousy was ridiculous. You couldn't be jealous of a man you were paying by the day. That would be like being jealous of a rented tuxedo.

But he was *good* at it. That was the thing that kept snagging on her like a fishhook. He was good at her family in a way she had never managed to be. He listened to Theo's long stories about the mainland and found the one detail worth asking about. He let her mother fuss over his portion sizes and said, gravely, that he'd never been fed this well in his life, which was a lie so smooth it had to be professional. He remembered that Ren had mentioned, once, at lunch, that she liked her tea scalding, and he'd brought her a fresh pot without being asked.

Oscar had lived on this island five years and she still forgot how her aunt took her tea.

"You're quiet," Silas said, under the noise of the family gathering coats in the hotel lobby.

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like someone thinking. It's different from your regular quiet."

She looked at him. He was rolling his sleeves back down, the cuffs of his borrowed shirt—her father's, from a box of clothes that still lived in the hotel's back room—buttoned with the same precision he gave everything. He caught her looking and didn't look away, which most people did.

"The kitchen," she said abruptly. "I want to check the kitchen before bed. Hotel habit. Come with me."

It wasn't a hotel habit. She'd never once checked a kitchen in her life. But he followed her out of the lobby anyway, past her mother's sharp, curious glance, down the service corridor that smelled of bleach and yesterday's soup, and into the big empty room where they'd rehearsed their story three nights ago over trays of hotel leftovers.

The kitchen at night was a different animal. By day it was tired and fluorescent and faintly greasy. At midnight, with the overheads off and only the pass lights on, it went soft and blue, all stainless steel turned to moonlit water. Silas reached past her and flipped on the small radio someone had left on a shelf, and it came up mid-song, some old crooning thing with more strings than sense, and he turned it down to almost nothing, a heartbeat of music in the corner.

"Checking the kitchen," he said, dry.

"I panicked. It was the first lie available."

"You lie fast for an honest woman."

"I lie *often* for an honest woman. Speed came with practice." She hopped up to sit on the steel prep counter, her heels knocking against the cabinet doors, and the sound rang through the empty room like a bell. "God, that dinner. Did you see my mother's face when you said the thing about the ferry coffee?"

"I saw your aunt's face. Your mother's face I couldn't read."

"Nobody can read my mother's face. It's the family superpower. Theo got the jaw, I got the unreadability."

He leaned against the opposite counter, arms crossed, and studied her in the blue light. "You did fine tonight."

"I did *adequate*."

"You did fine," he repeated, in the tone of a man who had decided the conversation was over, and then, because he was Silas and therefore constitutionally incapable of leaving a kindness unaccompanied by work, he pushed off the counter and opened the walk-in. "There's half a case of cream that expires tomorrow. The hotel will throw it out."

"So?"

"So." He set it on the counter beside her hip, along with a bag of shallots, a block of butter, and a bundle of thyme that had seen better weeks. "We could make something. Or we could stand here checking the kitchen."

Oscar looked at the cream. She looked at him. Something in her chest, the thing that had been wound tight since the ferry dock that morning, loosened by a single degree.

"I'll chop," she said.

* * *

*Silas*

Here was the thing Silas had figured out about Oscar Amberdale in five days of pretending to love her: she moved like a cook when she thought no one was evaluating her.

At the family dinner she'd been all elbows and economy, hands in her lap, a woman at a hearing. But put a knife in her hand and she changed. She'd taken the shallots now, and she was cutting them in quick even strokes, knuckles guiding the blade, her wrist doing that small rolling thing that took years to learn and couldn't be faked. She didn't look at her work. She looked at him.

"You're staring at my technique," she said.

"I'm assessing my investment."

"You're not invested. You're *retained*."

"Same thing, on this island."

She snorted and went back to the shallots, and Silas turned to the stove and set the butter in the pan and watched it go from solid to foam, because that was the only prayer he knew anymore. The kitchen was quiet except for the knife and the radio's distant strings and the small hiss of butter finding its temperature, and it struck him, not for the first time, that this was the first time in fourteen months he'd stood at a stove and felt his shoulders drop instead of climb.

At Marchand's, the kitchen had been a war room. Every station a front, every ticket an enemy, his mentor Étienne's voice cracking over the pass like weather. He had loved it. God help him, he had loved it right up until the review came out, four hundred words that gutted the restaurant and Étienne with it, and Silas had learned that loving a thing publicly was just painting a target on it. So he'd come here, where nothing he cooked mattered enough to be loved or hated, and he'd told himself that was peace.

And then this woman had walked into his kitchen asking for a bar job with ledgers in her eyes, and he'd recognized her the way you recognize weather.

"Tell me about her," Oscar said suddenly.

He glanced back. "Who?"

"Your mentor. The one you won't talk about. You get a look when cooking comes up. Like someone walked over your grave."

The butter was browning. He gave it a stir. "Who says I won't talk about it?"

"You change the subject with your whole body. It's very athletic."

Silas huffed something that was almost a laugh. He let the shallots go in—they sizzled, sweet and sharp, filling the kitchen with the smell of beginnings—and said, to the pan rather than to her, "His name was Étienne Marchand. He ran the best kitchen I ever worked in. He taught me everything I use. And a critic with a grudge and a thesaurus wrote him into the ground, and six months later the restaurant was gone, and Étienne doesn't cook anymore. He sells wine now. He looks *fine*."

"And you think it was your fault."

The pan hissed. He hadn't said that. He was almost sure he hadn't said that.

"I think," he said carefully, "that I was his sous-chef, and I was the one who told him we were ready for that critic, and I was wrong."

Oscar set down her knife. He heard it, the small click of steel on steel, and turned. She was looking at him across the kitchen with the blue light on her face and an expression he couldn't name, something that had too much understanding in it to be comfortable.

"Silas—"

"Cream," he said, turning back to the stove. "It's about to break if we don't—"

"Silas." She was off the counter now, beside him, close enough that he could smell the shallots on her hands and something underneath, soap and salt air. "That's not how restaurants die. One wrong call doesn't kill a place. I've watched a business die slowly for five years, from the inside, and I'm telling you—it's never one thing. It's weather and luck and a hundred small cuts."

"Then why does it feel like one thing?"

"Because guilt is lazy," she said. "It takes the whole story and boils it down to one ingredient. It's a bad reduction."

He stared at her. She stared at the pan, ears going faintly pink, as if she'd said more than she'd meant to, and Silas thought: *there it is.* The ledger behind her eyes. The debt she carried that had nothing to do with the bank. He didn't know its shape yet. But he knew its weight, because he'd been carrying its twin for over a year.

"Bad reduction," he repeated. "I'm putting that on the menu."

"Don't you dare."

"Too late. *Oscar's Bad Reduction*, cream, shallots, thyme, and unsolicited therapy."

She laughed—the real one, the one that cracked her whole face open and made her look twenty instead of thirty-something and tired—and something in Silas's chest did a thing he declined to examine. He tipped the cream into the pan. It went gold and thick, and the kitchen filled with the smell of the kind of food that didn't exist on menus, the kind you made at midnight with a person you weren't supposed to be standing this close to.

"Here," he said, and held out the spoon. "Taste."

She tasted. Her eyes closed for half a second, involuntary, and he watched her do it and thought: *she's starving and she doesn't even know it.*

"That's," she said, and stopped, and tried again. "That's what my grandmother's kitchen smelled like."

And then her face did something complicated, and Silas watched her swallow whatever came next, watched her build the wall back up brick by brick in real time, and he understood that he had just seen the edge of the map. The place where her country ended and the blank space began.

"Oscar."

"It's nothing. It's late." She set the spoon down with exaggerated care. "We should—this was practice, right? For the gala. Family dinners. We should debrief. My mother asked you three questions about our first date and you gave three different answers, so—"

"First date," Silas said, playing along, because she needed him to. "You tell me. We've never established it."

She seized the lifeline. "The—there's a noodle place on the mainland, near the ferry terminal. You took me there. It was raining."

"I hate rain."

"You *love* rain. You told my mother you love rain."

"I told your mother what she wanted to hear. I'm a professional." He leaned his hip against the counter beside her. "What did we eat?"

"Noodles. You gave me most of yours because I was nervous and barely touched mine."

"Did I?"

"You would have. You give people your food. I've watched you do it all week. Theo got the last of the pork tonight and you hate pork."

"I don't hate pork."

"You pushed it around your plate like it owed you money."

That got the laugh out of him, low and surprised, and Oscar smiled at her shoes, and the radio played its faint strings, and the sauce on the stove needed nothing from either of them, and the space between them had gone small and warm and dangerous.

"Silas," she said, and her voice had changed, dropped its armor somewhere between one word and the next. "There's something I should tell you. About why I—about the vineyard, about my grandmother, she—"

The kitchen door banged open.

They sprang apart like teenagers. Oscar grabbed the spoon. Silas picked up a dish towel and began drying a pan that was already dry.

Aunt Ren stood in the doorway in her dressing gown, squinting at the light. "There you are. Your mother sent me. She wants to know if you two are—oh." Her gaze traveled from Oscar to Silas to the pan of sauce to the two feet of clear air between them, and her eyebrows performed a slow, eloquent ascent. "Am I interrupting the debrief?"

"Kitchen inspection," Oscar said, with the crisp authority of a woman who had never once inspected a kitchen.

"Mm." Ren came in anyway, dipped a finger in the sauce, tasted it, and closed her eyes. "Oh, that's your grandmother's shallot cream. She used to make that for the harvest workers." She opened her eyes and looked at Silas with something that had gone soft and sharp at once. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody. It's just what you do with expiring cream."

"It's what you do with expiring cream," Ren agreed, "if you were raised in a kitchen by someone who loved you." She patted his arm, and then Oscar's, and said, "Your mother wants a word before bed, Oscar. Something about the gala seating. Come find her when you're done—" a pause, delicately weighted, "—inspecting."

She left. The door swung. The radio played.

Oscar stood very still, staring at the sauce.

"She's right," she said finally, quietly. "It is her recipe. I didn't— I didn't tell you that. I didn't tell *anyone* that. How did you—"

"Maybe you told me without telling me. You do that. You talk in recipes." He said it lightly, but his chest had gone tight, because he was beginning to understand that this woman had a whole country inside her that she'd sealed at the border, and he was standing at the checkpoint with no papers.

She looked at him for a long moment. The blue light. The strings on the radio. The sauce going golden in the pan behind him.

"Silas," she said. "About what I was saying. Before Ren came in. My grandmother—"

Whatever it was, he never got it. Because she crossed the two feet of kitchen in one step, took his face in her shallot-scented hands, and kissed him.

It was not a practiced kiss. It was not a smooth one. It was slightly desperate and slightly off-center and it tasted, absurdly, of cream and thyme, and Silas stood there for one full second with his hands hovering in the air like a man who'd forgotten what hands were for.

Then he put them on her waist, and he kissed her back, and the pan could have burned down to the metal and he would not have cared.

When they broke apart, she was breathing fast, and her forehead was still against his, and she said, in a wobbly voice that was trying very hard to be professional: "That's— for the gala. In case anyone. Observational consistency."

"Right," Silas said. His heart was doing something unsanctioned. "Practice."

"Practice."

"We should probably practice again. To be thorough."

"We should." She kissed him again, longer this time, and the radio played, and the sauce finally, gently, began to catch on the bottom of the pan, and neither of them noticed.

* * *

*Oscar*

They ate the sauce at two in the morning, standing at the pass, straight from the pan with two spoons, because neither of them wanted to break the spell by finding plates. It was the best thing Oscar had eaten in five years, and she knew—it *knew*, in the place where she kept the truth she never looked at—that it wasn't the sauce.

She had almost told him. The words had been right there, loaded and waiting: *my grandmother died because of me, she borrowed against everything for a daughter who never came home, she died alone in a kitchen waiting for a phone call I was too busy to make.* She'd had the whole confession queued up, and then he'd been standing there with butter on his knuckles and that careful, wounded look, and she'd looked at his mouth, and her cowardice had chosen the exit that led to kissing him instead.

Some things you could do with your body that your soul wasn't ready to do with your voice.

"You're doing the thinking-quiet again," Silas said. He was watching her over the pan, his hair mussed where her hands had been, and he looked younger and more dangerous than he had all week.

"I'm debriefing."

"About the kiss?"

"About the *gala*. The kiss was—logistics."

"Logistics." He said it slowly, tasting the word, and something flickered in his face that she couldn't read—hurt, maybe, or its cousin. "Right. Logistical."

"Silas."

"It's fine, Oscar. It's practice. I know what I'm for." He said it lightly, but not lightly enough, and the words landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there, because the terrible thing was that he *didn't* know what he was for. He'd stopped being a hire around the time he'd defended her to Theo with that surgical precision. He'd stopped being a hire, possibly, the moment he'd bought a ring with his own money rather than let her fund the fiction.

And she was kissing him in the dark and calling it rehearsal, which made her either a liar or a coward, and she'd had five years of evidence suggesting both.

"Come on," she said, instead of any of that. "It's late. My mother wants a word, and if I don't go she'll send Theo next, and Theo will make it weird."

"Everything's already weird."

"Everything's already *ours*," she corrected, and then heard herself, and went scarlet, and fled toward the door while he laughed behind her, low and warm, the laugh of a man who had just been handed something and knew exactly what to do with it.

At the door she stopped. Turned. He was still at the pass, the pan between his hands, the blue light on him, looking like he belonged in her kitchen more than she ever had.

"Silas. The thing I was going to tell you. About my grandmother. I'm going to tell you. I just—" Her throat closed on it. She tried again. "Soon."

He looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him set the pan down and give her the whole of his attention, quiet and total, like a held breath.

"Whenever you're ready," he said. "I'm not going anywhere."

*Not going anywhere.* She carried the words down the corridor like a coal in cupped hands, all the way to her mother's door, where she knocked and was let in and sat on the edge of the bed while her mother looked at her for a long, long moment with those unreadable eyes.

"You smell like shallots," her mother said.

"Kitchen inspection."

"Mm." Her mother folded her hands. "Oscar. That young man is in love with you."

The room went very still.

"Mother—"

"I've watched him for two days. He watches the door when you leave a room. He gave your aunt his seat by the window. He made that sauce without a recipe, from memory, from *her* memory, and don't ask me how, because I don't know, but I know what it means when a man cooks a dead woman's food like it's his own." Her mother's voice was even, almost gentle. "The question isn't whether he's in love with you. The question is whether you're going to keep pretending you hired him, or whether you're going to be brave enough to lose him honestly."

Oscar opened her mouth. Closed it. Her mother had always done this—found the exact seam in her and pressed one finger to it, lightly, like a surgeon.

"Goodnight, Mother."

"Goodnight, sweetheart." And then, as Oscar reached the door: "The sauce was excellent, by the way. Ren saved me some. It tasted like home."

Oscar made it three steps down the corridor before she had to stop and press her hand flat against the wall and breathe.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told anyone, the thing she'd almost told Silas at midnight with cream on the stove: her grandmother had died on a Tuesday, alone, in the kitchen, with a pot of that exact sauce on the stove and Oscar's name in the last unsent draft of a letter—and Oscar had been on the mainland, at school, too busy, too proud, too certain the vineyard would always be there, and by the time she'd come home the sauce had burned down to nothing and the kitchen had smelled like the end of something.

She was going to tell him. She was going to stand in front of him and say it out loud and let him see the whole ugly map of her.

But not yet. Not yet. First there was the gala, and the garden, and the ring on her finger that was starting to feel less like a prop and more like a debt of a different kind.

First, she had to survive being loved.

She pushed off the wall and walked back down the corridor toward her room, past the kitchen where the light still burned, where a man who cooked dead women's recipes from memory was washing a pan and humming, very quietly, to a radio with more strings than sense.

The line was crossed. There was no un-kissing him now.

The only question—the one that sat in her chest like the last ferry of the season, loading, boarding, ready to pull away—was whether she'd find the courage to say the true thing before the truth said itself.

In the garden, three days from now, at her grandmother's memorial, Aunt Ren was going to open her mouth, and everything Oscar had spent five years burying was going to come up out of the ground like something planted there on purpose.

She just didn't know it yet.
