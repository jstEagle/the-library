## 12. The Garden Dedication

The morning of the dedication, the sky did that thing island skies do when they want to be dramatic about grief—went silver and soft, like the whole horizon had been wrapped in gauze. Oscar stood at her bedroom window in the good black dress she'd bought five years ago for the funeral and had worn exactly twice since, both times to weddings where she'd spent the reception hiding near the cake table.

"You'll crease it," Silas said from the doorway. He was already dressed, dark slacks and a gray shirt with the sleeves rolled precisely to his forearms, looking less like a hired boyfriend and more like a man who belonged at a memorial. It was unsettling how good he was at this. He'd been on the island four days and he'd already charmed Aunt Ren into showing him photographs, gotten Theo to talk about sailing without once glancing at Oscar for rescue, and made her mother laugh—a real laugh, not the polite one, the one that came out of her like a bird escaping a cage.

Oscar smoothed the dress anyway. "I don't crease. I press."

"That's not a sentence that means anything."

"It means I'm holding up fine."

He studied her the way he studied a sauce that was about to break—one eyebrow slightly raised, head tilted, deciding whether to intervene or let it go. "You've rearranged your dresser three times since six o'clock," he said. "The top drawer is now organized by weight."

"By *frequency of use*."

"By weight." He crossed the room and, before she could object, took her hands. His were warm and dry and smelled faintly of rosemary, because the man apparently seasoned himself through osmosis at this point. "It's a garden," he said. "For your grandmother. You plant something, you say some words, you cry if you want to or you don't. Then there's food. I'm making the food. Everything after the food is optional."

"She hated gardens."

Silas blinked. "Then why are we dedicating one?"

"Because Grandpa loved them, and when he died she kept his going out of spite and then out of habit and then, I think, out of love, though she'd have denied all three under oath." Oscar's throat did something complicated. "She used to say gardening was just cooking with worse hours and no tips."

Something moved behind Silas's eyes—the look he got when she said something about her grandmother that was too specific, too alive, a detail she hadn't meant to give away. He'd been collecting those details all weekend like a man quietly filling a ledger. She kept catching him doing it: listening to Theo's stories, to Ren's, filing things away. She didn't know what he was saving them for.

"Come on," he said. "Let's go dedicate a spite garden."

* * *

The vineyard's old kitchen garden sat below the house, terraced down the hillside in stone beds her grandfather had built before Oscar was born. Five years of neglect had turned it feral—rosemary gone woody and enormous, tomato cages swallowed whole by vines that weren't tomatoes, a fig tree leaning at an angle that suggested it had given up on dignity sometime around year two. The family had spent yesterday afternoon clearing what they could, and even so it looked less like a memorial and more like an archaeological site.

They'd set up folding chairs in two crooked rows. A small podium. A banner someone had ordered online that read IN LOVING MEMORY OF ELEANOR AMBERDALE in a font that looked like it belonged on a grand opening. Her mother had already adjusted it twice, straightening it by degrees that only she could perceive.

The family assembled itself the way families do—Theo and Priya arriving early and helpfully, moving chairs; her mother directing; Aunt Ren drifting in last with a canvas tote bag full of seed packets and no explanation. And the neighbors, because on an island a dedication is a public event whether you invite people or not: the ferry captain, the hotel's ancient owner Mrs. Okonkwo, the woman who ran the chandlery, a scattering of others who had known Eleanor Amberdale for forty years and had opinions about her that ranged from "formidable" to "terrifying," often in the same sentence.

Silas stood at Oscar's shoulder through all of it, his hand resting at the small of her back with a lightness that was somehow more convincing than a grip would have been. When Mrs. Okonkwo squinted at him and asked, "And who is this?" he said, "Silas. I'm Oscar's—" and paused, and looked at Oscar, and let the pause do the work, and Mrs. Okonkwo's face went through delight, surprise, and fierce approval in the space of two seconds.

"*Finally*," Mrs. Okonkwo said, patting his arm. "I told your grandmother, Oscar, I said, that girl needs someone who cooks. You can't live on ambition alone."

"I never lived on ambition," Oscar said. "Mostly coffee."

But her chest had gone tight in a way she didn't examine. Because that was the trouble with this weekend, the thing she'd been refusing to look at since the ferry docked: everyone kept saying *your grandmother*, and every time they did, they handed Silas another piece of her she hadn't planned to give away.

Her mother opened the ceremony, because her mother always opened things. She spoke about Eleanor's strength, her standards, her refusal to be photographed smiling ("she said smiling for cameras was lying with your face"). Theo spoke next, polished and warm, a story about being allowed to drive the tractor at eight years old and immediately driving it into the irrigation ditch, which got the laugh it was engineered to get. Priya read a poem. The ferry captain told a story about Eleanor threatening to report him to the maritime authority over a delayed crossing and then bringing him a thermos of soup anyway, "because delays are the schedule's fault, not yours, you great useless man."

And then Aunt Ren stepped up to the podium, and Oscar felt the first wrong note, though she couldn't have named it yet—just a shift in the air, the way pressure changes before weather.

Ren was the youngest of the siblings, sixty-something with silver hair cut short and a habit of wearing men's watches, and she had been closer to Eleanor than anyone. She unfolded a piece of paper, looked at it, and folded it again.

"I wrote something," she announced. "And then I threw it away, because Eleanor would have called it sentimental rubbish. So instead I'm going to tell you something true, and it isn't a nice story, but she never told nice stories either, so I think she'd approve."

Oscar's mother shifted in her seat. "Ren—"

"Hush, Marta. This is mine to say." Ren's voice wasn't loud, but it carried the way voices carry outdoors, flat and clear across the rows of vines. "Five years ago, Eleanor came to me in the spring, very upset. Not about the harvest, not about the bank. About a letter she'd received. An acceptance letter." Ren's eyes found Oscar's, steady and unblinking. "Culinary school. On the mainland. Full tuition, room, board—the works. And a deadline to accept."

The world went very quiet and very loud at the same time. Somewhere behind Oscar, Silas went still.

"Eleanor was proud," Ren said. "Proudest I ever saw her, and I include the day the vineyard won its first medal. But proud doesn't pay tuition, and the bank had already said no to another loan—she'd been carrying this place on a thread for years by then. So she went to a private lender. Terrible terms. The kind of terms that eat a property slowly, from the inside." Ren's voice roughened. "She signed it in my kitchen. I watched her do it. I said, 'Ellie, this will bury you.' And she said—" Ren stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "She said, 'It's not a debt, Ren. It's a down payment. On her.'"

Oscar heard the sound she made herself, a small involuntary noise, half breath, half something else, and became aware distantly that several heads had turned toward her. She couldn't look at any of them. She was looking at the fig tree, at its defeated lean, at the stone wall her grandfather had built, and the numbers were rearranging themselves in her head with terrible, patient clarity.

Five years of ledgers. Five years of midnight arithmetic, of selling equipment, of skipping her own salary, of lying awake doing sums against a debt she had believed was inherited—simply inherited, the way you inherit eye color or a bad knee, a fact of blood and time. She had built her whole life on top of that belief. She had organized her guilt around it like a spine.

The debt was hers. Every cent of it. Her grandmother had traded the vineyard—had traded *this*, the terraces, the medal-winning slopes, the whole slow dream of fifty years—for a girl who had lasted eleven months in culinary school before quitting, coming home, and never telling anyone why.

"—and I know Eleanor would want that remembered today," Ren was finishing, "because she never once regretted it. Not once. I asked her, near the end. I said, was it worth it? And she looked at me like I was simple and said, 'Ask me if the grapes were worth the wine.'"

There was scattered, wet-eyed applause. Someone blew their nose. Her mother was crying openly, and Theo had his arm around Priya, and everyone was looking at the podium, at Ren, at the banner, anywhere but at the granddaughter sitting rigid in the third row with her hands locked in her lap like a woman gripping a rail in a storm.

Everyone except Silas. She could feel him looking at her. She could feel it the way you feel sun through a window.

* * *

Afterward there was planting, because Ren had brought seed packets and decreed that grief required dirt under the fingernails. The family knelt along the raised beds turning soil and arguing gently about spacing, and Oscar planted a row of basil with mechanical precision while conversations happened around her like weather.

"You okay?" Theo murmured, crouching beside her. His voice was careful in the way it got when he was managing her, which was the way it had gotten since they were children and he'd decided, at nine, that his little sister needed protecting from everything including her own decisions.

"Fine."

"Oscar."

"Theo. I'm fine. It was a lovely ceremony."

He looked at her a moment longer, and she saw the offer forming behind his eyes—the same offer that had been forming all weekend, patient as tide—and she stood up abruptly and said she should check on the food.

The food was fine. The food was always fine; Silas had taken over the hotel kitchen that morning and produced a spread that had Mrs. Okonkwo asking, audibly, whether he was single, and Silas answering, deadpan, "Taken, ma'am," with a glance at Oscar that made the chandlery woman sigh romantically. Oscar ate nothing. She held a glass of the vineyard's own wine and watched her hand tremble slightly and put the glass down before anyone noticed.

It was Ren who found her, eventually, standing alone by the stone wall above the garden.

"You're angry with me," Ren said. Not a question.

"No."

"You're angry with yourself. Different animal entirely, but people confuse them." Ren leaned on the wall beside her, gnarled hands folded on the stone. Below them the family laughed at something Theo said, and the new-turned soil lay dark between the beds like open pages. "I debated whether to say it today. Your mother voted no. Theo voted no. I outvoted them."

"Why?"

"Because secrets rot, Oscar. I watched one rot your grandmother from the inside for five years. She carried that loan like a stone in her chest, and she never told you, and do you know why?"

"Because she didn't want me to feel guilty." The words came out flat.

"Because she didn't want you to *quit living*." Ren turned to face her fully. "There's a difference. Guilt is a weight you carry. Shame is a room you lock yourself in. She was terrified you'd move into the room."

Too late, Oscar thought, and couldn't stop the thought, and hated it.

"Eleven months," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange, far away. "I lasted eleven months and I quit and I never even told her I quit—I let her find out from the school, and she never said a word to me about it, she just—made dinner like always, and I thought—" Her breath caught. "I thought she was disappointed. I built five years on her being disappointed. And the whole time she was paying for it. She was paying for my failure with the vineyard. With *everything*. And then she died and left me the bill."

"Oscar—"

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't tell me she didn't blame me. Don't tell me it wasn't my fault. I've done the math, Ren. I'm good at math. It's the one thing I'm actually good at." She was aware her voice was rising, aware of the family below going quiet, aware of Silas at the edge of her vision turning toward the wall. "Every payment I've made for five years has been penance. That's what I told myself. That's how I survived it. And now it turns out the penance was for a crime I committed against a woman who—" Her voice broke, cleanly, horribly, in front of everyone. "Who never blamed me at all. Who thought I was worth the wine."

The silence on the hillside was total. Even the wind seemed to hold its breath.

Oscar didn't wait for comfort. Comfort was the one thing she could not survive just now; she knew this with absolute certainty, the way you know not to touch a hot pan. She walked—fast, then faster—out past the garden wall, up through the vines, following the row she knew best, the south slope where her grandmother had spent every September of Oscar's childhood, until the voices faded behind her and there was only the rattle of leaves and her own ragged breathing and five years of ledgers burning down to ash in her chest.

She didn't hear footsteps behind her. But she felt them—steady, unhurried, familiar—and she knew, with a certainty that frightened her more than the debt ever had, exactly who had followed.

"Go away, Silas," she said, to the vines.

He didn't go away.
