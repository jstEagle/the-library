## 16. Aunt Ren's Kitchen

The morning after the gala, the house felt like a theater after closing night—chairs pushed back at wrong angles, glasses abandoned on every surface, and a strange hush where all that noise had been. Oscar moved through it with a trash bag and the particular numbness of someone who had been publicly informed, in front of her entire family, that she had thirty days to produce a sum of money roughly equivalent to a small moon.

Her mother had left on the early ferry without saying goodbye. That was its own kind of message.

Theo had stayed behind—"just one more day," he'd said, in the tone of a man who intended to make his offer again over breakfast—but Oscar had fled to Aunt Ren's cottage before he woke, claiming she needed help sorting Grandmother's kitchen things for the estate inventory. It was even true. It was also an escape route, and they both knew it.

Aunt Ren's cottage sat at the far end of the vineyard road, small and crooked and painted the color of weak tea, with a garden that looked like Grandmother's garden's better-behaved sister. Ren was already on the porch when Oscar arrived, wearing her gardening apron and an expression that suggested she had been waiting.

"You look terrible," Ren said fondly.

"Thank you. I've been working on it."

"Come inside. I made coffee strong enough to strip paint, which is what you need, not whatever your mother drinks."

Inside, the cottage smelled of rosemary and old wood and something else—something warm and yeasty that stopped Oscar just inside the door like a hand on her chest.

"What is that?" she asked. "What are you baking?"

Ren didn't answer right away. She poured two cups of coffee, set them on the table, and then stood looking out the window at her garden for so long that Oscar started to fidget.

"I found her recipe box last week," Ren said finally. "Going through things. For the dedication." She turned around. "There's a card in it I've never seen. Her handwriting, but late—shaky, from the last year. And I've been sitting on it like a coward, because I wasn't sure what to do with it."

Oscar's stomach did something complicated. "What kind of card?"

"A recipe," Ren said. "One I thought was lost."

* * *

The recipe box sat on the counter between them like a patient at an examination. It was tin, dented at one corner, with a faded picture of lemons on the lid. Oscar had grown up watching Grandmother pull cards from it without ever consulting them—the recipes lived in her hands, not the paper. After she died, Oscar had gone through the box twice, looking for the one thing Grandmother had never written down: the braised lamb she'd made every harvest festival, the dish everyone begged for, the dish she always said she'd teach Oscar "when you're ready."

She had died before Oscar was ready. Of course she had. That was how everything went, in Oscar's experience.

Ren slid the card across the counter.

It was Grandmother's handwriting, unmistakably, but thinner than Oscar remembered, the letters leaning like tired people. At the top: *Lamb, festival — for O.*

"For O," Oscar read aloud. Her voice came out strange. "For Oscar?"

"Who else do you know whose name starts with O?" Ren said gently. "Read it."

Oscar read it. The ingredients were there—shoulder, garlic, the wine, a scandalous amount of butter—and then the method, and then, crammed into the bottom margin in even smaller script, as if Grandmother had run out of room and refused to stop:

*The secret is you cannot rush it. You have to stay in the room. All day. That is the whole recipe, really. Stay in the room. She never learned that yet. She will.*

"She wrote that about me," Oscar said. "Before I—" Before culinary school. Before the debt. Before any of it. "She was writing this while I was still—" Still what? A kid who burned things? A girl who wanted to cook and hadn't yet learned to be ashamed of wanting?

"She was writing it about you," Ren confirmed. "I think she meant to give it to you herself. At some harvest festival when she decided you were ready. And then—" Ren's hands flattened on the counter. "And then she didn't get the chance, and I found it in a tin box a week ago like a coward who couldn't bear to open a drawer."

"Why didn't you give it to me at the funeral?"

"Because I'm a coward," Ren repeated, without shame this time, and something in her face cracked open. "Because giving it to you meant admitting she'd known she was running out of time. Because she wrote 'for O' and I couldn't say your name for three months after. Take it up with my therapist, who does not exist, because our family doesn't believe in those."

Despite everything, despite the thirty days and the called loan and Theo waiting somewhere with his papers, Oscar laughed—a wet, broken sound.

"There's more," Ren said quietly.

"Don't say there's more."

"There's more. Sit down."

* * *

They sat at the little table by the window, and Ren brought out an envelope. Not old—new, actually, the kind sold in packs at the island's single shop—but addressed in Grandmother's handwriting, which meant it must have been sealed long before it was ever mailed, or rather never mailed at all.

"This was with the recipe box," Ren said. "In an inner pocket. Addressed to you. I opened it last night because I couldn't stand not knowing whether it was something private or something the whole family should hear, and now I've committed a small crime against my niece and I'd like forgiveness, eventually, but first I'd like you to read it."

Oscar took the envelope the way you take something hot out of an oven—quickly, before you can talk yourself out of it.

The letter was one page. Grandmother had never been a woman of many words on paper; her letters were famously terse, mostly logistics and weather reports. This one was different.

*My Oscar,*

*If you're reading this, I've managed to die, which I always said I'd do on my own schedule and I hope I kept my word.*

*I want to tell you about the money. You'll find out someday anyway—you're too good with numbers not to—and I want you to hear it from me. When you got into that school, I was so proud I couldn't see straight. Your mother said we couldn't afford it. Your mother was right, the way she usually is, and I didn't care. So I went to the bank and borrowed against the vines, and I would do it again tomorrow, and I have never once regretted it, and I don't want you to either.*

*A vineyard is just dirt and weather and luck. I built nothing here worth building except a place where my granddaughter could grow up wanting things. If the price of that was the land, then the land was cheap.*

*Don't you dare feel guilty. Guilt is love with nowhere to go, and I have given you plenty of both.*

*You'll be better than me. You already cook better than me, and I'll deny saying that if anyone asks.*

*Stay in the room, my darling. Whatever happens. Stay in the room.*

*—Grandmother*

Oscar read it twice. Then a third time, because the words kept sliding off her, refusing to be absorbed, like water off waxed paper.

*Proud.* The word sat in the middle of her chest like a stone dropped in still water, ripples going out and out and out through five years of ledgers and skipped meals and nights spent doing arithmetic instead of sleeping. Five years she had believed she was paying off a sin. Five years she had carried the debt like a sentence, like proof of what she'd cost everyone, like the reason she deserved the small gray life she'd built—head down, books balanced, heart locked.

And the whole time, the truth had been sitting in a tin box in her aunt's kitchen: *the land was cheap. Don't you dare feel guilty.*

"Oh," she said. It was not an adequate word. It was the only one available.

Ren reached across the table and put her hand over Oscar's. Her fingers were garden-warm and steady.

"I watched you at that ceremony yesterday," Ren said. "When I told everyone about the loan. I watched you fold up like a chair. And I thought, well, now she knows the worst thing, and it's killing her. But sweetheart—it isn't the worst thing. It's the best thing. You just can't hear it yet."

"She spent her dream on me." Oscar's voice was doing something embarrassing. "The vineyard was her whole life, and she—"

"The vineyard was not her whole life." Ren said it firmly, the way you correct a child who has gotten a story wrong. "You were her whole life. The vineyard was the stage she built so you'd have somewhere to stand. Honestly, the number of people in this family who think that woman loved dirt more than she loved us—I could scream. She used to watch you chop vegetables through the kitchen window and forget she had anything on the stove. Burned more pots that way than I can count. Ask your mother."

"She never told me. Any of it. She let me believe—"

"She let you believe what you were going to believe anyway," Ren said. "That girl of mine knew you. Knew you'd carry it regardless of what she said, because you're an Ishikawa and we treat guilt like a family heirloom. She wrote the letter hoping it would reach you in time. It reached you late. That's not her failure. That's ours—all of us adults who let you sit alone with a story that was never true."

Oscar pressed the letter flat against the table, smoothing it with both palms, as if she could press the words into her skin.

"It doesn't feel true," she admitted. "Reading it. I want it to be true. I keep trying to make it fit and it keeps—sliding off."

"That's because you've worn the other story for five years," Ren said. "Five years is a long time to wear a coat. Even when someone tells you it's summer, you'll feel the weight of it for a while. That's not ingratitude. That's just seams."

* * *

They cooked the lamb.

It was Ren's idea, delivered with the brisk tyranny of a woman who had decided something and saw no reason to consult others. "We're not reading about her recipe, we're making it. Go down to Ferro's and get a shoulder. And don't come back talking to me about budgets, Oscar Ishikawa, or I'll tell everyone at church about your haircut in ninth grade."

So Oscar drove to Ferro's, and bought the shoulder, and came back, and for the rest of the afternoon the two of them worked in Ren's small crooked kitchen following a dead woman's handwriting.

It was slow work, deliberately. The recipe itself insisted on it. Brown the meat properly—*properly means wait, she will want to rush, do not let her*—and Oscar, reading that line aloud, heard her grandmother's voice so clearly she had to put the card down and breathe for a moment. Marinate overnight, though they cheated and gave it four hours. Then the long braise, hours of it, the oven low, the kitchen filling up gradually with the smell of garlic and wine and butter until the whole cottage seemed to be exhaling.

They stayed in the room. That was the whole recipe, really.

They peeled garlic and argued about whether Grandmother had ever actually measured anything in her life (Ren: no; Oscar: once, in 2009, under duress). They drank the cooking wine, which was a mistake, and then opened better wine, which fixed it. Ren told stories Oscar had never heard—Grandmother at nineteen, setting a haystack on fire; Grandmother losing a bet to the ferry captain and paying it off in pies for a month; Grandmother, the winter Oscar's father left, standing in the snow outside the house for an hour because she said she needed to "check the vines," and coming in with red eyes and frost in her hair and making pancakes for dinner without one word of explanation.

"She did that for you," Ren said. "The pancake winters. Every bad year of your childhood, pancakes for dinner. I asked her once why, and she said, 'Because it's impossible to cry while flipping a pancake, and I need her to see me try.'"

Oscar stirred the pan and said nothing, because her throat had closed entirely.

By evening the lamb fell apart at the touch of a fork, glossy and dark and fragrant, and they ate it at the little table with bread to mop the sauce, and it was—Oscar had braced herself for it to taste like grief, or like guilt, or like a test she'd fail—but it tasted like Sunday. It tasted like being eight years old and allowed to tear the bread. It tasted like a woman who had loved her enough to burn down her own dream and call it cheap.

"It's good," Oscar said, and then, because it was true and she was tired of not saying true things: "It's better than hers."

"Blasphemy," said Ren, taking another helping. "Accurate blasphemy."

* * *

Later, full and wine-soft, Oscar walked home along the vineyard road in the blue hour, the letter folded in her jacket pocket over her heart like something flammable.

Halfway home she stopped, took it out, and read it again by the light of her phone. *Don't you dare feel guilty.* She tried to obey. She stood there among the dying vines, in the cold coming down off the water, and tried with everything she had to simply put the coat down.

She couldn't. Not yet. The words were true—she believed, distantly, the way you believe in countries you haven't visited—that her grandmother had chosen freely, gladly, without regret. But five years of arithmetic didn't evaporate in an afternoon. Somewhere inside her, the ledger was still open, the columns still adding up, and a letter—however beloved the hand that wrote it—couldn't close the books.

But it could reopen them. That was something.

She thought of Silas. He'd texted once since the gala—a single line, *Are you okay*, sent at midnight, no punctuation, which from Silas was practically a sonnet. She hadn't answered. She hadn't known how. Everything between them was suspended mid-air: the almost-confession, the fight, the ring in her jewelry box that she still hadn't returned, the thirty days ticking down like a bomb with excellent manners.

He'd told her she was wrong about the guilt. He'd held her among the vines and told her, plainly, that she hadn't killed anything. And she hadn't believed him—hadn't been able to. But tonight, standing in the road with her grandmother's handwriting warming her pocket, she understood something she hadn't: it wasn't that Silas was wrong. It was that he'd been asking her to accept absolution from a stranger, when the only person authorized to grant it had finally spoken—from beyond, through a tin box, through her aunt's cowardice and courage, in shaky letters that leaned like tired people.

*You'll be better than me. You already cook better than me.*

Tomorrow, Theo would come with his papers and his patient, suffocating mercy. Tomorrow the clock would tick. But tonight—

Tonight Oscar walked the rest of the way home, went into her kitchen, and took down the heavy pan she only used for special occasions. She didn't know yet what she was going to cook. She only knew that for the first time in five years, she wanted to stay in the room.

Her phone buzzed on the counter. She glanced at it, expecting the bank, or Theo, or another silent hour of the night.

It was Silas. One line this time, and her breath caught:

*Your aunt called me. She says you have something of your grandmother's I should taste. I'll bring the wine.*
