## 21. Empty Kitchen

The ferry horn went off at six-forty in the morning like something wounded.

Oscar stood at the end of the dock with her hands jammed in her coat pockets and watched the *Merrilee Ann* load up in the gray half-light. The island's one working crane swung crates of return freight aboard. Gulls screamed their opinions. The water lay flat as poured glass, which felt like a personal insult—she'd wanted weather, something dramatic to match the wreckage, and instead the sea was offering her a mirror.

Silas came down the gangway at six fifty-five with his duffel over one shoulder and his knife roll in his hand. He'd shaved. That was the detail that undid her—not the silence, not the careful distance he kept between them on the dock, but the fact that he had shaved for a ferry ride home, because that was who Silas was. He did things properly even when his heart was in pieces. Especially then.

"You don't have to see me off," he said.

"I know."

He looked out at the water. She looked at him. Neither of them said any of the forty-seven things stacked up behind their teeth, because they had spent three weeks learning each other's language and only eleven hours proving how fluently they could use it to wound.

"The supper club," he said finally. "The one you're going to do."

"I haven't said I'm doing it."

"You will." He shifted the knife roll to his other hand. "You cook like that when you're scared. It's the only time you let yourself."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed quietly. "Most true things aren't."

The deckhand called the last boarding. Silas walked two steps toward the gangway, stopped, and turned around. For one absurd second she thought—he's going to say it, he's going to take it all back—and her whole body leaned toward him like a plant toward a window.

"Your grandmother's garden," he said. "The rosemary by the south wall is still alive. Everything else gave up years ago, but that bush didn't. In case that means something to you someday."

Then he was gone, up the ramp, swallowed into the ferry's belly, and Oscar stood on the dock with her arms wrapped around herself while the engines turned over and the *Merrilee Ann* pulled away from the pilings with the slow, indifferent grace of a thing that has left a thousand people standing exactly where she was standing now.

She watched until the ferry was small enough to cover with her thumb.

She held it there a long time.

* * *

By eight o'clock the house was full of the particular chaos of an Amberdale exodus.

Her mother moved through the front rooms collecting reading glasses and orphaned scarves with the efficiency of a woman who had been evacuating places for forty years. Theo carried suitcases to the rental car two at a time, jaw set, saying nothing, which from Theo was a shout. Priya sat on the porch steps drinking the last of the good coffee and watching Oscar with those dark, unhurried eyes that missed absolutely nothing.

"You could come stay with us for a week," Priya said. "Mainland. Breathe a little."

"I have thirty days of breathing to do here."

"That's what I figured." Priya smiled, small and real. "For what it's worth—I liked him. The fake part and whatever the other part was. And I think he liked you back, which is the rarest thing I saw all weekend, and I've seen my husband parallel park a boat."

"Priya—"

"I'm just saying." She squeezed Oscar's arm on her way past. "People who fake love that well usually aren't faking anything. They're just early."

Aunt Ren came last, as Aunt Ren always did, moving at her own tide. She stopped in the doorway with her canvas bag over her shoulder and looked at Oscar for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her reach out and tuck a strand of hair behind Oscar's ear the way she used to when Oscar was nine and crying over a dead hen.

"Come Sunday," Ren said. "I'll teach you the torta. Your grandmother's. The one nobody wrote down."

"I don't think I can—"

"It's not a question, tesoro. It's a schedule." She patted Oscar's cheek once, firm as a verdict, and followed the others to the car.

Her mother was the last one standing beside the open car door. She looked back at the house—at the vineyard climbing the hill behind it, brown and ragged at the edges, stubbornly, stupidly alive—and then at her youngest daughter.

"Call me when you know what you're doing," her mother said.

"I never know what I'm doing."

"Mm." Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but related to one, the way a cousin is related. "Neither did I. For about twenty years. Then I figured out that knowing isn't the point." She got in the car. "Thirty days, Oscar. Use them."

The car crunched down the gravel drive, took the coast road, and was gone.

And then it was quiet.

Not the ordinary quiet of the house, which was really just the sound of one person living—the kettle, the floorboards, the wind worrying the shutters. This was a different quiet, a post-performance quiet, the kind that falls over a theater after the last audience member leaves and the stagehands kill the lights and the whole building seems to exhale and wonder what it's for.

Oscar stood in the middle of her own kitchen and listened to it.

* * *

She lasted four hours before she broke her own rule.

The rule was: don't go to the hotel. Don't walk past the hotel. Don't so much as look down Harbor Street in the direction of the hotel, because the hotel was where Silas worked, except Silas didn't work there anymore, or maybe he did, except he wasn't there, and either way the building itself had no business having this much gravity.

But at noon she needed flour. That was the lie she told herself—that she needed flour, which was technically true, since she'd promised Mrs. Okonkwo at the co-op she'd bring bread for the ferry crew tomorrow, and the co-op was out, and the hotel kitchen had flour, and Chef Delacroix owed her a favor from the bar job she'd worked last spring.

The lie was airtight. Lies always were, right up until the moment they weren't.

Harbor Street smelled of low tide and diesel. The hotel slumped at the end of it like an old dog sleeping in the sun, its paint peeling in long curls, its sign missing the L in HOTEL in a way that had become part of the island's identity. Nobody had fixed it in eleven years. There was a certain comfort, Oscar thought, in a place that had given up so thoroughly it no longer pretended otherwise.

Delacroix was in the dining room arguing with a supplier on the phone. He waved her through toward the kitchen without breaking off his sentence—"No, Jean-Marc, I said *twelve* cases, if I wanted vinegar water I'd buy vinegar water"—and Oscar pushed through the swinging door into the kitchen.

It was empty.

Of course it was empty. It was one o'clock on a Monday in the shoulder season; the lunch rush had come and gone; the line cooks were on break. But some part of her had expected—had braced for—him. Had half-hoped to round the pass and find Silas at the stove with his sleeves rolled and that look on his face he got when a sauce was behaving, the look of a man briefly reconciled with the world.

Instead: stainless steel, scrubbed and gleaming. A stack of sheet pans. The big stockpot hanging on its hook where it always hung. And on the steel counter by the pass, precisely centered, a small paper bag folded over twice at the top, with her name written on it in pencil.

*Oscar.*

Just that. No surname. As if everyone in the world would know whose it was.

She stood very still for a long moment. Somewhere behind her, Delacroix was still on the phone, still fighting about cases. The refrigerator hummed. A drip from the prep sink counted seconds into the basin, patient as a metronome.

She picked up the bag.

Inside, wrapped in a square of clean linen, was the ring.

The cheap one. The pawnshop one, twenty-eight dollars, thin gold band with a stone so small it was less a diamond than a rumor of one. He'd bought it with his own money—she remembered the argument in the parking lot, her furious insistence that she would fund every piece of the fiction, and his flat refusal, and the way the argument had gone strange and warm somewhere in the middle, the way everything between them kept doing that, warmth leaking in through the cracks like light under a door.

He'd worn it on a chain sometimes, during the weekend, when he wasn't performing. She'd caught it once, glinting at his collarbone when he leaned over the stove, and had thought nothing of it, or had told herself she thought nothing of it, or had thought a great deal of it and filed the thinking somewhere she wouldn't have to look at it.

Now it sat in her palm, and the kitchen was silent, and the cost of self-protection came due all at once, itemized, in full.

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: he had offered her something real. Not the weekend. Not the performance. Saturday night, after the gala, after the creditor had stood up in front of her entire family and announced in his pleasant, apologetic voice that the loan was called due, Silas had found her on the porch and said, *the arrangement's over, I want something real,* and she had frozen. She had stood there with the man in front of her offering the exact thing she'd spent five years pretending she didn't want, and her body had done what bodies do when the cage door opens after enough years—it had pressed itself against the bars and waited for the trap.

*I need time,* she'd said.

He'd given it to her. That was the worst part. He hadn't argued, hadn't begged, hadn't done any of the dramatic things that would have let her dismiss him. He'd just nodded and said okay, and stepped back, and given her the space she asked for, and the space had turned out to be exactly large enough for her to lose him in.

And then Sunday—the fight. God, the fight. She could still feel it in her chest like a bruise. *You're buying your way into my life because you can't face your own failures.* His face when she said it. The terrible flash of truth in it, because it wasn't entirely untrue, and he'd known it, and instead of denying it he'd thrown hers back at her: *You hide behind the debt so you never have to risk wanting anything.*

Both right. That was the thing nobody warned you about, that the worst fights were the ones where both people were right, where there was no villain to assign, just two frightened people aiming their fear at each other because it was easier than aiming it at themselves.

She had wanted him to stay anyway. She had wanted him to stand in the wreckage of everything she'd said and refuse to leave, to prove that love was sturdier than her, to make her safe by force. And he hadn't. Because he was a person, not a plot device. Because he had his own wounds and his own pride and his own savings account sitting in a bank account earmarked for a restaurant dream he'd already buried once.

Because she had taught him, in eleven hours, that loving her was a losing position.

Oscar closed her hand around the ring.

The metal was cold. Of course it was cold—it had been sitting in a paper bag in an empty kitchen—but it felt like a verdict anyway. *Here,* the coldness said. *This is what caution buys. This is what you get for asking for time you didn't need and spending it on nothing.*

She should put it back in the bag. She should leave it on the counter. She should march into the dining room and hand it to Delacroix and say *give this back to him if he ever comes through again,* tidy and final, a closed ledger.

Instead she stood in the middle of the hotel kitchen where they had cooked together past midnight—where he'd shown her how he held a knife, correcting her grip with two fingers at her wrist, both of them pretending the touch was instructional—where they had chopped side by side in an easy rhythm that neither of them had named—where she had kissed him and called it practice and he had let her, and let her, and let her, until practice was the biggest lie either of them had told all weekend, which was saying something—

Instead she stood there and cried.

Not gracefully. Not the single photogenic tear of a woman in a novel. She cried the way she did everything, thoroughly and alone, bent over the steel counter with the ring clenched in her fist, sobbing into the smell of onions and bleach and yesterday's service, in the one room on the island that had ever felt like it belonged to more than just her grief.

Delacroix, to his eternal credit, did not come in. At some point the phone call ended. At some point his footsteps approached the kitchen door, paused, and retreated. Island men knew when to be elsewhere.

When it was over—when there was nothing left in her, when the sobs had rung themselves out like a bell—she wiped her face on her sleeve, unfolded the linen, and slid the ring onto her right hand. Ring finger, wrong hand. Where it would sit like a question she hadn't answered yet.

Then she looked up at the pass, at the ticket rail, at the order wheel with its one ancient yellowed slip still clipped to it from some service years ago, and she made herself do the thing she was best at.

She made a list.

Flour—she actually did need flour, the lie had accidentally become true, which felt thematically appropriate. Yeast. Butter. Eggs from the Kowalski farm, she'd trade bread for them. The supper club—forty covers, maybe more, Grandmother's recipes, priced to actually clear money for once instead of just clearing her conscience. Equipment she could sell: the old press, the second tractor, Grandmother's truck if it came to that, though the thought of it made her throat close. Every buyer on the mainland list, called again, starting tonight. Theo's offer sitting in her inbox like a loaded gun—*sign, and it all goes away*—which she would not sign, which she would read instead, every clause, like an owner, like a fighter, like a woman who had finally learned that rescue and erasure wore the same cologne.

Thirty days. Twenty-nine now.

She could hear her mother's voice: *Use them.*

She could hear Silas's voice too, worse, quieter: *You cook like that when you're scared. It's the only time you let yourself.*

"Well," she said out loud, to the empty kitchen, to the ring, to the ghost of a man who had left her a rosemary bush and a paper bag and no instructions. "I'm letting myself."

The kitchen didn't answer. Kitchens never did. But the light through the high window had shifted while she cried, sliding gold across the steel counters, and the room looked different now—less like a theater after the show, more like a kitchen before one. Mise en place. Everything in its place. The hour before service, when the whole day is still possible.

Oscar picked up the paper bag, folded it into her pocket, and walked out through the dining room past Delacroix, who glanced up from his invoices, took in her red eyes and her set jaw, and wisely said nothing at all.

Behind her, the swinging door rocked twice and went still.

Twenty-nine days. One ring on the wrong hand. And a supper club to feed to an island that had, without anyone asking it to, already decided it was on her side.

She just had to decide the same thing.
