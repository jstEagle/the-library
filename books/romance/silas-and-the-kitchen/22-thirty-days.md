## 22. Thirty Days

The morning after the gala, Oscar woke at four a.m. with a number in her head.

Not the bank's number—she'd stopped letting herself think of that one in full, the way you don't look directly at the sun. This was a different number: the sum total of everything on the property that could be sold without killing the vineyard itself. The old tractor that hadn't run since before she arrived. The stainless steel tanks in the cellar, three of them, only two ever used. Her grandmother's second truck, rusting under a tarp by the north fence like a sleeping animal nobody wanted to wake.

She got up, put the kettle on, and wrote the list on the back of an electricity bill. By the time the sun came up over the water, she had forty-one items and a plan so simple it was almost insulting: sell everything that wasn't nailed down, work every hour she could find, and call every buyer on the island until one of them said yes.

Thirty days. She'd survived five years on this rock by breaking impossible things into daily-sized pieces. She could do it again.

She just had to do it alone, which was the part she kept not saying out loud.

* * *

The first week, she sold the truck to a fisherman named Denny who paid cash and haggled for twenty minutes with the solemnity of a man negotiating a peace treaty.

"Your grandmother would've taken two hundred more," he said, counting bills into her palm.

"My grandmother liked me," Oscar said. "You'll have to settle for liking the truck."

He grinned, gap-toothed, and drove it off the property trailing blue smoke like a departing ghost. Oscar stood in the driveway until the smoke cleared, then went inside and crossed the item off the list with a hand that shook slightly. It wasn't grief exactly. It was the strange vertigo of watching your life get smaller on purpose and calling it strategy.

The tanks went next, to a cidery on the mainland who sent a barge and two men who complained about the dock the entire time they loaded. Then the tractor, to Theo's mechanic, which stung in a way she couldn't explain even to herself—selling a piece of dead equipment to someone who might mention it to her brother at Sunday dinner. She imagined the conversation. *Oscar's selling everything, did you hear? Even the stuff that doesn't work.* As if the failure were contagious. As if the whole island were keeping score.

She was keeping score. That was the problem. Every night she updated a spreadsheet with columns that grew more desperate as the days passed: *Sold. Pending. Called, no answer. Called, laughed at.*

That last column had one entry. A restaurant supply dealer in Port Anselm who'd listened to her describe the walk-in cooler, hummed thoughtfully, and said, "Honey, I've seen that model. Is it still standing upright?" She'd hung up on him and then, ten minutes later, called back and finished the negotiation through gritted teeth because pride was a luxury item and she was liquidating those too.

* * *

By day nine, she had a system.

Mornings: vineyard work, because the vines didn't care about her finances and neither did the weather, which had turned wet and cold and conspiratorial. Midday: whatever job the island offered. She cleaned rooms at the hotel—the new manager, a harried woman named Priya who'd replaced three managers in two years, took one look at her and said, "You're overqualified and I can't pay much," and Oscar said, "Perfect, we understand each other." Afternoons: phone calls, emails, listings photographed in flattering light. Evenings: cooking, because eating instant noodles over a spreadsheet felt like surrender, and if she was going down she was going down fed.

It was on an evening in the second week, braising short ribs in her own kitchen because the butcher had given her a discount on scraps in exchange for a recipe, that she caught herself talking to the empty room.

"Not a word about the salt," she told the air, where a person used to stand. "I know what you'd say."

The air said nothing, which was somehow worse than Silas arguing with her about salt, which he had done, repeatedly, with the flat certainty of a man who'd been trained by a tyrant. She stirred the pot and hated how big the kitchen felt. Kitchens weren't supposed to feel big. That was the whole point of them.

Her phone buzzed on the counter. Her heart did something stupid and hopeful before she saw the name.

*Theo.*

She let it ring twice, answered on the third, and kept stirring. "I'm cooking."

"I can hear it." A pause. "How are you?"

"Busy."

"Oscar."

"Theo." She turned the heat down. "If you're calling to check on me, I'm fine. If you're calling about the offer, my answer hasn't changed."

"It's been two weeks."

"Yes. I can count. It's one of my skills, along with spreadsheets and stubbornness."

He sighed, and she could picture him exactly: his office on the mainland, sleeves rolled, that patient expression he'd worn since childhood when she refused to come inside before dark. He wasn't a villain. That was the exhausting thing about him. He genuinely believed he was rescuing her, the way you rescue a swimmer who keeps insisting she's fine while going under for the third time.

"The bank rep told Dad the sale process starts in eighteen days if there's no payment," he said. "After that, you get nothing. No negotiation, no grace period. You lose the house too, Oscar. The house."

"I know what collateral means. I wrote the loan applications."

"You were twenty-six and drowning."

"And I'm thirty-one now, and I've learned to swim." She tasted the braise. Needed acid. "Thank you for the concern. Please file it somewhere I can't reach it."

"Mom's worried."

"Mom's always worried. It's her love language. Tell her I'm eating well—I'm literally holding a spoon."

He laughed despite himself, and for a moment they were just siblings again, and the line hummed with all the things they didn't say. Then, quietly: "The offer stands. It's not a trick, Osc. It never was."

"I know," she said, and meant it, and that was the worst part. "Goodnight, Theo."

She hung up and stood over the stove for a long time, watching the steam rise. The offer didn't go away when she hung up. That was its genius. It just sat there, patient as tide, wearing the face of everyone who loved her. Every refusal cost her a night of sleep, and she'd started budgeting for it—one bad night per phone call, amortized across the week like any other expense.

* * *

Day fourteen. The rain came sideways and stayed.

She spent the morning under the cellar eaves cataloguing barrels, and found, behind a rack of empties, a case of her grandmother's last vintage—six bottles, dusty, labeled in handwriting that made Oscar's chest hurt. *Amberdale Reserve, Year of the Good Rain.* There had been no good rain since. She'd checked.

She should sell them. A collector would pay real money; Aunt Ren had said so once, wistfully, over coffee. Six bottles could cover a month of interest. Could buy her eleven more days of fighting.

She carried the case upstairs, set it on the kitchen table, and looked at it for a long time.

Then she carried it back down.

"Not yet," she told the bottles. "You're the reserve. That's the whole point of you."

She was aware this was irrational. She was aware that sentiment was exactly the kind of luxury item she'd sworn to liquidate. But some part of her—the part that had walked past the hotel kitchen at midnight five years ago and kept walking—understood that if she sold the last six bottles of her grandmother's best year, she wouldn't be saving the vineyard anymore. She'd be embalming it. And she refused, absolutely, to preside over a funeral while pretending it was a rescue.

She wrote it on the list anyway, in the pending column, where it sat like a hostage.

* * *

Day seventeen, Priya cornered her in the hotel laundry room.

"You look terrible," Priya said, folding towels with the ruthless efficiency of a woman running a failing hotel on caffeine and spite. "No offense."

"None taken. I've been sleeping four hours a night."

"That explains the face. Here." She thrust a flyer at her. "Ferry crew needs a weekend cook starting Saturday. Short staffed since Marcus left. Pays badly, feeds you well, and Captain Okafor is a nightmare but he tips in fish."

Oscar read the flyer. *Cook wanted. Breakfast service. Must be punctual, unflappable, able to work in a space the size of a closet.*

"This is a boat kitchen," she said.

"This is a boat kitchen," Priya agreed. "Take it or leave it."

She took it. Of course she took it. By Saturday she was cracking eggs in a galley that rocked beneath her feet while Captain Okafor—a leathery woman of about seventy who looked like she'd been carved from driftwood and opinion—watched her with open skepticism.

"You're the vineyard girl," Okafor said.

"I'm the vineyard girl."

"Heard you lost the place."

"Heard wrong. Ask me again in thirteen days."

Okafor snorted, which Oscar would later learn was her version of approval, and ate the entire omelet, and left a tip in fish, as promised. Two mackerel, wrapped in newspaper. Oscar carried them home like trophies and pan-fried them for dinner, standing up, too tired to sit, and thought: *This is what it costs. This is the price, itemized.* Her hands smelled like fish and dish soap. Her shoulders ached from hauling crates. Her spreadsheet showed a number that was still laughably, catastrophically short.

And underneath all of it, quiet as a pilot light: she was good at this. All of it. The cooking, the hustling, the endless unglamorous arithmetic of staying alive. Nobody had ever told her that survival could be a skill you were proud of. They'd only ever told her to stop struggling and accept help.

* * *

Day nineteen, Aunt Ren appeared at the vineyard gate with a casserole dish and no warning, which was how Aunt Ren did everything.

"You look like a scarecrow that lost its farm," she announced, sailing past Oscar into the kitchen. "Sit. Eat. Don't argue, I brought enough for three."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. You just forget." She set the dish down—eggplant, tomatoes, breadcrumbs, the smell of it filling the room like an argument winner—and then, instead of leaving, she pulled out a chair and sat across from Oscar with her hands folded, which meant business.

"How much are you short?" Ren asked.

"Aunt Ren—"

"Don't. I'm old, I'm blunt, and I buried my sister. You don't get to manage me." Her eyes, sharp as a gull's, didn't waver. "How much."

Oscar told her. Saying it out loud, to someone whose opinion mattered, made it real in a way the spreadsheet never had. Ren absorbed the number without flinching, nodded slowly, and said, "All right," in the tone of a general receiving casualty figures.

"You can't help," Oscar said quickly. "None of you can. If the islanders pool money again, I'll—"

"You'll what? Refuse? You've gotten very good at refusing. It's practically a crop." Ren stood, straightened her cardigan, and collected her dish. At the door she paused. "Your grandmother borrowed money for you because she believed you were worth the debt. I notice you've never once considered whether she was right."

"She wasn't. I ran up culinary school bills and came home and hid."

"Mm." Ren looked around the kitchen—at the clean counters, the mended screen door, the garden visible through the window, half-wild but tended at the edges. "Funny kind of hiding. Everything you touch, you keep alive." She kissed Oscar's cheek, flour-soft, and left.

Oscar washed the casserole dish, dried it, and cried into it briefly, efficiently, the way she now did everything. Then she went back to the spreadsheet.

* * *

Day twenty-three. The calls were getting harder.

The easy buyers were gone. What remained were vultures and ghosts: dealers who promised callbacks and never called, brokers who quoted prices so low they were insults with decimal points, one man who asked, sincerely, if she'd throw in the "vintage decor" and then described her grandmother's dining chairs. She ended that call with a courtesy she was proud of and a slammed receiver she was prouder of.

That night she lay awake doing the math again, the moonless math, the kind where the numbers refuse to meet in the middle no matter how you shuffle them. Sold: a respectable pile. Earned: a drop in the ocean. Days remaining: seven. The gap between what she had and what she owed yawned like the channel between the island and the mainland—crossable only by ferry, and the ferries were all spoken for.

Theo's offer sat in her inbox, unread for weeks but never deleted. Patient. Suffocating. *Sign everything over, move to the mainland, start fresh.* Start fresh. As if fresh were a place you could move to. As if the last five years were a stain instead of a life.

She got up at three a.m., made tea, and sat at the kitchen table with the ledger open in front of her—the old one, her grandmother's, the one she'd found in the first week and never stopped using. The handwriting faded halfway through, from confident loops to a shakier script, the last entries dated eight days before the funeral. The final line wasn't a number. It was a note, in the margin, in that shaky hand:

*O. will fix this. O. fixes everything.*

Oscar pressed her palm flat against the page and held it there until the paper warmed.

"No pressure," she whispered to the dead. "None at all."

Then she closed the ledger, opened her laptop, and started drafting the listing for the last of the equipment—the good press, the one machine she'd sworn she'd never sell. Seven days. She'd fight to the last hour, and if the math won in the end, it would win ugly, over her objections, with her fingerprints on everything she'd tried to save.

Her phone lit up on the table. Unknown number, local prefix. She answered on reflex, exhaustion overriding caution.

"Miss Amberdale? This is Marta, over at the school. We heard you cook. Our autumn fundraiser is Saturday and our cook quit and—" a breathless pause "—I know it's short notice, but we can pay two hundred dollars and feed you first."

Oscar looked at the ledger, at the listing draft, at the dark window where the vines stood invisible and patient in the rain.

"What time do you need me?" she said.

* * *

Day twenty-five. She cooked for eighty schoolchildren's parents in a church basement kitchen built for twenty, on burners that heated unevenly and a stove timer that only worked if you hit it. She made pasta sauce from a recipe she invented on the ferry ride over, stretching three crates of donated tomatoes with wine she bought with her last discretionary funds, and when the parish priest asked what she called it, she heard herself say, "Amberdale Reserve," and had to turn away and pretend to check the garlic.

Two hundred dollars. It was nothing. It was everything. She biked home at midnight with the money zipped in her jacket and the taste of the sauce still in her mouth, and she stood in her kitchen—the big, empty, echoing kitchen—and admitted the thing she'd been refusing to admit for twenty-five days:

She loved this. Not the debt. Not the fear. This—the work, the feeding, the alchemy of making much from little. Somewhere between the fish and the fundraiser, the grinding had turned into something else. Still grinding. But hers.

Six days left. The gap remained. The offer remained. Sleep remained theoretical.

But tomorrow there was a buyer coming to look at the press, and Captain Okafor had hinted—hinted, in her language, which involved a grunt and a second mackerel—that the ferry co-op might want a regular weekend cook through winter, and Aunt Ren had mentioned, casually, dangerously, that she'd be stopping by Thursday with "some people" who "liked the supper club idea," and Oscar had pretended not to understand and understood completely.

She updated the spreadsheet. She sharpened her knives. She set the alarm for five.

And when sleep finally took her, it took her mid-plan, mid-sentence, mid-fight—which was, she reflected in the last lucid moment before the dark, exactly where she intended to be when the thirty days ran out: still swinging.
