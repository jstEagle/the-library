## 23. The Letter Again

The letter lived in the drawer of the nightstand, beneath a stack of unpaid invoices that had stopped bothering to be threatening and had settled into being merely present, like weather.

Oscar took it out every night.

This was not a ritual she had chosen. It was more like a habit her hands had developed without consulting her—she'd finish the day's work, wash up, climb into bed with the lamp on, and then her hand would simply go to the drawer the way a tongue goes to a chipped tooth. She would unfold the single sheet of paper. She would read it. And every night, without fail, she would arrive at the same sentence and stop breathing for a moment, as though the words were a step down in the dark that wasn't where her foot expected it.

*I was never prouder of anything I grew in that soil, and you were never one of my crops.*

The first time she'd read it, three weeks ago now, sitting on Aunt Ren's kitchen floor with the envelope shaking in her hands, she had thought: *this is a mistake.* Not the letter itself—the letter was unmistakably Grandmother's, right down to the way she crossed her sevens like she was striking them through, suspicious of them even in death. The mistake was the sentiment. Grandmother had been old, and tired, and writing to a granddaughter she loved; people said generous things when they loved you. That didn't make them true.

That was the theory, anyway. Oscar had become very good at theories. Five years of running a vineyard on fumes will teach you to construct a plausible narrative out of almost nothing.

The trouble was that the letter kept not going away. Every night she read it expecting it to have expired, the way painkillers expire, the way forgiveness was supposed to expire once you'd examined it honestly enough—and every morning it was still there, folded along its softening creases, saying the same impossible thing.

Tonight was no different. Rain ticked against the window of the little house above the winery, the same rain that was, presumably, falling on the vines she couldn't afford to keep. Day twenty-two of thirty. Eight days left before the bank's deadline, before Theo's patient, suffocating offer became the only door left open in the house.

She read the letter again anyway.

*You think the vineyard was my dream. It was never my dream. I planted grapes because your grandfather loved them, and I kept them because they fed us, but the dream—the actual dream, the one I'd have named if anyone had asked me—was you, Oscar. Was watching you cook. Was knowing there would be someone after me who made things with her hands that people needed.*

She put it down. Picked it up again. Read the last line, which was the worst one, the one that had undone her completely on Aunt Ren's kitchen floor:

*Whatever you're blaming yourself for—and I know you, so I know you are—put it down. It was never yours to carry. The loan was mine. The choice was mine. You were seventeen and you told me the truth about what you wanted, which is the bravest thing anyone ever did in my kitchen.*

"You didn't know me," Oscar said aloud to the empty room, because arguing with a dead woman at midnight had apparently become part of her routine as well. "You knew a girl who wanted to go to culinary school. You didn't know what she'd cost you."

The rain answered. The rain always agreed with her, which was somehow worse than if it had argued.

She folded the letter along its softening creases and did not put it back in the drawer. Instead she set it on the nightstand, face-up, which felt dangerously close to hope, and turned off the lamp, and lay in the dark doing the arithmetic she did every night: equipment sold so far, jobs taken, buyers called, the number still short, the gap still wide, Theo's signature page still sitting in its folder on the kitchen table like an unexploded device.

Somewhere in the middle of all that arithmetic, she fell asleep with her hand resting on the nightstand, near the letter, the way you rest a hand near a sleeping animal you haven't decided to trust yet.

* * *

Aunt Ren arrived the next morning with a basket of quinces and the expression of a woman who had decided something.

This was, historically, a dangerous expression. In the forty-odd years Oscar had known her, Aunt Ren's decisions had included repainting the church hall "a cheerful color" (it was orange), adopting a goat named Bishop (the goat had opinions), and once, memorably, informing the entire island at the harvest festival that the ferry company's new schedule was "an insult to God and to tide tables." When Ren decided something, the island generally found out, and the island generally adjusted.

"Those trees are loaded this year," Ren announced, setting the basket on Oscar's kitchen table directly on top of Theo's contract, which she either didn't notice or was pointedly ignoring. "Somebody should do something with them before the wind knocks them all down. Quince paste doesn't make itself."

"I'm a little busy," Oscar said, which was true. She had a call scheduled with a restaurant supply liquidator in forty minutes, and a list of eleven more buyers to work through, and a spreadsheet open on her laptop that glowed with the gentle red of imminent failure.

"You're a lot busy," Ren agreed, already opening cupboards to see what Oscar had. "Busy dying, by the look of you. Sit down and peel. Peeling is good for what's wrong with you."

"What's wrong with me is eight days and a shortfall."

"Mm." Ren found a paring knife in the second drawer, tested its edge against her thumb, and nodded approval. "Peel anyway."

And here was the thing about Aunt Ren: you could refuse her, technically, the way you could technically refuse the tide. Oscar sat down at her own kitchen table, moved the quince basket aside just far enough to reveal the contract, and took a fruit in her hand. It was heavy and hard and fuzzy, stubbornly pale—a fruit that refused to be eaten raw, that required hours of slow cooking to become anything worth having. Her grandmother had grown them along the south wall. Of course she had.

They peeled in silence for a while. Ren peeled fast, in long unbroken spirals, a skill that looked effortless and had taken, Oscar knew, fifty years. Oscar peeled slowly, in patches, leaving half the fuzz.

"You're reading it," Ren said at last. Not a question.

"Every night."

"And?"

"And what?"

Ren gave her a look over the top of the quince in her hand—the look she'd given the ferry company, the look she'd given Bishop the goat when he ate the festival bunting. "And does it work? The reading. Do you feel forgiven?"

"No," Oscar said. It came out faster and more honest than she'd meant it to. She stared down at her patchy, half-peeled fruit. "I read it and I think, she loved me, so she said kind things. People say kind things. It doesn't change what happened. She borrowed against everything she had—for me. For a school I went to for two years before I—" She stopped. Before I came home. Before I stood in this kitchen and told her I was quitting, that cooking had started to feel like drowning, that I wanted to come back and help with the harvest. And six months later she was dead, and the bank statements started arriving, and I did the math on what my wanting had cost her.

"You quit school because you were miserable," Ren said. "Not because you were weak."

"I quit school because I couldn't afford to be there anymore and I was too proud to say so. And she must have known. She must have known and she went to the bank instead of telling me, and then she worked herself—" Oscar's voice did something embarrassing. She pressed her lips together until it behaved. "I've read that letter forty times, Aunt Ren. Forty times. And every time I get to the part where she says it wasn't my fault, I think: she wrote that because she was dying and she didn't want me to feel bad. Love makes people lie. I know it does. I've done it myself recently enough."

Ren set down her knife. She wiped her hands on her apron with great deliberation, the way she did when she was about to say something she considered important, and then she looked at Oscar with an expression that had no festival-goat humor in it at all.

"When Harold died," Ren said—Harold, her husband, dead eleven years now, a quiet man who had fixed boats and spoken maybe two hundred words a week—"I didn't leave the house for four months. And everyone was very kind, and everyone said very sensible things, and none of it helped, because grief isn't sensible, it's just love with nowhere to go. That's all it is. All that love you had for a person, and nowhere to put it." She picked the knife back up. "Guilt is the same thing, only you've turned it inward. It's love with nowhere to go, and nowhere to go is unbearable, so you find somewhere. You found yourself. You made yourself the place to put it, because punishing yourself feels like doing something. Feels like loving her. But it isn't, Oscar. It's just love, stuck."

Oscar's throat was tight. "That's very poetic for a woman who once called the new ferry schedule an insult to God."

"The schedule *was* an insult to God." Ren resumed peeling, briskly, as though she hadn't just reached into Oscar's chest and rearranged something. "But I'm right about this, and you know I'm right, because you've read that letter forty times and it hasn't changed and neither have you, and a person can only stand in front of a mirror calling herself a murderer for so long before she has to notice the mirror isn't showing her anything true."

"She trusted me with everything," Oscar whispered. "And I killed her dream."

"No." Ren said it flatly, immediately, without a millisecond of hesitation, and the flatness of it cracked something. "No, child. Listen to me. Listen, because I'm only going to say this once and then we're going to talk about quinces like normal people." She turned in her chair to face Oscar fully. "Your grandmother and I used to sit on her porch every Sunday evening for thirty years. Thirty years, rain or shine, wine or tea. And do you know what she talked about? Grapes, sometimes. Money, when she had to. But mostly—you. What you were making. What you'd cooked for the hotel staff. How you'd sent her a photograph of a tart you'd built in school, and how she'd shown it to the entire post office." Ren's voice roughened. "The vineyard was never the dream. I told you that at the dedication and you weren't ready to hear it. The vineyard was dirt and debt and bad knees. The dream was you. It was always you. She didn't die of you, Oscar. She died of time, the way we all will, and the last thing she did with her time was write you that letter, and the last thing she put in it was the truth."

Oscar looked down at her hands. They were stained faintly gold from the quinces. Somewhere under the table, the contract sat in its folder, offering her a clean, bloodless escape: sign it, hand over the vines, move to the mainland, stop being the person who had failed. Theo hadn't pressured her in nine days. He just let the folder sit there, patient as tide.

"If she forgave me," Oscar said slowly, testing each word like a plank over water, "then why can't I—why won't it take? Why do I read it every night and feel exactly the same?"

"Because forgiveness isn't a thing you receive," Ren said. "It's a thing you allow. There's a difference, and the difference is you." She stood, gathered her peels into the basket, and patted Oscar's cheek with a floury, quince-scented hand. "You'll get there. You're stubborn. Stubborn people forgive last and hardest, because they've built the whole fort around the wound." At the door she paused. "Keep peeling. And Oscar—use the south-wall quinces next time. Your grandmother's trees. They make better paste than anything on this island, and she'd want them used, not mourned."

The door closed behind her. Oscar sat alone at her table with a bowl of pale fruit and a contract she hadn't touched and a letter waiting upstairs that she had read forty times.

Forty times, and it hadn't changed. Neither had she.

Or—she picked up the paring knife, and began again on the quince, slower, trying for an unbroken spiral—or perhaps that wasn't quite true either. Perhaps something had shifted, some small structural failure in the fort, some crack running quietly through the mirror. Because for the first time since Ren had handed her that envelope, the sentence hadn't felt like a kindness. It had felt like a fact. *It was never yours to carry.*

Her phone buzzed against the table. She glanced down, expecting the liquidator, expecting another rejection, expecting Theo.

It was neither. It was a message from the bank's representative, brief and businesslike, confirming a meeting Friday morning to review "final options regarding the Ishikawa property."

Friday. Two days earlier than the deadline. Her stomach dropped through the floorboards—but underneath the drop, underneath the old familiar terror, something else stirred, small and stubborn, like a green shoot finding light through a crack in concrete. Because she noticed, with a strange clarity, that her first thought was no longer *how do I survive this.*

Her first thought was: *what do I still have that they haven't counted?*

She looked at the bowl of quinces. At the south wall, visible through the rain-streaked window, where her grandmother's trees stood heavy with fruit nobody had harvested in five years. At the letter upstairs that said, in a dead woman's steady hand, that the dream had never been the vines.

Oscar picked up her phone, opened her notes, and began—not a list of things to sell, for once, but a list of things she had. It was a shorter list than the debts. It was also, she suspected, the beginning of a different answer entirely.

Outside, the rain eased. On the south wall, the quinces gleamed.
