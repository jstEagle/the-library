## 32. The Last Ferry of the Season

The alarm went off at four-forty-five, and Oscar was already awake.

She had been awake since three, in fact, lying in her grandmother's old bed with the quilt pulled to her chin, listening to the house tick and settle around her like an old man turning over in his sleep. At some point she had given up on pretending and gone downstairs, where she'd made coffee she didn't drink and reheated it twice and finally poured it out the back door onto the herb bed, which was either a waste of good coffee or a kindness to the rosemary depending on how you looked at it.

Now she stood at the kitchen window watching the sky do its slow gray work over the water, and she told herself—for the hundredth time, and with no more conviction than the first ninety-nine—that she was not going to the dock.

The last ferry of the season left the mainland at five-thirty. It arrived here at seven-ten. After today, there would be no more boats until spring. Whatever she had to say to Silas Ishikawa—no. *Silas.* Just Silas. She still couldn't say his surname without hearing it in her mother's delighted voice, *Ishikawa, what a lovely name*—whatever she had to say would have to be said by phone, or by letter, or never. The letter was already written. It had cost her two nights and most of a bottle of the cooperative's frankly terrible early pressing, and it was sitting in the post office box on the mainland because Aunt Ren had driven it over on Tuesday and mailed it without letting Oscar take it back.

"You'll thank me," Aunt Ren had said, pulling away from the dock with Oscar's whole future in a manila envelope in the passenger seat.

Oscar had not thanked her. She had spent Wednesday and Thursday in a state of low-grade nausea, checking the mail like a woman waiting for a diagnosis, and Friday night she had dreamed about the pawnshop parking lot—the ring, the argument that stopped being an argument—and woken up with her face wet and her heart going like a bird trapped in a lampshade.

So no. She was not going to the dock. Going to the dock would be pathetic. Going to the dock would be standing in the cold at dawn hoping a man she'd lied to, fought with, and driven onto a morning boat three weeks ago might step off the last ferry of the season like something out of one of her own ridiculous rehearsals.

She put on her coat anyway.

* * *

The walk down took eleven minutes if you went by the road and fourteen if you went by the vineyard path, and she went by the vineyard path because she wanted the extra time and also because she couldn't help herself—she touched the vines as she passed, the way she'd done every morning since the cooperative papers were signed, a little benediction per row. *Still here. Still here. Still here.* The grapes were long gone, harvested and pressed and bottled under a label Aunt Ren had designed that featured a drawing of Grandmother that looked less like Grandmother than like a disappointed owl, but the rows themselves were bare and honest and hers, or theirs, which amounted to the same thing now.

The island was different in the off-season. She'd known this, of course—she'd lived through five of them—but knowing and walking through it were different animals. The hotel was shuttered, its windows dark, the season's last guests long departed. The chandlery had a CLOSED FOR WINTER sign that someone had annotated in pencil: *and possibly forever, ask Gary.* Even the gulls seemed to have cut back their hours, wheeling lazily overhead like employees who'd stopped caring about performance reviews.

And yet. There was something in the emptiness that felt less like abandonment this year and more like held breath. The island wasn't dying; it was resting. She was trying to learn the difference. She suspected it would take her the rest of her life, and she was, cautiously, tentatively, almost looking forward to the lessons.

The dock came into view at the bottom of the hill, pale wood slick with dew, and her stomach performed a maneuver that would have gotten a lesser organ cited by maritime authorities.

Nobody was there. Of course nobody was there. The ferry wouldn't arrive for forty minutes, and even when it did, it would disgorge nothing but a supply pallet for the co-op, maybe a stubborn tourist who hadn't read the schedule, and Gary, returning from whatever mainland errands Gary conducted in the off-season. That was all. That was the entire cast list.

She sat down on the end of the dock anyway, boots dangling over the black water, and began, in the privacy of her own skull, to rehearse.

This was an old habit. She'd been rehearsing apologies since she was nine years old, standing outside her mother's study with a speech prepared about a broken vase, and she knew their anatomy intimately: the opening statement, the acknowledgment of harm, the expression of remorse, the proposed amends. What she had never mastered was the delivery. Rehearsed apologies were like rehearsed romances—they sounded perfect in the empty dining room and fell apart the moment another person walked in.

*Silas. I'm sorry.*

Too short. He deserved more than too short.

*Silas, I'm sorry I accused you of trying to buy your way into my life when actually you were trying to give me yours, and I threw it back at you because accepting it would have meant admitting I wanted it, and wanting things is the thing I'm worst at in the world, worse even than bookkeeping, which is saying something—*

Better. Worse. Both.

She pulled her knees up and watched the horizon, where the sky was doing something expensive-looking, bands of rose and pewter layered over the water like a cake nobody had ordered. The wind came off the sea with a raw edge to it, the first real cold of the year, and she was glad of it. Cold was clarifying. Cold meant you couldn't hide behind comfort; you had to mean what you were doing out here, sitting on a freezing dock at dawn like a woman in a painting called *Regret, with Gulls.*

Because here was the truth she'd finally written down, in ink, in a letter she could no longer retrieve: she loved him. Not the way she'd loved the idea of him during those rehearsal dinners in the empty hotel dining room—safe, fictional, costumed in somebody else's story—but the actual man, the one who bought a cheap ring with his own money so the lie would have one true thing in it, the one who defended her to Theo with surgical precision and then held her among the dead vines while she came apart, the one whose whole dream she'd nearly talked him out of burying and then, when he tried to bury it for her, had screamed at him for the crime of caring wrong.

Both things had been true. That was the part she kept stumbling over. She hadn't been ungrateful and she hadn't been proud—or rather, she had been both, simultaneously, the way people are, the way she was learning to let herself be.

*I won't ask you to give up your dream for me,* the letter said. And she'd meant it. She still meant it. If he stayed on the mainland, if Marcus's partnership came through, if he got his restaurant with his name over the door—then she would survive it. She'd survive anything. That was, she was coming to understand, both her greatest strength and the entire problem.

The engine sound reached her before the boat did, a low diesel mutter somewhere out past the point, and her heart did the bird-in-the-lampshade thing again, harder this time.

*Okay,* she told herself. *He's probably not on it. Statistically, he's not on it. Gary is on it. A pallet of flour is on it. Compose yourself.*

She did not compose herself. She stood up, sat down, stood up again, wiped her palms on her coat, and considered, seriously, for the first time, hiding behind the fuel shed until the ferry had come and gone, thereby preserving her dignity at the cost of everything else. This had been her signature move for thirty-one years and it had served her terribly.

Instead she made herself walk to the very end of the dock, where the mooring posts stood with their tires hanging off them like old jewelry, and she waited.

The ferry rounded the point.

It was a smaller boat than the summer vessel—a workhorse, white paint gone chalky, the kind of boat that smelled permanently of diesel and fish and patience. It came across the gray water with the unhurried confidence of something that had made this crossing ten thousand times and intended to make it ten thousand more, and Oscar stood at the end of the dock with her hands jammed in her pockets and rehearsed one more time, fast, the abridged version:

*I'm sorry. I was afraid. I love you. Stay if you want. Leave if you need. But know that the door is open, and the kitchen is warm, and I finally learned the difference between surviving and living, and it's you, it was always going to be you, you insufferable man who burns stock and keeps cheap rings—*

The ferry swung alongside. Lines went across. The deckhand—a teenager she vaguely recognized as the Hendersons' boy, home from school—caught her eye and gave her a look of profound teenage pity, as though she were obviously waiting for someone and obviously about to be disappointed, which was, she reflected, the most accurate read anyone had given her in years.

The ramp came down.

Gary stepped off first, carrying a cooler, nodding at her the way Gary nodded at everything, which was to say minimally, as if acknowledging her existence were a favor he was doing the universe. Behind him came a pallet jack with the co-op's supplies. Behind that—

A figure.

Tall. Coat collar up against the wind. One suitcase in one hand, and in the other, balanced against his hip, a rectangular box that she would have known anywhere, that she had packed tape around herself once upon a lifetime ago, that contained, she knew without looking, twelve knives wrapped in tea towels and a whetstone and a small dented copper pot he refused to replace.

Her whole body forgot what it was for.

He saw her. Twenty feet of ramp between them, and she watched it happen—watched him stop, mid-stride, the way you stop when the thing you've been telling yourself won't be there turns out to be there. The deckhand said something to him. He didn't hear it. The wind pushed at them both, impatient, and neither of them moved, and the gulls wheeled overhead making their comments, and Oscar stood at the end of the dock holding hope so tightly it hurt, the way you hold anything that has already broken once and cannot be allowed to break again.

He started walking.

Not fast. Deliberate. The suitcase bumped down the ramp boards, and the knife box rode steady against his hip, and his eyes never left her face, and Oscar discovered that every word of every rehearsal had evacuated her body completely, leaving behind only her heartbeat and a terrible, wonderful, terrified clarity.

He stopped one pace away. Close enough that she could see he hadn't been sleeping much either. Close enough to smell the diesel and the cold and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, like a ghost of a ghost, garlic.

"Your letter," he said. His voice was rougher than she remembered, or maybe the wind was just in it. "It arrived Thursday."

"I know. I—" Her voice cracked. She cleared her throat. "Aunt Ren mailed it. I tried to get it back."

"I know that too. She told me. At length." Something moved at the corner of his mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where smiles started on him, the tide coming in. "She also told me about the supper club. Forty covers. Grandmother's recipes."

"The duck was a disaster," Oscar said, because apparently her mouth had decided to lead with honesty. "The rest was good. The duck caught fire. Slightly. Aunt Ren says Grandmother would have found it funny."

"She would have." He set the suitcase down. Then the knife box, carefully, the way he handled anything with an edge. And then he just looked at her, and the look went on long enough that she felt it everywhere, in her hands, in her throat, in the place behind her ribs where five years of locked doors were rattling on their hinges.

"I turned down the partnership," he said.

The words landed in her chest and detonated quietly, like a depth charge in deep water. "Silas—you didn't have to—"

"I know I didn't have to." He said it simply, without drama, the way he said everything that mattered. "That's the point. I didn't have to. I *wanted* to. There's a difference, and it took me three weeks and one letter to understand it." He paused, and the wind filled the pause, and somewhere behind her the deckhand was pointedly securing lines and pretending deafness. "Marcus offered me half his restaurant. My name on the lease. Everything I spent six years working toward. And I stood in his office feeling absolutely nothing except that it was the wrong kitchen."

Oscar's eyes were burning. "You should have taken it."

"Probably," he agreed. "I've been told I make stupid decisions about kitchens."

"That was one time—"

"Twice. You're forgetting the sauce."

Despite everything—despite the cold and the fear and three weeks of rehearsing into the void—laughter broke out of her, wet and helpless, and she pressed the back of her hand to her mouth, and he watched her do it with an expression she had only ever seen him wear over a rescued pot: fierce, and quiet, and certain.

"Why?" she managed. "Why are you here? Really. Because if this is—if you're doing this out of guilt, or obligation, or because my letter made you feel sorry for me, I will push you back onto that boat myself, I swear—"

"Oscar."

"—because I meant what I wrote, I won't be anyone's charity case, not even yours, *especially* not yours—"

"*Oscar.*"

He said her name the way he'd said it in the vines, that night, with his arms around her while she shook apart. And she stopped.

"I'm not here out of guilt," he said. "I'm here because I don't want a restaurant. I never wanted a restaurant. I wanted a kitchen where the food mattered and the person across the pass mattered more, and I spent years thinking that was a compromise instead of the whole point." He glanced past her, up the hill, toward the vineyard, toward the shuttered hotel with its dark windows and its empty dining room where two liars had once rehearsed falling in love and accidentally done it. "So. I have a question."

Her heart stopped, restarted, stopped again. "Is it about dinner?"

"It's about dinner," he said, and picked up his suitcase.
