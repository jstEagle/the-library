## 14. Confrontation at the Hall

The concert hall smelled of dust and old varnish and, faintly, of every winter it had ever survived.

Orsen stood in the wings with his cello case still latched, though he had no intention of opening it. He'd come to say goodbye to the room, that was all. The demolition notice had gone up on the doors that morning—Voss & Sons Development, Public Comment Period Closes November 15—and something in him had refused to let the building die unvisited. He knew how that felt. Being written off while still standing.

The hall had been dark for six years, but the stage lights still worked when he found the breaker. He flipped them on one by one, and the room emerged from the dark like a face he'd once loved: the scarred maple floor, the rows of seats with their brass numbers worn to ghosts, the acoustic shells behind the stage that some long-dead architect had shaped like cupped hands. He had played here at nineteen. His first paid solo. He'd been so nervous he'd tuned his A string down a full step and played the entire first movement a comma flat, and the audience had loved him anyway, because audiences forgive the young everything.

He set the case down on the stage and didn't open it.

"You shouldn't be in here."

Her voice came from the back of the hall, and it went through him like a bow drawn across the wrong string.

Margrethe Voss stood in the center aisle, coat still on, hair damp from the rain outside. She was holding something—a folder, a sheaf of papers—and she was looking at him the way people look at a fire they can't decide whether to warm their hands at or run from.

"Neither should you," Orsen said.

"I work here." She started down the aisle, her boots loud in the empty room. "Library outreach. Astrid has me cataloguing the hall's archives before the demolition crew gets to them. Programs. Photographs. Forty years of this town embarrassing itself on stage." She stopped at the front row and dropped the folder onto a seat. "You, on the other hand, are trespassing on property my father legally owns."

"So are the mice."

"The mice didn't break in through the loading dock."

"I didn't break anything. The door was open."

"It was not."

"It was," Orsen said, "ajar."

Margrethe laughed, one sharp note with no humor in it, and it was such a familiar sound—the sound she made at him across the fish market, at the town meetings, at the fundraiser where she'd covered for his shaking hands in front of two hundred people—that something in his chest cracked along an old fault line.

He had known for eleven days. Eleven days since he'd seen the returned postcard in her handwriting at the library desk, those slanted capitals, the way she crossed her sevens like a woman striking matches. He had tested her that same afternoon—mentioned, casually, over a crate of fundraiser programs, that his pen pal had once written that the island in winter smelled like "a cold engine and a warm kitchen." Margrethe had gone still for exactly two seconds. Then she'd said, "Whoever wrote that has been spending too much time at the ferry dock," and changed the subject, and Orsen had known with the certainty of a tuning fork struck against bone.

He hadn't said anything. He'd kept writing. He'd kept receiving. He'd let the lie run another eleven days because the letters were the only place he was whole, and he'd been afraid—God, he'd been afraid—that saying it aloud would kill the one true thing he had.

And now she was here. In this hall. Holding a folder of dead programs, looking at him like that.

"Why did you come tonight?" she asked. "Really."

"To say goodbye to the room."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have."

She climbed the three steps to the stage. Up close, he could see she'd been crying—not recently, but recently enough. The skin under her eyes had the particular rawness of salt water. He wanted to ask. He wanted, more than he'd wanted anything in years, to reach out and touch the place where her jaw went tight.

Instead he said, "You've been crying."

"Don't."

"Margrethe—"

"Don't." She said it quietly, and it landed harder than a shout. She turned away from him, walked to the center of the stage, and stood where a soloist would stand, facing the empty seats. "Do you know what I was doing before I came here?"

"Cataloguing."

"Reading." She said the word like a confession. "Astrid keeps copies. Of everything, in the pen-pal program—did you know that? Routing copies, in case of complaints. I went back through the file tonight. Every postcard. Both directions." Her hands were shaking; he could see it even from behind. "Forty-one cards from you. Forty-one from—"

She stopped.

Orsen felt the hall tilt.

"From me," she finished.

The silence that followed was the longest silence of his life. Longer than the hall after the string broke. Longer than the silence in his hands.

"You knew," he said.

"I found out three days ago." She turned to face him. "The routing records. Your cards came through the library drop box with the return address of the cottage on Gull Road. There was no anonymity left in it, Orsen. There never was, not really—your handwriting is on every program in this building's archive, I've been staring at it for a week. I just—" Her voice failed and restarted. "I didn't want it to be true."

"Didn't want it to be true." He heard his own voice go flat and cold, the voice of a man protecting something. "You knew for three days."

"And you've known for eleven."

The accusation landed exactly where she'd aimed it. Orsen took a step back, and his heel caught the cello case, and the case rocked and settled and neither of them looked at it.

"How did you—" she started.

"The library. A returned card. Your handwriting." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, jagged. "I tested you. I told you a detail only my pen pal could know, and you lied to my face, and I let you lie, because I thought—if I say it out loud, it ends. Whatever this is. It ends."

"Whatever this is." She crossed her arms. "What is this, Orsen? What do you call it? Because I've been calling it a lot of things for three days, and none of them are printable."

"You call it a lie, I assume. You're the expert."

"Oh, that's—" She took a breath, visibly gathering herself, visibly deciding to be crueler. "That's rich. Coming from the man who told a stranger things he couldn't tell anyone with a face. You gave your truth to a stranger because you couldn't give it to me."

"Because you—" His voice cracked and he hated it, hated her, hated the room with its cupped acoustic hands. "Because you hate me. You have hated me since the night my hands failed, publicly, repeatedly, with witnesses. You called my performance at the harvest supper 'a eulogy with an intermission.' What was I supposed to do, hand you the contents of my chest? You'd have made a joke of it. You make a joke of everything."

"I make a joke of everything," she repeated softly, "because my father is tearing down this island one pier at a time and I am the only one standing in the room with him at Christmas. Because if I don't laugh, Orsen, I will say things that can't be taken back, and I have a name I have to carry, and the name is Voss." She was close to him now, close enough that he could see the rain in her hair. "You think I hated you? I watched you at the harvest supper, refusing to play, and I mocked you, and do you know why? Because I had already read your postcards by then. Because I already knew the man who wrote *I stopped playing for six years and the silence got so loud I could hear my own heartbeat apologize*, and then I stood in front of the actual man, and he wouldn't even look at the cello, and it made me insane. It made me insane that you gave everything to a stranger and gave me nothing but your back."

"You were the stranger!"

"I know!" she shouted, and the hall took her voice and gave it back multiplied, filling the cupped hands, filling the seats, filling the six dark years. "I know that. Don't you think I know that? Every night I sat at my kitchen table writing to you, I thought: he tells this person everything. He tells this person about his hands, about the silence, about the night the string broke. And then I'd see you on the ferry and you'd look at me like I was the weather. Like I was something to be endured. And I thought, what is wrong with me? What is so unforgivable about me that the man will bare his soul to a blank card but not to my face?"

"Nothing," Orsen said. "Nothing is unforgivable about you. That's the problem."

The words came out before he could stop them, and Margrethe went absolutely still.

"You want to know why I couldn't tell you?" he said, quieter now, and his hands—the hands, the ruined famous hands—came up between them, palms open. "Because it wasn't a stranger I was writing to. After the third card, it wasn't a stranger. It was a voice. It was the only voice that made the silence in my head quiet down. And if I found out the voice was you—if I let it be you—then it became possible to lose it. Do you understand? A stranger can't reject you. A stranger is safe. But you—" His breath went ragged. "You were never safe. From the first night, on this stage, when you were sixteen in the third row and you clapped until your hands were red. I remember. I have always remembered. You are the most dangerous person I have ever met, Margrethe Voss, and I have spent six years not feeling anything, and you made me feel everything through a hole the size of a postcard, and I could not survive it twice."

The hall held the words the way it held music.

Margrethe's eyes were bright and furious. "You knew," she said again, but the fury had changed key. "Eleven days. You let me write to you. You let me write—God, Orsen, the things I wrote. The letter after the storm. The one where I said—"

"I know what you said."

"You let me say it, knowing."

"And you let me say it, knowing," he returned. "We are exactly the same kind of coward. That's what I've been trying to tell you. That's the only honest thing between us that isn't on a postcard."

She made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "We are not the same kind of coward. I stopped answering because I was afraid. You kept writing because—" She stopped. "Why did you keep writing? After I stopped. Three weeks of nothing, and you kept sending cards."

"Because the last thing you wrote me was a question," Orsen said, "and I hadn't answered it yet."

"What question?"

"You asked what I would do if my hands came back." He looked at the cello case, latched, between them on the stage. "I never answered. I'm answering now."

"Orsen—"

"The hall," he said. "I would play the hall. This hall. The night they're supposed to take it down, I would stand on this stage with these hands, and I would play the piece I broke on, and I would do it badly or I would do it well but I would do it, and I would want—" His voice finally, finally broke all the way open. "I would want you in the third row. Where you were when I was nineteen. That's what I'd do."

Margrethe didn't move. The rain had started again against the high dark windows, a sound like fingers drumming on a case.

"You absolute," she said, and couldn't finish it. "You absolute man. You could have said this in a letter. You could have said this in September."

"You'd have run."

"I'm running right now," she said. "Can't you tell? Look at me. I'm in the one room on this island I'm not supposed to care about, with the one man I'm not supposed to care about, and my father closes the comment period in three weeks, and I have a folder of dead programs in my hands, and I have never in my life been less capable of running." She dropped the folder on the stage. Programs fanned out across the boards—decades of them, his name on a dozen covers, her father's company logo on the newest demolition notice tucked among them like a knife in a bread basket. "I stopped writing to you because I found out and I thought, if he knew and said nothing, then none of it was real. And then I read them all again tonight. Forty-one cards. And I thought: no. It was the realest thing I have ever done. The only real thing. And I came here to tell you that I'm furious at you, and I'm furious at myself, and I am not—"

She stopped. Looked at the case.

"Open it," she said.

"Margrethe."

"Open the case, Orsen."

"It's been six years. It's been a day of rehearsals in a dark cottage at midnight. My hands aren't—"

"Open it," she said, "or I will, and I have no idea what I'm doing with a bow."

Something in him—something older than the shame, older than the collapse, something that had been waiting in the dark of the hall the way the hall had been waiting in the dark of the town—moved toward her voice.

He knelt. The latches gave their small brass clicks, the loudest sounds in the world. He lifted the lid.

The cello lay in its velvet like a sleeping thing. Margrethe crouched beside him, close enough that her shoulder touched his, and neither of them pulled away.

"Play something," she said. Not a command. A request. The first one she had ever made him.

"It won't be good."

"I don't care if it's good." She was looking at him, not the cello, and her eyes were wet and defiant and known. "I've read your soul in slanted handwriting, Orsen Iversen. I think I can survive an open string."

He took the cello out. He took the bow. His hands, in the stage light, in front of her, in the room where he'd first been someone—his hands were shaking.

He set the bow on the string anyway.

* * *

The sound that came out was not beautiful. It was thin and wavering and it cracked in the second bar, and Orsen's breath caught, and Margrethe's hand found his knee and stayed there, and he kept going.

He played the open A. Then the D. Then, slowly, badly, a scale, and then the beginning of a piece he had not touched in six years—just four bars, just enough—and the hall took the sound into its cupped hands and held it, and held it, and gave it back to them multiplied, the way it had held her anger, the way it had held everything this island had ever been brave enough to say out loud.

When he stopped, neither of them moved.

"That was terrible," Margrethe said, her voice wrecked.

"It was."

"I've had better from the ferry horn."

"I know."

She turned to face him fully, kneeling on the scarred maple stage, rain on her hair and fury in her eyes and her hand still on his knee. "The comment period closes November fifteenth. There's a hearing. My father will be there with lawyers and renderings and a checkbook. And I am going to stand up in front of this entire town and say what I actually think, and I need to know—" her grip tightened, "—that when I do, I'm not doing it alone. Because I have spent my whole life being the only Voss in the room, Orsen, and I can't—"

"You're not alone," he said. "You haven't been alone since the first postcard. You just didn't know the address."

Margrethe laughed—really laughed this time, wet and cracked and real—and then she leaned forward and pressed her forehead against his, and the hall held them the way it held music: completely, and without judgment, in the dark.

Outside, the rain thickened. And somewhere across the water, in a lit office on the mainland, a phone was ringing on Henrik Voss's desk—a call he would answer in the morning, a call about his daughter, a call that would make everything they'd just built on this stage cost something.

Neither of them heard it.

They had better music to listen to.
