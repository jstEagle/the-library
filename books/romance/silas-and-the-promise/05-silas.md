## 5. Silas

The ferry was late, and Silas Reyes was grateful for it.

He stood at the rail of the mainland terminal with a coffee he didn't want and a folder he'd read four times, watching the water do what water does — nothing, beautifully, forever. The Merrow Point ferry came across the sound once an hour in season, twice a day now that October had thinned the schedule down to its bones. He'd timed his arrival for the last crossing, which meant he had twenty minutes to stand here being nobody in particular, and he intended to use every one of them.

Because the moment he stepped onto that boat, he would become someone again. Consultant. Reyes. The name his father had spent thirty years turning into a synonym for *whatever you can get away with*, and which Silas had spent eleven years trying to scrub back into something a person could say out loud without flinching.

*Oceana Crest Development, Environmental Impact Assessment — R. Silas Reyes, Lead Consultant.*

His own name on the cover page still gave him a small, private thrill, the way it had since the first contract he'd earned outright, no favors, no family shadow. Eight years of clean reports. Eight years of baseline studies done properly, of mitigation plans he'd fought for tooth and nail, of developers who learned quickly that the younger Reyes was not the older Reyes, and that if they wanted rubber stamps they should hire someone else. Some of them did hire someone else. That was fine. That was the point.

He took the Merrow Point job because of the reef.

That was the part he hadn't told anyone at the firm — not Marcus, not the partners, not the project manager who kept sending him emails with the word *exciting* in them, as though a marina expansion were a birthday party. The preliminary surveys showed a healthy patch of coastal reef off the eastern point, turtle nesting habitat, the kind of thing that in a proper report would mean the expansion got redesigned or relocated or, in the best case, scaled back until it fit the coastline instead of sitting on top of it. And Silas wanted — needed, in the way some men need to pay a debt — to be the one who wrote that report honestly. To prove, one more time, to an audience of no one, that a Reyes could look at something beautiful and not immediately calculate what it was worth flattened.

His father used to say there were two kinds of people in any room: those who owned the room and those who cleaned up after it. Silas had been ten when he first understood that his father counted himself among the owners, and thirty-two when he finally understood that the old man had never once cleaned up anything in his life — other people had always done that for him, usually at great cost, usually in silence.

Silas had done his share of cleaning. He thought of it as his inheritance: not the money, which was gone, not the name, which was stained, but the mop and bucket. The willingness to stand in the mess and deal with it.

The ferry horn sounded across the gray water. He drained the coffee, grimaced — airport coffee, terminal coffee, coffee as punishment — and picked up his bag.

Twenty minutes on the water. A town he'd never seen. A fresh start of the small, unglamorous kind: walk the site, test the water, write the truth, go home. Four weeks, maybe six. Clean hands, documented.

He was so busy rehearsing his own integrity that he almost missed her.

She came out of the terminal building ahead of him as the passengers disembarked, moving with the particular purpose of someone who worked here — a canvas jacket over a fleece, hair pulled back, a clipboard under one arm like an extension of her body. She was talking to the ferry deckhand, laughing at something, and the laugh went through Silas like a current through seawater.

He knew that laugh. He had spent one entire spring semester of college engineering reasons to hear it.

Paloma Okafor stood on the dock at Merrow Point, older by eleven years, sunnier by none, and Silas's plan — the tidy, four-week, clean-hands plan — quietly came apart in his chest like wet paper.

* * *

He did the math while pretending to study the harbor. It wasn't difficult math. Merrow Point was small; she lived here; the clipboard and the deckhand's easy familiarity said *resident*, and the way she crouched at the dock's edge to peer into the water said *marine biologist*, because Paloma had crouched at the edge of every body of water she'd ever met, including, memorably, the fountain outside the science library, which had gotten them both lectured by campus security and had been, Silas maintained to this day, entirely worth it.

Five years, she'd told people back then. Five years rebuilding after something. He'd heard it secondhand, thirdhand, through the grapevine of mutual friends who spoke about her in the hushed tones reserved for survivors of disasters. He'd never asked what the disaster was. He'd known. He'd always known. He just hadn't let himself finish the sentence.

And now here she was, five hours north of the town where they'd both been young, living in the exact kind of place his project was about to put a two-hundred-slip marina on top of.

"Sir? You can head down the gangway."

"Right." He moved. Walking toward the ramp felt like walking toward a door he'd nailed shut from the inside.

There was a version of this where he turned around. He saw it clearly: call Marcus from the terminal, cite a conflict of interest, reassign the contract. It would cost him the project, possibly the client's goodwill, certainly the chance to write the honest report himself. And it would cost him something else too, something he examined for exactly three seconds before putting it away: the chance to see her again.

He didn't turn around. He told himself it was because the reef deserved a consultant who wouldn't flinch, and that was even true. But underneath the true thing was another true thing, smaller and warmer and far more dangerous: after eleven years, some part of him wanted to stand in front of Paloma Okafor and be seen. Even if what she saw made her hate him. Especially then. Hate, at least, was contact.

He came down the gangway with his bag over his shoulder and his face arranged into professional neutrality, and she looked up from the water, and he watched recognition move across her features like weather.

Three full seconds. He counted them. Her eyes went from polite to puzzled to something that wasn't polite at all, and then she straightened up, and the clipboard came up too, like a small shield with tide charts on it.

"Silas."

"Paloma." His voice came out steadier than he had any right to expect. "Hi."

"Hi." A pause with eleven years in it. "You're — the consultant."

"I'm the consultant."

"The marina consultant."

"That's the one." He gestured vaguely at the harbor, the town, the whole beautiful, doomed geography of the situation. "Small world."

"It's not a small world," she said. "It's a very specific world, apparently, and someone has a sense of humor."

Despite everything — despite the dread climbing his sternum — he almost smiled. Eleven years, and she still talked like that. Like the universe was a slightly incompetent colleague.

"How long have you lived here?" he asked, and immediately wished he'd chosen any other sentence in the English language, because it implied he hadn't looked for her, which was true, and she heard it, which was also true.

"Five years," she said. Level. Watchful. "You?"

"Here? Twenty minutes."

"You know what I mean."

"New York," he said. "Before that Boston. Before that—" He stopped himself from saying *before that my father's house*, because they were standing on a public dock and he refused to give Eduardo Reyes free real estate in this conversation. "Around."

She nodded slowly. Around her, the dock went on being a dock: gulls arguing, fuel hoses coiled, the deckhand pointedly finding something to do with a rope. Paloma looked at Silas the way he imagined she looked at data that didn't match — carefully, completely, cataloguing every anomaly.

"So," she said. "You're going to be assessing the reef."

"And the shoreline, and the sediment, and the—"

"Yes," she said. "I know what an environmental assessment is, Silas. I do this work." A beat. "For the conservation trust. I've been surveying that reef for five years."

Of course she had. Of course the woman whose field notes he'd once transcribed for fun — whose abbreviations he could still read like a second language — had spent half a decade building the case against his project. He felt something in his chest that was either despair or awe, and suspected it was both, wearing each other's clothes.

"Then you know it better than anyone," he said.

"I do."

"Good." He shifted the bag on his shoulder. "Then I'll need to talk to you."

Her eyebrows went up. Whatever she'd expected him to say, it wasn't that.

"Not today," he added quickly. "I just got off a boat and I smell like terminal coffee. But I'm here for six weeks, and I'd rather do this right than fast. If you have data, I want to see it. All of it."

"Why?" The word came out flat, but the question under it wasn't flat at all, and they both knew it. *Why should I believe you? Why are you here? Why now, after everything?*

"Because a report is only as good as its worst shortcut," he said, "and I don't take shortcuts."

Something flickered across her face — not softening, exactly. Recalibration. The look of a scientist updating a model.

"Okay," she said finally. "Come by the trust office Monday. Bring your credentials." And then, with a precision that landed like a scalpel: "You'll want to have them handy. People here will ask."

"I imagine they will."

"They will." She hoisted her clipboard. "Welcome to Merrow Point, Silas. It's a good town. Try to leave it better than you found it."

She walked away up the dock, and he watched her go, and the terrible thing — the thing he'd examine later, alone, in whatever rental room the town had left him — was that even now, even with the clipboard shield and the scalpel voice, some animal part of him had simply been happy. Standing three feet from her. Hearing her say his name like a fact instead of a wound.

* * *

The rental was above the hardware store, one room with a hot plate and a view of the harbor, and the landlady, Mrs. Tench, was the kind of woman who asked questions the way customs officers ask them.

"Reyes, is it? Any relation to the Reyes Group? They did the Harborview project down south, '09, '10. Big scandal."

"Different Reyeses," Silas said, which was technically true in the way that mattered legally and false in every way that mattered otherwise.

"Mm," said Mrs. Tench, who had clearly sentenced him already and was merely deciding between community service and the gallows. "Six weeks, you said?"

"Maybe less."

"We'll see," she said, and handed him a key on a buoy-shaped fob, and left him alone with his conscience and the harbor view.

He unpacked methodically: laptop, field kit, the folder. Then he sat on the bed that folded out of the wall and did the thing he'd been not-doing since the dock. He opened the mental file marked *PALOMA OKAFOR* and let it hurt.

Here is what Silas had told himself for eleven years, in the voice of a reasonable man presenting evidence:

*She's better off.* That night in junior year — the night his father's scheme had come apart in Silas's hands, the night Silas had said the unforgivable things and let her believe them, because the alternative was telling her the truth and putting her directly in Eduardo Reyes's path — she'd been safer hating him. His father had made that explicit, in the way of men who never raise their voices because they don't have to: the scholarship, the internship, the letters of recommendation, all of it hanging by threads that Eduardo held. *The girl is a distraction,* his father had said. *A liability. Handle it.* And Silas, twenty years old and terrified, had handled it the only way he could see: he'd burned the bridge with both of them standing on it, and then he'd stood on his side and watched the smoke.

*She rebuilt,* the reasonable voice continued. *She's thriving. She has a life, a career, a town that knows her. What right does he have to walk back into it and reopen the file?*

All true. All beside the point. Because the reasonable voice had one load-bearing lie in it, the one he'd never let himself say out loud: that he'd stayed away all these years for her sake, rather than his own. That the silence had been protection and not cowardice. That a man who loved someone would spend eleven years able to find her — he'd found her tonight in forty seconds on a public dock — and choose not to.

He lay back on the fold-out bed and stared at the ceiling, where a water stain had spread itself into a shape like a coastline.

Six weeks. Walk the reef, run the tests, write the report. Keep it clean, keep it honest, keep it professional. Do not think about the fountain outside the science library. Do not think about how she said *try to leave it better than you found it* and meant *unlike last time*. Do not think about the fact that somewhere in this town there is a letter—

No. He didn't know about the letter. There was no letter in his knowledge of the world. There was only the letter he'd written and never sent, the one that had lived in a desk drawer through three apartments and one divorce-from-the-family and now existed only in his memory, word-perfect, damning, addressed to a girl who'd hated him fair and square.

*Some things you fix by going back,* his mother used to say, back when she still spoke in hopes instead of apologies. *Most things you fix by doing better going forward.*

Fine. Going forward, then. Six weeks of clean work. He'd show her — not tell her, show her — what kind of Reyes he was. And if the report came out the way the data said it should, if the marina shrank or moved or died on the table, then maybe, at the end of it, he'd find her and say the whole sentence out loud. The one about his father. The one about that night. The one he'd been carrying so long he'd grown around it, the way trees grow around fence wire.

He fell asleep with the harbor light coming through the window and the plan intact, more or less, the way a sandcastle is intact more or less right up until the tide comes in.

* * *

The tide, in this case, arrived Monday morning in the form of a phone call from Marcus.

"Good news," Marcus said, which at their firm was historically a warning sign. "The client loves your timeline. They want the draft assessment accelerated — end of the month instead of six weeks."

Silas stood in the trust office parking lot, credentials folder in hand, and felt the sandcastle shift. "End of the month isn't enough time for a proper benthic survey. I told them that."

"You did, and they appreciated it, and then they scheduled a dredge permit consultation for November anyway." Marcus's voice did the thing it did when he delivered bad news wrapped in corporate tissue paper. "Look, buddy, it's a big client. Play nice, hit the milestones, everybody wins. How's the town?"

"Charming," Silas said. "Full of scientists."

"Even better. Local expertise, right? Free data." A cheerful pause. "Hey — you'll figure it out. You always do. You're the guy who keeps things clean."

Silas looked across the parking lot at the trust office door, behind which Paloma Okafor was presumably waiting with five years of data and eleven years of grievances, and said, "Yeah," into the phone. "Clean. That's me."

He hung up and stood there a moment longer than he needed to, listening to the gulls and the water and the faint, rising sound of a future rearranging itself around him.

Then he knocked on the door.

It opened, and there she was — same jacket, different expression, the expression of a woman who had spent the weekend deciding exactly how much of the past she was willing to discuss with a man holding a clipboard of his own.

"Right on time," Paloma said. "That's new."

"Eleven years is a long time to practice punctuality," Silas said, and followed her in, and did not yet know — could not yet know — that the letter had already found her, that the past had beaten him to the dock by days, waiting with wet hands.

But that, as the town would soon teach him, was a matter for low tide.
