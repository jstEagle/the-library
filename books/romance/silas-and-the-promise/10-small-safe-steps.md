## 10. Small Safe Steps

My sister arrived on the Thursday ferry with a duffel bag, a bottle of wine, and the particular expression of a woman who had been rehearsing an argument the whole way across the water.

"You didn't answer my last three texts," Amara said by way of hello, dropping the duffel on my kitchen floor like she owned the place. Which, in every way that mattered, she did.

"I answered one."

"With a thumbs-up. A thumbs-up, Paloma. You're thirty-four years old and you sent me a thumbs-up about our grandmother's birthday. I thought you'd been taken by cultists."

"The reef survey ran long." I handed her a glass of water because it was eleven in the morning and we were both raised properly, even if only one of us still acted like it. "And Nana would have hated a long message. She thinks emojis are witchcraft."

"Nana thinks cardigans are witchcraft." Amara took the water anyway, drank half of it, and then set it down with the deliberate care of someone clearing space on the table for what was coming next. "Okay. Sit down."

"I'm standing fine."

"Sit down anyway."

I sat. This is the thing about little sisters that nobody warns you about: they remember you before you were careful. Amara knew all the versions of me — the loud one, the reckless one, the girl who once climbed the water tower in Providence on a dare and had to be talked down by a fireman named Gus who gave her a butterscotch candy at the top like she was a cat in a tree. That girl would never have sat when told to sit. That girl would have climbed higher out of spite.

But Amara also knew the version of me who came to Merrow Point five years ago with two suitcases and a flinch where her trust used to be, and I think she loved that version most stubbornly of all.

"So," she said. "Silas Reyes."

The name landed in my kitchen like a gull through an open window — sudden, flapping, knocking things over.

"There's no Silas Reyes," I said. "There's a consultant. Who happens to be named Silas Reyes. Coincidence of geography."

"A coincidence of geography that you've now mentioned four times in two phone calls without me asking."

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Outside the window, October was doing its best work — the marsh gone copper, the light low and gold, the kind of morning that made even the bait shop look picturesque. I had been living inside this view for five years and it had never once asked me a hard question. That was most of why I loved it.

"He's working the marina assessment," I said finally. "That's all. We're on opposite sides of a public fight. It's professional."

"Mm."

"It is."

"Mm-hm."

"You're doing the sound."

"I'm allowed to do the sound. I've earned the sound." Amara pulled out the chair across from me and sat, forearms on the table, close enough that I could see the same brown in her eyes as mine, though hers held less salt water. "Paloma. I flew— okay, I took the ferry, but spiritually I flew — because Dev said you called him last week and asked him how he knew he could trust his business partner. Out of nowhere. At nine p.m."

"That was a hypothetical question."

"At nine p.m.?"

"Some questions keep business hours and some don't."

"And then yesterday Mom said you called her and asked whether Dad ever regretted not going back for his master's degree. And today I get off the boat and the first thing you say is that there's no Silas Reyes here." She spread her hands. "You're interrogating everyone's life choices except your own, which is what you do instead of having feelings. I know because I invented this move. I taught it to you."

"You did not teach it to me."

"I absolutely taught it to you. You were a feral child who felt everything at full volume until I showed you how to file things away in neat little drawers, and now look at you. The drawer queen of Merrow Point."

Despite myself, despite everything, I laughed. It came out crooked, but it came out. "The drawer queen."

"Queen of drawers. Empress of sealed compartments. Ruler of the small safe steps." Amara's voice gentled on the last words, and that was worse than any teasing, because *small safe steps* was something I had actually said to her, two years ago, on the phone, crying after the anniversary of the worst spring of my life. *I'm healing,* I'd told her. *Slowly. Small safe steps.* And she had repeated it back to me like a blessing ever since, and I had built a life inside the phrase like a hermit crab builds inside a shell, and I had never once asked whether the shell was getting tight.

"Something happened," she said. Not a question.

I looked at the window. The marsh. The gold light. The tide chart pinned to the wall behind her head, because I measured my days in tides and turtle nests and other things that could be predicted, tracked, trusted to return exactly as promised.

"A letter came," I said.

Amara waited. My sister had learned patience the hard way, growing up behind me, watching me slam doors she then had to walk through carefully to find out what I'd broken.

"After the nor'easter. The post office cleared out the damaged boxes, and there was a letter in mine. Water-stained. Postmarked eleven years ago." I turned my glass in a slow circle on the table. "From Silas."

Amara went very still. In our family, stillness was the loudest reaction available.

"The letter," she said slowly, "that would have arrived right around when you stopped answering anyone's calls and changed your number and told us all never to say his name."

"The very same."

"Eleven years in a dead letter office."

"Or wherever they keep the drowned mail. There's a whole facility in Atlanta. I looked it up at three in the morning, which tells you how well I slept this week."

"What did it say?"

And there it was. The question underneath all my questions to Dev and Mom and Dad, the one I'd been circling for days like a bird that couldn't find its branch. I had read the letter eleven times now. I had it memorized the way I had nest locations memorized — precisely, helplessly, against my will.

I told her. All of it. His father's fraud, the scholarship threat, the night Silas chose to let me believe the worst of him rather than drag me into his family's ruin. How he'd stood in the rain outside my dorm and let me hate him because hating him was safer than the alternative. How the letter was supposed to explain everything a year later, once his father's trial was done, once it was safe — and how it never came, and how Silas had apparently decided that silence was the honorable ending, and lived inside that decision for a decade.

When I finished, Amara was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Okay, so, first: that's a movie. Second: are you okay?"

"I don't know what I am." I laughed, wet and surprised. "That's the problem. Five years, Amara. I spent five years building a person who doesn't need him, doesn't think about him, doesn't — and it turns out the whole foundation was wrong. He didn't betray me. He *sacrificed* me, which is somehow both better and worse, and I don't know how to grieve eleven years that were lost by accident. There's no villain. There's just weather. A letter caught in weather."

"Have you talked to him about it?"

"We argued about it on the reef walk. If you can call it arguing. It was more like two people throwing eleven years at each other in tide pools." I rubbed my face. "And then last week there was a storm, and Nest Nine was flooding, and we relocated two hundred eggs together in the dark, and it was—" I stopped.

"It was what?"

"Easy." The word came out small. "It was easy. Like no time had passed. Like my body remembered him before my brain finished voting." I looked up at her. "And then I spent four days avoiding him, which brings us to your visit, doesn't it? Because you got the thumbs-up text and the nine p.m. call to Dev and you put it together."

"I'm the smart one," Amara said. "It's documented."

* * *

We walked the bluff path in the afternoon, the way we always did when she visited, down past the lighthouse keeper's cottage to the bench facing open water. Amara wore her city coat and complained about the wind and held my arm anyway, and for a while we just watched the ferries come and go below us, small white sentences punctuating the gray-blue page of the Sound.

"Can I say the thing?" she asked eventually. "The annoying thing?"

"You'll say it either way."

"I will. Okay." She turned to face me, hair whipping sideways, entirely unbothered. "Is this life healing? Or is it a fortress?"

The question went into me like a hook set clean.

"Because from out here," she went on, "it looks beautiful. Genuinely. You have work you love, a town that knows your name, a house with a view people pay hotels for. But Paloma — you measure everything. You have routines like load-bearing walls. You haven't dated anyone longer than four months since you got here, and the last one ended because — what was it? He wanted to spend a weekend away and you had a nest survey?"

"It *was* a nest survey."

"It was a Tuesday, Paloma. There will be another nest survey. There will always be another nest survey. That's the whole point of turtles — they're extremely reliable." She softened, taking my hand. "I'm not saying your life is fake. I'm saying maybe you built it out of the wreckage of one bad night with a boy who lied to protect you, and you built it to be un-betrayable. Nothing can surprise you here. Nothing can get in. And now something got in — a letter, eleven years late — and the fortress has a crack in it, and instead of looking at the crack you're calling Dev at nine p.m. and asking him about trust like trust is a plumbing issue."

I stared at the water. A fishing boat was coming in, running lights already on in the afternoon dim.

"When he left after Nest Nine," I heard myself say, "he stood at the door of the field station and he said, 'Whenever you're ready, I'll tell you all of it. Everything. No time limit.' And I said 'okay' in a voice like I was agreeing to a dental appointment, and then I hid from him for four days."

"How's that working out?"

"Terribly. I reorganized the sample freezer. Twice. By color."

"By color," Amara repeated, with reverence. "You've reached the final stage."

I laughed again, and this time it turned into something else halfway through, and my sister — bless her, curse her — just put her arm around me and let me do it, standing on the bluff with the wind pushing us toward each other like it had opinions.

"I'm scared," I said, when I could talk again. "Not of him. Of me. Because if I let him explain — really explain, all of it — and it's true, then I have to admit that I spent five years grieving a betrayal that didn't happen. And if I forgive him, then I have to admit the walls were never necessary, and then what was all of it for? The careful life? What was any of it for?"

"Oh, honey." Amara squeezed my hand. "It was for surviving. That's what it was for. Surviving is not a waste, even when you don't need the armor anymore. You don't owe the fortress anything. It kept you alive. But you're allowed to outgrow it. That's not ingratitude. That's just... spring."

Leave it to my sister to make a metaphor about seasonal molting sound profound.

"And for the record," she added, "the fact that you're scared means it matters. The things that don't scare you are usually just chores."

* * *

That evening we ate chowder at the Galley with the rest of the town, and I saw him before I was ready to, the way you always see them before you're ready. Silas sat at the far table with the engineering plans spread out and a cup of coffee gone cold beside him, and he wasn't performing anything — no charm, no consultant polish. He was just working, tired, sleeves pushed up, and when he glanced up and saw me his whole face did something complicated and honest before he mastered it and looked back down.

He didn't cross the room. He didn't wave. He'd said *no time limit*, and he was keeping the terms of it, and somehow that restraint undid me more than pursuit would have.

"Go," Amara murmured into her chowder.

"I'm eating."

"You're stirring. You've stirred that chowder into a smoothie." She nudged my knee under the table. "Small safe steps, right? Here's one: walk over there and say, 'I'm ready. Tell me all of it.' That's it. That's the whole step. You don't have to forgive anybody tonight. You don't have to decide anything. Just open the door and let the man finish a sentence for the first time in eleven years."

"And if hearing it wrecks me?"

"Then you come home and I'm here till Sunday and we'll watch terrible television and I'll pretend not to notice you crying at commercials." She shrugged. "That's what sisters are for. That and teaching you to file your feelings in drawers, which, in hindsight, was a mistake, and I apologize."

I looked at him again. He was rubbing his eyes behind his glasses — glasses, since when did he wear glasses — and marking something on a map of the harbor, and eleven years ago he had stood in the rain and chosen my ruin over his own comfort, badly, wrongly, in the way that young people do terrible mercies, and he had carried it alone ever since because carrying things alone was the only skill his father had ever taught him.

I thought about the eggs from Nest Nine, tucked into their new sand, two hundred small safe steps toward a sea that would try to kill most of them anyway. You relocate them anyway. You give them the chance anyway. That was the entire job. That had always been the entire job.

I stood up.

Amara didn't say anything, just touched my elbow once as I passed, brief as a benediction.

The Galley was loud with Friday noise, and the twenty feet between our tables had never seemed longer or shorter. He looked up when my shadow fell across his maps, and I watched him brace — polite, prepared, braced for more distance, more tide-pool arguments, more of the careful stranger I'd made myself into.

"Silas." My heart was going like a hammer on a hull. "I'm ready. Tell me all of it. Everything, from the beginning. No time limit — but I'd like to start tonight, if that works."

For a second he didn't move, as if he'd spent so long waiting for the sentence that hearing it had knocked something loose in him. Then he closed the folder of plans — gently, deliberately, like closing a door on the whole machinery of his careful life — and stood up, and said:

"Yeah. Yeah, that works. There's a bench on the north bluff. It's where I've been practicing."

"Practicing."

"Telling you." He picked up his coat, and something in his face was terrified and lit from inside, both at once. "For about a week. The seagulls have heard the whole story twice. They were not impressed."

And despite everything — the fear, the crack in the wall, the eleven years — I laughed, and stepped out with him into the cold bright dark, toward the bench, toward the beginning.
