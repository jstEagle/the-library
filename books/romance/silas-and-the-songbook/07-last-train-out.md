## 7. Last Train Out

The storm came in off the western hills the way her grandmother used to say weather did—walking, not running, with its head down and its coat collar up. Ivo watched it from the kitchen window while she washed the supper dishes: a bruise-colored wall of cloud shouldering over the ridge, swallowing the last of the light, dragging its skirt of rain across the valley floor toward the orchard like something that had every intention of staying the night.

She had learned to read storms the way other people read faces. This one meant business. The apple trees were heavy now, three weeks from harvest, branches bowed with fruit that would not survive a hard wind without propping. She made a mental list—stakes, rope, the tarps for the pallets of early Gravensteins already picked and crated in the barn—and she was still making it when the lights went out.

Not flickered. Went out, all at once, the refrigerator's hum dying mid-note, the porch lamp vanishing, the whole house dropping into a darkness so complete it felt physical, like being held under water.

"Well," Ivo said to the empty kitchen, because saying things aloud to no one was a habit five years of living alone had carved into her, and there was no one left to find it strange.

She found the lantern on the top shelf where it always lived, struck a match, and adjusted the wick until the flame stood up yellow and steady. The kitchen reassembled itself around her in amber—the table, the chairs, the window gone black as poured ink except where rain began to streak it in long silver threads. Somewhere out in that dark, between the house and the road, Silas Marchetti was walking back from town.

He'd gone in that afternoon for supplies. He'd said so at the fence line that morning, in the flat, careful voice he used for all their conversations, the voice of a man reading terms aloud: *Going into town. Need brake fluid for the truck. Back by six.* And she'd nodded the way she always nodded, and they'd each returned to their half of the orchard like two countries observing a border neither had drawn but both enforced.

It was past eight now. The last train into Hollow Creek station—the one that connected, three transfers later, to anywhere—left the junction at 7:40. But he'd taken the truck. The truck, however, was another matter. Old trucks and flooded roads were old enemies, and the creek crossing at the bottom of Miller Road went under fast when the storm came down the hills. She'd seen it happen twice in five years. Once she'd been the one stranded on the wrong side, sitting in a borrowed car watching brown water eat the asphalt, waiting two hours for the county to come through.

He would be walking. Or he would be stuck. Either way, he would arrive wet, late, and furious about it, and she would have to decide whether to leave the porch light on.

The porch light required electricity, which the storm had already vetoed. So instead Ivo set the lantern in the kitchen window, where its small yellow circle would bleed out through the rain-streaked glass and mark the house against the dark. It wasn't kindness, she told herself. It was navigation. A person shouldn't walk into an orchard in a storm without a fixed point; people broke ankles that way, or walked into the irrigation ditch behind the north rows, which was running high tonight and didn't care who fell in.

It was practical. Everything she did was practical. That was the whole architecture of her life—practical, load-bearing, inspected annually.

* * *

The wind arrived before he did.

It came down the valley around ten o'clock with a sound like freight, flattening the grass along the drive and setting the trees to thrashing. Ivo spent forty minutes in it, tying props and hauling tarps, her slicker useless within minutes, her hair plastered to her skull, rain driving sideways with a violence that made the whole world feel temporary. She worked the north rows first, then crossed the property line without thinking about it—her hands simply kept going, staking the limbs on his side that were lowest and most loaded, because fruit was fruit and a lost crop was a lost crop regardless of whose name sat on the deed beside hers.

She was on the fourth tree when she registered what she'd done, and stood a moment in the roaring dark with her mouth full of rain, absurdly angry at herself. Not for helping. For forgetting, even for four trees' worth of minutes, that there was a line at all.

That was the thing about storms. They didn't respect paperwork.

She heard the truck before she saw it—or rather heard it failing, the engine note climbing and thinning somewhere up the lane, grinding, catching, dying. Then silence except the rain, and then a shape resolving out of it, duffel bag over one shoulder, moving with the particular hunched determination of a man who has already lost an argument with the night and is merely finishing the paperwork.

Silas came up the drive at a half-jog, boots throwing water, and stopped when the lantern light caught him. Even soaked through, even at twenty feet, she could see him recalibrating—the quick assessment of whether the lit window meant welcome or warning.

"Roads are out," he called, raising his voice over the wind. "Creek took the low crossing. Truck's in it up to the axles."

"Of course it is," Ivo called back, because sympathy was a currency she'd stopped carrying years ago, and sarcasm was legal tender everywhere.

"I can push through to the Henderson place. Their barn—"

"In this?" She gestured at the sky, which chose that moment to deliver a fistful of hail, small and white and stinging. "You'll break your neck and I'll spend harvest explaining a body to the county clerk. She's already suspicious of me."

Something moved across his face—there wasn't enough light to name it, but it wasn't quite a smile and wasn't quite anything else. "Bunkhouse, then."

"Bunkhouse," she agreed. "There's blankets in the cedar chest. Don't touch the box on the shelf above the stove. It's mine."

"Wouldn't dream of it," he said, and turned toward the bunkhouse, and Ivo stood in the rain watching him go, telling herself the odd pressure under her ribs was just fatigue.

* * *

The house creaked all night, the way old houses do in wind—not random sounds but conversational ones, the frame talking to itself, settling arguments between joists. Ivo lay in her bed under two quilts and listened to it, and listened to the rain, which had settled from assault into endurance, a long steady drumming on the roof that should have been the best lullaby in the world. Rain on a tin roof was the sound of being safe indoors. Her grandmother had sworn by it. *Best sleep you'll ever get, Ivo-girl, is storm sleep.*

She was not getting storm sleep.

Because underneath the rain, underneath the wind worrying the eaves, there was another sound: the small domestic noises of a second person occupying her property. A floorboard in the bunkhouse, thirty yards away—she could hear it, or imagined she could, which amounted to the same thing at two in the morning. The scrape of the woodstove door. Once, faintly, a cough. The knowledge of him out there was like a stone in her shoe: too small to justify stopping, too present to ignore.

And here was the thing that made her furious, lying flat on her back staring at a ceiling she couldn't see:

His presence didn't unsettle her.

That was the discovery, and she turned it over again and again like a stone with something fossilized inside it. Fifteen years ago, the thought of Silas Marchetti returning had been the nightmare she never named—the scenario she flinched from whenever a car slowed on the county road, whenever the mail brought anything with a lawyer's letterhead. She had built her whole quiet, careful, load-bearing life partly on the assumption that he was gone for good, and that if he ever came back, it would be like a flood: destructive, totalizing, something she'd survive only by clinging to whatever held.

Instead he'd come back, and it had been—what? Fence-line conversations about brake fluid. Splitting supply costs in a ledger they now both wrote in. Him holding the ladder steady last Tuesday without being asked, his hands on the rails, looking up at her with an expression she'd pretended not to catalog. Arguing about fertilizer ratios with a man who'd once taught her to whistle through a blade of grass, and finding the argument—she searched for a worse word and couldn't find one—*companionable*.

Companionable. With Silas Marchetti. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She rolled onto her side. The lantern in the kitchen had burned down to a red bead; she could see its glow faintly through the bedroom door, a thread of warmth in the dark house. Outside, the orchard was taking the storm the way her grandmother had taught her orchards take storms—roots down, branches flexing, nothing rigid enough to snap. *A tree that won't bend breaks, Ivo-girl.* The old woman had said it about trees. She had probably also meant it about granddaughters, because everything she said came double, like the grafted trees themselves, two varieties sharing one trunk.

Ivo thought about the grafts. She'd found them weeks ago now, working her rows—her grandmother's signature trick, two varieties knitted into a single tree, Honeycrisp and Winesap joined so seamlessly you'd miss the seam unless you knew to look. And Silas had noticed them too, days later, standing at the fence line with his hand on a branch, and neither of them had said a word about it, and the silence had been so loud she'd had to walk away.

Two varieties. One trunk. Somebody had believed that was possible once. Somebody had bet the whole orchard on it.

Her grandmother, specifically. Who had also, apparently, bet on two grandchildren, in ink, on a deed, with a clause that wouldn't die.

"You scheming old woman," Ivo said softly to the dark ceiling, and the ceiling declined to answer, and the rain went on drumming, and somewhere out in the bunkhouse Silas Marchetti turned over—a floorboard's confession, real or imagined—and slept.

She hated how much she wanted to know if he slept well. That was the true humiliation of the hour. Not the softness itself but the curiosity that came wrapped around it, tender and specific and entirely involuntary: Was he warm enough? Had he found the cedar chest? Did he remember, the way she remembered, that the third board from the door in that bunkhouse squeaked, because they'd jumped on it deliberately one summer afternoon while his mother sang in the kitchen and her grandmother pretended not to hear?

She remembered. Of course she remembered. She remembered everything, that was her curse—kept it all in the cellar of herself like fruit in cold storage, preserved past any reasonable season.

The wind rose again around three, a long sustained roar that bent the big Winesap by the gate nearly double, and Ivo got up and stood at the black window with her arms crossed, watching the shapes of her trees fight and sway and hold. Every one of them bending. None of them breaking. And her eyes, traitorously, went to the bunkhouse, where a single point of orange light still glowed in the window—he'd kept a candle or a lantern going, a small stubborn star in all that chaos—and she caught herself checking it the way sailors check a lighthouse, and had to physically turn away.

*This means nothing,* she told herself. *People check on fires. It's a fire hazard. That's all that is.*

The lie was so transparent that even the storm seemed embarrassed by it.

* * *

She must have slept eventually, because she woke to gray light and the stunned, rinsed quiet that follows a hard night—everything dripping, everything gleaming, the world looking scoured and new. The power was still out. She dressed in the cold, built up the woodstove fire, put the kettle on, and stepped out onto the porch with two mugs of coffee before her brain could file an objection.

The orchard had survived. Limbs down here and there, a sea of leaves and small green apples littering the grass, but the trees stood, all of them, even the old Winesap by the gate, which had lost one major limb but stood leaning on its prop like a tired soldier refusing to sit down.

The bunkhouse door opened while she was still surveying the damage.

Silas came out slowly, working his shoulder like it had stiffened overnight, and looked at the wreckage of the morning, and then at her, standing on her own porch with two mugs of coffee, one of which was very obviously extra.

They regarded each other across thirty yards of dripping grass.

"Storm took the crossing out," he said finally. "County won't have it passable for a day or two. Truck's going to need a winch."

"So you're stuck here."

"Looks like it."

"And the trains don't run on Sundays anyway," she added, though nobody had mentioned trains, and the words escaped before she could weigh them, and she saw him register the slip—the way his head tilted slightly, the way something almost gentle entered his face and was immediately, carefully escorted out again.

"No," he said. "They don't."

She held up the second mug, because retreat required cover fire. "You look like a man who slept badly on a mattress older than both of us. This is charity, not hospitality. Drink it on your side of the line."

He crossed the grass—crossed the line, technically, though neither of them observed the border anymore, not really, not since the storm had blown it flat—and took the mug, and their fingers didn't touch, and she noted with clinical precision that she had checked whether they would.

"Third board from the door still squeaks," he said quietly, into the steam.

Ivo looked at the mountains, which were out this morning, enormous and white-capped and indifferent, having scrubbed themselves clean overnight. "Everything in that bunkhouse squeaks. It's a museum of squeaking."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's not a question anyone sensible would ask." She drank her coffee. The morning smelled of wet earth and bruised apples and woodsmoke, and it was, infuriatingly, a beautiful morning, the kind her grandmother would have called *a keeper*. "There's a limb down on the Winesap by the gate. I'll need help clearing it before it tears more bark off. You owe me for the winch I'm going to have to rent."

"I owe you for considerably more than that," Silas said, and there was a weight under the words that she didn't examine, because examining it would have required asking what he meant, and she wasn't ready to know.

They cleared the limb together as the sun climbed, sawing and hauling in a rhythm so easy it frightened her, passing the saw back and forth without a word wasted, like a conversation conducted in a language they'd both pretended to forget. And it was while she was dragging brush, cheeks burning, hair full of sawdust, that she saw the figure coming up the lane through the wet grass—a man in a gray sedan that had parked at the gate, clipboard in hand, squinting at the fallen limb and the flooded crossing and the two of them working like a crew.

"Morning," the man called, cheerful as a bill collector, which was exactly what he was. "Looking for whoever's responsible for this property. There's a matter of a payment that's come due."

Ivo's stomach dropped through the wet grass, through the soil, through fifteen years of careful arithmetic, all the way to the bedrock.

Beside her, she felt Silas go very still.
