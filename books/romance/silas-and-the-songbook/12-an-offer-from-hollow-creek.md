## 12. An Offer from Hollow Creek

The letter came on a Tuesday, which Ivo would remember later because Tuesdays were the days she did the books, and the books that week had shown her a number she couldn't argue with: four hundred and eleven dollars. That was what remained after wages, fuel, the repair on the cider press, and the interest payments that moved through the orchard's accounts like slow animals drinking at a stream. Four hundred and eleven dollars, and six weeks of harvest still to pay for.

She was still staring at it when Priya's text came through—*check your mail, actual mail, the kind with stamps*—and there it was in the box at the end of the lane, wedged between a seed catalog and a flyer for a church supper: heavy cream paper, an embossed crest of a creek winding through stylized hills.

**HOLLOW CREEK DEVELOPMENT PARTNERS**
*An Opportunity for the Almeida-Marchetti Orchard*

Almeida-Marchetti. She stood in the gravel reading that hyphenated name three times before she could make her feet move toward the house. Someone had done their research. Someone knew exactly how the deed read, both names joined by that small bridging mark, like two people holding hands on paper who hadn't held hands anywhere else in fifteen years.

Inside, the letter was worse than the envelope. It was courteous. It was generous. It laid out figures in tidy columns—the purchase price, the debt retirement schedule, a line item labeled *Seller Discretionary Funds* that had more zeros than anything Ivo had ever been offered for anything, including the novel she'd written at twenty-three that no one bought and the one she'd never finished writing at all.

*We understand the property carries certain encumbrances,* the letter said, in the bloodless voice of people who called debts *encumbrances* and orchards *properties*. *Our offer is contingent upon execution by all parties of record.*

All parties of record. She set the letter down on the kitchen table, next to the salt dish shaped like an apple that her grandmother had kept there for forty years, and went out to check the irrigation lines because her hands needed something honest to do.

* * *

The trouble with an offer like that, Ivo thought, was that it didn't feel like losing. It felt like being handed back everything she'd postponed.

She spent the afternoon among the trees trying not to do arithmetic and doing it anyway. The figure on the second page would clear the bank debt. It would clear the equipment loan, the tax lien, the small brutal personal loans her grandmother had taken in the last year from a lender in Corvallis whose name Ivo refused to say aloud. It would leave enough over to rent an apartment in the city—no, buy one, a real one, with a door that locked and neighbors who didn't know her grandmother's name. Enough to finish the book without watching the mailbox for collection notices. Enough to be, finally, the person she'd imagined becoming before the will was read and the deed turned out to have two names on it like a scar.

She could see it so clearly it hurt: a life with clean edges. No Silas Marchetti appearing at the gate with his duffel bag and his lawyer's letter and his careful voice that never quite matched his eyes. No splitting the rows down the middle like children dividing a sandwich. No lying awake during storms listening to rain fall on the bunkhouse roof and hating herself for counting his footsteps under the eaves.

Freedom, the letter said, in its way. *Sellers frequently describe this transition as a weight lifted.* She could almost hear the developer's representative saying it, some man in a fleece vest with a handshake calibrated to seem warm.

And yet.

She stopped at row fourteen, where the grafts were—her grandmother's old experiments, two varieties knitted into a single trunk, Jonagold wood carrying Gravenstein branches, the cambium long since grown together into something you couldn't separate without killing both. Her grandmother used to say a good graft was just two trees agreeing to share a life. Ivo had rolled her eyes at that when she was nineteen. She wasn't nineteen anymore, but standing there with the offer folded in her jacket pocket like a stone, she found she still couldn't look at those trees without feeling seen.

Because here was the thing she didn't want to examine: the offer required both signatures. And some part of her—not the part doing arithmetic, the other part, the older and less honest part—had felt relief when she read that clause. Because if Silas signed too, then it wasn't her selling out. It was both of them ending it together, mutually, the way adults ended things. He'd get his half of the money and go wherever men like Silas went when they left, and she'd get her half and her clean edges, and neither of them would ever have to find out what the orchard was trying to become with both of them standing in it.

It was a temptation dressed as freedom, and the dress fit perfectly, and that was exactly why she should have been suspicious.

* * *

Silas found her at the bunkhouse at dusk. She heard him coming—she always heard him now, had learned the particular rhythm of his boots on the gravel, heavier on the left—and made herself keep sorting the crates instead of turning around.

"You got one too," he said. Not a question.

"Mail carrier works both sides of the road." She looked up. He was holding his copy, creased open, and his face in the last light was hard to read, which was nothing new. Fifteen years ago she'd been able to read him like weather. Now he was mostly sky.

"You're thinking about it," he said.

"I'm thinking about four hundred and eleven dollars." She said it flatly, to wound or to warn, she wasn't sure which. "You've seen the books. You know what we're carrying."

"I know what we're carrying." He came closer, stopped at the edge of the porch light's reach, as if even now he observed some border neither of them had negotiated. "I also know what Hollow Creek is."

"And what is it?"

He was quiet a moment. Rain had come through earlier and the whole valley smelled of wet dust and bruised apples, sweet and slightly rotten, the smell of October doing its work. "I grew up here," he said finally. "I remember when they tried to buy the Hollis place, up the ridge. My dad said developers don't want orchards. They want dirt. They'll take the trees off and grade the ground flat and put up thirty houses with views." His jaw worked. "Views of what, I don't know. Other houses."

"So you're saying don't sell."

"I'm saying know what you're signing." He held up his copy of the letter. "I haven't decided anything either."

That word—*either*—landed somewhere under her ribs and sat there, warm and unwelcome. She wanted him to have decided. She wanted him to refuse outright, so she could refuse out of spite, which was a currency she understood. Instead he stood there leaving the door open, and she hated how much she wanted to walk through it, and hated more that she couldn't tell whether the thing she wanted was the money or the man or just the ending of the waiting that had defined her whole adult life.

"Goodnight, Silas," she said, and picked up her crates, and went inside before her face could report anything to the enemy.

* * *

That night she took out the songbook.

She told herself it was about the debts—that the margins were full of her grandmother's notes on yields and buyers and the years the late frost took the blossoms—but she knew better by now. She went to it the way other people went to church or whiskey. The lamp threw its circle on the table; outside, the first real cold of the season pressed against the windows like a palm.

Her grandmother's handwriting marched down the page beside an old ballad about a false knight, annotations in pencil gone soft with age: *prices down again, H. says hold on, hold on.* And beneath, in different ink, different hand—his mother's, Rosa Marchetti's, the looping script Ivo remembered from prescription bottles and recipe cards—*hold on to what? the land or each other?*

Ivo ran her thumb along the words. Two women, one songbook, passing sentences back and forth across the years like cups of sugar over a fence. And then the notes simply stopped, mid-page, mid-thought, the year everyone stopped speaking, and the silence after them was fifteen years deep.

She thought about the letter in her jacket. *A weight lifted.* She thought about her grandmother, who had died owing money to a predator and owning an orchard with two names on the deed, and who had done it, Ivo was increasingly sure, on purpose. You didn't accidentally write someone into a deed. You didn't accidentally teach two children to graft side by side and then bind their inheritance together with legal thread. Somewhere in these pages, maybe, was the reason. Somewhere in these pages her grandmother might still be talking.

But tonight the pages only asked their old question—*the land or each other?*—and Ivo closed the book on it, because answering it meant admitting there was a choice at all, and she had planned, very carefully, for five years, to be a woman without choices. Choices got you hurt. Small safe steps. Last train always caught.

Except the train was already gone. It had left fifteen years ago, and she was standing on the platform still, and a man with a duffel bag was sleeping sixty feet away in the bunkhouse, and a stranger with cream stationery wanted to pave the platform and call it progress.

She slept badly, dreaming of graded earth and treeless hills, and woke once to hear rain starting again, and lay listening to it hit the bunkhouse roof, and hated how much the sound steadied her.

* * *

In the morning there was a voicemail from the Hollow Creek representative—a Mr. Voss, voice like a hotel lobby—requesting a meeting, *at your convenience, Ms. Almeida, though I should mention our offer window closes at month's end.* A deadline. Of course a deadline. Pressure was just another tool, and tools were how you knew a deal was a deal and not a gift.

Priya called at noon. "So? Are we rich?"

"It needs both signatures."

A pause on the line, long enough that Ivo could hear Priya deciding how to say what she was going to say. "Ivo. Listen to me. If he signs, you sign. That's the smart play. That's the play that gets you out."

"That's what I keep telling myself."

"But?"

"But it feels—" She stopped, walked to the window. Down the slope, Silas was on his knees in row nine, working the soil around a young tree with his bare hands, methodical, unhurried, like a man planting something he intended to stay for. "It doesn't feel like freedom," she said quietly. "It feels like erasure. Like they're not buying the orchard. They're buying the version of me that never existed—the one who left at eighteen and never came back. And the price is the version that stayed."

Priya was silent. Then, gently, in the voice she saved for truths: "You know what scares you isn't losing the money. It's that if you don't sell, you have to actually live this. Both names, one ground, no exit. That's the real offer on the table, and nobody's making you sign for it."

After she hung up, Ivo stood at the window a long time, watching Silas work, the letter lying on the table behind her like an open trap.

Month's end, Voss had said. Eleven days.

And somewhere in the house, in a battered songbook full of two women's interrupted conversation, an answer was waiting that she was suddenly afraid to find.
