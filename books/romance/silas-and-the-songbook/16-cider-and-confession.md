## 16. Cider and Confession

The frost came on a Thursday, silent as a held breath.

Silas woke before dawn in the bunkhouse with the cold pressed against the window like a face, and he knew without stepping outside what had happened in the night. The air had that particular stillness to it, that glassy hush, and when he breathed on the pane it bloomed and faded against a skin of ice ferned across the glass. First frost. The last of the apples—those stubborn late varieties his father had planted the year Silas was born, the ones that hung on out of pure orneriness—would be at their sweetest now, sugar-concentrated by the cold, and if they weren't picked and pressed within the week they'd drop and rot into the grass, feeding next year's wasps and nothing else.

He dressed in the dark. His hands knew the work even after fifteen years away: wool socks, two shirts, the canvas jacket with the torn pocket Ivo hadn't yet noticed him mending with dental floss because there was no thread in the house. He let himself out into the blue-black morning and stood for a moment on the bunkhouse step, breath smoking, listening to the orchard.

Frost has a sound, if you've grown up around it. A faint crystalline ticking, like something enormous thinking. Every leaf, every blade of grass sheathed in a million small mirrors, and the whole hillside catching the not-yet-light and throwing it back dimly, so that the trees seemed lit from within, ghost-fruit glowing silver in the black branches.

His mother used to say frost nights were the orchard's confession days. *Everything the tree's been holding all season comes clear,* she'd say, her breath fogging the kitchen window while she wrote in the songbook propped open beside the flour tin. *You can't hide sweetness from the cold.*

He hadn't thought of that in years. He thought of it now, standing alone in the freezing dark, and felt the weight of everything else he hadn't thought about in years settle onto his shoulders like the frost itself—thin, glittering, everywhere.

* * *

By seven the press room smelled of apples and cold iron.

It was the oldest building on the property, older than the feud, older than both houses—a stone-walled shed with a plank ceiling beaded with condensation, housing a screw press his grandfather had bought secondhand from a cider works that went under in the fifties. The wooden basket was cracked in two places and patched with straps; the screw groaned like a ship in weather. But it worked. It had always worked. There was something in Silas that found this deeply reassuring—that here, at least, nothing had been allowed to fall apart entirely.

He'd spent the previous afternoon picking the last rows alone, the ones on his side of the invisible line they'd drawn through the orchard back in October, back when the line had meant something like war. Now it just meant logistics. He filled three crates of the little russeted late apples, knobby and ugly as river stones, and carried them down through the frosted grass in the gray light, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge and came slanting gold through the press room's one dirty window, he had the first pressing going—the mash breaking down under the screw, juice running in a thin amber ribbon into the bucket, the smell rising up thick and cidery and impossibly, unbearably like being nine years old.

That was the trouble with this place. Everything smelled like being nine years old.

He heard her before he saw her—boots on the frozen gravel, the particular quick stride he could have picked out of a crowd of thousands, though he'd had no right to that knowledge for fifteen years and had never once used it. He didn't turn around. He kept cranking the screw, slow and steady, and listened to her stop in the doorway, and felt the whole small stone building rearrange itself around her presence the way it always did, the way rooms had done since he was eleven years old and she was ten and furious about something, arms crossed, daring the world to disagree with her.

"You started without me." Not a question. Not quite an accusation either. Something in between that they'd been trading back and forth for weeks now, a currency all their own.

"Apples won't wait for permission," Silas said. "First frost. It's now or they're compost."

A pause. Then her boots crossed the floor, and she came into view at the edge of his vision, hair tied back, sleeves shoved up, a second crate in her arms that she set down beside his with more care than the gesture strictly required. She'd picked too. Her side. She'd gotten up in the freezing dark on her side of the line and picked the last of the late apples and brought them here, to his press, without being asked.

Neither of them said anything about it. That was how they managed things these days—by the meticulous mutual silence of two people circling a fire neither would admit to warming their hands at.

"I'll do the crushing," Ivo said. "You'll flood the mash again. You always flooded the mash."

"I was twelve."

"You were twelve for four consecutive seasons."

The corner of his mouth moved. He turned away before she could see it, and fed more fruit into the crusher, and the morning settled into rhythm: the rattle of the crusher, the groan of the screw, the amber ribbon widening, the two of them moving around each other in the narrow stone room with the unerring choreography of people who had once shared a childhood and were pretending, badly, that muscle memory meant nothing.

* * *

The first gallon went into jugs. The second. By midmorning the light through the window had gone from gold to white, and the frost outside had burned off the branches but still clung in the shadows under the hedgerow, and the press room was warm now with the heat of work, both of them in shirtsleeves, forearms wet to the elbow with juice.

It was Ivo who broke the rhythm. She did that—broke things, named things, walked straight at the thing everyone else stepped around. He'd forgotten that about her, those first weeks. He'd never forget it again.

"My grandmother's songbook," she said, not looking at him, wiping down the press basket with a rag. "The last entry. Did you read it?"

The screw handle went stiff under Silas's hand.

He had read it. Of course he had. He'd read it forty times since the night he'd found the book in the bunkhouse, sitting on the bare mattress with a lamp balanced on a crate, turning pages written in two hands—his mother's looping slanted script, Rosa Almeida's small upright print—and then reaching the final page and finding, in Rosa's handwriting, dated barely a month before her death, a single line that had taken the floor out from under him:

*For Silas, who will understand why the last verse is left unfinished. Come home and finish it.*

"She wrote my name," he said. "In a book that was yours. In a book that—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Your grandmother and my mother hadn't spoken in fifteen years. Nobody in this town would tell me anything except that the Marchettis got what was coming to them. And then your grandmother dies, and the deed shows up with my name on it, which made no sense whatsoever, and I told myself—I told my lawyer, I told myself every night on the drive out here—that I was coming back for the property. Half an orchard. Fair is fair."

The ribbon of juice ran on into the bucket. Somewhere outside a crow said something sour and flapped off.

"That's not why you came," Ivo said. It wasn't a question either. She was looking at him now, rag forgotten in her red-stained hands, and her eyes had that quality he remembered from every important conversation of their childhood—the total, unnerving attention, like being the only lit window in a dark house.

"No," Silas said. "That's not why I came."

He wiped his hands slowly on his thighs, buying seconds, and understood that the moment had arrived the way frost arrives—not suddenly, but all at once, inevitable, everything leading here. He had rehearsed versions of this confession on the long drive west, in motel rooms, in the bunkhouse at two in the morning. None of the rehearsals survived contact with her actual eyes.

"The songbook's last entry named me," he said. "And I needed to know—" His voice caught on the plainness of it, the humiliating simplicity. "I needed to know if anyone had waited. If I came back and knocked on that door, would there be one person in this town who didn't slam it. Fifteen years, Ivo. I built a whole life on the assumption that nobody had. It was easier that way. And then a dead woman I hadn't seen since I was seventeen wrote my name in a book of songs and dared me from beyond the grave to come find out."

The press dripped. The light lay in a long pale bar across the stone floor between them, and Ivo stood on one side of it and he stood on the other, and neither of them moved.

"Why?" she asked. Just that. But he heard everything underneath it—the fifteen years underneath it, the summer underneath it, the empty road and the unanswered letters and the boy who vanished between one July and the next. "Why did you go, Silas? Why did you stay gone? You keep saying the town, the town, but the town wasn't writing you letters. I was—"

"I know."

"—I wrote you *eleven letters*, I counted them, I still know the number—"

"I know." Louder than he meant. The word hit the stone walls and came back at him. He pressed the heels of his hands briefly against his eyes, and when he lowered them she hadn't moved, hadn't softened, and he loved her a little for that, and hated himself a lot for what he was about to hand her.

"Mom got sick the spring before I left," he said. "Not sick-sick. Tired-sick. The kind doctors wave at. Dad was already drowning by then—you remember the debt, everyone remembered the debt, the whole town treated our name like a diagnosis. What nobody knew—what *I* didn't know until later—was where the debt came from. But that's not—" He stopped himself, jaw tight. One truth at a time. He'd learned that much. "She asked me to leave. My mother. She said if I stayed I'd spend my twenties carrying crates for creditors and learning to hate this valley, and she'd rather I hate it from far away. She said she'd write. She said—" His throat closed. He made himself finish. "She died fourteen months later. I was working a harvest in Yakima when the call came. And I didn't come back for the funeral, Ivo, because coming back meant walking past your house, and I couldn't stand the idea of you watching me bury her. So I stayed gone. And staying gone gets easier the way any lie gets easier. You just stop checking whether it's true."

The silence afterward was enormous. It filled the press room like water fills a hull.

Ivo's face had gone very still—not blank, worse than blank, the stillness of someone reordering everything they believed at speed. When she finally spoke, her voice was low and level and shaking only at its very bottom, like ice about to give.

"You let me think you chose to disappear. You let me think I wasn't worth one letter back. Eleven letters, Silas. I was sixteen, and I thought—" She stopped. Breathed. "My grandmother watched me write those letters. She watched me wait at the gate. She never said a word. And you're telling me you were grieving your mother alone in some orchard three states away, and I was here hating you, and *she knew*. Both of them knew, didn't they. They knew everything."

"Yes," Silas said quietly. "I think they did."

* * *

They pressed the rest of the apples mostly in silence.

But it was a different silence than before—stripped of its armor, raw-skinned. They handed crates back and forth. They racked jugs. At some point Ivo put a kettle on the camp burner in the corner, because the press room had always had a camp burner in the corner, and made tea in two chipped mugs that had clearly been waiting in the cupboard for exactly this, and the domesticity of it undid Silas more thoroughly than any accusation had.

It was nearly dusk when she spoke again. The light through the window had gone copper, then ash, and the first stars were pricking through over the ridge, and the day's pressing sat gleaming in its row of jugs—maybe eight gallons of the year's last cider, sharp and dark and honest.

"There's something you should see," Ivo said.

She went to the shelf above the old workbench—the one his father had built, the one with the burn rings from a hundred thermoses—and took down a wooden box, small, cedar, lid worn smooth. His heart recognized it before his memory did. He'd seen it a thousand times on that same shelf, in another lifetime, and assumed it held screws or blades or the miscellaneous archaeology of a workbench.

She opened it and turned it toward him.

Inside, nested in a bed of cloth gone soft with age, lay half a grafting knife.

The blade. Only the blade—broken clean at the bolster, edge still keen under a skin of oil, the bone handle gone honey-colored with decades of hands. And etched along the flat, in tiny capitals his father had cut with a scribe when Silas was eight years old: **S.M.**

"We each got half a knife," Ivo said. "Your dad's rule. You graft alone, you graft wrong. Two halves, two people, one cut." Her thumb moved over the broken steel without touching it. "When you left, I took your side out of the box. I told myself I was keeping it safe. For fifteen years I kept it safe. I moved apartments twice and it came with me both times, wrapped in a sock, in a box marked KITCHEN, because I couldn't put it in a box marked anything true." She looked up at him then, and her eyes were bright and furious and terrified, all three at once, and Silas felt the sight of it go through him like the frost had gone through the orchard—total, clarifying, merciless. "So don't stand there telling me you needed to know if anyone waited. Because somebody kept half a knife oiled for fifteen years, and she's standing right in front of you, and she is *so angry* at herself for it."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, full dark had come down over the orchard, and the first hard starlight lay along the frost-shadowed rows.

Silas reached into his jacket pocket—where it had lived for six weeks, carried daily like a splinter he couldn't dig out—and drew out the other half. The handle. Bone worn smooth, brass ferrule green with age, and etched in his father's same careful capitals: **I.A.** Ivo Almeida. He'd found it in his mother's things, sealed in an envelope marked with Rosa Almeida's initials, mailed to a forwarding address his mother must have begged out of someone before the end.

His mother had known. His mother had kept Ivo's half of the knife all those years, and Rosa had kept Silas's, and the two women had divided the children between them like a graft—two varieties bound at the wound, forced to grow as one tree or die as none.

"They planned this," Ivo whispered. She was staring at the handle in his palm. "From the beginning. The deed, the book, the knives—they *grafted* us, Silas. Before we were old enough to know we were being grafted."

"And it worked," Silas said. His voice came out rough. "Whatever else they did, whatever mess they made—it worked. We're standing in the same room. We pressed cider together today. You laughed twice, and I counted."

Her breath caught audibly. For a long moment neither of them moved, and the space between them was charged and narrow and full of every unsaid thing, and Silas understood that whatever happened in the next thirty seconds would decide the shape of the rest of his life.

Then Ivo reached past him—to the shelf, to the cedar box—and laid her half of the knife beside his on the workbench, broken edges aligned, the two pieces making one whole instrument lying in the lamplight. Whole, and still broken. Whole, and needing a hand to join them.

"There are letters," she said quietly. "In the songbook. Margin notes, coded ones—between her and your mother, going back years. I haven't finished reading them. Some of what's in there—" She stopped, and he watched her choose courage over safety in real time, watched it cost her. "Some of what's in there isn't about us. It's about the debt. About who really owed whom. And Silas—I don't think your father's ledger tells the whole story. I think somebody wrote parts of it that were never true."

The lamplight flickered. Out in the dark orchard, the frost was forming again, sheathing every branch in silver, bringing everything hidden all season up into the cold clear light.

Silas looked at the joined halves of the knife, and then at her, and felt the ground shift beneath the whole carefully rebuilt architecture of his return.

"Then tomorrow," he said, "we read them together."
