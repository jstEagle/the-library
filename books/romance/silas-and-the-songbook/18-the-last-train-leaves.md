## 18. The Last Train Leaves

**Silas**

The bunkhouse smelled of sawdust and cold coffee, and Silas packed it the way he'd learned to pack everything: fast, before he could change his mind.

He folded the flannel shirts first. Then the grafting knife, wrapped in a rag because he couldn't stand to see it bare. The songbook went in last, on top, where his hand could find it without looking—the way it had lived under his palm for three months now, that soft-worn cover, those two hands writing across decades like women passing notes under a door God had locked.

He'd read the last entry again this morning. He didn't know why. He knew exactly why.

*If my girl can't be brave, let her borrow the boy's name and make something brave of it.*

His grandmother's handwriting. His mother's ink beneath it, faded: *Agreed. But they'll have to choose each other, not the trees.*

And beneath both, in pencil, in a hand he hadn't recognized until last week—smaller, shakier, older—his grandmother again, or maybe Ivo's grandmother, the letters blurring into one woman by the end:

*They will need one winter alone to learn what silence costs.*

Well. They'd had their winter. He'd had his fifteen years of it.

Silas zipped the duffel and stood in the middle of the room he'd slept in as a boy, when it had smelled of his father's tobacco and the rain coming off the ridge. He'd come back for answers. He'd found them—every ugly one—and he'd found something else besides, something he had no right to keep: Ivo laughing with cider foam on her lip, Ivo swearing at a stuck ladder rung, Ivo at midnight in the press house saying *don't go* without saying anything at all.

But she'd signed nothing yet. And today the lawyer's letter had come, and he'd watched her read it through twice, her face going carefully smooth, the way it did when she was building walls out of arithmetic. *Enough money to erase everything,* her expression said. *Enough to never have to want anything dangerous again.*

She'd looked at him after, and she'd smiled, and it was the polite smile, the barbed-wire smile from the first day at the gate, and he understood.

She was going to sell. Of course she was. Five years of living small had taught her that safety was the only currency that never bounced, and he was the most dangerous thing this valley had ever offered her. He would not make her pay for choosing him. He'd watched his mother pay for choosing his father—paid in whispers, in closed doors, in a town that made her small until there was almost nothing left. He would not do that to Ivo Almeida.

So: the evening train. 6:42. The same train he'd taken out at seventeen with a duffel bag and a broken heart, except this time the heart was whole, which somehow hurt worse.

He carried the songbook to the kitchen of the big house, where she wasn't, and left it on the table with a note he wrote four times and settled for:

*I'm not making you choose. That's the only kind thing I have left in me. The deed stands either way—sell it, sign it, be free. You were the best part of coming back. —S.*

Then, because he was a coward about some things and honest about one, he added underneath, smaller:

*The graft took. Both varieties. Check the north row in spring.*

He set his key on top of the book. He did not look at the orchard on his way down the lane. If he looked, he'd stay, and staying unchosen would kill them both slower than leaving ever could.

The fog was coming up off the creek already, early for evening, thick as wool. Good. Fog meant he wouldn't see the trees in the rearview of his memory.

The station at Hollow Creek was one platform, one bench, one clock with a face gone amber from forty years of sun. Silas bought his ticket from the machine, sat down, and put the duffel between his feet like luggage and himself were the same category of thing: freight in transit.

6:14. Twenty-eight minutes.

He thought about the way she'd said *stay* once, back in October, drunk on frost-cider, and then pretended she'd said *stay warm.* He thought about the receipt she'd found, and how she'd come at him furious and ended up sitting on the bunkhouse floor beside him, shoulder to shoulder against the wall, neither of them moving for an hour. He thought about her grandmother's line, the one Ivo had finally let herself read aloud to him two nights ago, standing in the cold kitchen with tears she refused to acknowledge:

*'Truth is just loyalty to someone who isn't in the room.'*

"Then be loyal," he said to the empty platform, to the fog, to whichever dead woman was listening. "Be loyal to me while I'm gone. Just once."

The clock ticked. Somewhere down the track, faint as a memory, a whistle.

**Ivo**

Priya slapped the letter down on the kitchen table like she was dealing cards in a game Ivo was losing.

"He left you a note."

"I read the note."

"You read the note *out loud* in a flat voice like a weather report, which is not the same thing as hearing it." Priya pulled out a chair and sat on it backwards, arms crossed over the backrest, which was her negotiating posture, her interrogation posture, her *I love you but I am not leaving this kitchen* posture. "Ivo. Say the sentence. The actual sentence."

Ivo looked at the note instead. *I'm not making you choose.* The handwriting slanted hard to the right, urgent even when it was trying to be gentle. She'd known that slant since she was nine years old, watching him write their names in the dirt with a stick, hers always first.

"He thinks I'm going to sell."

"Aren't you?"

"I don't know." The honesty came out of her like a tooth. "That's the problem. I've spent five years not knowing things. Not knowing was safe. And now—" She pressed her palm flat over the songbook where it sat on the table, over the margin notes, over fifteen years of two women conspiring in pencil. "Now I know exactly what I want and it's the one thing that could ruin me."

Priya was quiet for a moment. Outside, the fog had swallowed the orchard whole; the window showed nothing but white, as if the world had been erased down to the kitchen and whatever was inside it.

"When your grandmother died," Priya said, "you told me the worst part wasn't the grief. It was that nobody had ever asked you what you wanted, so you'd forgotten how to answer."

"That's not fair."

"No. It's worse than fair. It's true." Priya stood, took her coat off the hook, and held it out. "The train leaves at 6:42. It's 6:03. Hollow Creek station is eleven minutes if you drive like you mean it, and you have never once in your life driven like you mean it, so really you have six minutes and a miracle."

"What am I supposed to say when I get there?" Ivo's voice cracked in the middle, and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "'Don't go'? He'll stay for 'don't go.' People stay for 'don't go' all the time and then spend twenty years resenting the platform."

"So don't say that."

"Then what?"

Priya smiled, and it was the soft smile, the rare one, the one she saved for moments she considered historic. "Say the thing you'd say if you weren't afraid. You told me once you'd forgotten how to answer. So answer."

Ivo grabbed her coat. She grabbed the songbook too—without deciding to, her hand just took it, the way her hand had taken his side of the grafting knife off the workbench last spring and hidden it in her sock drawer like contraband, like a relic, like proof.

She drove like she meant it.

The fog came at the windshield in ropes. The wipers beat a rhythm she'd have called a song once, back when the songbook was just a songbook. Her headlights carved tunnels that closed behind her. Eleven minutes became eight because she ran the light at Mill Road, because she had spent five years catching every last train and stopping at every yellow light and keeping herself so small that grief could barely find her, and she was done, she was *done*, being a person who arrived safely at places that didn't matter.

The station materialized out of the white like something developing in a darkroom: the bench, the clock, the single lamp burning amber, and Silas Marchetti standing under it with a duffel bag at his feet, looking like a photograph she'd spent fifteen years trying not to develop.

6:31.

She parked badly. She got out. The cold hit her lungs like water.

He turned at the sound of the door, and she watched his face do the thing her grandmother's letters had described across thirty years of margins: hope arriving without permission, then being wrestled down, tied up, made polite.

"Ivo." Her name in his mouth still fit like a hand in an old glove. "You shouldn't—"

"Don't." She walked toward him, boots ringing on the platform boards, the songbook clutched to her chest like a shield she intended to use as a weapon. "Don't tell me what I should or shouldn't do. Everyone's been doing that my whole life. My grandmother did it with a deed. Your mother did it with a songbook. The developer's doing it with a deadline. And now you're doing it with a *train*."

"It's not—"

"You left a note saying you're not making me choose." She stopped an arm's length away. Close enough to see the fog beading in his hair. Close enough to see that he was terrified, which was the single most consoling thing she'd learned in fifteen years: that he had always been afraid too, that the boy who left and the man who returned had both been running from the same country—the country of being unwanted. "You don't get to decide that. Choosing isn't a burden you can carry for me, Silas. It's the only thing that's actually *mine*."

The whistle came again, closer. Down the track, a headlight swelled in the fog, patient as fate.

"So here's my answer," Ivo said, and her voice shook, and she let it, because her grandmother's last letter had taught her that truth was loyalty to someone who wasn't in the room, and everyone who mattered was in the room now, including the dead, including the girl she used to be. "I'm not asking you to stay for the orchard. The orchard can burn. The deed can rot. I'll sign the developer's paper tomorrow if that's what the trees are worth to you—I don't care about the ground."

"Ivo—"

"Listen to me." She stepped closer. The train was coming; she could feel it in the boards, a tremor rising through the soles of her boots, through her knees, through the place behind her ribs where five careful years had built their careful dam. "I kept your side of the grafting knife. Fifteen years. In my sock drawer, like a lunatic, like a girl who couldn't throw away half of something. And I told myself it was about the orchard, about fairness, about the past—but it was never about any of that. It was about you. It was always, *always* about you, and I am so tired of being safe, Silas, I am so tired of catching trains instead of missing them—"

The headlight broke through the fog.

"—so I'm not asking you to stay," she said, and the word came out of her whole, undivided, the first complete sentence of her adult life. "I'm asking if I can *choose* you. Out loud. On purpose. With witnesses." She gestured wildly at the empty platform, the amber clock, the fog itself. "There are no witnesses. There's a train and a clock and my best friend's casserole getting cold, and I don't care. Choose me back or don't, but do it knowing that I chose you first—not the trees, not the ground, not the safe life. *You.*"

The train slid into the station, brakes sighing, windows lit gold, doors opening onto the dark with a hydraulic breath.

Neither of them moved.

Silas looked at the open door. He looked at the duffel bag. He looked at her—at her mouth, at her eyes, at the songbook crushed between them like a heart they were holding together—and when he spoke, his voice was wrecked.

"The north row," he said. "In spring. I grafted your grandmother's variety next to mine. All twelve trees. I wanted—" He stopped. Started again. "I wanted there to be something of us that survived regardless of what we decided. Something that didn't need permission."

"Did it take?"

"I won't know until spring."

"Then you'll have to stay to find out." She was crying now, fully, shamelessly, the fog taking the tears and giving nothing back. "Not for the deed. Not for the debt. Stay because I'm asking. Stay because I spent fifteen years learning how to live without you and it turns out I was terrible at it. Stay because—" and here she laughed, wet and disbelieving, because joy had arrived in her body without permission and she was letting it, she was *letting it*— "because somebody has to teach my niece the knife work, and I refuse to raise another generation of cowards."

The conductor leaned out of the doorway, bored, eternal. "Boarding? This is the boarding, folks."

Silas picked up the duffel bag.

Ivo's heart did not stop—it did the opposite, it went everywhere at once, flooding every field she owned—and then he turned and set it down on the bench, deliberately, the way a man sets down a weight he has been carrying since he was seventeen years old.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Okay." He crossed the space between them and took her face in both hands, and his palms were cold from the fog and shaking, and she put her hands over his and held them there, holding him to her the way you hold a graft—firm, aligned, believing in the joining before there's any evidence of it. "I choose you back. Out loud. On purpose." His forehead came down against hers. Behind them the doors sighed shut, the whistle blew long and low and mournful, and the train pulled away from Hollow Creek carrying no one they loved. "God help us both, Ivo. I choose you."

They stood like that while the rails sang themselves quiet and the fog closed over the track, erasing the train, erasing the escape route, erasing everything except the two of them and the amber clock ticking toward whatever came next.

"We're going to lose," she said into his collar. "Against the developer. We have no money and a forged ledger and a town that hasn't forgiven either family."

"Probably."

"And the debts—"

"Are ours now. Together. Isn't that romantic."

She laughed into his coat, and felt him laugh too, and felt underneath both of them the platform trembling faintly—not with trains anymore, but with something else, something arriving from far down the line.

Because at the edge of the lamplight, where the fog thinned, a car had pulled in. Headlights off. Engine idling. And behind the wheel, a silhouette she knew from newspaper photographs and her grandmother's oldest grievances sat perfectly still, watching them.

The developer had come to see the sale signed.

Instead, he had just watched the last train leave without them.

* * *

He didn't get out of the car. That was the first thing Ivo noticed—that he simply sat there, a shape behind glass, the way predators watch water holes, patient, certain the thirst would bring them close enough eventually. Then the headlights snapped on, full beam, pinning them to the platform like specimens, and the window hummed down.

"Miss Almeida. Mr. Marchetti." His voice carried across the cold air, pleasant as a radio jingle. "Lovely evening for sentiment. I do hope it was productive—my lawyers tell me the deadline moves to Friday whether or not the two of you have finished... deciding." A pause, silk over steel. "Friday at noon. After that, the offer comes off the table, and my attorneys begin proceedings against the estate. Forged instruments make such fascinating litigation, don't you find? All those old debts, resurfacing. I'd hate for the town to revisit any of it."

The window hummed shut. The car pulled away, taillights dissolving into the fog like embers into ash.

Silas's arm tightened around her shoulders. "Friday," he said quietly.

"Friday," Ivo said—and felt, absurdly, against all evidence, against every ledger and deadline and debt, the wild unfalling certainty that they were going to win.
