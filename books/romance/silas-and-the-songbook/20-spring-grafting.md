## 20. Spring Grafting

The morning of the first planting day, Silas woke before the birds and lay in the dark of the bunkhouse listening to the orchard breathe.

That was the only way he could describe it, even to himself. Fifteen years away had taught him the sound of cities—the hydraulic sigh of bus doors, the particular loneliness of a radiator in an empty apartment—but they had never taught him anything like this: the slow exhalation of two hundred acres of apple trees coming out of dormancy, buds swelling with a patience he was only now learning to imitate. The frost had broken sometime in the night. He could smell it, wet earth and green wood and the faint sweetness of last year's fallen fruit composting into the rows.

He got up, dressed in the work clothes that had finally stopped smelling like the train, and stood at the window while the light came up gray-gold over Hollow Creek ridge. Somewhere across the yard, in the house, Ivo would be awake too. She was always awake first. It was one of the hundred small things he'd relearned about her since winter—since the platform, since the word *stay* spoken like a hand held out over open track—and each one arrived with the same quiet astonishment, as if he were reading letters from someone he'd thought dead.

The lawsuit against Meridian Development sat in a folder on the desk behind him, thick as a hymnal. Their lawyer, a brisk woman named Okafor who wore her skepticism like jewelry, had told them in January: *This will take a year, maybe two. It will take money you don't have. And it will take you both showing up, every time, together.* So far they had managed all three. The town had managed the rest—tractors appearing on muddy mornings, casseroles left on the porch rail under dish towels weighted with river stones, old men who remembered his father stopping by the fence to say nothing much, which was its own kind of absolution.

But today there was no folder, no deposition, no ledger with its forged columns. Today there was only the bench under the lean-to, the whetstone, the bundle of scionwood wrapped in damp burlap, and the row of young rootstock waiting along the south field like a choir before the anthem.

Today they would finish what their elders started.

* * *

Ivo's niece arrived at eight, dropped off by Ivo's brother Marcus in a pickup that rattled like a maraca, and came barreling through the gate with the specific velocity of a nine-year-old who has been promised knives.

"Uncle Silas." She said it without hesitation, without irony, with the flat certainty of a child who has decided something and considers the matter closed. Her name was Junie, and she had been calling him Uncle Silas since February, since the afternoon he'd pulled her out of the creek bed after she'd gone in after a dog, and neither Ivo nor Marcus had corrected her, and so it had hardened into fact the way all the truest things in this valley seemed to do.

"Juniper Almeida," Ivo said, following more slowly with a thermos and a paper sack of biscuits, "what did we say about claiming relatives."

"That you have to mean it," Junie said. "And I mean it."

Silas felt something move through his chest that he had no clinical name for. He looked at Ivo over the girl's head. She was watching him with that expression she still wore sometimes—the one that said she couldn't quite believe the door had stayed open, that she kept testing the latch with her shoulder—and then she looked away, toward the rootstock, and handed him the thermos as if nothing had passed between them at all.

"Show her the knife first," she said. "Rules before blades."

"There are rules?" Junie asked, delighted.

"There are always rules," Silas said. "In grafting, especially. You're asking two trees to become one tree. That's not a thing you rush."

He knelt at the bench and laid out the tools the way his father had taught him, thirty-some years ago, in a shed that no longer existed: the grafting knife with its single beveled edge, the wax pot warming on the camp stove, the mallet, the bundle of scions. He unwrapped the burlap slowly, making a small ceremony of it, because Junie's eyes were the kind that rewarded ceremony.

Two varieties. That was the whole point, and both families' hands were in it. From the Marchetti side, the old heirloom pippin his nonna had brought cuttings of from the old country—a russeted, ugly, magnificent apple that tasted like honey cut with cider vinegar. From the Almeida side, the late-keeping dessert apple Ivo's grandfather had bred himself, pale gold, stubborn, famous in three counties for lasting until Easter in a cold cellar. His mother and Ivo's grandmother had spent years planting them interleaved down the long rows—one of theirs, one of hers, one of theirs—until the whole orchard read like a sentence written in two handwritings.

Then the feud. Then the silence. Then fifteen years in which the pattern had simply stopped, the last four rows planted with whatever stock was cheap, the sentence abandoned mid-clause.

"We're finishing the pattern," Ivo said. She had come to stand beside him at the bench, sleeves pushed up, forearms already freckling in the early sun. "Rows eleven through fourteen. Every other tree. Marchetti pippin, Almeida keeper, back and forth, all the way down."

"Why every other one?" Junie asked.

"So neither kind is ever alone," Ivo said, and Silas heard the second meaning land in her own ears a half-second after it left her mouth; she went pink at the cheekbones and became very interested in the wax pot.

* * *

He taught Junie the knife the way his father had taught him, which was to say with terror disguised as patience.

"Whittle grip," he said, folding her small fingers around the handle. "Blade comes toward your thumb, never away. Your thumb is the brake. If you can't trust your thumb, you put the knife down and you watch."

"I can trust my thumb."

"Prove it. Take bark off this stick. One long ribbon, thin as paper, no steps, no gouges."

She chewed her lip and worked. Her first ribbon tore. Her second was better. By the fifth she had a strip of bark curling off the stick like a wood shaving, and she held it up to the light with the fierce silent pride of a child who does not want to be praised but wants very badly to be *seen*, and Silas saw her, and said so, and watched her tuck the praise somewhere permanent behind her ribs.

"Now the cut that matters." He took a scion of the pippin—last winter's growth, pencil-thin, three good buds—and showed her the splice. "You're cutting a tongue. A long slanted slice, and then a little downward notch, so when it meets the rootstock's matching cut, the two tongues lock. Like holding hands." He made the cut in one motion, clean, the cambium showing green-white at the wound's heart. "This green layer right here—that's the living part. Bark is just a coat. Wood is just plumbing. This is where the tree actually *is*. And the whole trick, the entire secret, is that these two green layers have to touch. Yours and theirs. Cambium to cambium. If they touch, the tree figures out how to grow together. If they don't, no amount of wax or hope will save it."

"Like a puzzle," Junie said.

"Like a promise," Ivo said quietly, from the other end of the bench, where she was cutting her own scions with quick sure strokes. She didn't look up. "You're promising the root it gets to taste a different apple. You're promising the top it gets different roots. Neither of them knows yet. They find out later."

Junie looked from one of them to the other with the unnerving directness of children, as though checking whether the adults knew they were talking about something else entirely. Then she lost interest and demanded to make a cut herself.

He guided her through it—his hand over hers on the knife, the blade moving in increments, her breath held so hard he could feel it in his own chest—and when her tongue-cut finally opened clean, when the two green faces met and locked, she made a sound low in her throat that was almost religious.

"Wax," Silas said, and dipped the joint, sealing it, the warm paraffin running amber over the seam. "Now it's one tree. It doesn't know it yet. But it is."

They worked down row eleven like that, the three of them, and the morning unspooled. Junie lasted until nearly noon before her attention frayed and she decamped to hunt frogs along the irrigation ditch, knife confiscated and solemnly surrendered. The wax pot ticked as it cooled. Bees had found the first blossoms on the keeper apples and the air around them hummed like a struck string.

It was somewhere in the middle of row twelve that Silas realized Ivo had stopped working and was watching him.

Not watching the grafting. Watching *him*—the set of his shoulders as he braced a rootstock, the way he talked to the trees under his breath, a habit he hadn't known he'd kept. There was a smudge of wax on her wrist and dirt on her jaw and her hair was coming loose from its tie, and she was looking at him the way people look at a house they are deciding to live in.

"What," he said.

"Nothing." She picked up another scion. Cut. Locked. Dipped. "My grandmother used to say my grandfather hummed when he grafted. Drove her crazy. Said you could tell how the season would go by whether the humming was in tune." A pause. The knife moved. "You hum."

"Do I?"

"Flat," she said, and the corner of her mouth betrayed her. "Consistently, historically flat."

"My mother used to say the same thing." He braced the next rootstock and didn't look at her, because looking at her right then would have undone him. "She said it was genetic. She said the Marchetti men sang flat and loved hard and died stubborn, and that any woman who married into the family should know all three going in."

The knife paused. When he glanced over, Ivo was looking at the row ahead of them—at the finished trees, the interleaved pattern marching down toward the creek, his family's variety and hers alternating like verses—and her expression had gone somewhere private and far away.

"She'd have liked this," she said. "Both of them. They'd have stood right here and argued about spacing and pretended they weren't happy."

"They were very good at pretending," Silas said. "We come by it honestly."

"Yes," Ivo said. "Well. We're getting worse at it."

* * *

Afternoon came on soft and long, the light turning to honey in the west, and they finished row thirteen with the unhurried rhythm of people who have stopped counting hours. Silas worked the far end, Ivo the near, and between them the new trees went into the ground one by one—pippin, keeper, pippin, keeper—the old sentence continuing, clause by clause, in soil that had waited fifteen years to hear the rest of it.

His knees ached. His hands smelled of sap and wax. There was a blister opening on his palm in exactly the place his father's hands had been calloused smooth as saddle leather, and he found he was glad of it, glad of the pain's specificity, its lineage.

At some point Junie reappeared, frogless, and installed herself beside Ivo to help tamp soil, and Silas stepped back to fetch water and looked down the row at the two of them—his girl, he caught himself thinking, and did not correct the thought, because correcting it would have been a lie—their heads bent together over the same small tree, Ivo explaining something with her hands, Junie nodding with tremendous seriousness, both of them lit gold at the edges.

Fifteen years ago he had stood in a depot with a duffel bag and a head full of shame and believed, with the total conviction available only to the young and the guilty, that everything behind him was finished. That leaving was the same as ending. That the orchard would go on without him the way a sentence goes on after a word is crossed out—legible, diminished, complete enough.

Standing in the long light with sap on his hands, he understood at last how wrong he'd had it. Nothing here had ended. It had waited. The trees had waited. The songbook had waited. Two old women had spent years writing to each other in margins and codes because they refused, absolutely refused, to let the sentence die—and when they ran out of years, they had bound the pen into two other hands and trusted the ground to do the rest.

He carried the water bucket back down the row. Ivo took a drink and passed it to Junie, and for a moment the three of them stood in the middle of the unfinished pattern, and nobody said anything, and nothing needed saying.

"Uncle Silas," Junie said, wiping her mouth. "When these trees grow apples. Whose apples will they be? The pippin ones or the keeper ones?"

"Both," he said.

"But it's one tree."

"One tree," Ivo agreed. "Both apples. That's the whole trick, June. Nobody has to stop being what they are for the tree to hold together. It just grows both."

Junie considered this with the gravity of a supreme court, then shrugged with the magnificent pragmatism of nine and asked if she could name one. They let her name the first finished tree on row thirteen. She named it Gerald.

"Gerald," Silas repeated gravely, shaking its—her—small whip-thin trunk. "Pleased to meet you."

"You have to mean it," Junie warned.

"I mean it."

* * *

They stayed until the light failed. Waxing the last joints by lantern, packing the tools, wrapping the leftover scions back in damp burlap for tomorrow's rows. Fourteen was half done; they'd finish it Sunday, and then the pattern would be whole, the sentence complete, the work of two dead women carried at last across the finish line by the two children they'd wagered on.

Walking Junie up to the gate to wait for Marcus's headlights, Ivo fell into step beside Silas, and their hands found each other without negotiation, the way the grafts had, cambium to cambium.

"She's good with the knife," he said.

"She's good because you're good at teaching." Ivo squeezed his fingers. "My grandmother would have—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I keep catching myself building things. Little futures. Junie learning the whole orchard. Us growing old enough to argue about spacing. I keep doing it and then flinching, like the futures are breakable if I look at them straight."

"Don't flinch," Silas said.

"Easy for you to say. You've had practice at impossible optimism. You came back to a hostile orchard with a duffel bag."

"And a lawyer's letter," he said. "Don't forget the letter. Very intimidating."

She laughed—the real one, the one Priya had once claimed was rarer than rain in August—and the sound went out over the young trees and came back changed, the way sounds do among growing things.

At the gate, Marcus's headlights swung up the lane, and Junie launched herself at them both, hugging Ivo's waist and then, decisively, Silas's legs, and announced that she was coming back Sunday and she was naming a tree on every row and one of them was going to be named Gerald Junior and they couldn't stop her. Then she was gone in a rattle of tailgate and taillights, and the gate stood closed, and the orchard settled into dusk around them like a held breath released.

They walked back toward the house along row thirteen, past Gerald, past the small sealed wounds healing into strength, and it was Ivo who stopped, halfway down, and turned.

"The songbook," she said.

He'd known it was coming. He'd felt it in her all evening, a question gathering like weather.

"There are empty pages at the back," she said. "Years of them. My grandmother filled every margin, every scrap, and then just—stopped. Fifteen years of blank paper." Her thumb moved over his knuckles, back and forth, an unconscious rhythm. "I've been thinking about that all day. About how a book like that isn't really finished. It's just... waiting for whoever inherits it to pick up the pen."

Silas looked down the row at the new trees, ghost-pale in the last light, both families' blood rising in the same stems.

"Tomorrow," he said. "Bring it out to the bench. We'll start with row fourteen."

Her smile in the dark was the brightest thing in the county.
