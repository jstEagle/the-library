## 21. The Songbook Completed

The songbook had lived in the kitchen drawer for as long as Ivo could remember—the one that stuck in damp weather, the one her grandmother opened with a hip while her hands were full of flour or apples or somebody's mending. It was a fat, water-swollen thing, its cover once blue and now the color of a winter sky at four in the afternoon, its spine held together with surgical tape that had yellowed into something like amber. Her grandmother had called it *the ledger*, which Ivo had always taken for a joke until the year she understood it wasn't.

Now it lay open on the kitchen table between a bowl of Cortlands going soft and a stack of restructured loan papers, and Ivo was reading it the way you read a letter you already know by heart—slowly, letting the eyes rest where they wanted to.

Her grandmother's hand filled most of it. That looping, confident script, slanting rightward like wind through a row of trees. Song titles copied out in ink gone brown: *Shenandoah. The Water Is Wide. Red Apple Rag.* Chord diagrams sketched in pencil in the margins, corrected over years, some crossed out with such violence the paper had thinned to lace. And then, interleaved among them, another hand—smaller, more careful, the letters standing upright like fence posts. Silas's mother, Ruthie Marchetti, writing back.

Ivo turned pages slowly. She knew what came later now; she'd earned the knowing the hard way, through margin notes decoded and lies excavated and a developer's forged ledger spread across this same table under a lawyer's lamp. But knowing the ending hadn't dulled the middle. Here was a verse of *Barbara Allen* with Ruthie's note: *Too mournful for October. Save it for when we need a good cry.* Here was her grandmother's reply, penciled beneath: *We always need a good cry. That's why God invented cider.*

And here—page 114, near the end, where the entries stopped mid-autumn fifteen years ago—a song neither woman had finished. Just a title in her grandmother's hand, *The Grafting Line*, and beneath it two lines of lyric and then nothing. A blank that went on for forty pages. Forty blank pages, paper still clean, waiting the way an orchard waits in winter: not dead, only paused.

"You're doing the thing again," Silas said from the doorway.

Ivo didn't look up. "What thing."

"The thing where you hold the book like it's going to testify." He came in carrying two mugs and the smell of cold air and woodsmoke, and set one down beside her elbow without asking what she wanted, because by now he knew—black tea, too strong, honey stirred in against her stated preference. "Lawyer call?"

"An hour ago." She tapped the top sheet of the stack. "Restructured. Five-year term, interest-only for the first eighteen months. Marta got them down from their opening number by threatening to make them read the forged ledger out loud in open court." She allowed herself the smallest smile. "It's not mercy. It's arithmetic. They'd rather be paid slowly than lose publicly."

"Lean but alive," Silas said.

"That's the family motto now, apparently."

He pulled out the chair across from her—the chair that had been her grandmother's, that he had started sitting in sometime around the first frost without either of them discussing it—and turned the songbook so it faced him. His finger traced the empty stretch of pages the way you'd check a fence line.

"Forty pages," he said. "She left them blank on purpose. You know that, right? Ruthie did the same thing in her recipe book. Last third, nothing but ruled lines. Like they were saving seats."

"For who?"

"For us, probably. Or for whoever came after. Those two never did anything without a reason." He said it without bitterness now, which was new; the bitterness had been burned off somewhere between the platform scene and the filing cabinet full of evidence, and what remained was something quieter and harder to argue with. "My mother used to say an inheritance isn't what you're given. It's what you add."

Ivo looked at him. Fifteen years of silence, one autumn of truth, and he could still quote his mother like she'd said it that morning. "You believe that?"

"I'm living in your bunkhouse eating your eggs. I'd better."

* * *

They started writing in it three days later, though neither would admit afterward who began.

Ivo claimed it was her. There was a tune she'd been humming since harvest, something half-remembered from the radio in her grandmother's truck, and one gray morning with the rain coming down like a decision finally made, she sat at the kitchen table, opened the songbook to page 115, and wrote at the top in pencil—pencil, because ink felt like a vow and she wasn't ready for vows:

*Tune unknown. Four bars. Ask S. if he knows it.*

Then she hummed it into her phone, left the phone propped against the sugar bowl, and went out to prune like a coward.

When she came back at dusk, frozen to the bone and pleased about it, there was an answer waiting. His handwriting was worse than she remembered—slanted, hurried, the letters of a man who thought faster than he wrote:

*Don't know it. Sounds like something off a late-night country station, circa our childhood. Try: da—da-da—DUM. Also, you were flat on the third note.*

*I was not flat.*

*You were flat. I have perfect pitch and fifteen years of grievances. One of those is useful.*

She laughed alone in the kitchen, which was itself remarkable—Priya had a whole theory about Ivo's laughter being seasonal, migratory, nearly extinct—and wrote back: *Perfect pitch is a myth made up by people who want credit for humming.*

And that was how it went. The songbook became a correspondence conducted at the speed of chores. She'd leave a question about a chord progression before milking; he'd answer by lantern light after fixing the pump. He transcribed a fiddle tune his father used to play, noting underneath, *He learned it in prison, if you want the provenance. It's better than it has any right to be.* She copied out the two unfinished lines of *The Grafting Line* and added a third, then crossed it out, then wrote a different third, then left both and wrote *S., pick one,* and he picked neither and wrote a fourth, and she hated it, and kept it.

Arguments migrated into the book because arguments belonged there. Whether the north slope should go back to cider apples or stay in eaters (two pages, escalating margins, settled by a soil test that proved them both wrong). Whether the bunkhouse roof could survive another winter (his position) or should be replaced before it killed someone (hers), resolved when a branch came through it in January and settled the matter with authority. Whether her grandmother had been a meddler or a visionary—Ivo argued meddler, Silas argued visionary, and the argument ended, as their best ones did, with both of them right.

*She engineered us,* Ivo wrote one night, after the second pot of tea.

*She exploited us,* he wrote back. *Engineering implies competence. We nearly ruined it twice.*

*Nearly counts in grafting. Ask your father.*

A long pause before his answer, the graphite pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages: *Ask my father. Yeah. Someday maybe.* And beneath it, smaller: *Thank you for not making me say more tonight.*

*Ivo's rule of the orchard,* she wrote. *Nothing gets said before its season. Same as pruning.*

Underneath, in his terrible hand: *That's the first sensible thing anyone's written in this book since 1998.*

* * *

The debts, meanwhile, did what debts do when you stop feeding them shame: they shrank.

Marta Okafor worked through the winter like a woman defusing ordnance. The creditor who'd been circling hardest—a factoring company that had bought the orchard's paper at a discount and smelled blood—took the restructuring rather than face the fraud claim. The bank, embarrassed by the ledger evidence Marta had quietly shared with its own compliance department, agreed to terms that would have been unthinkable in October. Interest-only through next summer. Principal amortized over seven years. Not generous. Survivable. There was a difference, and Ivo had learned to live inside it.

"We'll be lean," Marta told them, snapping her pen case shut in the kitchen where all the important business of Hollow Creek apparently happened. "Lean means no new equipment, no expansion, no borrowing against next year's crop. Lean means every dollar has a job. You'll feel it."

"We've felt worse," Silas said.

"You've felt worse," Marta agreed, gathering her coat. "That's the only reason I took this case. People who've felt worse don't flinch at lean." At the door she paused, looking back at the two of them standing shoulder to shoulder at the kitchen table like a pair of defendants who'd somehow become partners. "One piece of free advice. Whatever you two are building—it's not just the lawsuit keeping the bank polite. Banks can smell a partnership. Don't give them a reason to smell anything else."

After the door closed, Silas said, "Was that a threat or a blessing?"

"With lawyers it's usually both," Ivo said, and wrote it in the songbook.

* * *

February came in hard, a week of ice that glazed every branch until the whole orchard rang faintly in the wind, a thousand small bells tuned to no key anyone could find. They worked through it anyway, because ice storms don't respect restructuring schedules—knocking down the limbs that were already compromised, cracking two of the old standards on the south row, burying the low trellis wire under a sleeve of glass.

They salvaged what they could. Silas went up the ladder with a pole saw while Ivo braced the base and called corrections, and the work had the wordless economy of a thing practiced past memory—her hands steadying, his blade falling, the cut limb caught between them before it hit the ground. Neither spoke except in numbers and directions. Neither needed to.

That evening, thawing out in the kitchen, Ivo found he'd written in the book again. Not a song. A list, headed *Spring Orders — Draft 1*, rootstock varieties in his cramped hand, quantities penciled and repenciled, and at the bottom, set apart:

*Grafting stock: M111 for the north block. Scion wood from the old dual-variety trees—your grandmother's pattern, continued. If we're doing this, we do it her way.*

Below it, in fresh ink, a line that stopped her breath for a moment:

*Also: teach me the songs I missed. All of them. Start with whatever you hummed in October. You weren't flat, by the way. I lied. It was the best thing I heard all year.*

Ivo sat with the book open on her knees for a long time while the kettle climbed toward boiling and the ice ticked against the windows like patient fingers. This was the inheritance, she thought. Not the deed with its two names, not the debt with its arithmetic, not even the trees themselves. It was this—a blank page, kept blank for twenty years by two women who believed in the future more than they believed in each other, and now filling up, entry by entry, argument by argument, with the handwriting of two people who had almost thrown it all away.

Her grandmother had written *the ledger* and meant it. Every song a debt. Every margin note a payment. And the estate wasn't settled yet—not by a long season—but for the first time since the county clerk had refused her loan application on a foggy October morning, Ivo understood that settling it was not the point. Adding to it was.

She sharpened a pencil, turned to the next clean page, and wrote:

*March orders, final. And a request: when the scions take—if they take—we plant one tree for Ruthie and one for Grandma, side by side, at the head of the north row. Dual varieties. Their names on the tags. Let the town talk.*

She carried the book out to the bunkhouse that night herself, through the last of the ice, and left it on his cot, open to her page.

By morning there was an answer in the margin, in ink this time, no erasures, no hesitation:

*Let them talk. We'll give them a song to talk about.*

And below that, dated, signed with both their initials the way the old entries had been signed by two women who never stopped corresponding even when the whole valley said they'd stopped speaking—

*I.M. & S.M., February. To be continued.*

The kettle whistled in the kitchen. Spring was six weeks out, the nursery catalog was on the table, and page 155 was still blank—waiting, the way everything worth having waits, for what they would write next.
