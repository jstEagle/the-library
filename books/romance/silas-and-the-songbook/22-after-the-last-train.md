## 22. After the Last Train

*Ivo*

The morning of the wedding, I woke before the birds and lay in the dark listening to the orchard breathe.

That's a thing I'd learned trees do—not breathe exactly, but something like it. The leaves taking in the cool of the night and letting it go again as the sun came up over Hollow Creek ridge, the whole hillside exhaling mist. For five years I had slept through it. I'd kept my windows shut against the cold and the damp and the sound of anything alive, because a sealed room is a small life you can control. Now I slept with the window open even in March, and the first thing I knew every morning was the smell of wet bark and turned earth, and the second thing was usually Silas's side of the bed going cold as he got up to check the frost line.

But not this morning. This morning he was still asleep beside me, one arm flung over his eyes, his breathing slow and unguarded in a way he only allowed himself in the hour before dawn. In the gray light he looked younger than forty. He looked, some days, like the boy who used to dare me to climb the tallest tree in the south block and then catch me when I fell, which was often, because I never learned to trust the branches—only him.

I lay there and did the arithmetic I no longer needed to do. One year since the lawyer's office. One year since the pen hovering over the deed, since the fine print that read like a love letter written in legalese: *joint condition, revocable only by mutual consent.* One year since we filed the countersuit with evidence we could barely afford to copy, let alone litigate, and the town—the same town that had picked sides fifteen years ago and let two families rot in the silence—started showing up at the gate with tractors and casseroles and, in Mrs. Okafor's case, an actual subpoena for the developer's accountant, because she'd been the county bookkeeper for thirty years and remembered every forged entry the first time around.

One year. And today there would be forty chairs under the grafted trees.

I slipped out of bed without waking him. The floorboards in the hallway still announced me—I'd stopped minding—and the kitchen smelled of the bread Priya had bullied me into starting the night before, dough proofing under a cloth like something patient and alive. On the wall above the table hung the frame Silas had made from a split grafting stave, and inside it, the deed. Both names. *Ivo Zhou. Silas Marchetti.* But inked over both, in the same hand—his hand, done the night we signed the refusal letter, so that no future clerk or creditor or coward could ever separate them again—a single line connecting them into one continuous stroke, like a branch grafted to rootstock. Underneath, in small letters: *one ground.*

My grandmother's handwriting was nowhere on it, but her fingerprints were everywhere. That was the thing about inheritance nobody tells you: it isn't what you receive. It's what you add. She had bound us together with a deed and a songbook and fifteen years of coded margin notes, and she had been right, and I had spent most of that year furious at her for being right, and now I mostly just missed her. Grief, it turns out, doesn't leave when joy arrives. It just moves to a quieter room and visits more politely.

I took the bread out, scored it, put it back. Outside, the fog was doing its October trick, pooling in the low rows until the trees stood waist-deep in cloud. The wedding wasn't until four. There were crates to move, chairs to set up, a sound system Priya had promised would "mostly work." There was a whole day of visible, public, witnessed happiness ahead of me, and the old Ivo—the one who caught the last train, who kept her name off things, who had built a life the size of a sealed room—that woman would have found a fever to develop.

I put the kettle on instead.

* * *

*Silas*

She didn't know I was awake when she left the bed. I never told her how long I lay there listening to her mornings—the kettle, the boards, the little pause she always took at the window before touching the curtain, like she was asking the day's permission. Fifteen years away taught me to memorize people fast, because you never know which memory will have to last. Thirty-odd years of knowing Ivo Zhou taught me the better lesson: pay attention slowly, because if you're lucky, the memory keeps getting longer.

I got up when the fog started burning off and found her at the kitchen table with the songbook open, which is where she went when she thought no one was watching. Our grandmothers' songbook. Two hands in the margins, arguing across decades about tempo and pruning and whether the Henderson boy was worth either of their daughters' time (he was not; they'd underlined it twice, independently). And now a third hand, and a fourth. Songs we'd added through the winter. Notes in pencil. A recipe for cider that Silas senior would have called criminal and then drunk all of. Last week Ivo had taped in a photograph of the harvest crew, and beneath it written: *year one of the rest.*

"You're supposed to be nervous," I said. "It's traditional."

"I'm supervising bread." She didn't look up, but her mouth did the thing it did when she was pleased and refusing to admit it. "Nervousness is inefficient."

"Your grandmother wrote in the margins that you'd say that."

"She did not."

"She wrote that you were 'a girl who counts her steps so she can pretend she isn't dancing.'" I sat down across from her. "Page sixty-one. You've read it forty times."

The fog outside was lifting off the rows, and the light came through the window the way it does in late October, low and gold and forgiving, the light photographers chase and orchards earn. Through it I could see the south block, where the grafted trees stood—her grandmother's varieties on my father's rootstock, and ours now too, the new stock we'd planted in spring with Ivo's niece solemnly holding the knife wrong and then, by the third tree, holding it right. Those trees were the reason for the chairs. Those trees were the reason for everything, if I was honest, though the lawyers preferred to talk about the ledger and the injunction and the settlement talks dragging on in the background like a radio left on in another room.

The developer had settled in August. Not gracefully—they'd dressed it up as a withdrawal "pending review"—but Mrs. Okafor's subpoena and the forged-ledger trail had done what truth usually does eventually, which is arrive. The debts were restructured. The orchard was lean but alive. We were, by any honest accounting, still poor.

I reached across the table and closed the songbook gently over her hand.

"Hey," I said. "Whatever happens today. Forty chairs, rain, Priya's speaker cutting out during the vows. It's already the best year I've ever had. Everything else is garnish."

She looked at me then, fully, the way she'd learned to do—staying visible, staying in the room, letting herself be seen looking—and said, "You're going to make me cry before I've even frosted the cake, and I want it noted that I resent that."

"Noted," I said, and kissed her knuckles, and went out to move chairs.

* * *

*Ivo*

They came from everywhere, which is to say from ten miles in every direction, which in Hollow Creek is everywhere.

Priya arrived at noon with her sister and a garment bag and the energy of a woman who had waited five years to officiate a wedding and had prepared remarks accordingly ("short," she'd promised, and I believed her the way I believe weather forecasts). The Okafor brothers came with folding tables and a barrel of last year's cider that Silas had pressed on the first frost night, the confession cider, though only the two of us knew that. Old Tomás from the co-op brought a fiddle. My sister came with the niece, who was seven now and had appointed herself flower guardian with the gravity of a supreme court justice, distributing crabapples from a basket because I'd told her they were "orchard roses" and she had decided to improve on tradition.

And people from town. That was the part I hadn't braced for. People whose families had chosen sides in a feud that turned out to be half manufactured, half grief, and entirely none of our making. They came anyway. They shook Silas's hand. One of them—Mr. Aldous, whose father had led the charge against the Marchettis back then—took Silas aside by the fence line, and whatever was said lasted four minutes and ended with both men gripping forearms, and Silas came back to me with his eyes bright and said only, "Later. Tell you later," and I let it be later, because I was learning that not every mystery needs solving the moment it appears.

We held it under the oldest grafted tree, the one her grandmother had cut as a double—two varieties sharing one trunk, apples that ripened red on one side and gold on the other, so that from a distance the tree looked undecided and up close looked like a miracle. Forty chairs. Fog burning off. Fiddle tuning. The niece losing her grip on the basket and crabapples rolling down the aisle between the chairs like small determined guests finding their seats.

Priya's remarks were, in fact, short. Mostly. She talked about the year I'd laughed more than in the previous five, which mortified me completely and satisfied her visibly. Then she said the part I'll keep:

"Ivo spent years building a life you couldn't see from the road. And I want to say, for the record, that the bravest thing she ever did wasn't fighting the lawsuit or refusing the money. It was letting herself be visible. Staying in the room. Because staying hidden is safe, but nothing grows in the dark except mushrooms, and honestly, even those are reaching for something."

There was laughter. There was, I'll admit, crying—mine, brief and furious, dabbed at with a handkerchief Silas produced from his pocket because of course he'd anticipated me, he always had, since we were children passing knives and secrets under the same trees.

We wrote our own vows. His were quiet and specific: about trains, about coming back, about learning that waiting can be its own kind of faithfulness. Mine were shorter, because I'd rewritten them eleven times and finally stripped them down to the load-bearing wall:

*"I spent my whole life catching the last train out. I'm staying. That's the whole vow. I'm staying, and I'm staying visible, and I choose you in daylight, in front of everyone, on purpose."*

He kissed me under the undecided miracle tree while the fiddle struck up and the crabapple girl applauded with the basket over her head, spilling the remaining fruit on the groom, which everyone agreed was the best omen available.

* * *

*Silas*

Evening came down soft, the way autumn evenings do after a warm day, gold going to copper going to blue. The chairs emptied by degrees, the party migrating to the barn where Tomás's fiddle had been joined by two guitars and Mrs. Okafor, who danced the way she did bookkeeping—precisely, relentlessly, and with terrifying authority.

I found Ivo at the fence line past the south block, away from the music, standing where the ground dropped toward the valley and the rail line ran along the river below.

She heard me come and didn't turn. "You'd think," she said, "after everything, I'd have outgrown checking the train schedule."

"You don't check it anymore."

"I know the times anyway. Eight-forty-two and nine-seventeen. Some things live in the body." She leaned back into me, her shoulder blades against my chest, her head tipping to my shoulder. "Fifteen years ago I stood right here and watched the last train go and told myself it was fine. Told myself leaving was just what people did, and the smart thing was to build a life small enough that nothing could take it from me."

"And now?"

"Now I look at it and feel—" she searched, "—nothing. Not nothing cruel. Nothing like a scar that stopped hurting. It's just a train."

Down in the valley, right on schedule, the nine-seventeen came through: three cars and a caboose-less tail, headlights sweeping the river bend, the sound arriving a beat late across the water, that long low note the rails sing that I'd once thought was the loneliest sound in the world.

Neither of us looked up.

We watched the orchard instead—the dark rows, the new plantings staked and tied, the old grafted tree a black shape against the last copper edge of sky, heavy next summer with fruit that would be half hers and half mine and all of it one tree's. Somewhere behind us the fiddle changed keys. The niece shrieked with laughter at something. The lights of the barn threw our shadow long across the grass, two figures grown into one outline, the way roots grow together underground where nobody thinks to look.

"One ground," she said quietly, quoting the deed, quoting us.

"One ground," I said.

The train's sound faded down the valley toward towns we'd never see tonight, carrying whoever was leaving toward whatever they were leaving toward. We stood in the dark of our own orchard, on our own dirt, with our names inked over each other's in the same hand, and neither of us turned to watch it go.

There was cake getting eaten without us, and a fiddle that wouldn't quit, and a whole lean, stubborn, hard-won life waiting in the morning—pruning season coming, the lawsuit's last paperwork, spring grafting after that, years of them, God willing, apples and arguments and songs not yet written in the empty pages.

Ivo laced her fingers through mine.

"Come on," she said. "They'll dance without us, but they won't eat the top tier without us, and Priya will consider that a war crime."

We walked back toward the light together, and behind us the last train went on alone into the dark, and the orchard stayed.
