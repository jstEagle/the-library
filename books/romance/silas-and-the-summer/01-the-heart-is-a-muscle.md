## 1. The Heart Is a Muscle

The heart is a muscle, my mother used to say, and muscles only atrophy when you stop using them. She said this while smoking on the back porch of a house we were about to lose, so I have always taken her cardiac advice with the appropriate grain of salt.

Still, the phrase has stayed with me the way her other lessons did — how to stretch a grocery budget across the last week of the month, how to smile at a landlord who's lying to you, how to cry in the shower where the water does the work for you. My mother believed in using things until they broke. I believe in counting them before they can.

Which is why, on the first Tuesday of June, I was standing in the middle of Nakamura & Daughter Books at 6:47 in the morning with a clipboard, a cup of coffee going cold in my hand, and a system.

The system is mine. I invented it over ten years and three ledgers, and it is beautiful. Every title in the shop has a place; every place has a number; every number lives in a spreadsheet that Juniper calls "the spreadsheet of doom" and I call *asset management*. When you know exactly what you have, nobody can take it from you without your noticing. This is not paranoia. This is arithmetic. There is a difference, and the difference is that arithmetic has never once broken my heart.

I started at the front table, because you always start at the front table. New releases, face-out, arranged by the color logic Juniper insists sells better than alphabetical order. She's probably right. She's usually right about selling things, which is an uncomfortable trait in a fourteen-year-old and one I blame entirely on her father's side, whoever and wherever he is — a question Juniper stopped asking around age nine, when she decided the answer was boring and replaced it with a better story involving a lighthouse keeper. I did not correct her. Some fictions are load-bearing.

"Mom." Her voice came down the stairs from our apartment above the shop, thick with sleep and accusation. "It's summer vacation."

"It's 6:50," I said. "Summer vacation doesn't start until seven."

"That's not a real rule."

"It's in the ledger."

"You don't have a ledger for wake-up times."

"I could make one." I heard her groan all the way through the floorboards, and I smiled at the poetry section, which didn't deserve it. Poets get enough unearned attention as it is.

She came down ten minutes later in yesterday's hoodie, hair like a crow that had lost an argument with a windstorm, and stood beside me at the front table surveying my inventory like a small disapproving manager.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The counting thing. You do it when something's wrong or when nothing's wrong, and I genuinely cannot tell the difference anymore."

"Nothing's wrong," I said. "Everything is precisely right. Look at this." I gestured at the table with my clipboard. "Forty-two new titles, all logged, all priced, all shelved by nine if you help me, which you will, because you're the Daughter half of this operation and the ampersand implies obligations."

"The ampersand implies love," Juniper said. "Obligations are extra."

"Then consider it love with a time card."

She rolled her eyes but she took the second clipboard — the junior one, the one I bought her when she was eight and insisted on having her own — and started on the children's section, calling out numbers in a voice gone flat with teenage boredom but accurate to the digit. We worked like that for an hour, the two of us, in the particular silence of people who know each other's rhythms too well to need words. The morning light came through the front windows in that early-summer way, all gold and generous, catching the dust motes above the fiction shelves like the shop was full of tiny floating miracles instead of dead skin and paper fibers.

I love this shop. I want to be clear about that, even to myself, especially to myself. I love it the way you love a thing you built out of wreckage: fiercely, specifically, and with a running tally of what it cost. Ten years ago I had a baby, no husband, a degree in literature that qualified me for approximately nothing, and a settlement check that wouldn't cover six months of rent anywhere with a zip code. What I had instead was my grandmother's lease on this storefront — a narrow slot between a vacant lot and a laundromat on Alder Street — and a stubbornness my ex-husband once described, in the last fight, as "weaponized bookkeeping."

He meant it as an insult. I had it framed in my head for years.

* * *

By nine the shop was open, the door propped with the brass whale doorstop Juniper made in third grade (it is a terrible doorstop and an excellent whale), and Mrs. Okafor arrived for her standing Tuesday browse, which is less a browse than a scheduled visitation. She is seventy-one, reads four books a week, and treats my recommendations table like a personal challenge.

"Mara," she said, in the tone of a woman about to ruin my morning. "You look tired."

"I look efficient."

"You look like a woman who slept next to her ledger again."

"I have never once slept next to a ledger."

"Because you sleep *on* it. I've seen the crease marks." She pulled a paperback off the new table and examined it with suspicion. "What's this one about?"

"A woman who inherits a vineyard and learns to live again."

"Pass. Give me the murder one. The one with the librarian."

"There are three librarian murders this month. It's a whole trend."

"Good. Librarians have it coming." She tucked it under her arm and patted my cheek with a hand that smelled like lavender and judgment. "And eat something today that isn't consumed standing up over a spreadsheet. You're forty-one, not forty-one going on ninety."

She swept out toward the laundromat, presumably to tell Rosa the same thing in a different font, and I stood there holding my clipboard and feeling, absurdly, seen. That's the trouble with a neighborhood. Everyone knows your patterns. In a city you can be a mystery; on Alder Street you're a character, and characters don't get to change without everyone commenting on the revision.

Juniper appeared at my elbow, restocking bookmarks. "Mrs. Okafor's right. You do the standing-over-the-spreadsheet eating thing."

"I'm building character."

"You're building scoliosis." She snapped the bookmark rack shut. "Also, question. Hypothetical."

"Hypothetical questions from teenagers are never hypothetical."

"If you weren't running the shop. If money wasn't a thing. If you could do anything." She looked at me with those serious dark eyes, my eyes, my grandmother's eyes, three generations of women who learned to count before they learned to trust. "What would you do?"

I opened my mouth to say something sensible — travel, maybe, or sleep, the two answers adults give when they've forgotten how to want things — and found, to my genuine surprise, that I didn't have an answer. Not a real one. I had systems. I had routines. I had a Tuesday schedule worn smooth as river stone. But *anything*? The word sat in me like a key looking for its lock, and after a moment of honest searching I came up empty, and I covered it the way I cover everything.

"I'd buy better coffee for the break room," I said.

"We don't have a break room."

"Exactly. First purchase."

Juniper laughed, but she kept looking at me a beat too long, the way she does when she's filing something away, and I turned to the register to hide behind the till. Fourteen-year-olds should not be able to see through their mothers. It violates some natural order. And yet there she was, seeing, and there I was, seen, and the hairline crack ran right down the middle of my well-managed morning, thin as a pencil line, invisible unless you knew where to look.

* * *

The afternoon brought the usual tide: tourists off the highway wanting maps we don't stock, a man who comes in every few weeks to read entire magazines in the armchair and buy nothing (I let him; his name is Doug and he once talked a drunk driver out of his car keys, so he's earned the chair), and Delia Chen from the flower cart next to the laundromat, who came in ostensibly to buy a novel and actually to deliver gossip wrapped in peonies.

"So," Delia said, arranging the flowers in the vase I keep for exactly this purpose. "Have you heard anything about the Hollis place?"

The Hollis place. The vacant storefront on my other side, empty for two years since the tax office moved out, its windows papered over with a realty sign that had faded from optimistic yellow to the color of surrender. I'd gotten so used to it that I'd stopped seeing it, the way you stop seeing a scar.

"Heard what? Finally rented?"

"Sold, I think. Or leased. There were men measuring the windows yesterday. Big truck." Delia lowered her voice as though the flowers might repeat her. "Rosa says she saw the paperwork on the door and the name on it made her spit out her coffee."

"Rosa spits out her coffee at most paperwork. She spat at her own water bill."

"This wasn't a water bill." Delia straightened up, satisfied with the arrangement, and looked at me with the frank pity of a woman about to hand over bad news she's been carrying around all day like a hot dish. "Wren," she said. "The sign said Wren."

The word landed in the shop and just — sat there. Wren. As in Wren & Sons Books. As in the eleven-store chain that opened a flagship forty minutes away in 2019 and spent eighteen months discounting bestsellers below cost until three independent shops on this side of the county folded, mine included, the first time. As in the reason I spent two years rebuilding from a kiosk table and a folding chair into the shop that now stood around me, shelf by numbered shelf, my whole careful kingdom.

As in the family whose youngest son, at twenty-three, in a borrowed cabin during a snowstorm that shut down the whole valley, taught me everything I have spent a decade carefully forgetting.

"That's impossible," I said. My voice came out level. I was almost proud of it. "They'd never open here. There's no foot traffic worth their margin. They're a volume operation."

"Maybe they're downsizing." Delia shrugged. "Maybe it's a distribution thing. Maybe Rosa misread it." She gathered her wrapping paper and headed for the door, then paused. "You okay, Mara? You've got a look."

"I have many looks. They're all inventory."

She laughed and left, the bell over the door doing its small bright job, and I stood alone among my counted books and felt the crack widen by a millimeter, hairline still, but running.

* * *

That night I closed the shop at six, climbed the stairs, and burned the garlic because I was thinking about something else, which never happens, which is itself evidence of something wrong. Juniper ate the garlic anyway, because she is loyal and also because fourteen-year-olds will eat anything with pasta attached.

"You're quiet," she said.

"I'm conserving energy."

"For what? You go to bed at ten like a Victorian."

"Victorians had excellent skin." I pushed a piece of zucchini around my plate. "Hey. Random question. If someone from — say, a company that hurt us before — moved in next door. Hypothetically. What would you think?"

Juniper considered this with her mouth full. "I'd think they were either stupid or sorry."

"Those aren't the only options."

"They're the only interesting ones." She pointed her fork at me. "Why? Who's moving in?"

"Nobody. Hypothetical nobody."

"Mom." She set the fork down, and I braced myself, because I know that tone; I invented that tone; I used it on my own mother across a kitchen table very much like this one. "You asked. So it's somebody. And you did the voice."

"What voice?"

"The customer service voice. The one you use when you don't want me to worry." She gathered her plate and stood, and on her way past she dropped a kiss on the top of my head, quick and fierce, the way she's done since she was small enough to reach only by climbing a chair. "For the record, I think you should date someone. Anyone. Mrs. Okafor agrees. We have a bet pool."

"You have a *bet pool*?"

"Doug's in it. Don't ask what he picked." She carried her plate to the sink. "Night, Mom."

I sat at the table after she went up, listening to the old pipes knock and settle, and I did what I always do when the ground shifts: I counted. Two hundred and eleven days until her school year starts. Four thousand and change titles downstairs, all logged. Eleven years of rent paid on time, every time. One daughter, healthy, sharp-tongued, asleep one floor above me. Zero debts I couldn't name. Zero men in the picture, per the terms of a policy I drafted at twenty-four and have never once amended, because amendments require admitting the original document was written in grief, and I prefer to think of it as good governance.

The heart is a muscle. Fine. But nobody tells you that a muscle you never use doesn't stay strong and waiting, patient as a held breath. It changes shape. It forgets its job. It gets stiff in the specific places where it used to move, and one day you reach for something — a hypothetical, a question from your daughter, a name on a piece of paper across the street — and you feel exactly how long it's been.

Outside, the June dusk went blue over Alder Street. Somewhere down the block, Delia was closing her flower cart, and Rosa was chasing the last dryer heat out of the laundromat, and the Hollis place stood dark behind its papered windows, keeping whatever it was keeping.

Tomorrow, I told myself, I would walk past it. Tomorrow I would look at the sign with my own eyes and confirm it was a mistake, a misprint, a coincidence of spelling. Tomorrow I would add it to the ledger under *external factors, monitored* and close the entry.

I went to bed at ten, like a Victorian, and lay awake until midnight doing sums that refused to come out even.

In the morning, the truck arrived at 7:15 — a big white box truck backing into the space in front of the Hollis place with a hydraulic sigh — and I was downstairs early, clipboard in hand, pretending to count a window display I had counted the night before. Through the glass, movers began unloading shelving. Good shelving. Expensive-looking. And printed on the side of the truck, in letters tall enough to read from my side of the street, in case there had been any doubt left to kill:

**WREN & SONS BOOKS — ALDER STREET BRANCH.**

The passenger door opened. A man stepped out, and even at thirty feet, even across ten years, even before he turned around, I knew him by the way he stood — weight on one heel, head tipped back to read the street like a page he'd been meaning to finish.

Silas Wren put his hands in his pockets and looked directly at my window.

I did not drop the clipboard. I want credit for that.
