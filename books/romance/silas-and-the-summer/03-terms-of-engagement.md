## 3. Terms of Engagement

There are three universal truths about moving day, and I learned all of them before nine a.m. on the first Monday of June: the boxes are always heavier than the inventory list claims, the truck ramp is always steeper than physics should allow, and someone always brings a box labeled *miscellaneous* that turns out to contain nothing but extension cords and one ceramic owl of unknown provenance.

The owl was mine, technically. My father had won it at a sales conference raffle in 1998 and had been transferring it from office to office ever since like a cursed heirloom. I set it on the counter of the Wren & Sons branch — our branch, for the next few months, though "branch" was generous; it was more of a twig — and it stared at me with its painted eyes as if to say, *Well? This was your idea.*

It was not my idea. It was my father's idea, delivered over the phone in the tone he used for things that were not negotiable: quarterly projections, holiday staffing, and now this. "You'll go up to Fairweather's town," he'd said, and I'd heard the pause, the small deliberate hitch where he waited to see if I'd flinch. "Close out the branch. Six months, maybe less if you're efficient. You're good at winding things down, Silas. You have a talent for endings."

He hadn't meant it as a jab. That was the worst part. My father did not deal in jabs; he dealt in assessments, and his assessment of me had apparently calcified sometime around my twenty-fourth year into a single line item: *the son who leaves.*

I flinched anyway. He probably heard it.

So here I was, standing in a storefront that smelled like fresh paint and cardboard dust, watching two movers I'd hired off a flyer at the gas station carry in pallets of remaindered stock — the books nobody wanted, shipped here to be marked down until somebody finally took pity on them. The Wren & Sons playbook for a closing store, which I had written three revisions of myself, back when I still believed the playbook was neutral. A system. Numbers on a page.

Numbers on a page were easy. Numbers didn't look at you across a street with ten years of unfinished business stacked behind their eyes.

I'd seen her yesterday. Orsen Fairweather, standing in the doorway of her shop with a box of returns under one arm, going very still in the way people go still when they've spotted something with teeth. She'd recovered fast — faster than me, certainly — and given me a nod so crisp it could have been notarized. "Mr. Wren." Two syllables, professionally chilled. Ten years ago she'd said my name like it was something warm she was passing me hand to hand. Now it was a formality, a receipt.

*Mr. Wren.* Fine. I could work with that. I'd spent a decade getting very good at working with less.

The bell above the door — a new bell, cheap brass, nothing like the old shop bells I'd grown up with — rang out, and I turned expecting another delivery.

It was her.

Orsen stood in the doorway of my father's failing branch with a paper cup of coffee in each hand, wearing an expression I recognized from childhood visits to the dentist: polite, braced, prepared to be hurt by a stranger. Her hair was pinned up with what appeared to be two pencils, which was either carelessness or a statement, and knowing Orsen it was a statement. Everything about Orsen was a statement, even the things she thought were secrets.

"Mr. Wren," she said again, and held out one of the cups. "Peace offering. Or reconnaissance. I haven't decided."

I took the cup because refusing it would have been a second declaration of war, and we weren't there yet. We were somewhere earlier than war. We were in the tense diplomatic phase, the part with envoys and formal language and everyone pretending they weren't counting their artillery. "Reconnaissance," I said. "Definitely reconnaissance. Nobody makes peace offerings this early in a conflict. There are rules."

"There are," she agreed, and stepped inside without being invited, which was very much her way. Orsen had never once in her life waited to be invited anywhere. She surveyed the room — the bare shelves, the taped floor markings, the ceramic owl — with the flat professional gaze of a woman assessing a competitor's position. Her eyes snagged on the owl. "Yours?"

"My father's. It travels with the company. Like a mascot, if mascots were ominous."

"It's looking at me like it knows my net worth."

"That's its function."

Something happened at the corner of her mouth. Not a smile — Orsen did not smile at me, not yet, possibly not ever — but a twitch in the vicinity of one, quickly suppressed, like a bird landing and immediately reconsidering. She covered it with a sip of coffee and I understood, with a lurch, that she'd brought the coffee specifically so she'd have something to do with her face.

Which meant she was nervous too.

This information settled into me with a warmth I had no right to feel and did not attempt to return. I filed it away instead, next to the other things I knew about Orsen Fairweather that I had no business still knowing: that she bit the cap off pens rather than uncapping them, that she alphabetized by feel when she thought no one was watching, that she went quiet in the specific way of someone doing arithmetic when she was frightened.

She was doing arithmetic now. I could see it behind her eyes — the ledger, the margins, the threat assessment. Me, my boxes, my father's name on the lease next door. The chain that had opened four stores within driving distance of hers in the span of eighteen months, eight years ago, and priced its paperbacks like loss leaders until her quarterly numbers bled. She'd told me about it once, years later — no, she hadn't told me at all; I'd read about it in a trade newsletter, sitting in an airport, feeling the particular shame of a man reading about his own family's sins committed against a woman he'd abandoned in a snowstorm.

"Let's skip ahead," she said, setting her cup down on a shelf bracket with a decisive click. "You've got your speech prepared. I can tell. You've got the face men get when they've rehearsed sincerity in a mirror. So say it, and then I'll say my part, and we can both get back to work."

"I don't rehearse sincerity."

"No," she agreed pleasantly. "You just happen to produce it fluently under pressure. It's worse, honestly."

Fair. I gave her that one. I gave her most of them, historically, and it had never once gotten me anything except further into trouble.

"The branch is closing," I said. "That's the whole speech, actually. You've heard it. The Wren & Sons location on Marsh Street will cease operations by end of summer. I'm here to wind it down — sell through the stock, terminate the lease, settle the accounts. There's no expansion plan. There's no scheme. There's no secret warehouse of discounted bestsellers waiting to bury you. This store opens in order to close. That's it. That's the entire corporate strategy."

Orsen looked at me for a long moment. The morning light came through the plate glass and cut her shadow long across the bare floorboards, and I thought, absurdly, *she used to fall asleep in exactly that kind of light*, and then I thought, *stop*, and then I thought about how the human brain is just an organ that betrays you on schedule.

"You expect me to believe," she said slowly, "that Wren & Sons opened a location across the street from a thriving independent bookstore in order to close it."

"I expect you to believe that Wren & Sons made a bad real estate decision eight months ago and has spent every month since trying to figure out how to exit it with minimal losses. My father signed the lease before anyone ran the foot traffic numbers. He fell in love with the block. He does that. He falls in love with blocks and buildings and ceramic owls, and then the numbers come in and he hands the mess to whoever's nearest, and currently the nearest is me."

"And you said yes."

"I'm good at winding things down," I said, and heard my father's phrasing come out of my own mouth, and hated it.

Orsen picked up her coffee again. Drank. Set it down. All of it precise, all of it deliberate, a woman handling her own tells like evidence. "Here's my part," she said. "I believe maybe forty percent of what you just said. Possibly fifty. The part about your father falling in love with the block tracks — he did try to buy the old theater building in '09, and everyone told him the roof was gone, and he bought it anyway. So that's consistent. But the rest of it—" She shook her head. "Silas. Mr. Wren. Whatever we're doing with names. You don't open a store to close it. Stores open to close *other* stores. I've watched it happen. I've got the scars and the spreadsheets."

"The spreadsheets survived?"

"Barely. I keep them in a fireproof box with my daughter's birth certificate and my grandmother's recipes. Sentimental items."

I laughed before I could stop myself — a short, surprised sound that escaped like a prisoner — and her expression flickered, annoyed at herself for having caused it, annoyed at me for producing it. We were doing this thing already, the thing we'd always done, the thing where conversation became a game of tennis played across a net of genuine grievance, and both players kept forgetting to keep score.

"We could do terms," I said.

Her eyebrows went up. "Terms."

"A truce. Formal. While I'm here. I'm not competing with you because I literally cannot — my mandate is liquidation, not growth. But you don't have to believe my mandate. Believe my incentives. Every book I sell full price is a book I have to process, count, and ship back or destroy. Liquidation is tedious, Orsen. I have no desire to do anything here but finish it and go."

"*Go,*" she repeated, and something moved through her voice too fast for me to catch, gone before I could name it. "Right. Of course. You're here temporarily."

"Six months. Maybe less."

"Maybe less," she echoed. "Efficient to the end."

"Endings are my talent. Didn't you hear?"

The silence that followed had teeth in it. She looked at me the way you look at a sentence you've read twice because you can't believe it says what it says. And I realized I'd done it again — reached for the joke that was also a wound, my oldest habit, the one she'd once put her hand over my mouth to stop. *You do that,* she'd said, ten years ago, in a cabin that smelled like woodsmoke, *you make the true things funny so nobody catches you saying them.*

Nobody had caught me since. Nobody had bothered.

"Terms," she said at last, briskly, like flipping a page. "Fine. Let's hear yours."

"One: I don't discount below your prices. If my stock would undercut you, I mark it up or hold it back. You'll lose customers to curiosity for the first month regardless — people can't resist a dying store, it's like rubbernecking — but I won't take money out of your register on purpose."

She narrowed her eyes. "Two."

"Two: I buy local where I can. Coffee, repairs, the movers are already local. If the branch spends money in this town, it spends it here, not at the regional supplier."

"Three."

"Three: you tell me what I need to know about this block. Who fixes the awning. Which landlord owns what. Why the bakery changes its hours in July. I'm going to be living above the store for six months and I'd rather learn the terrain from someone who knows it than from trial and error."

"That's not a truce term, that's asking me to be your tour guide."

"It's asking you to let me owe you something. Truces work better when both sides are holding debts."

For a moment she just looked at me, and I watched her run the offer through whatever internal machinery she trusted more than people. I watched her arrive at the answer I'd designed the offer to produce: that accepting cost her nothing and bound me to everything.

"And in exchange?" she asked.

"In exchange, you stop looking at me like I'm the storm."

"I'm not looking at you like anything."

"You're looking at me like June 14th, 2015."

The words were out before any sane internal committee could veto them. Orsen went absolutely still — that same stillness from yesterday, the prey-animal stillness, except now it wasn't fear, it was something older and more carefully maintained. Ten years of careful maintenance. I knew, because I'd done my own maintenance on the same structure, alone, in cities where nobody knew her name.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said.

"Of course not."

"It snowed in June once. Weird weather. Doesn't mean anything."

"Doesn't mean anything," I agreed. "Bad coffee?"

She glanced down at the cup she'd brought me, and for the first time all morning her composure genuinely cracked — a small, human fracture, quickly mortared over. "It's from the diner," she said. "Their coffee is objectively terrible. I chose it deliberately. Peace offerings between enemies shouldn't be good."

"That's the most honest thing you've said all morning."

"Don't get used to it."

We drank the terrible coffee standing among the bare shelves, not quite facing each other, both of us looking at the middle distance like two generals reviewing a map neither fully trusted. The coffee really was awful — burnt, over-extracted, faintly of the urn it had been brewed in sometime around dawn — and I drank it down anyway, all of it, because throwing away a peace offering, even a hostile one, even a deliberately terrible one, seemed like the kind of mistake a man only gets to make once with Orsen Fairweather.

"Truce accepted," she said finally. "Conditionally. Subject to revision. With prejudice."

"Naturally."

"If you undercut me, lie to me, or so much as glance at my ledgers, the truce dissolves and I go back to treating you as what you are."

"Which is?"

She considered it. Genuinely considered it, head tilted, pencils glinting in her hair, and I braced for *enemy*, for *competition*, for any of the clean categories she'd have an easier time carrying.

"A question," she said finally. "You're a question I answered once already, and I didn't like the answer."

Then she collected her cup, nodded — the notarized nod, the diplomatic nod — and walked out of my father's failing store into the June sun, leaving me alone with the ceramic owl, the empty shelves, and the distinct understanding that I had just agreed to a truce with a woman who intended to win it.

I looked at the owl. The owl looked at me.

"Don't," I told it.

* * *

The afternoon went to logistics, which was a mercy. Logistics didn't look at me like June 14th. Logistics didn't bring terrible coffee and leave behind the smell of it — her shampoo, actually, underneath the coffee, some green herbal thing I'd have sworn I'd forgotten and clearly had not, since my brain produced the name of it unprompted around two p.m., *rosemary mint, the drugstore kind, she bought it because it was cheap and smelled expensive* — and logistics didn't stand in my doorway with arithmetic happening behind its eyes.

By four o'clock the shelves were half-stocked and I'd learned the store's acoustics: the creak of the third floorboard, the way the register hummed in E-flat, the particular echo of a box cutter scoring tape. I was pricing a carton of remainders — literary fiction, marked 60% off cover, the saddest category, the one that sells worst and ships heaviest — when the bell rang again.

Not Orsen. A kid. Maybe eleven, maybe twelve, all elbows and a ponytail, wearing a t-shirt with a cartoon dinosaur on it and the fierce, slightly feral confidence of a child who has been sent on an errand and intends to exceed its parameters.

She marched to the counter, planted both hands on it, and looked up at me with dark eyes I recognized instantly, catastrophically, because I had spent one night ten years ago memorizing the exact shape of them on an adult face.

"Are you the Wren guy?" she demanded.

"I'm a Wren guy. There are several."

"My mom says you're here to close the store and go away."

"Your mom sounds well-informed."

"She says you're dangerous." The kid said it with the cheerful bluntness of a child reporting intelligence rather than issuing a warning. "She said, quote, *he's dangerous, Juniper, and I mean financially.*"

I gripped the counter. "Financially dangerous."

"That's what she said. She said it while laughing, though, so I think it's fine." Juniper — it had to be Juniper, the name arrived in my head whole, along with everything else — leaned forward, dropping her voice to a conspiratorial register. "Do you have the new T. Kingfisher? Mom's been hiding it behind the counter for me until payday, but she doesn't know I know. Don't tell her I told you. Also do you have a bathroom?"

"Behind the curtain, past the stockroom."

"Cool." She vanished. I stood at the register holding a pricing gun and experiencing something adjacent to vertigo.

Ten years. The math was merciless and I'd done it before, in parking lots, in hotel rooms, at 3 a.m. with a trade newsletter shaking in my hands. Ten years minus roughly fourteen months. Which meant Orsen had been pregnant during— no. No, the timeline refused to cooperate, the timeline kept sliding sideways, and I stood there doing arithmetic I had no right to do while a twelve-year-old flushed a toilet in my stockroom and the ceramic owl presided over everything with its painted, knowing eyes.

She emerged, washed her hands — I heard the water, absurdly loud — and stopped at the door on her way out.

"You're not what I expected," she announced.

"What did you expect?"

"Dustier. Older. Mom said you were ancient history." Juniper shrugged, elbows first. "You're not that old."

Then she was gone, the bell clattering behind her, and I stood in the half-built store surrounded by books nobody wanted, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the fact that I had just been assessed and found acceptably young by a girl with her mother's exact unimpressed stare.

*Financially dangerous.* She'd laughed when she said it. Orsen had laughed.

I priced the rest of the carton without seeing a single number. Outside, the light was going gold the way it does in early June, generous and unhurried, the light of a season that thinks it has all the time in the world. Six months, I reminded myself. Maybe less. Wind it down, sign the papers, go. Endings were my talent. I had references.

But when I locked up that night and stood on the sidewalk between the two storefronts — hers glowing warm behind its windows, mine dark, the awnings nearly touching overhead like two hands almost meeting — I caught myself doing the thing I'd promised myself I wouldn't do.

Counting days. Not down. Up.

And across the street, in the window of the diner, I could see Orsen sitting in a booth with Juniper, two heads bent together over milkshakes, and I stood there in the dark like an idiot until Juniper spotted me through the glass and pointed, and Orsen turned, and even from across the street I saw her expression do the thing — the flicker, the bird landing and reconsidering — before she turned back to her daughter with the studied calm of a woman who had absolutely not been thinking about me, and who would deny it under oath.

The truce, I reflected, walking home to the apartment above the store, had a fatal flaw in its design.

It required proximity.

And tomorrow, according to the delivery schedule I'd found in my father's files, the branch's first shipment of sale stock was due at eight a.m. sharp — addressed, through some catastrophic clerical error in the regional system, to *Fairweather Books, 12 Marsh Street.*

Both addresses. Both shops. One delivery.

I was going to see her at eight a.m., and she was going to be holding the misdirected manifest, and I was going to have to explain to a woman who believed maybe forty percent of what I said that the universe had a sense of humor and it was aimed at us.

The owl, I felt certain, would find this hilarious.
