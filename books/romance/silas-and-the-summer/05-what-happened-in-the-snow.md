## 5. What Happened in the Snow

**Orsen — ten years ago**

The snow started as a joke and ended as a verdict.

I want to be precise about this, because for a decade I have told myself the story in the most defensible terms available: *we were stranded, there was nothing to do, it was practically an act of God.* But precision is its own kind of betrayal, so let me try the other version too: I got in that truck on purpose.

It was December, my second winter running Fairweather Books alone after Dad's heart gave out, and money was a rumor more than a fact. The regional distributor had screwed up our holiday order — sent forty copies of a celebrity memoir nobody wanted and none of the picture books everybody did — and the only way to fix it before Christmas was to drive two hours to the warehouse myself and argue with a man named Denny. I'd borrowed Tom Ellery's truck because my Corolla had opinions about cold weather, all of them negative.

Silas Wren was loading his own truck outside Wren & Sons when I came out with the keys jingling in my fist like a bad omen.

"Road trip?" he said.

"Supply run."

"In that?" He nodded at the sky, which had gone the color of dishwater. "They're saying eight inches by morning."

"They're always saying something." I heaved the passenger door open. It groaned like it resented me personally. "Denny holds stock for walk-ins until five. After that it goes back into general inventory and I spend January explaining to children why Santa brought them nothing."

He leaned against his tailgate, hands in the pockets of a coat I recognized because I'd spent an embarrassing amount of time not looking at it across the street. Charcoal wool. He looked like an ad for a life I couldn't afford.

"I'm heading that way anyway," he said. "Warehouse district. Let me follow you out at least. Those roads are miserable alone."

"We're competitors," I reminded him. It was our ritual by then, the word passed between us like a coin we both pretended had value. His family's third branch had opened eleven months earlier, four aisles of discounted bestsellers and a café, and for one terrible quarter it had looked like it might actually bury us. It hadn't. Yet. But the ledger lived in my chest now, a permanent low hum of arithmetic.

"Tonight we're just two idiots driving into a storm," he said. "Compete tomorrow."

Here is what I should have done: driven alone, white-knuckled, righteous. Here is what I did: said, "Fine. But if you get us lost, I'm telling everyone the great Wren empire can't read a map."

* * *

We didn't get lost. We got worse than lost. We got *stopped* — Route 9 closed at the pass around six, plows pulled off, state police waving everyone into the lot of a motel that had already given up its last room. The parking lot filled with trucks and taillights and the particular silence of thirty strangers recalculating their evenings.

Silas stood in the falling snow with his phone pressed to his ear and his collar up, and I watched him go through the motions of a man solving a problem, and I understood, with the strange clarity that only comes right before you do something irreversible, that neither of us wanted the problem solved.

"There's a cabin," he said, hanging up. "Ellery place? My dad leases hunting land near here. There's a caretaker's cabin, wood stove, probably mice. Better than sleeping in the truck."

"Probably mice," I repeated. "You really know how to sell a night."

"I've been told my sales technique is aggressive."

The cabin was exactly as advertised: small, dark, smelling of pine and disuse, with a wood stove that took three matches and some profanity to convince. There were two bunks. We did not discuss the bunks. We discussed the fire, and the canned chili in the caretaker's pantry that Silas insisted constituted trespass and I insisted constituted fair trade for shoveling his father's hypothetical walkway, and the storm rattling the windows like it wanted in.

And then somewhere past midnight, with the fire down to embers and the chili cans empty between us like small tin moons, the conversation stopped being about anything.

That's how I remember it — the moment the topics ran out and there was just the two of us left standing there without a subject to hide behind. Ten years of versions of that night have worn the details smooth, but some things stay sharp: his laugh going quiet. The way he looked at me like he was asking permission for something he hadn't said yet. My own voice, smaller than I meant it, saying, "This is a terrible idea."

"Yes," he agreed, and crossed the room anyway.

* * *

I will not inventory that night. Some things shouldn't be itemized; it cheapens the stock. I'll say only this: it was not careless. That's the part nobody believes about mistakes like ours. It wasn't wine or loneliness or boredom. It was careful, and slow, and honest in the way that only things destined to hurt you are honest. He traced the scar on my elbow from the shelf ladder and asked where it came from, and I told him about being nine and falling off my father's stepladder reaching for *Charlotte's Web*, and he held my arm like the story was fragile. Nobody had ever treated my history like it was worth handling gently. I was twenty-three and I mistook that for permanence.

At some point near dawn, half-asleep against his shoulder, I said, "When we get back, this changes everything."

I felt him go still. Just for a second. A held breath dressed as sleepiness.

"Yeah," he said. "Everything."

I fell asleep believing him. That's the sentence I've carried for ten years, and every version of the story I tell myself has to walk past it: *I fell asleep believing him.*

* * *

I woke to gray light and an empty cabin.

Not dramatically empty. Not a note-on-the-pillow, lipstick-on-the-glass kind of empty. Just — tidied. The chili cans rinsed and stacked by the sink. The fire banked. His coat gone from the hook. A scrap of paper on the table that I told myself for years was a note, until honesty cornered me one insomniac night and admitted it was probably a receipt, or nothing, or a trick of the light, because wanting it to be a note doesn't make it one.

The roads were plowed. I drove home through a world scrubbed clean and glittering, rehearsing conversations the whole way. *Good morning. Last night. So. Us.* By the time I pulled onto our street, I had a whole speech built, load-bearing walls and all.

Wren & Sons opened at nine. I watched from my window, arms crossed, wearing my most armored cardigan, waiting for him to come across the street.

He didn't come across the street.

At 9:40 he appeared in his own doorway, helped a delivery man carry in boxes, laughed at something the man said, and went back inside. At noon he walked the length of the block to the diner — passing within fifteen feet of my window — and never once looked up.

I gave him three days. Then a week. Then I stopped counting days and started counting other things, which turned out to be my one true talent: margins, interest, distance. When he finally knocked on my door in February — I found out later his father had transferred him to the city, that the knock was his goodbye, that he'd stood on my step for four minutes by the neighbor's account — I wasn't home. Whether that was accident or instinct, I've never let myself decide.

By spring, the story had hardened into its final shape: *Silas Wren took what he wanted and left before the bill came.* It was a good story. It kept me safe. It just happened to be missing the part where I got in the truck on purpose.

**Silas — ten years ago**

People assume the worst night of your life announces itself. It doesn't. Mine arrived disguised as a supply run and a snowstorm and a woman swearing at a wood stove, and I didn't recognize it until it was over and I was doing the math on how to survive it.

Let me be exact, since she never will be: I followed her truck into that storm because I'd been looking for an excuse for eleven months. Since the day she'd come into our branch — our branch, God, even the possessive was a betrayal of her — and asked, with glacial politeness, whether we stocked local authors, and I'd said we could order anything, and she'd said, "I bet you could," in a voice that made me want to close the store and follow her out. My father called it market research. I called it Tuesdays.

I knew what she thought of us. Fairweather Books was the little shop that wouldn't die, and every quarter my father spread her numbers across the conference table like a general studying terrain he intended to take. "Another year," he'd say, tapping her sales figures, "maybe two." I sat in those meetings and said nothing, which is its own confession.

So: the storm, the cabin, the night. I'm not going to perform false modesty about it. It was the truest twelve hours of my life up to that point. She was funny at three in the morning, which is the hour that tells you who someone actually is, and she was fierce about her father's shop in a way that made me ashamed of mine, and when she fell asleep against my shoulder I lay awake cataloguing everything I was about to lose, because I already knew.

Here's what she's never known, what I've carried like a stone in my shoe for a decade:

My father called at dawn.

I stepped outside to take it, barefoot in the snow, watching my breath tear away. He'd heard the pass was closed. He'd heard I was unaccounted for overnight. And Silas Wren did not become unaccounted for; Silas Wren was an asset with a trajectory. There was a meeting Monday in the city. There was a promotion track. There was, delivered with the breeziness of a man discussing weather, the observation that Fairweather's owner was a pretty young thing but that pretty young things who owned dying bookshops were a detour, and Wrens did not take detours.

"You don't know anything about her," I said.

"I know her quarterly numbers," my father said. "Do you?"

I stood in the snow and understood that I was being asked to choose, and that the choice had been engineered so that either answer cost me everything. Stay, and I'd be the heir who threw away the succession for a fling, and my father would grind her shop down twice as fast out of principle, and she'd end up collateral in a war I'd started. Leave, take the transfer, play the long game — and maybe, someday, come back with enough leverage to protect her. That was the story I sold myself at twenty-three, standing barefoot in the snow: *the long game.* It's amazing what a man can believe when the alternative is admitting he's afraid.

I went back inside and she was asleep, and I sat on the edge of the bunk for an hour I did not have, memorizing her, building the speech I'd give her when I got back. *Give me a year. I'll fix this from the inside. Wait for me.* Every draft was worse than the last. Every draft sounded like a man asking a woman to put her life in escrow on the strength of one night.

In the end I left no note, because every note I wrote sounded like a lie, and I told myself I'd say it in person, properly, with my actual voice, in three days when I was back from the city.

Three days became a week. The week became my father moving up the timeline — the branch needed managing through the holidays, then the quarter, then there was a regional director position dangled like bait, and every time I reached for the door back to that town, he raised the stakes. I told myself each delay was strategy. I told myself a lot of things at twenty-three. It's the golden age of telling yourself things.

When I finally got back in February, I stood on her doorstep for — all right, four minutes, the neighbor was right — and I couldn't do it. Because I'd seen her face in my head the whole drive, and I knew what my news would look like from where she stood: *he stayed away two months and came back to ask her to wait longer.* There was no version of that speech that wasn't a theft. So I knocked, and she wasn't home, and I stood there feeling the relief move through me like something rotten, and I let the silence do my confessing for me.

I have spent ten years telling myself I spared her. That's the story I keep in the drawer with the receipt I took from the cabin table — yes, I took it, because leaving anything behind felt like leaving evidence of a crime, and I couldn't decide if the crime was the night or the leaving.

Both. It was both. That's the honest version, and I've only recently become a man capable of writing it down.

Because here is what changed everything, what dragged all of this up out of the drawer where I'd buried it: last month, going through my father's files to prepare the branch closure, I found the old quarterly reports. Fairweather Books, ten years of them, tracked like a tumor. And pinned behind the most recent one, in my father's handwriting, a note I was never meant to see:

*"Boy still carries a torch. Use it if you have to."*

Ten years, and my father had known. Had counted on it. Had filed my heart under *leverage.*

I sat in the back office of the failing branch next to her shop, holding that note, and I understood that the story I'd been telling myself — the noble long game, the strategic retreat — had been written by a scared kid and edited ever since by a coward. And I understood something else, something that made my hands shake: whatever I do next, whatever I build or buy or burn down, she deserves the truth first.

She won't want it. She's had ten years to build her case against me, and I've seen the way she looks at me — like a bill she's still deciding whether to pay.

But she's going to get it anyway. Starting with the part where I got in that truck on purpose too.

* * *

Tomorrow morning, I'm going to walk across the street, and I'm going to start telling her the truth in pieces small enough that she might not throw a book at me.

I just haven't figured out yet which piece goes first.
