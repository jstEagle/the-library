## 6. Inventory Day

The delivery truck arrived at 8:14 on a Thursday morning, which was the first sign the day intended to betray me. Deliveries never came on Thursdays. Thursdays were my day for pretending the shop ran itself while I reorganized the poetry section by mood instead of alphabet, a system only I understood and only I defended.

I signed for eleven boxes without looking at the manifest. This was my first mistake. The second was trusting that the universe, having already sent me Silas Wren, would have the decency to stop there.

I didn't open the boxes until noon. When I did, I knew immediately that something was wrong, because the top box contained forty copies of a maritime history I had never ordered, and beneath it, in careful brick-like stacks, were the books I *had* ordered — the summer display titles, the ones Juniper had helped me pick, the ones I'd promised the window by Friday.

I checked the packing slip. Then I checked it again, as if repetition were a form of problem-solving.

*Wren & Sons Books. Ship to: 114 Harbor Street.*

114 Harbor Street was my address. The slip said so. But the account number belonged to Wren & Sons, and the order — I could tell by the titles, the quantities, the smug little bulk discount — had been placed by Silas.

* * *

I carried the slip next door like a subpoena.

The bell over his door did that thing it always did, that bright indifferent *ding* that didn't care whose territory it announced me into. Silas was behind the counter with a box cutter and an expression of a man who had been having a perfectly reasonable morning.

"You stole my books," I said.

He looked up. Whatever he'd expected me to say, it wasn't that, and I had the small satisfaction of watching him recalibrate. "That's a serious accusation."

"It's a factual one." I slapped the slip on his counter. "Your order. My address. Forty copies of something about whaling nobody in this town asked for."

"Nobody in this town asked for *most* of what gets published," he said, "and yet you seem to do fine." He took the slip, read it, and I watched the exact moment he understood, because his mouth did a thing — a twitch at one corner, quickly arrested, the facial equivalent of a man swallowing a laugh whole.

"Don't," I warned.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing it with your face."

"It's a mix-up," he said. "The distributor transposed the account numbers. Or the addresses. It happens."

"It doesn't happen to me."

"Well." He set down the box cutter with the deliberate care of a man disarming himself. "Now it's happened to you, and the question is what we do about it."

"What we do about it is you come get your books."

"My books are currently in your shop."

"And my books are currently in your shop."

"Are they." He said it mildly, but I could see him doing the math, and I hated that I could see it, hated that after three weeks of living next door to each other in a state of armed neutrality I could still read his face like a large-print edition. "Because if the distributor transposed the addresses, then your order went to the Wren & Sons warehouse, which means your books are in a box somewhere in Ohio."

The floor did not open up and swallow me. I noted this as a missed opportunity.

"So," Silas said, and now the laugh he'd swallowed was fully in his voice, warm and terrible, "your inventory is in my shop, my inventory is in your shop, and the replacement shipment, if we call them today, arrives Monday."

"I have a window to fill by Friday."

"I know. I can see your window from here. It has a cardboard cutout of a lighthouse in it."

"That lighthouse is *beloved*."

"That lighthouse is a fire hazard with a smile."

I took a breath. I want it on the record that I took a breath, that I stood there in his shop with its unfamiliar shelving and its faint smell of fresh paint over old ambition, and I considered every available option, and every available option was worse than the one I arrived at.

"Fine," I said. "We trade back. Today. Before close."

"Today's delivery day for me too, you know."

"Then we'll both be shorthanded."

"Or," he said, in the tone of a man proposing something perfectly innocent while knowing full well it was not, "we could just do it together. Faster that way. Four hands, two shops, one afternoon."

"I have a daughter."

"Juniper's at the Delgados' until six. She told me this morning. She tells me a lot of things." He said it lightly, but something moved under it, some current I chose not to chart. "She's very forthcoming. You should try it sometime."

"I'm forthcoming."

"You're forthcoming about store hours."

* * *

And that is how, at two o'clock on a Thursday in late June, I found myself carrying a box of Silas Wren's maritime histories through my own front door while he held it open with his shoulder, close enough that I could smell the cedar in his cologne, which was a detail I resented knowing.

We established rules first, because rules were how I kept rooms from becoming situations.

"Your books go on your cart. My books go on my cart. We don't shelve each other's stock."

"Agreed."

"We don't rearrange anything."

"Even when it's wrong?"

"*Especially* when it's wrong."

"Your romance section is alphabetical by author," he said, with the delicacy of a surgeon noting a tumor. "That's wrong."

"That's *correct*."

"It's correct like a phone book is correct. Nobody browses a phone book for pleasure."

"People browse my romance section constantly."

"People browse your romance section because you hand-sell it like a woman defending a thesis. If it were shelved by trope—"

"It is not going to be shelved by trope."

"—enemies to lovers, small town, second chance—" he was already walking toward it, already reaching, and I moved to intercept him with the speed of a woman who had spent ten years guarding that particular shelf, and that was when it happened: we both arrived at the same moment, his hand and mine landing on the same book at the same time, his fingers over mine on the spine of some paperback with a shirtless man on the cover, and neither of us moved.

The shop went very quiet. The kind of quiet that has a pulse.

His hand was warm. That was the whole of my observation, and it was too much. His hand was warm and it covered mine and he was looking at me and I was looking at the shirtless man, because the shirtless man was safer, the shirtless man had no history.

"Second chance," Silas said, quietly, reading the cover like a man reading an omen.

"It's a popular trope," I said.

"Is it a good one?"

"Ask me in September."

I pulled my hand back. He let me. That was the thing about Silas — he always let me, always left the door exactly as open as I'd left it, never pushing, which was somehow worse than pushing, because pushing I could resist. Patience I had no defense against.

* * *

We worked. That was the strange mercy of it — that we could work. The boxes emptied, the carts filled, and the barbs kept coming, but somewhere around the fourth box they stopped being weapons and started being a language.

"You still shelve the poetry by mood," he said, passing me with a stack of his whaling books. "I saw it through the window."

"You still wear cologne like a man being interviewed for a yacht club."

"You still talk like someone who's never once lost an argument."

"I've never once lost an argument."

"You lost one in 2014. I was there."

The box in my hands went heavier by exactly one memory. He heard it too — heard the year land between us — and to his credit he didn't retreat from it, didn't paper it over. He just set his books down and said, "That was a low blow. I'm sorry."

"You're not."

"I'm a little sorry. Mostly I'm curious whether you've forgiven the rest of it."

"There's nothing to forgive. You left. People leave. It's practically a trope."

"Enemies to lovers," he said. "What's the trope for what we are?"

"Sequels nobody asked for."

He laughed — a real one, startled out of him, and I felt it in my chest like a struck bell, and I hated that too, hated how easily my body still answered his laughter, ten years and one bankruptcy attempt and one daughter later, as if no time had passed at all, as if my ribs were still tuned to his frequency.

We kept shelving. The afternoon light moved across the floorboards the way it did in the hour before closing, all amber and long shadows, and Juniper's reading chair sat empty in the corner, and Silas's tie was gone — when had he taken off the tie? — and his sleeves were rolled, and I made a policy of not noticing forearms, and the policy was failing.

"Truce holding?" he asked, at some point, from the other side of the biography section.

"Barely."

"That's the strongest truce we've had."

* * *

The last box was mine — the summer display titles, rescued from Ohio by nothing but luck, and I knelt to shelve them in the window table while Silas stood by with the empty cart like a man who didn't want to leave but couldn't invent a reason to stay.

"Juniper picked half of these," I said, because the silence had gotten loud.

"She has good taste. She told me the lighthouse cutout is 'a crime against windows.' She's not wrong."

"She's ten. Her opinions are not binding."

"Her opinions are the best thing in this shop," he said, and it came out so plainly, so without strategy, that I looked up at him before I could stop myself.

He was looking at the window, at the little table, at the books, but I knew — I *knew* with the sick certainty of a woman whose containment plan was visibly failing — that he wasn't talking about the books.

"The display," I said, standing, brushing off my knees, retreating into logistics the way other people retreat into prayer. "It'll be done by Friday. Your books are back on your side of the wall. We can pretend this never happened."

"We could," he agreed. He wheeled the cart toward the door, and stopped with his hand on the frame, and didn't turn around. "Orsen. For what it's worth — I'd have come to help even if the distributor hadn't made a mistake."

"That's not for it to be worth, then. That's just a thing you're saying."

"It's a thing I'm saying."

The bell rang him out. I stood in the amber light with forty whaling books on a cart and my heart doing something unscheduled in my chest, and I told myself, the way I'd been telling myself for three weeks, that it was nothing, that it was manageable, that it was contained.

Then I found the note. It had been tucked into the last box, into the packing paper, in handwriting I hadn't seen in ten years and recognized in half a second:

*Ask her about September. — S.W.*
