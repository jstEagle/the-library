## 9. Pieces, Not the Whole

**ORSEN**

The argument started over a stapler, which is how all great tragedies begin if you're paying attention.

It was nearly eleven at night, and I was in my shop with the lights half off, doing the thing I do when I can't sleep, which is alphabetize the poetry section by mood instead of author. Silas had come in an hour earlier with two coffees and a pretext — a shared distributor invoice he could have emailed — and had somehow migrated from the register to the poetry section to the rolling ladder to standing entirely too close while I pretended the ladder needed steadying.

The stapler was Juniper's. Bright pink, shaped like a flamingo, left on the counter because my daughter treats the shop like an extension of her bedroom. Silas picked it up, examined it, and said, "You know, when we were twenty-three, you would rather have died than own something pink."

"I was twenty-three," I said. "I was an idiot with excellent taste in terrible men."

"Flamingo staplers are terrible men?"

"They're commitments I didn't plan for."

He laughed, and the laugh did what his laughs always did — slipped past my defenses like water through a cracked foundation, finding every seam I'd spent a decade sealing. And then, because I was tired and the shop was dark and he was standing close enough that I could smell his stupid cedar cologne, I said the thing I should not have said.

"You can't just come back, Silas. You can't show up after ten years with coffee and charm and act like we're — whatever this is."

"And what is this?"

"I don't know. That's the problem."

He set the flamingo down with great care, the way he does when he's about to be careful with something bigger. "Then tell me what you want it to be."

And there it was. The question. The one I'd been dodging since June, since the snowstorm flashback I refused to have, since he started shelving my returns without being asked and remembering that I take my coffee with too much sugar and no apology.

"I want what I've always wanted," I said. "I want the shop. I want Juniper's flamingo stapler on my counter. I want my ledgers to balance and my awning to stop leaking. I want a life I can count."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the only answer I have."

He stepped back — one full step, deliberate, like a man retreating from a fire he'd been warming his hands over. "No," he said. "It's the only answer you *give*. There's a difference, Orsen, and you know it better than anyone."

I should have thrown him out. Instead I turned off the last lamp, which made it worse, because darkness is honest in a way I've never learned to be.

"Say what you came here to say," I told him.

So he did.

* * *

**SILAS**

Here is what I know about Orsen Fairweather, ten years and one flamingo stapler later: she will hand you the exact right book at the exact right moment, she will remember your dead mother's favorite author and shelve a copy face-out where you'll find it, she will give and give and give — and then, at the precise moment you reach for all of it, she will produce a receipt.

I learned that at twenty-three. I'm apparently a slow learner, because I'd come back for more.

"You lend pieces," I said. "That's what you do. You lend the piece of you that's funny, the piece that's generous, the piece that remembers everything. You're the best lender on this street. But you never give the whole. And I spent ten years thinking that was about me — that I'd done something, that the night in the cabin meant less than I thought, that I'd misread the whole thing. But it wasn't me. It's you. It's the way you're built. You'd rather lose a man than lose control of the ledger."

The words came out harder than I meant them to. I watched them land. I watched her take the hit the way she takes everything — spine straight, chin up, eyes going bright and furious and wet all at once, like a shop window in a storm.

"You don't get to psychoanalyze me," she said. "You left. You don't get the explanatory power of my entire personality."

"I know I left. I've told you why."

"You told me your *version*."

"Because you've never once told me yours! Not in June, not under the awning, not any of the forty times I've asked. You'll talk about the weather, the inventory, the distributor, my father, the lease, anything — anything except what actually happened to us. You know what I think? I think you'd rather keep me as a wound than risk me as a man. A wound you understand. A man is a variable."

The shop was so quiet I could hear the old radiator ticking, the streetlight humming, my own pulse doing something undignified in my ears.

"Say that again," she said, very quietly.

"No."

"Say it, Silas. Since we're being honest tonight."

"I think," I said, "that you keep everyone at arm's length with a smile, and you've gotten so good at it that you've forgotten the arm was ever for anything else. And I think I would rather have nothing — nothing, Orsen, not the coffee, not the almosts, not the way you look at me when you think I'm not watching — than keep collecting pieces of you like a man picking up coins off a floor. I'm not a charity. I'm not a lending library. And I am *not* going to spend another ten years wondering what the whole of you would have been like."

She flinched. Actually flinched, like I'd struck her, and for one terrible second I thought I'd finally done it — finally said the thing that would make her lock the door on me for good.

Then she said, "Get out."

"Orsen—"

"Get out of my shop."

I got. But at the door I turned, because I have never once in my life had the good sense to leave well enough alone, and I said: "The whole thing, Orsen. That's the only version of you I want. Decide if you have it to give."

* * *

**ORSEN**

He left. The bell over the door did its stupid cheerful jingle, and then I was alone in the dark with the poetry shelved by mood and my heart doing something it had no business doing at eleven-forty on a Tuesday.

I did what I always do. I cleaned. I squared the flamingo stapler with the edge of the counter. I straightened the pens in the cup. I alphabetized the already-alphabetized. My hands needed occupation because my head had gone somewhere I don't let it go.

Because here's the thing he got right, and I hated him for it, and I hated myself more for the hating.

I lend pieces. I always have. Even before him. Even as a kid — I was the girl who'd lend you her favorite book but not the sequel, who'd tell you her middle name but not her middle fear. My mother used to say I was "careful with myself," which is what mothers say when they mean *stingy*, and they're right, and it's not a character flaw so much as a survival strategy with a very good publicist.

And then Silas Wren at twenty-three, with his terrible car and his beautiful laugh and his family's money sitting on my family's ruin like a cat on a fresh grave. He was the one person I ever handed the whole thing to — the whole stupid, unguarded, unbudgeted heart — and the snow came, and the cabin happened, and it was the best night of my life, and then I woke up and he was gone. No note. No call. Nothing but a dent in the pillow and a decade of me deciding, with the full authority of a woman scorned, that the whole heart was a bad investment.

I never asked what he believed. I never told him what I believed. We each carried our own version of that morning like rival editions of the same book, never comparing notes, both certain the other one had the misprint.

And now he was standing in my shop telling me he'd rather have nothing.

The awful thing — the thing I sat down on the floor of my own shop, back against the poetry shelf, to fully confront — was that I understood him. Because I'd been doing the same thing. Every coffee, every almost-touch at the shelf, every night he stayed past closing: I'd been collecting *his* pieces too. His charm. His patience. His way with Juniper. I'd been sampling the menu for a month and calling it prudence, and calling it prudence is the oldest lie in my inventory.

Juniper deserves a mother who's whole. Not a mother who's careful. There's a difference, and I know it better than anyone.

I got up off the floor. I locked the front door out of pure reflex. And then I did something I haven't done since I was twenty-three.

I picked up the phone.

He answered on the second ring, like he'd been holding it. "Orsen."

"Come back," I said. "Not tonight. Tomorrow. Come back tomorrow and I'll — I'll tell you what happened. My version. The whole misprint."

A long silence, long enough that I checked the screen to make sure the call hadn't dropped.

"Tomorrow," he said finally, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do — it cracked, just slightly, on a man who is never anything but smooth. "I'll be there."

I hung up before I could take it back. Then I stood in my dark shop, holding a pink flamingo stapler like a talisman, and realized with a lurch that tomorrow I would have to say the words out loud — about the cabin, about the morning, about the ten years — and that some doors, once you open them, don't just swing. They come off the hinges.

I just didn't yet know that his version of the story would break mine in half.
