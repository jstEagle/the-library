## 10. The Offer

My father's phone calls have a particular weather system all their own. There's the opening front — a silence long enough to make you check whether the line dropped. Then the pressure drop: *Silas.* Just my name, delivered like a diagnosis. And then whatever storm he's been carrying rolls in over you, and you stand there holding the phone and getting wet.

I was in the back room of the branch, elbow-deep in a box of remaindered hardcovers nobody had wanted at full price and nobody would want at half, when the call came. The afternoon light through the loading door was doing that late-July thing where it goes amber and forgiving even over a dumpster. I'd spent the morning with the ledger — my father's ledger, the real one, the one that lived behind a password he thought I hadn't cracked — and I'd come away with the specific nausea of a man who has read his own autopsy.

The branch wasn't closing. The branch had closed some time ago; we were just the slow echo. What remained was the arithmetic of salvage, and my father wanted me to perform it.

*Silas.*

"Father."

"You got my email."

"I did."

"And?"

The email had been four lines long. Four lines is how my father says important things. *Come to the city office Saturday. Wear something that isn't a flannel shirt. We'll discuss your future.* I'd read it standing in Orsen's doorway, actually — she'd been restocking the poetry shelf, and I'd watched her mouth move around the titles like they were small prayers, and then my phone buzzed and the future arrived, punctual as ever, wearing its four lines.

"There's nothing to discuss," I said now, into the phone, into the amber light. "You want me to close it faster."

A pause. My father deploying silence the way other men deploy applause.

"I want you to stop embarrassing yourself," he said. "There's a difference."

* * *

Here's what I hadn't told anyone, including especially myself for the first few weeks: I came back to Marlow Bend intending to be gone by midsummer. Wind down the stock, settle the lease, ship the fixtures to whichever sister store could absorb them, and drive out with the mirrors folded flat and the name scraped off the glass. A clean amputation. That was the job. That was what sons of Gerald Wren were for — we were the hands that signed things.

Then I'd stood on the sidewalk outside Fairweather Books at seven in the morning and watched Orsen Fairweather unlock her own door with a key on a lanyard shaped like a whale, because her daughter had made it for her out of oven-bake clay, and something in my chest had performed an act of structural failure from which it has not yet recovered.

So no. Midsummer came and went. I was still here. I was shelving books in a dying store to be near a woman who still looked at me like I was a fire she'd already put out once and didn't trust not to catch again. I was drinking bad coffee across the counter from her every few days under the flag of truce neither of us had ever formally ratified but both of us observed with religious care. And last week — God help me — I'd asked her, hypothetically, over the counter, in a voice I'd tried to keep light and failed, whether she believed second chances existed.

She'd said, "Hypothetically?" and looked at me for a long moment with those gray eyes that have always been able to take a man apart without raising their voice, and said, "I believe people get them. Whether they deserve them is a separate audit."

And I had walked home — three blocks, past the vacant lot, past the diner with the hand-lettered pie specials — feeling like a man who'd been handed a map to a country he might never be allowed to enter.

Saturday, then. The city office. Something that isn't a flannel shirt.

* * *

Wren & Sons headquarters occupies the eleventh floor of a building that looks like it was designed by a man who hated paper. Glass and steel and a lobby fountain that runs on a timer so the sound of money trickling is only audible during business hours. I wore the navy blazer my mother bought me before she stopped buying things, and I sat in the chair outside my father's office — leather, high-backed, angled so visitors can't see the windows — and listened to him finish a call about a distribution contract in a voice like gravel being poured politely.

He hung up. He looked at me. I have his jaw and his height and, apparently, his talent for making silence do labor.

"You've seen the numbers," he said.

"I've seen the numbers."

"Then you know the branch is a wound. And you know what we do with wounds." He opened a folder on the desk — actual paper, because my father believes screens can be hacked and folders cannot be argued with. "Fast and quiet. That's the play. You liquidate the inventory to the regional wholesaler, you terminate the lease — there's a break clause, we paid extra for it years ago, which tells you everything about how long I expected that store to live — and you're done by Labor Day."

"Labor Day," I said.

"End of August," he said, which was the same sentence wearing different clothes.

End of August. I filed that away somewhere it would keep hurting later.

"And after," he said, and here his voice changed — shifted into the register he uses for gifts, which is worse than the register he uses for threats, because threats at least respect you — "there's a position. Regional operations. Your own territory, your own budget line, your name on the door of an office that isn't eleven hundred square feet of failing brick in a town whose entire economy is a diner and a woman's bookshop."

He let that land. He'd built the sentence carefully. I could see the scaffolding: *a town whose entire economy is... a woman's bookshop.* He knew. Of course he knew. Gerald Wren has never once in his life been surprised by information he could have gone looking for.

"The block," I said. "When you say fast and quiet. What does fast mean for the block?"

"It means," he said, leaning back, "that when Wren & Sons pulls out of a retail strip, the strip follows. That's not cruelty, Silas, that's gravity. Landlords reprice. Foot traffic drops. The little shops next to a dead anchor store don't die of anything dramatic — they just stop getting customers who came for us and stayed for them." He spread his hands, a gesture of magnificent false modesty. "Fairweather will be fine. She's scrappy. She'll move or she'll shrink. Either way it won't be because of you. It'll be because of math."

Math. My father's word for everything he doesn't want to feel.

"And if I don't take the job," I said, "if I drag the closure out—"

"Then you're a manager of a corpse," he said, "and the position goes to Dana Kessler, who, incidentally, would close that branch by the fifteenth and sleep fine afterward." He picked up his pen. Conversation over, in his cosmology. "Think about it. You have until Monday to confirm the timeline."

* * *

I drove back with the windows down and the radio off, which is my version of prayer.

Here is the shape of the trap, laid out flat so I can look at it honestly, the way Orsen inventories her shop every morning — measure everything, want nothing. Except I had wanted. That was the whole problem. I had stood in her shop and let Juniper beat me at gin rummy and felt the ten-year-old scar tissue go warm and new, and I had let myself believe — hypothetically, in the language of second chances — that there was a version of this summer where I stayed.

And my father had just priced that version. It cost one block. One street of small businesses that extended credit to each other and complained about parking and showed up to each other's events. It cost the diner's foot traffic. It cost the landlord's patience, because a gutted anchor store drags the whole building's valuation down and landlords respond to fear with rent increases — I'd seen the pattern in six other towns, printed neatly in internal memos my father circulated under the subject line GRAVITY.

It cost Orsen. Not dramatically. Gravity doesn't do drama. It would just lean on her, month after month, until the shop that held her and Juniper and the whale-shaped keychain shrank or moved or died quietly, and everyone would agree it was sad, and no one would agree it was murder.

Fast and quiet. By Labor Day. Which meant I had maybe five weeks left of this summer, and I hadn't told her. I hadn't told her any of it — not the deadline, not the promotion, not the fact that every day I stalled the closure was a day I was spending out of a fund that, when it ran dry, would leave her standing in the wreckage wondering why the man next door had smiled at her daughter and shelved her stock and drunk her terrible coffee while all along carrying the demolition order in his breast pocket.

Ten years ago I left without saying goodbye, and I told myself it was mercy. It was cowardice with good posture. And here I was, drafting the same letter again, older stationery.

* * *

The branch was dark when I got back except for the window display lights, which are on a timer my father set in 2011 and has never once reconsidered. I stood inside the door for a while, in the blue-white glow, surrounded by thirty thousand books that had been counted, valued, and condemned.

Dana Kessler would have them boxed by the fifteenth.

I walked the aisles. Children's section first — always children's first, my one inherited virtue — and I thought about Summer Reading Night, about Juniper sitting cross-legged on the carpet with her chin in her hands, watching me do the dragon voice, laughing with her whole body the way kids do before the world teaches them to ration it. About Orsen standing at the edge of the room with her arms crossed, not laughing, exactly, but not leaving either, which from Orsen is a sonnet.

She lends pieces of herself, she told me. Never the whole. And I'd told her I'd rather have nothing than pieces, and I'd meant it, and the universe — or my father, which at certain altitudes amounts to the same thing — had immediately responded by offering me a whole life in another city, purchased with the dismantling of hers.

Measure everything, want nothing. Her rules, not mine. Mine had always been the opposite: want everything, measure too late.

I sat down on the floor of the children's section, in the glow of the timer lights, and I took out my phone and pulled up the lease file. Break clause. Termination notice. Liquidation schedule. All of it sitting there waiting for my signature like a loaded thing.

And underneath the lease file, because I'm my father's son and I keep my own folders: the branch's supplier contracts. The landlord's contact. The building's parcel number, which I'd looked up at two in the morning three weeks ago for reasons I hadn't let myself examine.

The building. Their building. The one with the shared awning and the wall between the shops and Orsen's name on the ground-floor lease.

I stared at the parcel number for a long time. Then I closed the file, because wanting a thing and being able to afford a thing are separated by a canyon, and I had no rope.

Monday. I had until Monday to confirm the timeline.

Five weeks of summer. One promotion. One woman who had finally, hypothetically, left the door open a crack — and a father whose offer was designed, with perfect precision, to close it.

I turned off the timer lights myself, which my father would have called insubordination, and locked up, and stood on the sidewalk between the two stores in the dark, looking at the wall that divided them, thinking: *fast and quiet.*

Nothing about her had ever been quiet. Nothing about what I wanted was going to be fast.

Monday was coming either way.
