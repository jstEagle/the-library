## 11. Rain on the Awning

The storm arrived the way bad news does — first a rumor on the wind, then all at once, everywhere, no negotiation.

I was locking up at six when the sky went the color of a bruise over the hardware store, and the first fat drops hit the awning like someone throwing gravel at it from a great height. By the time I'd gotten the door bolted and the OPEN sign flipped, the street had emptied itself with the efficiency of people who owned umbrellas. I had not. I own four hundred books about weather. I own no umbrella. This tells you everything about how I prepare for life.

I stepped under the awning to wait it out, and there he was.

Of course he was. Silas Wren, arms crossed, watching the rain sheet off the edge of the canvas like it was a private screening arranged for his benefit. He'd rolled his sleeves up. He always rolls his sleeves up. It's a whole aesthetic, the forearms, and I resent how well it works on me specifically.

"Great," I said. "Trapped."

"Trapped," he agreed, with the ghost of a smile. "Ten years and you still say it like a diagnosis."

"It is a diagnosis. Acute exposure to Silas Wren. Prognosis guarded."

He laughed, and the rain got louder, drumming on the canvas until conversation at normal volume became impossible and we had to stand closer than the truce technically allowed. The awning is shared property — the lease says so, a fact I'd forgotten until this exact moment, when it became the most consequential clause in any document I've ever signed. His shoulder was maybe four inches from mine. The air between the shops smelled like wet asphalt and, faintly, like him, which is cedar and old paper and something I have never found on a shelf and stopped looking for years ago.

We stood there. The rain did its work.

"You know," he said eventually, "the last time weather did this to us, it was the opposite problem."

I should have changed the subject. I had a whole arsenal of subject-changing weapons — the delivery that was late, the gutter that needed cleaning, the fascinating subject of gutters generally. Instead I heard myself say, "It was a lot more snow than this is rain."

"Considerably more." He was quiet a second. "You've never wanted to talk about it."

"There's never been anything to say. It happened. You left. The end. It's a very short book, Silas. I don't stock short books."

"That's not true. You stock poetry."

I looked at him. He was watching the rain, not me, which I think was deliberate, which I think was him giving me the one thing he knew I needed: no eye contact, no demand, just the door left open a crack.

"I waited," I said, and then wished I hadn't, because my voice did something embarrassing on the word *waited*.

He turned his head. "What?"

"Nothing. Forget it."

"Orsen."

"The storm," I said, gesturing at the storm, usefully.

"The storm can wait. It's clearly not going anywhere." He shifted so he was facing me, and the rain filled the silence like a witness that wouldn't testify. "You waited?"

"Three days," I said, to the street. "The roads were closed for two. I told myself the third day was just — caution. That you'd been delayed. That your phone had died, because your phone was always dying, you charged it like once a week like some kind of Victorian." I heard myself going on and couldn't stop. "By day four I did the math. The cabin was forty minutes from your parents' house. Your mother answered on the first ring. She said, *Oh, Silas is in the city, he left Sunday morning.* Sunday morning. I was still asleep when he left Sunday morning. He stepped over me, Silas. He packed a bag and stepped over me and drove out of the storm like I was furniture."

"I didn't step over you."

"You did, though. That's the whole — that's the load-bearing fact of it."

"I wrote you a note."

The rain got very loud. Or maybe my ears did.

"You did not," I said.

"I did. I sat on the end of the bed — the creaky end, the end that woke you up every time I rolled over — and I wrote it on the back of a trail map. I told you I had to get back for the meeting with my father, that I'd call, that I—" He stopped. Ran a hand through his hair, which is what he does when he's losing an argument with himself. "I said I'd see you in the fall. That was the line. *See you in the fall.* I thought it sounded less terrifying than *I love you and I'm scared*, which was what I actually meant."

"You never sent it."

"I put it under your keys. On the nightstand. And then I drove home and my father was waiting in the driveway, and he'd already heard about the cabin — small town, one gas station, the gas station attendant was his golf partner — and he spent forty-five minutes explaining to me exactly what I'd be throwing away. The company. The trajectory. The girl with the failing bookshop and the chip on her shoulder." He said it flat, reciting, and I could hear the father's voice inside his voice like a recording. "And I told him to go to hell. I did. And then I didn't call you for two weeks, because every time I picked up the phone I could hear him saying *she'll want things you can't give her yet*, and I was twenty-three, Orsen, I had nothing, I was a name on a building I didn't own. I thought I was doing you a favor. I thought — give it six months, get the branch solvent, come back with something to offer. And then six months became a year, and by then it had been so long that calling felt worse than not calling, and I told myself you'd moved on, because that was easier than the alternative."

"Which was?"

"Which was that I'd broken the one good thing I'd ever done on purpose."

I stared at the rain. There is a specific kind of vertigo that comes from learning, ten years too late, that the story you've been telling yourself had a different ending sitting in a drawer the whole time. I had built a decade on top of *he left without a word*. I had used it as a load-bearing wall. He left without a word; therefore men leave; therefore want nothing; therefore measure everything, keep the ledgers balanced, keep the heart in a locked case like a first edition you're afraid to shelve spine-out.

And the whole time there had been a note. Under my keys. Which I never found, because I left that cabin in a fury the next morning and stripped the bed like erasing evidence.

"Where is it?" I asked. "The note."

"I don't know. The cabin belonged to Dev's uncle. It's probably still under a coffee mug in a house that's been rented four hundred times since." He laughed, but it came out wrong. "Ten years of me thinking you read it and never answered. That's what I believed, you know. That you got the note, read *see you in the fall*, and decided I wasn't worth a phone call. Which, fair. It was a coward's note. But I spent years being angry at you for it. Quietly. In the way you get angry at someone you can't afford to lose."

"You were angry at *me*?" The laugh that came out of me was not a kind one. "I stood in that cabin and watched the snow and rehearsed what I'd say to you for two days, and then rehearsed it for two more days after you didn't come back, and then I went home and didn't speak to anyone for a week. I was angry at you for ten years, Silas. I named the resentment. It had its own shelf."

"I know. I've seen the shelf. You put all my family's books on it face-down."

"That's different. That's merchandising."

The rain eased a little — not stopped, but softened, from assault to argument, the kind of rain you can talk over. Somewhere down the street a car hissed past, headlights smearing through the water. I became aware that we were standing much closer than four inches now, that at some point in the last ten minutes the truce's border had moved, and that I didn't remember crossing it.

"You really thought I read it," I said.

"I really thought you read it." He looked at me then, and his face did something I'd never seen it do in all these weeks of barbs and bad coffee — it came apart a little. "Orsen, I have spent ten years thinking you read that note, weighed me, and found me wanting. It's why I never — when I came back here, when I saw you through the window that first day, I nearly got back in the truck. Because I thought I was the villain in your book and I'd earned the role."

"You're not the villain."

"I'm not the hero either. I'm the guy who wrote *see you in the fall* because he couldn't write *I'm afraid*."

"Those are the same sentence," I said. "In some languages."

"In which languages?"

"Mine. As of approximately ten years ago."

We stood with that. The awning dripped a slow metronome off its edge. And here is the thing I could not say, standing there, and have been unable to say for ten years, and am only now setting down where someone might read it: grief is not a fact. Grief is a story you tell about a fact, and I had been telling mine so long I'd mistaken it for the truth. He left. That was the fact. Everything else — the cowardice, the verdict, the stepping-over — I had written in myself, in the margins, in ink, and then called it the text.

He hadn't stepped over me. He'd sat on the creaky end of the bed and written a note and failed, the way twenty-three-year-olds fail, catastrophically and in good faith. It didn't fix anything. It didn't un-break the decade. But it moved the wall.

"I used to come by the shop," he said quietly. "Before I moved back. Twice, maybe three times over the years. I'd stand across the street and look at the window displays. You do that thing with the spines where you spell something. I saw LOVE spelled in poetry once and I couldn't come in. I drove back to the city that same night."

"That was February. Two years ago."

"You remember which month."

"I remember everything," I said, before I could stop it. "It's my whole problem. I run a shop on a street where nothing changes and I remember everything."

The rain picked up again, hard, and the streetlight across the way stuttered on early, throwing our shadows long and doubled against the shuttered window of the pharmacy. His hand was on the awning post. Mine was on the brick beside it. Six inches of wet brick between them, and both of us looking at the rain like it was the most interesting weather in meteorological history.

"Ten years," he said. "We lost ten years to a note under some keys."

"We didn't lose them to the note." I made myself say it, because it was true and because if I didn't say it out loud it would keep growing in the dark. "We lost them to two people who were too scared to make one phone call. The note's just where we buried the bodies."

He huffed — almost a laugh, almost not. "When you put it that way."

"I own a shop. I put things that way professionally."

Silence, but a different kind now. The kind that has furniture in it.

"Orsen." He said my name the way he'd said it that night in the cabin, the second time, when the fire was low. I felt it in my sternum like a struck bell. "I'm not asking for anything. I want to be clear about that. I'm not asking. But I need you to know that what I believed — that you read the note and discarded me — it made every decision after that. And if you believed I left without a word, that made every decision for you. And we were both wrong, and we were both standing in the same storm, ten feet apart, for ten years."

"Twelve feet. There's a doorway between the shops."

"Twelve feet," he conceded.

I should have said something then. I had things — a whole shelf of them, sorted, the good stuff, the true stuff. That I'd thought of him every time it snowed. That Juniper — and there it was, the thought I always flinched from, Juniper, who has his exact way of tipping her head when she's about to argue, who I have never once explained to anyone, whose existence is a fact I have kept in a locked case for nine years.

The rain chose that moment to slacken, treacherously, the way storms do when they want to ruin your life. The drumming softened to a patter. The street came back — puddled, shining, ordinary.

"Storm's breaking up," Silas said, and made no move to leave.

"Storms do that. They're unreliable narrators."

He looked at me then, really looked, and whatever was in my face made him go still. "There's something you're not saying."

"There's a great deal I'm not saying. I'm a very reserved person. Ask anyone."

"Orsen—"

"Goodnight, Silas." I stepped out from under the awning into the thinning rain, because staying was suddenly more dangerous than getting wet, and because Juniper's sitter charged by the quarter hour, and because I am a coward with excellent time management. I got four steps before I turned around. I don't know why. Yes I do. I know exactly why.

He was still under the awning, watching me, rain dripping off the canvas between us like a curtain half-drawn.

"The note," I said. "If it's still under those keys — if some future renter ever finds it — I hope they know it wasn't a bad note. *See you in the fall* is a terrible sentence. But it was written by a person who meant well. I know that now."

I walked home in the rain without hurrying, and the strange thing — the thing I turned over all night like a stone with something underneath it — was that I felt lighter and heavier at once, the way you do after setting down something you've carried so long your body forgot it was optional.

I did not know, as I let myself into the apartment and listened to Juniper breathing in the next room, that she had spent her afternoon in his shop, and that she had come home with a question she was saving, the way she saves everything, for exactly the wrong moment.

She asked it at breakfast.
