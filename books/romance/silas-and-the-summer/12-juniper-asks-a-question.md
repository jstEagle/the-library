## 12. Juniper Asks a Question

There is a particular silence a child deploys when she wants something, and Juniper had mastered it by age six. She deployed it now across the dinner table, chin propped on her fist, spaghetti cooling in front of her like evidence at a crime scene.

"Mom."

"Mm." I was reading the back of the olive oil bottle, which is a thing people do when they are hiding from questions. It contains monounsaturated fat and 14% of your daily iron. Riveting stuff. Read it twice.

"Why does Silas look at you like that?"

I set down the bottle with great care, as though it were a rare first edition rather than a $6.99 store brand. "Like what?"

"Like the sad parts of books."

I want it noted for the record that I did not drop anything, spill anything, or make any sound at all, which I consider my finest maternal achievement to date. Inside, however, something performed a maneuver I can only describe as a controlled demolition — floors going down one after another, quietly, professionally, no dust.

"What does that even mean," I said, aiming for baffled and landing somewhere closer to guilty.

"You know." She twirled her fork without eating. Eleven-year-olds can do this indefinitely; it's how they hold the floor. "Like when a character finds out something at the end of chapter thirty and they just *look* at somebody and you have to put the book down because your chest hurts. He does that. When he thinks you're not looking. And then when you *are* looking he does the other face."

"The other face."

"The customer service face." She said it with the flat contempt of her generation, who have apparently identified customer service as a distinct emotional category already. God help us all. "He doesn't do the customer service face with me. Just you."

"That's because you're charming and I'm a business associate."

"Mom."

"Juniper."

We regarded each other across the table. She has my eyes, which means I was essentially staring into a smaller, braver version of myself, one who hadn't yet learned that some questions are load-bearing and should not be pulled on.

"He knew you before," she said. It wasn't quite a question. It was a hypothesis wearing a question's coat. "Before we moved here. Before the shop. You guys do this thing where you finish each other's — "

"*Pass the salt,*" I said, too loudly, and she did not pass the salt, because there was no salt on the table, and we both knew it, and the silence that followed had a shape. It had edges. It sat between us like a third person who'd pulled up a chair.

"I'm eleven," she said, "not stupid."

"I've never once thought you were stupid."

"But you think I'm breakable."

And there it was. The exact center of the thing, hit on the first try, the way she always did — no warm-up, no windmill pitch, just the ball arriving at the plate. I opened my mouth to deny it and found nothing in the inventory that would serve as a denial. Because of course I think she's breakable. Of course I do. I have spent eleven years building a life around the premise that she is the one irreplaceable item in a shop full of replaceable ones, and I have handled her accordingly, and somewhere along the way she noticed the handling, the way children notice everything except where they left their shoes.

"You're not breakable," I said finally. "You're eleven. You're made of rubber and bad decisions about bedtime."

She almost smiled. Then she didn't. "He looks at you like he lost something," she said, and picked up her fork, and that was the end of the interrogation, because Juniper understands timing better than any novelist I've ever read. She let it land and walked away from the wreckage, whistling, while I sat there holding the olive oil like a shield.

* * *

After dishes — after the small liturgy of dishes, soap and water and the window over the sink going dark with evening — I went upstairs to not sleep.

Our apartment sits above the shop, three rooms and a slanted ceiling, and my bedroom window faces the street. If I stand at a certain angle I can see the awning of Wren & Sons Books, or whatever the sign says now that Silas has been sanding it down at odd hours, preparing it for a name that doesn't exist yet. That night the light was still on over there. A silhouette moved behind the glass, shelving or pacing or simply existing in his own shop at ten p.m., which is either dedication or avoidance, and I know from experience it can be both.

I got into bed. I turned off the lamp. I lay there.

Here is what happens when an accountant of the heart tries to fall asleep: she begins counting.

It's not a discipline I chose. It's a condition. Somewhere around twenty-three years old I learned that wanting things is expensive, and I have kept books on it ever since, and tonight the ledger opened itself whether I wanted it or not.

Item: the shop. Fourteen years of inventory and reputation and a lease I have paid early every single month since Juniper was born, because early payment is a kind of armor. The shop is not mine free and clear — it belongs to Mr. Aldous, my landlord, who is seventy-eight and fond of me in the way of a man who likes rent arriving like weather, reliably. The shop survives on maybe two hundred regular customers and a location that works because the block works, because the florist and the diner and the hardware store and, yes, the bookstore next door form an ecosystem, and ecosystems collapse from the edges inward. If Wren & Sons closes badly — if it closes the way chain stores close, with dumpsters and plywood and a year of dead windows — foot traffic dies, and I die with it, slowly, in quarterly increments.

Item: Juniper. Who has, in six weeks, acquired a Silas-shaped habit. He taught her chess on rainy Tuesdays. He ordered her a book from a distributor I don't use because it ships faster. Last week she came home saying "Silas says" the way she used to say "my friend Zoe says," casually, constantly, the way you say a name that has become part of the furniture of your days. I could end that. One conversation, one cold front moving through the shop, and he would withdraw — he's too decent to fight for access to a child whose mother asked him to step back. But then I'd be the one who took it away, and she'd remember that, at eleven, in the way children keep everything, filed under a heading she won't show me for twenty years.

Item: the street. Mrs. Okafor at the diner, who has fed me free coffee through two slow winters and one flood. Dev, who repairs our awnings and once drove forty minutes on a Sunday because my register died during a school fundraiser. The book club that meets Thursdays and pretends to discuss the novel. These people chose me, or chose the shop, or chose the idea of the little store that survived the Wren expansion — because that's the part I keep forgetting, the part that lives under everything like groundwater: ten years ago, Wren & Sons opened a branch here specifically to bury me, and this street watched it happen, and this street chose sides, and the sides were drawn in a way that never fully washed out. Half of them still call him "the Wren boy" in a tone you'd use for weather damage.

Item: me. Which is the line item I never enter, because I was trained out of it young, but the ledger insists, lying open in the dark: what stands to be lost is also the woman who stood at a window last Thursday watching him sand a sign at dusk and felt something so large and so simple that it frightened her more than bankruptcy ever has. Bankruptcy has procedures. This has none.

I rolled over. The light across the street went off.

Good, I thought. Sleep. Be a normal person sleeping at a normal hour in a normal town where nothing is complicated.

The dark said nothing back, which is the dark's whole strategy.

* * *

I want to tell you about the snowstorm now, briefly, because it keeps arriving uninvited and I've learned it's cheaper to let it visit than to bar the door.

Ten years ago I believed a specific story about that night, and I told it to myself so many times it wore smooth: Silas Wren came to my cabin, we were snowed in, we were twenty-three and stupid, and in the morning he left without saying goodbye because men like Silas collect moments and don't pay for them. That story served me. It built the shop. It built the rules. Measure everything, want nothing.

But I've spent this summer learning the other versions. Learning that he waited at the cabin until noon. That he wrote a letter and left it with the property manager, who — I found this out in June, from a chatty woman at the county office — quit that same week and moved to her sister's in Ohio, taking a shoebox of undelivered mail with her. My letter might be in a landfill outside Columbus. Some nights I think about driving there. Most nights I don't, because what would it say? What could a twenty-three-year-old Silas Wren have written that would survive contact with the decade that followed? His father gutting my margins. My mother's illness. Juniper. All the weight that landed on the word *us* until it stopped meaning anything a reasonable person could lift.

And yet.

And yet he looks at me like the sad parts of books, according to a child who reads enough to know.

* * *

Wednesday morning I came downstairs at six-forty to find Juniper already at the kitchen table, dressed, hair brushed, eating cereal with the focused calm of someone executing a plan.

"You're up early," I said.

"Silas gets to the shop at seven."

I stood very still in my own kitchen, holding a coffee mug I had not yet poured. "Does he."

"We're finishing the chess game. I have him in a fork situation." She said *fork situation* with relish. "He doesn't know yet. Don't tell him."

"Juniper. School doesn't start for an hour."

"So? You go in early every day. You always say the best hours are before anyone else shows up." She looked at me over her spoon, blue eyes, my eyes, weaponized. "You could walk with me."

Every parenting manual ever written agrees that you should not negotiate with a small hostage-taker, and every parent knows the manuals are wrong, because sometimes the hostage-taker is right and you simply lose.

So we walked down the summer street together, seven a.m., the light doing that thing it does in June when it makes even the dumpster behind the hardware store look cinematic. The shops were shuttered and quiet. Dew on the awnings. Somewhere a sprinkler ticking its idiot metronome. Juniper skipped the cracks, or pretended to, at eleven she's too old to skip and does it anyway, which is one of the ten thousand reasons I would burn down a city for her.

Silas was unlocking the side door when we arrived. He saw us coming and did the thing he does — straightened slightly, like a man reported for duty — and then saw Juniper's chess bag and relaxed into a grin.

"She ambushed me," he said.

"I have him in a fork," Juniper announced, sailing past him inside. "Don't go easy."

"I would never," he said, and meant it, and followed her in.

Which left me on the sidewalk, holding two coffees I had apparently bought at the diner without consciously deciding to, standing in front of the man I am not dating, at the hour when nobody watches, and he looked at me — not the customer service face, not the sad-parts-of-books face, something newer and worse, something hopeful — and said, "Morning, Orsen," in a voice that had clearly rehearsed being casual and failed.

"Morning," I said.

And then, because I am who I am, because the ledger never sleeps, I heard myself add: "Silas. Can I ask you something?"

"Anything."

"When you close the branch—" I stopped. Restarted. "What's the plan. After. For you."

The grin stayed on his face but something behind it shifted, a curtain moving in a house where nobody's home. "Why do you ask?"

"Juniper's invested in her chess partner. I like to know the shelf life of my daughter's attachments." It came out colder than I intended. Colder than I wanted. It came out exactly the way the old Orsen would have said it, the one from chapter one, the one who measures everything, and I watched it land on him like a bill presented at the wrong moment.

"The branch closes when it closes," he said carefully. "Then I'll figure out the rest."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got right now." And he held my eyes while he said it, which is either honesty or excellent training, and I genuinely could not tell, and that — that inability to tell, after a whole summer of almost-touches and shared deliveries and one night under an awning where he told me things about the snowstorm that rearranged my furniture — that was the worst part.

Inside, pieces clicked on a cardboard board. Juniper crowed. "*Fork.* Pay up, Wren."

He laughed and went in to pay up, and I stood on the sidewalk with two cooling coffees, one of which was now mine alone, and understood that I had just done the thing I do. Asked a question shaped like a wall. Built a little more containment around a heart that keeps escaping anyway.

That night I lay awake again, running the numbers one final time, and for the first time the arithmetic refused to balance. Because here's what the ledger couldn't hide anymore: the shop, the street, Juniper, me — I'd been counting them as separate losses, as if I could lose one and keep the others. But they weren't separate. They were one column. And the entry at the bottom, the total, the number I'd been avoiding since June, was this:

If Silas Wren leaves at the end of August without telling me — if he lets me find out the way I found out ten years ago, from an empty room and a missing letter — then I will lose all of it at once, because I will have learned that the one time I finally risked the whole heart, I did it with a man who was packing a suitcase the entire time.

Across the street, past midnight, his light was still on.

I watched the silhouette move, and move, and stop — and then, unmistakably, it stopped and turned toward the window. Toward my window. Stood there a long moment in the dark of his shop, looking out at nothing, or at the street, or — though I ducked below the sill too fast to confirm it, heart slamming like a kid with a flashlight — at me.

In the morning, I decided, I would ask him directly. No walls. No hypotheticals.

But the morning, when it came, brought something else instead: a cream envelope on the shop's doormat, heavy paper, embossed return address.

Wren & Sons Holdings, LLC. Attention: S. Wren, Branch Manager.

And it wasn't addressed to Silas.

It was addressed to me.
