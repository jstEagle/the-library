## 13. Compromise

The ledger didn't get any less damning overnight. I'd checked at midnight, and again at six, as if the numbers might have spent the dark hours renegotiating with themselves. They hadn't. The branch owed the bank forty-one thousand dollars, my father's plan for that debt was a fire sale, and I had spent the night learning a new skill: sleeping in an office chair with a legal pad on my chest like a failed knight.

By seven I'd filled three pages. Not with the closure schedule my father wanted — that was easy, a week of grim arithmetic — but with something else. A structure. An independent buyout, sketched in pencil because ink felt like a promise I hadn't earned. The building's lease ran through next spring. The inventory could be revalued, not liquidated. If someone bought the branch out from under Wren & Sons corporate — if *I* bought it, technically, though "technically" was doing heavy lifting on a salary of nothing — it could reopen in September as its own shop. No chain. No name above the door unless I paid for the privilege, which I wouldn't.

It was either a business plan or a very elaborate way of staying near Orsen Fairweather, and I genuinely could not tell you which. That was the trouble with wanting things. You lost the ability to audit your own motives.

* * *

My father called at nine, which meant he'd been awake since five composing himself into disappointment.

"The Hendersons' order," he said, without hello. "Did you cancel it?"

"I moved it to next month. We're still trading."

"You're still *winding down*. There's a difference, Silas, and you keep confusing the two." Papers shuffled on his end; I pictured his desk, the model of efficiency, everything squared to the desk's edges like the man himself. "The bank wants a timeline. I told them end of August."

"August," I repeated. The word went through me like a splinter I'd have to dig out later. "That's specific."

"That's *final*. Which is more than I can say for your attention lately. Margins are down another point. Are you even looking at the reports?"

I looked at the reports. I looked at them the way you look at an X-ray of something you already know is broken. "I'm looking at them, Dad."

"Good. Because this is your audition, son. Close this branch clean, and the regional directorship is yours. Thirty percent bump, city office, real authority. Men twice your age would kill for it."

He said *son* the way he said *margin*: as a unit of measurement. And God help me, some traitorous part of me still stood up straighter when he used it. Ten years of trying to be legible to that man, and here I was, pencil-sketched treason on a legal pad three feet from the phone.

"Understood," I said.

"And Silas? Whatever you're doing with the Fairweather woman's stock orders — stop it. I've seen the transfer logs. We don't subsidize the competition."

"We're not competing. She's not competition. She's—" I stopped myself one syllable from a cliff. "She's a neighbor."

A pause, long enough to contain an entire childhood of silences. "Neighbors," my father said, "are how businesses die. Watch yourself."

He hung up. I sat there holding the phone like it might apologize.

* * *

The morning passed in the particular purgatory of retail: a woman who needed a book about grief for her sister but couldn't say the word *grief*, a kid who read the first page of four different dragon novels with the gravity of a judge, a man who asked if we carried anything by "the guy who writes the ones with the sea." I sold him two Melvilles and felt like a fraud, because the whole time my hands were shelving stock my head was restructuring a company.

At noon I walked the twenty feet between our doors, which I had come to think of as the longest distance in town.

Fairweather Books smelled the way it always did — paper and lemon polish and something faintly like cedar that I suspected was Orsen herself, though I'd never had the courage to verify. She was behind the counter with her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, wrestling with a roll of packing tape that had declared war on her.

"Hold that," she said when she saw me, thrusting the tape toward me like a subpoena. "If it curls again I'm switching careers."

I held the tape. It curled anyway. We both watched it do so with a kind of professional respect.

"That's the third roll this month," she said. "Juniper thinks the shop is haunted. I'm starting to agree."

"Could be the ghost of retail past. They tend to haunt places where someone once made a profit."

She gave me a look over the counter — the look, the one that had been ruining my concentration since chapter one of my life — and then, unexpectedly, laughed. Short, surprised at itself, gone too fast. But real.

"What do you want, Wren?" she said, still sorting mail. "Besides free labor."

"Actually, I came to ask you a hypothetical."

Her hands stopped moving. In my experience, Orsen Fairweather treated hypotheticals the way customs officers treat unmarked luggage. "Define hypothetical."

"A question with no consequences. Purely theoretical. No stakes, no history, no—"

"No," she said. "In my experience, when a Wren says *hypothetical*, there are approximately nine consequences waiting outside in the truck."

"That was my father's hypotheticals. Mine are load-bearing."

Something flickered across her face — amusement fighting suspicion in a close match — and she leaned her hip against the counter, arms crossed, which was either an invitation or a fortification. With Orsen it was usually both.

"Fine," she said. "One hypothetical. Make it count."

I had rehearsed this. On the walk over, I had rehearsed it four times, each version less honest than the last. What came out was none of them.

"If a person did something ten years ago," I said, "something they've regretted in a low-grade, chronic way, like weather — and then they found themselves back in the same town, same street, same—"

"Same sentence," she said quietly.

"—same sentence," I agreed. "And they wanted to fix it. Not apologize — apologies are cheap, they're the packing tape of human interaction. Actually fix it. Is that a thing that can happen? Do second chances exist, or is that just something we print on bookmarks?"

The shop went very quiet. Somewhere in the stacks, the radiator ticked. Orsen looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide, visibly, whether to give me the truth or the wall. She'd been giving me the wall all summer, in beautiful masonry. But it was July now, and the mortar was showing cracks.

"Second chances exist," she said finally. "But they're not given. They're built. Like—" she gestured vaguely at her shelves, "—like a shelf. You don't get handed a shelf. You measure, you cut, you level it, you put weight on it gradually and see if it holds. Most people want the shelf without the measuring. They want to just set their whole collection down on air and act wounded when it crashes."

"And if the person has measured?" I said. "If they've been measuring for ten years?"

Her eyes came up sharp. "Then they should probably stop talking hypothetically," she said, "and start cutting wood."

For a second neither of us moved. The radiator ticked. The tape roll sat between us on the counter like a chaperone who'd fallen asleep.

"Anyway," she said, brisk again, armor back on piece by piece, "hypotheticals are free. Everything else in this shop has a price. Even the advice." She slid a book across the counter — I hadn't seen her reach for it, that was the trick of her, sleight of hand disguised as tidiness. It was a paperback, dog-eared, about small business accounting. "You left this in the café last week. Your margins are a disaster, by the way. Whoever does your books should be arrested."

"I do my books."

"Tragic," she said. "There's still time to save yourself."

I took the book. Our fingers didn't touch, which I noted, catalogued, and resented.

* * *

That afternoon I did something I'd been circling for days: I called Marcus Ellery, the accountant who'd handled my mother's estate, the one person in my father's orbit who answered honestly when asked a question.

"It's possible," he said, after I'd laid out the sketch. "Not pretty, but possible. You'd need the bank to release the branch's debt from the parent company's guarantee — which they'll consider, because right now that debt is a corpse they're feeding. You'd need capital. And you'd need your father not to find out until it's done, because the moment he knows, he'll sell the lease out from under you out of spite."

"How much capital?"

He gave me a number. It was most of what my mother had left me, sitting untouched since I was twenty-four because touching it felt like losing her twice. I thanked him and hung up and sat in the office chair looking at the number, thinking: *this is the exact shape of a decision.*

Here's what nobody tells you about compromise: it isn't finding the middle. The middle is where both sides lose gracefully. Real compromise is deciding which thing you can't live without and paying full price for it, in public, with witnesses.

I couldn't live without the shop next door being safe. I'd known it since the day I stepped off the truck and saw Orsen in the doorway of Fairweather Books with her arms crossed like a fortress that had learned to walk around. Everything since — the truce coffee, the delivery mix-up, the reading night where Juniper had sat cross-legged at Silas's feet while he did the dragon voice — had only confirmed it. I was not winding down a branch. I was standing in a burning building trying to carry out the furniture, and the furniture was a woman who would be furious if she knew.

Because that was the flaw in the plan, the hairline crack running through all three pages of pencil: to save her block, I had to deceive her. Again. Ten years ago I'd left without saying goodbye, and I'd told myself it was mercy. Now I was going to build her a rescue she never asked for, funded by money she didn't know existed, and call it love. Somewhere in that arithmetic there was a difference. I kept squinting at it, hoping it would resolve.

* * *

Closing time. I locked the register, turned the sign, and stood in the empty shop listening to the specific silence of a place that used to be busy. My father's voice in my head: *this is your audition.* The regional office. The title. The life I'd been auditioning for since I was nineteen years old, performing competence in a language he spoke fluently.

On the other side of the shared wall — I'd started being able to hear it, the soft thump of books being squared, the radio she played low after hours — Orsen was closing up too. Two shops breathing in the same rhythm, separated by drywall and a decade.

I picked up the phone before I could talk myself out of it.

"Fairweather Books," she said, in her customer voice, then, recognizing the silence, "Silas." Just my name, no armor on it, which was somehow worse.

"Hypothetical number two," I said. "No stakes. Purely theoretical."

"I'm counting these, you know. You're at your limit."

"If a person were going to stay — really stay, not wind-down stay, not until-August stay — what would they have to build first? For it to hold?"

The line went quiet. Through the wall, faintly, I heard her radio, some old song about starting over that the universe had clearly chosen for irony.

"Why do you ask," she said, and it wasn't quite a question.

"Because I'm tired of renting my life," I said. "And I'm looking at property."

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again, her voice had that careful flatness I'd learned meant she was protecting something fragile behind it.

"Silas. Whatever you're doing over there — whatever scheme, whatever penance, whatever *compromise* — you should know that I can't survive another person deciding what's best for me from a distance. I've had a decade of people making decisions about my life in rooms I wasn't in. So if you're building something, build it where I can see it. Or don't build it at all."

The line hummed. I looked down at the legal pad, three pages of pencil, the whole secret architecture of my summer.

"Understood," I said.

"Goodnight, Silas."

"Goodnight, Orsen."

I hung up and sat in the dark shop with the number from Marcus Ellery glowing on my screen, and understood, with total clarity, that I had exactly two options: tell her everything and risk the plan dying in daylight, or finish it in secret and become, permanently, the man who decided for her.

Outside, the streetlights came on. August was three weeks away, and my father expected a timeline.

I picked up the pencil.
