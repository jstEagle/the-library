## 17. Running Out of Summer

The way I found out was not dramatic. That's the insult of it. Ten years ago he at least had the decency to vanish in a snowstorm, which is practically operatic — whiteout, footprints filling in, a whole mythology available to a wounded twenty-three-year-old. This time it was a calendar.

Juniper's calendar, specifically. The one on the fridge where she tracks swim practice and library due dates with the ruthless competence of a child who has already decided she will be the organized one in the family, since clearly someone has to be.

"Mom," she said Tuesday morning, mouth full of toast, "when does Silas leave?"

I was holding a coffee mug and the mug was full and I thank whatever gods govern small kitchens that my hands were steady, because everything else in me went very quiet, the way a shop goes quiet when the door opens and you don't yet know if it's a customer or trouble.

"Leave?" I said. Casual. I have a casual voice. I've practiced it for ten years; it's load-bearing.

"For the city. He told Mrs. Okafor he'd be gone by the end of August. She asked if we could still do story hour after that and he said he didn't know." Juniper shrugged, one shoulder, thirteen going on forty. "Is it a secret? It seemed like maybe not a secret."

"It's not a secret," I said, to my daughter, while thinking: *it was absolutely a secret.*

The end of August. Six weeks out. Six weeks from a man who had spent July learning how I take my coffee (too much cream, don't judge), who had memorized which shelf Juniper's favorites lived on, who had stood under the awning in the rain and told me — told *me*, Orsen Fairweather, keeper of ledgers, auditor of hearts — that he'd rather have nothing than pieces. Who had taken the whole thing, every unguarded inch, two weeks ago in my apartment above the shop, and apparently scheduled his departure before he even arrived.

I drank my coffee. I kissed the top of Juniper's head. I walked down the stairs and opened the shop at nine sharp, because that is what I do, that is what I have always done: when the ground moves, you check the inventory.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being betrayed twice by the same person: the second time, you can't even enjoy the outrage properly, because there's a humiliating voice in your head doing arithmetic. *You knew this. You knew this about him. You wrote the rule about him. You broke the rule about him.* The first betrayal gets to be a tragedy. The second one is just a pattern, and patterns are the reader's fault as much as the author's.

He came in at eleven, carrying two coffees and that smile, the one that used to make me feel like the room had been waiting for him.

"There she is," he said. "I brought a peace offering for whatever mood you're in today. Yesterday you were 'wry with a chance of thunder.'"

"What am I today?"

He looked at me. He's good at looking; it's one of his weapons and I keep forgetting to disarm it. "Today you're doing inventory."

"I'm always doing inventory."

"You're doing inventory like a woman hiding behind a shelf." He set the coffee down. "Orsen. What's wrong?"

And here is where I should tell you what I wanted to say. What rose up in my throat like floodwater: *Mrs. Okafor says you're leaving at the end of August. Say it isn't true. Say you forgot to mention it because it doesn't matter, because you're staying, because this — us, the awning, the night, the ridiculous hope of it — say it weighs more than a promotion.*

What I actually said was: "Nothing. Busy day."

Because that's the other thing nobody tells you. When you've been left once without a goodbye, you don't ask for the goodbye. You refuse to audition for it. Some twisted pride thing — if I don't ask him to stay, then when he goes, at least I never begged. As if dignity were a comfort. As if dignity ever kept anyone warm at three in the morning.

He lingered. He did that thing where he straightened a display that didn't need straightening, touching the spines of the poetry like they were piano keys. "The buyout paperwork came through," he said. "For the branch. It's real now. Independent. Ours — mine, I mean. Wren's, but mine running it." He laughed a little, nervous, and God help me, some traitor part of my chest went soft at the sound. "I keep thinking I should frame the first page. Hang it next to the register. 'Wren & Fairweather Feud Officially Over.'"

"Mm," I said.

He waited. I shelved. Eventually he touched my elbow — lightly, a question in the shape of a touch — and I moved toward the stockroom as if summoned by duty, and he let me go, and I hated him a little for letting me go, and hated myself more for making him.

* * *

That afternoon I did something I'm not proud of, which is to say I eavesdropped.

He was on the sidewalk between our shops, phone pressed to his ear, and through the open door I caught the shape of the conversation the way you catch weather through a window. His voice did the thing it does when he's managing someone — warmer, faster, a performance of ease.

"No, Dad, I understand the timeline... Yes, I know what I signed... The end of August, I haven't forgotten." A pause. A laugh with no floor under it. "Because the buyout changes things, that's why. It means I'm not handing you the keys, it means—" Another pause, longer. "Fine. Fine. We'll talk when I'm in the city. I'll be there the first week of September. I said I'll be there."

The first week of September.

I stood there among my books — my fortress, my ledger, my life measured to the quarter-inch — and felt something in me do what old buildings do in earthquakes: not collapse, exactly. Just settle. Just shift half an inch off true, so that every door afterward hangs slightly wrong.

He hadn't told me. Not in the night we spent together, when the words were coming easy as breathing and he told me things I'm fairly sure he's never told anyone — about his mother, about the year he almost quit the family business, about the snowstorm, about what he thought I believed back then. He told me everything except the exit. And why would he? You don't mention the exit to the person you're standing inside with.

Ten years ago he left without saying goodbye. This time he'd found something worse: he stayed long enough to let me stop bracing.

* * *

I closed early that Thursday. Told myself it was for inventory, which was even technically true — I sat in the stockroom with a clipboard and counted nothing, moving my pen down columns of numbers that refused to mean anything.

Juniper came down around six, drawn by the smell of her mother marinating in silence. She's got a radar for it. Kids of quiet mothers always do.

"You're doing the face," she said, hopping onto a crate of unsold remainders. "The face where you look like a book with a sad ending that everyone pretends is literary."

"That's very specific."

"Aunt Dee says it." She picked at the crate's splintered edge. "Did you and Silas have a fight?"

"No."

"Are you going to?"

"Junie."

"He looks like a golden retriever that got left at the groomer's," she said. "Every time you walk past the window. I'm just saying. If you broke him, that's on you, and also he does really good dragon voices, so think about the cost-benefit."

I looked at my daughter — this sharp, funny, entirely too perceptive kid I have built my entire architecture around — and felt the fear arrive on schedule. Because here's the ledger entry I've been avoiding since June: it's not my heart I was protecting anymore. It hasn't been for weeks. Silas Wren knows how my daughter takes her hot chocolate. He knows she hides in the biography section when she's sad. He has a key to the rhythm of our days, and when he leaves at the end of August, he won't just take himself. He'll take the version of our life where the wall between the shops had started to feel like it was made of paper instead of brick.

She'd survive it. That's the terrible part — she would, kids do, they fold new losses into themselves like paper cranes and keep flying. But I would watch her do it, and I would know it was my fault for opening the door, and I have spent ten years making sure the only person whose disappointment I had to manage was me.

"Mom?" Juniper said. "You went somewhere."

"Just counting," I said. "Go start dinner. I'll be up in a minute."

She went. I sat in the darkening stockroom and did the math I'd been refusing to do: six weeks. He'd known for — how long? Since the offer came, whenever that was. Through the rain on the awning. Through the night. Through all those mornings he brought coffee and asked about my day like a man building something.

Or like a man enjoying the view before the train leaves.

* * *

Friday, Saturday, Sunday, I perfected the art of the polite stranger.

It's a skill. I want credit for it. I answered his knock on Monday with a smile calibrated to the decimal — warm enough to avoid questions, cool enough to revoke access. I discussed the street festival planning committee. I recommended a mystery series for a customer of his. I let him fix the sticky latch on my back door because he was already holding the screwdriver and refusing would have required a conversation, and conversations were off the table.

"You're somewhere else," he said, halfway through the latch, not looking up. "You've been somewhere else since Tuesday."

"I'm right here."

"You're right *here*," he agreed, tightening the last screw, "the way a book is right here on a shelf. Present. Catalogued. Closed."

"Silas—"

"I get it," he said, standing, brushing off his knees. Whatever he saw in my face made him stop. Made him go careful, the way you go careful near a bird with a broken wing that might actually just be furious. "Whatever this is, I get it. Take the time you need. I'm not going anywhere."

And that — that was the moment I nearly broke, because he said it kindly, sincerely, believing it, and it was the single least true sentence spoken on this block all summer.

I watched him go back through the connecting doorway — we'd been propping it open most days, an arrangement I now understood as temporary scaffolding — and I stood in my shop surrounded by fifty thousand stories about people who tell each other the truth, and I said nothing.

* * *

Wednesday night it rained, a hard summer rain that came sideways off the lake, and I lay awake listening to it hit the awning we'd shared in July and cataloguing losses, because that's what I do instead of sleeping. Item: one awning. Item: one future with a shared doorway. Item: one daughter's dragon voices. Item: one heart, previously contained, now out in the weather with no shelter scheduled.

Item, final: the knowledge that I had done this to myself. That I'd read this book before. That I'd held the ending in my hands at twenty-three and still checked it out again, older, supposedly wiser, and was somehow shocked — shocked — by the same last page.

Except.

Except that at two in the morning, in the honest country that only exists after midnight, a smaller voice spoke up. The one that sounds suspiciously like Juniper. *You didn't ask him,* it said. *You had six weeks of knowing and you spent them doing inventory. You turned yourself into a closed book and you're furious at him for not reading your mind through the cover.*

I hated that voice. I lay there hating it thoroughly and completely until four a.m., when it added its closing argument: *And you still don't know why he's leaving. You built the whole betrayal on a calendar note secondhand from a twelve-year-old. You audited a man on hearsay.*

Which was true. Which was worse than untrue, because it meant the way out wasn't anger. The way out was the one thing I'd sworn off at twenty-three and again at thirty-three: walking through the door and asking.

Thursday morning I watched him unlock the shop next door. He looked tired. He looked like a man carrying boxes no one could see. He looked, God help me, like the sad parts of books.

I made two coffees. Too much cream in both. I carried them to the threshold of the connecting door, and I stood there with my hand on the frame, and I opened my mouth to finally, finally ask him —

— and that's when I saw, through his front window, the stacks of flattened cardboard. The empty shelves. The first boxes already taped shut and labeled in his handwriting, *CITY — DO NOT SHIP,* lined up by the door like passengers waiting for a train.

He'd started packing.

And he still hadn't said a word.
