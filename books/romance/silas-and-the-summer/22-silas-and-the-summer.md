## 22. Silas and the Summer

**Orsen — September**

The wall came down on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays are for demolition if you're the kind of person who believes in symbolism, which I am not, except apparently I am now, because I stood there with a crowbar in my hands and my heart doing something unscheduled.

Silas swung first. He'd insisted. "I've spent ten years building walls," he said, "let me take one down." It was the most romantic thing anyone had said to me in a decade, and it was about drywall, which tells you everything about where my standards had landed.

The dust was tremendous. Juniper supervised from a safe distance with a dust mask pulled up over her nose like a tiny bandit, offering commentary. "You missed a spot." "That's load-bearing, probably." "Mom, your face is doing the thing."

"The thing?"

"The soft thing. The one you do at him."

I did not confirm this. But she'd caught me, so I turned it into something useful: "Go paint the sign."

She'd been designing it all summer on butcher paper taped to the stockroom door — *Wren & Fairweather Books*, though we'd fought about the ampersand for a week. Silas wanted *and*. I wanted nothing between our names at all, which he called "aggressively romantic" and I called "typographically honest." We compromised on the ampersand because Juniper pointed out that it looked like two people leaning against each other, and neither of us had an argument for that.

When the last of the wall came down, the two shops breathed together for the first time. His tall windows met my crooked shelves. His father's ghost lingered in the carpet; my mother's ghost lived in the floorboards. Between them, a doorway wide enough for two people to walk through side by side without turning their shoulders.

Silas looked at me through the new opening, gray with dust, grinning like a man who'd just committed a beautiful crime.

"Well," he said.

"Well," I said.

We shook hands, formally, absurdly, and then he pulled me through the doorway and kissed me in front of God and Juniper and the entire inventory of two bookshops, and I let him, which is the whole story, really. Everything after this is just the letting.

**Silas — October**

My father's letter arrived on a Thursday, in an envelope heavy enough to be an apology or a lawsuit.

I let it sit on the counter for three days. Orsen noticed — she notices everything, it's her superpower and her curse — but she didn't ask, which is her other superpower: knowing when not to.

On Sunday I opened it at the kitchen table while she read across from me, close enough that our knees touched under the table, which is how we do most things now. Side by side, touching, pretending to be occupied.

*Son,* the letter began. My father has never once used my name in writing. Names are concessions, and he does not concede cheaply.

*I have reviewed the buyout structure you sent. It is reckless. It is sentimental. It will likely fail.*

I kept reading.

*It is also, your mother would have told you, the first thing you have ever done that reminds me of myself at your age, before I learned to be careful.*

There was more — figures, terms, a lawyer's name, the skeleton of a détente dressed in the language of due diligence. At the bottom, no signature. Just his initials, pressed hard enough to emboss the page. Even his surrender was abbreviated.

"He's coming around," Orsen said, without looking up from her book.

"He's negotiating."

"With you? Same thing."

I laughed, and she smiled at her pages, and I understood that this was what my father could never learn: that the best deals are the ones where everybody ends up holding less than they wanted and more than they needed.

I wrote back that night. Four drafts. In the end I kept it short: *Thank you. The shop opens its shared door next month. There's a chair by the window with your name on it, if you ever want to see what reckless looks like up close.*

He never answered. But in November, a delivery truck arrived with forty boxes of inventory — remaindered stock, good titles, no invoice. The packing slip said only: *S.W.* Two men named Wren, signing their names the same way, meaning entirely different things.

**Orsen — December**

Here is what nobody tells you about merging your life with someone: it is mostly logistics wearing a trench coat.

We argued about the thermostat. We argued about whose ordering system survived (mine) and whose got retired (his, mournfully, with full military honors). We argued about whether the romance section should face the door — he said yes, visibility; I said no, privacy, some things shouldn't be on display — and then we both heard ourselves and started laughing so hard we had to sit down among the biographies.

Juniper turned eleven in December. She asked for a party in the shop after hours, cake among the shelves, her friends running wild through fiction like small beautiful looters. Silas performed voices during the reading of her favorite book — he did the dragon, and the dragon sounded suspiciously like his father, low and imperious, and Juniper laughed until she couldn't breathe.

I watched him from behind the register and did the math I used to do automatically — the cost of this, the risk of this, the exact exchange rate between joy and eventual ruin — and found, for the first time in ten years, that the numbers wouldn't come. The ledger in my chest had gone quiet. Not balanced. Just... closed.

After the party, sweeping up cake crumbs at midnight, he said, "You're different."

"I'm tired."

"You're different when you're tired, too. Softer. Like a book with a broken spine that finally lies flat."

"That's the least flattering metaphor anyone has ever used on me, and I've been reviewed online."

"And yet." He set down the broom and crossed to me. "You know what I keep thinking? That night in the snow, ten years ago. I thought it was the best mistake of my life. Turns out it wasn't a mistake. It was a first draft."

"First drafts get thrown away."

"Not the ones worth rewriting."

I kissed him then, flour still on my hands, and left handprints on his back like evidence. Let the record show.

**Silas — March**

Spring came late and rude, the way it does here, and with it came the anniversary of nothing in particular and everything altogether.

Eleven years since the snowstorm. One year since I drove back into town with a truck full of my father's failures and a plan to leave by August. I'd meant to stay four months. I'd packed accordingly. Some of my clothes are still in boxes in the apartment above the old branch, now the annex, now the children's section, now the place where Juniper holds court on Saturdays, recommending books to customers twice her age with the confidence of a mob boss.

Orsen found me standing in that room one evening, looking at the unpacked boxes.

"You never finished unpacking," she said.

"I was waiting to be sure."

"And now?"

"Now I'm buying a dresser."

She leaned against the doorframe — my doorframe, technically, though the concept of mine and hers had been quietly dissolved somewhere around the ampersand — and studied me with those eyes that audit everything.

"You know what I did last week?" she said. "I threw out the old ledgers. The ones from the bad years. Ten years of numbers, every margin I scraped, every month I almost didn't make. Gone. Recycling."

I felt my stomach drop on her behalf. "Orsen—"

"I kept one page." She held it out. It was the first ledger page from the year she opened the shop, her handwriting young and fierce, every figure double-underlined. At the bottom, in the margin, she'd written: *Measure everything. Want nothing.*

"I don't write that anymore," she said. "So the page didn't belong anywhere. But I couldn't throw away who I was when I wrote it. She got me here."

I took the page and read it and understood that I was married to the bravest accountant in the world — not yet married on paper, but married in every way that requires daily signatures — and that she had just done the hardest thing in any ledger: written off a loss without pretending it hadn't happened.

"What do you write instead?" I asked.

She took the page back, folded it once, put it in her pocket like a talisman. "Nothing," she said. "These days I mostly just want things. It's very inefficient. I recommend it."

**Orsen — June**

One year since the movers unloaded Wren & Sons next door and my heart did its cold, controlled collapse. One year since Silas stepped out of that truck unchanged and impossible, and I thought: *not again, never again, absolutely again.*

We celebrated the way we celebrate everything now — badly, with cake, in public. Juniper made a banner: **HAPPY ANNIVERSARY OF THE SNOWSTORM THAT RUINED AND SAVED EVERYTHING**, which we hung over the merged doorway and which confused exactly everyone, including us, correctly.

Customers came. Mrs. Okafor, who'd taken my side during the Paper Cuts months and never fully apologized for it, brought flowers and pretended they were for the shop. The landlord — my landlord, our landlord, Silas's tenant-relationship, it's complicated — shook Silas's hand for a beat too long, the handshake of two men who have agreed to respect each other without liking each other, which is most of civilization if you're honest.

And in the evening, after closing, Silas and I sat in the window chairs — his and hers, facing outward, watching the street — and he said, "Do you ever think about that version of you? The one who locked the door on me that first morning and never opened it again?"

"All the time. She had excellent reasons."

"She did. She was also wrong."

"She was scared." I turned my chair toward his. "There's a difference. Wrong is an error. Scared is information."

"And now?"

"Now I'm still scared. Most days. But I stopped treating fear as a verdict." I reached over and took his hand, right there in the window, where anyone could see, because that was the point. "Fear's just data now. And I've decided what it's data about."

"What?"

"How much there is to lose. Which is another way of saying how much there is."

He squeezed my hand and didn't say anything, because he's learned that about me — that I occasionally say a true thing and need it to stand there unattended for a moment, like a deer at a clearing's edge, before it will come all the way out.

**Silas — August**

The promotion letter arrived in August, eleven months late, forwarded from my old city address. *Position withdrawn,* it said, as if I'd ever been holding it. As if I'd ever, even for a day, been holding anything but this street, this woman, this ridiculous merged kingdom of paper and dust and Juniper's hand-painted signs.

I burned it in the stockroom sink. Orsen walked in mid-conflagration, raised one eyebrow, and handed me the fire extinguisher without comment, which is marriage, basically.

"Feel better?" she asked.

"Immensely."

"Good. Because I have news." She was trying not to smile, which on her face looks like weather about to break. "The building next to the annex — the one with the empty upstairs — the owner wants to sell. Cheap. Juniper thinks we should buy it and knock through and make a reading room with skylights."

"Juniper thinks we should buy it."

"Juniper has excellent instincts. She gets them from me."

"We can't afford it."

"We can't afford *not* to. That's the whole lesson of the last year." She pulled the listing from her apron pocket, already creased from handling, already annotated in her fierce handwriting. "Look. I ran the numbers. Real ones. And then I stopped running them and looked at the space, and I could see it — kids on the floor, light coming down, your father in the corner chair pretending he's just passing through—"

"He'd never."

"He might. People surprise you. I'm the case study."

I looked at the listing. I looked at her. Somewhere below us, Juniper was teaching a customer's toddler to say "ampersand," incorrectly, adorably.

"When?" I said.

"Tomorrow. The owner's showing it at nine."

"Then tomorrow at nine."

She kissed me, tasting faintly of ambition and coffee, and went downstairs to close up, and I stood in the stockroom amid the ash of a life I almost chose and felt the strangest sensation: not relief, not even happiness exactly, but *belonging* — which turns out not to be a prize you win once, at the auction, at the altar, at the end. It's a practice. You show up for it daily. You sweep its floors. You argue about its thermostat. You buy the next building, and the one after that, and you keep choosing it in the morning, and it keeps choosing you back, inefficiently, extravagantly, without a single number in the margin.

Downstairs, Orsen was locking the door. Through the glass I watched her turn the sign — *Wren & Fairweather Books, Open Tomorrow* — and I went down to help her, because that's the job now, and there is no better one, and I intend to keep it for the rest of my life.
