## 13. The Unread Chapter

The envelope sat on my kitchen table like something that had died there.

Silas had sent it by courier—*courier*, because my brother had never once in his life trusted the postal service, or the weather, or other people's hands—with a note in his slanted, impatient script: *Read it before you decide what I am. That's all I ask. —S.*

That's all he asked. As if it were small. As if a man could hand you a bound manuscript with your own life inside it and call the asking of it *small*.

I hadn't touched it since Tuesday. It was now Friday morning, and the envelope and I had developed an arrangement: it sat there being unread, and I sat there drinking coffee and pretending I wasn't thinking about it, and we were both very dignified about the whole thing.

"You're staring at it again," said Dot, my shop cat, from her position on the windowsill. She didn't say this in words, obviously. She said it in the particular way she had of looking at me over her shoulder, which after eleven years of cohabitation I had learned to translate fluently.

"I'm not staring at anything," I told her. "I'm drinking coffee."

"You're drinking coffee *at* it."

She had me there. I was drinking coffee at it the way you drink coffee at an ex-boyfriend's car parked outside your house—at it, not with any hope of enjoyment.

The problem was this: I knew what was in the envelope. Not the words—I couldn't know the words—but the shape of them. Wendell Fairweather had written it, and Wendell Fairweather wrote beautifully, and beautiful writing about the worst thing that ever happened to me felt less like a gift than like a second theft, more elegant than the first. Silas had stolen my story when we were young and hungry and he'd needed a book and I'd needed him to love me more than he loved his career. He'd gotten a bestseller out of it. I'd gotten seven years of silence and a mother who still set two extra places at holidays out of habit and grief and stubbornness, depending on the day.

And now here came the apology, typeset and bound, and everyone—the dying brother, the ghostwriter with his careful eyes—wanted me to *read it*. To do the emotional labor of receiving it. To sit down like a good girl and consume my own pain as literature.

"No," I said to Dot, and to the envelope, and to the general injustice of the universe.

Dot yawned. She'd heard worse arguments.

* * *

The bell above the bookshop door did its little cheerful jangle at four o'clock, which was how I knew it wasn't a customer, because customers came in at hours that made sense, and the people in my life lately arrived exactly when I was weakest—mid-afternoon, low blood sugar, the light going gold and forgiving through the front windows.

Wendell stood in the doorway holding his hat, which he did when he was nervous, turning the brim like a man winding a watch that kept terrible time.

"We're closed," I said.

"The sign says open."

"The sign is aspirational."

He came in anyway. Seven years ago I would have found that maddening. Now I found it—God help me—a little bit wonderful, and I hated myself for it, and I hated the envelope on my kitchen table for making everything complicated, and I hated Silas for dying slowly enough to arrange all of this but not quickly enough to spare us the arranging.

"Marlowe." He said my name the way he always had, like it was a sentence he was afraid to finish. "Did you get it?"

"Got what?" I said, because I was thirty-four years old and apparently still capable of pettiness.

"The chapter."

"Oh, *that*." I straightened a stack of paperbacks that did not need straightening. "Yes. It's around."

"It's around."

"In the vicinity of my apartment. We don't interact."

Wendell set his hat on the counter with the care of a man disarming himself. "Marlowe."

"Don't say my name like that."

"Like what?"

"Like you're about to be reasonable. I'm not in the mood for reasonable. Reasonable is how they get you."

Something moved through his face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way light moves under ice. "You know," he said, "for someone who runs a business, you have a remarkable hostility to negotiation."

"I run a *bookshop*. Negotiation is for people selling things. I'm just the last stop before the books go home with somebody and become their problem."

He laughed then, briefly, and I let myself have it, the sound of it, the way I used to hoard small coins of him back when we were twenty-six and broke and certain. Then it faded, and we were left standing in my shop with the truth between us like a piece of furniture neither of us wanted to admit we'd both bumped into.

"He wants you to read it," Wendell said quietly. "Before the memorial. Before the publisher finalizes. He wants your—" He stopped. Started again. "He wants forgiveness, Marlowe. And he wants it while he can still hear it."

"And if I don't give it?"

"Then he dies without it."

The words landed in the quiet of the shop like a dropped glass. Somewhere in the back, the radiator ticked. Dot emerged from behind the register, assessed Wendell with her ancient yellow eyes, and—traitorously—wound once around his ankles before returning to her post.

"He stole my life," I said. My voice came out steadier than I expected, which was good, because inside I was anything but. "Do you understand that? Everyone keeps talking about it like it's a misunderstanding. A *rupture*. Like we're two countries with a border dispute. He took the worst year of my life—the year I lost—" My throat closed. I pushed past it; I'd had practice. "He took it and he made it *pretty*. He gave it a plot arc and a redemptive ending and a heroine who forgives everybody by page three hundred, and the world read it and wept and called him brave. Brave. For wearing my grief as a costume."

"I know."

"Do you? Because you're writing his memoirs, Wendell. You're—you're the tailor now. You're fitting him for a new costume, and this time it's called *remorse*, and it'll fit even better, because you're better at this than he ever was."

The moment I said it I wished it unsaid. It was cruel, and it was aimed, and cruelty aimed is a different animal entirely from cruelty spilled. But Wendell didn't flinch. That was the thing about him that had undone me at nineteen and was undoing me again at thirty-four: he absorbed blows like a man who believed, somewhere deep down, that he deserved them.

"Maybe I am," he said. "Better at it, I mean. Maybe that's the whole problem with me."

"Wendell—"

"No. Let me—" He pressed his palms flat on the counter, and I saw that his hands weren't steady. "I've been sitting in that house for weeks listening to him talk about you. About the book. About what he did. And every word goes through me, Marlowe. Every sentence I write, I'm the one shaping it, choosing it, making it land. If it hurts you—and it will, it's built to hurt, the true ones always are—then some part of that hurt passes through my hands first. I've known that since the beginning and I told myself it was his story to tell. His guilt, his confession, his risk. I was just the pen."

"You were more than the pen."

"Yes," he said simply. "I was more than the pen. And I think you know why I stayed anyway."

I did know. The knowing sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. He'd stayed because of me—because taking the contract had been the only legitimate excuse he'd ever find to be near my orbit again, seven years after I'd walked out of his apartment with a box of books and a lie about needing space. He'd stayed and written the weapon, and now the weapon was pointed at both of us.

"So what do we do?" I asked. My voice cracked on *we*, and I pretended it hadn't, and he pretended with me, because that was another thing we'd always been good at: mutual pretending, a duet.

"That's what I came to say." He reached into his jacket and pulled out a folded sheet of paper, laid it on the counter between us, and turned it to face me. It was a letter. His letterhead—*Wendell Fairweather, Editorial Services*—which was itself a kind of joke, a ghost advertising ghostwork. "It's a withdrawal notice. Effective immediately. I walk away from the contract."

The shop went very still. Or maybe I went very still, and projected it outward onto the shelves and the radiator and the dust motes hanging in the gold light.

"You can't," I said.

"I can. There's a clause. Confidentiality survives termination, so I can't tell you what's in the chapter—but I don't have to write it either. I've been re-reading the agreement all week. I drafted this Tuesday night, after the courier came. I've been carrying it around waiting for—" He gestured vaguely at me, at the shop, at the whole impossible situation. "Waiting for the right moment, which I realize now doesn't exist, so: this is the moment. It's not the right one. It's just the one we've got."

"Wendell." I picked up the letter. It was real. His signature at the bottom, that old-fashioned looping hand, the one I'd watched across a hundred café tables back when we were poor and happy. "This is the biggest contract of your career. You told me—it's going to set you up, it's the first time anyone's paid you like a *person* instead of a machine—"

"It's money."

"It's not just money and you know it. It's your name finally getting *close* to the light. It's the door. You've spent fifteen years writing other people's books and pretending you don't want your own, and this was—"

"This was what?" He smiled, and it was a tired smile, an honest one. "A memoir I'd never sign? Marlowe, listen to me. I have spent my entire adult life disappearing into other people's stories because disappearing was safe. Because if nobody sees you, nobody can leave you." His voice caught, just slightly, on *leave*, and we both heard it, and neither of us flinched away. "I thought that was humility. I think it might just be fear with good posture. And I am not going to stand in your brother's house polishing a confession you can't bear to read, collecting a check for it, calling that loyalty. Loyalty to whom? To a dead man's deadline?"

"To yourself," I whispered. "Be loyal to yourself for once. Take the money. Finish the book. Don't you dare burn down your own future to make a point about mine."

"It's not a point. It's a choice." He tapped the letter once, gently. "There are two versions of the next month. In one of them, I finish the memoir, it publishes, it's brilliant, everyone says so, and you never speak to me again, and I deserve that. In the other one, I walk away, Silas is furious, the publisher scrambles, and I'm standing in a bookshop watching you decide whether you can forgive either of us. I know which version I want to live in. It's not complicated."

"It's incredibly complicated."

"It's really not." He almost laughed. "That's the joke. Fifteen years of complicated contracts and clauses and subclauses, and the simplest thing I've ever done is fold a piece of paper in half."

I looked down at the letter. Withdrawal of services, effective immediately, terms per section 11.4. All those years of his voice living inside other people's sentences, and here it was on his own stationery, plain and warm and unmistakably *him*, and it was being offered up like something on an altar.

Because of me. Partly because of me. And partly, I suspected, because of the same sickness Silas carried and I carried and maybe everyone in our ruined little constellation carried: the belief that love has to be proven through sacrifice, that the bigger the wound you give yourself, the truer the feeling.

"No," I said.

Wendell blinked. "No?"

"No. Put it away." I slid the letter back across the counter. "I'm not going to be the reason you quit. Do you hear me? I have watched men ruin themselves *at* me my whole life—my father, my brother, half the poets who came through that poetry reading series I ran at twenty-three. I will not add you to the list. I refuse."

"Marlowe—"

"I haven't decided anything. About the chapter. About him. About—" I gestured between us, the great unnameable *us*, seven years wide and one fake date deep. "But whatever I decide, I'm deciding it without holding your career hostage. That's not forgiveness, Wendell. That's just a different kind of theft."

For a long moment he didn't move. Then he took the letter back, folded it along its existing creases, and put it in his jacket pocket—over his heart, I noticed, though I chose not to notice.

"Then we're at an impasse," he said.

"We're at an impasse."

"I withdraw the withdrawal."

"Noted for the record."

He retrieved his hat from the counter, and I watched him turn the brim once more, and I understood suddenly that he was stalling—that neither of us wanted the bell to ring behind him, that the shop with its gold light and its sleeping cat had become, over these strange weeks, the closest thing either of us had to a place where the truth could breathe.

"Will you read it?" he asked at the door. Quietly. No pressure in it, which somehow made it heavier. "Not for him. Not yet. Just—when you're ready. It's good, Marlowe. It's the best thing I've ever written, and I hate that, because the best thing I've ever written is your pain, and I'd give it back if I could."

The bell jangled him out into the late afternoon, and I stood there among my books—all those lives, all those confessions, shelved and spine-out and patient—and I realized my hands were shaking.

I locked up early. I walked home. The envelope was where I'd left it, patient as a grave.

I got as far as sliding one finger under the flap. Paper whispered against paper. And then the phone rang—my mother, according to the screen, which was strange, because my mother called on Sundays and lectured on Wednesdays and today was Friday, off-schedule entirely, which in the language of Edith Fairweather meant only one thing:

She knew something.

I looked at the envelope. I looked at the phone. Outside my window, the streetlights came on one by one, like witnesses.

I answered.
