## 19. What the Will Left Behind

The lawyer's office smelled of lemon polish and old carpet, the way every lawyer's office in the world seemed contractually obligated to smell. Marlowe sat in a leather chair that was trying very hard to be comfortable and not quite managing it, with her hands folded in her lap like a schoolgirl waiting for a grade.

Wendell sat beside her. Not touching her, but close enough that she could feel the warmth of him through the space between their arms—a distance measured in inches and in everything they hadn't yet said.

They had been saying other things, though. In the two weeks since the hospital, since the last chapter, since Silas had closed his eyes on a Tuesday afternoon with the window open and the smell of rain coming in—they had been talking. Slowly. Carefully. Like people carrying something breakable across an icy parking lot.

"Ms. Fairweather. Mr.—ah." The lawyer, a dry man named Prosser with spectacles that magnified his eyes into an expression of permanent mild surprise, consulted his folder. "Mr. Fairweather. Forgive me. I understood from Mr. Vane's instructions that you would both be here."

"You understood correctly," Marlowe said.

Prosser nodded and opened the folder. "Then let's begin. I'll warn you, Mr. Vane wrote much of this himself. He refused my drafts. Said—" Prosser cleared his throat, and for a moment the professional mask slipped and something almost tender moved behind it. "He said he'd spent enough of his life letting other people say things for him."

Marlowe felt Wendell go still beside her. She reached over without deciding to and put her hand on his knee. He covered it with his own.

"To begin," Prosser read. "'I, Silas Edmund Vane, being of sound mind and unsound schedule—'"

A laugh escaped Marlowe before she could stop it, sharp and startled, and then it turned into something else halfway out, and Wendell's arm came around her shoulders, and she let it.

* * *

The estate business took forty minutes. The house on Halloway Hill went to a foundation for emerging writers, which surprised no one who had ever heard Silas complain about his own early luck. His royalties were split between the same foundation and a literacy charity in the town where they'd grown up. There were specific bequests—the first-edition Dickens to his old professor, absurdly, because the man had once told him he'd never amount to anything and Silas had never forgiven or forgotten it; his father's watch to a nephew Marlowe barely knew.

And then Prosser turned a page, adjusted his spectacles, and said, "Now we come to the part he made me read aloud twice at our last meeting, to make sure I wouldn't soften it."

Marlowe's heart did something complicated.

"'Item four,'" Prosser read. "'I establish the Vane Literary Trust, to hold all rights, unpublished manuscripts, correspondence, and remaining obligations pertaining to my work. The trust shall be administered by two co-trustees, serving jointly, neither able to act without the other.'"

He looked up. "Ms. Fairweather. You are named as the first co-trustee."

The room tilted slightly. "Me," Marlowe said. It wasn't a question so much as a refusal to believe the word had been aimed at her.

"You," Prosser confirmed. "He was specific. He said—and I quote his exact wording—'My sister knows better than anyone what my stories cost and what they're worth. She'll guard the price.'"

Marlowe stared at her hands. Seven years ago, Silas had taken her life—her worst year, her private grief—and turned it into his masterpiece without asking. And now he'd left her the keys to all of it. Trust, Silas had called it, in the end. Not the kind you feel. The kind you build, plank by plank, after you've burned the bridge.

"And the second trustee?" Wendell asked quietly.

Prosser smiled—or attempted to; the expression sat oddly on his face, like a hat borrowed from a larger man. "That brings us to item five." He found his place again. "'Item five. The memoir presently titled *Silas and the Vow* remains unfinished. Its final chapters exist only in fragments and in the memory of its ghostwriter, who is, I am told, the only person alive who heard the whole of it. I therefore direct the trust to engage one Wendell Fairweather to complete the memoir.'"

Marlowe stopped breathing.

"'Not as a ghost,'" Prosser continued, his voice growing careful, the way voices do when they approach something sacred. "'Under no circumstances as a ghost. Under his own name. Full byline, full credit, full share of royalties as negotiated by the trust—which is to say, by my sister, whom I trust to negotiate harder than any lawyer I ever hired. If Mr. Fairweather declines, the memoir dies unfinished, and I want it noted in the record that this was my choice, freely made, and that I would rather leave one book half-said than have it finished by someone whose name isn't on it.'"

Prosser set down the folder. "There's a postscript. Handwritten. He insisted I attach it verbatim."

He handed a single sheet across the desk—not a copy, the original, in Silas's slanted, impatient hand, the hand she'd watched fill notebooks at the kitchen table when they were young, before everything.

Marlowe read it aloud, because it seemed meant to be heard.

"*Wendell—I know you'll try to refuse. You refuse beautifully; it's practically your genre. So let me make it difficult for you. You spent your career putting words in other people's mouths and calling it honesty. This is your chance to do the reverse: put your own name on someone else's truth and call it love. That's what the book is, if you haven't figured it out yet. It was always about the vow. The one I broke, and the one you two kept making and unmaking. Finish it. Sign it. And then—for God's sake, man—ask her properly. I'm tired of watching you two circle each other like it's a dance competition and nobody brought music.*

"—S.V."

Silence. The lemon-polish smell. The sound of Marlowe's own heartbeat.

She became aware, distantly, that she was crying, and that Wendell was crying too, silently, the way men cry when they've had practice doing it alone, and that his hand had found hers again and was holding on like the paper might blow away and take them with it.

"He knew," she managed. "At the end, he knew about us. About everything."

"He knew before the end," Wendell said. His voice was rough. "I think he knew before I did."

* * *

They walked out into a gray-bright afternoon, the kind of light that makes even parking lots look painted. They stood on the courthouse steps for a while without speaking, the letter in Marlowe's bag, weighing approximately nothing and several tons at once.

"The memorial reading is Saturday," Wendell said finally. "The publisher wants a date for the manuscript. They're being—surprisingly gentle about it, actually. Edith called me yesterday and said, and I quote, 'Take whatever time grief requires, but grief has a deadline, because books do too.'"

"That sounds like Edith."

"It is entirely Edith." He turned to face her. "Marlowe. I have to ask you, and I need you to actually hear the question and not just answer it fast, because you answer things fast when you're scared, it's one of my favorite things about you and also occasionally a problem."

She raised an eyebrow. "Ask."

"Do you want me to finish it? Not Silas. Not the trust. Not the legal machinery of item five. You. Because I will sign my name on that book or I will burn it, whichever you tell me—but it has to be your tell, not his. He doesn't get to arrange us from beyond the grave. We've had enough of other people writing our story."

Marlowe looked at him for a long moment. The man she'd walked away from. The man who'd broken a confidentiality clause for her. The man who'd sat in a hospital chair for eleven hours straight while she slept against his shoulder, and never once mentioned it.

"I keep thinking," she said slowly, "about what it cost you. The contract. Your biggest job. You gave it up in a hallway like it was nothing."

"It wasn't nothing."

"No. I know." She took a breath. "So here's my answer, and I'm going to answer it slow, on purpose, so you know I mean it." She stepped closer. "Finish the book. Put your name on it. Not because Silas asked. Because that last chapter—the apology—he didn't write that alone. I've read enough of your ghosted prose to know your fingerprints. He couldn't have said any of it without you translating him to himself. That book is already half yours. It's time the spine admitted it."

Wendell exhaled, long and unsteady, like a man setting down luggage he'd carried across a continent.

"And also," Marlowe added, "you should probably ask me properly. He asked. It seems rude to leave a dying man's request unanswered."

"Is that a yes in advance?"

"It's a 'stop stalling, Fairweather.' Which, historically, is how all my yeses start."

He laughed—wet and real—and pulled her in, and they stood on the courthouse steps of a small gray town, holding each other next to a bronze plaque about municipal parking, while inside a dead novelist's letter cooled in a canvas bag and waited to change everything.

* * *

Saturday came too fast, the way Saturdays attached to funerals always do.

Marlowe dressed carefully: the blue dress, the one her mother had once called "funeral-appropriate and little else," which today felt like exactly right. Her mother arrived at the shop at noon to help set up chairs, because Eleanor Fairweather's grief language was logistics, and in the past two weeks Marlowe had learned to read it fluently.

Her mother arranged folding chairs with military precision and then paused, hands on hips, surveying the room.

"Your brother," she said, "had terrible taste in most things and perfect taste in endings."

Marlowe blinked. That was, by Fairweather standards, a eulogy.

"He told me, you know. Last spring. Called me out of nowhere, talked for two hours about weather and wine, and then at the very end said, 'Mother, I stole from Marlowe, and I'm going to give it back, and I need you not to make it hard.'" Her mother smoothed a chair cover that didn't need smoothing. "I said, 'Silas, when have I ever made anything hard?'"

Marlowe laughed despite herself. "What did he say?"

"He said, 'You'll find out,' and hung up." Her mother's mouth twitched. "Insufferable man. Brilliant insufferable man. Sit down, darling, you're hovering, and the caterer is terrified of you."

By four o'clock the shop was full. Readers, neighbors, three of Silas's editors looking uncomfortable in a room without leather chairs, the nephew with the watch, Professor Okafor clutching the Dickens. Wendell stood near the front with a stack of programs, and every few minutes Marlowe caught him looking at her, and every few minutes she pretended not to catch him, and both of them were smiling about it, which was its own kind of conversation.

Edith from the publisher took the makeshift podium first. "Silas Vane hated memorials," she announced. "He said they were 'novels with the plot removed.' So instead of a memorial, we're going to do what he actually wanted, which is a reading. Of a book that isn't finished yet. Which he would have found hilarious."

Then she stepped aside, and looked directly at Wendell.

Marlowe saw it happen—the moment the room's attention swung to him, the moment he understood what Edith had arranged, the moment his eyes found hers across the crowded shop with a question in them that wasn't really a question anymore.

Because there, on the front table, stacked in a neat, deliberate pile, were advance copies of the unfinished manuscript. And on the cover, where a ghostwriter's anonymity used to live, was printed a name.

*Silas and the Vow. With Wendell Fairweather.*

Edith leaned toward the microphone. "Ladies and gentlemen—the co-author of Silas Vane's final book, who will be reading from it tonight, and finishing it for all of us."

The room turned. Wendell went pale, then pink, then—slowly, wonderfully—steady. He looked at Marlowe one more time.

She mouthed it, the thing she'd been saving: *"Stop stalling."*

He picked up the manuscript, walked to the podium, and began.
