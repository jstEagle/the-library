## 1. The Weight of Unsigned Paper

The rain came down on Harbor Row the way it always did on Tuesdays in October—not in dramatic sheets but in a fine, patient gray drizzle, as though the sky had all the time in the world and intended to use it. Freya Almeida stood at the back counter of Second Prose Books & Bindery, a cup of tea gone cold at her elbow, and read her own handwriting for the last time.

*Dear Elias,* it began, the way 116 others had begun before it. *It's been raining here for three days. June lost her first tooth this morning and insisted we bury it in the garden so it would grow into a tooth-tree. I told her teeth don't grow into trees and she told me I have no imagination, which is fair, because I have been imagining the same thing for ten years and it hasn't come true either.*

She almost smiled. Then she folded the letter along its careful thirds, slid it into an envelope, and wrote on the front, in block letters she'd practiced until they looked nothing like her own: *E. Vane, Fort Benning, GA.* An address that hadn't answered in a decade. An address she mailed to anyway, once a month, like a woman dropping coins into a well and listening, every single time, for a splash.

She sealed it. She did not mail it. That was the ritual, and rituals only worked if you performed them exactly.

The false drawer was Marguerite's idea, back when the bookshop was still new and Freya had still been flinching at the sound of the mail truck. A shallow compartment beneath the bottom drawer of the old oak desk, hidden behind a panel of wood that matched the grain so well even Freya sometimes forgot it was there. She knelt now, her knee popping in a way it hadn't ten years ago, and pressed the panel. It gave with a soft wooden sigh, and there they were—117 envelopes, bundled in twine, the oldest ones gone soft and yellow at the edges like leaves pressed too long in a book.

She added the new one to the bundle. She retied the twine. She closed the panel.

*One hundred and seventeen,* she thought, and the number sat in her chest with a weight she had long ago stopped trying to name. It was not grief, exactly. Grief was for people who had lost something. Freya had never lost Elias Vane. She had simply never had him, and the distinction mattered, the way the difference between a wound and a scar mattered, the way June's dark hair—hair that came from nowhere Freya could name, except that it could—mattered.

The clock over the door said 8:47. In thirteen minutes, she would flip the sign to OPEN, and the town would come in, and she would be Freya Almeida, proprietor, bookseller, mother, woman who was fine, thank you, always fine. The letters would go back to being a secret kept so well that even the keeping of it had become invisible.

She washed her teacup. She straightened the new arrivals table. She touched the spine of a battered copy of *Jane Eyre* that had sat unsold in the poetry section for two years because no one in Harbor Row wanted it and Freya couldn't bear to discount it. Then she unlocked the door and turned the sign, and the day began, and she was fine.

* * *

June arrived at 3:15, as she always did, bursting through the door with the particular chaos of a ten-year-old released from school, backpack first, opinions second.

"Mrs. Pruitt says the bread festival is going to have a *cheese* tent this year," June announced, dumping her bag behind the counter with the confidence of someone who owned the place, which, in every way that mattered, she did. "A whole tent. Of cheese. Can we go? Can we get the smoked one? The one you always say is too expensive?"

"We'll see."

"That means no."

"It means we'll see." Freya handed her the usual: an apple, cut into slices, and the stack of books to shelve that June had claimed as her official job sometime around age seven. June took the apple and the stack and paused, which was never a good sign. June pausing was like a kettle going quiet right before it screamed.

"You're doing the letter thing," June said.

Freya's hands did not stop moving. She'd had ten years of practice at that, too—keeping her hands busy while the rest of her went very still. "I don't know what you mean."

"You get the look. The Tuesday look." June bit an apple slice and chewed thoughtfully, watching her mother with eyes that were, God help her, *his* eyes—dark and level and impossible to lie to. "You do the thing with the desk and then you get the look and then you organize the poetry section for no reason."

"I'm organizing the poetry section because it's a disaster."

"It's alphabetical. I did it last week."

Freya looked at the poetry section. It was, in fact, alphabetical. Her daughter had her number, had always had her number, and Freya sometimes wondered what it would be like to be a person whose child couldn't read her like a large-print edition.

"Go shelve your books," she said gently, and June went, but Freya could feel the questions following her across the shop like a cat that had learned to wait.

That was the thing about June that no one warned her about—that children don't ask the questions you've rehearsed. They ask the ones adjacent to them, the flanking questions, the ones that come at the truth sideways. *Why do you write letters to nobody? Why do you keep a picture of a man in uniform in the drawer with the Christmas wrapping? Why do you flinch when the mail comes?* Freya had answers for all of them, small polished answers, and every answer was a stone laid carefully over a well.

*The picture is of an old friend. The letters are a writing exercise. The mail is just the mail.*

June had never once asked the real question—*who is my father?*—and Freya had never once stopped being grateful and terrified in equal measure for that silence. She knew it couldn't last. Children grow into their questions the way they grow into shoes. But today was a Tuesday in October, and the rain was patient, and she could put it off one more day. She had gotten very good at one more days.

* * *

The regulars began arriving at four, the way the tide arrives, without anyone deciding it.

First came Dot Carver from the hardware store, who bought one paperback a week and read it in the shop's armchair for two hours, ostensibly browsing. Then Harold Ives, retired ferry captain, who came in every Tuesday to ask if the new naval histories had arrived, and every Tuesday they had not, because Freya had stopped ordering them two years ago and Harold had stopped noticing, and this arrangement was, like so many things in Harbor Row, a kindness disguised as a habit. Then Marguerite Laurent from the bakery next door, still in her apron, carrying a paper bag that smelled of cinnamon and butter so intensely that Freya's whole shop seemed briefly to change its mind about being a bookshop.

"Pain aux raisins," Marguerite announced, setting the bag on the counter. "The ends. The best part. Don't argue."

"I wasn't going to argue."

"You were going to say something about calories."

"I was going to say something about how you're an enabler."

"I am a *professional*," Marguerite said, and then, lowering her voice, sliding her gaze toward the poetry section where June was shelving with the fierce concentration of a child who knows she's being discussed: "Well? Did you do it?"

Freya busied herself with the register. "Did what?"

"Freya."

"It's done. It's in the drawer. It's the 117th." She said it lightly, the way you'd announce a dental cleaning, and Marguerite's face did the thing it always did—that softening, that sorrow folded carefully into sympathy so it wouldn't look like pity, because Freya had told her, years ago, in the only conversation they'd ever had about it directly: *if you pity me, I'll have to stop telling you things, and I need one person to tell things to.*

"117," Marguerite repeated. "Ten years."

"Ten years in December."

"And you'll never send them."

"They go to an address that—" Freya stopped. Started again. "The army forwards mail for a while. Then it doesn't. I checked, the first year. I checked every way a person can check." This was not entirely a lie. She had written to the army, to the VA, to two of Elias's old squadmates whose addresses she'd found in a memorial program from a benefit concert she'd driven three hours to attend. No one had answered, or if they had, the answers had never reached her—which, she would learn much later, was its own kind of answer, but she didn't know that yet. On this rainy Tuesday, she believed what she had always believed: that Elias Vane had come home from his last deployment, hung up his uniform, and simply walked out of the story she'd been writing to him for a decade. That he had read her letters, all of them, and chosen silence.

It was a cruel belief. It was also, she'd found, a livable one. Cruelty you can survive. Uncertainty is what kills you.

"He could have written," she said now, to Marguerite, to the register, to the rain. "One letter. One phone call. A postcard saying *stop.* I'd have stopped."

"I know, chérie."

"He knows about—" She glanced at June. "He doesn't. He doesn't know anything. And that's—" Her voice did something traitorous in the middle of the sentence, and she smoothed it flat. "That's his choice too, I suppose. Silence is a choice. I've made it myself."

Marguerite put her flour-dusted hand over Freya's on the counter and said nothing, which was the correct answer, and Freya was suddenly, absurdly close to tears in her own shop at four in the afternoon on a Tuesday, and she hated it, and she breathed through it, and Dot Carver turned a page in the armchair, and Harold Ives asked for the third time about naval histories, and the world resumed.

* * *

The regulars knew everything and said nothing. That was the covenant of Harbor Row, or at least of the small kingdom that was Second Prose: Dot knew about the drawer because she'd once come in early and seen Freya kneel at the desk, and she had simply walked back out and returned at opening time as if she hadn't, and had never mentioned it in six years. Harold knew because his late wife had been the one who'd driven teenage Freya to the bus station the summer Elias shipped out, and Harold had watched Freya come back from that bus station with a face he recognized from his own mirror, and had never said a word about it in twenty years. Marguerite knew everything, because Marguerite had pried the whole story out of her over the course of one long winter and had then appointed herself keeper of the pain aux raisins, the Tuesday check-ins, and the false drawer's original carpentry.

They knew. They said nothing. And Freya understood, in the way you understand the load-bearing walls of your own house, that this silence was not indifference—it was the town holding its breath around her, decade-long, so as not to collapse something.

She sometimes wondered what they'd say if they could speak. Dot would probably say *honey, that man is a chapter, not a book.* Harold would say something nautical and gruff and secretly tender. Marguerite would say, as she had said once, only once, when the wine was more than half gone: *You are not in love with him, Freya. You are in love with the letter you keep writing. Those are not the same man anymore.*

Freya had thrown a dish towel at her. They had never spoken of it again. But the sentence lived in her like a splinter, because she suspected it was true, and because she suspected, underneath the truth of it, that it didn't matter. You cannot stop writing to a man just because the man has become a letter. The letter is still addressed to him. The love still has his name on the envelope.

* * *

At closing, June did her rounds—chairs pushed in, new arrivals faced spine-out, the little brass bell on the door wiped free of fingerprints. She was methodical about it, solemn about it, a small shopkeeper-in-training, and Freya watched her from the register and felt the old ache shift into its evening position, the one just under the ribs.

June had his hands. That was the thing no one ever mentioned but everyone surely noticed—the long fingers, the way she held a pencil like she was signing something important. She had his height trajectory, probably; she was already the tallest in her class. She had his way of going quiet when she was thinking hard, staring at a middle distance that contained more than the room did. Freya had spent ten years watching this child assemble herself out of two people, one of whom was a photograph in a drawer, and she had never once told June that half of her face came from a man named Elias Vane who had kissed Freya on the pier in August, ten years and two months ago, and said *wait for me, I'm coming back for you, I mean it, I'm coming back*—and then the letters had started going out into the dark, and nothing had ever come back.

"Mom." June appeared at her elbow. "Can we get the cheese at the festival? Real answer this time."

Freya laughed, because the alternative was something else. "Real answer: if the shop does okay this month, yes."

"The shop always does okay. The shop is the most okay shop in Maine." June grabbed her backpack. "Also Mrs. Pruitt says a soldier moved in over Marguerite's bakery. Above the ovens. She saw the moving truck. She says he had two bags and a limp."

The words went through Freya like a needle finding an old scar—quick, precise, absurdly painful for such a small entry point.

"A soldier," Freya repeated.

"From the army, probably. Mrs. Pruitt says she can always tell. She says they walk like the floor owes them money." June shouldered her bag, oblivious, already halfway out the door into the drizzle. "Can we get the *smoked* cheese? Not just regular?"

"Smoked," Freya heard herself say. "Yes. Fine."

The bell rang. The door closed. Freya stood alone in her shop with the lamps on and the rain against the glass and the word *soldier* ringing in her ears like a struck fork.

It was a coastal town in October. Soldiers moved through it the way gulls did—transient, unremarkable, gone by spring. It was nothing. It was a coincidence wearing a duffel bag. She had spent ten years teaching herself the difference between a door opening and a door opening *toward* her, and she knew, she *knew*, that most doors were just doors.

But she found herself walking to the front window anyway, standing in the dark of her own shop, looking across the wet street at Marguerite's bakery, at the windows above it—dark now, unlit, the apartment that had sat empty for two years and that she had never once thought about until this exact moment.

The rain kept falling, patient as ever. Somewhere in the back, in the false drawer, letter 117 lay against its 116 sisters, addressed to a man who had never answered, in a town that had just, quietly, acquired a stranger.

Freya pressed her hand flat against the cold glass, told herself it was nothing, and did not believe it for a single second.
