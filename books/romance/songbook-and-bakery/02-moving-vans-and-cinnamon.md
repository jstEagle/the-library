## 2. Moving Vans and Cinnamon

The bus from Boston smelled of wet wool and someone else's coffee, and Elias Vane spent the last forty minutes of the ride with his forehead against the cold glass, watching the highway unspool into two lanes and then into something that barely qualified as a road at all. Harbor Row announced itself the way it always had—not with a sign but with a smell: salt, diesel, woodsmoke, and beneath all of it, faint as a memory, the yeast-warm breath of Marguerite Okafor's bakery, which had been perfuming the town's mornings since before either of them was born.

He'd told himself the whole way down that this was a tactical decision. A sound one. Logical, even. His knee was held together with titanium and scar tissue now instead of cartilage; his file said *medical discharge* in language so bureaucratic it almost didn't hurt to read. Almost. He hadn't told his mother yet because telling his mother meant hearing her recalibrate her entire five-year plan for him in real time—the pause on the phone, the small sharp intake of breath she probably thought he couldn't hear, and then the pivot: *Well, that's all right, darling, because there's a position opening at Whitmore & Associates in the spring, and honestly, Elias, your father always said you had a head for—*

So instead he'd told her he was "taking some time at Gran's old place to sort the estate." Which was true. Just not the whole truth. The whole truth was that he had signed a lease that morning on a flat above a bakery, sight unseen except for three photographs and a phone call, because the flat above Gran's house on Larch Street had felt too much like an exhibit—*Here Lies the Life Elias Did Not Choose*—and because when the landlady had said *it's above the bakery, mind, you'll smell bread at four in the morning and either love it or lose your mind*, he had heard something in his own chest loosen for the first time in months.

Two duffel bags. That was what eleven years of service reduced to, once you subtracted the things the Army kept: the uniforms, the kit, the ruck, the versions of himself that had been issued and collected like equipment. Two duffel bags and a cardboard box of his grandmother's papers riding in the taxi behind him, and a discharge packet in a manila envelope he'd wedged into the side pocket where he wouldn't have to look at it.

The taxi let him off at the corner of Harbor Row and Salt Lane, across from the bakery, and Elias stood on the curb with a duffel strap cutting into each shoulder and looked at the street he'd grown up on and felt absolutely nothing, which frightened him more than anything else had in a long time.

That was the thing nobody warned you about coming home. They warned you about the grief, the alienation, the way the town would be smaller than you remembered. Nobody warned you about the numbness—that you might stand in the exact spot where you'd learned to ride a bike, where you'd kissed a girl behind the chandlery at seventeen, where you'd said goodbye to everyone you loved in a single summer—and feel like a tourist reading a plaque.

*It'll come back,* the psych eval had said. *Give it time.*

Elias shifted his grip on the straps and started toward the bakery door.

* * *

Marguerite Okafor had been expecting him, in the way that Marguerite expected everything—which is to say she had already formed opinions about him, his posture, his luggage, and his mother, and had decided to keep most of them to herself out of what she would later describe as professional courtesy.

"You're taller," she said, instead of hello, looking up at him from behind the counter with flour on her forearms to the elbow, as though she'd been interrupted mid-knead by the arrival of a ghost. "Last time I saw you, you were maybe nineteen, buying day-old rolls with quarters."

"Ma'am."

"Don't ma'am me, I've known you since you were in braces." She wiped her hands on her apron and came around the counter, and then, apparently reconsidering, did not hug him, which he was grateful for. Instead she looked at him the way she used to look at loaves through the oven glass—assessing, patient, unwilling to open the door before the crust set. "You look tired."

"I look like a man who took a bus from Boston."

"You look like a man who hasn't slept properly since—" She stopped herself. Marguerite knew exactly where to stop; it was one of her gifts. "Stairs are around back. Key's under the blue pot, but I'm handing it to you now because you'll forget. Rent's due the first, you're quiet or I'll know why, and if you burn something in that kitchen I will smell it before the smoke alarm does." She pressed a brass key into his palm and closed his fingers over it. "Welcome home, Elias."

Something in his throat did a complicated maneuver. "Thank you."

"And eat something. You've got that look. The look of a man who thinks hunger is a scheduling problem." She thrust a paper bag at him—still warm, still breathing cinnamon—and went back to her dough without waiting for thanks, conversation concluded on her terms, as always.

Elias climbed the outside stairs with both bags, unlocked the flat, and stood in the doorway of a room that smelled of lemon polish and, underneath it, bread—always bread, a permanent atmospheric condition, the whole building exhaling warmth through its floorboards. It was small and clean and had a window that looked out over the street, over the bakery awning, over—

Over the bookshop directly across the way.

He hadn't thought about the bookshop. That was the lie he'd been telling himself since Boston, anyway: that he could come back to Harbor Row, a town of four thousand people, and simply not think about the bookshop. Not think about the woman who ran it. Not think about the fact that every map of his childhood had her name written in the margins.

He set the bags down inside the door and stood at the window, and that was when he saw her.

* * *

She was carrying a box.

That was all. That was the entire event: a woman crossing a street with a cardboard box balanced on one hip, dark hair escaping a knot at the nape of her neck, rain starting to speckle the pavement around her in fat, unhurried drops. Ten years. He had done the arithmetic on the bus until the numbers stopped meaning anything—ten years, a hundred and twenty months, however many thousands of hours—and here was the sum total of it: Freya Almeida, older, her face carrying lines he hadn't given her time to earn, ducking her shoulder against a box of books while the first real rain of autumn came down.

And beside her—a child.

A girl, ten years old or nearly, walking backward in front of the box to catch it if it slipped, talking up at her mother with the urgent animation of a person making a case. Small. Dark-haired. Rain-jacketed. Laughing at something Freya said, head tipped back, and Elias watched that laugh cross the street and felt the world tilt on some axis he hadn't known was load-bearing.

He should have looked away. Some part of him, some well-trained part, understood even then that he was standing at a window doing reconnaissance on a life that had gone on without him, and that nothing good came of it. But his body had stopped taking orders somewhere around the second deployment, and so he stood there, duffel bags behind him, cinnamon rising through the floorboards, and watched Freya Almeida laugh in the rain with a child who had her exact same stubborn chin.

Ten years ago he had stood in a bus depot with a duffel bag and a promise, and she had kissed him and said *write to me, write every week, I mean it, Vane, I'll count.* And he had written. God, he had written—until the letters started coming back, or worse, not coming back at all, until the silence got so long and so complete that he'd finally done the only thing a man could do with a silence like that: believed it. Believed she'd met someone. Believed a small-town girl couldn't wait forever for a soldier, and shouldn't have to, and had made the sane choice, and that the ache in his chest was just the price of admission to a life he'd chosen over her.

He had built a whole architecture out of that belief. Load-bearing walls and everything. And now he was watching it stand in the rain across the street, holding a box of books, laughing with a daughter.

*A daughter.*

The word arrived without permission and refused to leave. He counted again—he couldn't help it, it was the way his mind worked, the way it had been trained to work, assess, estimate, extrapolate—and the arithmetic was obscene. Ten years. A girl of ten. A girl with Freya's chin and, when the streetlight caught her face as they reached the bookshop door, eyes that made his stomach drop through the floorboards of the flat, through the bakery, through the bedrock of the town itself, because he knew those eyes. He saw them every morning in the mirror, older and more tired, but the same gray, the same shape, the same way of looking at the world like it owed them an explanation.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty flat. "No, that's—"

Coincidence. Genetics are coincidence. Towns are full of people with gray eyes. There were a hundred explanations and every single one of them required him to stop standing at this window like a man haunting his own life.

Below him, the bakery exhaled another wave of cinnamon through the floor. Across the street, the bookshop door opened, closed. The bell above it—he could see it, the little green bell he'd forgotten existed until this exact moment—swung and settled.

Elias sat down on the edge of the bare mattress, because his knees had informed him they were done pretending, and put his face in his hands.

Home was supposed to be the refuge. That had been the plan, the whole plan, the only plan: come back to Harbor Row, sort Gran's estate, sleep in a place where no one shot at anything, figure out what a man did with a body that had been used up and a résumé that read like a threat. Simple. Contained. A decompression chamber.

Instead: her, across the street. A child with his eyes. Cinnamon in the walls like the past itself had moved in before he did and was already unpacking.

He thought about calling his mother and immediately, comprehensively, did not.

He thought about the manila envelope in the duffel pocket and left it where it was.

Outside, the rain thickened. Downstairs, Marguerite began the evening bake, and the smell rose through the building like a tide coming in—warm, sweet, relentless, the smell of every Saturday morning of his childhood, of Gran's kitchen, of the summer he was nineteen and in love and certain that love was the one thing the Army couldn't requisition.

Elias lay back on the bare mattress and stared at the ceiling, listening to the rain and the distant, muffled percussion of dough being worked below, and tried very hard not to do the math again.

He did the math again.

Ten years minus nine months. A girl born, if his arithmetic was right—and his arithmetic was always right, that was the curse of him—in late spring of the year after he'd shipped out. The year the letters stopped.

The year everything stopped.

He rolled onto his side and looked at the window, at the lit window of the bookshop across the street, warm gold against the gray evening, and he thought: *I could walk over there tonight. Knock. Say her name. Ask one question.*

He thought about what question, exactly, and found he didn't have one. Every question he owned had collapsed into a single point, and the point was a child's face in the rain, and he wasn't ready. He wasn't ready the way a bridge isn't ready, the way a wall isn't ready, the way a man with two duffel bags and a secret in an envelope isn't ready to stand in front of the woman he abandoned by believing a lie he never actually heard anyone tell.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow he'd sort Gran's papers. Tomorrow he'd call his mother, or he wouldn't. Tomorrow he'd figure out how a man asks a question that might unmake him.

Downstairs, the ovens ticked and breathed. The rain came down on Harbor Row like it had something to say and all night to say it.

Across the street, the light in the bookshop window went out—but not all the way. One lamp stayed on, upstairs, behind the curtain, burning steady and gold into the small hours, and Elias watched it until his eyes burned, and slept, finally, with the taste of cinnamon in his mouth and the word *daughter* sitting on his chest like a stone he could neither lift nor put down.
