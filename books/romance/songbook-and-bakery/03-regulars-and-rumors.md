## 3. Regulars and Rumors

By Wednesday morning, Harbor Row had done what Harbor Row always did with a piece of news: it had picked it up, turned it over, worried it smooth between its teeth like a sea glass, and set it out on every windowsill in town.

*The Vane boy is back.*

That was what they were calling him, of course. The Vane boy. As though thirty-two years could be folded away like a map, as though the man who'd stepped off the bus with two duffel bags and a limp he tried to hide could still be the sixteen-year-old who'd once climbed the water tower on a dare and gotten stuck there until the fire department came. Freya had heard the phrase eleven times before nine o'clock, and she was counting, because counting kept her from thinking.

"The Vane boy's renting Marguerite's upstairs," said Dot Carver, who came in every Wednesday at opening for a scone and an hour of other people's business. She said it the way she said everything—loudly, to the whole shop, while looking directly at Freya. Dot had been saying things *directly at Freya* for ten years now, little probes wrapped in pleasantries, waiting to see if anything flinched. "You remember Elias Vane, don't you, dear? He was in your class."

"I remember everyone," Freya said, sliding the scone onto a plate. "It's a occupational hazard of running a bookshop. You learn names or you die."

Dot laughed her dry cigarette laugh, though she hadn't smoked since 1998. Behind her, old Mr. Pemberton looked up from his newspaper long enough to give Freya a look over the top of it—a look that said, plainly, *I know, and I'm not going to say anything, but I want you to know that I know.* Mr. Pemberton had been doing this since the day June was born. It was, in its way, a form of devotion.

"Army, wasn't he?" Dot pressed on. "Or Marines? Harold says Army. Harold says he saw him carrying boxes up Marguerite's stairs yesterday and he walked like—" she paused, choosing her word with unusual care, "—like someone the war gave back used."

"Harold should write poetry," Freya said, and rang up the scone, and smiled the smile she kept for exactly these occasions: warm enough to close the subject, cool enough to keep it closed.

This was the trick of it. This was the thing she had practiced for ten years until it sat in her body like bone. When Elias Vane's name came up—and it came up more than you'd think, because small towns never fully release the people they lose—you did not stiffen. You did not flush. You made a joke, you changed the register, you asked about someone's grandchildren. Grief, properly maintained, was invisible. She had built a life the way you build a seawall: stone by stone, each one placed so nothing showed through, so the water could crash against it forever and find no purchase.

She had been very good at it for a decade.

She was appalled to discover, standing behind her own counter with a scone in her hand, how much effort it took today.

* * *

The trouble with rumors is that they have weather systems. They blow in from somewhere, and they bring pressure changes with them, and by Thursday the whole town's air had gone strange and electric. Freya felt it in the way people browsed. Three separate customers bought novels about homecomings. Mrs. Okafor, who had not purchased a book in two years and merely used the shop as a reading room with better lighting, suddenly wanted *everything* about soldiers returning from war, and when Freya suggested, mildly, that perhaps a mystery might suit her instead, Mrs. Okafor looked at her with such naked pity that Freya had to go into the back room and grip the edge of the sink for a full minute.

They knew. Not everything—they didn't know about the letters, they didn't know about the drawer—but they knew *enough*. Ten years ago a girl got quietly pregnant and quietly refused to name a father, and ten years ago a soldier left for deployment and never wrote back, and Harbor Row, which forgot nothing, had done the arithmetic privately and politely and never once aloud. That was the bargain of a small town: everyone knows, no one says, and in exchange you get casseroles when your mother dies and someone always willing to watch the shop when your daughter has a fever.

But a rumor was a different animal than knowledge. Knowledge slept. A rumor paced.

"He's back, then." Marguerite appeared at four o'clock, flour to the elbows, carrying a paper bag that smelled like heaven's own kitchen. She let herself behind the counter without asking, because after fifteen years of friendship she considered the space between Freya's register and the biography section to be jointly administered territory. She set the bag down. "Cinnamon knots. Day-old, which means perfect, which means eat them before June gets here or there won't be any evidence left."

"You're bribing me."

"I'm feeding you. The bribery is incidental." Marguerite wiped her hands on her apron and studied Freya with the frank, unembarrassed attention of a woman who kneaded dough for a living and had learned to read rising things. "He asked about you."

Freya's hand stopped halfway to the bag. "Who did."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you pretend the English language doesn't contain certain words." Marguerite leaned against the counter. "Elias. Yesterday, moving in. He stood on my steps for a solid minute looking across the street at your window like a man checking whether a house was haunted. And then last night he knocked on my door at ten p.m.—ten!—and asked me, very casually, in the voice of a man who had rehearsed it, how long I'd known you."

"And what did you tell him?"

"Fifteen years. That you run the best bookshop on the coast. That your lemon bars are superior to mine, which cost me something to say." Marguerite's eyes narrowed. "And that's all I told him, Freya Almeida, because some things aren't mine to tell. But I want you to know I watched his face when I said your name, and I've baked bread for forty years, and I know what proof looks like. That man rose."

"Marguerite."

"Yes?"

"Stop."

Marguerite held up both floury hands in surrender, but her eyes stayed on Freya a moment longer, gentle and relentless. Then she took a cinnamon knot for herself, bit into it, and said, around the pastry, "The festival committee meets Monday. You're on it. I nominated you."

"You *what*?"

"You're on it every year. You design the program. Don't look betrayed; it's tradition, and traditions are the only religion this town keeps." She brushed crumbs from her apron. "See you Monday."

* * *

June arrived at half past three, as she did every day, bursting through the door with the specific energy of a child who had been sitting still for six hours and intended to be compensated for it. She was ten years old and built entirely of questions and elbows, with Freya's dark hair and—though Freya had spent a decade refusing to look directly at this fact—eyes that were going to be a problem someday. They were already a problem. They were gray-green and level and they missed nothing, and every year they grew less like Freya's and more like a memory Freya kept locked in the false drawer of her desk.

"Mum. Mum. Mum." June dropped her backpack, claimed the last cinnamon knot, and hoisted herself onto the stool behind the counter, her official post. "Did you hear?"

"Hear what?" Freya said, in her flattest voice, which June correctly interpreted as *tell me everything immediately.*

"A soldier moved in over the bakery. Over Marguerite's. Everyone at school was talking about it. Tilly said he has a scar on his arm. Marcus said he killed somebody. Marcus lies." She took an enormous bite. "Mum, why does everyone care? We get tourists all the time."

"Because he's not a tourist. He grew up here. People remember."

"Do *you* remember him?"

Freya alphabetized a stack of receipts that did not need alphabetizing. "Everyone remembers everyone here. It's the town's whole personality."

June chewed. Watched. Chewed again. Her mother had raised her on books and honesty, which meant she had developed the instincts of a customs officer. "You're doing your voice."

"What voice?"

"Your *nothing to see here* voice. You use it when Gran calls. You use it when Dot asks about Dad." Freya's spine went cold. June continued blithely, swinging her legs. "Anyway, I saw him."

Freya turned around slowly. "When."

"This morning. Before school. He was on Marguerite's steps getting his mail, and I was walking past, and he looked at me." June frowned, working at it, trying to fit the experience into the vocabulary she had. "Not like people usually look at me. Not like *oh, a kid.* More like—" she squinted— "like a math problem. Like I was a math problem and he was really bad at math and it mattered a lot."

The shop went very quiet. Somewhere in the stacks, the radiator ticked. Freya discovered that her hands had gone still on the receipts, and that stillness, in her, was a kind of scream.

"What did you do?" she managed.

"I said good morning, because Gran raised me right even if you didn't." June rolled her eyes at the ancient parental failure of insufficient manners. "And he said good morning back, but weird. All strangled. And then I left, because he was giving me a whole *feeling*, and I didn't sign up for it."

"June—"

"Is he famous or something? Is that why everyone cares?"

"No."

"Then why did he look at me like that?"

There it was. The question, hanging in the air between the biography section and the register, small and bright and absolutely lethal. Freya had rehearsed answers to a hundred versions of it for ten years—in the shower, on the walk to school, at two in the morning staring at the ceiling. She had answers about donors, about family friends, about fathers who traveled, about fathers who died, each one polished and ready and each one a lie, and now that the moment had actually come she found that every rehearsed sentence dissolved on her tongue like sugar in rain.

What came out instead was the truth wearing a trench coat.

"Some people just have faces like that," Freya said. "Some people look at everything like a math problem. It doesn't mean anything about you."

June considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "Hm," she said, which was her word for *I am filing this under unresolved,* and Freya knew the file would not stay closed. June did not lose files. June was the kind of child who, at age seven, had demanded to know why none of her friend's stories contained a dad either, and had conducted a survey, and had presented the results at dinner.

"Can I shelve returns?" June asked, mood pivoting on a dime, already sliding off the stool.

"Cart's by the door."

June grabbed the cart and wheeled it into the fiction section, humming, and Freya stood alone at the counter with a day-old cinnamon knot she no longer wanted and a heart going like a trapped bird.

*Math problem.* Of course he'd looked at her like that. What else would he see? A stranger, a child, a sum that wouldn't balance—dark hair, those eyes, that stubborn set of the jaw that Freya knew intimately because she kissed its original every night at bedtime. Ten years ago Elias Vane had boarded a bus with a duffel bag and no idea, and now the answer to a question he didn't know existed had said *good morning* to him on a bakery step.

Had he recognized her? Could anyone recognize a face they'd never seen?

Freya pressed her palms flat against the counter and breathed.

Here was the thing she could not explain to Marguerite, or to Dot, or to the seawall of herself: she had spent ten years building a life where this could never happen. She had chosen a town he'd never visit, a profession with no overlap, a story with no sequel. She had mailed her heart to a dead address until mailing it started to feel like digging a grave deeper instead of filling one in, and then she had stopped, and told herself stopping was healing. She had been *careful*. Careful the way you're careful with a wound—keeping it clean, keeping it covered, never pressing to see if it still hurt.

And now the wound had rented an apartment. The wound was collecting mail on Marguerite's steps. The wound had looked at her daughter like a math problem, and the math, apparently, was starting to bother him.

"Mum?" June's head appeared around the end of the shelf, upside down, hair hanging. "You've got your thinking face. The sad one."

"I don't have a sad thinking face."

"You have four thinking faces. Happy, busy, shopping-list, and sad. That was sad." She righted herself and came back down the aisle, dragging the cart, and looked up at her mother with those gray-green eyes that missed nothing, that would eventually miss nothing about everything, that were patient as tide. "Was the soldier the math problem, or am I?"

Freya crouched down to her level. Up close, the resemblance was a lighthouse beam—impossible to look at directly, impossible to deny. She tucked a strand of hair behind June's ear and lied, gently, with love, with the full weight of ten years behind it:

"You're not a math problem, sweetheart. You're the only thing I ever solved."

June accepted this, hugged her briefly and ferociously, and returned to shelving. But as Freya straightened up, she caught sight of them reflected together in the shop's front window—mother and daughter, ghosted over the street outside—and beyond them, across the road, a light coming on in the flat above the bakery. A silhouette moved past the glass. Broad shoulders. Careful gait. A man learning the dimensions of a room he didn't plan to stay in.

He stopped at the window. For a moment—two seconds, maybe three—he stood looking down at the street, at the bookshop, at the reflection of the woman and child inside it, though Freya couldn't have sworn he saw them.

Then the light went out, and Freya stood in her own warm lit shop feeling, for the first time in ten years, completely exposed—and across the street, above the darkened bakery, Marguerite's oven timer began to ring.
