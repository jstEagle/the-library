## 4. Coffee, Overdue

The book Marguerite had pressed into his hands was called *The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter*, and Elias Vane had never seen it before in his life.

"I'm not returning this," he'd told her that morning, standing in her bakery with flour dust hanging in the light like weather. "I've never read it. I've never even *seen* it."

"Of course you haven't," Marguerite said, wiping her hands on an apron that had once been white and was now the color of good memory. "That's why it's overdue. Three weeks overdue. Terrible customer, this one. Never returns anything." She'd written something on a slip of paper and tucked it inside the cover. "Take it back to Freya at the bookshop. Tell her Marguerite sends her apologies and a promise of cinnamon rolls if she forgives the lateness."

"Marguerite—"

"She's open until six." The woman had already turned back to her dough, shoulders set in a way that made it clear the conversation was over, or had perhaps never really been a conversation at all. "And Elias? Take your time finding the checkout counter. It's a small shop, but the shelves are interesting."

He'd stood there holding a paperback he hadn't borrowed, from a woman who hadn't lent it, being sent to a bookstore he had no reason to enter, and he understood with perfect clarity that he was being managed. He'd spent eleven years in the Army learning to read terrain, and Marguerite Boudreaux was terrain of the most obvious kind: a hill someone wanted you to take, marked with flags.

He went anyway.

* * *

Harbor Row in late September smelled of salt and woodsmoke and, faintly, of the bread that came out of Marguerite's ovens at four each morning—a smell Elias had woken to for three days now and had not yet gotten used to, because it was the smell of mornings that asked nothing of him. No reveille. No briefings. Just yeast and warmth rising through the floorboards like the building itself was breathing.

The bookshop sat across the street and two doors down, wedged between a hardware store and a place that sold kayaks out of season. Its window display was arranged around a theme he could only describe as *autumn having feelings*: stacked novels, a ceramic owl, a hand-lettered sign reading COZY SEASON IS A STATE OF MIND. Painted above the door, in letters that had faded from gold to the color of weak tea: **ALMEIDA'S — BOOKS, NEW & SECOND EDITIONS**.

Second editions. He turned the phrase over as he crossed the street. Most shops said *used*. This one had chosen to believe in the possibility of improvement.

The bell over the door announced him with a bright, indifferent cheerfulness, and then he was inside, and the smell hit him first—paper and dust and something floral, lavender maybe, or the ghost of lavender—and then everything else hit him at once.

Freya Almeida stood behind the counter with a pencil behind her ear and a stack of hardcovers in her arms, and she was laughing at something, head tipped back, throat bare, and for one disorienting second Elias was twenty-four years old again, standing in her parents' backyard in June, watching her laugh at his terrible joke about military rations, thinking: *I could do this forever. I could do exactly this.*

Then she saw him, and the laugh died mid-flight, and ten years landed on both of them like a dropped ceiling.

"Elias." She said his name carefully, the way you set down something breakable.

"Freya." His voice came out lower than he intended. He cleared his throat. "I have—" He held up the paperback like evidence at a trial. "Marguerite says I owe you this. Apparently I borrowed it and forgot to return it, which is strange, because I also apparently forgot borrowing it."

Something moved through her face—not quite a smile, but the shadow a smile leaves on a wall after it's gone. "Three weeks overdue?"

"So she claims."

"That sounds like Marguerite." She set down her stack of books and came toward the counter, and he watched her decide, visibly, to be composed. She'd always been good at that—composure worn lightly, like a cardigan. "She's been running that scam since we were in high school. She once convinced Danny Pell that he owed the library forty cents from 1998. He paid it. In quarters."

"Danny Pell still lives here?"

"Runs the kayak place next door." She extended her hand for the book, and when their fingers brushed during the handoff—brief, accidental, electric enough that he actually flinched—she pretended it hadn't happened, and he pretended harder. "Well. Debt settled. Harbor Row's financial system remains intact."

"Good. I'd hate to start my residency in debt."

"You're staying, then." Not a question. Her eyes went to the duffel bag slung over his shoulder and away again, quick as a bird. "I heard you took the flat above Marguerite's."

"For now. It was available."

"It's been available for six years." She said it without malice, just fact, and then seemed to hear how it sounded and added, "Small town. Things stay empty a long time. People get attached to the emptiness."

There was a sentence with weight in it, and they both felt it settle between them like dust after a shelf collapses. Elias looked at the counter between them—the old wooden counter, scarred and ink-stained, and he remembered, absurdly, that he had kissed her behind a counter once, in a shop very much like this one, in another town, in another life where the future had been a country neither of them had visited yet.

"Can I—" He gestured vaguely at the shelves. "Marguerite said the shelves were interesting."

"Marguerite says a lot of things." But Freya waved him toward the stacks, and there was relief in the gesture, relief at having a task, a script. "Fiction's left, nonfiction's right, local history is the sad little corner by the radiator that nobody visits. If you're looking for something specific, I can—"

"Local history," he said, surprising himself. "Actually. Yeah."

Her eyebrows rose a fraction. "You want to know about Harbor Row?"

"I grew up two towns over. I spent every summer here until I was seventeen." He let that sit. Let her do the arithmetic, if she was doing arithmetic. "It changed. Or I did. Probably both."

"The radiator corner doesn't judge," she said quietly. "It just gets warm and full of facts."

* * *

He browsed. Or performed browsing, which amounted to the same thing: pulling books off shelves, opening them, seeing nothing, putting them back. What he was actually doing was listening—to the creak of the floorboards, to the scratch of her pencil at the counter, to the small domestic sounds of a life being lived in a room he wasn't part of.

And then there was a third sound. Small feet on stairs.

"Mom, the ladder in the biography section is broken again, and also Mrs. Okafor wants to know if you can hold the—"

June stopped halfway across the shop floor.

Elias had prepared himself for many things about coming home. He had prepared himself for his mother's phone calls, for the hollowed-out feeling of a discharge physical, for the way civilian grocery stores had too many choices and no urgency. He had not prepared himself for a ten-year-old girl with dark hair pulled into a lopsided braid, wearing a t-shirt with a cartoon rocket on it, staring at him from beneath a fringe of bangs.

He had especially not prepared himself for her eyes.

They were brown. Deep brown, nearly black in certain lights, ringed with a darker limbal edge—and Elias knew those eyes the way you know your own hands. He'd seen them in mirrors for thirty-four years. He'd seen them in photographs of his grandmother. He had seen them, once, in a hospital waiting room in Germany, reflected back at him from a window while he waited for news of a friend, and thought: *if I ever have a child, I hope they get her eyes instead of mine.*

Except these were his eyes. On a child. In a bookshop in Harbor Row, in September, ten years and some months after a night he had replayed so many times the memory had gone soft at the edges like cloth washed too often.

The girl looked at him the way people look at a math problem they suspect they already know the answer to.

"You're new," she said.

"June." Freya's voice came across the shop with careful brightness, a hand placed gently on a lid. "This is Mr. Vane. He's renting Marguerite's flat. Elias, this is June. She's—"

"Ten," June said. "Almost eleven. Eleven in March." She didn't say which March. She was still looking at him, unblinking, cataloguing. Kids did that—he remembered doing it himself, memorizing the details of adults who mattered—but there was something else in her attention, something older than ten. Something that was measuring.

"March," Elias repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "Spring birthday. Good month."

"It's all right." She shrugged with the elaborate indifference of childhood. "Are you a soldier?"

"June." Sharper now, from the counter.

"What? Mom, look at his posture. And his haircut. And he stands like Grandpa Dave does in his uniform pictures." She folded her arms. "I'm just asking."

Elias found his voice somewhere below his sternum. "I was. A soldier. I'm not anymore, mostly. It's complicated."

"Most things are," June said, with the gravity of a much older person, and then, mercifully, she lost interest—or appeared to—and wandered toward the biography section to investigate the broken ladder, though Elias noticed she took the long route, passing within six feet of him, close enough that he caught the smell of pencil shavings and strawberry shampoo, and he stood absolutely still, like a man trying not to spook a deer, like a man afraid that any movement would collapse the whole fragile scene into something he couldn't survive.

Freya had come around the counter. He saw it in her face—all of it, the terror and the fierce readiness, a mother positioning herself between her child and a threat, except the threat was him, was the truth, was ten years of silence standing in her shop holding a paperback he'd never borrowed.

"She's curious," Freya said, low. "She's ten. She asks everyone their business. Don't—"

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"I know." A breath. "I know you weren't."

But her hands had curled slightly at her sides, and he understood that *I know you weren't* was not the same as *thank you*. It was *please*. It was *not yet, not here, not like this*. It was a door held almost shut with a foot.

"Marguerite mentioned cinnamon rolls," he said, because someone had to say something ordinary or the room was going to crack along its fault lines. "If I apologize sufficiently for the overdue book. Is that a real offer?"

The tension in her shoulders eased by a single degree. "It's a real offer. Marguerite weaponizes baked goods. It's how she's controlled this town for thirty years."

"Smart woman."

"The smartest and the worst." And there—there, for half a second—was the old Freya, the one who'd laughed at his terrible jokes, surfacing through the composure like a fish rising, and then diving again before he could reach it.

* * *

He bought the local history book. *Harbor Row: A Working Port, 1850–2000*, water-damaged at the corners, priced at three dollars. He paid in cash and watched her make change, and her hands were steady, and he hated how steady they were, because his own were not.

At the door, he paused with his hand on the frame.

"Freya."

She looked up. Behind her, June had dragged the step ladder to the biography section and was testing its wobble with scientific detachment.

"The eyes," he said. Very quietly. He didn't finish the sentence. There was no version of finishing the sentence that either of them could survive at four-thirty on a Tuesday afternoon in a bookshop that smelled of lavender.

Freya went very still. For a long moment the only sound was the radiator ticking and June humming something tuneless to herself, and Elias watched Freya Almeida stand at the exact center of a decade of decisions, weighing them all in a single breath.

"She has your patience," Freya said finally. Which was not confirmation. Which was not denial. Which was, somehow, worse than both. "Goodbye, Elias."

The bell chimed behind him, bright and indifferent, and the door swung shut, and he stood on the sidewalk of Harbor Row holding a three-dollar history of a town he'd loved and left, watching his own reflection in the shop window superimposed over the warm yellow light inside, over the small figure of a girl re-shelving biographies, over the woman who had built a whole life in the space where his used to be.

Eleven in March.

He did the math right there on the sidewalk, standing under the fading gold letters, and the answer arrived in his chest like a round chambered: *ten years and some months.*

He started walking. He wasn't sure where. His feet chose for him, and they chose the harbor, and he walked it end to end twice while the gulls argued overhead, and by the time the streetlights came on he had made himself two promises.

The first was that he would not say a word to anyone—not to Marguerite, not to his mother, not to Freya herself—until he understood what had happened, all of it, every year of it.

The second was that whatever had happened, whoever had decided what he should and shouldn't know—

Someone had known. Someone had always known. And they had let him walk away believing he'd been left.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He didn't have to look. *Helena Vane, 3 missed calls.* He stared at the screen for a long moment, the harbor wind pushing at his back like a hand between his shoulder blades.

Then he turned the phone face-down in his pocket, bought a coffee he didn't want from the cart by the pier, and sat on a cold bench doing arithmetic until dark.
