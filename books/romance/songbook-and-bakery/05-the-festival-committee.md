## 5. The Festival Committee

The notice went up on the community board on a Wednesday, and by Thursday it had migrated to the counter of the bookshop, where Marguerite had pinned it beneath a jar of cinnamon sticks as though it were a hostage.

*HARBOR ROW AUTUMN BREAD FESTIVAL — ORGANIZING COMMITTEE — VOLUNTEERS NEEDED — FIRST MEETING SUNDAY, TOWN HALL.*

Freya read it twice while pretending to shelve a stack of poetry collections that did not need shelving.

"You're going," Marguerite said. It was not a question. Marguerite did not ask questions she had already answered on other people's behalf.

"I'm not," Freya said. "I have inventory."

"It's Sunday. You don't open Sundays."

"Personal inventory."

Marguerite wiped her hands on her apron—a gesture Freya had learned to read like weather, because flour-dusted hands meant calm and a wiped apron meant Marguerite was about to win an argument. "Dottie Okafor is chairing it this year, and Dottie has been asking after you since June was in diapers. If you don't show up, she'll come here, and she'll bring the casserole of guilt, and you'll say yes anyway, except you'll have wasted a casserole's worth of goodwill doing it."

"That's not how casseroles work."

"Everything works if you believe in it hard enough." Marguerite slid the notice across the counter. "Besides. I hear a certain someone volunteered."

Freya's hand stopped halfway to the paper.

She had spent eleven days—she had counted, because counting was a thing she did instead of feeling—perfecting a system of avoidance that would have impressed a military strategist. She knew his hours at the bakery above, or rather the hours he descended from it: mornings before seven, evenings after six, always with a canvas bag, always with that careful way of walking like a man relearning the weight of his own body. She knew which side of Harbor Row he favored on his walks, so she took June home the other way. She knew he'd come into the bookshop once, for *Coffee, Overdue*, and she had survived it, barely, by treating him like a customer who owed late fees on a decade.

But avoidance required intelligence, and intelligence required sources, and her primary source was currently smiling at her over a jar of cinnamon sticks with the serenity of a woman who had been engineering this exact moment for two weeks.

"What certain someone," Freya said flatly.

"The new fellow upstairs. Elias." Marguerite said the name lightly, too lightly, the way you set down something fragile. "He came in yesterday for sourdough starter and stayed an hour. He asked if the town needed anything. Can you imagine? A man asks what the town *needs*. I told him about the festival committee and he said—" she dropped her voice into a fair imitation of his low, careful register—"'I could do that. I'm good at logistics.'"

"He's good at logistics," Freya repeated.

"He carried forty-pound flour sacks up my stairs without complaint. That's logistics."

"Dottie will put him on the heavy-lifting subcommittee and he'll never have to speak to anyone."

"Mm," said Marguerite, who had already spoken to Dottie, and whose expression suggested the seating chart was already drawn.

Freya looked at the notice. Autumn bread festival. Three weeks of meetings. Budgets, vendor lists, the great annual argument about whether the ovens should be gas or wood-fired, which was less an argument about ovens than a proxy war between the Hendersons and the Pruitts that had outlived both original combatants. And Elias Vane, sitting at a folding table under fluorescent light, close enough to pass her a pencil.

Close enough for weeks.

"I'll think about it," she said, which in the language of Harbor Row meant *I've lost and we both know it.*

"Take your time," Marguerite said sweetly, and rang up a customer who hadn't bought anything.

* * *

Sunday came gray and gold at once, the kind of October morning where the light came in low through the harbor mist and turned everything it touched the color of toast. Town hall smelled of floor wax and old coffee. Twelve folding chairs had been arranged in a lopsided circle, and Freya chose one near the door with the precision of a woman selecting an escape route.

Dottie Okafor stood at the front with a clipboard she clearly did not need, because Dottie kept everything she needed in her head and used the clipboard purely as a scepter of office. "Wonderful turnout," she announced, to a room of nine people, four of whom were related. "Now. Subcommittees. Baking operations, vendor coordination, children's activities, and—" she peered at her clipboard theatrically— "logistics and infrastructure."

Freya raised her hand for baking operations. She liked bread; bread was honest; bread did not require conversation.

"Actually," Dottie said, "we need strong organizational minds on logistics. Freya, you ran the bookshop's anniversary week flawlessly last spring. And Elias—you volunteered, I hear?"

And there he was.

He had come in quietly—of course he had, he moved like a man trying not to take up space—and taken a chair on the far side of the circle, which meant the circle now functioned as a diameter with Freya at one end and Elias Vane at the other, and every sightline in the room crossed the empty middle where their eyes might accidentally meet.

He looked better than he had at the bookshop. Or worse. It was hard to tell. He'd gained back some weight, maybe, or the flannel shirt simply fit better than the jacket had. There was a new crease between his brows that hadn't existed ten years ago, a line carved by something she hadn't been there to witness, and she hated that line with a personal fury, because it was evidence of years she had no access to. He had a history she hadn't read. He was a book she'd loved in first edition and now here was the second printing, revised, with errors corrected and whole chapters added without her.

"Logistics," Elias said. His voice was even. Only someone who had once known him very well would have heard the effort in it. "Happy to."

"Wonderful. Freya?"

Every eye in the room turned to her. Dottie's smile was benevolent and immovable, like a lighthouse.

"Fine," Freya said. "Logistics."

"Splendid. You two can coordinate directly. Exchange numbers."

The room performed the small collective inhale of a town that lived for exactly this. Mrs. Pruitt actually leaned forward. Freya recited her number in a voice she kept pleasant and dead, and watched Elias write it down on the back of a receipt with a pen that didn't work, shake the pen, borrow one from Dottie, and write it again, and something about the small domestic clumsiness of it—the soldier who could disassemble a rifle blindfolded defeated by a ballpoint—cracked something loose in her chest that she immediately sealed back up.

"Great," she said.

"Great," he said.

They did not look at each other again for the remainder of the meeting, which was a feat of navigation requiring more skill than either of them had ever needed in actual traffic.

* * *

The first phone call came Tuesday night, after June was asleep.

Freya sat at the kitchen table with a mug of tea going cold and the festival budget spreadsheet open on her laptop, and when the unknown number lit the screen she stared at it for three full rings, because answering felt like opening a door she'd boarded shut.

"Almeida," she said, using her surname like a shield.

"It's Elias." A pause. Background noise—wind, footsteps, the particular echo of a man walking outside. "Sorry to call late. I figured email was—I don't know. Email felt like a memo."

"This is a logistics call. Memos are appropriate."

Another pause. On the line she could hear him almost laugh—not quite, just the shape of it, breath moving differently. "Fair. Okay. Logistics. Dottie sent me the vendor list and half these businesses don't exist anymore. The number for Bayview Bakery goes to a boat repair shop."

"Bayview closed in 2019. Boat repair took over the lease. Try Salt & Crumb—they're new, they're on Elm, and Marta will say yes to anything if you compliment her rye."

"Salt & Crumb. Compliment the rye. Got it." Paper rustled. He was taking notes, she realized. Actually writing it down. "You know all of them."

"It's a small town. Knowing things is the local industry."

"How long has it been since—" He stopped himself. Restarted. "How long have you been running the bookshop?"

"Nine years. Ten in March." The words came out before she could vet them, and she heard, in the silence that followed, both of them doing the same arithmetic at the same time. Ten years. Ten years ago she had been twenty-four and pregnant and mailing letters to an address that never wrote back, and he had been—

"Right," he said finally, quietly. "Ten years."

"Elias—"

"The Hendersons want wood-fired ovens," he said, over her, smoothly, a man closing a door on his own hand. "Pruitts want gas. Who wins?"

"Pruitts. They always win. But let the Hendersons think they might win until the second week of October, or they'll sabotage the permits."

"Noted. Let Hendersons hope. Crush later." Another rustle of paper. "You're good at this."

"I'm good at small towns."

"Yeah," he said, and there was something underneath it, some sediment of meaning she chose not to dredge. "Goodnight, Almeida."

"Goodnight."

She sat with the cold tea for a long time after the call ended, looking at the spreadsheet, at the column labeled *VENDORS CONFIRMED*, thinking: ten years, and the first real conversation we've had is about oven fuel.

It was, she admitted to the dark kitchen, the safest conversation they'd ever had. Maybe that was why it was the easiest.

* * *

The meetings multiplied, as meetings do. Twice a week, then three times. Town hall gave way to the back room of the library, then to Marguerite's bakery after close, because Dottie declared the coffee superior and because Dottie, Freya was increasingly certain, was running her own covert operation.

The strange thing—the thing Freya could not have predicted and did not know how to hold—was that it got easier.

Not easy. Never easy. But the antagonism she'd armored herself with wore thin from handling, the way a coin's face wears smooth. They fell into a working rhythm with the inevitability of tide tables. He brought spreadsheets; she brought local knowledge. He built a master timeline color-coded with a rigor she found faintly ridiculous and secretly impressive; she edited it down to something human beings could follow. He learned the town through her—learned that the Harbormaster's wife could not be seated near the Pruitts, that the church group would volunteer only if scheduled around choir practice, that the children's pie-eating contest required a tarp, always a tarp, the year without the tarp was spoken of in whispers.

And she learned him again, in fragments, the way you learn a house you used to live in by the changes its new owners made.

He still tapped his pen twice before writing. He still read upside-down documents across the table without meaning to—he'd done that at nineteen, sprawled on her dorm bed, narrating her sociology reading aloud to annoy her, and seeing the habit survive a decade and a war landed in her ribs like a fist wrapped in velvet. He drank his coffee black now. He used to take sugar. She noticed that and then hated noticing it, cataloguing the small deaths of the man she'd known: *sugar, gone; laughter, slower; left hand, a scar along the thumb she didn't ask about.*

"You're staring at my hand," he said one Thursday, not looking up from the permit forms.

"I'm staring at the permit forms. Which are in your hand."

"Mm." He signed the last page. "Shrapnel. Kandahar. It's fine. It aches when it rains, which feels like a joke someone wrote badly."

Freya looked down at her own notes. Her throat was tight. "I wasn't going to ask."

"I know. That's why I told you." He gathered the papers, squared their edges against the table, a soldier's tidiness. "You always hated not knowing things. You'd rather have bad news than a mystery."

"You remember that."

"I remember most things," he said, and then seemed to hear himself, and stood abruptly to refill the coffee neither of them needed. "More coffee? Marguerite left a pot."

"We've had three pots."

"Then water. Hydration is also logistics."

It was absurd. It was the stupidest conversation, and she caught herself smiling at the table where he couldn't see, and pressed the smile flat with her palm like smoothing a page.

* * *

The trouble with ease, Freya thought, watching him carry chairs back to the stack at the end of the fourth week, was that ease was a slope, and slopes led somewhere.

June had started appearing at the tail end of meetings, doing homework at the bakery's corner table with a hot chocolate Marguerite supplied in violation of all nutritional law. June had also started *looking*—at Elias, then at Freya, then at Elias again, with the frank forensic interest of a child assembling a case. Last night she'd said, around a mouthful of marshmallow: "Mom, does the festival guy live upstairs?" and Freya had said yes, and June had said, "Huh," in a tone that contained entire chapters.

Tonight June had left early with Marguerite, and the bakery was empty but for the two of them and the smell of tomorrow's proofing dough, and Elias was stacking chairs, and Freya was packing the binder, and the silence had gone soft in a way that was dangerous.

"The lantern walk," she said, to fill it. "Route needs marking. I'll do it Saturday morning."

"I'll help."

"You don't have to—"

"Freya." He set down the last chair. Turned. In the low light of the bakery, with flour dust hanging in the air like held breath, he looked younger and older at once, both versions of him superimposed. "Can I say something that isn't logistics?"

Her heart performed a maneuver she did not authorize. "That sounds like a liability."

"Probably." He laughed briefly, rubbed the back of his neck. "These weeks have been—the best part of being back. That's all. The meetings are ridiculous and Dottie runs them like a war crimes tribunal, and they're still the best part. So." He picked up his coat. "Saturday. Lantern route. Early?"

"Seven," she managed.

"Seven." At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and for one suspended second she thought he was going to turn around and say something else, something with weight in it, something about ten years or letters or the way he'd looked at June like a math problem—but he only said, "Bring gloves. The marker paint stains," and stepped out into the dark.

Freya stood alone in the warm bakery, surrounded by rising dough and the ghost of his coffee cup, and understood with terrible clarity that she was in trouble.

Because the plan—the decade-long plan—had been distance. Distance was safe. Distance was survivable. But he was here, in the town, in the building, in the rhythm of her weeks, learning her again across folding tables, and every meeting filed another groove into her, and grooves, she knew better than anyone, were how a heart got worn smooth enough to break all over again.

She locked up, climbed the stairs past his dark window without looking at it, and lay awake listening to the harbor.

Saturday, she thought. Seven a.m. Lantern route. Just the two of them, and paint, and miles of quiet street.

What could possibly go right?

Across the hall of her mind, unopened, the question she'd been refusing to mail for ten years stirred in its drawer—and downstairs, in the shop below, the false drawer itself waited with its 117 secrets, patient as bread proving overnight, unaware that everything was about to rise.
