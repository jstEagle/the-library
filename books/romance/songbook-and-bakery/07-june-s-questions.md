## 7. June's Questions

The mirror had been in the bookshop longer than June had been alive. It was a tall, tarnished thing with a frame of carved ivy that Marguerite swore came off a shipwreck, and it stood at the end of the poetry aisle where the afternoon light fell through the front window at a slant, gilding everything it touched. Freya dusted it every Saturday and avoided looking into it every other day of the week, because mirrors in this shop had a way of showing a person more than she wanted to see.

It was Thursday, the last week of September, and the light was doing its autumn trick—coming in low and honey-colored, catching the dust motes, laying long stripes across the floorboards. June was supposed to be shelving returns. Instead she was standing in front of the mirror, turning her face from side to side, studying herself with the particular seriousness she brought to everything lately, as though her own face were a homework problem she intended to solve.

Freya watched her from behind the register, a stack of invoices forgotten under her hands.

June had his mouth. Freya had known it since the day she was born—had seen it in the squalling red fury of that first hour, before the nurses cleaned her up, before anyone said congratulations: *that is Elias Vane's mouth on my daughter's face.* But mouths were easy to miss. Mouths moved. What was not easy to miss, what no angle or expression could disguise, was the eyes—the deep brown, nearly black, with that faint upward tilt at the outer corner, the eyes that Elias had turned on her across the street three weeks ago when he'd stepped out of a moving van with two duffel bags and a look like a man who had walked into a wall he'd built himself.

June had those eyes. And now she was looking at them in the mirror, and then looking past her own reflection toward the window, where across the street Marguerite's bakery glowed warm against the graying afternoon, and where—for reasons Freya did not want to examine—a certain man had taken to sitting at the corner table most evenings with a coffee he barely drank.

"Do I have a dad?"

The question landed in the shop like a dropped glass. Freya heard it ring in the silence after, in the tick of the old radiator, in the creak of the floorboard by the biography section that nobody ever stepped on but always seemed to shift anyway.

She had rehearsed answers for ten years. She had answers for the playground version (*every family looks different*), for the school-forms version (she wrote *N/A* in the father's line and hated the abbreviation every single time), for the drunk-at-the-lobster-boil version, for the kind-grandmother version, for the cruel-kid-on-the-bus version. She had an answer ready for the moment June found the box of baby things in the closet, and another for the moment June did the math on dates and faces, because June was good at math and Harbor Row was a small town and faces repeated here like refrains in a song.

But she had never rehearsed an answer for *this* moment—Thursday light, tarnished mirror, the question asked not with tears or anger but with the flat, forensic calm of a girl who had already worked most of it out and only needed confirmation.

Freya set down the invoices. Her hands were steady. She made them be steady.

"What makes you ask that, sweetheart?"

June didn't turn around. In the mirror, their eyes met—hers and her daughter's, which were also, though June didn't know it yet, someone else's entirely. "The man who moved in over Marguerite's," June said. "He looks like me."

There it was. Ten years of careful architecture, and her daughter had simply looked in a mirror and drawn the straightest line between two points.

"He's got my same eyes," June went on, matter-of-fact, turning back to the shelf of returns as if she hadn't just detonated anything at all. "And Mrs. Pruitt told Kayla that he's a soldier, and Kayla told me, and I did the math." She slid a paperback into place with a small, precise motion. "I'm ten. He left town ten years ago. Mom." Now she turned, and there was nothing accusatory in her face, which was somehow worse. There was only patience. "Do I have a dad?"

*Tell her the truth.*

The thought arrived whole and immediate, the way truth does—as a single clean bell-note before all the rationalizations start their noise. *Yes. You have a dad. His name is Elias Vane. He doesn't know about you because my letters never reached him, because I believed for too long what I was too proud to test, and because after a while I stopped sending them at all. He is drinking bad coffee forty feet away from you right now, and he has your eyes, and he looked at you last Tuesday outside the bakery like a man solving a math problem because he was. He was doing your math. He was doing mine.*

That was the truth. Freya held it in her mouth like a stone.

But truth, she had learned, was not a single thing. Truth was a house with many rooms, and some of them were load-bearing, and if you knocked out the wrong wall the whole structure came down on the people you loved most. There was June's truth—*you are loved, you were wanted, you were never a secret to be ashamed of*—and there was Elias's truth, which was not hers to tell, and there was Helena Vane's truth, which was a poison Freya would not bring into her daughter's bloodstream, and there was her own truth, which was the hardest one, the one about pride and fear and 117 sealed envelopes hidden in a false drawer twelve feet from where her daughter was standing.

And underneath all of it, older than all of it, ran the simple animal instinct that had governed her for a decade: *protect her.* Protect her from the story that she was the reason a man left, or the reason a man stayed away. Protect her from the grandmother who had decided, before June could walk, that she was an obstacle to be managed. Protect her from hope, which was the cruelest thing of all to hand a child and then take back.

"Come sit with me," Freya said.

June hesitated—she was at an age where being summoned to *sit* felt like a prelude to a lecture—but she came, folding herself onto the old velvet bench beneath the window, tucking her feet up, waiting. The light caught the fine hairs at her temple, the same dark brown as—

*Stop,* Freya told herself. *You can't spend the rest of your life flinching at genetics.*

"Yes," Freya said. "You have a dad."

June's breath caught. Just once, just a small hitch, quickly mastered—and Freya saw in that hitch how much the question had cost her, how long she must have carried it, how carefully she must have waited for a moment when asking wouldn't look like wanting too much. Ten years old, and she had already learned to ration her wants. Freya's chest ached with something that was equal parts love and self-loathing.

"His name is Elias," Freya said. "Elias Vane."

June absorbed this. Outside, a delivery truck rattled past; the windowpanes shivered in their frames. "The soldier."

"The soldier," Freya confirmed. "He grew up here. He went away when I was nineteen. He—" *He loved me. He asked me to come with him. I said the town needed me and my mother needed me and I couldn't, and I let him go because I was twenty-two and stupid and I thought love that real would survive anything, even silence.* "We lost touch," she said, which was true, and also the largest lie she had ever spoken aloud, because they hadn't *lost* touch; touch had been taken, intercepted, burned in a fireplace in Boston by a woman who thought she was saving her son.

"So he doesn't know about me."

Here it was. The fork in the road. Freya felt it open beneath her like weather.

She could say yes. She could let June believe her father had simply never known, that the absence was an accident of geography and timing, that no one had chosen it. That was almost true. It was true enough to be defensible, true enough to sleep on. But it wasn't the whole truth, and the whole truth had a shape June would eventually find, the way water finds the crack in a foundation. And if June ever learned that her mother had known where Elias was—that Freya had known his unit, his base, his mother's address, had written to all of them, and then, somewhere in year three, grief curdling into pride, had simply *stopped*—then the partial truth would curdle too, into something worse than a lie. Into evidence.

"No," Freya said carefully. "He doesn't know about you."

Not: *he never knew.* Not: *no one told him.* Just: *he doesn't know.* Present tense. A door left technically open. Even now, even mid-deception, some part of her was leaving herself an exit, and she despised herself for the cowardice of the phrasing and clung to it anyway.

June nodded slowly, processing. Then: "Does he know about *you?*"

"I think so. Yes."

"And he moved here anyway?" Something flickered across June's face—not hope exactly, but the place where hope would go if it were ever allowed to build. "To Harbor Row?"

"It's a good town," Freya said, and heard how thin it sounded. "People come back to good towns."

June looked at her for a long moment. She had Freya's stubbornness—everyone said so, Marguerite said it weekly, with delight—and she had Elias's stillness, the ability to hold a gaze without blinking, and right now both inheritances were trained on her mother with terrible combined force.

"You knew him," June said. "Before. Didn't you."

It wasn't a question. Freya realized, with a lurch, that she had answered it already—in the way she'd said his name, in the pause before *we lost touch*, in whatever had passed over her face when June said *the soldier*. Her daughter had read her the way June read everything: closely, quietly, completely.

"Yes," Freya said. "I knew him. Before."

"Were you friends?"

"We were more than friends."

June considered this with visible effort, assembling a world. "So you loved him. And he loved you. And then he went away, and I happened, and he doesn't know, and now he's *here*, eating muffins across the street." She said it all in one breath, flatly, like reciting a proof, and then her voice cracked on the last word despite herself. "Mom, why doesn't he know?"

*Because his mother burned my letters. Because I stopped writing. Because I was afraid that if I reached out and he chose, again, not to come—if I handed him the truth and he set it down—it would finally, officially, be over, and I would have to stop living in the space between sent and unsent where I have kept myself alive for ten years. Because telling him means risking him, and I have survived losing him twice, but I do not know if I survive watching him learn about you and hesitate.*

What she said was: "Sometimes grown-ups make mistakes. Big ones. Ones that take a long time to fix."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Freya admitted. "It isn't."

June pulled her knees tighter to her chest. For a moment she looked five years old again, small enough to fit in the crook of Freya's arm, back when the questions were easy—*why is the moon following us, where does the wind sleep*—and the answers could be invented freely, generously, without consequence. "Kayla says kids whose dads leave don't get picked for stuff. Like, teachers assume things."

Freya's whole body went cold. "Is that happening? At school?"

"No." June shrugged, too fast. "But it could."

"Oh, sweetheart." Freya crossed the aisle and sat beside her on the bench, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Listen to me. Whatever happens next—you hear me?—you were never left. Do you understand? You were never left, not for one second, not by anyone who mattered. You were wanted before you existed. I used to write letters about you before you could even—" She stopped herself, one syllable too late.

June's head came up sharply. "Letters? What letters?"

The radiator ticked. Somewhere in the stacks, the floorboard creaked its phantom creak. Freya felt the false drawer in the back office the way you feel a bruise—aware of it constantly, guarding it without thinking, twelve inches of cedar and a lock Marguerite had sworn no one would ever find.

"Letters I used to write," Freya said. "A long time ago. To—to people I missed. It helped, writing them. Even when I couldn't send them."

"Why couldn't you send them?"

Because I was proud. Because I was scared. Because his mother taught me that reaching for him was reaching into a fire, and I believed her, and then I made my own fire out of silence and called it dignity. Because I am asking you to trust me with your whole heart while I show you a fraction of mine.

"Because sometimes," Freya said slowly, choosing each word like a step across ice, "the person you need to say something to feels very far away. Even when they're close. And you keep practicing what you'd say, and practicing, until the practicing becomes its own kind of—of home. And it's hard to leave home. Even to say something true."

June stared at her. "That's the weirdest thing you've ever said."

"I know."

"It doesn't make any sense."

"I know that too."

But something in June's face shifted—some child's intuition catching an adult's grief and recognizing the family resemblance. She leaned her head against Freya's shoulder, the way she had when she was small, and for a while neither of them spoke. Across the street, through the honeyed window, Freya could see the corner table of the bakery. Empty now. His cup still there, half-finished, the way he always left it, as if he meant to come back for it. As if he meant to come back for everything.

"Mom?" June said at last, drowsy with the weight of the day and the strangeness of it. "If he lives here now... will I get to meet him?"

And there it was—the question under the question, the one Freya had truly been dodging. Not *do I have a dad* but *will I have one*. Future tense. Hope, building its little house on Freya's silence.

"I don't know," Freya said honestly, because that much, at least, she owed her. "Maybe. Soon. I promise I won't decide things about your life without telling you. Not anymore."

*"Anymore,"* June repeated, catching the word like a fisherman catches a glint in the water. "What do you mean, anymore?"

"I mean," Freya said, rising, smoothing her apron, retreating toward the register and the safe arithmetic of invoices, "that we should figure out dinner. Pasta or soup?"

"Pasta," June said automatically, but her eyes stayed narrowed, cataloguing the dodge, filing it away. She was her mother's daughter. She would wait. She was very good at waiting; she'd learned it from watching.

* * *

That night, after pasta, after homework, after the lamp in June's room clicked off and the small sounds of her breathing settled into rhythm, Freya sat alone in the shop with the lights low and unlocked the false drawer.

One hundred and seventeen envelopes. She counted them out of habit, fanned them like a losing hand. The earliest ones were soft-cornered, handled often; the recent ones were crisp, untouched, written more out of discipline than belief. Letter 40-something was where the tone changed—where hope gave way to something harder. Letter 90 was where she'd started describing June in detail, unable to help herself, writing *she lost a tooth today, she has your exact scowl when she loses at cards* to a man who would never read it, because if the letters were going nowhere they might as well carry everything.

She took out a blank sheet of paper. Letter 118, unwritten, unaddressed, impossible.

*Dear Elias,* she wrote, and then stopped, pen hovering, because every opening she owned belonged to the girl she'd been at twenty-two, and she no longer knew how to speak to the man who drank abandoned coffee across the street and looked at her daughter like a math problem he was afraid to solve.

In the morning, she would seal nothing new. In the morning, June would ask follow-up questions over toast, sharp ones, and Freya would give her more partial truths and hate herself more thoroughly each time. And in the evening, when the bells above the shop door chimed and Elias Vane stepped inside pretending to browse military history he clearly had no interest in, Freya would look up and see her daughter's eyes in his face and understand that the drawer was not going to stay locked much longer—not because of locks, but because secrets, like bread, only rise so far before they break their crust.

She just didn't know yet that it would be June who broke it—or how soon.
