## 8. Flour on His Hands

The bakery at four in the morning was a different country.

Elias had learned this over the past week, standing in Marguerite's kitchen with flour dusting his forearms like a second skin, watching her hands move through dough with the certainty of someone who had made ten thousand loaves and intended to make ten thousand more. The ovens breathed heat into the dark. The windows above the sink were black except for the streetlamp across the way, and beyond it, the bookshop, shuttered and sleeping, its upstairs window dark too—the window he had caught himself checking every night since he'd moved in, the way a man checks a wound to see if it's still bleeding.

"Again," Marguerite said, nodding at the dough on his board. "You're fighting it."

"I'm not fighting it."

"You are. You're treating it like an enemy combatant." She said it without looking up, her attention on the tray of cinnamon buns she was icing with long, unhurried strokes. "Bread doesn't respond well to that. It wants patience. It wants you to stop trying to control the outcome and just—pay attention."

He looked down at his dough, which had gone lopsided and sullen under his hands. "This is why I was infantry," he said. "Nobody asked me to be gentle."

"Everybody's asked to be gentle eventually." She set down the spatula and came around the counter, and without ceremony she took his wrists and adjusted them—turned his palms flat, eased the pressure from his fingers. Her hands were small and strong and smelled of yeast and cardamom. "There. Like that. You're not shaping it, you're listening to it. Feel when it stops resisting?"

He felt it—or thought he did—a softness in the dough where there had been tension, a kind of surrender that wasn't defeat. Something in his chest pulled tight. He hadn't been asked to be gentle in a long time. He hadn't been very good at it even before.

Marguerite released him and went back to her buns. "My mother taught me that," she said. "She ran this shop for thirty years before me. She used to say you could tell everything about a person by how they handled bread. Whether they were afraid of it. Whether they were angry at it. Whether they loved it enough to wait."

"What does my bread say about me?"

"That you're terrified," she said pleasantly, "and furious about being terrified, and that you'd rather do push-ups than sit still with a feeling." She glanced up, and her eyes—dark, shrewd, kind—held him for a moment longer than the joke required. "But we're working on it."

* * *

They worked in something like silence after that, broken only by the tick of the cooling racks and the low groan of the ovens. Elias found, to his surprise, that he liked the work. There was no ambiguity in it. You measured, you waited, you turned the heat on and off. The bread rose or it didn't, and either way you got up tomorrow and did it again. After ten years of decisions that ended lives and saved lives and never once came with clear instructions, the simplicity of it was almost holy.

It was also, he understood now, a trap. A benevolent one. Marguerite had not invited him here to teach him to bake. He'd known it since Tuesday, when she'd appeared at his door with a tray of rolls and the announcement that she needed extra hands for festival prep and that his rent could stand a small adjustment in labor. He'd known it since she'd watched him fumble the first batch and said, mildly, "You have soldier's hands. Everything's a task. Freya was the same when she first started helping me—everything had to be done perfectly or not at all. Grief does that."

She'd said the name lightly, the way you'd mention weather. And then she'd kept saying it, all week, in passing, in parentheses, until Elias understood that the whole week was a parenthesis built around that name, and that sooner or later the sentence would close.

It closed at five-forty, when the light outside went from black to bruised gray and Marguerite slid two cups of coffee onto the flour-dusted counter and sat down across from him on a stool, folding her hands like a woman settling in for exactly what she'd come for.

"So," she said. "Ask me."

"Ask you what?"

"Elias." She said his name the way you'd tap a glass to get a room's attention. "You've been circling it for six days. You ask about the festival, about the shop hours, about whether June takes cream in her cocoa—you asked me that, by the way, as if I wouldn't notice—and every question is a door you're standing in front of and refusing to open. So. Ask me. I've known that woman since she was nineteen years old and crying in my back room because a boy she loved was leaving. I will tell you almost anything. But you have to ask."

The ovens ticked. Somewhere below the floor, pipes knocked. Elias wrapped both hands around his cup because his hands wanted something to hold that wasn't shaking.

"How old is she?" he said finally. "June."

Marguerite didn't blink. "Ten. She'll be eleven in March."

March. He did the arithmetic the way he'd been trained to do it—fast, cold, precise—and the answer arrived like a round: nine months after he'd left. Nine months after the last night, the night before deployment, when they'd stood in the rain behind the bookshop and he'd told her he'd write, he'd call, he'd come back, and she'd pressed her face into his coat and said nothing at all, which he'd taken then for grief and now understood might have been knowledge. Had she known? Had she stood in that rain with a secret already turning inside her and let him walk away believing the only thing he'd leave behind was her?

"She never wrote to me," he said. His voice came out strange. Flat. "In ten years. Not once. I want to know why."

Marguerite studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face seemed to decide something. "And you never wrote to her."

"I wrote. First year, constantly. Then—" He stopped. The truth was uglier than the summary. The truth was that the letters had stopped coming back marked undeliverable, which he'd taken for an answer; that he'd written fewer, then fewer, then none; that somewhere in year three he'd told himself a man couldn't spend his life mailing letters into a silence, and had called it moving on, and had believed it right up until three weeks ago when he'd seen Freya Almeida crossing the street with a paper bag of flour in her arms and the ground had opened under him like it always should have and never had.

"The letters stopped coming back," he said. "I checked. I would have—I'd have driven here. I'd have walked. If I'd known she was—"

"If you'd known," Marguerite said, not unkindly, "you'd have what? Quit? Come home to a town with no base within two hundred miles? Told your mother no?" She shook her head slowly. "Boy, I'm not asking that to be cruel. I'm asking because Freya asked herself the same question for ten years, and the answer she got was that you'd chosen your career, your mother's plans, the life that had no room in it for a girl who smells like bread flour and reads poetry to customers. That was the story she lived inside. Somebody built that story for her. I've never been able to prove who."

Something cold moved down his spine. "What do you mean, somebody built it?"

"I mean," Marguerite said, wrapping her hands around her own cup, "that Freya wrote letters. For years. Real ones, stamped ones, mailed ones—I took her to the post office myself the first time, because her hands were shaking so badly she couldn't manage the little box. Dozens of them. Maybe hundreds. And she never got one back, and neither did anyone else ever see you at the holidays or hear from you through friends, and after a while a person starts to believe the silence is the message." She lifted her eyes to his. "Did you get any of them?"

"No." The word came out of him like something dislodged. "Not one. I never—" His mind was already moving, fast and cold, down corridors he didn't want to enter. His mother's visits. His mother's habit of arriving unannounced and staying for weeks. His mother's cheerful efficiency with his mail, his paperwork, his life. *Your father and I only want what's best for you.* The P.O. box his mother had insisted on managing during deployments, so he wouldn't be burdened. "I need to make some calls," he heard himself say.

"You need to finish your coffee first," Marguerite said, "and you need to hear the rest, because if you run out that door right now you'll go to her with rage instead of questions, and rage gets you nothing but a slammed door and another decade." She waited until he sat back down. He hadn't realized he'd stood. "Now. Ask me the rest. You're allowed."

He looked at his coffee. When he spoke, his voice was barely above the hum of the ovens.

"What was she like? These ten years. What did she—how did she live?"

"Like a woman keeping a lamp lit," Marguerite said simply. "She came back here with a baby and no explanation and took a job shelving books, and everyone in this town knew whose eyes that child had, and nobody said a word, because that's what Harbor Row does—it protects people by pretending not to see them. She worked. She raised June. She read to her every night—still does, I think, though June pretends she's too old for it. She never dated. Not once. Men tried. She'd smile that polite smile of hers and talk about inventory." Marguerite paused. "Every year on the same day she closes the shop early. Third Saturday of September. Nobody knows why. She comes in here afterward and drinks tea and doesn't talk, and I don't ask, and she goes home."

Third Saturday of September. He counted backward without meaning to, and landed on a date that made his ears ring: the day he'd shipped out. The day the bus left from the depot on Route 9, and Freya had stood in the lot with her arms wrapped around herself and hadn't cried until the bus was out of sight.

"That's the day I left," he said.

"I know," said Marguerite. "I drove her home from the depot. She didn't speak for two days."

The ovens ticked. Elias put his head in his hands. Ten years. Ten years of a woman closing her shop on the anniversary of his leaving, lighting a lamp for a man who had never once received her letters, raising a daughter who had his eyes and his stubborn chin and apparently his talent for asking uncomfortable questions—and he had spent those same ten years becoming excellent at his job and terrible at his life, telling himself the silence was an answer, letting his mother curate his mail, coming home at last to a town he'd chosen because some part of him, some part he hadn't examined until this exact moment, had known she'd still be here.

"Why didn't she tell me?" he asked. It came out raw. "About June. All she had to do was—one letter. One phone call. I'd have—"

"Would you have?" Marguerite asked, and there was no cruelty in it, only the terrible gentleness of a woman who had watched this whole story from the counter of her shop for a decade. "Think carefully before you answer, because she asked herself that question every single day for ten years, and if you answer wrong, I will defend her choice with everything I have." She leaned forward. "A nineteen-year-old girl writes dozens of letters and gets nothing back. What is she supposed to conclude? That the letter announcing a baby would land differently than the others? She was protecting that child from being half-wanted. From being a line item in a custody negotiation conducted over a satellite phone between missions. She chose to give June a whole love instead of a divided one. Right or wrong—that was the reasoning. I've heard it at two in the morning more times than I can count."

"And you never told me either."

"I wasn't asked," Marguerite said. "And it wasn't mine to tell." She rose, collected his empty cup, and refilled it without asking, setting it in front of him like a penance. "But here's what is yours to know, and I'll say it once. That child is not a secret, Elias. She's a person. She does her homework at that table by the window. She alphabetizes my pastry case when she thinks I'm not looking. Last month she asked me, very casually, why her mother keeps letters in a locked drawer, and I nearly dropped a tray." She held his gaze. "Whatever happens next—and something is going to happen next, because this town has the patience of a saint and the memory of an elephant—you do not get to arrive late and make demands. You get to earn. Slowly. The way bread rises. Understand?"

He understood. He understood, also, that his hands were trembling again, and that the tremor had nothing to do with the discharge, the nightmares, the reason a twenty-nine-year-old career soldier suddenly had time to bake bread at four in the morning.

"One more thing," Marguerite said, softer now. She nodded toward the window, where the gray had turned to pale gold and the streetlamp clicked off. Across the street, the bookshop's upstairs light flicked on. A silhouette moved behind the curtain—small, quick, unmistakably a child getting ready for school. "Her birthday," Marguerite said. "March fourteenth. She was born at 3:17 in the morning, in the middle of a storm that took the power out for two days. Freya delivered her in the bathtub with old Mrs. Okafor holding her hand, because the roads were flooded and the ambulance couldn't cross the bridge." She smiled faintly. "Freya says June arrived the way she does everything—early, loud, and on her own schedule."

March fourteenth. 3:17 a.m. A storm. A bathtub. A hand to hold that wasn't his.

Elias looked at the light across the street—at the small silhouette that was his daughter, pulling on her boots, arguing presumably with her mother about breakfast—and felt the full weight of the arithmetic settle over him like snowfall. Ten years. Three thousand six hundred and fifty days. A hundred and seventeen months of mornings he hadn't been there for. Birthdays. Fevers. First days of school. Lost teeth. Storms. All of it accumulated, itemized, filed away in a ledger he hadn't known existed, and the balance due was not a number he could pay. Not today. Maybe not ever in full.

But Marguerite had said *earn*. And he was, he was discovering, a man who could follow instructions.

"Teach me the buns again," he said quietly. "The cinnamon ones. The ones June likes."

Marguerite's face did something complicated—grief and approval and something older than both. "She likes them with extra icing," she said. "On Saturdays. She comes in at eight sharp and orders one and eats it at the window table and pretends she isn't reading over the shoulder of whoever sits nearest." She tied her apron tighter. "All right, soldier. Flour first. And this time—"

"Gently," he said. "I know."

* * *

By seven the bakery smelled like a promise. Trays of festival bread lined the racks—cardamom knots, rye rounds, the honey loaves Marguerite was famous for—and Elias stood at the counter with his sleeves rolled, icing buns with a concentration he usually reserved for weapons maintenance. His first attempts were ugly. His fifth were passable. By the tenth, Marguerite pronounced them "almost edible," which he suspected was high praise.

At 7:52, the bell over the bakery door rang.

He didn't look up immediately. He'd learned the rhythm of the morning regulars—fisherman Tomás at 6:15, the schoolteacher Mrs. Pruitt at 7:30, the retired ferry captain who ordered the same rye toast every day of his natural life. But this was earlier than June's usual Saturday, and something in the quality of the silence told him—

He looked up.

June Vane—no. June Almeida. June Almeida stood in the doorway in a yellow raincoat two sizes too big, her backpack slung over one shoulder, her dark hair escaping its braid in every direction. She was staring at him with an expression he recognized from mirrors and photographs: the particular narrowed look of a person doing math.

"You're here early," she said.

"I'm helping Marguerite," he said. His voice held. He was almost proud of it. "For the festival."

June's eyes tracked from his face to the tray of buns to the flour on his forearms and back. Behind the counter, Marguerite had become very interested in the espresso machine.

"Do you know how to ice those?" June asked. "Because last week you made the ones that looked like roadkill."

"They were structurally sound."

"They were *tragic*." She crossed to the window table, dropped her backpack, and held up one finger to Marguerite. "Cinnamon bun. Extra icing. Please." Then she sat, pulled a battered paperback from her bag, and opened it upside down.

Elias stared. She'd done it deliberately—he was almost certain she'd done it deliberately—to see if he'd correct her. He crossed the room, took the book gently from her hands, turned it right side up, and handed it back.

June looked at the book. Then at him. Something passed across her face—too fast to name, gone before it landed.

"Huh," she said, and took her bun when Marguerite set it down, and ate it at the window with her back to him, and did not speak to him again.

But when she left at 8:20, backpack on, yellow coat bright against the gray street, she paused at the door and looked back—not at him, exactly, but at the tray of buns he'd iced, the ones Marguerite was boxing up for the festival, and she said, to the room in general, to the bell above the door, to the flour hanging gold in the morning light:

"Those ones are better."

Then she was gone, and Elias stood in the middle of the warm bakery with flour on his hands and his heart going like a drumline, and Marguerite said, from behind the espresso machine, very quietly:

"Now you know what you're fighting for. Go home. Sleep. And Elias—" she waited until he turned. "Tomorrow, when you see her mother across the street, don't freeze this time. Say good morning. It's a start."

He nodded. He picked up his duffel. At the door he paused, looking back at the trays of buns, the window table, the chair where a girl in a yellow coat had sat doing math with his eyes.

"Marguerite," he said. "Does Freya know you told me all this?"

Marguerite wiped down the counter, unhurried, unrepentant. "Freya knows," she said, "that I keep a bakery, and that I keep my own counsel, and that some things rise whether you want them to or not." She glanced up, and her eyes were kind and absolutely immovable. "But you should hurry home, soldier. Because Freya's about to find out that her daughter spent twenty minutes alone in a room with her father—and knowing that child, she's going to have questions of her own."

Across the street, the bookshop lights were coming on.
