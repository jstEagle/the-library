## 10. Almost Told

The midway went up the way all good things in Harbor Row did—slowly, argumentatively, and with more string lights than any fire marshal would ever approve.

Freya stood in the middle of Harbor Lane at seven o'clock on setup night, a clipboard against her hip and a pencil tucked behind her ear, watching Otis Pruett's ladder wobble dangerously near the awning of the hardware store while he strung bulbs across the lane like a man decorating for a war he intended to win.

"Otis, left side first," she called. "You're fighting gravity and losing."

"I'm *improvising*," Otis called back, with the dignity of a man who had hung these lights for thirty-one years and considered every suggestion an insult to his ancestors.

The autumn bread festival had swallowed her whole for three weeks now—committee meetings twice a week, vendor calls, a spreadsheet Marguerite called "obsessive" and Freya called "the only thing keeping me sane." She had thrown herself into it with the particular ferocity of a woman who needed somewhere to put her hands so they wouldn't shake. The festival was flour and yeast and small-town pride, and it was also, though she'd never say this aloud, the perfect place to hide. There was always another form to fill out. Always another crate to count. A person could drown productively in logistics, and no one would ever know she was drowning at all.

Except that Elias Vane had volunteered for the committee too.

He'd shown up to the second meeting with a legal pad and the quiet competence of a man who had spent a decade organizing things under pressure, and he'd said, when Marguerite asked why, "It gets me out of the apartment." Which everyone had accepted at face value, because everyone in Harbor Row understood the universal language of needing to be elsewhere. But Freya had watched him across that folding table and thought: *he isn't escaping his mother's phone calls. He's escaping something else. He's escaping me, or he's coming toward me, and I don't know which is worse.*

For three weeks they had orbited each other through spreadsheets and stall assignments, polite as diplomats, careful as bomb technicians. He'd learned to make her laugh twice—twice, exactly, both accidental, both immediately regretted. She'd learned the way he rubbed the scar along his jaw when he was thinking, the way he counted things under his breath, the way he looked at June—

No. She wasn't going to think about that tonight.

"Freya!" Marguerite appeared at her elbow carrying two paper cups of cider from the church stand, steam curling into the cold air. She pressed one into Freya's free hand with the subtlety of a woman throwing a switchblade. "Elias needs someone to walk him through the stall layout. I would do it, but my knees are acting up, and also I am a coward."

"Marge—"

"The map's in your clipboard. Go. Be helpful. It's your whole personality." And Marguerite was gone, retreating toward the bakery with the speed of a much younger woman, leaving Freya holding warm cider and a trap she could see perfectly well and couldn't refuse.

* * *

Elias was at the far end of the lane, kneeling over a stack of folding tables, and the sight of him like that—sleeves pushed up, hair falling into his eyes, hands busy—did something unfair to her chest. He'd filled out again since September. The hospital thinness was gone; there was weight on his shoulders now that was muscle instead of absence. He looked like a man being rebuilt, plank by plank, and she hated how much she wanted to hand him nails.

"Marguerite deserted you?" he asked, looking up. His smile was small and real and dangerous.

"She has traitorous knees."

"And here I thought she was matchmaking." He said it lightly, testing the word in the air between them, and Freya felt her whole body go still the way water goes still before it freezes.

"She's seventy-one," Freya said. "She thinks everyone should be paired off like socks."

"And are we?" He rose, brushing sawdust from his jeans. "Paired off?"

"We're co-committee members." She opened the clipboard with more force than necessary. "Stall layout. The bakers go on the north side where the wind won't kill their proofing dough, the cider press goes by the church because Father Anselm insists on being near the exits, and the children's games—" Her voice caught, just slightly, on *children's*, and she covered it by flipping a page. "The children's games go in the square, away from the road."

"You've got it all mapped," he said. Not mocking. Something softer than that, which was worse.

"Somebody has to."

They worked. That was the mercy of festivals: there was always something to carry. They unfolded tables together, measured distances with a twine line, argued amiably about whether the honey stall belonged next to the rye bread (it did not; Mrs. Okonkwo and the Hendersons had a feud older than June). The string lights came on above them one strand at a time, gold and crooked and beautiful, and the whole lane turned soft-edged and amber, and Freya let herself forget, for almost twenty minutes, that she was standing inside the exact scenario she'd spent ten years avoiding.

Almost twenty minutes.

Then Elias straightened up from the last table, wiped his palms on his thighs, and said, "Can I ask you something? Not committee business."

Her stomach dropped through the cobblestones. "That sounds ominous."

"It's not. I don't think it's not." He laughed, short and unsteady, and rubbed the scar on his jaw—the tell, the tell, she knew all his tells, that was the whole problem, ten years hadn't erased a single one of them. "It's about June."

The name landed in Freya's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it fall and fall and never hit bottom.

"What about her?" she said, and was proud of how level it came out.

"She came into the shop last week. The bookshop. I was dropping off the committee minutes and she was shelving returns, and she—" He stopped. Started again. "She alphabetizes by color when she thinks no one's watching. Did you know that?"

"No," Freya said, which was a lie, because of course she knew that, she'd known it since June was six, and hearing him say it—*did you know that*, like they were two parents comparing notes over a kitchen table—made her vision swim at the edges.

"She's funny," Elias said. "She made a joke about my handwriting and didn't even look up. And she's got this—" His voice did something complicated. "When she squints at a book spine, she does this thing with her mouth. Like she's arguing with it."

"Elias."

"And today at the harbor she was skipping stones, and she got four skips, and she turned around to see if anyone saw, and I—" He exhaled. The string lights buzzed faintly overhead. Somewhere down the lane, Otis swore at a knot in the wiring. "I know what I'm about to say is going to sound insane, and I need you to let me finish before you decide anything, okay? Because I've been doing the math for weeks, and I keep getting the same answer, and I don't know what to do with it, but I think—"

"*Elias.*"

The word came out sharper than she meant it to, sharp enough that he actually stopped, and the silence between them went taut as wire. Freya gripped the clipboard like a shield. This was the moment. She could feel it standing right there in the lamplight with them, enormous and patient: the moment she'd rehearsed in the shower, on the stairs, in the dark of her bedroom at 2 a.m., a thousand times, ten thousand times. *June was born on March ninth. She has your eyes. Her middle name is Wren because you told me once that wrens sing louder than birds ten times their size, and I wanted her to have something of yours that wasn't just bone.*

One sentence. One true sentence. Ten years of rehearsal, and all she had to do was open her mouth.

What came out instead was: "The permit for the generator hasn't come through."

Elias blinked. "What?"

"The generator. For the stage. The town office said five business days and it's been eight, and if it doesn't come tomorrow I have to call Councilman Reyes, and he only answers before noon, so I really—" She was talking fast now, faster, the words tumbling out like coins from a torn pocket. "And the Hendersons want to move their stall again, which means renumbering the whole east row, and the ribbon for the opening ceremony is the wrong blue, apparently *historically wrong*, according to Otis, who has opinions about ribbon the way other men have opinions about war. So if you'll excuse me, I should—I need to go deal with that. Tonight. Before it becomes a bigger problem."

"Freya." Gentle. Terribly gentle. "The permit can wait until morning."

"Nothing waits until morning in this town. You learn that fast or you drown." She was already backing up a step, then another, the clipboard clutched to her chest like armor plating. "I'll email you the revised stall numbers. Check your spam folder, the town server flags everything."

"Freya, wait—"

"Goodnight, Elias. Thanks for the tables."

And she turned and walked away from him, down the lane, past the crooked golden lights, past Otis muttering at his ladder, past the church where Father Anselm was locking up the cider, her boots striking the cobblestones in a rhythm that almost sounded calm. Behind her she could feel him watching. She didn't turn around. Turning around would have been the end of her.

* * *

She made it three blocks before her legs gave out on her, and she sat down hard on the low stone wall outside the shuttered chandlery, the clipboard sliding to the ground, her breath coming in ragged pulls that fogged the cold air.

*Idiot,* she told herself. *Coward. Ten years. Ten years of writing to a dead address, ten years of practicing one sentence in the dark, and when the moment finally stands in front of you wearing a flannel shirt and asking permission to hope—you talk to him about generators.*

But her hands were shaking too badly for self-contempt to stick. Because here was the truth she couldn't say even to herself, sitting on a cold wall in the dark: it wasn't the telling she feared. She'd have told him years ago if telling were all it cost. It was what came after. The arithmetic. Every birthday he'd missed. Every fever, every first day of school, every scraped knee, every night June had asked some version of the question and Freya had handed her a partial truth wrapped in a bow. If she spoke the sentence, all those years became real—not abstract, not survivable, but *counted*, itemized, laid out between them like evidence. He would look at her the way people look at a debt they've just discovered they owe. Or worse: the way people look at a debt they've been robbed of paying.

And underneath all of it, quieter, meaner: *what if he does the math and wishes the answer were different?*

A paper cup of cider sat forgotten in her hand, gone cold. Down the lane, faintly, she could hear the festival taking shape without her—laughter, the scrape of table legs, Otis finally winning his war with the lights until the whole street glowed like something out of a life she'd been too afraid to live.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Elias.

*Permits can wait. Some things shouldn't. — E*

She stared at it for a long time, thumb hovering, the rehearsed sentence rising in her throat like a tide and breaking apart against her teeth, as it always did, as it always had.

Then she locked the screen, picked up her clipboard, and walked home through the dark to a house where her daughter was doing homework at the kitchen table, alphabetizing nothing, asking nothing, waiting for nothing she knew to wait for.

In her coat pocket, her fingers found the corner of an envelope she'd started that morning and abandoned—letter one hundred eighteen, unsent, unwritten, addressed to a man who now lived forty feet above cinnamon and flour and had almost, *almost*, said her daughter's name out loud.

Tomorrow, she promised herself. Tomorrow she would find the words.

Down the street behind her, unnoticed, Elias Vane stood alone under the crooked golden lights, holding the cider he'd bought for her, watching the empty space where she'd been—and making, quietly, a decision of his own.
