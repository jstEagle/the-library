## 12. Confrontation at Closing Time

The bell above the bookshop door had been broken for three weeks. Freya had meant to fix it—had even bought the little brass mechanism from the hardware store on Delancey—but it sat in the drawer behind the register like a small unfinished apology, and so when Elias Vane walked into Almeida Books at four minutes past closing, he did it silently, without announcement, the way grief always entered her life.

She was crouched behind the poetry section with a stack of returns in her arms, and she heard him before she saw him: not footsteps exactly, but the particular quality of silence a large man makes when he is trying to decide whether to exist in a room. She knew that silence. She had catalogued it for ten years across kitchen tables and harbor benches and one disastrous church funeral where they had stood on opposite sides of a coffin and said nothing at all.

She stood up too fast. A paperback slid from the stack and hit the floorboards with a slap.

"June called you," she said. Not *hello*. Not *how did you find out*. There was no use pretending she didn't know why he was here; there had been no use pretending anything for ten years, really, and she was tired down to the marrow of pretending.

"She called me." His voice was rough, scraped raw, as though he'd been shouting at the sea or at himself or at the long gray stretch of road between his flat and her shop. "From the payphone outside the chowder place. She said—" He stopped. Pressed two fingers against the bridge of his nose. "She said, *You should come see what my mom keeps in a drawer.*"

Freya set the remaining books down on the shelf with exaggerated care, because if she didn't give her hands something precise to do they would shake, and she refused—she absolutely refused—to let him watch them shake.

"It's not a drawer of secrets," she said. "It's a drawer of letters."

"I read eleven of them." He came further into the shop, and the lamplight caught him—the same jaw, the same too-serious mouth, but thinner now, carved harder, a man who had been remade by things she hadn't witnessed. "Eleven before I couldn't—I had to stop, Freya. I had to sit down on the floor of your stockroom like a—" He laughed, and it was an awful sound, airless. "Like a man finding out his whole life happened without him."

"The flip the sign," she said stupidly. "You're supposed to flip the sign to closed."

"Freya."

"You didn't flip the sign."

"*Freya.*"

The word landed like a hand on her chest. She turned to face him fully then, and saw that his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and wet all at once, and that fury, she understood with a strange floating clarity, was not aimed at her so much as through her—at the decade itself, at the machinery of absence, at every person and circumstance that had conspired to make him a stranger in his own story.

"Say something," he said. "You've written a hundred and seventeen letters. You clearly know how. Say something now, out loud, to my face."

* * *

She had rehearsed this moment. That was the humiliating truth of it. In the shower, on the ferry, in the dead hours after June went to sleep, she had rehearsed a hundred versions of this confrontation—the tearful one, the dignified one, the angry one where she made him understand that she had been young and scared and twenty-three years old with a positive test and a mailbox full of silence. In every version she won. In every version she said her piece and he gathered her up and the ten years folded themselves away like linen.

None of the versions survived contact with the actual man standing in her shop smelling of cold salt air and something like despair.

"I wrote the first letter the week you deployed," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Do you remember? You were gone nine days and I already couldn't stand it. I wrote about the bakery burning the cinnamon rolls, about Mrs. Okafor's cat having kittens under the poetry table. Trivial things. Everything trivial. Because the true thing felt too enormous to put in an envelope."

"How many?"

"One hundred and seventeen."

"And how many did he answer?" His voice cracked on *he*, and she realized he was talking about himself in the third person, the way people do when they can't bear to be the person in the sentence. "How many letters did Elias Vane write back to the woman who claims she loved him?"

"None." She held his gaze because looking away would have been worse. "Because none of them reached you. Your mother—"

"I know what my mother did." He said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "I found out yesterday. She burned them. All of them. She kept one—for leverage, God help me—as if our lives were a negotiation she was winning." He dragged a hand through his hair. "I know, Freya. I'm not here about her. I'm here about the part you haven't explained yet. The part June read while sitting cross-legged on your stockroom floor."

Freya's stomach dropped through the floorboards, through the foundation, through the bedrock of Harbor Row itself.

"What part—"

"Letter forty-one," he said. "Dated March, six years ago. You wrote—*"I will keep writing these even though I stopped mailing them last spring. It feels dishonest somehow to stop entirely, like abandoning you twice."*" He recited it from memory, which meant he had read it more than once, which meant the words were already living inside him the way her words had lived inside her for a decade. "You stopped mailing them. Three years in. Before that, you—you sent them. To an address you knew wasn't answering. And then you just... stopped. And never told me any of it. Never once in ten years did you walk across the street and say, *Elias, I need to tell you something that will rearrange your entire life.*"

"There was nothing to walk across the street to," she said, and her voice rose despite herself. "There was no Elias. There was a deployment, and then another one, and then silence, and then your mother telling me—with such kindness, such *surgical* kindness—that I would ruin you. That a girl from Harbor Row with flour under her nails and a baby on the way was a career-ending complication, and wouldn't it be noble of me to disappear. And I believed her, Elias. God forgive me, I believed her, because I was twenty-three and she was wearing pearls and certainty, and those look identical to authority when you're young."

"So you disappeared."

"So I *disappeared*." The word came out of her like a blade leaving a wound. "And then I came back, because disappearing with your child is a fantasy for women with savings accounts, and I opened this shop, and I raised June, and I wrote you letters I no longer mailed because mailing them required believing in a version of you that had chosen silence—and it was easier, do you understand, it was *easier* to grieve a man who'd abandoned me than to live in the uncertainty of a man who might simply not care enough to reply."

The shop was very quiet. Outside, the harbor light came on, the great slow sweep of it, painting the front windows gold and then dark and then gold again.

"That's the lie," Elias said quietly.

"Don't."

"That's the lie you tell yourself. Because I never got a single letter, Freya. Not one. There was no silence to choose. There was a woman intercepting envelopes and a man overseas thinking he'd been forgotten." He stepped closer, and she could see the tremor in him now, the soldier's discipline holding a collapsing building upright. "But you—after year three, you chose it. You chose the story where I didn't answer. You built a whole life on top of a grave you dug yourself and never checked whether anyone was buried in it."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed, and his voice broke clean in half. "It isn't. None of this is fair. Your mother-in-law burned a hundred and seventeen letters and I missed the first ten years of my daughter's life and you spent a decade rehearsing sentences instead of saying them, and *none of it is fair*, and we are going to have to figure out how to be fair to each other anyway, because there is a ten-year-old girl who called me tonight from a payphone and asked me—" He stopped. His hand was over his mouth. When he lowered it, his eyes were streaming and he didn't seem to know it. "She asked me if I was her father. She said, *Are you the man from the letters? Are you the dad-shaped hole in everything my mom writes?* Those were her words. Dad-shaped hole. She reads your mail, Freya. She's been reading your grief her whole childhood like it was weather."

Freya sank onto the stool behind the register because her legs had resigned their post.

"I was going to tell you," she whispered.

"When?"

"At the festival. After the closing ceremony. I had it planned—a whole speech, ten years of drafts—"

"A speech." He laughed again, that airless terrible laugh. "Of course. Another letter. Another unsent thing. Freya, do you hear yourself? You love me in the conditional tense. You love me in drawers and drafts and speeches scheduled for later. Meanwhile I'm standing here trying to learn my daughter's favorite color from a woman who won't even fight me in real time."

"My favorite color is green," said a small voice from the doorway.

They both turned.

June stood in the entrance with her schoolbag still on her shoulder and her chin lifted at the exact angle Freya used when she was terrified, and Freya's heart performed a maneuver that would have concerned a cardiologist.

"June. Baby. You're supposed to be at Marguerite's."

"Marguerite said I should come home." June's eyes moved between them, missing nothing, cataloguing everything, a ten-year-old auditor of adult catastrophe. "She said some things are better heard than described." She looked at Elias. "You're crying."

"I am," Elias said. He didn't wipe his face. "Your mom wrote me a hundred and seventeen letters and I just found out today, and I'm crying about it, and that's the truth."

June considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she looked at her mother, and whatever passed across her face was old—older than ten years, older than the letters, some inherited sorrow finding its next generation.

"You told me Dad died," she said quietly. "When I was five. You said he died before I was born. That's not the same as what's in the drawer."

Freya opened her mouth. Closed it. Every rehearsed answer—the partial truths, the gentle deflections, the decade of careful architecture—lay in ruins around her, and there was nothing left to build with except the truth, which she had handled so rarely it felt foreign in her hands.

"You're right," she said. "It's not the same. I lied to protect both of us, and it was wrong, and I'm sorry, and I don't expect sorry to be enough tonight or maybe ever, but it's what I have."

June nodded once, a small formal nod, a treaty signed under protest. Then she shrugged off her schoolbag, set it on the reading chair with the deliberateness of someone claiming territory, and looked at Elias.

"You can stay for dinner," she said. "But you have to eat what we eat, and Mom makes terrible rice, and you're not allowed to leave until you finish it. That's the rule. House rules apply to everybody."

Elias made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and Freya watched—helpless, hollowed out, hopeful against every instinct—as her daughter walked past him toward the back stairs, pausing only to add, over her shoulder:

"And you have to dry the dishes. Dad-shaped holes don't get out of chores."

* * *

He stayed for dinner. He ate the terrible rice. He dried every dish, holding the towels awkwardly, like a man handling evidence.

And when June went up to brush her teeth, Elias stood in Freya's kitchen with his sleeves rolled and his hands braced on the counter, and he looked at her across ten years and one ruined evening and one impossible child, and he said:

"I don't know how to do this. I don't know how to be furious with you and grateful to you in the same breath. I don't know how to walk back into that shop tomorrow and not read the other hundred and six letters, and I don't know what I'll be when I finish them." His voice dropped. "But I know I'm not leaving Harbor Row. Whatever happens between us—whatever you decide I'm owed or not owed—I'm not leaving my daughter again. So you'd better decide what you want, Freya. Because I'm done letting other people make our choices for us. Including you."

Then he took his coat, and he let himself out, and the broken bell said nothing at all.

Freya stood alone in her kitchen, listening to the harbor light sweep its patient gold across the ceiling, and upstairs she could hear June's toothbrush, and downstairs in the false drawer lay a hundred and six unread letters, and somewhere between all of those things was the sentence she had rehearsed for a decade, still stuck behind her teeth, still waiting for a courage she hadn't yet located.

In the morning, there would be a festival to run. Tonight, there was only the quiet, and it was louder than anything she had ever heard.
