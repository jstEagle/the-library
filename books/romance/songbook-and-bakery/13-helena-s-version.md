## 13. Helena's Version

His mother arrived the way weather arrived in Harbor Row—without permission, without apology, and with a certainty that she belonged there regardless of what anyone else had planned.

Elias heard her before he saw her: the sharp knock on the flat's door at seven in the morning, three raps spaced like punctuation, like a sentence she'd already decided the ending of. He'd been standing at the window in yesterday's shirt, watching the street below, where Freya was unlocking the bookshop with June beside her, both of them hunched against a wind that smelled of coming rain and woodsmoke. He had not slept. Letter 118 sat unfinished on his desk, four sentences long, each one crossed out and rewritten until the page looked like a battlefield.

He opened the door.

Helena Vane stood in the hallway in a camel coat that probably cost more than Marguerite's oven, her silver hair pinned back with architectural precision, a leather portfolio under one arm—the Boston offer, no doubt, printed fresh, annotated, weaponized. She looked at him the way she always looked at him after an absence: as though assessing whether he'd held up in storage.

"You didn't answer my calls," she said.

"I've been busy."

"Busy." Her eyes moved past him into the flat—the bare walls, the duffel bags still unpacked, the desk buried in paper. "This is temporary, Elias. We discussed this. The position opens Monday. I took the liberty of drafting your acceptance letter; you only need to sign."

"Mom." His voice came out lower than he intended, scraped raw from a night of reading. One hundred and seventeen letters. He'd finished them at four in the morning, sitting on the floor with his back against the bed, and when the last page ended—*I hope wherever you are, someone is telling you the truth about yourself*—he had put his face in his hands and stayed there for a long time, in a silence so complete it felt structural, load-bearing, like it was holding up the whole ruined decade.

"Come in," he said now. "Sit down. There's something you're going to tell me."

Something flickered across her face—fast, quickly smoothed—but she stepped inside anyway, because Helena Vane did not retreat from rooms. She set the portfolio on the table with a click and removed her gloves finger by finger, buying time the way she'd taught him to buy time before difficult conversations. *Control the tempo, darling. Whoever rushes, loses.*

"Freya Almeida," he said.

Her hands went still over the second glove.

"She wrote to me," Elias said. "For years. Do you know how many? A hundred and seventeen letters, Mom. I counted them last night. I read every one. And then there's the one you kept—the one in your safe, or your drawer, or wherever you keep things you think you might need later. The one from the third year, the one where she says—" His voice caught. He hated that it caught. "The one where she tells me about June."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Helena sat down. Slowly, without grace for the first time in her life, lowering herself onto the edge of the chair as if her knees had finally filed their complaint. When she looked up at him, the polish was still there but something underneath had shifted—a hairline fracture running through all that composure, visible only because he had spent thirty-four years learning to read her.

"Elias," she said carefully, "whatever you think happened—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't manage me. Don't frame it. Don't give me the version with the spin on it. I have spent two weeks in this town piecing together my own life like a man reading his own obituary, and I am done being the last one to know things. So here is what happens now. You are going to tell me the truth. All of it. And then you are going to leave."

* * *

She told it in pieces, the way people confess anything—reluctantly at first, then in a flood they can't stop.

It began, she said, the summer before his first deployment. She had come to Harbor Row uninvited, ostensibly to meet the girl her son wouldn't stop talking about, actually to conduct an inspection. She described Freya to Elias as if he hadn't grown up here himself, hadn't kissed Freya behind the bookshop shelves, hadn't memorized the way she laughed with her whole body. *A sweet girl. Small ambitions. A bookshop job, a rented room above her aunt's place, plans that ended at the county line.* Helena had sat across from Freya at a café table and asked her, politely, what she imagined the future looked like—and Freya, nineteen and honest, had said she didn't need it to look big. Just true.

"And I thought," Helena said, her voice flattening into the register she used for boardrooms and funerals alike, "*this will destroy him.* Not because she wasn't good enough. Because he would choose her. He would choose her over the academy, over the career, over everything we had built toward since he was six years old. And then some war would take him anyway, and she would be a widow at twenty, and he would be a flag on a mantel, and I would spend the rest of my life knowing I let him throw away his only future for a girl who sold books in a town with one traffic light."

"So you decided for him."

"I protected him."

"*You decided for him,*" Elias repeated, louder, and heard his own voice crack against the walls. "You sat in my apartment while I was packing my duffel and you told me she'd stopped writing. You told me she said she couldn't do the distance. You watched me grieve her, Mom. You watched me carry that through basic training, through deployment, through—" He stopped. Through the convoy. Through the medic who'd pulled shrapnel out of his side while he'd thought, absurdly, of a girl in a bookstore, and been ashamed of it, because he believed she'd left him. "Every time I almost got over it, you found a way to remind me she'd chosen to go. You made sure the wound stayed open. Why? Why would you want that?"

"Because a man with nothing to lose takes risks," she said quietly. "And a man with a broken heart fights harder. God forgive me, Elias, I thought grief would make you excellent. It did make you excellent."

The words hung there, obscene in their honesty.

"The letters," he said. "Tell me about the letters."

She closed her eyes. "They started arriving three weeks after you deployed. I had keys to your apartment—I was paying half the rent, remember, you were a private on a private's salary. They came addressed to you in her handwriting. I recognized it from the forms she filled out at the café that day. Round letters. Letters that leaned forward, like they were in a hurry to reach you." Her mouth twisted, and for one unguarded moment she looked less like Helena Vane and more like an old woman sitting in a cheap chair in a rented room above a bakery. "I burned the first one in your kitchen sink. I told myself it was the only one. Then another came. And another. I bought a fireproof box to hold them between burnings, which I understand is a kind of madness, keeping them safe just to destroy them properly."

"How many?"

"All of them. For three years. Every letter she sent. She never missed a month, Elias—not once, not even when the envelopes stopped having return addresses, as if she'd learned not to hope for an answer. I read them all. I told myself I was vetting them for operational security, which was a lie I let myself believe because the truth was—" Her breath hitched, an actual hitch, and he realized with distant surprise that his mother was crying, silently, without changing expression, tears running into the collar of a coat worth more than the building they sat in. "The truth was I couldn't stop reading them. She wrote you like you were the best thing that had ever happened to anyone. She wrote you like a person worth becoming. And I had thrown that away with both hands, and I needed to know if I'd been right, and every letter proved I hadn't, and I burned them anyway."

"Why did you stop?" he asked. "After year three. She stopped writing eventually too—she told me. She said she gave up."

"Because of the child." Helena opened her eyes. "Because in the fourth year, the letters changed. They weren't just to you anymore. They were *about* someone. A little girl who liked the sound of rain. Who lined up her shoes by color. Who asked questions her mother couldn't answer." Helena's hands were shaking now; she pressed them flat against her knees. "And I understood that this was no longer a romance I could argue was impractical. This was a family. This was a granddaughter. And if you ever found out—if you came home and saw her face—you would never forgive me, and worse, you would leave. You would fold your entire life around that child the way paper folds around a stone, and everything I had spent twenty years engineering would be over. So I did the only thing left. I made sure the letters stopped reaching you, and I made sure you never came looking."

"The phone call," Elias said. "My last year overseas. Someone called the base and told me Freya had moved on. Married. That I should let it go."

Silence.

"Was that you too?"

"I hired a service," she whispered. "A woman. She was very convincing."

Elias stood up. He walked to the window because if he stayed near her he was going to say something unforgivable, and some part of him—the part she had built, the disciplined part—still wanted to walk out of this conversation as a man rather than a detonation. Below, the street was waking up. Marguerite was dragging flour sacks into the bakery. June sat on the bookshop steps with a book open on her knees, reading in the gray light, and even from three floors up he could see the way she pushed her hair behind her ears exactly the way Freya did, the way she mouthed the hard words to herself, patient with herself, ten years old and already braver than everyone in this room.

"The one you kept," he said without turning around. "You said you kept one. Show me."

* * *

She took it from her handbag—she had brought it, of course she had brought it, Helena Vane did not enter any negotiation unarmed—and held it out to him without meeting his eyes.

The envelope was soft with age, its edges furred. His name in round, forward-leaning letters. He opened it with fingers that did not feel like his own.

It was dated eleven years ago, nearly to the day. He read it standing at the window while his mother sat motionless behind him, and the letter was short, shorter than most of the others, written in the voice from the early years—the hopeful voice, the one that still believed answers were coming:

*Elias —*

*I don't know if these are reaching you. I've started to suspect they aren't, because you were never once bad at writing back, not even at your laziest. But I'm going to keep sending them anyway, because the alternative is admitting that you're gone, and I'm not ready to admit that yet.*

*I have something to tell you and nowhere safe to say it, so I'm saying it here, to the version of you that reads these. In March, you're going to be a father. I found out Tuesday. I cried for an hour and then I laughed for an hour and then I ate an entire loaf of Marguerite's cinnamon bread, which I'm choosing to believe counts as celebrating on your behalf.*

*I'm not asking you to come home. I know what you signed up for and I know you'll do it well, because you do everything well. I just need you to know that somewhere in Harbor Row there's going to be a person with your stubbornness and my terrible singing voice, and that whatever happens, whoever they turn out to be, they'll grow up knowing their father was brave.*

*That's the story I'll give them. It's a good story. It's even mostly true.*

*All my love, always,*
*Freya*

Elias read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting each line land like a stone dropped into deep water. *In March, you're going to be a father.* Eleven years ago. He had been in Kandahar in March, eleven years ago, sandblasting a Humvee axle and complaining about the coffee, while in a small town on the coast a nineteen-year-old girl sat alone in a bookshop learning how to be brave without him.

"You kept this one," he said. His voice sounded foreign to himself. Calm. Terribly calm. "Out of a hundred and seventeen, you kept the one announcing your own granddaughter."

"It was leverage," Helena said. "If Freya ever came forward—if she ever threatened to tell you—I could prove I knew first. I could control the narrative. Control the narrative, Elias, that's—" She stopped herself, hearing it, hearing the whole architecture of her life collapse into that single phrase.

"That's what you taught me," he finished.

He turned around. He looked at his mother—at the coat, the pinned hair, the portfolio with the Boston offer, the gloves folded in her lap like shed skin—and he felt something in his chest finish breaking, quietly, without drama, the way ice finishes breaking on a lake in spring: not a crack but a shift, a whole season ending.

"I used to think you were the strongest person I knew," he said. "When Dad left, you held us together with tape and willpower and I swore I'd never disappoint you. Everything I did, I did partly to see you look at me the way you looked at me after the academy. And you knew that. You built a career on it. You built *me* on it." He laid the letter on the table between them, face up, deliberately, so it sat there like evidence at a trial. "But you didn't protect me, Mom. You didn't even protect your ambition. You protected your fear. You were so afraid of being nobody's mother-of-someone-important that you erased a family. You erased *June*. A child who lines up her shoes by color. You knew that detail, from a letter, and you burned the rest."

"Elias—"

"No." He picked up the portfolio and held it out to her. "Take this. Take the Boston thing, the acceptance letter, the whole future you drafted for me. I'm not signing it. I'm not going anywhere. And I need you to hear this clearly, because I'm only going to say it once: I love you. You're my mother, and I love you, and I suspect some part of you genuinely believed you were saving me. But I cannot trust you right now. Maybe not for a long time. So you're going to stand up, and you're going to walk out that door, and you're going to go back to wherever you're staying, and you are not going to approach Freya. Not to apologize, not to explain, not to manage. If she wants a reckoning with you, she'll ask for it. Until then, you don't exist to her. Do you understand me?"

For a long moment Helena Vane sat perfectly still, and Elias watched her decide—watched the machinery run through its options, the charm, the argument, the tears deployed strategically—and then, for perhaps the first time in his adult life, he watched it all shut down. She rose. She took the portfolio. She put on her gloves.

At the door she paused, her hand on the knob, her back to him.

"She named the girl June," she said softly. "After the month you two met. I read that in one of the ones I burned. I've thought about it every day since."

Then she was gone, and the door clicked shut behind her with the finality of a chapter closing, and Elias stood alone in the rented room with a hundred and seventeen letters and one survivor, and below him in the street, June closed her book, tucked it under her arm, and followed her mother inside out of the cold—unaware that the whole shape of her life had just shifted, tectonic and silent, beneath her feet.
