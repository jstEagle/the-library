## 14. June Runs

The phone call came at 11:42 on a Thursday morning, which was how Freya would always remember it—not by the day, not by the weather, but by the precise cruelty of the timestamp. She was elbow-deep in a shipment of remaindered poetry when her phone lit up with the school's number, and she knew before she answered. Some part of her had known since Tuesday night, since June had come down the stairs with three letters fanned in her small hands like losing cards and asked, in a voice too flat for a ten-year-old, *Why are these addressed to my dad?*

"Mrs.—Ms. Almeida?" The secretary's voice did that thing voices do when they're trying to be gentle and failing. "June wasn't in homeroom. Her teacher thought maybe she was at the nurse, but—" A pause, paper shuffling. "She signed out at the office. Forged your signature, I'm afraid. It's very good, actually."

*It's very good, actually.* Freya closed her eyes. Of course it was good. June had been practicing being an adult in miniature for years—the way children of secrets do, learning early how to forge the signatures of a life that looked normal from the outside.

"I'm coming," Freya said. "Don't call anyone else. Please."

She hung up and stood in the middle of the stockroom surrounded by discounted Neruda, and for one long moment she could not move. This was the thing she had feared for ten years, the exact shape of it: not the letters found, not even Elias found, but this—June running, June gone, June out in the world carrying a weight Freya had spent a decade trying to keep off her shoulders, and failing so completely that the weight had become the reason her daughter walked out of a school building and into an October morning alone.

*You did this,* said a voice in her head that sounded like Helena Vane, which was the worst part, because Helena Vane had never once been wrong about the damage.

"No," Freya said aloud, to the empty stockroom, to herself. "I kept her safe. That's different." But her hands were shaking as she grabbed her coat.

* * *

Marguerite was already in the doorway of the bakery when Freya came out of the bookshop, flour on her forearms and her reading glasses pushed up into gray curls, looking like every emergency grandmother in every story ever told. "I heard," she said. "Where?"

"I don't know where. That's the problem. I don't—" Freya pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "She knows everything now. Or enough. She read the letters, Marguerite. She read them standing in my shop, in my drawer, and I caught her with letter forty-something in her hands and I didn't—I didn't handle it. I told her we'd talk. I said *we'll talk tonight*, like it was a dentist appointment, like I could schedule her heartbreak around my closing hours."

"And instead she scheduled hers around school hours." Marguerite came down the step and took Freya's face in her floured hands, leaving white prints on her cheekbones like war paint. "Listen to me. Harbor Row is four streets and a waterfront. There is nowhere in this town a child can disappear to. We will find her. Where would you have gone, at ten, when the world turned out to be made of lies?"

Freya thought about it. It took no time at all. "The water," she said. "I'd go where I could see the water. Water doesn't lie to you. It just is what it is, all the way to the horizon."

"The pier, then. And the rocks past the cannery. Go left, I'll go right, and call me the second—" Marguerite stopped. Her gaze had drifted over Freya's shoulder, up the street, and something moved through her expression—relief and alarm tangled together like yarn dropped by two knitters.

Freya turned.

Elias Vane was coming down Harbor Row at a pace just short of running, still in the clothes he'd apparently slept in, hair uncombed, a look on his face that Freya recognized because she had worn it for ten years in mirrors: the look of a person who has finally understood the size of what they've lost and is now trying to outrun the arithmetic.

"She's not at the school," he said, stopping in front of them, breathless. "Marguerite called me. I've checked the pier already, the playground, the—" He stopped himself. His eyes went to Freya and there was no anger in them, which somehow was worse than anger. Anger they had done already, at closing time, both of them bleeding all over the checkout counter. This was past that. This was the raw place under it. "I checked everywhere I could think of."

"Not everywhere," Freya said. "The rocks past the old cannery. There's a gap in the fence. Kids go there to be dramatic." Her voice cracked on *dramatic*. "I used to. Before I was the reason other people got to be dramatic."

Elias was already moving. Then he stopped, turned back, and did something that rearranged something inside Freya's chest: he waited. He stood there on the cobblestones with his ruined morning and his unspoken everything, and he waited for her to walk beside him.

They went together, and neither of them spoke, and the silence between them had changed texture overnight—it was no longer the silence of things unsaid but the silence of two people listening for the same sound.

* * *

The gap in the fence was exactly as Freya remembered it, rusted open like a held breath, and beyond it the rocks ran down toward the water in gray shelves, and there, sitting on the flattest one with her knees pulled up and her backpack beside her like a companion in crime, was June.

Freya's whole body started forward. Elias's hand came up—not touching her, just raised, a stop sign made of fingers—and when she looked at him he shook his head, once, slowly.

"Let me," he said.

"You don't get to—" The words came out sharper than she meant, loud enough that June's head lifted on the rocks below. Freya dropped her voice to almost nothing. "You don't get to decide that. She's mine. She has been mine alone for ten years, and you don't—"

"I know." His voice was low and rough. "I know what I missed. I know I have no right to anything. But look at her, Freya. Look at who she's waiting for."

And Freya looked, and saw what he meant.

June had seen them. Of course she had—there was nowhere to hide on those rocks, no cover but the wind. And she had seen them and turned back to the water, deliberately, shoulders set, the way she turned back to her math homework when she wanted Freya to know she was displeased. But she hadn't run. She hadn't gotten up and scrambled away deeper along the shore, though there was plenty of shore. She sat exactly where she was, in plain view, angry and visible and *waiting*, and Freya understood with a lurch that her daughter had run not away from something but toward someone—or at least toward the question of someone—and that the cruelest, truest thing about it was that June had chosen the stranger with her father's eyes as the person whose presence she could tolerate while her whole world reorganized itself.

"She wants to be found," Freya said quietly. "But not by me."

"Then let it be me." Elias stepped past her, careful on the wet rock, and Freya stood at the broken fence and watched him descend toward her daughter like a man approaching a fire he both feared and needed.

* * *

What follows, Freya witnessed from thirty feet and ten years away, crouched behind the fence gap with salt wind pulling her hair loose, close enough to see and too far to hear—which was its own kind of penance, she supposed, or its own kind of gift. She couldn't decide which. Probably both. Everything lately was both.

Elias did not sit next to June. That was the first thing. He stopped a few feet away, at the edge of her rock, and then—to Freya's astonishment—he lowered himself onto the colder, rougher stone beside it, the one with the barnacles, the one no one sensible would choose, and settled there with his forearms on his knees, facing the water, not facing her.

He didn't say anything.

A minute passed. Two. Five. The gulls argued overhead. The water did its ancient work against the rocks, patient and indifferent, saying nothing to anyone. June glanced sideways at him once, sharply, the way she glanced at new kids at school, assessing threat. He didn't meet the glance. He just sat, and looked at the sea, and existed in her vicinity without demanding anything from it.

Freya's heart broke and mended itself approximately nine times in the first fifteen minutes.

Because she knew what he was doing. She had learned it secondhand, from a book she'd read during June's infancy—one of those parenting books bought in desperation at 2 a.m. from a drugstore rack—that some children cannot be talked to in crisis, only accompanied. That presence precedes permission. That the most powerful sentence in a frightened child's vocabulary is the one nobody says. Elias had never read that book. Elias had missed every bedtime of the child's entire life. And yet here he was, doing it perfectly, instinctively, choosing the barnacle rock and the silence and the long patience of it, and Freya stood behind a rusted fence watching the father of her daughter be, without instruction or rehearsal, exactly what June needed, and she did not know whether to weep or scream or both simultaneously, in harmony.

Twenty minutes in, June spoke first. Freya saw it more than heard it—saw her daughter's mouth move, saw Elias turn his head slightly, not all the way, giving her the dignity of not being stared at while she detonated.

June talked for a long time. Her hands did most of it—Freya knew those hands, expressive as her mother's, carving shapes in the air: *three letters, why, why, why.* At one point she pulled the backpack around and extracted something—a piece of paper, folded and refolded soft as cloth, one of the letters, Freya realized, carried out of the house like contraband, like evidence. She held it up. She let it fall back into her lap. She said something short and final and turned her face fully to the sea.

Elias nodded. Freya could see him nodding, slow, taking it in, absorbing whatever verdict June had delivered without defending himself, without explaining, without doing any of the ten thousand things a man in his position would burn to do. *That was your mother's whole heart and I burned none of it and also I was not there and both of those things are true and I will not ask you to sort them out today.*

An hour passed. Maybe more. Freya's legs cramped on the cold ground and she did not move. Somewhere in the middle hour, June shifted—six inches closer on her rock, then twelve, the tectonic drift of a child deciding. At some point Elias took something from his jacket pocket: a granola bar, absurdly, the kind sold at the gas station, and set it on the rock between them without ceremony, and June glared at it for a full five minutes before eating it with the ferocity of someone who had skipped breakfast on principle.

Once, June laughed.

It was small and startled out of her—something Elias had said, some dry observation about the gulls, probably, because one of them had been harassing them for the better part of an hour, strutting and screaming like a tiny feathered landlord. June laughed, and then immediately looked furious at herself for laughing, and Elias pretended not to notice, and Freya, behind the fence, put her hand over her mouth and cried silently into her own palm, because that laugh was the sound of the door opening one inch, and one inch was everything, one inch was the whole distance between drowning and the surface.

Toward the end of the second hour, June said something brief and pointed at the town. Elias stood, joints cracking audibly even at this distance, and offered her his hand.

June looked at it for a long moment.

She did not take it. She picked up her backpack, slung it on, and walked beside him up the rocks—beside, not behind, not ahead, beside, matching his steps with the fierce precision of someone negotiating terms—and as they passed the fence gap, June saw her mother.

She stopped. The wind filled the pause. Freya opened her mouth and had absolutely nothing, no sentence in any language, no rehearsed line, nothing in the drawer.

"You were going to tell me *tonight*," June said. Not a question. An indictment, entered into the record.

"Yes," Freya said. "I was a coward about the timing. I'm sorry."

June considered this with the gravity of a judge. Then she looked at Elias, then back at her mother, and delivered the ruling: "He didn't explain anything. He just sat there. For two hours. Like a *rock*." A beat. "It was weirdly okay."

And she walked on toward town, and Elias fell into step beside her, and over his shoulder he gave Freya a look she would keep for the rest of her life—not triumph, not apology, just a quiet acknowledgment across the ruins: *I didn't earn this. I know I didn't earn this. I'm going to spend the rest of however long earning it anyway.*

* * *

They walked home the long way, the three of them, an arrangement nobody proposed and nobody objected to: June in the middle, Freya on her left, Elias on her right, a formation that looked from a distance like a family and felt from the inside like a ceasefire. Nobody spoke until the bakery, where Marguerite was standing in the window pretending very badly to arrange croissants, and June muttered "everyone in this town is so *much*" and disappeared inside to the warm smell of cinnamon and the one adult in her life who had never lied to her about anything larger than the number of chocolate chips in a scone.

Which left Freya and Elias on the sidewalk, in the gold light of mid-afternoon, with nothing between them but ten years and one shared afternoon of silence.

"He didn't explain anything," Freya said. "That's what she said. You sat on a barnacle rock for two hours."

"It seemed fair," Elias said. "She'd earned the right to be silent at somebody. I had two hours free."

And Freya laughed—cracked, wet, real—and Elias smiled, tired and crooked, and for a moment they stood there in the ordinary miracle of a Wednesday-adjacent afternoon, two people who had lost a decade finding out what one afternoon might buy back.

Then his phone buzzed. He glanced at it, and Freya watched his face change—the smile draining, the jaw setting, something old and heavy settling back onto his shoulders like a coat he couldn't take off.

"My mother," he said. "Third time today." He looked up at Freya, and there was something new in his eyes, something that had hardened overnight into resolve. "There's something I have to show you. Something she kept. And after that—" he glanced toward the bakery window, where June's silhouette moved behind the glass, "—after that, I'm going to need to borrow your letters. All of them. If June will allow it. There are a hundred and fourteen more, aren't there?"

Freya's breath caught. "How did you—"

"June does math problems in her head when she's upset," Elias said quietly. "She told me she read three. She said the drawer was full. She estimated." He almost smiled. "She's very good, actually."

The words landed somewhere deep, and Freya had to look away, down the street, toward the water that didn't lie.

"One hundred and seventeen," she said. "One hundred and eighteen, if you count the one I never finished."

Elias went very still. "The one you never finished," he repeated, like a man committing coordinates to memory.

"After year three," Freya said. "When I stopped mailing them. I kept writing anyway. I couldn't—writing to you was the only place I was honest. Even when I thought you'd never read a word." She met his eyes. "Letter 118 was going to say goodbye. I never could finish it."

The wind came up Harbor Row, smelling of salt and bread and October, and Elias Vane looked at her with the expression of a man who had just decided to burn a bridge behind him.

"Don't finish it," he said. "Not yet. Give me a week first."
