## 15. What the Letters Said

The letters came home with him in a shoebox that had once held hiking boots, and Elias set it on the kitchen table of the flat above the bakery and did not touch it for three hours.

He told himself he was being practical. He made coffee he didn't drink. He unpacked the duffel bags he'd never fully unpacked, hanging shirts in a closet he still thought of as borrowed. He stood at the window over Harbor Row's main street and watched the lights come on in the bookshop across the way—Freya turning the sign, June's small silhouette darting between shelves—and he understood, finally, that what he was doing was stalling. Ten years as an Army medic had taught him to triage. The worst wound first. The one bleeding fastest.

He sat down at the table and lifted the lid.

One hundred and seventeen envelopes. He'd counted them twice on the walk from the bookshop, because counting was something his hands could do while the rest of him reeled. Freya had bundled them with twine in batches by year—the first year fat and soft with handling, the later years thinner, the paper changing brands somewhere around letter forty, then again around ninety, cheaper stock, the kind sold in drugstores, the kind you bought when you were writing out of duty to yourself rather than hope. The last batch was tied so neatly it looked like something prepared for an archive. For evidence. For a trial she'd always expected to have.

He untied the first bundle with hands that were not entirely steady.

*Dear Elias,* began Letter One, dated eleven days after he'd shipped out. *I don't know if this will reach you or if you've simply stopped writing, but I'm choosing to believe the mail is slow and the war is busy and you are fine. You are fine. I've decided this, and since I can't verify anything, my decision is now fact.*

He laughed—a broken sound in the empty flat—and pressed his fist against his mouth. That was her. That was exactly her, the woman who'd once argued him into a draw over whether the harbor fog counted as weather or mood, who'd refused to lose an argument even when losing would have been kinder.

*I got a job at Marguerite's bakery doing mornings,* Letter Three said. *I burn things. She says I have a gift for burning things, which is the kindest review my cooking has ever received. I think about how you'd laugh at me. I think about you constantly, which is embarrassing to admit to paper, but paper doesn't smirk.*

Letter Nine: *I'm late. Two weeks late. I'm writing this before I take the test because I need one letter where nothing has changed yet, where it's just us and the fog and your terrible handwriting. Whatever happens next, I want you to know that the night before you left is still the truest thing I own.*

Elias set that one down face-up on the table, as if it might need air. His heart was doing something arrhythmic and unhelpful. He read Letter Ten standing up, then Eleven sitting down again, then Twelve through Twenty without moving at all, the coffee going cold beside him, the streetlights coming on below, the bakery's ovens exhaling their last warm breath of cinnamon up through the floorboards like the building itself was trying to comfort him.

She'd written to him about the pregnancy. About June's birth—*Seven pounds, two ounces, born at 4:17 in the morning during a rainstorm, and Elias, she has your exact scowl, I'm sorry, I know you'll deny it, but I've seen your scowl and this baby has it, furrowed brow and all, furious about being alive already.* About learning to nurse. About the terror of the first fever. About Marguerite holding June so Freya could sleep. About the town that knew everything and said nothing, or said everything and pretended it was kindness.

And every letter ended some version of the same sentence: *Write back when you can. I'll keep writing until you do.*

By the second bundle, the voice had begun to change. Not breaking—Freya didn't break; he could see that now, reading ten years of her in a single evening—but bending, the way green wood bends before it becomes something else. The early letters had been written *to* him. These were written *around* him, a life built in the negative space where he should have been.

*June asked today why the moon follows the car,* Letter Forty-Three said. *I told her it doesn't, it just feels that way because it's far away. She thought about that for a long time and then said, "So things that are far away seem like they're following you." She's four. I don't know where she gets it. Yes I do. It's you. Everything stubborn and strange in her is you, and I love it, and some nights it's like being haunted by someone who's still alive.*

He had to stop after that one. He went to the window. Across the street the bookshop was dark now except for one lamp upstairs, and behind it, faintly, a shadow moving—Freya, closing out the day. He watched until the lamp went out, and then he went back to the table because there was nowhere else in the world for him to go except deeper into the box.

Somewhere around Letter Sixty, the letters stopped asking him to write back.

That was the detail that undid him most thoroughly—not the grief, though the grief was everywhere, soaked into the paper like water damage—but the *practicality* of it. She had simply, quietly, adjusted. *I know you're not going to answer,* Letter Sixty-One said, matter-of-fact as a grocery list. *I've known it for a while. I keep writing anyway because these letters are how I talk to the part of myself that loved you, and I'm not willing to lose that part too. So this isn't for you anymore, Elias. Consider yourself cc'd.*

*Consider yourself cc'd.* He put his head down on his arms on the kitchen table and stayed there a long time, breathing the smell of old paper and cold coffee and cinnamon rising through the floor, until the ache in his chest settled into something he could carry.

The third bundle was thinner. The fourth thinner still. And in these, the letters changed again—became shorter, steadier, stripped down to essentials, like a field report from a long campaign. *June lost a tooth. June won the spelling bee. June asked about you directly for the first time. I told her you were far away. She asked how far. I said farther than the moon. She said, "Then the moon thing works both ways."*

Letter Ninety-Nine was one line long: *I saw a man today with your walk and hated him for a full minute before I realized he was just a man walking.*

Letter One Hundred and Four: *Marguerite says I should stop. She says I'm keeping a shrine. Maybe I am. But a shrine is just a place where you tell the truth, and this drawer is the only place in my life where I get to say that I miss you without anyone worrying about me. So no. I won't stop. I'll just stop expecting. There's a difference. I learned it the hard way, but I learned it.*

And Letter One Hundred Seventeen, the last one, dated four months ago, was this:

*I opened the shop late today because June wanted pancakes and I let her win the argument about chocolate chips. She's ten now. Ten. I keep trying to remember if you ever told me what you wanted to be when you were a kid and I can't find it anywhere in my memory, which means either you never told me or I've lost it, and both possibilities make me want to burn something. Marguerite says I have a gift.*

*Here's what I know tonight, Elias. I know I built a whole life without you and it's a good life and I'm not sorry for it. I know our daughter is extraordinary and funny and stubborn and mine. I know that loving you stopped hurting somewhere around year six and became something quieter, like a scar that aches before rain. And I know that if you walked into my shop tomorrow I wouldn't know what to say, because everything I've needed to say for ten years is in this drawer, addressed to a man who may not exist anymore.*

*But I'd like to find out. That's the truth. After all this time, I'd still like to find out.*

*—F.*

Elias sat in the dark of the rented flat for a long time after that, the shoebox empty, the letters spread around him in drifts like snowfall, like sediment, like the geological record of a decade he had slept through. Outside, Harbor Row breathed its small-town breath. Somewhere below, Marguerite's timer went off and was silenced. Somewhere across the street, Freya and June were asleep under one roof, ten feet and ten years apart from him.

His mother had burned these. Every one. Had held them in her manicured hands and fed them to a fire and then called him on Sundays to ask if he was eating enough. Had decided—with perfect confidence, with love, God help him, with *love*—that his life was worth more than this. Than her. Than the girl with the scowl and the chocolate-chip arguments.

He wasn't going to think about Helena tonight. Tonight belonged to the evidence.

Because that's what this was. Not a romance, not a tragedy—an *evidence file*. A case built one envelope at a time by a woman who had prosecuted her own hope for ten years and never once dismissed the charge. And the verdict was in, had been in for years, was sitting in 117 pieces of cheap drugstore paper: Freya Almeida had never stopped. Not when it hurt. Not when it stopped hurting. Not when it became something quieter than pain and stranger than memory.

Neither had he. He'd just been denied the paperwork.

Elias got up, found a pen in a drawer that still smelled of the previous tenant, and pulled a sheet of paper toward him across the ruined landscape of her decade. His hand hovered. In the Army he'd written reports under fire, concise and clean, language stripped to bone. This was harder. This was the hardest thing he'd ever done with a pen.

Finally he wrote:

*Dear Freya.*

*Letter 118.*

*I read all of them. Every one. In one night, because I couldn't not—I kept thinking I'd stop and rest and instead I just kept finding you, year after year, burning things, raising her, deciding I was fine and making it fact. You wrote to a man who wasn't answering and you built him a life anyway. I'm sorry. I'm sorry doesn't cover it. I'm sorry is the first word of a very long document I intend to spend the rest of my life drafting.*

*A few things I need you to know:*

*I never got a single letter. Not one. I wrote to you from every post, and I thought—God, I thought you'd chosen the quiet life over the hard one, and I let myself believe it because believing it hurt less than wondering. We were both lied to. Someone decided for both of us, and I know who, and I'm dealing with it, but that's not what this letter is for.*

*This letter is for the arithmetic. Seven pounds, two ounces, 4:17 a.m., rainstorm. A scowl I apparently own. Chocolate chips won in negotiation. A girl who understands that faraway things seem to follow you. I have ten years of questions and I intend to ask all of them, badly, probably, at the wrong times.*

*You wrote that you'd like to find out. So would I. I'm done finding out from a distance. I'm done being the moon.*

*—Elias.*

He read it over twice. It wasn't enough. Nothing would be enough; that was the whole lesson of the shoebox. But it was true, and true was the only currency he had left.

He folded it into an envelope and addressed it to her shop, and then he sat holding it, turning it over in his hands, and understood the problem: he could not mail this. Mailing it would make it one more letter traveling through channels other people controlled. He'd spent ten years at the mercy of other people's channels.

No. This one he would deliver himself. But not yet—not across a checkout counter, not in passing, not with June's eyes watching from between the shelves. It deserved ground. It deserved ceremony, or at least intention. It deserved the moment when he could stand in front of her with ten years of her own words in his blood and say: *I read them all. I'm still here.*

He put the envelope in his jacket pocket, where he could feel its weight against his chest, and looked out the window at the dark bookshop across the street.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would start figuring out how a man hands a woman a decade.

Below him, faint through the floorboards, Marguerite's oven ticked as it cooled, and Elias fell asleep at the kitchen table with his arms around an empty shoebox, holding it the way you hold something that has just been returned to you—carefully, gratefully, and with absolutely no intention of letting go.

* * *

He woke at dawn to the smell of bread and the sound of the harbor gulls arguing over the fishing boats, and for one disoriented second he was twenty-six again, deployed, waking in a cot with Freya's name in his mouth. Then he felt the envelope in his jacket pocket and remembered everything, all at once, in order.

He showered. He dressed. He did not shave, because the man in the mirror looked like someone who had read 117 letters in one night and deserved the beard. He bought two coffees from the cart by the pier—one black, one with the absurd amount of cream and sugar Freya had always ordered, a detail he'd carried for ten years like a coin in his pocket—and he crossed the street toward the bookshop as it opened, the bell above the door chiming his arrival.

Freya looked up from the register. June looked up from a shelf of paperbacks. And Elias stood in the doorway with two coffees and a letter in his pocket and absolutely no plan beyond the next thirty seconds.

"Morning," he said.

Freya's eyes went to the coffees—to *her* coffee, the ridiculous one—and something flickered across her face that he couldn't read, something between suspicion and hunger. "We're not open for—"

"You are now," he said, and set the cup on the counter, and reached into his pocket.
