## 16. The Festival Opens

The morning of the festival, Harbor Row woke up smelling like a promise.

Freya stood in the bookshop doorway at six a.m., a mug of tea going cold in her hands, and watched the fog lift off the harbor in slow, reluctant ribbons. Someone—probably the Hollis brothers—had already strung the bunting across the street, mismatched flags of orange and cream that fluttered like the town had decided, overnight, to dress up for something. Down the block, Marguerite's ovens had been running since four; the smell of cinnamon and proofing dough rolled out the bakery door in waves, thick enough to lean against. Folding tables marched down the center of the lane. A hand-painted banner over the fishmonger's read HARBOR ROW AUTUMN BREAD FESTIVAL in letters that wobbled with love.

Ten years of festivals. Freya had helped set up nine of them. But this one felt different, and she knew exactly why, and she refused to look at why directly, the way you refuse to look directly at the sun.

He would be here today.

* * *

By nine, the street had filled. Tourists from the coast road, families with strollers, old men in wool caps who had opinions about sourdough and no hesitation about sharing them. June had claimed a table near the bookshop's festival stall—Freya was selling secondhand cookbooks and Marguerite's morning loaves, a partnership born three festivals ago—and was arranging the books by color, which drove Freya quietly mad and which she had learned, over ten years of motherhood, to let happen.

"Mom." June didn't look up from a stack of blue-spined Julia Childs. "He's here."

Freya's hands went still on the cash box. "Who's here?"

"You know who's here." June's voice was flat with the particular contempt of a ten-year-old for adult pretense. "You've been looking at the corner of Main Street for twenty minutes like a train's coming."

"I have not."

"You have. You do the thing with your jaw."

Freya touched her jaw, then stopped touching her jaw, because that was worse.

And then Elias came around the corner of Main Street, and she understood, with a fresh and humiliating clarity, why she'd been watching for him.

He was carrying a crate of Marguerite's rye rounds, and he was wearing a flannel shirt she'd never seen, and he was laughing at something Marguerite said—actually laughing, head tipped back, the sound carrying across the crowd and landing somewhere under Freya's ribs like a dropped coin. Ten years ago he had laughed like that. Ten years ago she had catalogued that laugh the way she catalogued everything about him, in the private museum she kept behind her sternum, and she had believed the collection closed. Apparently not. Apparently the museum had stayed open the whole time, lights on, docent waiting.

He looked up. He saw her. And the laugh died in his throat in a way that was, she was almost certain, visible from where she stood.

They orbited. That was the word for it, and she hated how accurate it was. All morning they moved through the same small solar system—her stall, his table, the cider stand, the stage where the mayor was already testing a microphone with a feedback whine—drawn into the same orbit, never touching, each of them pretending the gravity wasn't there. He came to buy a loaf and stood at her table counting exact change with enormous concentration. She went to return a folding chair and found herself three feet from him, saying something about weather, about turnout, about the bunting, anything, while the air between them hummed like a struck string that neither of them would stop touching.

"Your mother called again last night," he said at one point, apropos of nothing, apropos of the bunting.

"Oh?"

"Twice." He set the folding chair down with more care than it needed. "She has opinions about me being here."

"Helena has opinions about everything. It's her best quality."

He almost smiled. "You used to be scared of her."

"I used to be a lot of things." Freya picked up the chair and walked away before the sentence could grow a tail.

* * *

The afternoon went golden. The crowd thickened. June sold a cookbook to a tourist from Portland and negotiated, unsupervised, a trade involving two cinnamon twists and a dog-eared copy of *Treasure Island*. Freya watched her daughter across the crowd—the dark hair, the quick hands, the way she squinted when she was thinking—and felt the old double ache: love like a fist around her heart, and beneath it, the low constant dread that had lived there for ten years, the one shaped like a question June hadn't asked yet and Freya hadn't answered.

She was restocking the bread basket when the shadow fell across her table.

She knew before she turned. Some shadows you learn by heart.

"Helena."

"Freya." Helena Vane stood on the cobblestones in a camel coat that cost more than Freya's monthly rent, immaculate, unhurried, as though the festival were a museum exhibit she had graciously agreed to attend. Her hair was silver at the temples now. Her eyes hadn't changed at all. "This is charming. Very... earnest."

"It's bread, Helena. It's allowed to be earnest."

A thin smile. Helena's gaze traveled over the stall, the cookbooks, June—lingered on June, one heartbeat too long, and Freya saw something flicker across that composed face before it was smoothed away. "You've done well here. I'll grant you that. It's a small life, but you've made it... tidy."

"I'm going to need you to arrive at a point. You came a long way to call my life tidy."

Helena's smile didn't waver. She turned slightly, presenting her back to the crowd, and her voice dropped into the register Freya remembered from ten years ago—the one that had dismantled her in a hospital corridor and a train station and a hundred unsent replies she'd never received. "I'd like to make you an offer."

"No."

"You haven't heard it."

"I heard it ten years ago. You offered it in different words. *Take the settlement. Take the distance. Take the silence.*" Freya kept her hands busy with the bread basket, because if she stopped moving she would shake. "I declined then. I decline now."

"This one is more generous." Helena produced an envelope from her bag—thick, cream-colored, the kind of envelope that contained a number with commas in it. She set it on the table between a loaf of rye and the cash box. "Elias is at a crossroads. Boston. A directorship. The career he was always meant for. And this town—" her eyes flicked, briefly, contemptuously, at the bunting, the dog, the wobbly banner, "—this town is a tide pool, Freya. Everything here is small and everything here dies when the sea forgets it. He doesn't belong in it. And you—" the pause, surgical, "—you know better than anyone what it costs him to stay."

Freya looked at the envelope. Cream paper. Heavy. It looked exactly like the envelope she had once, at twenty-four, been too proud and too broke to open, standing in a bus depot with everything she owned in two bags.

She pushed it back across the table.

"Ten years ago, you took my letters," Freya said, quietly, and watched Helena go very still. "You took ten years from him. From June. From me. And you're standing here, at a bread festival, offering me money to do it again. To disappear *properly* this time. As if the first time was a rough draft."

"I did what—"

"You did what you thought was necessary. I know. You've never once been sorry, only efficient." Freya's voice didn't rise. It had learned, over a decade of shopkeeping and mothering and surviving, not to rise. "But here's what you've never understood, Helena. I'm not the girl in the bus depot anymore. I have a shop. I have a daughter who negotiates in baked goods. I have a life you would call small and I call *mine*. And I am not for sale. Not the first time, not now, not ever. Take your envelope and go back to wherever you came from."

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind Helena's composure. Not guilt. Freya doubted the machinery for guilt still functioned. But something. A flicker of the woman who had once, maybe, loved her son more than her plans for him.

Then it closed, like a drawer.

Helena picked up the envelope. And as she tucked it away, she delivered the second blow with the same calm she'd used for the first, the way she always did—two strokes, one blade.

"You should know," she said, "that Elias has accepted the Boston position. He signed yesterday. He flies up Monday to finalize the contract."

The street noise went on around them—the fiddle player tuning up, children shrieking over the cider press, the mayor's microphone squealing—and Freya stood inside all of it, in a sudden pocket of silence, hearing only that.

"He wouldn't—" she started.

"He would. He did. He's very good at doing the sensible thing at the last possible moment; he learned from the best." Helena adjusted her coat. "I'm telling you because I respect you, Freya. I always have. You're the only one who ever understood the arithmetic. And because I'd rather you hear it from me than watch him leave on Monday without a word. You of all people know how that feels."

She turned and walked away down Harbor Row, camel coat and silver hair, unhurried, certain, leaving nothing behind but the shape of the sentence.

* * *

Freya didn't remember closing up the stall. She had some memory of June's hand in hers, of answering a question about dinner, of the fiddle music starting for real. The festival went on around her, golden and loud and utterly beside the point.

*He signed yesterday.*

She stood behind the bookshop counter after dark, the CLOSED sign turned, the festival lights still glowing through the front window, and did the arithmetic she'd sworn off years ago. Boston. Monday. A directorship. A life with commas in the numbers, a life Helena had built for him out of other people's removed obstacles—Freya's letters, Freya's daughter, Freya's whole small tidal world.

And he hadn't told her.

He had stood at her table that morning counting exact change. He had talked about bunting. He had laughed at Marguerite's joke with his head tipped back, and not one word about Boston, not one word about Monday, not one word about the fact that he had already chosen—already *signed*—while he let her orbit him, let the gravity hum, let her start, God help her, to hope.

The old wound, dressed in new clothes. Same blade. Better tailoring.

She thought of the false drawer, and the 117 letters, and the sentence she had rehearsed for a decade and never said. *I never stopped choosing you.* It rose in her now like bile, like a joke, like the cruelest thing she'd ever almost said. She had spent ten years not choosing anyone else. And he had spent two weeks back in Harbor Row choosing Boston with a signature.

"Mom?" June appeared in the doorway in her pajamas, hair wild, holding the book she was supposed to be asleep reading. "You okay? You've got your mad face but also your sad face. It's a weird combination."

Freya smiled. It cost her everything she had, and she paid it, because that was the job.

"I'm fine, bug. Go to bed."

June went, slowly, looking back twice. And Freya stood alone in the dark shop, listening to the festival wind down outside—music, laughter, the whole town happy—while inside her chest the museum closed its doors, turned off its lights, and began, quietly, furiously, to pack.
