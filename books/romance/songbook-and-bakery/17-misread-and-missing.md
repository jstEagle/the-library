## 17. Misread and Missing

The morning after Helena Vane's visit, Freya woke before her alarm with the particular clarity of someone who has been punched in her sleep. The words were already waiting for her, arranged in the dark like a verdict: *He took the Boston job.*

She lay still beneath the quilt June had picked out at the church rummage sale—yellow ducks, faded to the color of weak butter—and let the sentence settle into her bones the way cold water settles into a coat. It didn't rush. It didn't need to. It had ten years of practice finding its way into every seam of her.

*He took the Boston job.* Of course he had. Of course. She had known this ending since she was twenty-six years old, standing on the platform at Harbor Row station watching a train carry him toward a life that had never once included room for her. Some stories you don't read; you just recognize them when they come around again. And she had stood in her own bookshop—her own shop, surrounded by other people's endings—and let herself believe, for one reckless autumn, that this time the plot might bend.

Foolish. She said it aloud to the ceiling, testing the weight of it. *Foolish.* The word fit like an old shoe.

Downstairs, the bakery smell was already rising through the floorboards—Marguerite's cinnamon rolls, which meant it was Thursday, which meant the world was continuing its ordinary business whether or not Freya Almeida intended to participate in it. She got up. She dressed in the dark. She braided her hair with the efficiency of a woman boarding up windows before a storm, and when she caught her own reflection in the bathroom mirror—pale, jaw set, eyes that had learned long ago how to look steady when nothing inside them was—she gave herself the smallest nod. *All right. We've done this before. We can do it again.*

The trick, she told herself as she descended the stairs to open the shop, was not to feel less. The trick was to feel less *out loud*. Grief, she had learned, was mostly a logistics problem. You contained it the way you contained anything else: shelving, scheduling, inventory, lists. There was always a list.

She made one before the first customer arrived.

---

By nine o'clock the bookshop smelled of coffee and old paper, and Freya had reorganized the front table three times. The festival inventory spreadsheet glowed on the tablet behind the register—vendor confirmations, tent permits, the bread-stall map she'd redrawn twice because Elias's handwriting kept appearing in the margins of the first draft, little arrows and notes in his careful engineer's print, *load-bearing here?* and *what if we angled the ovens toward the water?*

She'd deleted his annotations that morning. All of them. It had taken four minutes and cost her something she refused to price.

"Freya." Marguerite came through the connecting door between the bakery and the shop, flour on her forearms and a look on her face that Freya knew by heart—the look of a woman approaching a skittish animal with an open hand. "You're up early."

"I'm always up early."

"You're up *early early*. You've got the face you had the year the roof leaked."

"The roof is fine."

"The roof is fine," Marguerite agreed mildly. She leaned against the counter, wiping her hands on her apron, watching Freya straighten a display of local histories that did not need straightening. "Elias came by the kitchen this morning. Before dawn. Wanted to know if I'd seen you."

Freya's hands went still on the books. One heartbeat. Two. Then she resumed aligning spines, each one flush, each one perfect. "I expect he wanted to say goodbye. He'll be busy. Boston's a long commute from here."

Marguerite was quiet long enough that Freya could hear the tick of the shop's old clock, the one shaped like a lighthouse that June had won at the fair two summers ago. "Freya. What exactly did Helena tell you?"

"That he accepted the position." Freya kept her voice level, pleasant, the voice she used for customers asking if a book was in stock. "Director of something. Strategic operations. Very impressive. Very him." She smiled, and hated how easily the smile arrived, how well-practiced it was after all these years. "It's what his mother always wanted. It's what he was always going to do. I'd have to be a fool to be surprised."

"And did he tell you that himself?"

"He didn't have to." She turned to face Marguerite fully now, arms crossed, chin up—the posture of a woman defending a position she could not afford to examine. "Marguerite, I watched him stand in this shop three weeks ago and talk about staying like it was a real option. Like people get to just *stay*. I built a whole—" Her voice caught, and she smoothed it flat with visible effort. "I'm not doing this again. I'm not spending another decade being the footnote in somebody else's career. If he wants Boston, Boston is a fine city, and I hope it snows beautifully there."

"Did you ask him?"

"There's nothing to ask."

"There's everything to ask," Marguerite said, gentle and immovable, "and you know it, and the fact that you won't is the loudest thing in this room."

Freya picked up the tablet. "I have forty-one vendor confirmations to chase down and a permit problem with the harbor committee. If you'll excuse me."

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment—long enough that Freya felt it like weather on her skin—and then she sighed, untied her apron, and headed back through the door. At the threshold she paused. "For the record," she said, without turning around, "that man has not signed anything. I'd bet my oven on it."

The door swung shut. Freya stood alone among her books and did not believe her, because believing her would mean hoping again, and hope was a debt collector she had already paid too much.

---

She threw herself into the festival the way some people throw themselves into the sea.

There was so much to do that it was almost merciful. The bread festival ran on volunteers and stubbornness, and Freya had spent ten years being the town's quiet engine—she who remembered which vendor needed a ramp for their cart, which family baked the rye loaves for the memorial table, which corner of the green flooded when it rained. For six days she worked from seven in the morning until eleven at night, and the work was good, the work was *useful*, the work asked nothing of her heart.

She answered emails. She confirmed the string quartet. She argued the fire marshal down from his position on candle lanterns and lost gracefully. She measured the green with a tape measure in the rain, alone, her umbrella abandoned somewhere, and when she came home soaked to the skin June took one look at her and said, "You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you're everywhere except here." June was ten going on forty, and she said it while doing her homework at the kitchen table, not even looking up, which somehow made it worse. "Dad used to do that."

The word landed in the kitchen like a dropped glass.

June had started saying it a week ago—not *my father*, not *the man from the festival*, but *dad*, casual as breathing, testing it out loud the way you test ice before putting your weight down. Freya had frozen the first time, mid-dinner, fork suspended. She had meant to explain. She had rehearsed explanations for a decade—there was a whole drawer of them, next to the letters. *Your father loved you before he ever met you. Your father didn't know. Your father would have stayed if the world had let him.* But now there was a new explanation to give or withhold, and it had Helena Vane's perfume on it, and Freya could not bring herself to speak any version of it aloud.

So she said, "Eat your vegetables," which was not an answer to anything, and June looked at her with those gray eyes—his eyes, God help her, his exact eyes—and said nothing, and the silence between them grew one ring thicker.

That night Freya sat at the kitchen table after June was asleep and wrote out the seating chart for the closing ceremony in her neatest hand. Mayor's row. Vendor families. The memorial table for the fishermen. A blank space where the guest speaker's name should have gone, because the guest speaker had been supposed to be Elias—he'd agreed to it weeks ago, standing in the shop with flour on his sleeves from Marguerite's kitchen, laughing at something June had said—and Freya wrote *TBD* in the space and then crossed it out and wrote it again, pressing hard enough to tear the paper slightly.

He would come to the ceremony anyway, probably. He'd stand at the back with the tourists. He'd clap politely. And then he'd drive east on Route 9 toward a life with a salary and a title and no gray-eyed daughter in it, and Harbor Row would fold him up like a letter and put him away.

*Like a letter.* She laughed at herself, once, bitterly, alone in the kitchen. Everything came back to letters. Ten years of writing to a man across an ocean, and she still couldn't manage five sentences to his face.

---

On the seventh day, Marguerite stopped knocking.

Freya was in the back of the shop, elbow-deep in a crate of donated books for the festival's charity table, when the connecting door opened without preamble and Marguerite came through it carrying two mugs of cider and the expression of a woman who had decided that diplomacy had failed.

"No," Freya said immediately.

"He hasn't taken the job."

"Marguerite—"

"Listen to me." Marguerite set the mugs down on the crate with a decisive clink. "I've known that boy since he was nine years old stealing day-old croissants out of my reject bin. He has not signed anything. He called the Boston office Tuesday and told them he needed more time. He has been walking past your window four times a day like a ghost in his own town because you won't look at him. And yesterday he sat in my kitchen for two hours asking me questions about June's birthday—her *birthday*, Freya, he did the math right there on a napkin, and then he put his head in his hands and didn't move for ten minutes. Does that sound like a man leaving?"

Freya's hands had gone cold around the book she was holding. *Wuthering Heights,* said the spine, ironically. She set it down very carefully.

"She showed me the offer letter," Freya said quietly. "Helena. She had it printed out. His name on it. The start date."

"And did she show you a signature?"

"It doesn't matter—"

"It matters *enormously*," Marguerite said, and for the first time in their twenty-year friendship her voice cracked upward, sharp with exasperation and something underneath it, something fierce and protective. "Freya Almeida. That woman burned a hundred letters. She stole ten years off your life and that child's life, and you are letting her do it *again*, right now, today, because she handed you a piece of paper and you believed her. Because some part of you was always waiting for the proof that you were right to hide. Well, here's your proof, honey: you weren't right. You were just scared. Both things can be true, but only one of them gets to run your life."

The shop was very quiet. Rain had started against the front window, soft and indifferent, blurring the streetlights into smears of gold.

"You don't understand," Freya said finally, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "If I ask him—if I stand in front of him and ask him to choose, and he chooses wrong—I can survive it. I've survived worse. But June can't. She's already practicing saying *dad*. She looks at the door when the bell rings. If I let her hope and then he drives away—" Her breath hitched. "I will not watch my daughter learn what I learned. Not at ten years old. I'd rather she learn nothing at all."

"So you're teaching her nothing instead." Marguerite's voice gentled. "You're teaching her that questions don't get answers. That mothers go quiet and work too much and stand in the rain measuring things. Baby girl, she's already learning. You're not protecting her from the story. You're just making sure she reads it alone."

Freya turned away, back to the crate, back to the books, because if she kept looking at Marguerite's face she was going to come apart in the middle of her own shop, and she had a schedule, she had a list, she had forty-one things that needed her and none of them required her heart to be intact.

"Thank you for the cider," she said.

Marguerite stood there a moment longer. Then, softly: "Closing ceremony's Saturday. He asked me for a favor. I'm not telling you what it is, because you'd find a way to sabotage it, and honestly, sweetheart, you've earned one good ambush." The door closed behind her, and the rain filled the silence she left.

---

Friday night, Freya didn't sleep.

She sat up in bed with the lamp on and the false drawer's contents spread across the quilt—no, not the letters, she couldn't bear the letters—but the napkin. Elias's napkin, the one Marguerite had pressed into her hands on the way out, folded small, and Freya had sworn she wouldn't look at it and had looked at it within the hour.

His handwriting covered it in pencil. June's birthday, March 14th. His deployment dates, recalled with military precision. Subtraction performed twice, checked, circled. And beside the arithmetic, in letters that had grown unsteady halfway through, a single line:

*Nine months minus two weeks. She was already on the train with us. I just didn't know to look down.*

Freya held the napkin until the lamp burned low and the rain came harder against the glass, and she thought about the platform at Harbor Row station ten years ago, and the train, and the way she had stood there with a secret under her ribs like a second heartbeat, and how he had looked down at her from the window and mouthed *write to me* —and she had. Oh, she had. A hundred and seventeen times.

And he had never written back. And she had built her whole cathedral of silence on that foundation, and now Marguerite was telling her the foundation was a lie, that the ground had been pulled out from under her by a woman with good posture and a printing habit, and Freya found that she did not know how to rebuild. She did not know how to be a woman who had been robbed instead of a woman who had been left. Being left had a shape she understood. It had routines. It had lists.

Hope had no list at all.

At 3 a.m., June appeared in the doorway in her flannel pajamas, dragging her blanket, the way she did maybe twice a year anymore—she was getting too old for it, and every time it happened Freya felt time passing like a tide going out.

"Bad dream?" Freya whispered, making room.

June climbed in and settled against her side, and for a while neither of them spoke. Then, into the dark, in a small voice carefully stripped of all expectation: "Mom. Is he coming tomorrow? To the ceremony?"

Freya stared at the ceiling. Every rehearsed answer she owned rose up and offered itself—*we'll see, sweetheart, maybe, ask me later*—and she opened her mouth to deploy one, the safe one, the nothing-at-all one.

What came out instead, barely audible, was: "I don't know, baby. I truly don't know."

June was quiet a long moment. Then she said, "Okay," and burrowed closer, and Freya lay awake holding her daughter in the dark, listening to the rain, feeling the old story and the new possibility tearing her cleanly in half—and downstairs, through the floorboards, faint as a memory, she could hear Marguerite's oven ticking as it preheated, hours before dawn, baking something for a ceremony that was now fewer than twelve hours away.

Whatever Marguerite was baking, whatever favor Elias had asked—it was happening with or without her.

The bell above the bookshop door would ring tomorrow. Someone would walk through it.

She just didn't know yet who she'd be when they did.
