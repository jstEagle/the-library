## 18. Letter 118

Elias woke before dawn on Thursday with the particular clarity of a man who has finally stopped lying to himself.

He had been awake most of the night, not from pain—though his shoulder ached in the cold the way it always did now—but from arithmetic. The kind Marguerite had taught him over flour and butter three weeks ago, the kind that kept compounding no matter how many times he ran the numbers. Ten years. One hundred and seventeen letters. A daughter who did long division at the kitchen table of a woman who had loved him since she was nineteen years old, and who had every reason on earth to hate him.

He lay in the narrow bed above the bakery and listened to Marguerite's ovens tick and warm below him, and he made himself a promise that felt less like hope than like a decision: today, he was going to stop rehearsing and start doing.

The first thing he did was wrong, and he knew it was wrong while he was doing it.

He went to the window with his coffee and looked across the street at the bookshop, because he always did, because ten days of silence had not cured him of the habit any more than ten years of silence had cured him of her. The lights were on. He could see Freya's silhouette moving behind the front windows, turning chairs down off tables, unlocking the door for the early regulars. She moved the way she had always moved—efficient, unhurried, like someone who had learned to make peace with small rooms—and then she stopped, mid-stride, and stood very still in the middle of her own shop, one hand pressed flat against her sternum, and Elias felt the distance between their two windows like a physical weight.

She wasn't looking at him. She hadn't looked at him in ten days. Not since the midway setup night, when he'd opened his mouth to say *your daughter has my eyes* and she'd cut him off with a question about generator permits and walked away so fast her cider sloshed over her wrist.

He had let her walk. That was the mistake he kept circling. He had told himself she needed space, that the truth deserved a better moment than a folding table half-loaded with bunting, and meanwhile the truth had sat in his chest growing heavier, until now it was the only thing in him that had any mass at all.

He dressed, went down the outside stairs into the cold, and pushed open the bakery door into warmth and cinnamon.

Marguerite was alone, pulling the first trays. She was seventy-one years old and built like a keel, and she looked up at him with the expression of a woman who had been waiting eleven days for him to walk through that door looking exactly this stupid.

"Well," she said. "The ghost speaks."

"Marguerite." He crossed to the counter. "I need to ask you something, and I need you to answer me straight."

"I'm famous for it."

"Freya thinks I took the Boston job."

The tray in Marguerite's hands stopped halfway to the rack. Flour drifted. For a long moment the only sound was the tick of cooling metal and, faintly, the radio murmuring weather to an empty room.

"She told you that," Elias said. It wasn't a question anymore; her face had answered it. "When?"

"Monday." Marguerite set the tray down with exaggerated care, the way people do when they're buying time. "Helena Vane came into the bookshop during the festival launch. Offered Freya money to disappear. And when that didn't work—" Marguerite's jaw tightened— "she mentioned, very casually, that you'd accepted a position in Boston. Starting after the new year. That you'd be gone by Christmas."

Elias's hand found the edge of the counter. The room tilted slightly, righted itself.

"That's not true," he said. "I never accepted anything. I turned them down. Three weeks ago. I called Daniel Okafor myself and told him no."

"I know," Marguerite said.

"You *know*?"

"Elias." She wiped her hands on her apron and came around the counter, and her voice dropped into something gentler and more terrible. "I've known that woman for forty years. I watched her burn through two husbands and half this town's goodwill. When she walked out of that bookshop Monday with her chin up and her coat swinging, I knew something rotten had just happened. So yes. I suspected. But suspicion isn't proof, and I wasn't going to stand in your doorway speculating about a mother to her son." She paused. "Also, frankly, I was waiting to see how long it would take you to notice that the woman you love had stopped making eye contact with you across an entire street."

"Eleven days," Elias said hollowly.

"Eleven days," Marguerite confirmed. "You're lucky she's stubborn instead of proud. A proud woman would have already rented out her apartment and moved to Vermont."

He thought of Freya standing in the middle of her shop, hand against her chest. He thought of what that gesture would look like to a woman who believed he had chosen Boston again—chosen the career, chosen his mother's architecture of his life, chosen everything over her, a second time, with a decade of evidence to make it believable. Of course she had shut down. Of course she had thrown herself into festival logistics and stopped looking at his window. She wasn't grieving him. She was *surviving* him, again, and this time she'd had practice.

"She didn't even ask me," he said. His voice came out strange. "Ten years ago she never got the chance to ask. And now—"

"Now she assumed the worst," Marguerite said, "because the worst is the story she already knows by heart. People don't abandon the story they know, Elias. They just reread it." She picked up her scoring blade and nodded toward the door. "So. What are you going to do about it?"

And there it was—the question underneath all the other questions, the one he'd been circling all night. He had spent eleven days drafting and redrafting a letter in a green notebook, writing to a woman the way she had once written to him, because written words were the only language their whole catastrophe had left intact. But a letter left in a drawer was just paper. A letter had to arrive.

"I need a favor," he said.

Marguerite raised one gray eyebrow.

"The closing ceremony," Elias said. "Saturday night. The lantern walk, the last bread coming out, whatever it is you people do. I need five minutes at the end of it. In front of everyone."

Marguerite set down her blade. She studied him for a long moment—not unkindly, but thoroughly, the way she studied dough to judge whether it had risen enough to survive the oven.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. "That girl has spent ten years being private about her heart. You're proposing to take the most private thing she owns and hold it up in front of four hundred people who've been gossiping about her since September."

"Yes."

"And if she says no? If she stands there in front of the whole town and tells you to go to Boston and stay there?"

"Then I'll go," Elias said. "But she deserves to hear it in my voice, in public, where it can't be intercepted or burned or edited by anyone. That's the whole disease, Marguerite. Everything we ever said to each other got taken apart by someone else before it arrived. So this time—" his hand closed briefly, uselessly, on nothing— "this time it arrives whole, or it doesn't arrive at all."

Marguerite was quiet for a moment. Then she turned back to her dough, scored three swift lines across the top of a round, and slid it into the oven.

"The committee meets at six tonight," she said. "I'll move the lantern lighting to eight-forty. That gives you the last slot before the harbor lights go up." She glanced over her shoulder at him, and something in her face was almost soft. "One condition."

"Name it."

"When it's done—whatever happens—you come back here and eat something. Win or lose. I'm not having a man faint on my boardwalk."

* * *

He wrote the final version of Letter 118 that afternoon, at the little table by his window, with the green notebook open and the harbor going silver and then copper and then dark beyond the glass.

It took four attempts. The first was too much apology—he read it back and heard a man begging, and Freya had never once begged him for anything, not even in the letters he'd read Tuesday night, all hundred and seventeen of them, spread across his floor like the rings of a felled tree. The second was too careful, all hedged clauses, a man building exits into his own declaration. The third he burned in the sink because it sounded like his mother: polished, persuasive, and fundamentally about winning an argument.

The fourth he wrote in twenty minutes without stopping, and when he read it back, his hands were shaking, and he knew it was done.

*Freya.*

*I'm not going to say sorry first, because you've had enough of men leading with the easy part. I'm going to say this instead: I never accepted Boston. I turned the job down three weeks ago, before I ever read your letters, before I knew about June, before any of it. My mother told you otherwise because telling the truth has never once served her, and because she has spent thirty-five years building my life the way other people build model ships—in glass cases, where nothing real can touch them.*

*But here is the part I actually need you to know, the part I'd shout from the breakwater if you'd let me: even if I had taken the job, it wouldn't have mattered. Do you understand? I read all one hundred and seventeen of your letters in one night, and somewhere around number forty I stopped being able to imagine a version of my future that didn't have your handwriting in it. You wrote to a dead address for ten years. You kept a drawer full of grief under a cash register and opened the shop every morning anyway. You raised our daughter alone and taught her to be sharp and funny and brave, and you did it in a town that watched you do it and said nothing. There is no city on earth worth trading that. There never was.*

*I have questions. About June—her birthday, her allergies, whether she likes the ocean as much as you do, whether she knows any of the songs. I have ten years of questions and I intend to spend the rest of my life asking them badly and getting some of them wrong. I'm not asking you to hand me a family. I'm asking to practice. To be someone practicing, in the doorway, in the margins, until I earn the center of the page.*

*You said once, in letter sixty-two, that you were afraid you'd spent so long loving a memory that the real man wouldn't fit the shape you'd kept warm. Freya—I don't fit. I'm worse than the memory. I'm slower and more damaged and I burn toast. But I'm here. Across the street. Every morning. For as long as it takes you to believe that the door between us opens from both sides.*

*Come to the closing ceremony tomorrow night. Or don't, and I'll say it anyway, to the whole town, because you deserve a sentence spoken out loud where no one can steal it.*

*Yours, still, and without an address to hide behind,*
*Elias*

He read it twice. Then he folded it into an envelope, and on the front, in block capitals, he wrote:

**FREYA ALMEIDA — DELIVER BY HAND — THIS ONE ARRIVES**

* * *

Getting it to her was the problem. Handing it to her himself meant a conversation, and a conversation meant either the whole truth now, in a doorway, exhausted—or the ceremony tomorrow, prepared, witnessed, irreversible. He wanted the ceremony. He wanted her to hear the beginning of it in his voice, not in ink.

Which left the envelope sitting on his table all evening while he paced, until nearly nine o'clock, when the light in the bookshop across the street went out and Freya emerged with June, locking up, walking the three blocks toward home the way they did every night—June orbiting her mother like a small moon, talking with her hands about something, Freya nodding along with the particular half-attention of a woman carrying too much.

They passed directly beneath his window. He stepped back from the glass, heart hammering like a boy's, feeling absurd and desperate and certain.

And then he saw it: June's schoolbag, slung over her shoulder, unzipped at the top because June Almeida had never in her life met a container she respected. Books crowded the opening. A library card stuck out sideways.

Elias was down the stairs and out the door before he had finished deciding to be.

"June!"

She turned. Streetlight caught her face—his face, younger, unguarded—and for a second she just looked at him, wary and curious, the math-problem look he'd earned on day one and never shaken. Freya had stopped three steps ahead, and he felt her attention land on him like a hand on his shoulder, heavy and refusing.

"My mom says you're leaving," June said. Flat. Ten years old and armored. "For Boston."

"I'm not," Elias said. He crouched—not all the way, he'd learned that, not looming, just level—and held out the envelope. "I need you to give this to her. Tonight. It's important, and I couldn't risk it getting lost."

June looked at the envelope. Then past him, at her mother, whose face in the streetlight was unreadable and white. Then back at him.

"What is it?"

"It's a letter," Elias said. "Number one hundred and eighteen. Your mom will explain the numbering."

Something flickered across June's face—she'd found the drawer, he remembered suddenly, she'd read three of them before Freya caught her; she knew exactly what the numbering meant—and she took the envelope with both hands, solemn as a courier accepting state secrets.

"If it's mean," she said, "I'm reading it first."

"It's not mean."

"Everyone says that." But she tucked it into her schoolbag, deep, zipping the top halfway closed—an act of containment so out of character that Elias almost laughed. Almost.

He stood. Freya hadn't moved. The three of them stood in a triangle of lamplight on Harbor Row, and the whole town might as well have been watching, because the whole town probably was, curtains twitching up and down the street.

"Goodnight, Freya," he said quietly.

She didn't answer. But she didn't turn away either, and her eyes dropped—to the envelope disappearing into the schoolbag, to him, to the space between them that eleven days had widened into a canyon—and for one suspended second Elias saw something in her face that wasn't anger. It was fear. The specific fear of a woman being handed a letter she has spent ten years both writing and dreading.

Then June tugged her sleeve—"Mom, I'm starving"—and the moment broke, and they walked on, and Elias stood alone on the cold sidewalk watching the two of them shrink down Harbor Row, one tall, one small, one carrying his entire future zipped inside a canvas bag next to a math workbook.

Tomorrow night, eight-forty. The lanterns. The whole town.

He turned and walked back toward the bakery, and above Marguerite's darkened windows the autumn stars were out, hard and clear, and for the first time in ten years Elias Vane went to bed believing that the morning might actually arrive.
