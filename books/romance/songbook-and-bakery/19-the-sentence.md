## 19. The Sentence

The last night of the festival arrived the way all important nights do—unremarkably, with a list.

Freya stood behind the folding table at the edge of the green, clipboard in hand, checking off items she had already checked twice: lanterns for the harbor walk, the string quartet Marguerite had bullied into playing for free, the bread contest ribbons folded in their envelope, the cider urn that had a habit of sputtering. Above her, the sky was doing its October trick—bruised purple at the edges, gold burning low over the water, as if the whole town were lit from inside by an oven door left open.

She had not slept. She had not expected to.

June's schoolbag sat under the table where Freya had stowed it that morning, and inside it, folded into June's math workbook, was a letter Freya had read eleven times and still could not stop reading. Letter 118. Elias had left it there two days ago without explanation, only a look—a look like a man setting down something heavy and stepping back to see if it would hold. *Read this when you're ready,* he'd said. *Or don't. But I'll be at the closing ceremony either way.*

She had read it standing in the back room of the bookshop among the boxes of unsold remaindered novels, and by the third paragraph her legs had given out and she'd finished it sitting on the floor with her back against the returns cart, one hand pressed flat over her mouth as though she could push the words back in.

*Dear Freya,* it began. *I have been writing to you for ten years too. I just didn't know the address.*

He wrote about the years she had spent guessing at—the silence after deployment, the postings she'd imagined, the woman his mother had once mentioned at a Christmas party Freya hadn't attended. He wrote about the day he'd found her letter in his grandmother's things, unopened, and how he'd sat on the floor of an estate lawyer's office and understood, all at once, that grief has an arithmetic and someone had been cooking his books. He wrote about June—*I have seen her exactly forty-one times now, I have been counting, I count everything since I learned what counting costs*—and every question he had for her, numbered, patient, unashamed: *Does she like the sea or just the idea of it? Does she know about the lighthouse? Will she let me be wrong in front of her while I learn?*

And then, near the end, the sentence that had undone Freya entirely:

*I am not asking to come back. There is no back. I am asking to begin, badly, in front of everyone, and keep beginning until we're good at it.*

Freya had folded the letter along its original creases and put it in her apron pocket, where it had lived ever since, going soft at the corners from the heat of her hands.

Now the green filled around her. Harbor Row turned out for the closing ceremony the way it turned out for nothing else—not even storms, Marguerite liked to say, though that wasn't strictly true; storms got better attendance. Blankets spread across the grass. Children running the last laps of sugar-high energy before the lantern walk. The quartet tuning with the cheerful incompetence of people who had known each other since kindergarten. Someone's dog wearing a scarf knitted to look like a baguette, which had been Marguerite's idea and which the dog, a saintly old retriever named Biscuit, bore with dignity.

"Stop fidgeting," Marguerite said, materializing at Freya's elbow with two cups of cider and pressing one into her hand. "You've straightened that clipboard so many times it's going to file a complaint."

"I'm fine."

"You're luminous with terror. It's very becoming." Marguerite sipped her own cider, eyes scanning the crowd with the satisfaction of a general surveying a won field. "Everyone's here. Even the Hendersons, who haven't attended anything since the incident with the chili cook-off."

"That wasn't an incident, it was a fire."

"A *small* fire." Marguerite's voice gentled, which was how Freya knew the next thing was coming. "He's here, you know. Been here since four. Helped set up the lantern poles. He's terrible with knots, by the way. Ten years in the army and not one usable knot."

Freya looked despite herself. Across the green, near the stage that was really just a flatbed trailer with bunting stapled to its sides, Elias Vane was handing a coil of rope to a teenager, and even from thirty feet away she could see the way he held himself—carefully, like a man who had rehearsed something and was terrified of dropping it. He wore a sweater she didn't recognize, dark green, civilian-soft, and he kept touching his breast pocket as if checking that something was still there.

"He asked me for a favor," Marguerite said. "Two days ago."

"I know. The letter."

"Not the letter." Marguerite turned to face her fully, and beneath the flour-dusted humor of her there was something older and more serious, the face of the woman who had held Freya together through a pregnancy and a birth and ten years of Tuesdays. "He asked me to make sure you stayed. He said—" She paused, and Freya realized Marguerite was quoting carefully, holding the words exactly as they'd been given. "He said, 'Tell her she doesn't have to say anything. But if she does, I want her to know no one will laugh. Not even Helena.'"

Freya's throat closed. "Is Helena coming?"

Marguerite's mouth went wry. "She's here. Third row of blankets, sitting like she's at a shareholders' meeting. Elias called her himself. Told her if she wanted to be part of his life, this was the door, and it was open tonight only." A beat. "She came. Draw your own conclusions."

Freya drew none. Her conclusions had all fled at the sight of Helena Vane's silver-streaked chignon above a wool coat worth more than the bookshop's monthly rent, seated primly on a tartan blanket beside a woman Freya vaguely recognized from the garden society.

The quartet stumbled into something resembling a waltz. The mayor—blessedly brief this year—took the flatbed and said words about community and harvest and the enduring spirit of Harbor Row, and Freya heard none of them. She was doing arithmetic of her own: one hundred and seventeen letters written, one hundred and seventeen never answered, one letter received, one sentence rehearsed for a decade in showers and stairwells and the long dark before dawn. *I never stopped choosing you.* Six words. She had said them aloud perhaps fifty times in her life, always alone, always to no one, testing them like a coin bitten for gold. They had never once come out steady.

"And now," the mayor said, "a new tradition, proposed by one of our own—Elias Vane, recently returned to us, who has asked for a few minutes before the lantern walk."

A scatter of applause. Freya's heart performed a maneuver that should have required a permit.

Elias climbed onto the flatbed. Up close, under the strings of festival lights, she could see what the distance had hidden: the shadows under his eyes, the way his hands shook slightly as he unfolded a piece of paper—her paper, she realized, her own stationery, the cream-colored stock from the shop—and the moment he found her face in the crowd and held it, the way a drowning man holds a rail.

"Ten years ago," he said, and his voice carried because the green was quiet, because Harbor Row loved nothing more than a story told properly, "I left this town believing I would come back to everything I left. I was wrong. And I want to say publicly, because privately hasn't worked, what happened to the years between."

Helena Vane did not move. But Freya saw her hands fold tighter in her lap, saw the cost of sitting still paid line by line in her face.

Elias lifted the paper. "This is a letter," he said. "It's number one hundred and eighteen. The first hundred and seventeen weren't mine—they were hers." His eyes found Freya's again. "She wrote to me every week for years, to an address that had stopped existing, because someone made sure I'd never see them. I'm not going to tell that story tonight. Tonight I'm going to answer."

And he read.

He read about the first year, when he'd grieved her like weather—constant, ambient, unnameable. About writing letters of his own in a notebook he later burned in a burn pit in Kandahar because hope felt like contraband. About the second year, when he'd gone back to the bakery on leave and found it closed and stood in the rain for an hour convincing himself she'd moved on, deserved to move on, and how he'd hated himself for being relieved, because relief meant he could survive it, and surviving it felt like betrayal. About the fifth year, when he'd stopped saying her name even to himself. About the ninth, when he'd caught himself unable to remember whether her laugh went up at the end or down, and had sat in a barracks bunk at two in the morning grieving a sound.

The crowd had gone utterly still. Somewhere near the front, Biscuit the retriever laid his baguette-scarfed head on a child's knee, as if even the dog understood.

Then Elias folded the first page, and took out another, and his voice changed—lighter, rougher, alive.

"These are my questions," he said. "For June Almeida. Who is ten years old, who is smarter than both of her parents put together, and who is currently hiding behind the cider table pretending she isn't listening, which is fair, because I taught her mother that trick."

A ripple of laughter, warm and startled. Freya felt June press against her side—had felt her there for several minutes, actually, having abandoned all pretense of helping—and put her arm around her daughter's shoulders and did not let go.

"Question one," Elias read. "Do you like the sea, or just the idea of it? Because your mother loves the idea of it and your grandmother—" a breath, a small private smile "—a woman named Ruth, whom I never met but who apparently left you a house, loved the actual ocean, and I'd like to know which team you're on. Question two: is the mirror thing real? Do you actually see it? Because I see it every time you look at me, and I need to know if it's as strange for you as it is for me. Question three: what are you reading right now, and can I borrow it, and will you judge me if I don't finish it?"

June laughed—a wet, cracked sound against Freya's shoulder—and Elias's own voice broke on the next line, and he let it break, standing in front of three hundred neighbors with his whole chest open.

"Question twelve," he said. "The last one. It's not really a question. It's this: I missed your entire childhood so far, and I can't fix that, and I'm not going to insult either of us by pretending a speech fixes it. All I can do is show up tomorrow, and the day after, and be wrong a lot, and stay anyway. That's my whole plan. It's not much. But it's mine, and it's yours if you want it."

He lowered the pages. The lights strung overhead swayed in the harbor wind, throwing moving gold across the crowd, across the blankets, across Helena Vane's pale, unreadable face.

"So," Elias said, to the whole green, to one woman in particular. "That's my ten years. That's what I did with them. I spent them looking for a door back to a house that had changed, and I finally understand there isn't one. There's just this. Tonight. All of you watching. And her."

Silence, vast and soft as snowfall.

Freya's body made the decision before her mind caught up—or perhaps her mind had been making it for ten years, one unsent Tuesday at a time, and this was simply the moment the vote was finally counted. She stepped forward. June's hand slipped into hers and stayed there, and they walked together through the parted crowd, past Marguerite's fierce wet-eyed smile, past the tartan blanket where Helena Vane watched with her chin high and her eyes shining in a way that might have been fury or might have been the closest thing to surrender she knew how to perform.

The flatbed was higher than it looked. Elias reached down and gave Freya his hand, and his palm was shaking as much as hers, and somehow that steadied her—two trembling people, holding each other upright.

"There were a hundred and seventeen letters," she said. Her voice came out small. She cleared her throat and tried again, louder, letting Harbor Row hear what it had waited a decade to hear. "One hundred and seventeen. I wrote one every week. Then every other week. Then—I stopped mailing them, in year three, but I never stopped writing them, because writing them was the only place you still existed, and I couldn't—" Her breath hitched. June squeezed her hand. "I couldn't make you stop existing. So I kept you in a drawer."

"I know," Elias said softly. "I've read them. All of them. Last night. Every word."

"Then you know what I almost said, ten years ago, at the bus station. You know the sentence. You've read it a hundred times in a hundred envelopes."

"I know it," he said. "But I want to hear it out loud. Once. From you. In front of everyone, so neither of us can take it back."

The wind came off the water and stirred the bunting. The quartet had gone silent. Three hundred people held their breath, and Freya Almeida—who had faced down landlords and blizzards and the worst day of her life alone in a delivery room—felt her knees shake like a girl's.

She looked at Elias. She looked at June, whose eyes—his eyes, God, they had always been his eyes—were wide and waiting. She thought of the drawer, and the rain, and the ten thousand ordinary mornings she had survived by not saying it.

"I never stopped choosing you," she said.

It came out broken on the second word and strong on the last three, and it was not steady, and it was not polished, and it was the truest thing she had ever said in her life.

Elias made a sound that was half laugh and half something older, and then he was down off the flatbed and his arms were around her and June both, gathering them in like a man collecting everything he'd lost in one place at last, and the green erupted—cheering, clapping, Marguerite audibly sobbing into her apron, Biscuit barking at the sheer emotional volume of it all. Somewhere in the crush Freya felt June's arms come around her waist from the other side, and heard her daughter say, muffled and dry-eyed and devastatingly practical:

"You both owe me so many pancakes."

Elias laughed into Freya's hair. "Name a price."

"Walk me to school Monday," June said, pulling back to look up at him with the flat appraisal Freya knew meant negotiations had merely opened. "Not as my dad. As someone practicing."

"Someone practicing," Elias agreed, his voice wrecked. "Best job I've ever applied for."

Above them, the mayor recovered enough to announce the lantern walk, and the crowd began to move toward the harbor, hundreds of small flames kindling one from another down the darkening street. Freya stayed on the flatbed a moment longer, Elias's hand in hers, watching the light spill toward the water.

Then she saw Helena Vane rise from the tartan blanket, smooth her coat, and turn—not toward the lanterns, but toward the dark end of the green, walking away alone.

Elias followed Freya's gaze. His hand tightened around hers.

"She stayed to the end," he said quietly. "That's more than I expected." He exhaled. "Come on. Before the lanterns are lit—I think it's time somebody walked her to her car."
