## 21. June's Turn

June had prepared for this the way she prepared for everything: with a notebook.

She'd been writing in it since Tuesday, since the night at the festival when Elias Vane stood up in front of half of Harbor Row and read a letter out loud — *her* mother's handwriting, copied out in his careful soldier's print, and then his own words underneath, all about years and questions and a girl named June who was apparently the whole point of everything. The whole town had cried. Mrs. Okafor had cried so hard she'd dropped her cornbread. June hadn't cried at all, because someone in that crowd had to keep their head on straight, and apparently it was going to be her.

The notebook was blue, with a lighthouse on the cover, and inside it were forty-one questions written in her smallest, neatest hand. She had crossed out nine of them already because Marguerite said some questions were traps you set for yourself, and June could tell which ones those were. *Why didn't you come back?* was a trap. She already knew why. Her mother had explained it twice, calmly, in the kitchen, with the good tea out — which meant it was serious — and Elias had stood there holding his own elbows like he was afraid he'd come apart if he let go.

But knowing why wasn't the same as being done. That was the thing grown-ups didn't understand. Knowing why was just the beginning of the part where you decided what to do about it.

So: pancakes. Saturday morning. Neutral territory, sort of — the bakery below Marguerite's shop, which smelled like cinnamon and burnt sugar and had the corner table by the window where June did her homework when the bookshop got too quiet. Her mother had set it up and then made herself scarce, which was transparent even by Mom standards, but June appreciated the effort. A trial needed witnesses, not judges.

Elias was already there when she came down at eight sharp. He'd gotten there early — she could tell because his coffee cup was empty and he kept turning it in his hands like it might say something useful. He was wearing a flannel shirt that looked new, tags-removed-new, and his hair was shorter than it had been at the festival, like he'd tried to fix himself up and then worried he'd overdone it. There was a little bandage on his left thumb.

"You cut yourself," June said, sliding into the seat across from him.

He looked down at his thumb like he'd forgotten it existed. "Buttering toast. At home. Which sounds ridiculous now that I'm saying it out loud."

"It is a little ridiculous."

"I'm glad we're establishing that early." He almost smiled. It was a careful smile, the kind you'd give a dog you didn't know yet. "Marguerite's bringing pancakes. I asked her not to tell me what kind. She said that was 'a coward's order' and I said I'd live with it."

"It's apple," June said. "It's always apple in October. She does pumpkin in November and everyone pretends it's better but it isn't."

"Noted." He pulled out a small notebook of his own — an actual notebook, black, military-looking, with an elastic band — and wrote something in it. June leaned over before she could stop herself.

*Apple > pumpkin. October.*

"You're taking notes?"

"I'm told," Elias said, capping the pen, "that I have ten years of things to learn, and I don't intend to miss any of them."

Marguerite arrived with a stack of pancakes the size of a hatbox, two plates, real maple syrup, and an expression that said she would be within earshot whether they liked it or not. She ruffled June's hair — June allowed it, because Marguerite had earned hair-ruffling privileges through a decade of free day-old scones — and pointed a spatula at Elias.

"She asks the questions here," Marguerite said. "You answer them honest. Those are the rules."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And if you cry, that's fine, but do it quietly. I've got customers."

Then she was gone, retreating behind the counter with the world's least convincing show of wiping down an already-clean espresso machine.

June opened her notebook.

* * *

The first questions were easy ones, warm-up ones, the way Coach Delgado started every practice with laps.

Favorite color. (Green, "but not the bright kind — the kind pine trees are from far away.") Favorite food. ("Your mother's bread, but don't tell her, she'll get insufferable.") Did he snore. ("According to three roommates and one very judgmental medic, yes, catastrophically.")

June wrote down all the answers. Elias watched her write them, and she noticed he didn't rush her, didn't fill the silences, just waited with his hands around his second coffee like waiting was something he was good at. Maybe ten years of letters taught you that. Maybe ten years of silence taught you that too. Both of them had done their time.

Then she turned the page, and the easy part was over, and she felt it in her stomach like the first drop of a big hill.

"Question twelve," she said. "What was your favorite thing about me. Before you knew me. When I was just — a thing in a letter."

Elias went still. Not frozen, exactly. More like a man standing very carefully on ice, testing how much weight it would take.

"That's a hard question," he said.

"You can skip. I marked the skippable ones with a star. Twelve doesn't have a star."

"No stars today." He set his coffee down. "Okay. My favorite thing about you, before I knew you." He looked out the window at the street, at the leaves coming down gold and red along Harbor Row, and June watched him think, really think, not reach for something smooth. "There was a line in one of your mother's letters. She wrote that you'd fallen asleep in a laundry basket, and she couldn't move you because you looked too comfortable, so she sat on the floor next to the dryer for an hour reading a book she didn't care about, just so you could keep sleeping there. And I remember thinking — I remember it physically, like a knock — *I hope she looks like Freya when she sleeps.* Because I already knew what your mother looked like asleep, on a train once, from Boston, and it was the best thing I'd ever seen. And then I thought, whoever this kid turns out to be, she comes from the person who writes sentences like that. So my favorite thing about you was that you were proof of her. You were evidence." He shrugged, embarrassed. "That's the answer. It's probably not the one you wanted."

June wrote it down. Her hand was doing something unsteady, so she pressed harder on the paper to hide it.

"It's fine," she said. "It's actually — it's a good answer. Question thirteen."

* * *

Question nineteen took twenty minutes.

*Did you ever think about finding us? Even after you thought Mom stopped writing?*

He answered it honestly, which was worse than lying and better than lying at the same time, the way most true things are. Yes. Every year on his birthday. Every year on hers, which he'd calculated from a letter and never forgotten. Twice he'd drafted letters to the bookshop and burned them in a barrel behind his barracks because his mother had told him Freya had married, had a life, wanted nothing from him — and he'd believed it, because believing it hurt less than hoping. And then he'd hated himself for believing it, and then he'd believed it again the next year, and that loop, he said, looking directly at the syrup bottle instead of at her, was the thing he was most ashamed of in his whole life. Including the parts with explosions in them.

June wrote all of it down. The notebook page filled up. She underlined nothing, because underlining was for things you needed to remember, and she could already tell she'd remember every word of this until she died.

"Question twenty-two," she said, when her voice worked again. "Are you going to leave?"

And there it was. The question. The one she'd rewritten eleven times because none of the versions sounded brave enough or casual enough, and finally she'd settled on the shortest one, because short questions were harder to dodge.

Elias didn't answer right away. He turned his coffee cup a full rotation. Outside, Marguerite flipped the OPEN sign to CLOSED FOR FAMILY, which was a sign June had never seen before and which made her chest do something complicated.

"I'm going to be honest with you," he said, "because I think you can tell when I'm not, and I'd rather lose points than lose that."

"Okay."

"I don't know how to promise forever. I've seen people promise forever and break it without meaning to — I've *been* that person, kind of, without meaning to. So I'm not going to stand here and swear I'll never leave, because you're smart enough to know nobody can actually guarantee that, and I refuse to start this by lying to you." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and his eyes were wet but his voice was steady, the voice of a man who had learned to talk through weather. "Here's what I can promise. I signed a lease upstairs. Not a month-to-month. A year. I turned down Boston — the real job, the career, the whole thing my mother built her life around — and I did it in writing, so there's a paper trail, because I've learned that in this family you need receipts. And every single morning, I am going to be up those stairs, across that street, or around that corner. If I go anywhere, I'll tell you where and when I'm coming back. And if I'm ever late, you can call Marguerite, and Marguerite will yell at me, and I will deserve it."

Behind the counter, Marguerite nodded once, gravely, like a judge accepting testimony.

June looked at her notebook. Forty-one questions. She'd only gotten to twenty-two, and she found, sitting there with her pen hovering, that she didn't need the rest today. Some of them weren't really questions anyway. They were just hurts wearing question costumes, and she could take them out one at a time later, on ordinary days, over ordinary dinners, which was probably how it was supposed to work. Her mom had a shelf in the bookshop now called SECOND EDITIONS, and she'd explained it to June last week: books that got a bad first printing, with the misprints and torn pages, and got fixed and reissued. People loved second editions, her mom said. Sometimes more than the originals, because the flaws were known and accounted for, and the story survived anyway.

June closed the notebook.

"Verdict," she said.

Elias sat up straighter. Actually sat up straighter, like a soldier, like she was a general, and something about that made June feel seven feet tall.

"The trial isn't over," she said. "I want to be clear about that. This is — it's like a probation thing. But I've decided you can have supervised visits with the truth." She held up fingers as she counted. "One: you eat dinner with us on Thursdays. Non-negotiable. Two: you teach me chess, because Grandpa Almeida taught Mom and Mom's terrible at it and I need someone who won't let me win. Three—" and here her voice wobbled and she pushed through it, because she'd rehearsed this part too, in the mirror, four times, "—three. Starting Monday. You can walk me to school."

Elias's breath caught audibly.

"But," June said, holding up a hand before hope could get ahead of itself, "not as my dad. Not yet. Dad is a job title and you haven't been trained. You can walk me to school as—" she checked her notebook even though she didn't need to, "—as *someone practicing*. Someone practicing to maybe, eventually, if they do a good job, apply for the position. That's the terms. Take it or leave it."

For a long moment Elias Vane — decorated sergeant, man who had faced things June never wanted to hear about, man who had read a letter aloud to a whole town without his voice breaking more than twice — put his face in his hands and cried. Quietly, like Marguerite had instructed. His shoulders shook. June slid the napkin dispenser across the table and waited, because her mom said the kindest thing you could do for crying people was act like it was normal, and it was normal, probably, in families. In practicing families.

When he looked up, his eyes were red and he was smiling like a man who'd been handed something fragile and told he was allowed to hold it.

"Someone practicing," he repeated. "That's — yeah. That's the best job title I've ever been offered. Terms accepted."

"Shake on it."

They shook on it. His hand swallowed hers whole and was very careful about it.

"One more thing," June said, gathering her notebook and her dignity. "Monday. Seven-forty. Don't be early, it's weird, and don't be late, it's worse. And Elias?" He looked up. "Mom doesn't know about the walking part yet. I'm telling her. Me. Not you. That's also the terms."

"Understood completely," he said, and June could see he understood — that this was hers to give, the way the letter had been his to read, the way the sentence at the festival had been her mother's to say. Everyone in this family got to hand over their own piece. Nobody got handed for.

She went upstairs two steps at a time, heart hammering, already composing the announcement. Through the window over the stairwell she could see the street below, gold leaves coming down, and her mother crossing toward the bakery with two cups of tea and a face full of the terrified hope of a woman about to ask how it went.

June pushed open the door, held up the blue notebook like a decree, and grinned.

"So. Mom. Clear your Thursday evenings — and somebody's got a new job application pending."
