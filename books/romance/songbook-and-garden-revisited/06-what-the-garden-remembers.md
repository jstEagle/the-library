## 6. What the Garden Remembers

The garden had been dead longer than it had been alive, if you counted by my grandfather's ledger, which I did, because counting by anything else felt like cheating.

*April 14th, 1987 — turned the beds. Good loam, third row still wet. Ruth wants tomatoes where the beans were. Told her the beans know their business better than we do.*

That was how he wrote about a garden: like it had opinions. Like it was a colleague he occasionally lost arguments to. My grandmother Ruth had died before I learned to walk, but she came back every spring in those ledgers, wanting tomatoes in the wrong place, and my grandfather arguing with her through forty years of dirt.

I stood at the gate on a Tuesday morning with a pair of rusted shears and a wheelbarrow with a flat tire, and I understood that I had inherited an argument I could not win.

The kitchen plot sat on the landward side of the lighthouse, tucked into a hollow where the wind couldn't quite reach it — which is why he'd chosen it, and also why everything that ever fell in Port Merrow eventually ended up there. Nine years of neglect had done what nine years of neglect does. The raised beds had slumped into brown suggestion. Bindweed had climbed the old bean trellis like it was training for something. There was a rosemary bush in the corner that had gone woody and strange, half-dead, entirely stubborn, and I recognized myself in it immediately and resented the recognition.

"Fine," I told the garden. "You win."

This is what my life had become: negotiating with plants. My agent had stopped emailing in February. My novel sat at sixty-two thousand words with a heroine standing in a doorway she refused to walk through, and I had begun to suspect she wasn't refusing at all — she was waiting for me to admit I didn't know what was on the other side of it either.

But the garden didn't ask questions. The garden just needed the bindweed pulled, and pulling bindweed is the closest thing to prayer that doesn't require believing anyone's listening.

I worked until my shoulders burned and my hands went green-black at the seams, filling the wheelbarrow twice, three times, hauling it up the path to the compost heap my grandfather had built from pallets in 1994 and which had outlived him, as most of his projects had. The soil underneath the weeds was dark and smelled like rain that hadn't fallen yet. It was good soil. That was the insult of it. All those years of nobody caring, and the ground had kept its end of the bargain anyway.

By noon I'd cleared two beds and found, buried at the corner of the third, a tin box with a rusted lid. Inside: seeds. Paper packets, soft as cloth with damp, labeled in his handwriting. *Scarlet runner. 2009.* Fourteen years old. Dead, almost certainly. I put them in my pocket anyway, because throwing them away felt like a decision I hadn't earned.

* * *

Dear Pen Pal,

I have discovered that I hate gardening. This is not strictly true, but you're a stranger, so I'm going to lead with my strongest material.

Here is my situation: I am clearing an overgrown plot behind my house. It belonged to someone who loved it, and I did not, and now I'm doing penance with a pair of shears older than I am. Today I fought a plant that fights back. Bindweed. Do you know it? It grows a root system nine feet deep — NINE FEET — which means every piece you leave behind becomes a new plant, which means the garden is essentially immortal and I am not. I spent four hours losing to a weed.

Also the wheelbarrow has a flat tire, the shears are rusted shut at the hinge, and I found a tin of seeds from 2009 that I'm keeping for reasons I can't explain to myself, let alone to you.

Why do people do this? Grow things? It seems like a long con you play on yourself. You spend all summer feeding dirt to plants so the plants can feed dirt back to you in October. Meanwhile the sea is right there, doing nothing, beautifully, and asking nothing of anybody.

Tell me I should quit. I want to quit. I would accept encouragement to quit as a form of friendship.

— C.

* * *

The reply came four days later, and I knew it was the reply before I opened it, because Odette had left it propped against my door instead of mailing it — she ran the program out of the library and had appointed herself its postal service whenever the mood took her, which was always.

"You look happier," she said, when I called to accuse her of loitering.

"I look tired."

"Tired and happier. It's a good look on you. Very rustic." A pause, the kind Odette engineered rather than fell into. "Your pen pal writes real letters, you know. Not like some people, who write letters like they're filing taxes."

"Some people are busy."

"Some people," she said, "are hiding behind a lighthouse," and hung up before I could construct a defense.

I sat on the step with the envelope. The handwriting was the same as the first letter — small, upright, controlled, the kind of hand that had been corrected in school and never forgiven the correction. But this letter was heavier. More pages. Whatever I'd written about bindweed had struck something.

Dear C.,

Don't quit. That's the last time I'll say it kindly; after this I'll say it the way my mother said things, which was once, loudly, and then never again.

Your problem isn't the bindweed. Your problem is you're fighting it instead of reading it. Bindweed tells you exactly what your soil is doing — it loves compacted, exhausted ground, poor in nitrogen, the kind of soil that's been left alone too long or worked to death. It's not the villain. It's the symptom. Everyone wants to kill the messenger.

So here's what you do, and I mean all of it, in order:

1. Stop digging. I can hear you digging from here. Every cut root fragments into new plants; you've been planting bindweed for four hours and calling it weeding. Cover the beds instead — cardboard, black plastic, even old carpet if you have it. Starve it. It takes a season. Yes, a whole one. No, there is no faster way. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

2. While the beds sleep, fix the soil you can reach. That dark smell you described — that's organic matter doing its job. Don't dig it in. Lay it on top, two inches, and let the worms do the freight-handling. They've been doing it longer than you have and they don't take lunch breaks.

3. The rosemary in the corner. Woody, half-dead, still alive. Cut it back hard — harder than feels polite, a third of it, into living wood. It will either come back furious or die with dignity, and both outcomes are respectable. Rosemary doesn't do halfway. I respect that about it.

4. The seeds from 2009 are probably dead, but not certainly. Beans are tougher than their packaging. Lay them on a wet paper towel in a warm spot for five days. If they swell, they're alive. If they don't, bury them anyway — some things deserve a funeral in the ground they were meant for.

5. And the sea: the sea does nothing beautifully because it has no obligations. You do. That's not a flaw in your setup. That's the whole difference between a view and a life.

You wanted permission to quit. I don't issue permits. Write back when the cardboard's down.

— W.

P.S. If your shears are rusted shut, soak the hinge in white vinegar overnight. Then oil them. This is not gardening advice, this is basic citizenship.

* * *

I read it three times. The first time for the advice, the second time for the tone — bossy, exact, faintly amused, like someone who had been right before and knew it — and the third time because I couldn't work out why the handwriting bothered me. Small, upright, controlled. I'd seen it somewhere. On a form, maybe. A sign in a shop window. It happens in a town this size; everyone's hand looks familiar eventually.

I set the feeling down next to the seed tin and forgot about it, which is a thing I do, and which — I understand now — is a thing I have always done.

The vinegar trick worked. I want that on the record. I soaked the shears overnight in a jam jar and by morning the hinge moved like it had been waiting nine years for someone to ask. I laid cardboard over the two cleared beds and weighted it with stones from the beach path, and it looked terrible, like the garden had been wrapped for shipping, and I stood back and felt the specific satisfaction of a job done badly but correctly.

Then I tested the seeds.

Five days on a wet paper towel on top of the water heater, which is the warmest spot in a house that runs on fog and duty. On day four I checked and three of the twelve scarlet runners had split their coats and pushed out a pale hook of root, absurd and determined, like something climbing out of a very long sleep.

I sat on the laundry room floor for a while, holding a paper towel with germinating beans on it, feeling something I didn't have a word for. Not hope exactly. Hope implies expectation. This was more like — witness. Something old had said yes, quietly, in the dark, and I had been there to hear it.

I planted them along the south fence, in the one strip of open ground, because W. had said bury them anyway, and because my grandfather's ledger had opinions about runners (*May 3rd, 1991 — Ruth's beans up. She was right about the corner bed. Don't tell her.*) and because some instructions you follow without needing to know who gave them.

* * *

Dear W.,

The beans came up. Three of twelve. I'm telling you first because you'll understand the statistics and no one else in my life will pretend to.

Also: the cardboard is down. The beds look like a crime scene. The rosemary got its haircut and sulked for two days and then, I swear, stood up straighter. Either you know things or you made all of it up and got lucky, and I haven't decided which annoys me more.

Here's what I can't figure out. You write about soil like it's a person you used to argue with. The nitrogen thing, the worms, the "don't dig it in" — that's not book knowledge. Book knowledge sounds different. It hedges. Yours doesn't hedge. Where did you learn this?

Don't answer if it's a sad answer. Actually — no. Answer anyway. I've decided strangers owe each other true answers, since we're the only ones positioned to give them. Everyone I know locally has a version of me they're being polite to. You've never seen me. You get the unedited draft.

Speaking of drafts: I'm writing a novel. Sixty-two thousand words of it, stalled, featuring a heroine named Maren who has been standing in a doorway for eleven chapters. I mention this so you understand the scale of my failures and can calibrate your disappointment accordingly.

— C.

P.S. The wheelbarrow tire is fixed. I fixed it myself. I'm telling you because you're the only entity in my correspondence who will appreciate that the valve stem was corroded and required pliers and profanity.

* * *

His reply — hers, I'd learn later, though the hand gave nothing away then — came fast, faster than the mail usually allowed, as if the letter had been written before mine arrived and was only waiting for an excuse.

Dear C.,

Three of twelve is a germination rate any seed bank would respect. Tell the beans I said well done. Tell yourself nothing; you'll only ruin it.

Where I learned soil: my grandmother, mostly, and then a stretch of years when I had a garden and then didn't, and the difference between those two states taught me more than the having did. You asked for a true answer, so there it is, unedited: I know what ground looks like when it's been left alone too long. I know what comes up in it. Some of it's worth keeping.

As for Maren: eleven chapters in a doorway is not a stall. Eleven chapters is data. Ask her what she's afraid is on the other side, and then ask yourself the same question, and notice whether the answers rhyme. Writers think they invent characters. Characters wait us out. She'll move when you stop writing around whatever it is and start writing into it.

And keep complaining about the garden. Complaints are how you tell the truth in this country.

— W.

P.S. Pliers and profanity are the official tools of the trade. Any gardener who claims otherwise owns a shed full of lies.

* * *

I read that line about the grandmother twice, and something in it snagged — a stretch of years when I had a garden and then didn't — grief wearing work clothes, passing itself off as horticulture. I thought: whoever you are, you've buried something in a bed somewhere and you go back to it. I thought: I recognize the technique.

I didn't think: I know you.

That's the part I keep returning to now, turning it over like a stone to see what was living underneath. Because the signs were all there, weren't they, arranged in plain sight like my grandfather's tools on their pegboard. The local knowledge. The handwriting I'd almost placed. The way W. wrote about leaving and staying like someone who'd done both badly. The way the letters arrived sometimes within a day, as if they'd only crossed the harbor.

But you don't look for your enemy in a kindness. Nobody teaches you that. You learn to expect ambushes, and so you watch the roads, and meanwhile the thing you should be watching walks in through the garden gate carrying a watering can and starts giving you better advice than your own dead family.

I pinned the letter above my desk, next to the ledger page about Ruth's beans. Two sets of handwriting, fifty years apart, both of them telling me to stop digging and start paying attention.

Maren walked through her doorway that night. I didn't plan it. I was writing the complaint letter — the one about the rosemary sulking — and somewhere in the second paragraph she sighed and stepped through, and I followed her, and the chapter wrote itself in ninety minutes, and when I came up for air the lamp was sweeping and the house was dark and I was, for the first time in months, not alone in my own head.

I mailed the news to W. before I could talk myself out of it. *She moved,* I wrote. *Eleven chapters and she moved. What did you do?*

The reply came back in two days, short, in that small controlled hand:

*Nothing. I just stopped blocking the light.*

I read it standing at the garden gate with the fog coming in off the water, and I felt the strangest thing — gratitude, yes, but under it something more unsettling. Recognition. The sense of a voice I'd heard before, saying something true, in another room, in another year, in a register I had filed away and locked.

It passed. Fog passes. That's the arrangement fog makes with you.

I went inside and sharpened the shears, and out along the south fence, in the dark, three scarlet runners put out their first leaves toward a light they couldn't see yet, and the garden went on remembering everything I was trying to forget.
