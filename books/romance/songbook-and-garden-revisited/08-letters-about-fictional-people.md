## 8. Letters About Fictional People

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, except that Tuesdays were the days Cyrus had begun to dread and look forward to in equal measure.

She found it wedged between the electric bill and a flyer for the church rummage sale, her name written in that handwriting she still couldn't place — slanted left, urgent, as though the writer were always slightly ahead of the sentence. She carried it up to the lamp room, because that had become the ritual: coffee, the automated beacon clicking through its noon self-check, and whatever this stranger had decided to say to her this week.

*Dear Neighbor,* it began. The program's rules forbade names, so they'd settled on *Neighbor*, which Cyrus suspected was a compromise neither of them liked but both had stopped fighting.

*I owe you an apology. Last week I told you your garden plan was wrong. It was worse than wrong — it was optimistic. You can't plant tomatoes where the wind comes off the water like that; you'll grow lovely green skeletons and nothing else. But then you wrote back and said you'd already lost two seasons of tomatoes, so apparently we agree. This is what passes for friendship with me, apparently: shared failure.*

Cyrus laughed out loud, alone, forty feet above the harbor. It startled a gull off the gallery rail.

*You mentioned once — obliquely, the way you mention everything real — that you write. Or tried to. Or are trying. You said "the novel is stalled" the way people say "the roof leaks," like it's weather, like it's nobody's fault. I've been thinking about that. So here is my offer, and you can refuse it: tell me about the book. Not whether it's good. Tell me about the woman in it.*

Cyrus read the letter three times. Then she folded it along its creases, put it in her cardigan pocket, and stood at the rail looking down at the town she mostly avoided, feeling something she hadn't felt in years: *asked*. Not pitied. Not managed. Asked.

That night she wrote back.

*Dear Neighbor,*

*Her name is Maren. She keeps a lighthouse she doesn't own, on a coast that isn't specified, and she's waiting for someone who isn't coming back. That's the whole plot so far. Two hundred pages of a woman keeping a light for a man who left, and I can't make him come back because I don't actually want him to, and I can't let her stop waiting because then the book has no reason to exist.*

*There. Now you know why it's stalled. A novel is just a machine for answering one question, and mine won't answer.*

*Your turn. You critique like someone who's been hurt by books. What did fiction do to you?*

She hesitated over the last line. It was too sharp, maybe. Too much like the letters they'd exchanged in the first weeks, all that hostile-adjacent politeness, each of them fencing with pleasantries. But she'd learned something about this stranger in six weeks of correspondence: the woman could take a hit. She gave them, but she could take them. Cyrus sealed the envelope before she could soften it.

* * *

Wren read the letter standing in her kitchen with Juniper's cereal bowl still in the sink and the paint swatches for the hallway fanned across the counter like a losing hand of cards.

*Maren.* She read the name twice. A keeper's daughter, or granddaughter — Wren already knew that much about her pen pal, had known since the third letter when the lighthouse came up, unavoidable, like a stone in a shoe. Which meant she almost certainly knew whose heroine this was. Whose guilt wearing a fictional coat.

And God, the book sounded bad. Not badly written — Wren would have bet money it was beautifully written, because everything this woman wrote had that taut, salt-cured precision, every sentence doing exactly the work it claimed to be doing. Bad in structure. Bad in the way that mattered.

She sat down at the kitchen table while Juniper watched cartoons in the next room, and she wrote:

*Dear Neighbor,*

*You asked what fiction did to me. Fiction lied to me. It told me love was a thing that happened to you, a weather event, and that if you stood in the right place at the right time you'd get soaked. Nobody told me love was more like gardening — mostly unglamorous labor, mostly choosing, mostly showing up on gray days when nothing blooms. So now you know my damage. Your turn's done. Here's mine about your book:*

*Maren is a coward.*

*Don't throw this letter in the stove yet. I mean it structurally. You've built a woman whose entire personality is waiting, and you're asking the reader to find that noble. It isn't noble. Waiting is what people do when they're afraid to choose. You say you can't let her stop waiting or the book has no reason to exist — but that's not true, and I think somewhere you know it. The book's reason to exist isn't the man coming back. It's the moment she realizes the light was never for him.*

*Also — and I say this with respect — if he doesn't come back, good. Let her build something instead. Let her plant something. You clearly know gardens. Give her hands something to do besides wring.*

*One more thing and then I'll stop being insufferable: you wrote "I don't actually want him to come back." Read that sentence again. That's not a plot problem. That's the whole book.*

Wren read it over, and for a moment her pen hovered, wanting to add things she couldn't say — *I know why you can't finish it, I think I'm in it, I think I might be the reason the man left* — but the rules were the rules, and besides, she wasn't sure. She wasn't sure of anything except that writing to this woman had become the steadiest hour of her day, and that frightened her in a way she filed under *later*.

She mailed it Thursday, after walking Juniper past the harbor at a careful distance from the lighthouse, close enough that Juniper pressed her face to the truck window and said, "Mama, does somebody live there?" and Wren said, "Somebody keeps it," which was true, and left it there.

* * *

Cyrus read the reply in the lamp room and had to set it down twice.

*A coward.* Her first instinct was fury — hot, reflexive, the old defensive flare. Her second instinct, arriving about four seconds later and considerably more humiliating, was recognition. Because the stranger was right. Maren was a coward. Cyrus had built her that way without noticing, had dressed the cowardice in duty and called it devotion, and here was some anonymous woman in town — it had to be town; the postmark never varied — taking the whole architecture apart in four paragraphs.

*The light was never for him.*

Cyrus sat with that line for a long time. Below her, the sea did its patient work against the headland. Somewhere in the house behind her was a manuscript in a box, two hundred pages she hadn't opened since spring.

She wrote back that night, faster than she'd written anything in years:

*Dear Neighbor,*

*I hate you. I want that on the record. I read your letter standing up and I had to sit down, and I resent the sitting down most of all.*

*You're right. That's the unbearable part. Maren is a coward, and I made her one because I didn't know how to make her anything else. Here's what I haven't told anyone, including you, including especially you: I started this book nine years ago. Before the automation, before Grandpa died, when I still believed I'd leave. There was going to be a life somewhere else — a residency, a city, a person. And then the light needed keeping, and I stayed, and everyone assumed I'd been jilted or dutiful or both, and I let them assume it because the truth was smaller and uglier: nobody jilted me. I froze. I chose the tower because the tower couldn't leave me and couldn't ask me to be brave.*

*So Maren waits because I waited. And you're telling me the book ends when she stops. Fine. FINE. But understand what you're asking. If she stops waiting, she has to admit the waiting was a choice, and if it was a choice, then it was mine, and if it was mine—*

*Well. You see the problem.*

*Tell me something true about yourself now. Fair is fair. You've taken apart my entire life disguised as literary criticism; give me a brick of yours.*

*— N.*

*P.S. The tomatoes are dead. All of them. Green skeletons, exactly as forecast. I've cleared the bed and I'm leaving it fallow until spring, which feels less like gardening and more like apology.*

* * *

Wren read the letter in the truck outside the hardware store, engine off, rain ticking on the roof, and by the end of it her eyes were burning and she couldn't have said exactly why.

*Nobody jilted me. I froze.*

Nine years she'd carried a version of events in which Cyrus Sterling was the wronged party — the sister abandoned, the engagement broken, the woman left holding a ring and a town's sympathy. Wren had built her whole architecture of shame on that foundation: *I took something from her.* And now here was this letter saying the foundation was sand. Saying Cyrus had chosen the lighthouse the way Wren had chosen the road out — not from nobility, not even from fear exactly, but from some private collapse no one else ever saw.

It didn't absolve her. Nothing would. Daniel had still been hers to lose, and Juniper was still hers to raise alone, and the letter she'd sent Daniel's family — the one she'd never sent — was still unwritten. But it shifted something. It meant the story had two women in it who'd frozen, not one woman who'd fled and one who'd been left.

*Give me a brick of yours,* the letter said.

Wren looked out at the rain, at the gray harbor, at the lighthouse standing its post through the weather like it always had, and she wrote:

*Dear Neighbor,*

*A brick, then. You'll see why I don't hand these out often.*

*When I was twenty-four I did something that hurt people I loved, and instead of staying to fix it, I left. Not immediately — that's the part I can't forgive myself for, the months of pretending — but eventually. Thoroughly. I went so far away that coming back took nine years, and now I'm back, and the strange thing is that the town hasn't changed enough to forgive me and I haven't changed enough to ask it to.*

*There was a child, eventually. She's six. She thinks the lighthouse is haunted in a friendly way and asks me questions about it I can't answer honestly. She's the best thing I've ever done and the only thing I've done without flinching.*

*Here's the true part, the part I've never written down: I don't regret leaving. I regret how. I regret the silence. There's a difference, and it took me a decade to learn it, and it cost me — well. It cost me a correspondence like this one, with someone I owed better than silence.*

*Your turn again. But softer this time. We've earned a soft turn.*

*— W.*

*P.S. Maren should get a dog. Trust me on this. Every woman rebuilding a life needs something that loves her without requiring her to explain herself.*

* * *

They fell into it after that, the way ice goes out of a harbor — not breaking, just thinning, thinning, until one morning the boats go out.

Cyrus rewrote the opening of her novel for the first time in two years. She mailed Wren a page of it, unasked, and got back a critique that began *"Better. Still hiding in the weather descriptions, but better"* and ended with three sentences that made her cry into her coffee, though she'd have denied it under oath. Wren, for her part, started describing Juniper in the letters — never by name, never directly, but in pieces: a drawing of a lighthouse with too many windows, a question about whether fish sleep, a missing tooth that had been "sworn to the tooth fairy in writing." Cyrus found herself looking forward to these fragments the way she'd once looked forward to letters from publishers, back when she'd still sent manuscripts out.

*Your daughter sounds like the kind of person who will run the world by thirty and apologize to no one,* Cyrus wrote.

*She gets it from no one,* Wren wrote back. *She invented herself entirely. It's the most impressive thing I've ever witnessed.*

*What does she call the lighthouse?*

*"The tall house." She waves at it when we drive past. Every time. Like it waves back.*

Cyrus read that one in the lamp room and looked down at the coast road, at the small distance, and felt — she didn't have a word for it then. Something between being watched and being waved at.

* * *

In November, Cyrus mailed a chapter instead of a letter. Twelve pages, Maren newly awake in the story, planting things, arguing with the ghost of her grandfather, the absent man demoted to a postscript. The reply came back in four days, and Cyrus could feel the change in the weight of the envelope before she opened it — heavier, thicker, the handwriting pressing harder.

*Dear Neighbor,*

*I read it three times. Once as an editor, once as a reader, once as — never mind the third time.*

*As an editor: the grandfather is your best character and you know it. As a reader: I forgot I was supposed to be critiquing, which almost never happens to me. As the third thing: I'm proud of you, and I recognize that's a strange thing to say to a stranger, and I'm sending it anyway.*

*Finish the book. I mean it. Finish it and dedicate it to no one, or to everyone, or to me if you like — "to my pen pal, who was right about Maren." I'd frame it.*

*Winter's coming. The program runs one season, which means the clock is running, which means at some point we'll be in a room together not knowing it, or knowing it, and I find I'm — I find I'm not ready to think about that yet. So let's keep talking about fictional people. They're safer. They hold still.*

*Tell me one more thing about Maren. Tell me what she's afraid of. Not the lighthouse. Under the lighthouse.*

Cyrus sat with the question for a long time, the lamp clicking through its checks above her, the first snow squall of the season smudging the horizon.

Under the lighthouse. Under the duty, under the grief, under the wry voice she used on Odette and the whole forgiving town. What was she afraid of?

She picked up her pen.

*Dear Neighbor,*

*Maren is afraid that if she stops keeping the light, she'll find out there was never anyone to shine it for. That the duty was the excuse. That underneath the keeper there's just a woman nobody chose, and the tower was the only thing that ever pretended otherwise.*

*There. That's the soft turn. Don't make me regret it.*

*Your turn. Same question. What are YOU afraid of — not the obvious thing, not the leaving, not the past. Under it.*

She sealed it, stamped it, and walked it down to the box at the end of the lane in the blue half-light, breath smoking, gulls complaining overhead. On the walk back she realized she was smiling, and that she'd been smiling for most of a mile, and that she could not remember the last time that had happened.

Behind her, the lamp came on automatically, right on schedule, sweeping the darkening water. Ahead of her, in a house she couldn't picture, a woman she couldn't name was opening her mail.

Neither of them knew yet how few letters were left.
