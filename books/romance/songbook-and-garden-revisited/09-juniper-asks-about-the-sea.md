## 9. Juniper Asks About the Sea

The questions started on a Tuesday, over toast.

"Mum," Juniper said, with the particular gravity she reserved for matters of state, "why does the light go round and round?"

Wren didn't look up from her coffee. She'd learned in six years that if you made eye contact during a question, you were in it for the duration. "Because it's a lighthouse, love. That's what lighthouses do."

"But *why* does it go round?"

"So ships can see it from far away. It's like —" Wren searched for an analogy a six-year-old would accept. "It's like waving. A very slow wave that never stops."

Juniper considered this, chewing. Through the kitchen window of the house on Larkspur Lane — her great-aunt's house now, hers, theirs, still smelling faintly of camphor and other people's decades — the lighthouse stood on its headland like a sentence Wren had never finished reading. White tower, red gallery rail, the beam turning its patient circles even in daylight, though you couldn't see it then. She could feel it anyway. She suspected she always would.

"Who waves back?" Juniper asked.

"The boats."

"What if a boat doesn't wave back?"

"Then the lighthouse keeps waving anyway." Wren drained her coffee and stood, briskly, the way she did when a conversation was approaching water she didn't want to swim in. "Shoes on. We'll be late for school."

But Juniper, who missed nothing and filed everything, was not so easily redirected. She followed Wren to the door with her toast crust held aloft like evidence.

"You know a lot about lighthouses," she said.

"I grew up here," Wren said. "Everyone here knows about lighthouses."

"You went all stiff." Juniper pointed at her with the crust. "Your shoulders went up to your ears."

"My shoulders did not —" Wren caught herself arguing shoulder position with a first-grader and stopped. "Go put your shoes on, Juniper Rose."

Juniper went, but at the bottom of the stairs she turned around, and her face was so open, so entirely without guile, that Wren felt it like a hand pressed flat against her sternum.

"When can we go see it?" she asked. "*Close.* Not just driving past. Close-up close."

And because Wren was a coward in small daily installments, she said: "Maybe when it's warmer."

It was September. It was, by every measure Port Merrow offered, still warm.

* * *

Here is what Wren knew about lying to children, learned the hard way over nine years: they don't catch the big lies. The big lies are too large for their nets. What they catch are the small ones — the flinch, the deflection, the maybe-when-it's-warmer said in a voice pitched half a tone too high. They catch those and hold them up to the light, turning them, examining them with the terrible patience of scientists who have all the time in the world.

Juniper had been asking about the sea since before they'd moved back. In the flat in Leeds, above the dry cleaner's, she'd press her nose to the window when it rained hard and say, *is that what the ocean's like?* And Wren, folding someone else's laundry for money, would say *bigger,* and Juniper would say *how much bigger,* and Wren would say *you'll see,* and hate herself a little each time, because *you'll see* was a promise she'd been making since Juniper was three and Port Merrow was a word Wren couldn't say without her chest tightening.

Now they lived forty minutes' walk from the thing itself, and Wren had developed a whole cartography of avoidance. There was the route to school along Harbor Street that kept the water on their left and the headland behind them. There was the grocery run that went out past the church instead of along the quay. There was the beach below the point — the good beach, the sandy one — which Wren had quietly reclassified as *too windy* and replaced with the shingle cove south of town, where you couldn't see the lighthouse at all unless you climbed the rocks, and Juniper hadn't thought to climb the rocks yet.

Yet. That was the problem with children. Everything was *yet.*

"Why is it called Sterling Light?" Juniper asked one Saturday, legs swinging off the end of the pier while Wren pretended to be fascinated by the price of mackerel at the fish stall ten feet away.

"Because of the family that kept it," Wren said, to the fishmonger, who gave her a look.

"Are they still keeping it?"

"I think so."

"Did you know them?"

The mackerel looked very fresh. Wren paid for two fillets she had no plan for. "Everyone knew everyone here, back when I lived here. It's that kind of town."

"Did you know them *well?*"

"Juniper."

"That's not an answer, Mum. Miss Okafor says that's called *deflecting.*"

Miss Okafor, Wren decided, was going to receive a strongly worded note.

* * *

She should have known it would come to a head at the library, because everything in Port Merrow eventually came to a head at the library. It was less a building than the town's central nervous system.

They'd gone in for Juniper's weekly book ration — sharks, this week, always sharks, plus one picture book about a lonely tugboat that made Wren's throat tight in ways she chose not to examine — and while Juniper negotiated with the children's librarian over whether a seven-book limit was a human rights issue, Wren drifted past the front desk.

There was a corkboard there. On the corkboard was a flyer, sun-faded at the edges, with a drawing of two envelopes and a seagull wearing what appeared to be a beret.

**HARBORLIGHT ANONYMOUS PEN-PAL PROGRAM**, it read. **One season. One stranger. One letter a week. Names stay sealed until the reveal event. Ask at the desk.**

Below it, thumbtacked at a jaunty angle, was a second sheet: **SEASON'S MIDPOINT — KEEP WRITING! YOUR STRANGER IS COUNTING ON YOU.**

Wren stood in front of it longer than a person stands in front of a flyer.

She had signed up in her third week in town, in a fit of something between loneliness and penance. The librarian — a brisk, kind woman named Mrs. Adeyemi who wore cardigans like armor — had taken down Wren's details, handed her a card stock form to fill out for handwriting matching, and told her she'd be paired within the fortnight. The fortnight had passed. Then another. Wren had assumed the program had forgotten her, which had been, honestly, a relief. She had enough ghosts. She didn't need a correspondent.

Except.

Except that every Thursday for seven weeks now, an envelope had arrived at Larkspur Lane with no name on it, only a number — *Correspondent No. 14* — and handwriting that made Wren's stomach do something complicated and unwelcome. Because she knew that handwriting. She'd have known it anywhere. She'd spent one feverish spring, nine years ago, watching it cross a page across a café table, writing out wedding guest lists for a wedding that was never hers to be listed on.

Cyrus Sterling wrote her letters the way Wren remembered her doing everything: precisely, wryly, with the ink pressure of someone who meant what she said and resented having to say it.

Wren had realized it by the second letter. She had not quit. She had told herself she would quit after the third letter. Then the fifth. Then she'd written a letter about soil pH and compost ratios for a ruined kitchen garden she had no business knowing so much about, except that she'd grown up two streets from the Sterling garden and had once, briefly, been allowed inside its fence, and the knowledge had poured out of her like water finding a crack.

Her stranger was counting on her. Her stranger was Cyrus Sterling. Both of these things were true, and Wren carried them around like stones in opposite pockets, and some days she was sure they'd weigh her through the floorboards.

"Mum!" Juniper materialized at her elbow, hauling a canvas bag of shark literature. "Can we walk home the sea way?"

"There isn't a sea way—"

"Mrs. Adeyemi says there's a sea way. You go down Quay Lane and past the chandlery and you can see the whole harbor AND the lighthouse AND—" she lowered her voice to a reverent hush, "—sometimes seals."

"Seals," Wren said faintly.

"On the rocks below it. She's seen them. She has a *photograph.*"

Wren looked at her daughter — at the flushed cheeks, the bag nearly as big as she was, the absolute shining certainty that the world was full of things her mother would show her — and felt the old familiar split open down her middle: the mother who wanted to give her everything, and the woman who could not walk down Quay Lane without risking the exact encounter she'd structured her entire geography to avoid.

"Tell you what," Wren heard herself say. "Sea way today. But we're getting chips on the way, and if your hands get cold we're going straight home."

"Deal," said Juniper, who would have agreed to anything involving chips, and possibly to several things that didn't.

* * *

Quay Lane smelled of tar and salt and frying oil, and Wren walked it like a soldier crossing a field she'd been told was mined. Juniper trotted ahead, shark bag bumping, narrating the harbor as they went — *that boat's blue, Mum, that boat's REALLY blue, why are boats always blue or white, why isn't there ever a PINK boat* — and Wren answered in the automatic murmurs of maternal autopilot while her eyes did the work her feet wanted no part of: scanning, sweeping, cataloguing exits.

The chandlery. The chip shop with its queue of teenagers. The quay wall where old men sat with lines in the water catching nothing, happily. All clear. The lighthouse stood up on its headland to the north, white against a sky going soft and gold at the edges, and from here — from the flat of the harbor — it was almost bearable. Almost scenic. A postcard. Something that happened to other people.

"It's smaller than I thought," Juniper announced, disappointed.

"It's bigger than it looks. Things on headlands always are."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been closer than this."

Juniper stopped walking. Wren felt the stop before she saw it, the way you feel a change in air pressure. When she turned, her daughter was looking up at her with the expression Wren privately called *the lawyer face* — narrowed eyes, tilted head, the whole small apparatus of interrogation deployed.

"You've been *close* to it?"

"Everyone here has been close to it. It's a lighthouse, not a castle."

"Did you go *inside?*"

"Juniper—"

"Were you ever *inside the lighthouse, Mum?*"

A gull screamed somewhere, theatrically. Down the quay, the chip shop door banged open and shut, spilling vinegar-smell and laughter. Wren knelt, bringing them eye to eye, and bought herself a moment by tucking a windblown strand of hair behind Juniper's ear. Her daughter's hair was her father's color — Daniel's color, that ordinary brown that Wren had loved once with a violence that still embarrassed her — and she tried, as she always tried, not to think about that while touching it.

"When I was young," Wren said carefully, "I knew the people who lived there. And yes. I went inside. Once or twice."

"What's it like?"

"Lots of stairs. Smells like brass polish and paint. At the top there's the lamp, and it's enormous, and warm, and it hums."

"Hums how?"

"Like a cat the size of a car."

Juniper's mouth fell open. "I want to hear it."

"Well." Wren stood. Brushed off her knees. Performed, with what she hoped was convincing casualness, the act of checking the sky. "That's not really — it's a working place, love. Someone lives there. You can't just knock."

"*Why not?* People knock on doors. That's what doors are FOR."

"Not all doors."

"That's silly."

"Yes," Wren said. "It is."

They got their chips. They ate them on the quay wall with their ankles crossed, and Juniper fed an illegal quantity of them to a gull she'd named Gerald, and for twenty minutes Wren almost relaxed. This was the trick of Juniper, the mercy of her: her attention moved like weather. The lighthouse could hold her for an hour and then be displaced entirely by a dog, a tide pool, the question of whether mermaids got seasick.

But walking home, with the light going amber and long, Juniper took Wren's hand — she still did that, and Wren hoarded each instance like contraband — and said, conversationally, staring ahead:

"When I'm big, I'm going to live in a lighthouse."

"Oh?" said Wren, whose heart had begun, quietly, to hammer.

"A lighthouse or a shark boat. Probably both. And I'll let anyone visit who wants to. Even strangers." A pause, judicial. "*Especially* strangers. Miss Okafor says kindness to strangers is the whole job of being a person."

"Miss Okafor," Wren said, "sounds like she should run the country."

"She says that too."

* * *

That night, after the shark books and the tugboat book and the elaborate bedtime negotiation that ended with Wren sitting in the hallway outside Juniper's door for eleven minutes as agreed, Wren sat at the kitchen table with a blank sheet of paper and Correspondent No. 14's latest letter.

It was a good letter. They were all good letters, that was the hell of it. This one described a night spent in the lamp room during the first autumn gale — the backup power failing, the keeper's old routine resurrected by necessity, checking the mercury bath and the clockwork rotation by hand the way her grandfather had done for thirty years. *I stood up there at 3 a.m.,* Cyrus had written, *and I understood for the first time that he wasn't trapped by this place. He was holding it. There's a difference, and it took me thirty-one years and one dead fuse box to feel it.*

Wren read it twice. Then a third time, slower, letting the words do what words from strangers were allowed to do — land without history, arrive clean.

*I'm glad someone was holding it,* she wrote back, before she could stop herself. *I'm glad it was you.*

Then she sat with that sentence for a long time, because it was more than she'd meant to give, and because somewhere under it was the truest thing she knew: that she had spent nine years telling herself she'd left Port Merrow to protect everyone, when the plain ugly fact was that she'd left because staying required a courage she hadn't yet grown. That the lighthouse had been waving the whole time, patient and blind, and she was the boat that had turned away.

She took the sentence out. Put it back. Took it out again.

In the end she wrote a paragraph about her daughter instead — safe ground, or what passed for it — about a small person who had decided she would live in a lighthouse someday and let in all the strangers. *She gets this idea that doors exist to be knocked on,* Wren wrote. *I keep trying to teach her that some doors are complicated. She remains unconvinced. I envy her.*

She sealed it. Addressed it: *Correspondent No. 14, c/o Harborlight Program, Port Merrow Library.*

In the morning she'd mail it from the box by the harbor — the far box, the one on the church side, the one that didn't require passing the headland road.

From upstairs came a small voice, sleep-thick, calling down the stairwell: *"Mum? Do lighthouses dream?"*

Wren closed her eyes. Held the envelope against her chest for one breath, two.

"Everything dreams, love," she called back. "Go to sleep."

And in the dark of the hallway, listening to her daughter settle, Wren admitted the thing she'd been circling for weeks: that sooner or later — at the reveal event, at the hardware store, at some ordinary crosswalk where the town's small geography finally ran out of room — the distance she'd been walking was going to close. And she had no idea whether she'd be ready, or whether she'd simply be standing there, empty-handed, when her daughter finally got her wish and knocked on the door.
