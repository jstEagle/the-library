## 10. The Storm Season Opens

The gale came in on a Tuesday, the way the bad ones always did in Port Merrow — unannounced by any forecast worth trusting, announced instead by the gulls, who went silent and inland all at once, like a congregation called home.

I was in the garden when I felt it coming. That's the thing about a keeper's granddaughter: you don't check the weather app. You feel the pressure drop behind your teeth, you notice the light going brass-colored on the water, and you start moving before your brain has finished the sentence *storm*. I'd spent the afternoon pulling dead tomato vines, and I stood there with my hands full of rot and looked out at a sea that had gone the color of gunmetal, and I said, out loud, to nobody, "Well. Here we go."

The lighthouse is automated now. Has been for eleven years. The Coast Guard installed a system that monitors itself, corrects itself, files its own reports, and needs me about as much as a houseplant needs a eulogy. My grandfather kept this light by hand for thirty-eight years — climbing those stairs four times a night in every weather God saw fit to throw at the coast, winding the clockwork, trimming the wick, polishing the lens until it threw a beam clean across six miles of black water. He died in the lamp room, they say, though I've never confirmed it because I don't want the details. I want the version where he simply became part of the mechanism.

That Tuesday night, the mechanism failed.

* * *

It started at 9:40 p.m. with a sound I can only describe as a large animal clearing its throat somewhere inside the tower. Then the backup generator kicked on, ran for ninety seconds, and died with a smell of hot copper that drifted down the stairwell like a warning. The monitoring panel lit up red in three places. I stood in the base of the tower in my rain jacket, holding a flashlight, reading error codes my grandfather would have understood instantly and which meant nothing to me except: *the light will go dark at 10:15 when the battery reserve drains.*

I called the Coast Guard station. The line was down — lines go down in gales here; the whole peninsula's infrastructure is held together with salt air and stubbornness. I called Odette. She answered on the fourth ring, shouting over wind.

"Cyrus? Are you okay?"

"The backup's dead. The main power's flickering. If the reserve drains—"

"Then keep the light on yourself." No hesitation. That's Odette. "You know how. You've watched the tapes, you've read his logs. Go up there and be your grandfather."

"I'm not—"

"You are. Go."

So I went.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about climbing a lighthouse stairwell in a gale: the tower moves. Not much — an inch, maybe two, a slow lean and return, like something enormous breathing. My grandfather's logs mentioned it once, offhandedly, the way he mentioned everything, in that dry ledger hand: *Tower swaying. Normal. Wind NW 60 gusting 75.* Reading it, I'd always assumed he was being stoic for posterity. Standing on step eighty of one hundred and twelve, feeling the whole structure flex around me like a living thing deciding whether to hold me or not, I understood he'd just been taking notes.

The lamp room door fought me like it had opinions. I got it open, got inside, slammed it shut, and stood in the round glass room at the top of the world while the storm tried to take the roof off.

The lens is still there — the old Fresnel, retired from service but never removed, because removing it would have required a crane and a permit and a reason, and nobody ever found one. It sits in the center of the room like a beehive made of crystal, six feet tall, catching even the flashlight's weak beam and throwing back little galaxies. Beside it, the modern beacon sat dark, its battery indicator bleeding toward empty.

There was a cabinet against the wall. Grandfather's cabinet. I'd kept it locked for years the way you keep a shrine locked — not from reverence exactly, but from the suspicion that opening it might hurt. That night I didn't have time for reverence. I broke the small padlock with a wrench and found:

Lamps. Actual oil lamps, three of them, their reservoirs full — he'd left them full, the way you leave a house ready for guests who might arrive at any moment, even after you're gone. Wicks in wax paper. A brass funnel. Matches in a tin, rusted shut around the edges but sealing dryness inside. And a laminated card, yellowed at the corners, in his handwriting:

*Emergency illumination procedure. Read twice. Do once. Panic never helped anyone.*

Underneath, smaller: *Cyrus — if you ever read this, mind the third step from the top. It lies.*

I laughed. Alone at the top of a lighthouse in a seventy-knot gale, I laughed until my eyes stung, and then I did what the card said. Read twice. Did once.

* * *

I won't pretend it was easy. The first lamp wouldn't draw. The second flared and guttered. The third caught on my fourth match and settled into a steady flame, and I carried it to the lens with both hands, walking like a woman carrying water across ice, and set it inside the optic where the old rotation table used to sit. The lens took the flame and multiplied it — that's the whole genius of a Fresnel, rings upon rings of glass bending one small fire into a beam that could reach a ship six miles out — and suddenly the room was full of turning light, because the storm wind came through some gap I couldn't find and moved the lens's old shutter vane, and the beam swept, irregularly, drunkenly, but it swept.

Not regulation. Not automated. Not correct by any modern standard.

But visible. From the water, if anything was out there, visible.

I sat down on the floor with my back against the lens housing and my grandfather's logbook open on my knees, and I kept watch. Every fifteen minutes I checked the flame. Every hour I wrote in the log, because that's what you do — that's the whole covenant. *2240: Backup generator failure. Main power intermittent. Emergency illumination established, oil lamp, non-standard rotation. Visibility approx. 1/4 mile and worsening. Keeper on watch.*

*Keeper on watch.* I wrote it and then stared at it for a long time. Eleven years I'd been the caretaker, the custodian, the woman-who-lives-there. Somewhere between the second lamp failure and the third match, I'd become the keeper, and I hadn't noticed the promotion.

The storm peaked around two in the morning. The tower leaned and groaned. Rain hit the lantern glass sideways and the sea below was a churning white chaos, and once — just once — I thought I saw a running light out there, far off, a fishing boat beating for harbor, and I pressed my palms flat against the cold lens housing and whispered, *come on, come on, see me,* like the light was mine, like the boat could hear me, like my grandfather was standing behind me with his hand on my shoulder saying *steady, girl.*

The light went past the point around four. Or maybe it never needed me at all. Maybe it was the Coast Guard cutter, maybe it was nothing. But I watched until the horizon went gray, and then rose-pink, and then ordinary, and the storm blew itself out against the dawn like a fist unclenching, and I climbed down one hundred and twelve steps on legs made of wet rope and slept four hours in my grandfather's chair with the logbook still in my lap.

* * *

Three days later, my hands had stopped shaking and the electricians had come and gone, muttering about surge protectors and coastal corrosion, and I sat down at my kitchen table and wrote to my pen pal.

I hadn't planned to write about the storm. Our correspondence had settled into something like a chess game conducted in prose — she needled, I parried, we discussed soil pH and fictional heroines and never, ever touched anything real. But I sat down to write something deflecting and funny about the electrician's mustache, and instead this came out:

*I don't know why I'm telling you this. Maybe because you're the only person I can tell it to precisely because you don't know me. Last week the lighthouse's systems failed in a gale and I climbed the tower and kept the light by hand, with oil lamps, the way my grandfather did for thirty-eight years. I found his emergency instructions taped inside a locked cabinet, addressed to me, written years before I ever needed them. There was a joke in it. About a lying stair. He'd been dead eleven years and he made me laugh at two in the morning at the top of a tower in a hurricane, and I sat down afterward and wrote 'keeper on watch' in his logbook and cried into my rain jacket like an idiot.*

*Here's the part I actually want to say: I've spent eleven years thinking I was keeping his lighthouse. Standing in that room, I realized I was keeping his watch. There's a difference. One is a job. The other is a promise you make to someone who can't hear you anymore. And I don't know if I've been faithful to it or hiding behind it. Both, probably. Probably both is the honest answer, and I hate that I only figured that out at the top of a tower in a storm, alone, talking to a dead man.*

*You'll probably say something cutting about sentimentality. Go ahead. I can take it. I've been taking things for eleven years.*

I sealed it before I could regret it. Mailed it at the harbor box at dawn, when the post-storm light was doing that washed-clean thing it does, everything rinsed and new, and I walked home feeling strangely lighter, the way you do after you've told a stranger something true.

* * *

Her reply came nine days later. Nine days — long enough that I'd convinced myself I'd finally crossed whatever invisible line existed in our arrangement, that I'd been too much, too raw, too real, and she'd do the polite thing and let the season lapse.

Instead I found a thick envelope, heavier than usual, and inside, several pages in that handwriting I'd stopped trying to place and started, privately, to love — a slanted, urgent script, like someone writing fast before they lose their nerve.

*Cyrus — (no, wait, we're not supposed to use names, forgive me, I nearly wrote yours) —*

*Friend. Let me call you friend, since that's apparently what you are now, though neither of us signed up for it.*

*I read your letter four times. I'm not going to say something cutting about sentimentality. I'm going to say something worse: I read your letter four times and on the fourth reading I cried, sitting in my car outside the hardware store, and a man asked me if I needed help and I said no and he gave me a strange look and drove away, and I sat there crying about a woman I've never met keeping a light for a dead man.*

*Here's what I think, and you can throw this back at me the way you throw everything: I think you weren't talking to a dead man at all. I think you were talking to yourself. I think you've been standing in that tower for eleven years asking someone's permission to live your own life, and the storm knocked the electricity out and left you alone with the question, and the answer was in his handwriting the whole time: read twice, do once, panic never helped anyone. He wasn't leaving you instructions for emergencies. He was leaving you permission. Keepers don't wait for the light to be given to them. They climb the stairs.*

*I don't know if you've been faithful to the promise or hiding behind it. But I know this: a person who was only hiding wouldn't have climbed. A person who was only hiding would have let the light go dark and filed a report Monday morning. You climbed one hundred and twelve steps in a gale because something in you believes the promise matters more than the fear. Whatever else is tangled up in you — and something is, I can feel it through the paper, we're both carrying things we haven't said — that part of you isn't broken. Don't let anyone tell you it is. Including you.*

*P.S. Your grandfather sounds like he was a good man. The kind who fixed things before they broke and left the lamps full. The world doesn't make many of them. Hold onto the cabinet. Break the lock again if you have to.*

*P.P.S. I checked. Third step from the top. In my experience it's usually the people closest to us who lie.*

I read it standing in the doorway, mail still in hand, the wind pushing at my back like it wanted to read over my shoulder. Then I read it again. Then I carried it inside, sat down at the kitchen table, and did something I hadn't done since the program started: I put her letter in the drawer with my grandfather's logbooks. Not the letter drawer. The other one. The one that mattered.

Because here's the thing I couldn't explain to Odette, couldn't explain to myself, could barely hold onto as a thought without it slipping away like bait off a hook: a stranger had just described the exact shape of my life — the guilt, the duty, the hiding, the permission — without knowing anything about me. As if she'd read the manuscript of me and marked up the margins in red ink, and every correction was right.

And underneath the gratitude, underneath the strange loosening in my chest, there was a colder thought, small and sharp as a fishhook, that I turned over once and then deliberately swallowed back down:

*How does she know?*

How does a stranger know that something is tangled up in me? How does she write *we're both carrying things we haven't said* like she's counted the weight herself?

I told myself it was empathy. Good writers recognize patterns; she was clearly a reader, a sharp one, the kind who sees the bones under the skin of a story. I told myself the familiarity I sometimes felt in her handwriting — that slanted, urgent script, like someone I'd once watched write a grocery list across a kitchen table — was coincidence, the way all handwriting looks a little like all handwriting.

I told myself that, and I almost believed it.

Then I took out a fresh sheet of paper, and instead of answering her letter, I wrote at the top of the page, in letters I immediately crossed out: *Do I know you?*

And beneath it, after a long time, I wrote: *Thank you. Tell me something true about yourself, and I'll tell you something true about me.*

I sealed it, and walked it down to the harbor box, and stood there a moment too long with my hand on the slot, the way you stand at the edge of cold water.
