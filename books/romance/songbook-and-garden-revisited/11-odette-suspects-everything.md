## 11. Odette Suspects Everything

The trouble started, as most of my troubles did, at the Harborlight Diner on a Tuesday.

I'd come in for coffee and a cinnamon roll I would later describe to my pen pal as "a structural failure in pastry form," and Odette was already in my usual booth, which was the first clue that this was not an accident. Odette Marchand did not do accidents. She did operations. She had once organized my grandfather's funeral like a military campaign and gotten three separate people to cry on schedule.

"You're late," she said, sliding the syrup toward me without being asked.

"I'm exactly on time. You're early."

"I'm always early. It's how I catch people." She watched me sit down, and her eyes did the thing — narrowed slightly, head tilted two degrees, like a heron deciding whether the fish was worth the strike. "You're humming."

"I am not humming."

"You were. Something with a lot of minor chords. You never hum. You complain in rhythm at best."

I picked up the menu I didn't need. "It's a diner, Odette. There's ambient noise. Maybe the ambient noise was humming."

"The ambient noise is Gus's radio playing the same four songs from 1987." She leaned back, arms crossed, and let the silence do its work. This was another Odette technique: she could out-silence anyone in Port Merrow, including the ocean. I lasted eleven seconds.

"Fine. I slept well. Is that a crime?"

"Slept well." She said it the way a coroner says a suspicious phrase. "You slept well. And last Thursday you laughed. Out loud. In the library. In the *local history section*, where laughter goes to die. Marjorie told me herself. She said you laughed so hard you knocked over the microfilm."

"That was a sneeze."

"A sneeze with a punchline."

I ate my cinnamon roll. It was, indeed, structurally compromised. Odette watched me chew with the patience of a woman who had known me since we were nine years old and had once gotten me to confess to breaking the school window by simply standing next to me for forty minutes.

"New novel's going well?" she asked, deceptively casual.

"Same as ever."

"Same as ever meaning stalled, or same as ever meaning you've written forty pages in three weeks?"

The cinnamon roll turned to cement in my throat. Because the truth — the truth I hadn't said out loud to anyone, hadn't even fully admitted to myself — was that I'd written more in the past month than I had in the past two years. Not the novel, exactly. Letters. Pages and pages of letters to a stranger whose handwriting slanted left like it was leaning away from its own secrets, who had once called my description of a storm "technically accurate and emotionally illiterate" and then, three letters later, written me something about her grandmother's kitchen that I'd read four times and then put face-down on the table like it was too bright to look at.

"It's going," I said.

"*Going.*" Odette repeated the word like she was testing a floorboard for rot. "Cyrus Sterling. Let me tell you what I know, and you can stop me when I'm wrong."

"This should be quick."

"I volunteer at the library Tuesdays and Fridays. I see the pen-pal program's outgoing mail. I don't read it—" she held up a hand, genuinely offended at the suggestion, which was almost funny given everything else she was capable of, "—but I see it. Volume. Postmarks. Handwriting, if someone leaves an envelope face-up, which people do, because people are animals." She took a sip of her coffee. "Since October, there's been one participant whose letters go out regular as tide charts. Thick envelopes. Good paper. The kind of person who owns a letter opener."

"Sounds like a menace."

"And this week—" she leaned forward, and I swear the whole diner got quieter around her, or maybe that was just my pulse, "—that same participant came in wearing a sweater I have never seen before. A *nice* sweater. Soft-looking. The color of, and I'm being precise here, hope."

"What color is hope?"

"Moss green. Obviously." She spread her hands. "And here's the thing, Cyrus. That participant used to mail her letters looking like she was dropping off evidence. Shoulders up, sunglasses in October, in and out in ninety seconds. Now she lingers. She talked to Marjorie about stamps. *Stamps,* Cyrus. Nobody talks about stamps unless they're happy or deranged."

"Or both."

"Or both." Odette's eyes glittered. "So. My question is simple. My question has been simple for two weeks. Who is writing you?"

The diner noise rushed back in. Somewhere behind the counter, Gus's radio promised love was a battlefield, which felt editorial.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said, and heard my own voice come out too fast, too smooth, the verbal equivalent of shoving all the laundry into a closet before company arrives.

Odette smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of a woman watching a lock turn under a key she'd been carrying for weeks.

"*Too* fast," she said softly. "That's how I know."

* * *

Here is what I will say in my own defense: I panicked.

Not the useful kind of panic, the kind that gets you up a lighthouse stairs in a gale with your heart going like a drum line. The other kind. The small, stupid, human kind where your best friend asks a direct question and every answer in your head is a lie, so you pick the fastest lie available, and then you have to live inside it.

Because here was the problem, the one I'd been circling for weeks like a moth around a bulb: I didn't know who my pen pal was. But I knew things. Small things. A phrase here, a cadence there. She wrote "anyway" at the ends of paragraphs like she was physically turning away from her own feelings. She used semicolons correctly, which in Port Merrow narrowed the field to roughly four people, two of whom were dead. She had mentioned, in passing, a hatred of the smell of turpentine; a memory of a garden with a crooked fence; a sister who was actually a cousin but "functioned as a sister, which is worse, because you can't unfriend family."

And underneath all of it, under the words like a current under a boat, was something familiar I refused to name. The way she argued. The way she'd concede a point mid-argument and then reattack from a new angle. I'd told myself it was coincidence. Everyone argues like that. People are made of clichés.

But Odette sat across from me with her forensic smile, and the thought I'd been keeping in the cellar of my skull came upstairs and stood in the light:

*What if it's her?*

Wren Calloway. Nine years gone. Nine years of a name I only said out loud when I had to, usually into the wind off the gallery rail where nobody could hear me over the gulls. If Wren Calloway walked into the library every week and mailed me letters in good paper with correct semicolons — if she'd been sitting across some invisible table from me all season, trading confidences while I complained about my garden to the woman who burned my life down —

"Cyrus." Odette snapped her fingers. "You went somewhere. Your face did a thing."

"My face doesn't do things."

"Your face is the most legible document in this town. Your face is practically large print." She flagged Gus for refills without looking at him, which was its own kind of power. "Let me make this easy. I'm not asking you to unmask your correspondent. Program rules are program rules; Marjorie would have my head, and frankly the anonymity is half the fun. I'm asking a simpler question." She held up one finger. "Is it someone local?"

I looked at my coffee.

"Is it someone you know?"

I looked at the window, where the harbor lay flat and silver and completely unhelpful.

"Is it," Odette said, and now her voice dropped into the register she saved for emergencies and really good gossip, indistinguishable, "*someone you used to know?*"

"No," I said.

Too fast again. God. I was a broken metronome.

Odette sat back slowly, and I watched her assemble it behind her eyes — not the answer, she didn't have the answer, but the shape of the answer, the negative space of it. She'd known Daniel. She'd been at the engagement party. She'd helped me carry chairs afterward, and neither of us had said his name or hers the entire afternoon, which in Odette's world was a signed confession.

"Huh," she said.

"Don't 'huh' me."

"I'm just saying huh. Huh is free." She stirred sugar into her coffee she didn't need sweetened, buying time, thinking. "You know what's interesting? You didn't ask who I think it is. Most people would ask. Most people would be dying to hear my theory."

"I don't want your theory."

"Because you already have one." She pointed her spoon at me like a tiny silver accusation. "You have a theory and the theory scares you, and that's why you're lying badly in a public diner instead of laughing at me."

"I laugh at you constantly."

"Only when I'm wrong." She drank her coffee, watching me over the rim. "Okay. New offer. I drop it. Completely. I won't guess, I won't investigate, I won't cross-reference postmarks with grocery store receipts like some kind of postal detective."

"That's a very specific thing to promise not to do."

"Isn't it though." She set down the cup. "On one condition."

"There it is."

"You finish the novel." Her voice went gentle, which was worse than the interrogation, somehow. Gentleness from Odette meant she'd seen all the way through you and decided to be kind about it, which was its own form of terrifying intimacy. "Whatever's unstuck you — letters, hope-colored sweaters, whatever — it's working. I haven't seen you write like this since before Daniel. So I'll stay out of your mailbox if you stay at your desk. Deal?"

Outside, a fishing boat came in slow against the tide, and I thought about my pen pal's last letter, the one where she'd written: *You talk about duty like it's a room you can't leave. Some rooms are just doors you haven't opened.*

"Deal," I said.

We shook on it. Odette Marchand's handshake had ended stronger negotiations than mine.

* * *

I walked home along the seawall because the road felt too full of witnesses. The light was doing that late-autumn thing, low and gold and forgiving of nothing, and the lighthouse stood up ahead white against it, my grandfather's tower, my inheritance and my alibi.

My alibi. That was the ugly word that surfaced as I walked. For nine years I'd had a perfect story: the girl who stayed, the girl who was left, the keeper's granddaughter who gave up the city and the book deal and the man for the light. Everyone in town believed it. I'd built my whole personality on it, the way you build a house on a foundation, and I'd never once checked the foundation.

And now some anonymous stranger with good semicolons was writing me things like *some rooms are just doors* and making me want to check.

At the top of the lane I stopped at my mailbox out of habit. Empty, except for a flyer for the winter fair. But standing there, hand on the cold metal, I let myself actually consider it — properly, for the first time, without flinching away.

If it was Wren. If Wren Calloway had come back to Port Merrow — and Odette would know, Odette knew everyone's arrivals before their moving trucks did — and signed up for a seasonal letter exchange, and been matched, by clerical error or cosmic joke, with *me* —

Then either she didn't know it was me. Or she did.

And I honestly couldn't decide which possibility frightened me more. Because one of them meant I'd been pouring myself out, letter after letter, into the ears of the woman who broke my brother's heart and wrecked mine by collateral damage. And the other meant she'd known all along — known whose garden advice she was giving, whose storms she was soothing, whose unfinished heroine she was editing — and she'd kept writing anyway.

I stood there until my fingers went numb on the mailbox flag.

Then I went inside, sat down at my desk, and wrote her back. Because that was the thing I couldn't stop doing, the thing that scared me most of all: whatever the truth was, whoever she was, I would rather keep the letters than be safe from them.

Halfway through the second page, telling her about Odette and the diner and the hope-colored sweater (carefully, no names, no details — though even as I disguised it I understood I was handing her the story of our own conversation), I paused over a sentence and realized my hand had written something I hadn't planned:

*Sometimes I think you might be someone I already know. I've decided I don't want to check. Is that cowardice or faith?*

I looked at it for a long time.

Then, because I was apparently braver on paper than in diners, I left it in.

Three days later her reply came, and the first line stopped my breath where I stood in the cold by the box:

*I've been afraid of the same thing. Ask me at the reveal, and I'll tell you the truth — if you still want it then.*

She'd underlined *then* twice.

And beneath the signature — unsigned, as always, just a small drawn wren, the bird, inked quick and sure — there was a smudge of green on the corner of the page. Moss green. The exact color of hope, or of paint, fresh from a can, carried home from the hardware store in someone's careful hands.
