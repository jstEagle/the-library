## 12. Paint and Proximity

The hardware store on Harbor Street still smelled the same — sawdust and linseed oil and something faintly metallic, like coins left in rain. Wren stood in the doorway for a moment longer than a person buying paint needed to, letting the smell do what smells do: drag nine years out of her chest and lay them on the counter.

She'd been avoiding this store. That was the truth of it. She'd driven forty minutes to the big-box place outside Millbrook twice already, paying too much for paint that wasn't even the right color, because the alternative was walking into a building where someone might say *Wren? Wren Calloway?* in that particular tone towns reserve for women who leave.

But the trim needed a second coat by Sunday, Juniper had school pictures Monday, and the box store's "weathered white" was really just white with a lie in it. So here she was, 9:40 on a Thursday morning, when the sensible people were at work and the store would be empty except for Gus Ferro behind the counter, who was seventy if he was a day and had probably forgotten her name along with most of the twentieth century.

Gus was not at the counter.

Cyrus Sterling was.

Wren knew it before her brain finished assembling the facts — the dark hair pulled back carelessly, the ink stain on the side of one hand, the way she stood with her weight on her heels like a woman perpetually about to step backward. Cyrus was writing something in a small notebook, frowning at it, and she looked — this was the obscene part — she looked *good*. Older, sharper at the jaw, but with the same stubborn set to her mouth that Wren remembered from church picnics and graduation parties and one terrible night in the Sterling kitchen that she had spent nine years trying to sand down to nothing.

*Turn around,* Wren told herself. *Walk out. Come back Saturday.*

But Cyrus looked up, and there was nowhere to go. The bell over the door had already announced her. Running now would be its own confession.

"Can I help you find—" Cyrus said, and then stopped, because recognition moved across her face like weather. Not a flinch. Worse than a flinch. A kind of careful blankness, the face of a woman deciding in real time how much of herself to show.

"Hi," Wren said. Her voice came out steady, which felt like a betrayal of everything happening inside her ribcage. "I need exterior paint. Trim work."

A beat. Two. The fluorescent lights hummed. Somewhere in the back a compressor cycled on with a shudder.

"Cylinder B or the porch enamel," Cyrus said finally, as if reading off an internal card. "For salt air you want the enamel. It holds up better."

"Enamel, then." Wren stepped forward, and the floorboard near the door gave its familiar groan, and absurdly that groan nearly undid her — because it meant the store hadn't changed, which meant *she* hadn't changed, which meant the last nine years might be negotiable after all, and that was a thought she could not afford.

Cyrus came around the counter without looking at her directly, the way you'd approach a dog of unknown temperament. She walked to the paint wall and pulled a fan deck, flipping through it with brisk competence.

"What color?"

"Off-white. Warm, not yellow."

"Cloud Cover." Cyrus held up a card. "Or Antique Lace, if you want people to think you tried harder."

There it was. That dryness. Wren had forgotten — no, she hadn't forgotten, she'd *buried* — how funny Cyrus was, in that flat way that took a second to land. Nine years ago the humor had been aimed elsewhere, mostly at Daniel, mostly with love. Now it was aimed at Wren like a small, well-thrown stone.

"Cloud Cover," Wren said. "One gallon should do the north side."

"How much trim are we talking?"

"The whole house, eventually. I'm doing it in sections."

Cyrus wrote on a slip of paper. Her handwriting — Wren caught it sideways, quick and slanted, letters leaning forward like they were trying to get somewhere faster than their writer. Something cold slid down Wren's spine. She'd seen that handwriting recently. She'd seen it every week for two months, arriving in envelopes with a Merrow Point postmark, full of complaints about gulls and soil pH and a fictional heroine named Iris who couldn't finish anything she started.

*No,* Wren thought. *It can't be. The world isn't that small.*

Except the world was exactly that small. The world was a hardware store on Harbor Street where the only person minding the counter on a Thursday morning was the woman whose life she'd broken, who was also — possibly, probably, *God* — the stranger she'd been confessing to in increasingly unstrangerly letters.

"House on Larkspur Lane?" Cyrus asked, still writing. "The Aldous place?"

"Yes." Wren blinked. "How did you—"

"It's the only house on Larkspur with a tarp on it." Cyrus tore off the slip. "People noticed when you moved in. Small town. We notice things. It's our whole economy."

"I remember."

"Do you." Not a question, really. A door held open exactly wide enough to see whether Wren would walk through it.

Wren looked at the fan deck instead. "Nine years is a long time," she said. "You forget things."

"Some things." Cyrus set the slip on the counter and rang it up with more force than a gallon of paint required. "Thirty-eight fifty."

Wren paid in cash, counting out bills with hands that wanted very much to shake and did not. This was a skill she'd perfected — the still surface, the level voice. She'd used it in hospital waiting rooms and DMV lines and the day she told Daniel she was leaving, and she used it now while Cyrus bagged the can with the efficiency of someone who wanted the transaction over.

"You're keeping the garden?" Cyrus asked suddenly, as Wren reached for the bag.

Wren froze. "What?"

"The Aldous garden. Old Mrs. Aldous kept herbs along the south wall. If you're doing the house in sections, the garden's probably a disaster by now." A pause, so brief it might have been nothing. "My pen pal's been telling me all about ruined gardens lately. Seems to be the season for them."

The words went through Wren like a fishhook finding purchase. *Pen pal.* Testing. Or not testing. Or just talking, the way people talk, the way strangers talk about the library program that half the town was in because Odette Marchand had bullied the entire population into it.

"I wouldn't know anything about that," Wren said, and heard how it sounded — too fast, too flat — and hated herself for it.

"No," Cyrus agreed pleasantly. "Of course not."

They stood there. The compressor shut off. In the silence Wren could hear the harbor itself, faint through the walls, the water doing what water does whether anyone watches or not.

"You look well," Cyrus said, and it cost her something; Wren could see the cost in the muscle that jumped in her jaw. "That's — good. That you're well."

"You look well too." *Devastating*, Wren thought. *We are devastating. Two grown women discussing wellness over a gallon of paint.* "Is your family — are they—"

"My mother's fine. Daniel's in Boston." Cyrus's voice did something complicated on the name. "He doesn't come home much."

"Oh." Wren picked up the paint can. It weighed nothing and everything. "Well. Thank you for the — for the help with the paint."

"Enamel," Cyrus said. "Two coats. Don't skip the primer on the north side, the salt gets under it."

"I won't."

And then, because the universe was cruel and also apparently fond of theater, the bell over the door rang and Juniper came barreling in from the sidewalk where Wren had left her drawing chalk on the boards, six years old and fearless, trailing a stick of pink chalk and a question mark of hair.

"Mum! There's a dog outside and he's *enormous*—"

She stopped dead when she saw them. Children have radar for atmospheres; Juniper read the room in one sweep and planted herself at her mother's hip, staring up at Cyrus with open, unembarrassed curiosity.

"Hello," Juniper said.

"Hello," Cyrus said. Her voice changed — softened, some gear shifting down. "Are you the painter's helper?"

"I'm the *foreman*," Juniper said with dignity. "Mum does what I say."

"That's the correct arrangement." And Cyrus smiled — actually smiled, tired and real around the edges — and Wren watched it happen and felt something crack along a seam she hadn't known was load-bearing. Because Cyrus liked children. Because Cyrus had always wanted children, had said so once, years ago, at a picnic table, in a conversation Wren had eavesdropped on and never forgotten. Because this woman was being kind to her daughter with no idea — *no confirmed idea* — what Wren had taken from her.

"We should go," Wren said. Her hand found Juniper's shoulder. "Thank you again."

"Mind the primer," Cyrus said.

Wren got them both out the door and three shops down before her legs remembered how to feel like legs. She sat on a bench with Juniper and breathed and did not cry, because Juniper was watching her with those eyes that missed nothing.

"Mum, who was that lady?"

"A friend of the family. From a long time ago."

"She seemed sad."

"She's not sad, sweetheart."

"She seemed like she was trying not to be sad. Like Mrs. Petrelli when Grandpa died."

Wren looked down the street toward the hardware store, where through the window a figure in dark hair was standing very still behind the counter, notebook forgotten, watching the door.

"Junie," she said. "Do you remember the letters I write? The ones I mail by the harbor?"

"The ones to your secret friend?"

"They're not secret, exactly. Just — private."

Juniper considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "Is the sad lady your private friend?"

The world tilted slightly on its axis. "Why would you ask that?"

"'Cause you made your face do the thing." Juniper demonstrated: eyebrows up, mouth carefully neutral, the exact expression Wren had worn in the store. "You always make your face do the thing when you're hiding something. You did it when we got the cat."

"We don't have a cat."

"Exactly."

Wren laughed — a short, startled sound that turned into something dangerously close to a sob halfway out, which she converted, mid-breath, into a cough. Six years old. God help her.

"Come on, foreman," she said, standing. "We've got trim to paint."

* * *

That night, after Juniper was asleep, Wren sat at the kitchen table with the harvest of the day spread out before her: a receipt for paint, a stack of her own unsent drafts, and the letter that had come that morning, which she'd shoved in a drawer unread because she'd known — she'd *known* since the store — and couldn't bear to open it yet.

Now she opened it.

*Iris has decided to tear down the greenhouse,* Cyrus's handwriting said, leaning forward urgently off the page. *She says it's rotten and beyond saving, but I think she's lying to herself. I think she wants permission to stop restoring things. Do you ever feel that way? Like it would be easier to let a place fall down around you than to keep fixing it and admitting you live inside the ruin?*

Wren put the letter down. Pressed both palms flat against the table until the wood bit back.

*It's her,* she thought. *It's been her the whole time.*

She should quit the program. That was the sane move — write one last letter, claim the season ended early, disappear from the correspondence the way she'd disappeared from everything else. The rules allowed withdrawal. Odette Marchand had a whole form for it, probably color-coded.

Instead Wren found a pen.

*Dear friend,* she wrote. *No. I don't think Iris wants permission to stop. I think she wants someone to tell her the greenhouse is worth saving even broken — especially broken. There's a difference between giving up and putting down a burden you were never supposed to carry alone.*

She stopped. Read it back. Heard Cyrus's voice in the store — *my pen pal's been telling me all about ruined gardens* — and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that Cyrus didn't know either. That they had sat across a counter from each other, two women holding the same secret from opposite ends, each waiting for the other to flinch first.

Neither had.

Which meant tomorrow she could mail this letter, and next week another would come, and the whole ridiculous machinery would keep turning, and at the end of the season there would be a reveal event in the library with folding chairs and a punch bowl, and Cyrus Sterling would turn around and find Wren Calloway standing there holding every letter.

Or she could tell her now. Tonight. Walk down to the lighthouse in the dark and knock on the door and say *it's me, it was always me, I'm sorry, I've been sorry since before I knew what I was sorry for.*

Wren looked at the letter. At the pen. At the window, where far out on the point the lighthouse beam swept its slow circle, patient as breathing, lighting the water and the rocks alike without asking either of them to be anything other than what they were.

She sealed the envelope instead.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow I'll tell her.

She was already learning, again, how easily tomorrow became a country she never arrived in.
