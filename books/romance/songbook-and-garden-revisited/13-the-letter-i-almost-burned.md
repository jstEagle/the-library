## 13. The Letter I Almost Burned

The paint can sat unopened on Wren's kitchen table for two days.

She'd bought it — "Sea Glass," the swatch card said, a green so pale it was barely a color at all — and then she'd driven home with her hands at ten and two and her heart going like a trapped bird, and she'd set the can down and hadn't touched it since. Every time she looked at it she saw the hardware store again. Cyrus Sterling's face doing that terrible polite rearranging. The way her eyes had gone to Wren's left hand, then away, then to the shelf of wood screws as if wood screws were the most fascinating subject in the world.

*Small town,* Cyrus had said. *You'll be back before you know it.*

*That's the idea,* Wren had said, and had wanted to die.

Juniper was at school. The house was quiet in the way old houses are quiet — not silent but murmuring, the radiators ticking, the sea audible through the walls like someone breathing in another room. Wren sat at the table with her second cup of coffee going cold and thought about the letter in her cardigan pocket.

She'd taken it out four times since Tuesday. She'd taken it out now, a fifth time, and unfolded it on the table beside the paint can, smoothing the creases flat with her palm like she was calming a small animal.

*Dear Harborlight,* it began. *I've been thinking about what you said about your garden, and about your grandfather, and I have to tell you something, and I need you to hear it before you decide I'm someone worth writing to.*

It was the letter she'd been not-writing for six weeks. Every letter she'd sent had been a negotiation with this one — circling it, feeding it pieces of herself small enough to swallow, keeping the center dark. And now she knew. She knew who was on the other end of it. She'd stood in a hardware store aisle and heard Cyrus Sterling say *the keeper's granddaughter* in that flat, careful voice and felt the whole architecture of her anonymity come down like a bad chimney.

Her pen pal was Cyrus. Her pen pal had been Cyrus since the first stiff, courteous page.

She had written to the woman whose engagement she'd ended, and the woman had written back.

Wren got up, dumped the cold coffee in the sink, and stood with her hands braced on the counter looking out at the yard, where the previous owner's rose bushes had gone feral and leggy, blooming anyway, the way desperate things do.

Here was the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for nine years. Daniel had been twenty-six and already tired of her, though neither of them had named it. She had been twenty-four and so in love with being loved that she'd mistaken the temperature of the room for the season. And Cyrus — Cyrus had been the one who paid for all of it. Cyrus had stood in her grandfather's kitchen the week after it ended, white as milk, and said, *It's fine. It's fine, Wren, honestly,* in the voice people use when they are bleeding and don't want to ruin the floor.

Wren had left town eleven weeks later with a positive test in her jacket pocket and Daniel's mother's voice in her ears — *this family has expectations, Wren, and you were never going to meet them* — and she had told herself for nine years that leaving was mercy. That a Sterling baby growing in a Calloway belly would have been a bomb with a nine-month fuse. That she'd spared everyone.

She had spared herself. That was the true entry in the ledger. She'd spared herself the look on Daniel's face, the look on Cyrus's face, the slow daily proof that she'd traded one kind of ruin for another. She'd driven until the ocean was behind her and started a life in a city where nobody knew her name meant anything, and she'd raised a daughter who thought her mother had simply always been from somewhere else.

*You wronged someone,* she'd written in the letter. *Someone good. And I have spent nine years being the person who did it, and I came back here because I was tired, and I don't know how to be sorry in a way that helps anyone.*

She read it standing up, from a distance, the way you read a diagnosis.

Then she folded it, put it back in her cardigan pocket, and went to find the matches.

* * *

The stove in the old house was gas, which was a problem for her plan, so she took the matches out to the yard, to the fire pit the previous owner had left half-full of wet leaves. It was drizzling, a fine harbor drizzle that didn't so much fall as hang. Wren crouched by the pit with the letter in one hand and the matches in the other and found that she couldn't do it.

Not because she was noble. Because she was a coward in a new direction.

Burning it meant the confession stayed hers. Burning it meant she got to keep being the only one who knew the whole shape of her, which was, she understood suddenly, the entire architecture of her life. She had built a house out of not-telling. Juniper didn't know who her father was beyond *someone I knew when I was young.* The town didn't know why she'd left. Her own mother, three states away, thought the move to Port Merrow was about the house, the inheritance, the schools.

And the pen pal — Cyrus — thought she was writing to a stranger who gave good advice about soil.

Wren struck a match anyway. Held it to the corner of the page. The paper caught, curled, went brown at the edge — and she pinched it out with her fingers, fast, hissing at the small pain, and stood there in the drizzle with a half-burned letter and blistered fingertips and the absolute clarity of a woman who has just watched herself flinch.

*You don't get to burn it,* she told herself. *You don't get the clean version. You had the clean version for nine years and look what it grew.*

She went inside, sat down at the kitchen table, and rewrote the whole thing by hand, because the burned one had been too careful and she was done being careful. Her fingertips throbbed. She let them.

*Dear Harborlight,*

*I almost burned this letter. That's the first true thing. The second true thing is that I have done something unforgivable, or at least something I have never managed to forgive, which may be the same thing.*

*You wrote about your grandfather keeping the light by hand, and about how you stayed when you could have gone, and you asked me once — letter six, I still have it — whether I had ever chosen the harder thing over the easier one. I lied to you. I said yes. Here is the truth: I have only ever chosen the thing that let me leave the room.*

*Nine years ago I was in love with someone who was promised to someone else. I let myself be loved anyway. When it ended — and I ended it, I want that on the record, I was not a bystander to my own life — the person he was promised to was kind to me. That is the part I cannot get past. Not the guilt of wanting. The guilt of being forgiven too easily by someone who should have hated me, and who had every right to.*

*I left. I was pregnant and alone and frightened and I told myself I left for the baby's sake, and some of that was even true, but most of it was that I could not stand to be looked at. I have a daughter now. She is six. She thinks the sea is the best thing about this town, and she is right, and I am trying to raise her to be someone who stays in rooms, who says the hard thing while she is still in the room to say it in.*

*I don't expect you to write back to this. I expect you to be a person with sense. But the rules of this program say we correspond for the season, and I am a rule-follower when the rules protect me, so I will keep writing unless you tell me to stop.*

*Don't tell me to stop. That's the third true thing.*

*— Your pen pal, still unnamed, still here*

She read it three times. She put it in an envelope, addressed it to the library in her careful non-handwriting — the slanted, unremarkable script she'd adopted for the program, a stranger's penmanship worn like a coat — and she walked it down to the post box on Harbor Road in the drizzle, and she stood there with it in the slot up to her knuckles for a long moment, feeling the metal cold around her wrist like a cuff.

Then she let go.

* * *

The four days until the reply were the longest of Wren's adult life, and she had once waited nine hours in a hospital corridor for a test result that decided her whole future.

She cleaned rooms that didn't need cleaning. She repainted the downstairs bathroom with the Sea Glass paint, finally, and discovered it was exactly the color of the harbor in February and hated it and loved it. She walked Juniper to school along the cliff path, taking the long way around so they wouldn't pass the lighthouse, and Juniper noticed — Juniper noticed everything — and asked why they never walked the short way anymore, and Wren said the short way was muddy, which was even true.

On Thursday she checked the mail twice. On Friday she made herself wait until Juniper was in bed before she walked down to the box, and there it was: a library-stamped envelope, her false name on the front in handwriting she now knew better than her own.

Cyrus Sterling's handwriting. She'd known it in the first letter — that was the awful joke of it, the clerical error that wasn't an error at all, because some part of her had recognized the loop of the g and the way the y dove like a bird before her brain caught up. Nine years and the handwriting hadn't changed. People didn't change, she'd thought. That was the whole thesis of her return to Port Merrow: nothing here would have changed, and neither would she, and she would simply install her new life inside the old one like a ship in a bottle.

She sat on the harbor wall under the one working streetlamp and opened it with her thumbnail.

*Dear pen pal,*

*You said you almost burned your letter. I'm glad you didn't. I've burned letters — real ones, in the sink, with the window open so the smoke wouldn't set off the alarm — and I can tell you what I learned, which is that burning a letter doesn't burn the thing the letter was about. It just means you're carrying it and the mail carrier isn't.*

*I'm not going to tell you that whatever you did is forgivable, because I don't know what it is, and because I don't think forgiveness is something you can hand someone like a coupon. But I'll tell you what I know about guilt, which is more than I'd like to.*

*I have a brother. I'm not going to say more than that, except that there was a woman once, and a choice, and I have spent years being angry at the wrong people because being angry at the right one — myself — required a flashlight and a long walk down a dark hallway. I stayed at the lighthouse when I could have left. Everyone in town calls that loyalty. Some nights I call it cowardice with a view.*

*You wrote that you want to raise your daughter to say the hard thing while she's still in the room. That's the finest sentence anyone has sent me in this program, and I've gotten some very fine sentences, including one about compost that I intend to have framed.*

*So here is what I think. I think you did something, and you've been doing time for it — nine years, you said, which is a long sentence for a crime no one charged you with. I think the person you wronged, if she is anything like the person I wronged, is probably tired too. Guilt is heavy for the carrier but it's not weightless for the carrier's people. They feel you flinch every time they walk into a room.*

*You asked me not to tell you to stop. I won't. But I'll ask something in return: stop doing your time in advance. Whatever reckoning is coming, you don't have to serve it tonight, and tomorrow night, and every night for nine years. Serve it once, when it comes, and then get up off the floor.*

*The garden is nearly clear, by the way. Your advice about the clay was correct and I resent it. There's a rosemary bush under the weeds that I'd written off as dead, and it isn't. It's been alive under there the whole time, waiting for someone to stop burying it.*

*I think you're the rosemary. Don't make me regret saying that.*

*— Your pen pal, also unnamed, also here*

Wren read it twice. The second time, her vision blurred at *guilt is heavy for the carrier but it's not weightless for the carrier's people,* and she had to set the letter down on the cold stone of the harbor wall and look at the water for a while.

The lighthouse stood out on its point, the automatic lamp turning its patient circle, and she thought: *she's up there. She's up there right now, or she's in the keeper's house with the logbooks, and she wrote this to me — to me — without knowing, and she said serve it once and then get up off the floor.*

The mercy of it was unbearable. It was worse than hatred. Hatred would have been a door closing; this was a door opening onto a room Wren had no right to enter, lit and warm, with a chair pulled out.

She thought about the woman at the hardware store, the careful voice, the eyes going to the wood screws. *You'll be back before you know it.* Cyrus had been kind to her in person, too — that terrible, armored, polite kindness — and now she was kind on paper, and Wren sat on the harbor wall with both kindnesses in her lap like two halves of something that used to be whole.

*She forgave a stranger,* Wren thought. *She forgave me without knowing it was me. Which means the only thing left — the only thing — is to let her do it knowing.*

The lamp swept the water. Sweep, dark, sweep. Wren folded the letter into her pocket, next to the blister on her fingertips, and walked home, and did not sleep, and in the morning she woke with the answer sitting on her chest like a cat that had come in through the window: not another letter.

The garden. Cyrus had written about the garden — the rosemary, the clay, the clearing. Wren knew where the keeper's plot was; she'd known since she was nineteen and Daniel had walked her out along the point and said, *my sister's the real keeper, I just visit.* The gate was a green wooden thing with a latch that stuck. She'd climbed it once, a lifetime ago, to steal currants while Cyrus was in town.

She could go back as a stranger. That was the coward's version and she was done with the coward's version — no, that wasn't true, she'd be a coward about it, of course she would, but she'd be a coward *moving forward.* She'd knock on that gate with a pair of gloves and a spade and no name offered and none asked, and she'd stand in the room. The hard thing, while she was still in the room to say it in.

Wren got up, put the kettle on, and looked out the kitchen window at the harbor, where the morning fog was lifting off the water in long slow ribbons, and past the harbor to the point, where the lighthouse went on keeping its faithful, blind watch over everything she had ever failed.

The gate latch stuck, Cyrus had written once, early on, complaining. *You have to lift and push. Everyone in town knows it. It's the only gate in Port Merrow with a personality.*

Wren knew it too. She'd known it at nineteen.

She finished her coffee, found her gloves, and went to find a spade.
