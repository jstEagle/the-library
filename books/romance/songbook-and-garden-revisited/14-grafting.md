## 14. Grafting

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing to anyone except me and the tide table.

*Rootstock first,* my pen pal had written, in that handwriting I still refused to find familiar. *Don't start with the scions. Start with what you're grafting onto. If the rootstock is weak or wrong for your soil, it doesn't matter how good the cutting is — you're just performing surgery on a corpse.*

I read it three times at the kitchen table with my coffee going cold, and then I read it a fourth time because there was a sentence underneath that had nothing to do with apples:

*Also: you keep writing about this tree like it's a headstone. It's not. It's a tree. Trees are interested in living.*

I put the letter down. I looked out the window at the garden, where my grandfather's apple tree stood in its usual attitude of magnificent neglect — leaning east, half its crown dead, the other half still producing fruit so tart it could strip paint. Nine years I'd let it do whatever it wanted. Nine years of calling that respect.

"It's a tree," I told the kitchen. The kitchen did not disagree.

The rootstock arrived by post two days later, five bare-root whips in damp sawdust, looking less like trees than like the idea of trees — pale sticks with ambitions. I'd ordered them from a nursery in Oregon after an hour of research that was really just me obeying instructions from a stranger whose name I didn't know and whose handwriting made something in my chest go quiet and alert, like a dog hearing a car in the driveway.

I am aware of how that sounds. I'm choosing not to examine it.

* * *

Saturday morning came up gray and mild, the kind of October light that makes everything look like memory. I carried the rootstock down the hill to the garden, along with my grandfather's grafting knife, which I'd found in the toolshed wrapped in oilcloth like a relic. Which, I suppose, it was.

Here is what nobody tells you about grief and gardens: they require the same posture. Kneeling. Patience. A tolerance for dirt under your fingernails and for things taking longer than you want them to. My grandfather had spent forty years kneeling in this plot, and when he died, I inherited both the garden and the kneel, and I'd done neither properly since.

But the pen pal's advice was specific enough to hide behind. Dig the holes. Soak the roots. Match the cambium — the green layer just under the bark — that's where the two plants have to meet, wood to wood, wet to wet. Bind them tight. Then wait, and don't dig them up to check, because checking is just doubt wearing gloves.

I dug. The soil was cold and smelled like iron and rot and, faintly, like every summer of my childhood. The first hole went easy. The second hit a rock the size of a skull, and I swore at it with vocabulary I usually save for the Coast Guard, and hauled it out, and kept going.

By the third hole I was talking to my grandfather. Not out loud — I'm not sentimental, I'm a novelist, which is worse — but in the running commentary of my head. *You would've done this in February, you old goat. You'd tell me the soil's too warm now, the scions will wake up confused.* And then, unbidden, the thought I'd been stepping around all week: *You'd tell me to stop treating everything you left behind like it might break if I touched it.*

The fourth hole was for the cutting from his tree.

I'd taken it that morning — a pencil-thick whip from the living side of the crown, cut at the angle the letter specified, wrapped in a wet cloth. His tree, grafted onto new roots. The same fruit, theoretically, the same tartness, growing out of a different foundation. I held the two pieces together in my lap, lining up the green layers, and felt the metaphor arrive like weather.

"No," I said out loud this time. "Absolutely not."

Because here's the thing about metaphors: once you notice one, you can't unnotice it, and this one was so obvious it was practically wearing a name tag. Old life, new roots. Cut from the living part, bind it to something young, wait through the winter without digging it up to check. I could write that scene in my sleep. I had written adjacent scenes; my unfinished novel had a whole subplot about a widow replanting an orchard that I'd abandoned precisely because it was getting too close to something true.

I bound the graft anyway. Wax string, tight, the way the letter said. Cambium to cambium. I pressed the two woods together and thought, furiously, about nothing.

That was when the gate creaked.

* * *

Wren Calloway stood at the garden gate holding a paper bag and an expression like someone who had rehearsed being casual and lost her nerve halfway through.

She'd been appearing in town for weeks now — the hardware store, the library steps, the bakery with a small dark-haired girl in tow — and I'd been executing a series of maneuvers I'd have called dignified avoidance if anyone asked. We'd spoken exactly once, briefly, in the paint aisle, and the conversation had the warmth of two strangers trapped in an elevator. Before that, nine years of nothing. Before *that*, she'd broken my brother's engagement apart — broken *my* engagement apart, though everyone politely filed it under Daniel's — and left Port Merrow like a tide going out.

"Hi," she said. "Sorry. The gate was open."

"The gate's always open," I said. "It doesn't latch anymore. Like most things around here."

"I brought—" She lifted the bag. Inside were apples. Small, hard, green-gold ones. "These are off the tree behind my house. I think it's the same variety as yours. I was going to leave them on the porch, but then I saw you down here, and I thought—"

"You thought you'd ambush me while I'm kneeling in mud," I said. "Smart. Harder for me to leave."

Something moved across her face — surprise, maybe, or relief that I'd gone for dry humor instead of the door. "Is that grafting?" She nodded at the rootstock laid out on the tarp.

"Attempted grafting. I'm following instructions from a stranger who writes letters like a foreman."

"Good instructions?"

"Annoyingly good." I sat back on my heels before my knees filed a complaint. "How do you know what grafting looks like?"

"My grandfather grew apples." She said it carefully, the way people say things when they're deciding how much of themselves to hand over. "In Vermont. He had forty trees and strong opinions. I spent every September of my childhood on a ladder with a picking bag."

"And now you're here."

"Now I'm here." She looked at the garden — really looked, at the collapsed bean trellis, the bramble advancing from the north fence, the apple tree leaning its ruined crown against the sky. "This used to be beautiful. I remember it being beautiful."

"You remember it from the outside," I said, and immediately wished I hadn't, because it was cruel in a way that was more accurate than kind.

But Wren didn't flinch. She set the bag of apples down inside the gate, deliberate, like paying a toll. "Can I help? I won't touch anything without asking. But you're about to do the cuts wrong."

I stared at her. "Excuse me?"

"The angle." She pointed at the scion in my hand. "Your stranger's letter is right about everything else, but if you're doing whip-and-tongue, the tongue has to interlock, not just sit flat. Flat surfaces slip. Interlocking surfaces hold." She hesitated. "My grandfather made me practice on willow sticks for a whole summer before he let me near the real trees. I complained the entire time. It's the only useful thing I know how to do."

There was a long moment in which I considered several responses, ranked them by pettiness, and discarded all of them, mostly because my knees hurt and the alternative was standing up.

"There's a spare knife in the shed," I said. "If you cut yourself, bleed over there. This plot's had enough grief."

* * *

It should have been unbearable, working side by side with her. Some part of me had budgeted for unbearable — had stocked up on silence and short answers and the particular armor of being busy. What actually happened was worse, or better, depending on which account you'd want me to file: it was easy.

Wren worked the way she wrote — precise, unhurried, hands that knew what they were doing and didn't need applause. She showed me the tongue cut twice, slow, narrating without condescension. *Down-slope, one clean pull, don't saw. The cambium is the same color as the flesh of an apple. You'll see it.* She was right. Of course she was right. There was a stretch of twenty minutes where neither of us spoke and the only sounds were the knife, the wind in the dead crown of the tree, and Juniper — no, not Juniper, she wasn't here — the gulls, arguing about something out past the point.

"So," she said eventually, fitting a second scion to its rootstock, binding it with strips of cloth she'd torn from the hem of some rag in her bag. "Your novel. Is it going?"

"That's a question with a body count."

"That bad?"

"That stalled." I tightened the wax string. "Two hundred pages of a woman keeping a lighthouse and refusing to want anything, and then I ran out of ways to describe fog."

"Fog is a way of describing refusal," Wren said, and then went pink, visibly appalled at herself. "Sorry. That was — unsolicited. People hate notes."

"People hate *bad* notes." I looked at her. She was concentrating very hard on the binding. "Say the rest of it."

She took a breath. "Fog isn't weather in books. It's what characters use instead of saying what they mean. If your heroine keeps walking into fog, the reader knows she's hiding, and after a hundred pages of it they stop trusting you to bring her out. So either burn the fog off or make it cost her something." She glanced up, quick, defensive. "That's all. That's the whole note."

I turned it over in my head, the way I'd turned over her letters' critiques of my fictional keeper, Marguerite — sharp, correct, delivered like a scalpel wrapped in a dish towel. Somewhere in the last month I had started looking forward to being corrected by a stranger. Now the stranger was kneeling six feet away with dirt on her wrists, and the correction stung in exactly the productive way.

"Burn it off," I said. "Or make it cost her."

"Cost her," Wren said. "Burning it off is mercy. Mercy in chapter eight is theft. Take something from her in the fog and let her spend the rest of the book getting it back."

"You've thought about this a lot for someone who claims their only skill is grafting."

"I read a lot as a kid." A pause, and then, quieter: "We didn't have a television. Or much else. Books were free at the library." She tied off the last knot and sat back, surveying our work: five little surgeries lined up in their holes, bandaged, waiting. "They'll take or they won't. Probably half. That's normal. Don't take it personally."

"Everything I plant, I take personally."

"I noticed." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't quite match the lightness, and for a second we were having two conversations, the one about apples and the one underneath, the one we'd apparently agreed without agreeing not to have.

The wind shifted. Out on the water, the light was turning silver, the hour when the lighthouse starts mattering again even in daylight. I found myself watching Wren watch it — the tower above us, white and patient, my grandfather's whole life and mine.

"He kept it by hand," I heard myself say. "Forty years. Every night, up those stairs, lighting the lamp, winding the clockwork. No automation until '89. He used to say the light didn't need him anymore, but he needed the climbing." I picked up another scion, mostly to have something in my hands. "I tell myself I stay for the same reason. Duty. Somebody has to."

"And do you believe that?"

The question landed soft and went deep, the way her questions in letters always did — no armor-piercing, just a key finding the lock it was cut for. I could have deflected. I'd had months of practice deflecting Odette, the town, myself.

"I believe somebody has to," I said finally. "I believe I stayed because leaving would've required deciding what I actually wanted, and it was easier to be needed than to be honest." I cut the tongue into the scion, one clean pull. "Don't quote me. I deny everything."

"Your secret's safe." She smiled — small, real, gone quickly, like a fish surfacing. "I'm excellent at secrets."

And there it was again, that flicker under her voice, the weight I'd caught in the hardware store and in certain sentences of her letters — the one that sounded like a woman carrying something heavy and pretending it was a handbag. I almost asked. The question was right there, fully formed: *What are you carrying, Wren Calloway? Why did you come back? Why does your kindness look like penance?*

Instead I handed her the last rootstock and said, "Show me the tongue cut again. Slower."

* * *

We finished as the light went bronze. Five grafts in the ground, watered, mulched, labeled with pencil on cedar stakes in two handwritings — hers slanted and quick, mine square and stubborn. Standing back, they looked like nothing. Sticks in dirt. In April, if the world was kind, they'd be leaves.

"They look like nothing," Wren said, echoing me, and laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her, and I hated how much I liked the sound of it.

"That's the deal," I said. "You bind two living things together and then you don't get to check. You just wait through winter and trust the green layer did its work."

She went quiet. When I looked over, she was staring at the stakes with an expression I couldn't file — grief and longing and something that looked terrifyingly like hope, all trying to share one face.

"Cyrus," she said. My name, unguarded, and it did something to the air between us. "There's something I—"

The phone rang up at the house. Then rang again. The harbor line — the one that only rings when something's wrong, a boat overdue, a buoy adrift, weather coming in sideways.

I was already moving. "I have to take that."

"Of course. Go." She gathered her rag and her knife, and at the gate she stopped, half-turned, the late light catching her face. "Thank you. For letting me help."

"Wren." She waited. I had a dozen exits and chose none of them. "Come back Thursday. If the grafts survive the week, they'll need watching. And I'll need someone to tell me what I'm doing wrong."

Something crossed her face — fast, complicated, gone. "Thursday," she said. "I'll be here."

I watched her walk up the path toward the road, small against the big gray afternoon, and then I took the stairs two at a time and grabbed the ringing phone, and the voice on the other end was Odette's, breathless with news I wasn't ready for:

"Cyrus. You need to sit down. The library just announced the reveal event — end of season, all the pen pals meet face-to-face. Six weeks, honey. Six weeks, and then you find out who's been writing to you."
