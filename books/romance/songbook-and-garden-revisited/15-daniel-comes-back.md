## 15. Daniel Comes Back

### Cyrus

My mother's birthday fell on the last warm Saturday of October, which meant the whole Sterling family ritual would unfold in her kitchen: a lemon cake with too much frosting, my father's record player stuck on the same four Sinatra albums, and Daniel arriving late with an apology shaped like a gift.

I'd known he was coming for two weeks. He'd called from Boston to warn me, which was itself a warning — Daniel only pre-announced himself when he wanted something or dreaded something, and I couldn't imagine what he wanted from me anymore.

"You'll be civil," he'd said. Not a question.

"I'm always civil."

"You know what I mean." A pause on the line, long enough that I could hear him deciding whether to say the next thing. "Is she still in town? The librarian mentioned — people talk."

"Port Merrow talks," I agreed. "It's our chief industry."

"Cyrus."

"I don't know where Wren Calloway buys her groceries, Daniel. We're not friends." The lie sat in my mouth like a stone. It wasn't even a good lie. Three days earlier she had stood at my garden gate with dirt on her knees and shown me how to stake the raspberry canes so they wouldn't snap under their own fruit, and I had thought about her hands for an embarrassing length of time afterward. But the phone was not the place where truths lived, not between me and my brother.

"Good," he said. "Good."

He hung up before I could ask why it mattered. Nine years married elsewhere and he still kept a weather eye on the coast where Wren had washed out of his life, the way you check a scar in the mirror without meaning to.

* * *

The kitchen smelled of lemon and butter when I arrived, carrying flowers I'd cut that morning from the garden — the first real bouquet the restored beds had produced, dahlias in colors my grandfather would have called improper. My mother loved them. She buried her nose in them and said, "Your grandfather's soil always did run sweet," and I felt the small private pleasure of having done one thing right.

Daniel arrived forty minutes late, as scheduled by the laws of his nature. He filled the doorway the way he always had — tall, tanned from some conference in some sunny place, wearing a jacket that cost more than my annual lamp maintenance budget. His wife, Priya, followed with a wine bottle in each hand and the expression of a woman who had been briefed on turbulence ahead.

"There's my sister the lighthouse keeper." He said it warmly. It was his warm voice. I had learned young to hear the machinery underneath it.

"There's my brother the whatever-he-is-now," I said. "Regional vice president of something?"

"Solutions." He kissed my mother's cheek. "Happy birthday, Mom."

"Solutions," I repeated. "To what?"

"Everything, ideally." He laughed at his own joke, and Priya laughed too, a half-beat late, and I watched her do it and understood she was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

Dinner was roast chicken and small talk with a hairline fracture running through it. Daniel talked about Boston. My father asked careful questions. My mother steered us away from the two reefs she knew were there — the lighthouse, meaning my life; and the past, meaning everything else. For forty minutes we sailed clean water.

Then Daniel set down his fork and looked at me with the expression he'd worn since he was nine years old and I'd broken his model ship: the look of a man preparing to be reasonable.

"Odette told Priya's cousin that you joined the pen-pal program at the library."

The table went quiet the way a room goes quiet when a storm front arrives — everyone still talking, but lower, faster, listening underneath.

"It's a town program," I said. "Half the town's in it."

"And did they match you with anyone interesting?"

"Why do you care who I write letters to?"

"Because—" He stopped. Recalibrated. My brother was very good at recalibrating; it was most of what he'd learned in business school. "Because I worry about you out there alone. You don't let anyone in. You never have. You chose that tower over—"

"Don't," I said.

"—over your own life, Cyrus. Over people who loved you." He turned his wine glass a quarter turn, precise, deliberate. "Over me."

There it was. Nine years compressed into a sentence delivered like an invoice. I felt my mother go still beside me, felt my father study his plate with the concentration of a man defusing something.

"That's an interesting way to remember it," I said.

"It's the accurate way to remember it." Daniel's voice stayed pleasant, which was how I knew we were in danger. "She walked out on me, Cy. No note. No explanation. I found out from her landlord that she'd gone. And my own sister took her side then, and apparently you're all ready to take her side again now, sight unseen, because Port Merrow has decided nine years is long enough for anybody's sins."

"Has it?" I asked. "How long does it take, do you think? What's the standard sentence?"

"I'm not talking about forgiveness. I'm talking about loyalty."

"So am I." I heard my own voice come out level and cold and unfamiliar. "You want to talk about that night? Fine. Let's talk about the part where you came to the lighthouse at eleven o'clock and told me she'd left, and I asked if you'd fought, and you said no, she just packed. She *just packed*, Daniel. People don't just pack. Not her. She alphabetized her spice rack. But I was twenty-six and grieving Grandpa and keeping a light burning every single night, and you needed someone to be angry with you, so I was. I performed it. I said terrible things about her for years because it was easier than asking my brother one honest question."

"What question?" His jaw had gone tight.

"Why she really left. Whether you already knew." I hadn't planned to say it. It came up from somewhere below the floorboards of me, old and load-bearing. "And now you're sitting here telling me I chose the tower over love, and I want you to sit with the fact that maybe I chose duty because love, in this family, meant performing whatever emotion you required. That's what I froze. Not her leaving. You."

Priya put her hand flat on the table — not touching anyone, just resting it there, a small white flag. My mother reached over and covered it with her own.

Daniel looked at me for a long moment, and I saw something move behind his face that might have been the truth trying to surface, and then he smiled instead, the boardroom smile, and said, "Well. Happy birthday, Mom," and refilled his glass.

* * *

I stepped out onto the porch to breathe. The evening smelled of woodsmoke and salt, and the lighthouse beam swept past above the tree line, steady as a pulse, and I stood there letting it count seconds for me until the door opened behind me.

Not Daniel. Priya.

She wrapped her cardigan tighter and stood beside me looking at the dark garden. "Your dahlias are ridiculous," she said. "In a good way."

"They're my grandfather's. Rescued."

"He sounds like he was quite a man."

"He was." I hesitated. "Priya. Does he talk about her? About Wren?"

She was quiet long enough that the beam made two full turns. "Once a year, maybe. Usually after his company holiday party. He gets nostalgic and then furious and then goes to bed." She glanced at me. "He told me once that she was the best thing that ever happened to him and he drove her away. Then he denied saying it. I've learned not to keep receipts in this marriage. It's simpler."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I knew what I was marrying." She said it lightly, but her hand found my arm and squeezed once. "Whatever happened here nine years ago — you're allowed to change your mind about whose side you're on. Sides aren't hereditary."

She went back inside. I stayed on the porch a while longer, watching the light turn, thinking about sides, and about a woman who staked raspberry canes with surgeon's patience, and about a letter in my coat pocket I'd read four times that week — *I want to stop being a person who leaves* — and I realized I was already standing somewhere I hadn't chosen to stand yet, and that my feet had gotten there ahead of me.

### Wren

Juniper had a tooth coming loose — the bottom left, wiggling like a loose plank — and she spent the entire drive to the Harborside Inn narrating its progress to me in the rearview mirror.

"Mum. Mum. Look. LOOK." I looked in the mirror. She leaned her head forward to display it. "It moved. It MOVED."

"I see it. Very historic."

"Miss Okafor says the tooth fairy gives a pound but Nana says a dollar and which is it here?"

"Here it's whatever's in my coat pocket, probably buttons."

"MUM."

We were going to my mother-in-law's — no, not mother-in-law, I corrected myself, the word had been legally dissolved along with everything else — Mrs. Calloway's birthday dinner at the inn, the annual gathering of the reduced remnant of the Calloway clan: Aunt Bess, Cousin Rob, Rob's loud children, and me, invited out of politeness and guilt in equal measure, the prodigal daughter-in-law returned with a grandchild as proof of continued relevance. Mrs. Calloway had been kind to me since I came back. Kind in the specific way of people who have also survived this town's memory.

I had dressed carefully in armor disguised as a sweater. I had rehearsed answers to the questions I'd get: *Where are you living now? The old Hollis house, yes, fixing it up. And work? Freelance, mostly, soil consulting, yes, it's real, people pay me.* And the question nobody would ask but everyone would think: *And Daniel Sterling, is he still—*

He was. Odette had told me, in the merciless way Odette tells people things, that Daniel Sterling was coming home for his mother's birthday, and that the Sterlings' dinner was Saturday, and that I should be aware. As if awareness were a garment I could put on.

Port Merrow was small enough that collision was arithmetic. Two families, one inn district, one Saturday. I'd done the math and come anyway, because Juniper had been promised cake and because I was tired of organizing my entire existence around the coordinates of a man who had told me to leave.

That was the sentence I still couldn't say aloud, even inside my own skull. *He told me to leave.* I'd spent nine years building the softer version — *it didn't work out, I had reasons, I had to go* — and the soft version had held up fine until I started writing letters to a stranger about honesty, and now the true version knocked at odd hours like a bill collector.

"Mum, can I have the window seat at Nana's?"

"You can have any seat you want, bug."

"Can we see the lighthouse on the way home? The real close way?"

"The real close way is closed tonight."

"It's not closed, it's a road."

"It's a road I'm not taking tonight." I caught her eye in the mirror and softened. "Soon. I promise. Soon we'll walk all the way up to it."

"Inside? Can we go inside? Does someone live there?"

"Someone keeps it," I said, and heard how my voice changed on the word, and changed the subject fast. "What flavor cake do you think Nana got?"

* * *

The inn's dining room was all candlelight and noise, and for ninety minutes I almost relaxed. Juniper charmed the table with her tooth. Rob's kids taught her a clapping game of dubious lyrical content. Mrs. Calloway held court, tiny and sharp-eyed at eighty-one, and when she paused beside my chair she pressed her hand briefly on my shoulder and said, "You look well, Wren. This town suits you better than it deserves to," and I had to pretend to sneeze.

Then, through the window, across the parking lot, I saw them arrive.

The Sterling family, spilling out of a car under the inn's lanterns. An older couple, silver-haired, the father holding his wife's elbow on the gravel. And Daniel — taller than I remembered, broader, city-cut, laughing at something. And beside him, a woman with dark hair pulled back, and even at that distance and after nine years I knew her posture before I knew her face: Cyrus. Cyrus Sterling, holding a paper-wrapped bouquet, looking up at the inn sign with the expression of someone attending a dental appointment.

My stomach dropped through the floorboards of me.

They were eating here. Tonight. Of course they were. One inn, one Saturday, one town doing what Port Merrow did, which was fold everyone eventually into the same room and watch.

"Wren?" Aunt Bess was peering at me. "You've gone white as the tablecloth, dear."

"Fine. Wine. I need — I'll be right back."

I didn't go for wine. I went to the ladies' room and gripped the sink and breathed the way the midwife had taught me, seven years ago, alone in a hospital in a city where nobody knew my name, and I told myself: *You're a different person now. You're a person who stays. You're a person who writes letters and means them.*

Then I went back out, and the universe, being efficient, arranged the collision in the corridor by the restrooms, where there was no room to pass without acknowledgment.

Cyrus was walking toward me. Alone — Daniel and the others already seated, voices drifting from the dining room. She saw me and stopped, and for one suspended second we simply looked at each other, two women holding grocery lists of things unsaid.

"Mrs. Calloway's party," Cyrus said. Flat. Careful.

"My former mother-in-law. Yes." My voice sounded borrowed. "And yours, I heard. Happy birthday to her."

"Thank you." Her eyes moved over my face like a reader checking a page for revisions. "You look well."

"So do you. The garden agrees with you." I heard myself say it and nearly flinched — too intimate, too ours, a line from the letters worn as small talk. Something flickered across her face too, quick as a fish under ice. Neither of us named it.

"Your daughter," Cyrus said. "I saw her through the window. She has your—"

Whatever she was going to say, we never found out, because Daniel appeared in the corridor behind her, drawn by some husband's radar for exactly this frequency, and the temperature of the hallway dropped ten degrees.

"Well," he said. "Look what the tide brought in."

"Daniel." Cyrus's voice had an edge I'd never heard in it before — not in the old days, not ever. "Don't."

"I'm just greeting an old friend." He smiled at me, and it was the smile from the last night, the one that had told me to leave without using those words — *this family has expectations, Wren, and you understand, don't you, what a mess this would be* — and nine years collapsed like a folding chair. "How's the city? Or did that not work out either?"

"Danny," I said quietly, using the name I knew he hated now, "congratulations on your marriage. Your mother looks wonderful. Excuse me."

I stepped past him. My hands were shaking and I kept them moving, natural, unhurried, a woman merely going to fetch her coat, and behind me I heard Cyrus say, low and clear, in a voice that carried:

"If you speak to her that way again, Daniel, you and I are going to have a conversation that will ruin Mom's birthday, and I promise you won't enjoy your half of it."

I didn't stay to hear his answer. I collected Juniper from the table — "But MUM, the cake—" — thanked Mrs. Calloway, who squeezed my hand and said, "Go on, love. Some rooms are worth leaving early," and drove home with my daughter chattering about teeth and the radio off and my heart going like a gull in a gale.

At a red light outside the harbor, Juniper went suddenly quiet in the way children do when they've been saving a thought.

"Mum? Who was that lady who was nice to you in the hall?"

"When, bug?"

"At the restaurant. The tall one. She looked mad at that man but she looked nice at you."

I watched the light. Across the water, high on the point, the lighthouse beam swung its slow white arc, once, twice, patient as anything.

"She's an old friend," I said. Which was, depending on the hour, either the biggest lie I'd ever told my daughter or the truest.

That night I wrote to my pen pal. I sat at the kitchen table after Juniper slept and I wrote: *Tonight I ran into someone from the worst year of my life, and the strangest thing happened — someone defended me. Someone I'd have sworn hated me. I keep turning it over like a stone. I don't know what to do with kindness that arrives from the direction you least expect it. Do you?*

I sealed it before I could decide whether I was writing about Cyrus or to her.

The mailbox at the end of the lane was cold and rusted and it swallowed the envelope the way it swallowed everything, faithfully, without judgment, and I stood there in the dark a moment too long, watching the light on the point turn, and turn, and turn.
