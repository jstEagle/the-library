## 16. What You Said in Your Sleep

Juniper said it on a Tuesday, over toast, with the terrible casualness of six-year-olds who do not yet know they are carrying lit matches.

"Mum," she said, licking jam off her thumb, "do you write to your friend at the lighthouse?"

Wren's hand stopped halfway to her teacup. The kitchen went very quiet — the kind of quiet that has a sound to it, like the second before a wave breaks.

"What friend at the lighthouse, sweetheart?" She kept her voice level. She was proud of how level it was. Inside, something was already running for the door.

"The one you post letters to." Juniper swung her legs under her chair, heels drumming the wood. "I saw you. At the harbor. You put them in the blue box and then you looked around like—" she screwed up her face, searching for the word, and found it with relish— "like you were *sneaking*."

"I was not sneaking."

"You were absolutely sneaking," Juniper said, delighted. "Like in my book. The fox who mails secret letters."

"There is no fox, Juniper."

"There is a fox in the book."

"*In the book.*" Wren stood up, gathered plates that didn't need gathering, ran water over them though the dishwasher was half full. Her reflection swam in the dark window above the sink: a woman doing dishes at seven in the morning as if dishes were an emergency. "Eat your toast."

Juniper ate her toast. Juniper, mercifully, was easily redirected by toast. But the question hung in the kitchen like smoke, and Wren carried it with her all day — into the hardware store, where she mislabeled two shades of white; into the car, where she sat in the parking lot after closing and did not drive home for eleven minutes.

Because here was the arithmetic that had been keeping her up at night, done now in daylight, in ink:

If Juniper had seen her mailing letters near the harbor — and Juniper had, had seen her, had catalogued the sneaking — then other people might have seen her too. Port Merrow was not a city. Port Merrow was a fishbowl with a lighthouse in the middle of it. The librarian knew everything. Odette Marchand knew more than the librarian. And Cyrus Sterling lived at the end of every road that led anywhere, in a tower visible from half the coastline, walking a garden path that Wren had been standing on three weeks ago with dirt on her knees and cider on her breath and the words *I'm not who you think I am* dying in her throat.

Had Cyrus seen her? At the mailbox? On the harbor road? Had some part of Cyrus already done this same arithmetic — the handwriting, the phrasing, the way a certain sentence turned just so — and said nothing?

Wren thought of the hardware store, of those four minutes of frozen small talk, of Cyrus's eyes going carefully neutral when Wren mentioned she'd moved into her grandmother's house. Neutral wasn't nothing. Neutral was a choice.

*She doesn't know,* Wren told herself. *She can't know.*

But she wrote the day's letter anyway with a hand that wouldn't quite steady, and she caught herself, twice, almost signing a name.

* *

The trouble with children is that they are sentries without discretion. That night, bath time became interrogation.

"Is your friend a lady or a man?" Juniper asked, pouring water over a plastic whale.

"A lady."

"Is she old?"

"No."

"Is she the one from the garden? With the apples? You came home smelling like apples and you were smiling weird."

"I don't smile weird."

"You smiled *weird*, Mum. Nana used to say you smiled weird when you were hiding something."

Their weekly video call with Wren's mother had been five days ago. Five days, and already the child was deploying surveillance intelligence. Wren scrubbed shampoo into Juniper's hair with perhaps more vigor than the situation required. "Your nana says a lot of things."

"Do you love her?"

The question landed like a dropped glass. Wren's hands went still in Juniper's hair. Foam slid down the little girl's neck.

"Who, sweetheart?"

"Your friend. Do you love your friend?"

And Wren — who had spent nine years becoming excellent at answering questions she hadn't been asked, at deflecting, at the smooth administrative lie — found that she had no answer ready. Not because the answer was complicated. Because it wasn't.

"I don't know her real name," Wren said finally. "That's the rule of the program. You write all season and nobody tells."

"That's a silly rule."

"It's actually quite a good rule."

"Why?"

Why. Six years old and asking the only question that mattered. Wren lifted her out of the tub, wrapped her in the hooded towel with the shark fin, held her a moment longer than drying required. Through the small window over the tub she could see, distantly, the lighthouse — a white finger against the early-dark sky, its beam beginning its patient rotation. Somewhere inside it, presumably, Cyrus Sterling was logging weather or burning dinner or writing about a fictional keeper who forgave a fictional runaway, and none of it — none of it — was about Wren, except that all of it was.

"Because sometimes," Wren said into the damp crown of Juniper's head, "it's easier to tell the truth to someone when you're not looking at them."

"Are you telling her the truth?"

"Some of it."

"Which parts?"

"The easy parts."

Juniper considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. Then: "You should tell her the hard parts too. Miss Okafor says secrets get heavy if you carry them too long. That's why camels have humps."

"That is not why camels have humps."

"It's *metaphorical*, Mum."

Wren laughed — really laughed, the first time in days — and tucked her daughter in, and turned off the light, and stood in the hallway listening to the small sounds of Juniper arranging her stuffed animals into their nightly parliament. And it struck her, standing there in the dark, that her daughter had just handed her the whole truth in a shark towel: the hard parts were the ones that weighed. She'd been mailing the easy parts to the lighthouse for months, and keeping the heavy ones here, in this house, where a six-year-old could trip over them any morning over toast.

* *

Thursday, she cracked.

Not dramatically. There was no scene. She simply found herself, at lunchtime, driving the long way back from the school run — the way that passed the harbor road, the way past the turnoff to the point — and she pulled over at the pull-off where tourists photograph the lighthouse, and she sat there with the engine ticking and watched the tower stand in the cold bright noon.

Does she know?

The question had teeth. It had been chewing on Wren for days. Because there were two possibilities and both of them were unbearable. Either Cyrus Sterling read her letters as a stranger's — received her confessions, her guilt, her clumsy tenderness — without knowing, which meant Wren had been lying by omission to a woman who had offered her grace blind; or Cyrus knew, and had known, and had chosen to keep writing anyway, which meant — what did that mean? Mercy? Pity? A trap being sprung slowly, letter by letter, until reveal night when the whole town would watch Wren Calloway walk into the library and be unmasked in front of the woman whose life she'd broken?

Or — third possibility, the one she circled and never landed on — Cyrus knew, and kept writing, because she wanted to.

Wren couldn't afford the third possibility. She'd learned that lesson nine years ago: wanting things in Port Merrow was how you ended up pregnant and alone on a bus with one suitcase, learning that hope was just the interest paid on a debt you couldn't see coming.

Her phone buzzed. Odette Marchand, of all people, texting the store's group thread about a delivery of winter-grade caulk. Ordinary. Municipal. Wren stared at the name and felt the fishbowl close another inch. Odette volunteered at the library. Odette handled the pen-pal cards. Odette had a nose like a customs beagle.

*He knows something,* Wren thought, absurdly, about no one. *Everyone is going to know something.*

That evening she took out the ribbon-tied bundle of letters — hers kept copies, always, a habit from the years when paper was the only witness she trusted — and reread the last month of correspondence looking for what Cyrus might have seen. The soil advice. The storm-night tenderness. The harvest letter, hers, the most naked thing she'd ever posted: *a daughter, a leaving, a wish to stop being a person who runs*. And Cyrus's reply, grace given blind, forgiveness offered *for whatever you did* —

*For whatever you did.*

Unless it wasn't blind. Unless somewhere in that careful, wry, generous prose there was a line drawn for her alone, a hook set quietly, waiting.

She read Cyrus's last letter three times. She could not find the trap. That frightened her more than a trap would have.

* *

Friday morning the match finally dropped.

Juniper's school ran late — a fire drill, apparently, though Juniper reported it as "an emergency but a fake one" — and Wren arrived at pickup among the last parents, stamping cold out of her boots in the gymnasium doorway. And there, across the floor, helping unfold folding chairs because of course she was, stood Cyrus Sterling.

Cyrus saw her. Wren watched it happen — the small arrest in her movements, the chair leg set down a half-second too carefully. Neither waved. Nine years ago they had known each other well enough to share a Thanksgiving table; now they performed the polite nod of acquaintances across a gymnasium, and Wren felt the nod cost her something physical, a stitch pulling loose somewhere under the ribs.

Then Juniper happened.

Juniper happened the way weather happens. One moment the children were being released in a ragged flood; the next, Wren's daughter had spotted Cyrus — whom she had met exactly once, at the garden gate, an encounter Juniper had since described as "the apple lady" — and was crossing the gymnasium floor at a dead sprint with the total moral freedom of the very young.

"Apple lady!" Juniper announced, arriving. "Do you still have apples?"

"In spring," Cyrus said. Her voice was warm. Her eyes, over Juniper's head, found Wren's and stayed there. "Trees need winter first."

"That's what my mum says about lots of stuff." Juniper seized Cyrus's hand with proprietary confidence. "Mum, it's the apple lady. From the lighthouse. Where you mail your letters."

The gymnasium did not go silent. That was the horror of it — the fire drill aftermath roared on, forty children and a hundred echoes, chairs scraping, someone's brother crying about a lost mitten — and in the middle of all that ordinary noise, Juniper said it again, louder, because children repeat themselves when adults go strange:

"MUM. Your LETTERS. For your FRIEND. At the LIGHTHOUSE."

Cyrus's face did something very small and very controlled. Wren would think about it later the way you think about a photograph of a disaster: the exact degree of stillness, the smile that arrived a beat late and left early.

"Well," Cyrus said mildly, to Juniper, to the air, to Wren. "Small town. Everyone writes to everyone eventually."

It was gracious. It was perfect. It was, Wren understood with absolute clarity, a lie — or rather, a door held open, an invitation to walk through it and confess right there between the folding chairs and the lost-mitten crier. *Say it,* the mildness said. *Say it now, and I'll let it be funny.*

And Wren — nine years practiced, expert-level, gold-medal deflector — heard herself say: "The pen-pal program. From the library. It's sweet, isn't it? Very good for the community."

"Yes," said Cyrus. "Very good for the community."

They stood there. Juniper beamed between them, unaware she had just detonated the last of Wren's cover, holding Cyrus's hand like it belonged to her.

"We should—" Wren began.

"I've got—" Cyrus began, and gestured vaguely at the chairs, the gymnasium, the entire concept of obligation, and retreated behind a stack of folding chairs with a courtesy so complete it felt like a slammed door.

* *

That night Wren sat at the kitchen table with a blank sheet of paper and the good pen, the one she used for letters that mattered, and she wrote:

*Dear friend,*

*My daughter told a room full of people that I write to someone at the lighthouse. She doesn't know what she said. I do. And I've been sitting here for an hour trying to decide whether to tell you the hard parts — the camel-hump parts — or whether I'm already too late, whether you've known for weeks, whether this whole season has been you being kind to me from behind a wall I built myself.*

*I keep almost saying it. At the garden gate. In the rain. Today, in a gymnasium, while my child held your hand. There is a version of me who is brave, and I keep scheduling meetings with her and she keeps canceling.*

*So here is one hard part, delivered while neither of us is looking: I think I know who you are. I've thought so for a while. And I haven't stopped writing, which tells me something about myself I'm still afraid to read.*

She stopped. Read it back. The words *I think I know who you are* sat on the page like a lit fuse, and Wren realized, with a lurch, that she had no idea which of them the fuse was burning toward — that if she mailed this, the next letter she received would either free her or end her, and she had built her entire adult life on never finding out which.

She folded the letter. She did not seal it. She put it in the drawer with the ribbon-tied bundle, unmailed, unsent, one more hard part carried instead of given.

Down the hall, Juniper called out sleepily — "Mum? Is the fox going to tell the truth in the end?"

Wren stood in the doorway of the dark bedroom. "What fox, sweetheart?"

"In my book." A yawn. "You have to find out if the fox tells the truth. That's the whole story."

"Go to sleep, Juniper."

But Wren lay awake a long time after, listening to the wind come up off the water, thinking: the forecast says gale by Sunday. Thinking: the lamp will need watching. Thinking: *if the storm takes the light, and I see it go dark from town, I will get in the car. I already know I will get in the car.*

The mask was slipping. She could feel it going, inch by inch, like paint peeling in salt air — and underneath it, terrifyingly, there was no longer any question of what she wanted.

Only whether she'd say it before the sea decided for her.
