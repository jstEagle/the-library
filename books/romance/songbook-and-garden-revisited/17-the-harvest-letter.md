## 17. The Harvest Letter

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which meant nothing, which is why I remember it.

Tuesdays were the day the mail boat came out to the point, weather permitting, and the mail boat had been running late all month because the sea had opinions about schedules. So when I heard the engine stutter around the headland at half past two, I was on my knees in the garden with dirt to my elbows, arguing with a row of kale that had decided, collectively, to die out of spite.

I almost didn't go up for it. That's the thing I keep turning over now, the hinge of the whole day: I almost let the boat pass and the mail sit in the box overnight, because my knees ached and the kale was winning and I had a paragraph of the novel stuck in my throat like a fishbone. But Odette had sent me a postcard the week before — a picture of a lighthouse, which was her idea of a joke, with the message *write your pen pal, you're getting weird* — and I'd promised myself I'd answer before the week was out, and the week was out.

So I went up. I wiped my hands on my thighs, which ruined the thighs, and I took the envelope out of the box, and it was heavier than usual.

My pen pal's letters had been getting heavier all season. Not in weight exactly — in gravity. The first ones had been all surface, all fencing: *your soil is probably compacted, have you tested the pH, no wonder your kale is sulking.* Then they'd warmed by degrees, the way a house warms when someone's been in it all day and you come home late. Somewhere around the letter where she'd told me my fictional heroine was "brave in every direction except the one that mattered," I'd started reading them standing up, like they might require me to defend myself.

This one required me to sit down.

I did it right there on the top step of the lighthouse, in the wind, because I couldn't make it to the door. The paper was good stock — she always used good stock, which struck me as a strange extravagance for someone who wrote like she was apologizing for existing — and her handwriting, which I'd long since stopped trying to place, was pressed so hard into the page that I could read the letter from the back, like braille, like a scar.

*Cyrus —*

*No. I don't know why I wrote your name. It's not your name, is it, it's just the one I've decided you have, the one I've been writing to all season in my head. The program says no names. Fine. Stranger, then. Except you stopped being a stranger around the third letter and we both know it, so let me just say what I have to say and you can decide what to do with it.*

*I have a daughter.*

*She's six. She has my stubbornness and her own opinions about shoes and she asks questions I can't answer. Not hard questions. Easy ones. Where did you grow up. Who was your best friend. Why don't we visit your old town more. Easy questions with answers that would take a year to give honestly, and she's six, so I give her pieces, and the pieces are starting not to fit together, and she's starting to notice.*

*I left this town — my town, the one I'm writing to you from, the one you probably drive through on your way to wherever you go for flour — nine years ago. I left in the night. I left with a suitcase and a secret and I told myself it was for the best, which is what people say when they mean it was for the worst but survivable, and survivable is not the same as best, I know that now. I've had nine years to know it.*

*I was twenty-three and pregnant and the father chose his family over me — no, that's not fair, he was twenty-four and terrified and his family made the choice easy, and I let them, because letting them was easier than fighting, and I have been letting people make my choices for me my whole life, and then blaming them, and that is the thing I am trying to stop being.*

*A person who leaves.*

*You said once — I keep your letters in a shoebox, did you know that, I keep them like a girl keeps ticket stubs — you said once that your grandfather used to say the light doesn't choose which ships to save. It just turns. It turns for the ones that are coming home and the ones that are leaving and the ones that are going to wreck no matter what anyone does. I have read that sentence forty times. I have read it in the bath. I have read it to my daughter, edited, as a bedtime story about a lighthouse, and she asked me if the light ever gets tired and I said no and then I cried in the hallway where she couldn't see, because I am the light's opposite. I am the thing that goes dark and calls it weather.*

*I'm not writing this to make you carry it. You've been kind to me all season and kindness is a debt I already don't know how to repay. I'm writing it because it's harvest, or nearly — I can tell by what you say about the garden, the tomatoes coming in all at once like they've been holding a meeting — and my grandmother used to say you don't get to keep what you won't name. That the garden forgives you for everything except not paying attention.*

*So I'm paying attention. I'm naming it. I did something, nine years ago, that hurt people who didn't deserve it. I can't tell you what, not yet, maybe not ever, because the program says one season and the season is running out and I am a coward in the ways that count. But I wanted you to have the shape of it. I wanted someone to know that the woman writing to you about soil pH is also a woman who ran, and left wreckage, and has spent nine years telling herself the wreckage was someone else's fault, and is done with that, or trying to be.*

*Forgive me for whatever I did. Not to you — I don't think I did anything to you, I think you'd have said so by now, you don't seem like someone who lets a thing slide out of politeness. Forgive me for whatever I did to someone else. Say it like a stranger, because you are one, and strangers are the only people who can forgive cleanly, without history getting in the teeth of it.*

*Or don't. Burn this letter. I'll understand. I've burned a few myself.*

*But if you do write back, tell me something true. You always do. It's the only currency I trust anymore.*

*— Your stranger, who is less of one every week and more afraid of it*

* * *

I sat on the top step until the wind came around to the north and started taking the heat off my shoulders, and then I sat some more, because my legs had gone to sleep and because I didn't trust what I'd do if I stood up. Walk, probably. Walk the whole point, out to the rocks and back, which is what I do when a thing is too big for the house.

A daughter. Six years old. Left in the night nine years ago, from a town small enough that leaving in the night was a whole production — you'd need the ferry or the coast road, and the coast road in those days flooded every time it rained hard, so probably the ferry, probably the early one, the six a.m., the one nobody took unless they didn't want to be seen taking it.

I tried to think who that could be. I want to be honest in this account, since honesty is apparently the season's theme: my first instinct was to run the roster. Nine years back, who left Port Merrow in the night? And the roster was short, because Port Merrow is not a place people flee from so much as a place they flee to, and the ones who leave leave by degrees, for jobs, for spouses, for cities, with garage sales and forwarded mail. A night departure with a secret was a rare vintage.

But the strange thing — the thing I keep coming back to — is that the roster-keeping only lasted about a minute, and then it fell away, and what was left underneath was just her. My stranger. The woman who told me my kale was sulking. Who read my grandfather's line about the light not choosing its ships and carried it around like a stone in her pocket until it wore smooth.

She'd asked me for something no stranger had ever asked me for. *Forgive me for whatever I did.* It was an absurd request. It was the most reasonable request I'd ever heard. Both at once, the way real things are.

Because here's what I knew, sitting on those steps with her letter going soft in the sea damp: I knew exactly what it was to owe a forgiveness I couldn't deliver. I owed one to myself. Nine years of it, banked and accruing interest — for the way I'd stayed when I should have gone, or gone when I should have stayed, I've never fully sorted which; for the engagement I let everyone believe was broken *to* me rather than *by* me; for the novel I didn't write and the life I didn't live and the slow respectable calcification of a woman who called her cage a lighthouse because lighthouses are noble. If someone had stood on my step and said, *Cyrus Sterling, I forgive you for whatever you did,* without knowing a single detail, without the whole town's inventory of my choices in hand — I don't know that I'd have believed them. But I know I'd have wanted it so badly my teeth would have ached.

So I went inside, and I put the kettle on, and I sat at my grandfather's table with his ledger open beside me the way I always do when I write to her, because it feels less like lying to have him in the room, and I answered her.

*Stranger —*

*I burned nothing. I want that on the record before anything else, because I could hear you bracing for it all the way through the letter, that flinch you do at the end of paragraphs where you offer me the exit. You do it every time. You did it when you told me my heroine was a coward — "you can disagree, most people would" — and I disagreed, by the way, I disagreed completely, and I've been meaning to tell you that your cruelty is the most useful kindness anyone's done my writing in years. The heroine is a coward. I wrote her that way because I couldn't figure out how she'd forgive herself, and now I know it's because I couldn't figure out how I'd forgive myself, and a novelist who can't tell her own secrets from her characters' shouldn't be surprised when readers see through her. You saw through her. Thank you.*

*Now. The thing you asked.*

*I don't know what you did. I've spent the evening trying to guess and I've decided that's a betrayal, actually — you gave me the shape of it, you said, and the shape is enough, and trying to fill in the outline with my own guesses is just me putting my face on your wound. So I won't. Here is what I know instead: you left in the night with a baby growing in you and no one at the dock to wave you off. You raised a girl alone well enough that she asks questions, which means she feels safe asking them, which means you did something right in a life you insist on describing as wreckage. And you came back. People don't come back to the scene by accident. They come back to pay.*

*I can't absolve you. I want to be careful here, because I think absolution is a thing that gets handed out too easily and it curdles. What I can do is this: I can tell you what I'd want a stranger to tell me.*

*You were twenty-three. Twenty-three is not an excuse, it's a fact, and the fact is that at twenty-three almost everyone is running from something and the ones who aren't are running toward something and it takes years to learn the difference. You made a choice with the information and the fear and the people you had. It hurt people. Fine. Hurt is not the same as unforgivable, and shame is not the same as penance, and nine years of punishing yourself is not the same as making it right — it's just nine more years of the same choice, the leaving, performed inward. You said you want to stop being a person who leaves. Then here's your first act of staying: stay in this letter. Stay in the season. Come to the reveal, if you can stand it. Let someone know you. That's the penance, I think. Not the dark. The being known.*

*You asked me to say it like a stranger. All right. Stranger to stranger, no history in the teeth of it: whatever you did, I forgive you. Not because it was small. Because you carried it here and set it down in front of me and asked, and asking is the whole hard thing, and you did the whole hard thing. Whatever it was, I forgive you. There. It's said. It's yours. Do with it what you like — I'd suggest planting something over it, but I'm biased.*

*Now you have to let me be a coward in my own turn, because there's a thing I owe you too and I'm not ready. You'll get it before the season's out. That's a promise, and I keep my promises, which is the one virtue my family managed to breed into me along with the stubbornness and the salt air in the lungs.*

*The tomatoes did come in all at once. Forty pounds in three days. I've been giving them away at the church and to Odette, who has started leaving her door unlocked so I'll just put the bag inside, and to a family I don't know that moved into the old Pell place — there's a girl about six, I've seen her at the harbor, dark-haired, all elbows and questions, the kind of kid who'd take apart a crab to see how it works. She reminds me of you, if that's not strange to say. Of how you write. Like someone assembling a thing to find out what it's made of.*

*Tell your daughter the light doesn't get tired. Tell her it turns because turning is what it is, and nobody ever had to deserve it.*

*Come to the reveal. I'll be the one in the bad coat.*

*— C.*

I read it over twice. I almost crossed out the part about the girl at the harbor — it felt like too much, too specific, like I was showing off my small town at her — and then I left it, because she'd asked me for something true, and that was true, and I was tired of editing myself down to the safe size. The whole season had been teaching me that. Every letter I sent her went out a little less armored than the last, and every one came back with the armor stripped a little further off her, and somewhere in there — I couldn't have said when — the correspondence had stopped being a program and started being a load-bearing wall.

I sealed the envelope. I put it in the box for the morning boat, and then I took it back out, because the evening boat hadn't gone yet, and I ran the coast path to the harbor with my bad knee complaining the whole way and got it on the *Petrel* with four minutes to spare, and old Manousos looked at me like I'd lost my mind and I said, "Important letter," and he said, "They're all important, that's the trouble," and stamped it anyway.

* * *

Walking back, the light came on behind me — six forty-five, the photoeye tripping, the beam swinging out over the darkening water with that slow patient sweep I've heard in my sleep my whole life. I stopped on the path and watched it turn. Three seconds on the water, three seconds on the dark, three seconds on the water.

*The light doesn't choose which ships to save.*

I'd written that to her in September, quoting my grandfather, and I'd thought it was about the boats. Standing there in the cold coming on, with her letter folded against my ribs and my answer already out of my hands and on its way to hers, I understood it was about something else. It was about the keeper. It was about what you do when someone steers for your rocks on purpose — whether you turn them away or let the light fall on them anyway, steady, unearned, whether they deserve it or not.

I'd let it fall. I didn't even know who I'd let it fall on.

That was the part that should have frightened me, I suppose — that I'd handed a stranger the cleanest, largest thing I had, forgiveness with no name attached, and that some part of me had done it gladly, had been *waiting* to do it, the way the garden waits all season for the one week everything comes ripe at once.

But what actually kept me up that night, what I wrote three false starts about in the ledger before giving up and going to bed, was smaller and stranger. It was the girl. The dark-haired one at the harbor, all elbows and questions. I'd seen her twice that week alone — once buying penny candy at the chandlery, once standing at the seawall with her mother, and I'd noticed the mother too, the way you notice anyone new, a flicker of *who's that* before the tide of the day took it out again.

I couldn't shake the feeling I'd seen that mother before. Not recently. Years ago. That I'd known her face back when it was younger, back when it belonged to someone I —

But that was nothing. That was the town doing what the town does, recycling faces, everyone in Port Merrow related to everyone eventually. I fell asleep with the beam sweeping the ceiling and the letter gone and a strange lightness in my chest, like a room with the furniture finally sold.

I never once thought to connect the girl at the seawall to the daughter in the letter. Why would I? My stranger had left nine years ago. And the woman at the seawall had only just arrived.

Hadn't she?

I'd have sworn to it, that night. I'd have sworn it on my grandfather's ledger, on the light itself.

I was wrong. I just didn't know yet how wrong, or how close to home the answer had been standing all along — close enough, in fact, that I'd handed her a bag of my tomatoes and told her the soil at the old Pell place ran clay-heavy and she should add sand.
