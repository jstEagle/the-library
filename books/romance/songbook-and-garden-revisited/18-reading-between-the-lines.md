## 18. Reading Between the Lines

The reply came on a Thursday, in an envelope with a lighthouse postmark, and Wren made herself wait until Juniper was asleep before she opened it.

This was discipline. This was new. For the first month of the correspondence she'd torn the envelopes open on the library steps, in the car, once standing in the checkout line at the grocery store, holding up the whole line while she skimmed. But lately the letters had gotten heavy — not in weight, in gravity — and she'd learned to open them the way you open a door to a room where something might be waiting.

She made tea she didn't drink. She sat at the kitchen table under the bare bulb because she still hadn't chosen fixtures for most of the house, and she read.

*You asked what I'm afraid of,* her pen pal wrote. *Fine. Here it is. I'm afraid I've spent nine years being the wrong kind of faithful. Faithful to a house, a job, a grave. Faithful is just stubborn with better lighting. I'm afraid that if I ever stopped keeping things — the light, the garden, the grudge — I'd find out there was nothing underneath holding the shape.*

Wren read that paragraph four times.

Then she did the thing she'd been circling for a week, the thing she'd told herself was paranoid, the thing she'd promised Odette-without-knowing-Odette she would never do.

She went to the drawer where she kept the letters, and she took out the grocery list.

Not a letter. A grocery list. Written on the back of a paint chip at the hardware store, nine days ago, in the four minutes she'd spent trapped in aisle three with Cyrus Sterling, making small talk with the one woman in Port Merrow she had no right to make small talk with.

She'd written: *eggs, milk, the blue paint if it's on sale, Juniper's markers, coffee — real coffee, not the sad tin.*

And she'd kept the list. Why had she kept the list? She'd told herself it was because the paint chip had the color code on it. The color code was also on the receipt, which was in the trash, which had gone out with the recycling. She'd kept the list because her hands had been shaking when she left the store and she'd needed to hold something Cyrus Sterling had touched.

Now she laid it flat on the table beside the letter and looked at the two handwritings side by side.

The letter was careful. The list was quick. But handwriting is like a walk — you can disguise your pace, not your gait. The same hard crossbar on the T. The same way the R kicked its leg out like it was impatient to be done with itself. The same slightly crooked S that leaned left, against the direction of the whole word, like a person who'd learned to write left-handed over right-handed training and never quite surrendered.

*Faithful is just stubborn with better lighting.*

At the hardware store, standing between the paint and the putty knives, Cyrus had said — Wren closed her eyes and it played back with terrible fidelity — *"I keep the light because somebody has to, and because if I stopped I'd have to figure out what I'm for."*

The same sentence. Not word for word. Word for bone.

Wren sat very still in the bare-bulb kitchen and let the certainty come, because it was going to come anyway and it was better to meet it standing.

It came like cold water. Not a wave — water rising. First the ankles: the handwriting. Then the knees: the phrasing, the *keeping things*, the grudge mentioned in a letter that arrived the same week Cyrus had stood in aisle three and said, with a small bitter laugh, *"I've got a grudge I've been maintaining like a hedge. Keeps my hands busy."* Then the waist, the chest: the garden. Her pen pal had written, two letters ago, about clearing her grandfather's ruined kitchen garden, about the apple tree with the split trunk, about the *third stair in the keeper's house that sings under your foot.* Wren had stood at that garden gate eleven days ago and watched Cyrus Sterling come down a path she'd described to Wren in ink, and Cyrus had stepped on the third stair, and it had sung, and Wren had felt the note go through her like a tuning fork struck against her own secret.

Then the water closed over her head, and she let it, and she thought, with a clarity that felt almost like relief:

*It's her. It's been her. It was always going to be her.*

* * *

Here is what Wren had expected certainty to feel like, in the years she'd rehearsed disasters in the dark: it would feel like a verdict. A door slamming. *Run.*

It didn't. It felt like standing in the shower after a long day. It felt like the moment after you finally say the true thing out loud, even if only to yourself, and your body unclenches one muscle at a time.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been refusing to do: the woman at the hardware store — frozen, polite, devastating, nine years of ice in every syllable — had also written to Wren, anonymously, for two months. Had written *I'm afraid if I stopped keeping things I'd find nothing underneath.* Had written, after the storm letter, *I don't know why I told you all that. I've never told anyone. I think it's because you're not anyone. I think it's because you're safe.*

*You're not anyone. You're safe.*

Wren laughed out loud in the empty kitchen, one short bark that startled her, and pressed her hand over her mouth.

Safe. She was the least safe person in Cyrus Sterling's life. She was the wound with a forwarding address.

And yet. And yet Cyrus had told her things. The night of the first gale, when the backup systems failed and Cyrus had sat up all night in the lamp room the way her grandfather used to, she had written it down and mailed it to a stranger, and the stranger — Wren, guilty, shaking Wren — had written back something tender, something true, and Cyrus had written back: *You have a way of seeing people that I suspect you'd hate to have turned on yourself.*

She'd hated it. She'd been right to hate it. It had seen her exactly.

Wren got up and paced the kitchen, three steps, turn, three steps, the way she did when Juniper was at a sleepover and the house was too quiet. The bare bulb swung slightly on its cord and threw her shadow around the room like a slow metronome.

Options. She was good at options. She'd had nine years of practice reducing her life to options.

Option one: stop writing. Fold the correspondence closed, claim the season got busy, let the program lapse. She'd almost done it once before, in October, the week she realized who her pen pal must be. She'd written the withdrawal letter and everything. She remembered it — she'd kept it too, in the drawer, because she apparently kept everything now, she was a curator of her own cowardice. The letter had said *circumstances prevent me from continuing.* Circumstances. Six feet tall, gray-eyed, with a grudge maintained like a hedge.

She hadn't mailed it. Why hadn't she mailed it?

Because of the soil advice. Because Cyrus had complained about the garden and Wren — who had spent three years on a farm outside Ashford learning things with her hands because her head was unbearable company — had written back about drainage and cold frames and the difference between soil that's tired and soil that's poisoned, and Cyrus had written back: *I followed your instructions exactly, which is unlike me, and the bed drained, which is also unlike me. What else do you know?*

*What else do you know.*

Nobody had asked Wren that in nine years. Daniel had asked her to leave. Her mother had asked her to come to her senses. The farm manager had asked her to lift with her knees. But *what else do you know* — that was a question you asked someone you were beginning to think might be worth knowing. And Wren had sat on the floor of her half-painted living room and answered it for four pages.

Option two: keep writing, but carefully. Pull back. Get bland. Become a pleasant anonymous acquaintance until the season ended and the reveal event came and went unattended. She could skip it. She could claim illness. The program had no enforcement arm; it had Odette and a card catalog.

But she'd already tried bland, in the early letters, and Cyrus had called it out with a novelist's scalpel: *You write like someone holding the door of herself open exactly one inch. I keep waiting for the hinge to give.* If Wren pulled back now, Cyrus — sharp, wounded, watchful Cyrus — would feel the temperature drop and would know, the way you know a storm is coming from the gulls. And worse: Wren would feel it too. She'd already had two months of the hinge giving. You couldn't un-give a hinge.

Option three.

Wren stopped pacing. The bulb stopped swinging. Her shadow settled.

Option three was the one she'd been carrying around since the hardware store, the one that had woken her at 3 a.m. twice this week: keep writing. Knowingly. With her whole chest. Knowing it was Cyrus on the other end, and knowing what that meant — that every kindness Cyrus mailed to a stranger was a kindness Wren had no right to, that she was receiving mercy under false pretenses, that the forgiveness in the harvest letter —

Oh God, the harvest letter.

Wren went back to the table. Her own last letter was in the outbox of her memory, and she could recite it: *I wronged someone here. I was young and I was a coward and I did damage I've never repaired. I don't deserve kindness, but I'm asking for it anyway, which might be the most selfish thing I've ever done.*

And Cyrus — not knowing, thinking she was comforting some anonymous stranger with a generic sin — had written back: *Whatever you did, you did it at an age when you didn't yet know what you were made of. That's not an excuse. It's just the order things happen in. You learn what you're made of, and then you spend the rest of your life deciding what to make. I'd bet the farm you're making something better now.*

*I'd bet the farm.* Cyrus had never told her pen pal about the farm. Wren had told her, in letter nine — the Ashford farm, the three years of dirt therapy. Which meant Cyrus remembered. Which meant Cyrus read closely. Which meant —

Which meant that the woman who had every reason to hate Wren Calloway had spent an evening writing tenderness to her, by hand, sealing it, walking it to the postbox in the wind, and had meant every word.

Wren sat down again. Her legs had opinions.

* * *

She should have felt like a thief. She kept checking for the thief feeling, the way you probe a sore tooth. It wasn't there. What was there was something much more dangerous.

She read the letter again, slowly, the way you'd reread a message from someone you were falling for, because — she made herself say it, in the privacy of her own skull, where Juniper couldn't hear and Odette couldn't deduce and the whole town of Port Merrow couldn't watch —

because she was.

Falling. For Cyrus Sterling. For the wronged party. For her victim, her enemy, the woman whose engagement she'd ended, the sister of the man who'd told her to *take care of it, Wren, I can't* in a voice she still heard in the drain-gurgle of every bad plumbing moment. She was falling for the woman who had looked through her at the hardware store like she was a smudge on glass, and who had written to her, unknowing, *I'd bet the farm you're making something better now.*

It was obscene. It was probably, on some level, pathological. Wren catalogued the objections and found she didn't care about a single one.

Because here was the thing about the letters, the thing she'd been refusing to name: the woman in the letters and the woman in the hardware store were the same woman, and Wren loved — fine, *liked*, *was intrigued by*, she could hear her own hedging and hated it — the same woman in both rooms. The ice at the store wasn't a different Cyrus. It was the same stubborn faithful keeper, holding her door open one inch. And Wren had spent two months on the other side of that inch, in the warm, and she could not now pretend the cold was the whole story.

She knew things about Cyrus Sterling that no one in Port Merrow knew. She knew she was afraid of being only a keeper. She knew her novel was stalled at chapter nine because she couldn't decide whether her heroine deserved the ending. She knew she'd chosen the lighthouse over a life abroad and had let everyone believe she'd been left, because the truth — that she'd frozen, that she'd chosen duty like a hiding place — was heavier than the lie. Wren had read that confession twice and thought: *I know exactly what that's like. I built my whole exile on a similar lie.* They were the same. That was the horror of it. That was the whole horror: they had both frozen, both fled into duty or distance, both let other people write their story, and both were, in these letters, quietly trying to take the pen back.

* * *

Juniper woke at eleven, as she sometimes did, with a bad dream or a full bladder, and padded into the kitchen in her sock feet with her hair standing up like a dandelion gone to seed.

"Mum? Why are you up?"

"Just finishing a letter, love."

"To your friend?" Juniper climbed onto the chair beside her, all elbows and sleep-warmth, and peered at the page upside down. "The one at the lighthouse?"

Wren's heart did a thing it would need to do more of before this was over — a complicated gymnastic thing. "What friend at the lighthouse?"

"The one you walk past the harbor to mail letters to." Juniper said it the way children say obvious things, with the mild impatience of the unfooled. "I saw you. You always stand there for ages before you put it in."

"I'm checking the address," Wren said, which was even true.

"You stand there for ages," Juniper repeated, unconvinced, and then, mercifully, her attention slid sideways to the grocery list on the table. "Is that the blue paint? Can I have the blue paint for my room? Not the whole room. Just one wall. Like a wave."

"We'll see." Wren smoothed the girl's hair down. It sprang back up. Everything in her life sprang back up; that was the problem; nothing stayed smoothed.

Juniper went back to bed. Wren sat alone with the letter and the list and the bare bulb and the certainty, which had stopped feeling like cold water and started feeling like ballast — something heavy in the hold that kept the boat upright in weather.

* * *

She wrote the reply at midnight, and this time she didn't perform.

That was the difference, and she could feel it happening under her hands. For two months she'd written as a stranger — which had meant, without her noticing, writing as a version of herself, a curated self, the self that gave good soil advice and sharp editorial notes and kept her sins in the abstract. Now the stranger had a face. Gray eyes, a laugh she'd heard exactly once in nine years and remembered exactly, a way of standing at a garden gate like the gate might charge admission.

Now she wrote to *Cyrus*, even though the letter still said nothing but the truth.

*You wrote that faithful is just stubborn with better lighting, and I've been thinking about it all week, because I think you're wrong, and I think you know you're wrong, and I think you wrote it because you wanted someone to argue with you. Consider this the argument. Faithful is a choice you make every day, and you can choose it honestly or you can choose it as a hiding place, and the difference isn't visible from the outside. Only the keeper knows. So: are you keeping the light, or hiding in it? Don't answer me. Answer you.*

She read it back and flinched. Too far. That was the kind of thing that would make a wary woman bolt — or make her look closer, and Wren could not afford to be looked at closely by Cyrus Sterling, not yet, not until —

Not until what? Not until she confessed? Not until the reveal event in six weeks, when Odette would read two names off cards in the library and the whole room would turn?

Wren thought about the reveal event with the calm of a woman reading her own prognosis. Six weeks. She could write twenty more letters in six weeks. She could be twenty times more known by then. And when the cards were read — *Cyrus Sterling, you've been writing to* — Cyrus would stand up and look across the room and find her, and every letter would rearrange itself in Cyrus's memory at the speed of betrayal, and *I'd bet the farm* would curdle into *she knew, she knew and she let me say it all.*

Unless she confessed first. She kept deciding to confess first. She'd decided it at the hardware store, in the parking lot, gripping the steering wheel. She'd decided it again after the harvest letter. She had a talent for deciding it in private and a corresponding talent for losing her nerve in the presence of the person who needed to hear it.

*There's something you should know,* she'd almost said at the garden gate, eleven days ago. She'd gotten as far as the doorway of the sentence and then Cyrus had handed her the pruning shears and asked her opinion on the grafting, and the sentence had died in Wren's throat like a bird hitting glass.

She picked up the pen again. Below the argument about faithfulness, before she could stop herself, her hand wrote:

*P.S. I keep every letter of yours. I don't know why I'm telling you that. Yes I do. I'm telling you because you should know that whatever you say here, it lands somewhere it's kept.*

She stared at the postscript for a long time. It was the most honest thing she'd written in nine years, and it was addressed to a woman who didn't know it was honest, who would read it as a stranger's kindness, who would maybe — Wren let herself imagine it, just for a second, the way you let yourself look at the sun — who would maybe read it and feel what Wren felt: that being kept was its own kind of love, and that love had already started, against all sense, against all justice, in the middle of a lie.

She sealed the envelope. She wrote the program's box number in her own handwriting, which was apparently no longer disguised, which had maybe never been disguised well enough, which Cyrus Sterling might already be comparing to grocery lists of her own.

Six weeks, she thought. Twenty letters. One confession she kept deciding to make.

She'd mail it in the morning. She'd stand at the harbor postbox for ages, checking the address, and if a certain gray-eyed keeper happened to pass on her morning rounds — well.

Wren turned off the bare bulb and stood in the dark, holding the letter against her sternum like ballast, like a kept thing, like the first honest thing she'd owned in nine years.

Outside, far down the coast, the lighthouse beam swept the water, turned, and swept again. Patient as a heartbeat. Faithful, she thought, and not stubborn at all.

She just had to survive six weeks of her own handwriting.
