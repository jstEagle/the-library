## 20. The Program's Deadline

The notice went up on the library corkboard on a Tuesday, between a flyer for the church rummage sale and a laminated warning about jellyfish.

**HARBORLIGHT ANONYMOUS PEN-PAL PROGRAM — SEASON'S END REVEAL EVENT. Saturday, November 14th, 7 PM, Harborlight Community Room. All participants will meet their correspondents. Refreshments served. Attendance strongly encouraged.**

Odette pinned it there herself, Cyrus would learn later, with a certain theatrical flourish she probably practiced in the mirror.

Cyrus saw it three days after it went up, because she only came into the library when she absolutely had to, and she had to return a book on grafting that was two weeks overdue and that she had, in fact, already read twice and copied out passages from into a notebook she kept in the lighthouse kitchen. She was standing at the corkboard with her arms crossed, reading the notice for the third time, when Odette materialized beside her with the smugness of a woman who has been waiting for exactly this moment.

"Well," Odette said. "Look at that."

"I'm looking."

"Six weeks."

"I can count, Odette."

"You're going to go, obviously." Odette said it as fact, not question, and leaned against the corkboard like she owned it, which in a spiritual sense she did, since she'd been volunteering there since high school and considered the corkboard an extension of her own personality. "You have to go. It's the whole point of the program. The reveal is the *payoff*, Cyrus. Otherwise you've just been writing love letters to a wall."

"They are not love letters."

"Mm." Odette examined her nails. "What do you call them, then?"

"Correspondence."

"With someone whose soil advice made you cry a little? I heard about the apple trees. You grafted them. *You.* The woman who once told me gardening was just slow disappointment."

"That was about my brother."

"Same thing," Odette said, and then, because she was not entirely without mercy: "Look. Whoever they are, they signed up too. They knew the rules. Six weeks, and then you stand in a room and find out if the person on paper is the person in front of you. That's the deal. That's what makes it honest."

Cyrus looked at the notice again. *Refreshments served.* As though this were a potluck and not a potential detonation. As though whoever she'd been writing to all season — the person who knew about her grandfather, about the novel, about the night of the storm when she'd admitted, in ink, that she was afraid of becoming nothing but a keeper of other people's things — would simply be standing there under the fluorescent lights next to the cider urn.

She thought, briefly, wildly: *what if I don't go.*

But she already knew she'd go. She'd known since the first letter, maybe. Since the handwriting that felt familiar in a way she couldn't place and hadn't let herself try to place, because placing it would mean something she wasn't ready to know.

"Fine," she said. "I'll go."

"Good." Odette pushed off the corkboard. Then, casually, in the tone of a woman setting a small fire and walking away: "You could always tell them first, you know. Before the event. Lots of people do. Beats finding out in front of an audience."

Cyrus stared at her.

"Who says I haven't thought of that?"

"Nobody," Odette said. "That's the problem."

* * *

Wren found out from Juniper.

"Mum. There's a paper at school about the letter friends." Juniper was upside down off the couch, hair hanging, holding the flyer with both hands the way she held everything — with total commitment. "Miss Priya says everybody meets everybody at the end. In the *big room*. With snacks."

Wren took the flyer. Read it. Felt the floor tilt slightly and corrected for it by sitting down very carefully, as though the couch were a boat.

*All participants will meet their correspondents.*

Of course. Of course there was a reveal. She'd read the program rules nine months ago — well, skimmed them, honestly, in the library while Juniper pulled picture books off shelves — and she remembered something about *a season* and something about *confidentiality until the close of the program,* and she had filed the rest under *later,* which was where Wren Calloway filed most things that frightened her. Later. After the paint dried. After the house was livable. After she figured out how to be a person again.

Later had arrived, wearing a flyer and promising snacks.

"Mum, are you sick?"

"No, sweetheart."

"You went gray. Miss Priya says people go gray when they're scared."

"Miss Priya sounds very wise." Wren folded the flyer in half, then in half again, until it was a small dense square she could hold in her fist. "Juniper. If you met your letter friend — your pen pal — would you want to meet them at a party? Or would you rather tell them who you were yourself, first? Just you."

Juniper considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. "Just me. Because parties are loud and then they'd be looking at you."

"*Then they'd be looking at you,*" Wren repeated softly. Out of the mouths of six-year-olds. "Exactly, love."

"So are you gonna tell yours first?"

The question hung there, small and terrible. Wren looked at her daughter — at the daughter who drew lighthouses on every available surface, who had no idea that the woman her mother wrote to every week was the woman her mother had wronged so thoroughly that the wrong still had its own weather system — and said, "I'm going to try."

"When?"

"Soon."

"That's what you said about fixing the porch step."

"This is different."

"How?"

"It's scarier," Wren said, and Juniper nodded, satisfied, because in Juniper's cosmology scary things were simply true things that hadn't happened yet.

That night, after Juniper was asleep, Wren sat at the kitchen table with the box of letters. Not the ones she'd received — those lived in her nightstand drawer, tied with the ribbon from a dress she never wore anymore — but her drafts. The failed ones. The letters she'd written and never sent, going back years, back before Port Merrow, back to the apartment in the city where she'd written to Daniel and burned it. She kept them all now. It was a habit she'd developed somewhere along the way, this archiving of her own cowardice, proof that she had always meant to be brave eventually.

She took out a fresh sheet of paper. *Dear Friend,* she wrote. And then:

*I need to tell you something, and I need to tell it before someone else does.*

Her pen hovered. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and gave up.

She wrote: *Nine years ago I—*

And stopped. Because here was the thing she hadn't let herself understand until this exact moment, sitting in her inherited house with her daughter asleep upstairs: it wasn't the reveal she feared. It was the look on Cyrus Sterling's face when the name landed. Not anger. Anger she could survive; she'd rehearsed anger for nine years. It was the *recalculation* — the moment Cyrus would take every kind thing she'd ever written, every piece of grace she'd handed over blind, and reprice it. *Oh. It was pity. Oh. It was guilt.* Every generous word suddenly counterfeit.

Better, some cowardly part of her whispered, to be revealed by the librarian, by the process, by fate — anything but to hand Cyrus the knife herself and watch her decide whether to use it.

Wren looked at what she'd written. *I need to tell you something, and I need to tell it before someone else does. Nine years ago I—*

She folded it. She didn't burn it. She put it in the box with the others, in the section she privately labeled, in her head, *almost.*

* * *

Cyrus tried too. That same week, actually, which neither of them would know for years — that they'd sat down within days of each other, both reaching for the same confession across the same dark water.

She did it properly, at the desk in the lamp room, because if you were going to fail at honesty you might as well fail at altitude. The light turned above her, steady, indifferent. Below, the garden lay in its first frost-blackened rows, the grafted cuttings wrapped and waiting for spring, looking — she allowed — a little hopeful.

*Dear Friend,* she wrote.

*I've started this letter four times. My pen pal deserves better than what I'm about to do, which is tell you something sideways instead of straight, but here goes:*

*There's a person in this town I've hated for nine years. I built a whole life on top of the hating, the way you build a house on bad soil and pretend the cracks are character. And lately — and you're the reason, which is inconvenient — the hate has started to feel less like a foundation and more like a weight I've been carrying for someone else's benefit.*

*I think I might be ready to put it down. I think I might be ready to forgive her. I wanted you to know first, because you're the only one who's ever asked me the right questions about it.*

*Anyway. See you at the reveal, apparently. Save me a seat near the exits.*

*C.*

She read it over. It was good. It was honest-adjacent, warm, even brave in its way. It was also, she realized as she sealed the envelope, completely evasive — a confession about forgiving someone without confessing the forgiveness, a letter that cleared its throat beautifully and said nothing. She had done the thing she did. She had chosen duty-shaped words over true ones, the way she'd chosen the lighthouse over Lisbon, the way she'd chosen everyone's expectations over her own novel for eleven years.

She mailed it anyway. Some letters were practice. You wrote your way toward the real one.

* * *

They ran into each other at the hardware store the following Thursday, because Port Merrow had exactly one hardware store and it functioned as the town's involuntary meeting ground.

Wren was buying weatherstripping. Cyrus was buying burlap to wrap the young trees. They saw each other across the aisle of paint thinner and winter supplies and performed the now-familiar ritual: a pause, a nod, a careful approach, two women conversing politely about insulation and frost like diplomats from countries with a long war behind them and a fragile new treaty nobody had signed.

"Heard about the reveal event," Cyrus said, testing.

Wren's hand tightened on the weatherstripping. "From Juniper. Apparently there are snacks."

"There are always snacks. Odette believes no emotional crisis can't be improved by a cheese cube."

A laugh escaped Wren before she could stop it — quick, startled, real — and Cyrus watched it happen and felt something in her chest turn over, the way it had been turning over more and more lately, in ways she refused to examine under direct light.

"Are you going?" Wren asked.

"I said I would." Cyrus picked up a roll of twine, put it down. "Are you?"

"I—" Wren looked at the weatherstripping like it contained instructions. "I haven't decided."

"You should come," Cyrus said, and heard herself say it, and didn't take it back. "It's better than it sounds. Odette runs it. There's a slideshow."

"A slideshow."

"Last year she did a montage of the town's cats set to Enya. People wept."

Wren laughed again, longer this time, and for a moment they were just two women in a hardware store laughing about a cat slideshow, and the nine years got very quiet, and Cyrus thought: *tell her. Tell her now. Tell her you're the pen pal, tell her you know about the letters, tell her—*

And Wren, in the same instant, thinking: *she's right there. Say it. Say 'it's me.' Three syllables. You've survived worse—*

Neither said it. What Cyrus said was, "Well. Six weeks." What Wren said was, "See you there, maybe." And they paid for their separate purchases at opposite ends of the counter and walked out into the cold separately, each carrying a confession home unopened, like groceries neither had the appetite for.

* * *

That night, two letters crossed in the mail.

One said: *I think I might be ready to forgive her.*

The other said: *I need to tell you something, and I need to tell it before someone else does.*

Both writers opened their mailboxes the next morning with their hearts in their throats, and both found — nothing. The postal delay that would haunt them both had already begun, a plain brown envelope sitting in a sorting facility forty miles away, patient as a landmine.

Cyrus stood at her mailbox in the wind and told herself the silence meant nothing. Mail was slow in autumn. Everyone knew that.

Wren stood at hers and told herself the silence meant everything. Of course it did. Grace always arrived too late; that was practically its definition.

Six weeks, said the flyer on the corkboard.

Five weeks and six days, said the clock inside each of them.

And in a sorting facility forty miles away, two envelopes lay side by side in a canvas sack, saying everything, reaching no one.
