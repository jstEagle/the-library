## 22. Juniper's School Play

The gymnasium of Port Merrow Elementary smelled like floor wax and construction paper, and Wren Calloway stood at the back of it with her heart in her throat.

Juniper was a starfish.

Not a lead starfish — there were no leads in the first-grade pageant, a fact the teacher had explained twice at orientation with the firmness of someone who had survived parent email chains before — but a starfish nonetheless, one of nine, positioned third from the left in the tide pool scene. She wore a cardboard headband with five painted points that kept sliding over her left eye, and she had already adjusted it four times during the warm-up song, each adjustment more theatrical than the last.

Wren loved her so much it felt like a structural problem, like something load-bearing giving way inside her chest.

*Third from the left,* she thought. *My daughter is third from the left.*

She had a phone full of photos already and the play hadn't started. Juniper mid-wave. Juniper adjusting the headband. Juniper catching sight of Wren across the gym and waving with her whole arm, whole body, until Mrs. Okafor gently turned her back around.

"You're going to wear out your camera," said Odette Fennimore, materializing beside her with the ease of a woman who had attended every school event in this town for forty years, first as a student, then as a substitute teacher, now as a professional aunt and busybody. "Save some memory for the actual performance."

"I'm saving it all."

"Sentimental." Odette said it like an accusation and meant it like a compliment. She was holding a program folded into quarters and a thermos of coffee she would not share. "You know I'm only here for my nephew."

"Of course."

"He's the lobster."

"I heard. He's very excited about his claws."

"He's been practicing his claws for three weeks." Odette sipped her coffee. "He told me if anyone laughs at his claws he's going to quit theater forever. I told him that's called being an artist."

Wren laughed, and Odette looked at her sideways with that quick librarian's attention she sometimes deployed, the look that made Wren feel like a misfiled card. But whatever Odette saw or suspected, she let it go, turning instead to survey the crowd.

And that was when Wren saw her.

Cyrus Sterling came through the gym doors the way weather comes through a window left open — all at once, changing the temperature of everything. She wore a coat Wren recognized from the hardware store, wool the color of wet slate, and her hair was down, which it almost never was at the garden gate. She scanned the room with the wary expression of someone who had been talked into attending and was still deciding whether to forgive the person who'd done the talking.

Odette raised one hand. Cyrus saw it, sighed visibly even from thirty feet away, and started toward them.

*Don't sit here,* Wren thought, with no conviction whatsoever. *Anywhere else. Sit anywhere else.*

Cyrus sat directly beside her. The folding chair creaked under them both like a shared confession.

"Odette," Cyrus said.

"Cyrus." Odette beamed at them both with the transparent delight of a woman watching two cats she'd introduced begin to circle each other. "Wren, you know Cyrus. Cyrus, Wren Calloway. She just moved into the old Pruitt house on Larkspur Lane."

"We've met," they said, nearly in unison, and then neither of them said anything else, which was somehow louder.

The lights dimmed. Saved by the bell, literally — a handbell rang backstage, and twenty-six children in sea-creature costumes shuffled into formation while a piano stumbled through an introduction.

* * *

The play was called *The Tide Pool Comes Alive*, and it was, objectively, a catastrophe.

The narrator — a small girl with enormous glasses and total command of the room — lost her place on page two and simply began again from the top, which no adult corrected because no adult wanted to discourage that kind of confidence. The lobster, Odette's nephew, pinched a sea urchin during the second scene and had to be separated. A wave made of blue fabric held by four kindergartners collapsed entirely, taking two anemones down with it.

And Juniper — third from the left, cardboard crown slipping — performed every single movement with the ferocious sincerity of a child who has decided that this moment matters and will not be talked out of it. When the tide pool "came alive," she stretched all five points of her arms wide and spun in a slow circle, mouth open, absolutely transfigured, and Wren felt tears arrive without permission.

She wiped them fast, before anyone could see. Too late.

"She gets that from you," Cyrus said quietly.

Wren turned. Cyrus wasn't looking at her; she was looking at the stage, at Juniper spinning, her profile lit by the wash of the gymnasium lights. Her voice had come out low, almost private, as if it had escaped before she could stop it.

"Gets what?"

"That." Cyrus nodded at the stage. "The way she commits. Like the whole thing depends on her." A pause. "It's a good quality. In a starfish."

Something in Wren's chest went loose and dangerous. "She practices everything," Wren heard herself say. "Everything. Spelling words, cartwheels, how to whistle. She'll do a thing fifty times until it's right, and then once it's right she never doubts it again." She watched her daughter hold her starfish pose through what should have been a rest in the music, trembling slightly with effort, refusing to drop it. "I don't know where she got it. It isn't me. I've never been sure of anything in my life."

"That's not true," Cyrus said.

It came out fast, unguarded, and then Cyrus seemed to hear herself, and her jaw tightened, and she turned her attention back to the stage with the studied interest of someone who had just stepped on ice and was waiting to see if it would hold.

But she'd said it. *That's not true.* Plainly, certainly, like a fact she'd carried around for years and finally set down on the seat between them.

Wren stared at the stage and didn't see any of it. Nine years ago, a girl named Wren Calloway had stood in a kitchen on Harbor Road and been told, kindly, terribly, that she didn't belong in this family's future, and she had believed it so completely that she'd built a whole life on top of the belief. And here was Cyrus Sterling, who had every reason to confirm it, saying the opposite into the dark of a school gymnasium like it cost her nothing.

It hadn't cost her nothing. Wren knew enough about Cyrus Sterling now — from letters signed with a false name, from a garden, from cider on lighthouse steps — to know that nothing cost that woman nothing. Everything was weighed. Everything was paid for.

*She means it,* Wren thought. *God help me, she means it.*

Onstage, the tide pool sang its finale. Juniper hit a note that was technically not any note at all, with total conviction.

* * *

Afterward there was punch, which was warm, and cookies, which were gone, and a scrum of parents near the stage that Wren navigated with Juniper on her hip, the cardboard starfish crown still jammed over one eye because Juniper refused to remove it. "I'm keeping it forever," Juniper announced. "Even in the bath."

"That seems unwise," Wren said.

"You're unwise."

"Where did you learn that word?"

"Grandma's shows."

They were making their way toward the doors when Juniper went rigid against Wren's shoulder and pointed with the imperiousness of royalty.

"Mum! That's the lighthouse lady!"

Every parent within earshot turned. Wren closed her eyes briefly. "Juniper—"

But Cyrus was already crossing the room toward them, and there was nowhere on earth Wren could have gone that would have helped.

"The lighthouse lady," Cyrus repeated. She crouched down to Juniper's level, which put her face-to-face with the cardboard crown and the paint smudge on Juniper's cheek. "Is that what they're calling me now?"

"At school," Juniper said solemnly. "Tommy said you live all alone in a tower and you shoot fire out of the top."

"It's a lamp, actually. Not fire. And I don't live alone." A pause Wren felt in her teeth. "I have a cat."

"What's its name?"

"Captain."

"Because he drives the boat?"

"There's no boat. Because he acts like he's in charge."

Juniper considered this with the gravity of a scholar. Then: "Can I see him?"

"June," Wren said, "we don't invite ourselves places."

But Cyrus was looking up at Wren over the small head between them, and something passed across her face that Wren couldn't name — something that had softened since September, since the garden gate, since the shed, since all the almosts stacking up between them like unread mail. "The Captain receives visitors," Cyrus said. "Sundays are best. He's less judgmental before noon."

"Sunday," Juniper repeated, sealing the contract. "Before noon."

"Juniper," Wren said again, weaker.

But Cyrus had straightened up now, and they were face to face with nothing between them, and the gymnasium noise seemed to recede the way water does before a wave. Up close Wren could see that Cyrus had dressed for this — not for Wren, exactly, but for public, for town, for the effort of appearing like a person who did things — and that under the effort she looked tired in a way Wren recognized, the tiredness of someone who had spent years being strong in an empty building.

"You were good tonight," Cyrus said. "Both of you."

"I stood in the back and cried."

"I know. I saw."

"You *saw*—"

"Third row," Cyrus said. "Good sightline." And then, before Wren could assemble a response to that, she glanced down at Juniper, and her voice changed — went quieter, rougher at the edges. "She looks like you. When you laugh."

Wren stopped breathing.

"When I—" she managed.

"You used to laugh at Daniel's jokes," Cyrus said. "All of them. Even the bad ones. Especially the bad ones." Her eyes stayed steady, and there was no blade in it, which was somehow worse and better than anything Wren had braced for. "I haven't thought about that in years. Tonight I did. It didn't hurt the way I expected it to."

"Cyrus—"

"It's fine." And Cyrus smiled — small, real, exhausted — and touched the cardboard point of Juniper's crown with one finger, a gesture so gentle it undid something in Wren permanently. "Sunday, then. Before noon. Bring the crown."

She walked away across the gymnasium, slate-gray coat, unhurried, and Odette fell into step beside her immediately, talking, and Cyrus let her, and the two of them went out through the double doors into the cold.

"Mum," Juniper said into Wren's neck, drowsy suddenly, the adrenaline of performance draining out of her small body. "Was that a friend of yours?"

Wren stood in the emptying gymnasium, holding her whole world in her arms, and answered honestly, because her daughter deserved honesty even when her mother couldn't manage it.

"I think she might be," Wren said. "I'm still finding out."

* * *

That night, after Juniper was asleep with the starfish crown on the pillow beside her ("just for tonight, forever"), Wren sat at the kitchen table of the old Pruitt house among the boxes she still hadn't unpacked, and wrote a letter she would not send.

*Dear stranger,* she wrote. *Today my daughter was a starfish, and a woman I have no right to call anything told me my daughter laughs like I used to. She remembered how I laugh. She carried it nine years and set it down gently, like it was worth keeping.*

*I keep thinking about the rules of this program. One season. No names. At the end, we reveal ourselves, and whatever we built either survives the light or doesn't. Six weeks now, maybe less. I should tell you who I am before the library does it for me, in front of everyone, under fluorescent lights, with punch.*

*Here is what stops me: I know who you are. I've known for months. I know the way you write about your grandfather's garden is the way you talk about it when you don't know I'm listening. I know your handwriting from grocery lists and a hardware store receipt. And knowing hasn't made me stop writing — it's made me write truer, which feels like a crime I haven't been charged with yet.*

*So maybe the honest thing is to tell you that too. That I've known. That every letter since October has been written by someone standing in the light pretending to be a shadow.*

*Or maybe the honest thing is to wait six weeks and let you decide if you hate me with the whole story or half of it.*

*Your friend, whether you'd agree to the word or not,*

*W.*

She read it three times. She did not burn it. She folded it into thirds and slid it into the drawer where she kept the ribbon-tied bundle of every letter she'd received since September, and she pressed the drawer shut with both hands, like closing a wound or a door.

Six weeks. The reveal event was printed on a card stock flyer taped to the library door: *HARBORLIGHT PEN-PAL REVEAL NIGHT — ALL PARTICIPANTS AND WELL-WISHERS WELCOME.* The whole town welcome. Odette hosting, probably, with punch and a guest book.

Wren turned off the kitchen light and stood in the dark for a moment, and in the dark she admitted the thing she'd been circling for weeks, the thing that had arrived somewhere between the garden gate and tonight, unwelcome as weather:

She wasn't afraid of Cyrus hating her anymore.

She was afraid of the opposite. Of Cyrus looking at her across a library full of people the way she'd looked at her tonight in the gymnasium — soft, remembering, undone — and Wren having to stand there in the truth of what she'd done, holding a name instead of a shadow, and find out whether that softness could survive being aimed at someone real.

Outside, far off past the dark houses and the black water, the lighthouse turned its light. Wren watched it sweep — once, twice — the way she always did now, the way Juniper did, the way the whole town did without admitting it.

Then she went upstairs, lay down beside her sleeping starfish, and did not sleep at all.
