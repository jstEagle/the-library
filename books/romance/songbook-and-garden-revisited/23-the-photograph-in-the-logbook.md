## 23. The Photograph in the Logbook

The rain had been coming down for three days straight, the kind of gray, patient rain that made the whole coast feel like it was holding its breath, and I'd run out of excuses not to deal with the logbooks.

They lived in the bottom of my grandfather's old sea chest, forty-one years of them, one per season, each with his blocky handwriting marching across the pages like a man who didn't trust weather to behave. The automated system logged everything now — wind speed, visibility, lamp hours — into a computer file nobody would ever read. But Elias Sterling had written his logs by hand until the day they took the light out of his hands, and he'd written them the way he did everything: thoroughly, stubbornly, and with occasional opinions that had no business being in an official record.

*Oct. 14. Fog. Fog. More fog. If the fog were a person I would have words with it.*

I'd been meaning to sort them for two years. Two years of "next weekend" and "after the garden" and "when I have time," which is what I said about everything I couldn't bear to do. But the novel was done, mailed off, out of my hands at last, and the garden was asleep under its mulch, and the rain had closed the roads to anything but memory. So on Sunday afternoon I carried the chest up from the cellar, set it by the woodstove, and started.

It was worse than I expected. Not because of the grief — I'd made a truce with the grief somewhere around the third winter after he died. It was worse because he was *funny*. Page after page of a man I'd known only as stern and salt-crusted and disappointed in everyone's posture, and there he was in 1987, complaining that the supply boat captain watered down the coffee, drawing little diagrams of gulls he'd named after politicians. A whole person, preserved in ink, and I'd spent nine years mourning a monument instead.

I worked through the decades backward. 2004. 1999. The year Daniel brought home a girl.

I want to say I steeled myself. I didn't. I just turned the page and there she was, in the summer of 2013, before everything, when Wren Calloway was still just a name that made my brother smile like an idiot.

*July 3. Daniel brought his girl up to see the light. She climbed all 112 steps without stopping once and then asked me twice as many questions as the steps. Good questions. Asked how the lens turns, asked what happens in a power cut, asked if I ever got lonely up here. Told her loneliness is a tool, same as a wrench. You learn to use it or it uses you. She thought about that a long time. Smart girl. Too smart for this family, probably.*

I sat very still.

I remembered that day. Of course I remembered it — I remembered every day of that summer, because it was the last summer before the fall, before the engagement I'd already begun to dread, before Wren Calloway became the woman who ended it and vanished and left my family in pieces. I remembered being twenty-six and furious at Daniel for proposing to me at all, furious at myself for saying yes because it was easier than explaining why I couldn't leave, furious at the lighthouse for needing me, furious at the sea for existing. And I remembered Wren, sunburned and laughing on the gallery deck, asking my grandfather questions while I stood by the rail doing arithmetic on my own cowardice.

I had spent nine years building her into something. That's what hatred is, mostly — construction work. You take a woman who broke your brother's heart and you add floors to her. She was selfish. She was cruel. She came here, saw what we had, and tore it down for sport. Every year the building got taller, until she was less a person than a tower herself, something dark on the horizon you kept the light turning against.

And here was my grandfather — who missed nothing, who forgave nothing easily, who once didn't speak to Odette's father for six years over a disputed fence line — writing about her like she was a guest he'd have back anytime.

I kept reading.

*Aug. 19. Girl came back alone. Daniel was working. We sat on the steps and she helped me wind the clockwork. Strong hands. Said she liked work where you can see what you've done at the end of the day. I said most people don't. She said most people are afraid to look at what they've done.*

I turned pages faster now, the way you do when you're looking for the thing that will let you stop looking.

*Sept. 2. Rain. Girl helped me carry oil up before the changeover. Asked about Daniel. I told her he's a good boy but the family bends people. She went quiet. Then she said, "Somebody should tell them that." I laughed. First time I've laughed in weeks.*

The woodstove ticked. The rain wrote its long gray sentence against the windows. Somewhere below, the automated lamp turned through its programmed arc, faithful and soulless, keeping watch over water that didn't need watching anymore.

Then I found the photograph.

It had been tucked into the spine of the 2013 log, loose, the way he tucked everything loose — receipts, tide tables, the occasional pressed flower he'd deny pressing. A snapshot, slightly curled, colors gone soft with age. Taken on the gallery deck, from the angle of someone holding the camera at arm's length, which meant my grandfather had taken it himself, which meant he'd wanted it.

Wren stood at the rail with the wind pulling her hair sideways, mid-laugh, caught in the unguarded moment cameras are supposed to miss and almost never do. Beside her, arm slung easy around her shoulders, grinning like the sun had been invented for his personal benefit: Daniel. My brother. My almost-fiancé. Twenty-eight years old and completely unaware that in four months he'd be standing in our mother's kitchen telling Cyrus that Wren had left him, that Wren had never loved him, that Wren was a liar and worse.

Behind them both, blurred but unmistakable, the lamp room. The light I keep. The light she once climbed 112 steps to ask questions about.

I turned the photograph over.

There was writing on the back. Not dated, but the hand was unmistakable — my grandfather's blocky capitals, the ones that marched across forty-one years of weather:

**THAT GIRL WAS KIND TO ME IN THE HOSPITAL.**

I read it three times. The words didn't rearrange themselves. They just sat there, small and certain, in the handwriting of the only man whose judgment I had ever trusted without audit.

The hospital. I knew which hospital. St. Brendan's, forty minutes inland, where my grandfather had gone in the winter of 2013 for the surgery on his hip — the surgery during which, according to the official family version, he'd been looked after by hired nurses and my mother's casseroles. I'd driven out twice a week. Daniel had come when he could. It had been a hard winter, the kind where the family closes ranks and counts who shows up.

I had counted. I had always counted. That was my specialty — showing up, keeping the light, doing the duty, and filing away exactly who else did theirs.

Wren Calloway had shown up. In the winter when she was — when she must have already been — when everything was already coming apart inside her, she had driven forty minutes inland to sit with a crotchety old keeper and presumably listen to him explain that loneliness is a tool.

She hadn't told anyone. Of course she hadn't told anyone. If she had, the story would be different. The story would have a floor under it.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the fire burned down to coals and I had to feed it again. Long enough that the rain stopped and started and stopped again, undecided.

Here is what I had believed, cleanly, for nine years: Wren Calloway came to Port Merrow, charmed my brother, discovered our family wasn't good enough for her ambitions, wrecked my engagement — an engagement, I should note, that I accepted in a moment of weakness and mourned in secret relief — and left. Cruelty, then exit. Simple as a lighthouse: fixed position, known range.

Here is what the logbook suggested instead: that she had loved my brother enough to climb his grandfather's stairs twice. That she had seen the bending the family does — my grandfather's phrase, not mine, though I'd felt the bend myself for thirty years — and had tried, in her careful way, to warn us. That when it all collapsed, she had taken the blame for a destruction that had at least two architects, possibly three, and had walked into the sea of elsewhere without correcting a single account.

That she had been kind to a lonely old man in a hospital, and had never once mentioned it, even when kindness would have bought her back a scrap of standing in a town that had decided what she was.

I thought about the letters. God help me, I thought about the letters — the stiff first ones, the soil advice, the harvest letter with its confession of a daughter and a running and a wanting to stop being a person who leaves. I had answered that letter with grace, I'd thought, generous anonymous grace, forgiveness offered blind to a stranger. *For whatever you did.*

For whatever she did.

What *did* she do? I sat with the question like a stone in my lap. She ended an engagement that I had secretly wanted ended. She left town pregnant — I still didn't know the whole of that story, wouldn't until she told it herself, if she ever did — and whatever drove her out, it hadn't been ambition. People don't flee their whole lives over ambition. They flee over fear, or shame, or a man who tells them to go.

Daniel. Something cold moved through me, slow as the tide coming in over flat sand. Daniel, who had narrated the breakup to the family as pure abandonment. Daniel, who had needed the story to be simple because the complicated version had him in it. Daniel, who was coming home next month for Mother's birthday, married now to someone else, bitter as green walnuts, still telling the old story to anyone who'd hold still.

Had I ever actually heard Wren's version? Nine years, and I searched honestly, and found nothing. Not one sentence. She'd left before the town finished writing hers, and I'd signed it without reading.

My grandfather hadn't. My grandfather, who trusted nobody's account of anything, had written his own in blocky capitals on the back of a photograph and hidden it in a logbook, where a sentimental granddaughter sorting boxes on a rainy Sunday might someday find it. Or maybe he hadn't hidden it at all. Maybe he'd just put it where things he couldn't say out loud went. Either way, it had waited eleven years for me, patient as seed stock.

"That girl was kind to me in the hospital."

Not *that girl broke your brother's heart.* Not *that girl caused trouble.* Kind. He'd chosen the word the way he chose everything — deliberately, and with opinions that had no business being in an official record.

I laughed. It came out wrong, half sob, and I wiped my face with the heel of my hand and laughed again at myself, sitting on the floor of a lighthouse crying over a photograph of two people who weren't even in my life anymore. Except one of them was. Except one of them had been, all autumn, in blue ink, advising me on grafting cuts and telling me her daughter asked about the sea.

I set the photograph on the mantel, facing out. It looked strange there, cheerful, the wind-blown laughing girl and the grinning man and the blurred lamp room behind them. Evidence. Not of a crime. Of the opposite of a crime.

Then I did what I always do when the ground shifts under me. I got out paper.

Dear Pen Pal [I wrote], —

I found something today. Not in the garden, for once. In my grandfather's logbooks, which I've been avoiding for two years the way you avoid a room where somebody died.

I'm not going to tell you what it was yet. I need to sit with it first. But I'll say this much: it was a reminder that the stories we tell about other people are usually load-bearing walls, and if you pull one out, you'd better check what the roof is resting on.

You wrote once that most people are afraid to look at what they've done. I think I've been afraid to look at what was done. There's a difference, and the difference is nine years wide.

Tell me something true. Anything. I find I'm in the market for true things tonight.

— Your Keeper

I sealed it before I could soften it. Tomorrow I'd walk it down to the library box, and tomorrow-after I'd get an answer in that familiar blue ink, and the roof would hold or it wouldn't.

But as I banked the fire and climbed toward bed, past the lamp turning its faithful rounds overhead, one more thing surfaced — small, ordinary, devastating. A memory I'd filed and never examined: the hospital, that winter, a Tuesday. Me, arriving with clean laundry, finding my grandfather in improbably good spirits, asking him what had him so cheerful.

Him, smiling at the window: *"A friend came by."*

I'd assumed Odette's mother. I'd assumed half the town. I'd assumed everyone except the one person the evidence pointed to, because assuming her required believing she was capable of the visit, and I had already finished building the tower.

I hadn't checked the foundation. Nobody in my family ever had.

Outside, the rain began again, soft and steady, and down the coast, in a house I'd never seen the inside of, a woman I had hated for nine years was probably putting a child to bed — a child who asked about the sea, who drew lighthouses, who had never done anything to anyone.

Tomorrow I'd mail the letter. Next month Daniel would come home with his bitterness and his simple story, and I would have to decide, before he arrived, which version of events I was going to stand in.

The photograph watched me from the mantel, laughing in the wind, and for the first time in nine years, I let it laugh.
