## 25. Odette Plays Detective

Odette Fenn arrived at the lighthouse on a Tuesday, which was suspicious in itself, because Odette never came on Tuesdays. Tuesdays were her day for the food co-op and the crossword and a standing argument with the pharmacist about generic brands. Tuesdays were sacred. So when I heard her truck grinding up the keeper's road at four in the afternoon, gravel popping under the tires like distant applause, I knew she'd come armed.

I was in the garden, elbow-deep in the third bed, wrestling out a root system that had apparently been planning a coup since the Reagan administration. The grafted cuttings from my grandfather's apple tree were holding — two of the four had taken, which the pen pal said was better than average for first attempts, which the pen pal said in a way that made me want to write back *define average, you smug stranger* — and I'd gotten possessive about the whole operation. The garden and I had reached an understanding over the autumn: it would forgive me for the years of neglect, and I would forgive it for the blackberries, which forgave nothing and no one.

"Cyrus." Odette stood at the garden gate with her arms crossed, wearing the expression she usually reserved for men who catcalled near her bookshop. "Put down the fork. We need to talk."

"It's a spade, and I'm working."

"You've been 'working' for two hours. I drove past at two. You were holding the same clump of dirt."

"It's a complicated clump."

"Cyrus." She let my name hang there, one syllable stretched into a whole weather system. Then she pushed open the gate, marched to the low stone wall at the garden's edge, and sat down on it with the deliberation of a judge taking the bench. "I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to think very carefully before you answer, because I've known you since you were nine years old and you bit Marcus Teague at the harvest fair, and I know exactly what your lying face looks like."

"I have never had a lying face."

"You have a *terrible* lying face. It's your whole face."

I leaned on the spade and looked at her. She was fifty-one, built like a lighthouse herself — narrow at the top, immovable at the base — and she had the particular stillness of a person who has already won and is simply waiting for the loser to be informed.

"Ask," I said.

"You're happier."

"That's your question?"

"That's my *observation*. The question comes after. You've been happier since September. You hum. You *hum*, Cyrus. You hummed at the co-op last week, in the cheese aisle, and Mrs. Pell asked me if you'd gotten a diagnosis or a man, and I said neither, because I assumed it was the letters."

I kept my face still. I'd gotten better at that over the season — the letters had taught me things about my own face, about what it gave away, because I had a correspondent who read people like proof sheets and marked every error in red.

"What about the letters?" I said, which was a mistake, because it confirmed the letters mattered, and Odette pounced the way she always pounced, with the whole weight of her.

"There it is. The letters. One letter a week, every week, since the program started. You used to mail them at the harbor box on Thursdays. Then you switched to the library drop slot. Do you know why I noticed?"

"Because you're a nosy woman with too much time—"

"Because the harbor box gets emptied at six and the library slot at noon, and you started going at *seven in the morning*, Cyrus, before the library even opened, like you didn't want to be seen. And I thought, well. That's not the behavior of a woman writing to a stranger in Duluth."

I said nothing. The clump of dirt sat on my spade, accusing.

"And then," Odette went on, warming now, leaning forward on the wall, "last week I'm shelving returns — I volunteer Thursdays, you know this — and Marta asks me to help sort the reveal-event cards, because the season ends in three weeks and they're doing the matching ceremony, and there's a whole system, little card stock samples the writers filled out at registration, handwriting and all, filed by number. And I'm filing, filing, filing, and I come across a card in a hand I know."

The wind came off the water and moved through the garden, rattling the dead bean stalks. Somewhere below the bluff, the sea did its patient work against the rocks, the sound my grandfather called the housekeeping.

"I know a lot of hands," I said. "You run a bookshop. Half the town writes checks to you."

"Not this hand." Odette's voice dropped, and that was the thing about Odette — she could be a bulldozer about your love life and then go quiet and gentle at exactly the wrong moment, the moment you needed her to keep bulldozing so you'd have something to push against. "This hand slants left. Big loops on the capitals. Crosses the t's like she's slashing them. I've seen it on a hundred birthday cards and one very angry letter to the editor in 2016 about the state of the boat ramp. It's Wren Calloway, isn't it."

* * *

I want to tell you I laughed. I want to tell you I said something wry, something in the register I keep for the world, the keeper's voice, the one that turns everything into weather talk. What I actually did was stand very still in my ruined garden with a spade in my hands and feel the ground tilt under me, the way it tilts in the first seconds of a quake, when the body knows before the mind consents to.

"That's absurd," I said.

"Is it?"

"It's absurd. Wren Calloway left Port Merrow nine years ago and never wrote to a soul. She's not in a pen-pal program. She's not — Odette, she wouldn't."

"Wouldn't she?" Odette wasn't gloating. That was the worst part. She was watching me with those library-shushing eyes, patient as tide. "A woman comes back to town after nine years, moves into her aunt's house, keeps to herself, and signs up for an anonymous letter program the same season you do. A woman who, as far as I can tell, knows exactly one person in Port Merrow and it's the paint guy at the hardware store. Why would she sign up, Cyrus? Who would she be hoping to write to?"

"Anyone. Strangers. That's the point of the program."

"The point of the program," Odette said, "is that it's anonymous. The point of a woman who's hiding is anonymity too. I'm just saying they rhyme."

"She has a kid," I said, and heard my own voice go strange. "A little girl. She's not writing letters, she's — she has a whole life, she's repainting a house, she doesn't have time to—"

"To write one letter a week?" Odette said softly. "You write one a week. You're a novelist. She'd find the time."

I turned back to the garden bed. I jabbed the spade into the soil with more force than any root deserved. "You're wrong."

"Maybe."

"You're wrong, and even if you weren't wrong, it wouldn't matter, because I don't know Wren Calloway's handwriting. I haven't seen it in nine years. Nobody has. You're matching a card to a memory."

"I matched it to the letter to the editor," Odette said. "It's in the archive box. 2016. 'Re: the boat ramp, which this town has allowed to rot out of pure stinginess.' Signed, Wren Calloway, then an address on Larkspur Lane. I held the two pages up to the window light, Cyrus. The loops match. The slashes match. Even the way she writes her sevens — she puts a little dash through them, European style, did you know that? I didn't know that. But I know it now."

* * *

She left eventually. Odette always leaves eventually; it's one of her better qualities, that she knows when a siege has done all it can do and the rest is the city's own work. At the gate she turned around, and for a moment she wasn't the bulldozer or the judge, just my oldest friend with salt-and-white hair coming loose from its clip.

"I didn't come to wreck your season," she said. "I came because if I'm right, you deserve to walk into that reveal night with your eyes open instead of getting flattened in front of the whole town. And if I'm wrong, you get to be smug at me for a decade, which I know you'd enjoy."

"You could be wrong," I said.

"I could." She looked at the garden, at the two grafted whips staked in their bed, the ones my pen pal had talked me through, bud by bud. "Whoever she is, she's good for you. That part I'm sure of. Just — check, Cyrus. Before the reveal. Check so it doesn't check *you*."

Then the truck ground back down the keeper's road, and I was alone with the housekeeping sound of the sea and a spade full of dirt and a sentence lodged in me like a splinter: *the loops match, the slashes match, the sevens have dashes through them.*

* * *

I didn't sleep. I want that on the record. I lay in my grandfather's bed in my grandfather's house with the lamp turning its patient circles below the window, and I told myself stories. I told myself Odette wanted drama the way she wanted first-editions, and had found some. I told myself that Wren Calloway, who had once dismantled my brother's engagement and then vanished like a tide going out, would sooner swallow glass than sit down weekly to write to me. I told myself that my pen pal was a retired teacher in a neighboring county, a man named something like Gerald, someone with strong opinions about soil pH and no history with me whatsoever.

The stories held until about three in the morning. Then they didn't.

Because here's what I knew, and had known, and had been declining to know since September: the handwriting felt familiar. From the very first letter — the stiff one, the one that read like a business memo from someone furious about being assigned to me — I had felt that prickle at the back of the neck, the one you get in a house when a door you closed is suddenly open. I'd told myself it was the generic familiarity of anyone's cursive, everyone's aunt's hand. But it wasn't generic. It was specific. It was a leftward slant and slashed t's and sevens with dashes, and I had seen that hand before, years ago, on a card tucked into a bouquet I never thanked her for, back when she was the woman my brother loved and I was the sister who was supposed to hate her, and did, and hated her with a thoroughness that had a lot of other things buried inside it.

At four I got up. I made coffee I didn't want. I went into the front room where I keep my correspondence in a wooden box that had once held chart instruments, and I took out the season's letters, and I laid them on the table under the lamp like evidence at a trial I didn't want to attend.

Then I did something small and unforgivable, and I want to be honest about it, because this whole account is supposed to be honest: I went looking for corroboration. Not confirmation — corroboration. There's a difference. Confirmation is when you check because you want the truth. Corroboration is when you check because you want to be wrong, and you want to be wrong in a way that lets you keep everything.

Wren Calloway shopped at the Harborlight Market. I knew this the way the whole town knew it — she was a fixture at the register by nine on Saturdays, the little girl on her hip, the two of them like a matched set of dark heads. And the Harborlight Market, in a mercy of small-town infrastructure, still ran a corkboard by the door where people pinned things: lost cats, guitar lessons, and grocery lists that had fluttered out of pockets and coats and been pinned up by Mrs. Pell, who believed all lost paper should have a second chance.

There were three lists on the board. I stood in front of them with my coffee going cold in my hand and read all three twice, because I was a coward, and cowards read every list.

The first was a man's hand, cramped and joyless: milk, eggs, *fix the gutter*. The second was a child's, all wobbling capitals: FISH STICKS, ICE CREAM, DOG?? The third—

The third was written in a leftward slant with big loops on the capitals and t's crossed like slashes and a seven with a dash through it, and it said:

*olive oil (the good kind)*
*lemons*
*thread — blue*
*7 apples for the girl*

I stood there for a long time. Mrs. Pell, rest her, was stocking oranges and watching me the way small-town women watch other small-town women, which is to say completely, without apology, while appearing to work.

"Lost something?" she asked.

"No," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised us both. "Found something, maybe."

I walked out to the truck. I sat in the cab with my hands on the wheel and the harbor going silver-pink around me, the fishing boats coming in with their night's work, and I let myself do the arithmetic I'd been refusing since Odette's truck came up the road.

A pen pal who knew soil. A pen pal who knew apples — who'd told me which rootstock would take, who'd said *your grandfather's tree, if it's the Gravenstein I think it is, wants a late graft, not an early one*, and how would a stranger know what my grandfather's tree was? A pen pal who wrote about wanting to stop being a person who leaves. A pen pal with a daughter. A pen pal who, once, in a letter that had kept me up half the night, had said *I wronged someone in this town and I think about it every time I buy paint*, and I had written back grace, blind, generous grace, grace I would now have to look at in daylight and decide whether I meant it.

And underneath all of it, the thing I'd felt at the hardware store in October, the frozen small talk, the two of us standing in the paint aisle being polite at each other like survivors of the same wreck politely not mentioning the wreck: the way she'd looked at me. Like she was reading something. Like she was waiting for me to read her back.

*The loops match. The slashes match. The sevens have dashes.*

"Damn it, Wren," I said to the empty cab, and it came out not angry at all, which was its own kind of answer.

I drove home. I took the season's letters out of the chart box and I held the most recent one up to the morning light, and beside it I set the grocery list I had — I will confess the whole shape of my cowardice — taken off the corkboard, because Mrs. Pell was watching and Mrs. Pell sees everything and Mrs. Pell would understand, and I would pin it back Monday.

Leftward slant. Big loops. Slashed t's. A seven with a dash through it, sitting right there between *thread — blue* and *apples for the girl*.

The same hand. It was the same hand, and it had been the same hand since September, and I had written to it every week, told it things I've never told anyone, let it into the garden and the lighthouse and the draft of my novel and the whole locked room of my grief — and it had written back, and somewhere in those months of letters there was a woman who knew my name the whole time, who had stood in a hardware store three feet from me knowing, who had eaten my words and answered them with soil advice and tenderness while I sat there being generous to a stranger I was already—

I put the pages down. My hands weren't steady. The lamp turned below the bluff, sweeping its light across water that didn't care, and I thought: *she knew. All season, she knew, and she stayed, and she kept writing.*

And then, underneath that, in a voice I didn't recognize as mine, quieter, more dangerous: *she stayed. All season, knowing, she stayed.*

I had three weeks until the reveal. Three weeks to decide which of those two sentences was the true one.

I pinned the grocery list back on the corkboard myself, Monday morning, smoothing the corners flat. Mrs. Pell watched me do it and asked no questions, which in Port Merrow is its own kind of mercy. And on the walk back to the truck I caught myself doing the thing I do instead of feeling things, which is planning — and what I was planning, with my heart going like a gaffed fish, was a visit. Wren Calloway's house had a porch. Porches have doors. Doors open.

I had a letter in my hand and an accusation in my throat, and I got in the truck, and I drove.
