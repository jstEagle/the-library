## 26. The Confrontation That Isn't

The knock comes at four-forty on a gray Thursday, and Wren knows before she opens the door that it's trouble, because nobody knocks at her house at four-forty on a gray Thursday except delivery drivers and the occasional lost tourist, and delivery drivers don't knock like that — three sharp raps, the knock of a person who has rehearsed what they're going to say and is terrified they'll forget it.

She's halfway through making Juniper's after-school snack — apple slices, the last of the good cheddar, the kind of plate you arrange when you're procrastinating on something harder — and she wipes her hands on her jeans and crosses the hall and opens the door without looking through the peephole, which later she will understand was the last innocent decision of the day.

Cyrus Sterling is standing on her porch.

She's holding an envelope. Not a fancy one — one of the plain manila kind you buy in boxes of fifty at the hardware store, which Wren knows because she bought the same box two months ago. Her hair is pulled back in a way that means she's been working, garden dirt still on her knuckles, and her face is doing something Wren has never seen it do in person: it's trying to be hard and failing at the edges.

"Cyrus," Wren says, and her voice comes out steady, which surprises her, because inside her chest something has dropped like a stone down a well.

"I need to talk to you." Cyrus's jaw is set. Her eyes go past Wren's shoulder into the hall, cataloguing — the paint cans by the stairs, Juniper's boots, the ribbon of afternoon light — and Wren watches her do it and thinks, *she's building a case.* "About something that's been — there's something I've been trying to understand, and I think you're the only person who can help me."

*Help me.* Not *I know.* Not *it's you, isn't it.* Wren's mind is moving fast now, faster than it has any right to, sorting possibilities the way she used to sort inventory at the restaurant in Portland — what does she know, what does she suspect, what did Odette say, what did the handwriting give away. The envelope in Cyrus's hand is sealed. That means something. A person carrying a sealed envelope is a person who hasn't decided yet.

"Come in," Wren says, because the alternative is having this conversation on the porch where Mrs. Pell from across the lane can watch through her curtains like it's a matinee, and because — though she'd never admit this to anyone — she has imagined Cyrus in her house so many times that having it finally happen feels like a door she's been holding shut with her shoulder finally swinging open.

But Cyrus doesn't move.

"Juniper's here," she says. Not a question. She must have seen the small jacket on the hook, the small boots. Her voice has gone strange around the name, careful, the way you handle a word that might cut you.

"She's doing her reading practice." Wren steps back, opens the door wider. "You can wait in the kitchen. Or — " and here she makes a mistake, or maybe it's not a mistake, maybe it's the truest thing she's said all week — "you can listen, if you want. She's gotten good. She likes reading out loud now."

Cyrus's face does something complicated. She looks at the envelope in her hand as if she's forgotten it's there, then back at Wren, and whatever war is happening behind her eyes, Wren can see the front line of it. Then, to Wren's astonishment, Cyrus steps inside. She doesn't take off her boots, which is unlike her — Wren knows it's unlike her, knows it from letters that never named her, from a lifetime of watching the Sterling family be scrupulous about small decencies — and she stops in the hall like a woman who has walked into a room and forgotten the speech she came to give.

From the living room, Juniper's voice floats out, small and certain and slightly too loud, the way six-year-olds read when they're proud of themselves:

"*'The keeper climbed the tower at dusk, though there was no ship to save and no one to see her do it. She climbed because the light was hers to keep, and because — because some promises you make to the dark itself.'*"

Wren hears Cyrus's breath change.

It's a small sound, a caught-breath sound, the sound of someone hearing their own words in a stranger's mouth, and Wren knows — she knows with a certainty that arrives all at once, like cold water through a cracked hull — that Cyrus recognizes it. Of course she recognizes it. It's her novel. Her unfinished, unshared, guarded, three-a.m. novel, the one she told her pen pal about in letter after letter, the heroine with the tower and the guilt and the promise to the dark. The excerpt Wren found three weeks ago in a lit magazine, tucked between an ad for a writing retreat and a story about a man and his dead dog, printed under Cyrus Sterling's name in small proud type. *From a novel in progress.*

Wren has been reading it to Juniper every afternoon for a week.

She has not told Cyrus this. She has not told anyone this. It is the one secret she has that isn't a secret at all, just a private joy she's been hoarding like a child hoards the last piece of cake — *my pen pal wrote a book, and my daughter loves it, and neither of them knows.*

And now the author of the book is standing in her hallway holding an envelope full of accusation, hearing her own sentences come back to her in a six-year-old's voice.

"Who's reading that?" Cyrus asks, and her voice is not the voice from the porch. The hard thing has cracked clean through.

"Juniper." Wren's throat is tight. "It's her favorite. She calls it the tower lady book. She — " Wren stops. There is no version of the next sentence that doesn't confess everything. *She asks me to read it every day. She doesn't know the tower lady is you. She doesn't know the tower lady is the woman who writes me letters about soil and storms and forgiveness.*

But Cyrus is already moving past her, drawn down the hall the way people are drawn to the sound of their own music, and Wren can only follow.

* * *

The living room is small and still half-unpacked, boxes along one wall labeled in Wren's handwriting — *BOOKS, MISC, KITCHEN (?)* — and Juniper is curled in the armchair by the window with the magazine open on her knees, sounding out the hard words with her lips moving. She's got a blanket over her legs and one sock off and she looks, Wren thinks with the sudden helpless ache that is motherhood, like the best thing that has ever happened to anyone.

"Juniper," Wren says gently. "We have a visitor."

Juniper looks up. She has Daniel's coloring, Wren's stubborn chin, and an openness that neither of her parents ever managed — she looks at strangers like they're presents she hasn't opened yet. She looks at Cyrus now with exactly that expression, unguarded, curious, and Wren watches the look land on Cyrus like a physical blow.

Cyrus has gone very still. Wren knows why. She's doing the arithmetic every adult in Port Merrow has done since Wren came back — the age, the timing, the nine years, the resemblance that is and isn't there. Wren has watched half the town do this math at the grocery store, the library, the fish counter. She has never once watched Cyrus do it, and she finds she cannot bear it, and she cannot look away.

"Hi," Juniper says. "Are you the lighthouse lady?"

The silence that follows is the longest of Wren's life.

"Mum says there's a lady who lives at the lighthouse," Juniper continues, because she is six and does not yet know that some questions are landmines. "In the tower book, the keeper lives all alone and she talks to the sea. Do you talk to the sea?"

"I — " Cyrus's voice fails and restarts. "Sometimes. Yes."

"That's what the keeper does." Juniper nods, satisfied, and then — with the terrible generosity of children — she lifts the magazine and holds it out. "Do you want to hear the best part? Mum does the voice, but I can do it too."

"Juniper," Wren says, a warning wrapped in a smile.

But Cyrus has crossed the room. She's standing at the edge of the armchair's orbit, looking down at the open page, at her own words in the big friendly typeface of the lit magazine, and Wren watches her hand come up halfway — reaching for the page or reaching for the child, Wren can't tell — and then stop and fold back down to her side.

"That's very kind," Cyrus says to Juniper, and her voice is wrecked, absolutely wrecked, though Wren suspects only she can hear it. "But you're doing fine. Better than fine."

"It's sad, though." Juniper frowns at the page, suddenly grave. "The keeper's brother was mean to her friend, and the friend went away, and the keeper thinks it was her fault but it wasn't, Mum said." She looks up at Cyrus, earnest, matter-of-fact, delivering a verdict no one asked for. "It wasn't her fault. Mum says people leave because they're scared, not because you made them."

Wren closes her eyes.

She said that. Three nights ago, at bedtime, when Juniper asked why the keeper's friend went away, and Wren — exhausted, off guard, reading her own guilt rendered in someone else's prose — had said it without thinking about who she was really talking to, or about the fact that she was, in a sense, talking about herself, in front of her daughter, to the dark.

And now she's said it in front of Cyrus Sterling. Who wrote the scene. Who has spent — how many letters? — circling that same wound from the other side, the keeper's side, the side that stayed.

Wren opens her eyes and looks at Cyrus and finds her looking at the envelope in her own hand.

* * *

Here is what Wren will never be able to explain afterward, not to Odette, not to the pen-pal reveal committee, not to herself at three in the morning: she could have ended it in that moment. Cyrus was standing in her living room holding what was obviously a letter — and Wren knew, with the certainty of a woman who has been writing to this person for a season, that the letter was the harvest letter's answer, or a confrontation, or a confession, or all three. Cyrus had come here to say something true and terrible. The envelope was the shape of it.

And Wren could have said, *Open it. Or better, I'll save you the trouble: it's me. I'm your pen pal. I've been your pen pal since October, and before that I was the woman who broke your brother's heart and ran, and I have a daughter who reads your novel like it's a hymn, and I am so sorry, I have been sorry since before I knew I was coming back.*

The words were right there. They had been right there for weeks, through the garden gate and the cider on the lighthouse steps and the rainstorm in the shed, always one sentence from spoken, always swallowed.

But Juniper was looking up at Cyrus with the magazine in her lap, waiting to see if the visitor would sit down, and Cyrus was looking at Juniper like a woman looking at a door she thought had closed nine years ago and found standing open, and Wren — Wren could not do it. Not here. Not with the child in the room and the light going gold on the boxes and Cyrus's face doing that thing, that breaking-and-mending thing. Some truths need a room with no one innocent in it. Some confessions can't be made in front of the person they'd cost the most.

So Wren did the coward's kindness. She said, "I'll make tea," and left the two of them — the author and the reader, the keeper and the daughter of the woman who left — standing in her living room, and went into the kitchen, and stood with her hands on the counter and shook.

* * *

Through the doorway she can hear them.

Not words, exactly. Juniper's voice, rising and falling, working through the next paragraph — *the keeper finds a letter in the logbook, a letter the old keeper never sent, and she doesn't know who it was meant for, but she keeps it in her pocket anyway, for luck* — and Cyrus's voice answering sometimes, low, one word, two, the rhythm of an adult listening to a child read, that patient encouraging murmur every parent knows.

And then a silence, and then Juniper again: "Do you want to see my drawing? It's the lighthouse. Mum helped with the light part."

A pause. Then Cyrus: "I'd like that very much."

Wren fills the kettle she doesn't put on the stove. She takes down two mugs and then three and then puts the third back and then takes it down again, because what is she doing, who is she making tea for, what is this scene, what is Cyrus doing here, what was in the envelope, what was she going to say — *I think you're the only person who can help me* — and Wren presses her palms against her eyes and breathes the way she learned to breathe in the waiting room in Portland, in the months when everything was decided for her, in and out, four counts, again.

When she comes back with the tray — because she is her mother's daughter and her hands needed a job — the scene in the living room has arranged itself into something she will remember for the rest of her life.

Juniper has fetched the drawing. It's the lighthouse as only a six-year-old draws it: a white tower with a red stripe, a beam rendered in yellow crayon sweeping across the whole top half of the page, and two small figures at the base. Cyrus is crouched beside the armchair, the drawing held in both hands, and her face — Wren stops in the doorway with the tray and simply looks — her face is the face of someone being forgiven for something they haven't confessed yet.

"That's you," Juniper is saying, pointing at one of the small figures. "That's the keeper. And that's—" she squints at the second figure, a blob with hair, "—that's the friend who comes back. I didn't know what color her hair is. The book doesn't say."

"No," Cyrus says. Her voice is barely there. "It doesn't say."

"I made it brown," Juniper says, "like mine. Because friends can look like whoever."

Cyrus looks at the drawing for a long moment. Then she looks up, and her eyes find Wren in the doorway, and everything that was in the envelope — Wren can see it, the whole accusation, the whole terrible arithmetic of handwriting and grocery lists and Odette's detective work and nine years of silence — everything in it rises to the surface of Cyrus's face and hangs there, one breath from spoken.

And then it goes back down.

"I should go," Cyrus says. She stands. She sets the drawing down on the arm of the chair with a care that undoes Wren completely — squared to the edge, the way you'd set down something valuable. "Thank you for the — thank you for reading. Both of you."

"Come back," Juniper says, with the terrifying ease of children issuing invitations. "You can hear the ending. We haven't got to the ending."

Cyrus looks at her. Wren watches her look at her — watches her take in the small stubborn chin, the magazine, the drawing, the whole small architecture of a life Wren built in exile — and something passes over Cyrus's face that Wren will spend the next several chapters, the next several years, learning to name.

"Maybe I will," Cyrus says.

She walks past Wren in the hall. She doesn't touch her. But she slows, just slightly, the way you slow passing a lit window, and she says, so quietly that only Wren can hear it, so quietly it might be to herself:

"She has your way of reading. Like the words might get away."

And then she's gone. The front door clicks. Boots on the porch steps, gravel, the car, the road.

Wren stands in the hallway with a tray of tea for no one.

* * *

She finds the envelope an hour later.

Not thrown away — Cyrus wouldn't. Tucked into the door of the mailbox at the end of the lane, weighted under a stone, because Cyrus Sterling, even mid-flight, even with her speech still lodged in her throat, could not simply litter. Wren walks down in the blue dusk to check the mail and finds it waiting, and stands there in the cold holding it, knowing what it is, knowing she should burn it unread or open it and be done with all this circling, knowing that whatever is inside will change the shape of everything.

She opens it at the kitchen table while Juniper does a puzzle on the floor.

It's not the accusation. She can see that from the handwriting — it's not the hard, rehearsed hand of the porch knock. It's Cyrus's letter hand, the one Wren knows better than her own signature now, the one that goes soft at the ends of lines. There's no salutation. There's no name. It's one paragraph, written fast, maybe rewritten once, and it says:

*I came here to ask you a question I had no right to ask, and then I heard my own book in your daughter's voice, and I couldn't. I'll ask it some other way. Or maybe I already have. Check your mailbox.*

Wren reads it four times.

On the floor, Juniper holds up a puzzle piece — the corner piece, the lighthouse's beam, the yellow wedge that completes the picture. "Mum, does this go here?"

Wren looks at the letter. At her daughter. At the window, where the first star is out over the water, in the direction of the tower, where somewhere a light is turning whether anyone watches or not.

"Yeah, love," she says, and her voice doesn't shake, and she has no idea how. "That one goes right at the top."

She gets out her good pen. She gets out the good paper, the paper she's been saving for the letter she swore she'd write when she was finally brave enough.

She's still not brave enough. But she's something adjacent to it, tonight, and she begins: *Dear friend — I think it's time I told you what I did.*
