## 28. Two Letters, One Envelope

**Cyrus**

The envelope sat on the kitchen table for three hours before I picked it up, and in those three hours I did the following: washed dishes that were already clean, repotted a rosemary cutting that did not need repotting, read the same paragraph of my grandfather's logbook four times, and stood at the window watching the harbor road for a mail truck that wouldn't come until two.

The letter inside was four pages. I had written it the night before in one sitting, which for me is either a fever or a miracle, and I still couldn't tell which. It said everything. It said: *I know who you are. I've known since the hardware store, or maybe since the harvest letter, or maybe since the first frost when you said* I'm not who you think I am *and I was too much of a coward to ask what you meant. It said:* I'm not angry. I don't know when that stopped being true, but it did. It said: *Come to the lighthouse. Not to the reveal event, not to the library with its card stock and its folding chairs. Come here. Come first.*

It said, in the last line, the thing I'd been circling for weeks the way a moth circles a bulb it can't stop touching: *I think I've been writing to you my whole life and only just got your address.*

My grandfather used to say the hardest part of keeping a light wasn't the storms. It was the clear nights, when there was nothing to do but watch and wait and trust that the mechanism would hold. That's what the three hours at the kitchen table were. Clear nights.

At 1:40 I walked down to the harbor road with the envelope in my coat pocket, and at 1:52 I dropped it through the slot at the post office, and the sound it made — that soft, final *shk* of paper meeting the bottom of a metal box — was the loudest thing I'd heard all week.

Then I walked home and started waiting.

* * *

**Wren**

I addressed the envelope twice because I got Cyrus's street wrong the first time. Not her name. Her street. Nine years away and my hand wrote *Sterling* without hesitating, wrote *Lighthouse Road* in a steady line, and then I sat at Juniper's kitchen table with the wrong-addressed envelope in front of me and understood that some part of me had never left Port Merrow at all. It had just been waiting, like a dog left outside a shop, for the door to open.

The letter had taken me until two in the morning. I'd written three drafts. The first was an apology, which I threw away because apologies are what you give people you want something from. The second was an explanation, which I threw away because explanations are just apologies wearing a nicer coat. The third was the truth, laid out flat, no varnish: *I was your pen pal from the beginning. I knew it was you by the second letter. I stayed anyway. I don't know how to be sorry in a way that undoes anything, so I'm going to stop trying to undo and start trying to be here. If you'll have me. If the answer is no, I'll take Juniper and go, and I'll never write to you again, and I'll deserve that too.*

Juniper was at school. The house was so quiet I could hear the paint drying on the hallway walls — literally, if I stood close enough, which I did, because standing near things I'd fixed with my own hands was the closest thing I had to proof that I was a person who stayed.

I mailed the letter at 1:47, five minutes after Cyrus mailed hers, though neither of us would know that for a long time. The postal clerk, a kid I didn't recognize, stamped it without looking up. I stood at the counter a beat too long, my hand still on the envelope, and he said, "It'll get there, ma'am."

I almost laughed. If only.

* * *

**Cyrus**

The trouble with mail on the coast in November is the ferry.

Everything from Port Merrow goes out on the morning boat to the mainland sorting facility and comes back on the afternoon boat, weather permitting, and the first week of November is not famous for weather permitting. I knew this. I had grown up with this. My grandfather had a whole ledger column for *post delayed — sea* going back forty years, because the man tracked everything, including, apparently, the moods of the postal service.

So when nothing came on Thursday, I told myself: ferry. When nothing came Friday, I told myself: weather. When nothing came Saturday, I stood at my mailbox at the end of the drive with my breath fogging and told myself nothing at all, because I had run out of reasonable explanations and the unreasonable ones were lining up like gulls on a rail.

*She read it and she's gone. She packed the car. She's driving inland right now with Juniper asleep in the back seat and my letter on the passenger side, unread, or worse, read, and either way I'll never know which.*

Odette called Saturday evening. I let it ring twice before answering, which in our friendship is the equivalent of a formal lie.

"You sound terrible," she said.

"I sound fine."

"You sound like a woman who mailed something and regrets it."

I looked out the window at the lighthouse, its beam turning its slow patient turn. "Odette."

"Did you confess to your pen pal?"

"I — what makes you think I —"

"Because you only get this voice when you've done something brave and immediately hated it. Last time was when you submitted the story to the *Merrow Quarterly* in 2011. You didn't sleep for a week."

"I slept fine."

"Liar. Listen." Paper rustled on her end. She was at the library, I realized. She was always at the library; she'd started volunteering after the pen-pal program launched and I'd stopped asking why. "The ferry didn't run Thursday. Ice fog. It ran Friday with a double load and the sorting facility is backed up through the weekend. I heard Marlene complaining about it at the desk. Your letter is sitting in a canvas sack in Rockport with four hundred catalog orders and somebody's prescription. It's not silence, Cyrus. It's logistics."

The relief hit me so hard I had to sit down on the kitchen chair, and I was glad she couldn't see me do it.

"Okay," I said. "Good. That's — fine. I wasn't worried."

"You were extremely worried."

"I was mildly curious about the mail schedule."

"You're a terrible liar and a good friend, and one of those is why I keep you. Monday," she said. "Everything comes Monday. Try to be a person until then."

I hung up and stood in the kitchen a while, hand flat on the table where the envelope had sat. Monday. Two days. I had kept vigil through hurricanes. I had climbed the tower in a gale with my grandfather's flashlight between my teeth. I could survive a weekend.

I wrote that in my notebook, and then I looked at it, and then I crossed it out, because it wasn't true, and I was trying — God, I was trying — to stop lying on paper.

* * *

**Wren**

Juniper asked me Sunday morning why I kept looking out the window.

"I'm watching for birds," I said.

"There's no birds."

"There might be birds."

"There's *no birds*, Mum." She considered me over her cereal with the flat blue gaze she'd gotten from somewhere — not me, not Daniel, some older well of Calloway or Sterling blood — the gaze of a person who has never once accepted a deflection. "Are you waiting for the postman?"

"Something like that."

"Is it from your friend?"

My spoon stopped halfway to my mouth. "What friend?"

"Your friend you write to." She said it the way children say obvious things, with a small shrug, already bored, already back to her cereal. "You write letters all the time. You go to the blue box by the fish shop."

The kitchen tilted, very slightly, the way a boat tilts. I hadn't known she'd seen. I hadn't known she was keeping track. Six years old and already keeping a ledger, and I thought, with a lurch that was half terror and half something I refused to name: *she gets that from me.*

"Yes," I said. "It might be from my friend."

"Is your friend nice?"

I thought about four months of letters. About soil advice and storm reports and a woman who wrote *I think I've been waiting my whole life to be argued with properly* and meant it as a compliment. About a voice I'd known was Cyrus's from the second letter and kept writing to anyway, because the letters were the first honest thing I'd done in nine years and I couldn't make myself stop.

"Yeah," I said. "She's the nicest person I ever hurt."

Juniper absorbed this with the gravity it deserved, then asked if she could have more cereal, and the moment passed, and I carried it with me all day like a stone in my pocket.

Because that was the other thing about Sunday. The ferry ran — Odette's information would have been useful to me too, if I'd had a friend in town who volunteered at libraries, which was its own small indictment — and the mail came, and there was nothing. A flyer for a chowder supper. A dentist reminder for Juniper. No envelope in Cyrus Sterling's handwriting. No envelope in anyone's handwriting at all.

And the silence said, in the voice I'd been hearing since I was nineteen and Daniel Sterling first looked at me across a bonfire: *What did you expect? You told her everything. You gave her every reason. She's had four months to remember what you did, and now you've reminded her in your own words, on paper, in your own hand, and she's done the sensible thing, the thing everyone sensible would do, which is nothing.*

I unpacked a box that evening. Not to stay — I wasn't ready to admit it was to stay. Just the books. Just in case.

* * *

**Cyrus**

Monday came in gray and still, the sea like hammered tin, the kind of morning my grandfather called "holding its breath." I was at the mailbox by 11:30, which was ridiculous, because the boat didn't dock until noon and the mail didn't run the road until at least one, and I knew this, and I stood there anyway, hands in pockets, doing my impression of a person who happened to be standing at the end of her own driveway in November for no particular reason.

At 1:20 the little white postal truck came around the headland.

I made myself walk back to the house. I made myself put the kettle on. I lasted ninety seconds before I was back down the drive, and the truck was already gone, and the mailbox door was open — the carrier, bless her, had left it ajar so I'd know — and inside was a flyer for a chowder supper and a dentist reminder addressed to nobody I knew.

No envelope.

I stood there with the tin sea at my back and the dead garden at my side and the lighthouse turning its light over a bright empty afternoon, and I let the unreasonable explanations have their way with me. *She read it. She's gone. She's packing right now. I told her to come and she read* come *as* run, *and why wouldn't she, why would anyone believe a Sterling saying* stay, *when the last one told her to leave—*

I walked back up the drive and I did not cry, which I counted as a professional achievement, and I sat at the kitchen table and opened my notebook to the page where I'd been drafting the next letter — the one you write when the last one goes unanswered, the graceful retreat, *I understand, I won't write again, I wish you—* — and I couldn't do it. I sat there for two hours with the pen in my hand and could not write *I understand,* because I didn't understand, and I was so tired of writing things that weren't true.

At 4:40, in the last of the light, I heard a car on the gravel.

Not the mail truck. A car, slowing, the engine note unfamiliar, and my heart did something complicated and humiliating before I even got to the window — hope arriving faster than thought, the body knowing before the mind permits — and then I saw it: a sedan I didn't recognize, out-of-county plates, and a man getting out of it who moved like my brother, who was my brother, who stood in my driveway with a suitcase and an expression I hadn't seen since the funeral.

Daniel.

I hadn't called him. I hadn't told him anything. And he was here, now, of all the weeks, of all the days, standing between me and the empty mailbox with the chowder supper flyer still in my hand.

"Cy," he said, through the glass of the kitchen door. "We need to talk."

* * *

**Wren**

Monday, 4:40. I know the time because Juniper and I were walking home from the bus stop and she stopped dead on the harbor road and said, "Mum, look," and pointed.

The lighthouse beam, which swept past every twelve seconds, regular as a heartbeat, had stuttered. I'd have missed it. I didn't know what it meant — I'd only lived here four months — but I knew what it looked like. It looked like a pulse skipping.

"Is it broken?" Juniper asked, and her voice had that small wobble it got when the world did something it wasn't supposed to do.

"It's old," I said. "Old things stutter." But I was already looking down the road toward the lighthouse, toward the keeper's house at its base, toward the lit kitchen window where — I couldn't see from here, I couldn't possibly see from here — I let myself imagine a woman at a table with an empty mailbox at the end of her drive and my letter still in a canvas sack in Rockport, and no way to know, the way I had no way to know, that the silence meant nothing, that it was just ice fog and a backed-up ferry and the sea doing what the sea does, which is come between people who are standing forty minutes apart.

"Come on," I said, and took Juniper's hand. "Let's get home before dark."

Behind us the beam steadied, swept, held. Behind us, at the end of a gravel drive, my letter to Cyrus Sterling was still traveling — and so, though neither of us knew it, was hers to me, both of them folded in the same delayed mail, two confessions crossing somewhere over cold water, closer to each other than we were, and getting closer every hour.

I just didn't know yet that Daniel Sterling's car was already in the driveway.
