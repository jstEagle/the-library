## 29. The Gale

The barometer had been falling all day, and by four o'clock it fell off the edge of the chart.

Cyrus had watched it happen with her grandfather's ledger open on the kitchen table, one hand flat against the page as if she could hold the needle steady through sheer stubbornness. The glass dropped like a stone down a well. Outside, the harbor went the color of hammered tin, and the gulls gave up flying and sat hunched along the breakwater like old men waiting for bad news.

"Storm of the decade," the radio said, in the cheerful voice people use for weather that might kill you. "Historic low pressure system. All vessels advised to seek shelter. Coastal residents should secure loose objects."

She secured the loose objects. She battened what needed battening. And then she did what she always did when the sky turned ugly — she climbed the tower and checked the lamp.

The automated system was supposed to make her obsolete. That was the joke everyone in town made, kindly enough: *you keep a lighthouse nobody needs anymore.* But Cyrus had learned, in nine years of watching the machinery, that automation was a promise the sea never signed. The systems failed. The backups failed. The backups to the backups failed. What remained was a woman with a flashlight and a grandfather's habits in her hands.

By six the wind was screaming. Not howling — screaming, a sustained high note that made the windows flex in their frames and set her teeth on edge. Rain came sideways, freight-train rain, and the whole lighthouse shuddered around her like something alive trying to shake off its own skin.

At 7:42 p.m., the main lamp died.

Not flickered. Died. One moment the beam was sweeping out over black water; the next, the tower went dark from the inside, and Cyrus stood in the sudden absolute blackness of the lamp room listening to her own heartbeat.

*Backup generator,* she told herself. *You have a backup.*

She took the stairs two at a time, which was stupid, because the tower was swaying — not visibly, but underfoot, a slow sickening lean-and-return that her body understood before her mind did. In the base room she threw the transfer switch. The backup generator coughed, caught, coughed again.

And then it didn't catch at all.

She stood there in the flashlight's cone, water dripping off her jacket onto the concrete floor, and heard the silence where an engine should be. Fuel line, maybe. Something the last inspection had missed. Something her grandfather would have known by smell.

*Okay,* she thought. *Okay okay okay.*

There was a hand-crank auxiliary light — ancient, absurd, a relic from before her grandfather's time even. She'd oiled it once, years ago, mostly as a gesture of respect. Now she hauled it up ninety-one steps with arms that burned and set it in the lamp room and cranked until the little lens began to turn, throwing a weak white pulse out into the storm. A candle compared to the sun. Better than nothing.

It wasn't enough, and she knew it wasn't enough, and then the radio confirmed it.

*"Mayday, mayday, mayday, this is fishing vessel* Marigold *—"*

Cyrus's blood went cold in a way the wind hadn't managed.

The *Marigold*. Tom Ellery's boat. Forty feet of fiberglass and stubbornness, out past the point when the storm turned faster than anyone expected, now limping home with a failing engine and no working radar and a crew of three who were, right at this moment, trusting their lives to a light on a hill.

*"— engine trouble, taking water, requesting guidance to harbor entrance, we have visual on your light but it appears intermittent—"*

*Intermittent.* The crank light stuttered every time her arms did. Every time she paused to breathe, the beacon vanished, and somewhere out in that black chaos three men watched the only thing between them and the rocks blink out of existence.

She cranked harder. Her shoulders screamed. The wind hit the tower and the whole structure groaned like a living thing in pain, and through the salt-smeared glass she could see nothing of the world except darkness and the occasional white flash of waves detonating against the outer ledges.

The harbor entrance was a narrow throat between two rock shelves the old-timers called the Teeth. On a clear night, with a full lamp, threading it was a matter of skill. Tonight, with a dying light and forty-knot gusts, it would be a matter of grace.

She reached for the radio handset with one hand while cranking with the other, which was physically impossible, so she wedged the crank handle with her hip and shouted into the storm.

"*Marigold*, this is Port Merrow light. My primary is down. I've got auxiliary only — weak, intermittent. Do *not* attempt the entrance without me talking you in. Do you copy?"

Static. Then, thin and ragged: *"Copy... Port Merrow... we're coming in on your light... engine's gone... we're drifting..."*

Drifting. Toward the Teeth. With a candle for a lighthouse.

Cyrus looked at the dead machinery around her — the beautiful, expensive, automated machinery that was supposed to make her unnecessary — and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very clear.

Her grandfather had kept this light by hand for thirty-one years. Through storms worse than this one, they said. He'd climbed these stairs in weather that peeled shingles off roofs, because that was the job: you didn't get to be tired, you didn't get to be afraid, you didn't get to decide tonight was too much. The light kept turning. That was the whole covenant.

She picked up the crank again.

* * *

In town, Wren Calloway saw the dark beacon from her kitchen window.

She'd been packing — boxes half-filled, tape gun in hand, the house around her in that state of dismantled suspension that said *I'm leaving and haven't admitted it yet* — when the lights flickered and the wind hit the side of the house hard enough to rattle the sash windows in their frames.

Juniper was asleep upstairs, or pretending to be. Wren had checked twice.

Through the rain-streaked glass, she looked toward the headland out of habit — nine years away and she still looked toward the headland, some compass needle in her chest that had never been uninstalled — and what she saw stopped her breath.

The lighthouse was blinking. Wrong.

She knew its rhythm the way you know a family member's footsteps on the stairs. Four seconds bright, two dark, steady as breathing, visible from her childhood bedroom window, from the school bus window, from every important window of her first twenty-three years. Tonight it pulsed erratically, weakly, like a heart in trouble.

And Wren Calloway, standing in a half-packed house with a tape gun in her hand, felt certainty arrive whole and complete: *something has happened to the light, and Cyrus Sterling is up there alone.*

She didn't decide anything. Deciding implies deliberation, weighing options. There was none of that. There was just her body already moving — keys off the hook, boots, the heavy parka she hadn't worn since moving back, Juniper's door eased open an inch to check (asleep, thumb in mouth, stuffed rabbit under arm), a note scrawled on the back of an envelope and left on the kitchen table:

*Gone to help at the lighthouse. Back soon. Lock the door and stay inside. Love, Mum.*

Then she was out into the storm.

Driving to the hardware store through a gale was an act of either courage or insanity; Wren couldn't tell the difference anymore. The streets were rivers. A trash can cartwheeled across Main Street like a startled animal. The power was out — the whole town dark except for the odd emergency glow behind shuttered windows — and she drove with both hands strangling the wheel while branches came down around her like artillery.

The hardware store had a generator; she knew because she'd bought paint there, weeks ago, from a man named Gus who talked too much about epoxy. She pounded on the loading door until Gus appeared, astonished, in a rain slicker, and listened to her shout over the wind.

"I need marine-grade bulbs, fuses, whatever fits a Fresnel lens assembly, and any spare parts for a diesel generator — fuel filters, lines, anything."

Gus blinked water out of his eyes. "That's — the lighthouse? It's down?"

"It's down and there's a boat coming in on auxiliaries. Give me everything you've got."

He gave her everything he had. Two canvas bags of parts, a can of diesel additive, spare fuses, a box of heavy-duty lamps, and a look that said he wanted to come along and knew better than to argue with a woman in that particular state of face.

The coast road north was the worst drive of Wren's life. Trees down across it twice — she went around through the field verge, wheels spinning in mud, heart hammering. The wind shoved the car sideways like a hand on its shoulder. Spray came over the seawall in sheets and crashed across the windshield, blinding her for whole seconds at a time, and each time she emerged from the white blindness she checked the headland first, always the headland, and the broken pulse of the light, and drove faster.

Once, briefly, she let herself think about what she was doing. Driving into a historic storm at night to deliver generator parts to Cyrus Sterling, of all people on earth. The woman whose life she'd cracked open. The woman who had every reason to watch her drown.

*You don't get to think about that now,* she told herself. *You get to drive.*

Because here was the truth she'd been circling for months, in letters she hadn't signed and words she hadn't said: she would walk into the sea for that woman. She'd known it since the harvest letter, since the cider on the lighthouse steps, since the shed. Maybe since the hardware store, standing frozen in aisle three while nine years collapsed between them. The confession was already written; it was sitting in a mailbox somewhere, delayed by the same storm, and if she died tonight on the coast road it would arrive posthumously and Cyrus would finally know everything, and that was — God help her — almost a comfort.

The road bent. The headland appeared. And there was the lighthouse, huge and black against the boiling sky, its beam stuttering like a dying star.

Wren parked crooked at the base of the tower, left the hazards on, and fought her way to the door through wind that tried to peel her off the earth.

* * *

Inside, Cyrus heard the knocking and nearly shot out of her skin.

Nobody knocked at the lighthouse during a historic gale. Nobody knocked at the lighthouse, period. She descended the ninety-one steps on legs made of wet rope, flashlight in hand, mind running through possibilities — Coast Guard, Ellery checking in, a tree through the roof — and hauled open the door.

Wren Calloway stood on her threshold, soaked to the bone, hair plastered to her skull, holding two canvas bags like offerings, shouting to be heard over the wind:

"I brought parts!"

For one long second, neither of them moved. The storm roared. The doorway framed them against each other — the keeper and the runaway, nine years of silence and months of letters hanging unspoken in the gap between their bodies.

Then Cyrus stepped aside and Wren blew past her into the base room, already talking, already moving, shaking out the bags onto the workbench with hands that shook only slightly less than the tower above them.

"Gus helped me pick. Fuel filters, spare fuses, heavy-duty lamps — I didn't know exactly what fits, so I got everything, and there's diesel additive because if your fuel's old that could be why the generator won't catch—"

"You drove here." Cyrus's voice came out strange. Flat with shock. "Through *that*."

"The road was fine," Wren lied, dripping on the floor.

"The road is a river. Half of it's probably under trees."

"Then I drove around the trees." She looked up, and in the flashlight's glow her face was pale and fierce and utterly stripped of the careful politeness she'd worn at the hardware store, at the garden gate, everywhere their two lives had touched since her return. This was not Wren being civil. This was Wren with the varnish burned off. "Your light's failing. There's a boat out there. What else was I going to do?"

Cyrus stared at her. Somewhere overhead, the wind took another run at the tower and the whole structure leaned and groaned, and neither of them flinched, because they were both looking at each other instead.

*She came,* Cyrus thought, and the thought landed somewhere below language, below argument, below nine years of grievance. *She drove through the end of the world to bring me fuses.*

"Fuel filter," she said aloud, because the boat was still out there and the light was still dead and feelings could wait their turn. "If the filter's clogged the engine will crank but won't run. Show me what Gus sent."

They worked. That was the miracle of the next hour — that two women with a history like theirs could fall into wordless collaboration as if they'd done it all their lives. Wren held the flashlight; Cyrus crawled under the generator with a wrench, arms elbow-deep in machinery, cursing with her grandfather's vocabulary. The old filter came off black and choked with sludge. The new one went on clean. They primed the line, bled it, and Cyrus hit the starter.

The generator caught. Roared. Held.

"Yes!" Wren's fist in the air, and then they were both laughing — wild, disbelieving laughter, the kind that lives next door to crying — while the backup power hummed through the building and the lights came up gold in the base room.

"Main lamp," Cyrus gasped. "Help me carry the spares up. Ninety-one steps."

"Ninety-*one*?"

"Don't count. You'll hate me."

They climbed together, hauling bulbs and tools, the tower swaying around them, and when they burst into the lamp room — soaked, panting, alive — Cyrus killed the stuttering crank light and threw the transfer switch, and the great lens spun up, and the beam leapt out across the water, whole and white and steady.

Four seconds bright. Two dark. Breathing again.

Below them, far out in the black chaos, a tiny cluster of running lights swung toward the harbor mouth. The *Marigold*, coming home on a mended light.

Cyrus grabbed the radio. "*Marigold*, this is Port Merrow light. Full power restored. Harbor entrance is lit. Come in on my beam — slow and steady, aim for the green marker once you're inside the Teeth."

*"Copy, Port Merrow,"* came the reply, and underneath the static and the fear in Tom Ellery's voice there was something else, something close to reverence. *"God, it's good to see you. We see you clear as day. Coming in."*

They watched it happen. Both of them, shoulder to shoulder at the lamp room glass, hands braced against the frame, while the fishing boat crawled through the storm toward the narrow throat of the entrance. The beam swept. Swept again. Each pass lit the waves like a hand turning pages, and each time the light came around, the *Marigold*'s lights had crept closer, steadier, surer.

When the boat slipped inside the Teeth and the green marker swallowed it safe, Wren exhaled a breath she'd apparently been holding for twenty minutes, and Cyrus realized her own eyes were streaming — salt water, she'd have claimed, though the lamp room was dry.

"She's in," Wren said softly.

"She's in." Cyrus's voice cracked on it. She turned, and found Wren looking at her — really looking, the way you look at something you've decided to stop hiding from. Wind-burned, drenched, mascara ruined, absolutely unguarded.

"Cyrus." Her name, in Wren's mouth, over the roar of the gale. "There's something I need to tell you. I should have told you a hundred times by now. I'm—"

The tower shuddered. The radio crackled to life — the Coast Guard, relaying damage reports, asking Port Merrow light to confirm status — and duty, that old iron ring in Cyrus's chest, closed its hand around the moment.

"Hold that thought," she said, and reached for the handset. "*Port Merrow light confirms operational. Full power. Standing by through the storm.*"

When she turned back, Wren was still watching her, patient as the tide, and outside the glass the beam swept on — four seconds bright, two dark — over water that was slowly, slowly beginning to remember how to lie flat.

Whatever Wren was about to say, Cyrus understood suddenly, had been waiting nine years to say.

"Wren," she said. "Tell me."
