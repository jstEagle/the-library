## 30. Lamp Room Confession

### Cyrus

The gale came in off the water like something with a grudge.

Cyrus had watched storms her whole life from the keeper's window — her grandfather's window — cataloguing them the way other people catalogued birds. This one had a different grammar. The barometer had been falling since noon, the kind of steady, remorseless drop that made old-timers check their moorings twice, and by four o'clock the harbor was a churned gray thing and the radio was issuing warnings in that flat bureaucratic voice that always sounded, to Cyrus, like a man reading his own eulogy.

By seven the power went.

She'd been ready for it. She'd spent the afternoon the way her grandfather would have: fueling the backup generator, testing the emergency circuit, taping the windows on the seaward side of the cottage. The automated systems were supposed to handle everything now. That was the whole point of automation; that was why the Coast Guard had stopped staffing stations like this one, why her grandfather's job had become her inheritance instead of her employment. The light didn't need a person anymore.

Except when it did.

The generator caught on the third pull, coughed, steadied. Cyrus climbed the spiral stairs two at a time with the flashlight in her teeth, because she wanted to see the lamp turn over onto backup power with her own eyes, and what she saw made her stomach drop through ninety feet of masonry.

The backup circuit was dead.

Not flickering. Not struggling. Dead — the indicator panel dark except for one amber fault light blinking its small idiot semaphore. She tried the manual override. Nothing. Somewhere below her, rain was finding its way into conduits that hadn't been properly sealed since before she was born, and somewhere above her, the great lens sat dark for the first time in — she did the math without wanting to — possibly decades.

She got on the radio to the Coast Guard station at Cape Ann and heard, through the static, a voice telling her what she already knew: there was a fishing boat out there, the *Marta Lee*, overdue, last reported running for the harbor mouth. And between the *Marta Lee* and safe water lay Gull Rock, black, unlit, and patient.

"Can you get the light working?" the voice asked.

"I'm standing in the lamp room," Cyrus said. "I'll call you back."

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking with a strange detachment, as if they belonged to someone else, some other woman who had never been trained for this, who had only ever inherited it. Then she thought of her grandfather's hands — how they'd moved over this machinery like a pianist's, unhurried, certain — and she made herself breathe, and she opened the maintenance panel, and she began.

It was the fuel line. Of course it was the fuel line; it was always the fuel line; her grandfather had said so in the margins of the logbook in 1987 and again in 1994 and presumably every year in between until the automation made his marginalia obsolete. Ice or grit or salt in the feed, starved the burner. She could hear the wind trying to peel the lantern room off the tower like a lid off a tin. The glass flexed. The whole structure groaned in a register she felt in her sternum.

She worked by flashlight, kneeling on the cold iron floor, and she talked to her grandfather under her breath the way she always did in emergencies — *I know, I know, you told me, check the feed first* — and when the line cleared and she primed the burner and the mantle caught with a soft white bloom that filled the lens and threw her shadow enormous against the glass, she laughed out loud, alone, a wild sound the wind took and shredded.

The beam swung out. Swept the water. Swept it again.

Below, impossibly far down, the sea was a chaos of white and black, and somewhere in it the *Marta Lee* was running blind toward home.

Cyrus braced herself against the pedestal and watched the light go around, and around, and around, and thought: *this is what I chose.* Nine years ago, when Daniel had put his ring on her finger and a life in Lisbon on the table and she had said yes to both and meant neither — this was what she'd chosen instead. The tower. The watch. The long faithful turning. Everyone in town believed she'd been left at the altar of her own engagement, jilted by a brother too weak to say no to Wren Calloway, and Cyrus had let them believe it because the truth was worse: nobody had jilted her. She had frozen. She had looked at the wide open door of her own life and stepped backward into duty, and duty had held her, the way the tower holds, without ever once asking if she was happy.

The wind screamed. The light turned.

And then, through the stairwell door she'd propped open, she heard footsteps — impossible footsteps, someone climbing, in this, in the teeth of it — and Wren Calloway came up into the lamp room soaked to the skin and carrying a canvas bag like a woman walking into a burning building.

### Wren

Wren had never driven in anything like it. The wipers were a joke; the road was a rumor beneath the water sheeting across it; the trees along the coast road bent and thrashed like things trying to tear free of the earth. Juniper was at Odette's — thank God, thank God, thank God — doing puzzles by candlelight, oblivious, and Wren had stood in Odette's doorway for ten full seconds after hearing the news, torn clean in half.

*The lighthouse is dark. The Marta Lee's still out.*

Odette had grabbed her arm. "You know anything about generators? Because Cyrus is up there alone."

And Wren had thought — absurdly, in the middle of all that fear — *of course she's alone. She's always alone. We made sure of it, all of us, we let her be alone because it was easier than looking at what we'd done.*

She knew engines. Her father had been a mechanic before he drank himself out of it, and she'd spent her childhood handing him wrenches and learning which failures were fatal and which were just stubborn. Backup generators for lighthouses ran on diesel. Diesel lines clogged. It wasn't nothing, but it also wasn't nothing she couldn't help with.

"I have parts," she heard herself say. "My house has a cellar full of my uncle's stuff. Fittings, hose, filters. I can be there in twenty minutes."

"Cyrus won't want—" Odette started, and then stopped, because they both understood the sentence and neither wanted to finish it. Cyrus might not want Wren Calloway climbing her grandfather's tower in a hurricane. But the *Marta Lee* had three men aboard, and want had nothing to do with anything tonight.

"Tell her I'm coming," Wren said. "Or don't. Either way, I'm coming."

The drive took forty minutes and cost her about nine years of her life. Twice the wind shoved the car sideways and she corrected with both hands and her heart going like a bird in a box. When she pulled into the lighthouse lot, the tower rose out of the murk with the beam suddenly, miraculously alive, sweeping overhead in great slow strokes, and Wren sat for a moment with her forehead on the wheel and gave thanks to whatever kept the light — to Cyrus, to the dead grandfather, to the machinery itself, to anything.

Then she got out, and the storm hit her like a wall of hands.

The walk from the car to the tower door was thirty feet and the longest thirty feet of her life. Rain came horizontally, freighted with salt, and the wind picked her up by the shoulders and tried to throw her back toward the sea. She dropped the canvas bag twice. She crawled, near enough, the last ten feet, and then she was inside the base of the tower, gasping, drenched past any meaning of the word, and the door boomed shut behind her and cut the roar to something almost conversational.

She climbed. Ninety feet of spiral stairs, her legs shaking, water streaming off her everywhere, and with every step she was aware of what waited at the top: not just a broken generator and a woman with a flashlight, but the conversation. The one she'd been circling for months. The one she'd almost had in the garden shed, in the letter she'd nearly burned, on the frost-silvered steps with the cider going warm in their hands.

*There's something you should know.*

Tonight the storm had stripped away every excuse. If the world could end — if a boat full of men could be running blind toward black rock while she stood here breathing — then she could not keep carrying this. Whatever happened after, she could not carry it into another season.

She pushed open the lamp room door.

Cyrus turned from the lens, flashlight in hand, hair plastered to her face, and for one second they simply looked at each other across the rotating beam — lit, dark, lit, dark — like figures in an old film.

"You're insane," Cyrus shouted over the wind. "It's a hurricane!"

"The fuel line," Wren shouted back, holding up the bag. "Did you clear it?"

Cyrus blinked. Of all the responses she might have prepared for, that wasn't one. "It was grit. How did you—"

"My uncle was a marine engineer. I grew up elbow-deep in engine rooms." Wren crossed the floor, unsteady with the tower's sway, and knelt at the open panel. The burner was running, the lens turning, but the generator below was still down, and if the mains failed again — and it would, look at that sky — they'd be back to darkness. "Show me your setup. If your primary fails again you'll want redundancy, and right now you've got nothing."

For a moment Cyrus just stared at her. Then, wordlessly, she handed over the flashlight and pointed.

They worked. That was the miracle of it, the thing Wren would remember afterward: how quickly they fell into rhythm, two women who hadn't spoken a civil sentence in nine years, shouting part numbers at each other over the shriek of the gale, passing fittings hand to hand in the strobing light. Cyrus bled the secondary line while Wren replaced a corroded fitting from the bag. Their hands touched more than once, cold and wet and trembling, and neither of them pulled away fast.

It was while Wren was tightening the last coupling, her cheek almost against the warm iron of the housing, that the words came up out of her — not chosen, not planned, just rising like floodwater.

"I have to tell you something," she shouted. "And I can't — I can't hold it anymore, Cyrus, I physically cannot—"

"Not now!" Cyrus's face appeared above her, lit from below by the fault panel. "Whatever it is, it keeps! The boat—"

"The boat's fine, the light's fine, I fixed the light, and I need you to listen to me—"

"Wren—"

"It was me!" The words tore out of her louder than the wind, louder than she'd ever said anything in her life. "The letters — the pen pal — it was me, it was me the whole time, the librarian misfiled us and I knew and I stayed, I stayed because I couldn't stop writing to you—"

The beam swept over them. Dark. Light. Dark.

Cyrus had gone absolutely still, kneeling there with a wrench in her hand, rain or spray or maybe tears running down her face — impossible to tell, in all that water.

"And nine years ago," Wren went on, because now that the dam was broken there was no dam, there was only the flood and her voice riding it, raw and cracking, "nine years ago I broke your engagement, I took Daniel, I got pregnant and he told me to leave and I went, I ran, I let this whole town believe whatever it believed about me because I was ashamed — I was ashamed of all of it, Cyrus, do you understand? And I was sorry. I was sorry before I ever came back. I was sorry in every letter before I knew the handwriting was yours. I've been sorry since the day I got on that bus, and I have written it to you a hundred times in a hundred ways, and you forgave me — you forgave *me*, a stranger, you wrote *whatever you did, whoever you are* — and I couldn't even give you my name—"

She ran out. Out of breath, out of words, out of everything. The lamp room roared around them. The beam turned its slow white wheel over the black water, over the rocks, over the harbor where a fishing boat was even now coming home.

Cyrus knelt in the light and the dark and the light, and said nothing.

* * *

### Cyrus

Later — much later, years later, in the book she would write about all of it — Cyrus would try to describe that moment and fail, and try again, and finally settle on the truth, which was that she felt nothing at all for exactly four seconds, and then everything, all at once, in no order whatsoever.

Because the radio crackled.

"*Keeper Sterling, Keeper Sterling, this is Cape Ann, do you copy?*"

She fumbled for the handset with hands that no longer felt like hers. "Sterling copies. Go ahead."

"We've got visual on the *Marta Lee*, one mile off Gull Rock, running on your bearing. They see your light. They see your light, Sterling. Keep it turning."

"Copy," Cyrus said. "Keeping it turning."

She lowered the handset. The beam swept out over the water, and far off, at the edge of visibility, she saw it — a running light, small and stubborn, swinging wide around the black hump of Gull Rock, coming home.

Keep it turning.

That was the whole job. That had always been the whole job. Not the feelings, not the forgiveness, not the woman kneeling six feet away from her looking like a drowned ghost with the truth finally, finally out of her mouth. The light. The boat. The three men who could not see any of this and needed the beam to go around one more time, and one more, and one more after that.

So Cyrus did the only thing she knew how to do, the thing her grandfather had done, the thing the tower demanded: she turned back to the machinery. She checked the primary. She checked the secondary Wren had just given her. She checked the fuel, the pressure, the rotation. Her hands moved and her mind was a white roaring blank, and Wren knelt beside her in the shaking light and did not speak, did not press, just handed her tools when she reached for them, as if the confession had never happened, as if it were a package set down gently on a table to be opened later, when there was time.

An hour. Maybe more. The *Marta Lee* came up the channel slow and careful, riding the swells, and Cyrus watched her the whole way in, and the light turned, and turned, and turned.

When the boat's engine noise finally faded into the harbor sounds — horns, faintly, someone cheering, the whole town awake and at its windows — Cyrus set down the wrench. The wind had lost a little of its fury, or her ears had simply stopped listening to it. The lamp hummed above them, steady now, fed by two lines instead of one.

Wren was shivering violently, blue-lipped, wrung out in every sense the word had. Cyrus looked at her — at the woman who had wrecked her life, who had written her the harvest letter, who had taught her garden to grow, who had climbed a hurricane to bring her a bag of fittings — and found that she could not yet feel anything she could name. The feeling was too large. It was still out at sea.

"There's blankets in the watch room," Cyrus said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "Come down before you freeze to death."

Wren nodded. She started toward the stairs, then stopped, one hand on the rail, and looked back — and Cyrus saw that she understood. Understood that the answer was coming, but not tonight. Tonight the light had to keep turning, and so did Cyrus, one revolution at a time, around and around the thing Wren had said, until it either wrecked her or brought her home.

"Thank you," Wren whispered. "For letting me help."

Cyrus didn't answer. She killed the flashlight, and in the great sweeping glow of the lens — dark, light, dark, light — she followed Wren down the stairs, into the tower's stone heart, where the truth sat waiting between them like a third person neither of them dared look at directly.

Outside, the storm went on. Inside, ninety feet up, the lamp turned over the water, faithful and blind, lighting everything but the two women standing beneath it.
