## 32. Daniel's Version

**Cyrus**

He came on a Tuesday, which was wrong. Daniel had always arrived on Fridays — the ferry schedule made him a Friday man, all his life, the way some men are left-handed or loud. A Tuesday Daniel was a stranger wearing my brother's coat.

I saw him from the garden, standing at the gate with his hands in his pockets, looking up at the lighthouse like it owed him money. He'd gotten soft around the jaw and hard around the eyes, and he wore a wedding ring I hadn't seen before. Gold. New enough to still shine.

"You could have called," I said, wiping dirt on my jeans.

"I did call. Twice." He pushed the gate open without asking. That too was familiar — Daniel never asked for anything, he just assumed doors would be open, and for thirty years they had been.

"The phone works both ways."

"Does it." He looked at the garden — at the grafted apple rows, the turned soil, the new fence posts Wren and I had set in October — and something moved across his face that I couldn't name and didn't like. "You've been busy."

"Fall planting. It keeps me honest."

"It keeps you *here*," he said, and there it was. The old note. The one he'd been playing since we were teenagers: Cyrus stays, Cyrus tends, Cyrus is the family's ballast while the rest of us get to be ships.

I let it go. I'd gotten good at letting things go; it was either that or spend my whole life clenching.

Inside, I put the kettle on because our mother raised us to believe every crisis could be delayed by tea, and Daniel sat at the kitchen table with his hands flat on the wood like a man about to negotiate a loan. The house smelled of rain and of the bread I'd baked that morning out of pure domestic spite — Odette said I couldn't bake, so now I baked constantly, furiously, well.

"Mum's birthday," Daniel said. "You didn't come."

"I came. I left early."

"You left when—"

"When she did. Yes." I set his cup down harder than necessary. "We're doing this, then."

"We're doing what?"

"You know what. You drove four hours on a Tuesday to say her name in my kitchen, so say it."

Daniel wrapped both hands around his cup. For a moment he looked nine years old, caught breaking something, deciding whether to confess or blame the dog. Then the adult face reassembled itself, colder, and I understood that whatever he'd driven here to do, he'd rehearsed it.

"She's back," he said. "Wren. She's living here. In Gran's old house on Larkspur Lane. With a kid."

"Yes."

"And you knew."

"Yes."

"And you didn't tell me."

"There wasn't a version of telling you that ended well," I said. "I ran them all. Every one ended with you on this doorstep saying exactly what you're about to say."

His jaw worked. "You don't know what I'm about to say."

"Daniel. I've known you since the womb. Say it anyway. Get it over with. The tea's getting cold and I have grafting wax to melt."

That stung him — I watched it land, watched him decide to be wounded rather than heard. He set the cup down with a click.

"She destroyed my marriage," he said. "She walked into our engagement dinner and told your fiancée — told *Margaret* — that I'd been seeing her for months. Do you remember? Do you remember what that did to Mum? To this family? And now you're out here planting apples with her like none of it happened, like she's some lost sheep come home, and nobody — *nobody* — thought I deserved a phone call."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a gull argued with another gull about something neither would win.

"You're right," I said slowly. "I should have called. Not to warn you. But because you're my brother and you deserved to hear it from me instead of from the hardware store gossip, which is where you actually heard it, isn't it? Mrs. Pell?"

His silence confirmed it. Mrs. Pell's intelligence network remained the most reliable institution in Port Merrow.

"But that's not why you're here," I went on. "You're not here because I failed to call. You're here because you found out she's back and it reopened something, and you want me to stand where I've always stood. On your side of the line. You want me to say she's poison and I was a fool and the family was right about her all along."

"Wasn't the family right?" His voice cracked upward. "Cyrus. She broke us. She broke *you*. You were going to move to Lisbon. You had the apartment picked out. And then Margaret called it off and you just — stayed. You stayed and took the lighthouse and you never wrote another word of that book, and everyone pretended it was noble, but I watched it kill you. I watched it for nine years. So don't tell me what I want. I want my sister back."

And there it was. The whole speech, laid out like a hand of cards, and every card was true, and every card was a lie.

I sat down across from him. I made myself look at him properly — the ring, the tired eyes, the way he held himself like a man braced against a wind that had stopped blowing years ago.

"Daniel," I said. "When did you find out she was pregnant?"

**Wren**

Juniper had a fever of a hundred and one and the specific, tyrannical needs of a six-year-old who has decided her mother exists solely as furniture. I'd carried her to the sofa, dosed her, covered her, been informed the blanket was wrong, replaced the blanket, and finally been granted permission to sit nearby and do nothing useful.

So I did nothing useful. I thought about Cyrus.

It had been eleven days since the lamp room. Eleven days since I'd shouted the worst and best of myself into a hundred-mile-an-hour wind and watched her turn away to save a boat, because of course she did, because she was Cyrus Sterling and duty came out of her like breath. Eleven days of silence that I had earned and hated and kept checking the mailbox for anyway, like a dog that had been hit but still came to the door.

Mum used to say guilt was a debt you paid in installments. I'd been paying mine for nine years and the principal hadn't moved an inch.

"Mum." Juniper's voice, small and hot with fever. "Is the light working again?"

"Yeah, bug. It's working."

"Cyrus fixed it?"

"Cyrus and me. Mostly Cyrus."

"Did I meet her? At the play? The lady who laughed at the funny part?"

My chest did its new thing, the ache that wasn't entirely pain anymore. "Yes. That was her."

"She has nice hands," Juniper announced, with the authority of a fever dream, and rolled over, and slept.

I sat there in the gray afternoon light with my sleeping daughter and let myself imagine — just for a minute, just as an experiment — the letter I would write if I were brave. *Dear C. Here is everything I didn't say in the wind.* And then the other imagination, the older one, the one with teeth: Daniel's voice on a payphone outside a bus station in Reno, nine years ago, saying *if you ever contact this family again I will make sure you regret it,* and meaning it, and being able to mean it, because he was a Sterling and Sterlings closed ranks like a fist.

I'd spent nine years thinking the fist was the whole family.

Eleven days ago, in the lamp room, soaked through and screaming the truth, I'd started to wonder if maybe the fist only had one finger in it.

* * *

**Cyrus**

The question landed on the table between us and sat there, ticking.

Daniel's face did something complicated. "What kind of question is that?"

"A simple one. When did you find out Wren was pregnant?"

"That's—" He stood up. Sat back down. Stood again, because Daniel had never once in his life finished a difficult sentence sitting down. "That's not relevant."

"It's extremely relevant. It might be the most relevant thing in this kitchen."

"Why?" he demanded. "Because of your pen pal? Because of your little letters?" He laughed, ugly. "Odette told Mum. Did you know that? Odette figured it out weeks ago and told Mum, and Mum told me, and we've all been sitting here watching you fall for—" He stopped himself, but too late.

The kitchen went very quiet.

"So you knew," I said. "Before today. Before the Tuesday drive. You knew I was writing to her, and you came here to stop it, and you opened with a speech about how she broke this family." I felt strangely calm, the way I felt in storms — the calm that comes after you stop pretending the weather cares about your plans. "Okay. Answer the question, Daniel. When did you find out she was pregnant?"

"I don't see what that has to do with—"

"*When.*"

The word came out of me with the lighthouse behind it, with Grandad's voice behind it, and Daniel flinched like I'd rung a bell inside his skull.

He looked at the window. At the sea. Anywhere but me.

"June," he said finally. "Nine years ago. June."

"June," I repeated. "She left in July."

"Yes."

"So you knew for a month. You knew for a month while she was deciding what to do, and then she left, and you let this entire family believe—" My voice caught on something sharp. "You let *me* believe she abandoned you. That she torched your engagement out of cruelty and ran. That's the story. That's the story Mum tells at Christmas. That's the story I built nine years of anger on top of."

"She DID abandon me!"

"After you told her to leave." I said it quietly, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "Didn't you. You knew she was carrying your child and you told her to go."

Daniel's face went white, then red, then settled into something I'd never seen on him before: cornered. "You don't know what it was like. Dad had just died. Mum was barely functioning. You'd given up Lisbon to take the lighthouse and the whole town was watching us like we were a play they'd bought tickets to. And she comes to me — crying, terrified — and says she's pregnant, and what am I supposed to do? Marry her? Into THAT? Her mother was—"

"Don't." One word, flat as a keel. "Finish that sentence and you leave through the window."

He didn't finish it. But he didn't apologize either, which told me everything.

"I told her it wasn't the time," he said instead, quieter. "I told her we'd figure it out later, after things settled, after Mum was okay, after — I don't know. After. And she looked at me like I'd slapped her, and two weeks later she was gone, and honestly, Cyrus? Part of me was relieved. Part of me thought, good, problem solved, no scandal, no baby at the funeral, no mess. I was twenty-six and a coward and I told the woman I supposedly loved to disappear, and she did, and then I got to be the victim." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound. "I got to be the victim for nine years. It fit great. Everyone was very sympathetic."

The rain had started again, soft against the glass. Somewhere below us the sea worked at the rocks, patient, indifferent, keeping its own time.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "Really. Not the speech version. The real version."

Daniel sank back into his chair. All the fight had gone out of him and left something smaller sitting there, wearing his clothes.

"Because you're happy," he said. "I saw you at Mum's birthday, before you left early. I saw you look at her. And I thought — if Cyrus forgives her, then it's real. Then it wasn't a tragedy, it was just... me. What I did. Just mine." He rubbed his face with both hands. "If you forgive her, I lose the story where I'm the one she wronged. And I've been living in that story so long I don't know what's on the other side of the wall."

I should have comforted him. Some part of me, the part trained by thirty years of being the family's ballast, reached for the comforting words automatically — *it's okay, we'll figure it out, you were young* — and I let those words rise, and I watched them, and I did not say them.

That was the moment. I want to be precise about it, because it took me years to understand and it happened in about three seconds: my brother confessed to destroying a woman's life, and my first instinct was to make HIM feel better, and I looked at that instinct, and I declined it.

"No," I said.

Daniel looked up. "No?"

"No, I won't side with you. No, I won't help you keep the story. No, I'm not going to stand on your side of a line you drew to hide what you did." I stood up. My legs were steady. Nine years of steady, apparently, saved up for this. "Here's what I see, Daniel. I see a man who told a scared twenty-four-year-old to vanish, and then spent nine years letting his sister hate that woman so he wouldn't have to hate himself. And I see that I helped. I held the line. I was the best wall you ever had, because my anger was real even though my reasons were wrong, and you hid behind it like a seawall."

"That's not fair—"

"Nothing about this is fair. Ask Wren. Ask her daughter, who's six and has never met her father's family because you told her mother to disappear." I picked up his untouched tea and poured it down the sink. "You want your sister back? Fine. Here she is. This is her. She plants apples with the woman you exiled. She's going to keep doing it. And whether she ever forgives you is between you and God and Wren Calloway, and I promise you, all three of them are less interested in your comfort than you think."

For a long moment Daniel just breathed. Then he stood, and straightened his jacket, and became once more a man who assumed doors would be open.

"You sound like Grandad," he said at the door, and it was meant as an insult, and I received it as the finest compliment of my life.

After the door closed, I stood in the quiet kitchen with my heart going like a gale and realized I was shaking — not from fear. From the specific vertigo of putting down a weight you've carried so long you'd forgotten it was heavy.

Then I remembered what else he'd said. *Odette told Mum. Mum told me.*

Which meant Mum had known for weeks. Which meant Mum had sent Daniel here — or at least hadn't stopped him — to break this up before it grew roots.

Family spell, broken. Family spell, apparently, having one more layer.

I got my coat.

* * *

**Wren**

The knock came at seven, when Juniper was finally asleep and I was standing in the kitchen eating cold soup over the sink like a person with no future.

Through the window: Cyrus. Wet from the walk up Larkspur Lane, no umbrella, jaw set in a way I recognized from the lamp room — the look of a woman arriving at a decision at speed.

I opened the door before she could knock twice.

"Your brother was just here," she said. "He told me about June. Nine years ago. He told me he knew, and he told you to leave."

The soup tin was cold in my hand. The hallway was cold. Everything was cold except, absurdly, my face, which had gone hot enough to steam.

"Okay," I managed.

"He also told me the family's known about us for weeks and Mum's been managing it." Cyrus's breath fogged in the doorway. "So I have two choices tonight. I can go home and be furious about that. Or I can be here." She looked past me, down the hall toward the sleeping house, toward the small room with the glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling that I'd never explained to anyone. "Can I be here?"

And I stepped aside, and the door swung wide, and somewhere in Port Merrow a mailbox sat empty, holding nothing, waiting for nothing — because everything we'd been mailing each other for a season had just walked up the garden path and knocked.
