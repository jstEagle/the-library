## 33. The Empty Mailbox

The library closed at seven. Wren knew this because she had looked it up three times that week, the way a person checks the tide tables before deciding not to swim.

At six forty-five she stood in her kitchen with a mug of tea she hadn't drunk, watching the window go from gray to blue to the color of bruised plums, and told herself — for the fortieth time since noon — that she was not going.

The reveal event started at seven. Odette Marchand had pinned flyers to every post in town: *HARBORLIGHT ANONYMOUS PEN-PAL PROGRAM — SEASON'S END REVEAL! Come meet the stranger who knows your heart!* There would be paper lanterns. There would be a folding table with two stacks of cards. There would be Cyrus Sterling, standing under the fluorescent lights of the community room, waiting for a stranger who was never going to walk through that door.

Wren set the tea down untouched and went upstairs to pack.

* * *

She had gotten good at packing over nine years. She could break down a life in an afternoon if she had to — she'd done it in three cities before Port Merrow, always leaving before the boxes got labels, before the neighbors learned her name, before anyone could look at her walking away and call it what it was. Running was a skill like any other. You got faster. You got quieter. You stopped crying in the car until you were already on the highway.

But Port Merrow had been different. She'd meant it differently. She'd signed the deed on the house her great-aunt left her and told herself: *this one is arrival, not escape.* She'd bought paint the color of sea glass. She'd let Juniper enroll in school without keeping a bag packed by the door, which for Wren was roughly equivalent to other people skydiving.

And then there were the letters.

She sat down on the floor of her bedroom, surrounded by open boxes, and pulled the ribbon-bound bundle out of the nightstand drawer where it had lived all season. Forty-one letters. She'd tied them with the grosgrain ribbon off a birthday present months ago, back when they were still a joke between her and herself — *look at you, archiving your own disaster* — and now the bow wouldn't come undone and she didn't try very hard.

She didn't need to reread them. She knew them the way you know a song you've sung in the shower for a year. She knew the first stiff one, hers, all *I trust this finds you well*, written in a hand she'd forced into unfamiliarity so the librarian's matching card wouldn't give her away. She knew the soil letter, the storm letter, the harvest letter — *a daughter, about six, I imagine; someone who ran once and has been running ever since* — and she knew Cyrus's reply, the one Wren had read eleven times, the one that said:

*I don't know what you did, and I'm glad I don't. It means I forgave the person and not the crime. Whoever you are: come home. Whatever home is now.*

Come home. And Wren had sat at her kitchen table with that letter and known — cold water certainty, chapter and verse — that the woman writing it was Cyrus Sterling, that the woman offering blind forgiveness was the woman whose engagement she'd broken, whose brother she'd loved and lost and fled from, whose grandfather's grave she'd never dared visit. And she had kept writing anyway. Knowing. That was the part she couldn't forgive herself for, tonight, with the lanterns presumably lit across town: not the leaving, not even the lie, but the greed of it. She had taken forgiveness under a false name the way you'd take bread from a table where you weren't welcome, and eaten every crumb, and called it hunger.

The mailbox had been empty this morning. Of course it had been empty. The last letter had gone unanswered for nine days — nine days since the gale, since the lamp room, since Wren had shouted the whole ugly truth into the wind while the beam swept over them and Cyrus had looked at her with an expression Wren would be unpacking for the rest of her life, if she lived long enough to unpack anything again.

*You knew,* Cyrus had said afterward, on the wet gravel, hair plastered to her face, the fishing boat's crew safe and celebrating behind them. Not shouting. That was the worst part. Cyrus Sterling, it turned out, did her devastation quietly. "You knew it was me. The whole time."

"I did." No point lying in a gale's aftermath. "From almost the beginning."

"And you stayed."

"You stayed too," Wren had said, and immediately wished she hadn't, because it sounded like a defense and it wasn't one, and because Cyrus's face had done something complicated and gone shut.

That was nine days ago. Nine days of silence, and today the flyer on the library door said *REVEAL*, and Wren understood the shape of the thing perfectly: Cyrus had waited for the event because the program gave her a graceful exit. Why write a rejection when you can simply not show up? Why say *I despise you* when the empty chair says it for you, politely, in front of the whole town?

Except Wren wasn't going either. So the chairs would be empty on both sides, and somewhere Odette Marchand would be standing at that folding table with two unmatched cards, and the story would end the way these stories ended — not with a bang but with a clerical error quietly corrected.

"Right," Wren said aloud, to the boxes. Her voice came out steady, which felt like fraud. "Books first."

* * *

Packing took until ten. She worked methodically, top floor down, the way she always did — books, linens, kitchen last, because the kitchen was the room that argued. Every cup she wrapped in newspaper seemed to have a history. The blue mug Juniper had picked out at the church rummage sale. The colander her great-aunt had left in the cupboard, dented, still good. She wrapped them anyway. Sentiment was a luxury for people who weren't leaving.

Juniper was supposed to be asleep. At half past ten Wren went up to check and found the bed empty and the closet door ajar, and her heart did the parental free-fall it did maybe twice a year, before she heard the small sounds from behind the door — the careful rustle of a child trying to be quiet, which is louder than any noise a child makes on purpose.

"Juniper."

A pause. Then, guiltily: "I'm helping pack."

"You're supposed to be asleep."

"I couldn't sleep." Juniper emerged from the closet holding her pillowcase, which was not full of pillow. It bulged strangely. Six-year-olds are terrible smugglers. "Can we not move?"

Wren crouched down. In the hallway light Juniper's face was doing something Wren recognized from mirrors — the effortful blankness of a person holding a feeling underwater. "Sweetheart, we talked about this. We're going to stay with Gran for a little while, just till I figure out—"

"You said that about the last house." Juniper's chin came up. She had Daniel's chin, God help them both, stubborn as a mooring post. "You said a little while and then we moved again and my fish died in the car."

"We'll get another fish."

"I don't want another fish." And then, in the voice that undid Wren more thoroughly than any accusation ever had, the flat, reasonable voice of a child stating facts: "I want the lighthouse."

Wren's hands went still on the pillowcase. "What?"

"The lighthouse." Juniper hugged the pillowcase to her chest. "My friend at the lighthouse. You write to her. I saw you put the letters in the box by the harbor, the blue one, lots of times. And you always come back happy. Even when you're sad you come back happy." A pause, the devastating pause of a six-year-old assembling a sentence she doesn't know is a knife. "Is she why we're leaving?"

The hallway was very quiet. Downstairs the refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside, late October wind moved through the bare maple, and Wren thought: *this is it, this is the bill coming due.* She had lied carefully all season — to Cyrus by omission, to Odette by charm, to herself by craft — and she had somehow forgotten that she lived with the one witness who watched her mail the evidence.

"Her name isn't 'my friend,'" Wren said finally. "Well. It is. But she has a real name too."

"What is it?"

"It's — complicated, bug."

"You always say that." Juniper's lower lip set. "Miss Odette says secrets are just stories that aren't finished yet."

"Miss Odette says a lot of things." Wren pulled her daughter in, and Juniper came, smelling of sleep and crayons and the particular warm dust of childhood, and Wren held her and looked over her head at the dark window, past their reflection, toward the point where the lighthouse stood. Even from here she could see it — the white flash, four seconds, patient as breathing. Automated now, needing no one, and yet Cyrus climbed to it anyway, tended it anyway, kept faith with a dead man's habit. *Duty,* Wren had thought once, dismissively, back when duty was the thing that had ruined everything. Now it looked like something else. Now it looked like love that had nowhere else to put itself.

"Go to sleep," she whispered into Juniper's hair. "We'll talk tomorrow. I promise we'll talk."

Juniper pulled back, studied her mother's face with those too-honest eyes, and nodded — but at the door of her room she turned around, and instead of getting into bed she knelt at the little desk in the corner, and Wren, pretending not to watch from the hallway, watched her daughter draw.

It took ten minutes. Juniper drew the way she did everything, with total commitment and no eraser: a tall white tower, a fat yellow starburst at the top, rays going out in all directions like the sun had decided to live there. Below it, three figures. One tall, one medium, one small, holding hands in a row, standing on a green rectangle that was unmistakably, given the wobbly rows of dots, a garden.

"There," Juniper said, satisfied. She folded it — badly, a trapezoid — and before Wren could ask what it was for, she marched to the suitcase open on Wren's bed, lifted the flap of the outer pocket, and pushed the drawing inside. Deep. Deliberate. The way you plant something.

"That's for later," she announced. "When you change your mind."

"Juniper—"

"When you change your mind," she repeated, with the terrifying serenity of the very young, "you'll know where it is," and she got into bed and was asleep, apparently, within minutes, breathing slow, one arm flung out, done with the whole conversation in the way children are permitted to be done with things.

Wren stood in the doorway a long time.

Then she went downstairs, took the drawing out of the suitcase, and put it back. She didn't know why. She told herself it was because repacking it would crease it further, but that was a lie, and she was too tired to make better ones. The truth was that some part of her — the part that had kept forty-one letters tied in ribbon, the part that had stayed in a town she'd sworn was temporary, the part that had driven spare parts through a historic gale with her heart in her throat and no thought in her head but *she's up there, she's up there alone* — that part wanted the drawing in the suitcase. Wanted future-Wren, some ordinary Tuesday in some other town, to unzip the pocket and find the tower and the three figures and have to sit down on the floor of wherever she was and fall apart properly, once, for all the times she hadn't let herself.

Let her suffer accurately, her great-aunt used to say. It saves time.

* * *

Midnight found her in the kitchen with the last box half-packed and the ribbon bundle in her lap.

She should burn them. That had been the plan, hadn't it — the letter she'd almost burned back in the early days, the confession she'd sent instead, the whole season of paper evidence of the worst thing she'd ever done. Burning was clean. Burning was what a sensible woman did with proof. She even had the matches right there, in the jar by the stove, because Port Merrow kitchens still kept matches for the pilot lights like it was 1975.

She struck one. Held it to the corner of the bundle. The ribbon caught first — grosgrain burns fast, curling black — and Wren looked at the flame eating toward the top letter, the first one, *I trust this finds you well*, and thought: *this is the handwriting she'll remember me by. This is the only version of me she ever got to keep.*

She shook it out.

Sat there in the dark with her heart slamming and the smell of singed ribbon and the unburned letters in her lap, and laughed — one short, awful sound, nothing like humor. She couldn't even destroy things properly. Nine years of practice at leaving and she still couldn't leave.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been avoiding all night, the sum that wouldn't come out right: if Cyrus despised her, the mailbox would be empty. Fine. But Cyrus was not a woman who left things unsaid. Cyrus was a woman who wrote letters to strangers forgiving crimes she didn't know the names of. If Cyrus were done — truly done — there would be a letter. Cold, final, well-composed, probably with a paragraph structure. There would be *something*. The silence wasn't contempt. Wren knew contempt; she'd earned contempt; contempt had a temperature.

This was something else. This was a woman standing in a burned field deciding whether anything could grow there. And Wren had spent the season teaching her, letter by letter, that things could — and then torched it herself in a lamp room with the truth.

Or. Or.

Or the silence meant *I am done with paper*, and the answer was coming in person, and it would not be kind.

Both possibilities kept her up until two. Both possibilities fit the facts. She lay in bed listening to the wind and understood, finally, the specific torture she'd designed for herself: she had made Cyrus Sterling into a letter-writer, and now she had to live like everyone else, reading the silence, guessing at the postmark of a heart.

In the morning she would finish packing. In the morning she would load the car, and wake Juniper gently, and drive out past the harbor one last time, and not look at the point, or look at it once, quickly, the way you look at a wound to check it's still healing wrong.

She slept badly. She dreamed of envelopes.

And at six-forty-something in the morning, gray light, gulls starting up, she was awakened by the sound every letter-writer knows — the small iron clank of the mailbox flag at the road, raised by a hand or dropped by a delivery, impossible to tell from the house, ambiguous as everything else in her life.

Wren was out of bed and at the window before she was fully awake, breath fogging the glass, looking down the long gravel drive to the road.

There was a car at the mailbox. Dark green, parked at an angle, engine running. And standing beside it, coatless in the cold, hair loose, holding something white in both hands like an offering or a verdict—

Cyrus Sterling looked up at the window, and did not look away.
