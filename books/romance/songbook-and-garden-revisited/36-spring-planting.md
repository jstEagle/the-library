## 36. Spring Planting

The apple trees bloomed the third week of April, all at once, as if they'd been holding a meeting and finally reached consensus.

I came out at six in the morning with my tea and stopped dead at the garden gate. Five grafted trees in a row, scrawny things I'd babied through a winter that had tried its best to kill them — and every one of them wearing white. Blossoms so thick they looked like snow that had decided to stay. Behind them, the lighthouse stood gray and patient against a pink-washed sky, and the whole keeper's plot smelled like something I didn't have a word for. Sweet, but not sugar-sweet. Older than that. Like a room you'd been away from for years and walked back into.

My grandfather had grafted from the original tree — the one his own mother had planted in 1948 — onto rootstock I'd bought mail-order from a nursery in Oregon because nobody within fifty miles sold anything but ornamentals. He'd written the technique out in his ledger in his cramped, patient hand, and I'd followed it like scripture, and I'd told myself the whole time that it was about the trees. About the garden. About finishing something he'd started.

It had not been about the trees.

"You're staring at them again."

Wren came through the gate with two mugs and Juniper trailing behind her in rain boots she'd insisted on wearing despite the total absence of rain. Juniper had opinions about the boots. The boots were, as far as I could tell, her entire personality some days. I respected that in a six-year-old.

"I'm assessing," I said.

"You're staring."

"Assessing is staring with a clipboard."

Wren handed me a mug. Tea, not coffee — she'd learned in February that I drank tea in the morning and coffee only when I was writing, and she'd filed that away the way she filed everything, quietly and completely. She stood beside me and looked at the blossoms and didn't say anything for a while, which was one of about four hundred things I loved about her and had not yet said out loud, because we were both still living in the country of Almost, where the borders were friendly but nobody had quite stamped anyone's passport.

"They took," she said finally.

"They took." I heard my own voice do something embarrassing and cleared my throat. "Grandfather said they would. He said the tree remembers what you do for it. I thought that was just a thing gardeners said."

"Everything he said turned out to be a thing that was true." Wren blew on her tea. "Which is annoying, in a dead man."

"He'd have liked that you find him annoying. He found everyone annoying. It was how he loved people."

Juniper had already abandoned us for the raised beds, where she crouched with the gravity of a field researcher. She had a stick. The stick was, I gathered, a surveying instrument.

"Cyrus," she called, without looking up. "The worms are back."

"Are they."

"Eleven of them. I counted." She held up a worm on her palm with the tenderness of someone presenting a crown jewel. "This one is named Deborah."

"Deborah," I said gravely. "Welcome back."

"She was in the compost. Mum says that means the compost is working."

"Your mum is right. Worms in the compost means the compost is alive."

Juniper considered this, then deposited Deborah back into bed three with the care of a nurse tucking in a patient. Then she looked up at me with the expression that had been dismantling me piece by piece since October — the expression of a child who had decided, without ceremony or negotiation, that I was part of the architecture of her life.

"Can we plant our row today?"

"Today," I confirmed. "Peas, radishes, carrots, and the sunflowers along the fence."

"And the pumpkins?"

"The pumpkins go in after the last frost. Your mum will tell you it's still too cold."

"Mum always says it's too cold."

"Your mum is from California. She has no natural relationship with cold. She's an immigrant to the concept."

"I heard that," Wren said, into her tea.

"You were meant to."

* * *

The town had absorbed us the way the tide absorbs a piece of driftwood — without comment, without fuss, as though we had always been part of the shoreline.

I noticed it first at the hardware store, which felt fitting, given that the hardware store was where the whole thing had nearly come apart in a frozen silence two winters' worth of Octobers ago. Dale behind the counter now said "the usual for both houses?" when either of us came in, and had, at some point, quietly started a tab we shared. Mrs. Okonkwo at the bakery put an extra roll in the bag when Juniper came in with either of us, and had long since stopped asking which of us it was for, because the answer had stopped being a thing with edges.

Nobody said *the couple at the lighthouse*. Nobody said anything at all, really. That was the Port Merrow way — the town took you in the way the harbor took boats, by simply making room and not remarking on it. Odette said it was because the town had watched the whole pen-pal thing unfold with the patience of people who read serials in the newspaper and had simply decided the ending was satisfactory. I said Odette had watched the whole thing unfold because she'd cheated and matched the handwriting in November. She said that was called *editorial initiative*. We had this argument approximately monthly. It was one of the pleasures of my life now, which told me something about how my life had changed.

But it was more than the town's discretion. It was that we had stopped being an event and started being a fact. Wren's car in my drive on a Tuesday meant nothing except that Wren's car was in my drive on a Tuesday. Juniper's drawing on my refrigerator — the lighthouse, the three of us holding hands, a worm labeled DEBORAH — was not a statement. It was a refrigerator.

I had spent nine years being a fact in a different way: the keeper's granddaughter, the woman who stayed, the sad story people were polite around. Facts could be load-bearing. They could also be a wall you lived inside. The difference, I was learning, was whether anyone had chosen the fact or whether it had simply accumulated around you like weather.

This one had been chosen. Repeatedly. On days when choosing it would have been easier not to. Wren had unpacked one box in March of last year — one box, in a driveway, with her whole life still in the car — and then unpacked the rest, and then signed Juniper up for school in September, and then in October she'd stood in my kitchen and said, "I'm going to keep being here. I want you to know that's a decision and not an accident." And I had said, "Good. Because I'm bad at accidents," and we'd both laughed at how badly we'd said it and how completely it had worked anyway.

Domestic joy, I thought, watching Juniper dig a hole for a pea seed with the enthusiasm of someone excavating a tomb, is not a small thing. It's a large thing that has agreed to be quiet.

* * *

We planted the row in the late morning, the soil finally warm enough to crumble instead of clump. Wren had brought her own tools — she'd bought them in November, a small trowel and a hand fork, and had said nothing about what it meant to buy tools for a garden that wasn't technically hers, and I had said nothing back, and the tools had lived in my shed ever since, which made the shed a thing with her in it even when she wasn't.

Juniper planted peas in clusters of four and five because she could not bear to put a single seed in a hole alone. "They get lonely," she explained. I did not tell her that peas actually do better crowded. Some truths you just get handed.

Wren worked the carrot row, her hands moving with the competence I'd first met through an envelope — *your soil is nitrogen-starved, add aged manure in fall, and for God's sake stop watering the leaves, you're growing mildew, not kale* — the letter that had made me sit down on the garden wall and think, *whoever this is, I'd follow their advice off a cliff.* I had followed it into something better than a cliff. I'd followed it into this.

"Your hair's doing the thing," Wren said, not looking up.

"What thing."

"The thing where the wind takes it and you pretend not to notice."

"I'm wearing it as a crown."

"You're wearing it as a warning to other hair."

Juniper looked up from her peas, delighted. "Do mine," she demanded, and Wren, hands filthy, made a show of sculpting Juniper's already-wild curls into a shape that defied both gravity and taxonomy, and Juniper ran a lap of the garden to test its aerodynamics, and I stood there with dirt under my nails and tea going cold in my hand and thought: *this is the scene. This is the scene I could never write.*

Because that was the thing I'd finally understood, finishing the novel in that three-day fever last autumn — the thing the letters had taught me without either of us intending to teach it. I'd spent years thinking the story was about the grand gesture. The storm, the lamp, the confession shouted over the wind. And those things had happened, and they'd mattered, and I'd never trade them. But the novel hadn't come alive until I wrote the quiet parts. The keeper's heroine, feeding the cat. The runaway, learning which cabinet held the good mugs. The whole architecture of two people deciding, in increments, in Tuesdays, to be a household.

The storm was just the day the quiet became visible.

"Deborah's watching," Juniper reported, crouched at bed three again. "She likes sunflowers."

"Deborah has excellent taste."

"When the sunflowers grow, can they be as tall as the lighthouse?"

"No," Wren said.

"Taller?"

"No, Juniper."

"Almost as tall?"

"Almost," I said, and Wren shot me a look over Juniper's head that said *you're making my life difficult*, and I shrugged in a way that said *I'm making your life better*, and Juniper, who missed nothing and understood less and was happy about both, went back to her worms.

* * *

In the afternoon, while Juniper napped in the window seat of the keeper's cottage — a nap that had migrated, over the winter, from her own house to mine without anyone scheduling it — Wren and I sat on the lighthouse steps with the last of the tea and the first of the sun.

"I got a letter from the library today," she said.

My stomach did the thing it did. It still did the thing, even now. "Odette?"

"Odette." Wren turned the envelope over in her hands. She'd opened it; a card slid halfway out, cream-colored, the program's stock. "She wants to do a make-up reveal event. For the season we — " She made a small gesture that encompassed the entire catastrophe and miracle of it. "Apparently the town's been asking. Apparently people want closure."

"People want a party. Odette wants an audience."

"Both things can be true." Wren looked at the card but didn't pull it all the way out. "She says we could read the first letter. And the last one."

The first letter. *Dear Pen Pal, I am participating in this program under protest and at the insistence of a friend who has threatened my livelihood, such as it is. I have been told to 'share something true,' so: I do not want to be here.* I had written that at my kitchen table with the wind coming up off the water, furious at Odette, furious at the world, certain I was sealing myself into another season of comfortable loneliness. And Wren had read it in her inherited house full of boxes, knowing — she told me later, the night of the gale, shouting it over the wind — knowing from the handwriting that it was me, and had written back anyway.

*Dear Pen Pal, I'm glad you don't want to be here. Neither do I. Perhaps we can be unwilling together.*

"Odette will cry," I said. "Odette will orchestrate her own crying. There will be a moment where she dabs her eyes with a handkerchief she brought specifically for the purpose."

"There will be a moment where she takes credit for the entire relationship."

"She did match the handwriting."

"She cheated."

"She *initiated*."

Wren laughed — the laugh I'd first heard across a hardware store aisle, frozen and polite, and had since heard a hundred times, unfrozen, unpolite, aimed at me. Then she went quiet, and I knew the quiet. It was the quiet she still went into sometimes, the one where nine years stood in the doorway.

"You could say no," I offered. "It's your story too. More of it's yours than mine, honestly."

"I know." She turned the card over. "It's just — reading them out loud. The first one. Everyone will know what we were to each other before we knew."

"Everyone already knows. Odette's known since November. Dale's known since the tab. Mrs. Okonkwo has known since the extra rolls. The only person who thought it was a secret was Juniper, and Juniper thinks Deborah the worm is a secret too."

"Deborah IS a secret."

"Then we're all compromised."

Wren smiled, but she was still holding the card like it might go off. I understood that too. For a woman who had once left a town rather than be seen in it, standing up in the library and reading aloud the evidence of being known — being known *on purpose*, by a whole room — was not a small thing. It was a large thing that had not yet agreed to be quiet.

"Think about it," I said. "No deadline. Odette's deadline is a suggestion with stationery."

"It's not the reading." Wren looked out at the water, at the light just starting to think about its evening shift. "It's that she's asking us to stand up there as the *example*. The success story. And I keep thinking about the people in the audience who signed up this season and got matched with someone who didn't write back. Or wrote back mean. Or — " she stopped, and I let her stop. "I keep thinking about who I was when I mailed that first letter. I don't want to stand on a stage and pretend the distance between then and now was easy."

"It wasn't."

"No."

"But you crossed it."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Then she leaned her head against my shoulder, a small weight, a fact, a thing chosen. "You always know which sentence to say."

"I'm a novelist. It's the only skill I have."

"You have others." She nodded toward the garden, the white row of trees, the beds with their crooked rows and their crowded peas. "You grew this."

"We grew this."

"We grew this," she conceded, and I felt the concession like a blossom opening — small, white, absurdly hard-won.

Inside, Juniper slept on, and the lamp room above us stood ready for its shift, and the garden held its breath in blossom, and I thought: this is what taking root looks like. Not the dramatic taproot in the storm. The fine white threads, underground, everywhere, holding.

* * *

I walked them home at dusk, because I always did, because the walk was twelve minutes each way and I had never once resented a single one of them. Juniper rode on my shoulders and narrated the neighborhood's cats by name, most of which she had invented. Wren carried the empty seed trays and walked with her shoulder against mine, which was its own kind of sentence.

At her gate she paused. "I'm going to do it," she said. "The reveal. The reading. I'll tell Odette tomorrow."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah." She looked back toward town, toward the library's lit window at the end of the street. "But Cyrus — she said the whole town's been asking. And she said something else in the letter. Something about *special guests*."

"Odette's idea of a special guest is anyone she can put in a chair facing an audience."

"She said, and I quote, 'I've invited someone I think you'll both want to see, and no, I won't tell you, and yes, I've already sent the invitation, and no, you can't unsend an invitation, this is a library, we believe in the finality of the post.'"

The wind came up off the water, carrying the blossom-sweet smell back down the hill from my garden, and I stood there at Wren's gate with Juniper asleep against my neck and felt the first cold thread of premonition go through the warm evening.

"Odette," I said, "has never in her life respected the finality of the post. She returns things."

"I know." Wren's eyes had gone careful. "I've been trying to figure out who she'd invite that she thinks we'd *both* want to see. And Cyrus — I've only come up with one name. And I don't want it to be that name."

Down the hill, the lighthouse lamp came on, right on schedule, and swept its long white arm across the water, across the town, across the two of us standing in a doorway with a question neither of us wanted to hold.

"Daniel," I said.

Neither of us said anything else. The light turned, and turned, and the evening got very quiet around the name.
