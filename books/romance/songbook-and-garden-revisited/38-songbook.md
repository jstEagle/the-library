## 38. Songbook

The email arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Nothing in my life worth having had ever arrived on a dramatic day — not grief, not forgiveness, not the letter that changed everything. It all came on Tuesdays, between the grocery flyers and the harbor association minutes.

I was standing at the kitchen counter with a mug of coffee going cold when I saw the subject line: *Re: Submission — The Keeper's Daughter.*

My hands did something strange. They went very still, the way they do at the top of the tower when the wind shifts and you have to decide, instantly, whether to trust it.

*Dear Ms. Sterling,* the email began, and then a paragraph of publishing language I read three times without absorbing a word of it, until I found the sentence buried in the middle like a lamp in fog:

*We would be honored to publish this novel.*

I sat down on the kitchen floor. Not dramatically — there was no chair within reach, and my legs made the decision for me. I sat on the cold tile in my grandfather's old sweater and read the email a fourth time, out loud, because some things need air in them before they're real.

"We would be honored," I told the refrigerator.

The refrigerator declined to comment.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about finishing a book: the ending isn't the hard part. The hard part is the dedication page, which is one line long and took me eleven days.

I drafted forty of them. I wrote them on envelopes, on the backs of seed packets, on the margin of the tide table. *For Odette, who bullied me into everything good.* True, but incomplete. *For my grandfather, who kept the light.* Also true, also incomplete — he'd had his dedication already, in the book I'd abandoned at twenty-six. *For Juniper, who asked about the sea.* That one made me cry into the grafting wax, so I set it aside.

In the end it was Wren who solved it, though she didn't know she was solving anything. She said it one evening in October, standing in the garden with her hands full of the last of the carrots, dirt to her elbows, hair escaping its clip in every direction. I'd been complaining — a hobby of mine by then — about how the novel felt finished but I couldn't find the words to give it away properly.

"You're overthinking it," she said. "You always overthink the small sentences. The big ones come easy; it's the little ones that cost." She snapped a carrot top off and flicked it toward the compost. "Just tell the truth in under ten words. You're a keeper. Keep it short."

So I kept it short. When my editor asked for a dedication, I typed one line and hit send before I could draft a forty-first version:

*To my pen pal, who edited my life.*

* * *

Odette found out before anyone else, because Odette finds out about everything approximately four days before it happens, a phenomenon I've stopped trying to explain.

She arrived at the lighthouse on Saturday morning carrying a lemon cake with no stated occasion, which in Odette's language means *congratulations, I know, don't ask how.* She let herself in through the garden gate — she'd earned gate privileges years ago — and found me repotting the rosemary cuttings Wren had rooted from the plants we'd saved during the first frost.

"Harborlight Books," she said, setting down the cake. "Small press, good taste, decent advance. Your agent called my cousin's wife, who works in their publicity department, who mentioned a debut novel by a lighthouse keeper from Port Merrow with a dedication that's already making the interns cry."

"Nothing's announced."

"Everything's announced. You just haven't been told yet." She helped herself to coffee without asking, which was also traditional. "Dedication page, Cyrus. *To my pen pal, who edited my life.* Do you understand what you've done? By spring, every woman in Maine who ever wrote to a stranger is going to buy this book and sob on the ferry."

"That was not the goal."

"What was the goal?"

I thought about it honestly, because Odette can smell a dishonest answer the way gulls smell fish guts. "I wanted her to see it," I said. "Before anyone else. And I wanted it to say what actually happened, which is that a stranger read my sentences and refused to let me lie in them. Everything else — the plot, the characters, the whole architecture of the thing — I built. But she's the reason any of it stands up straight."

Odette looked at me for a long moment over the rim of her mug. Then she said, quietly, in a voice she almost never used, "You love her."

It wasn't a question, but I treated it like one anyway, because I'm a coward in exactly one arena and we both knew which one.

"I'm working up to saying it," I said.

"To her?"

"To anyone. Out loud. In weather." I wiped my hands on my thighs, leaving a rosemary-scented smear. "You have to understand, Odette. I spent nine years being fluent in silence. I can go months without saying a true thing. She got me writing letters before she got me talking. The letters were easy — paper doesn't look at you while you say it. But standing in front of her, with her actual face—" I shook my head. "I've written an entire novel. I've climbed a tower in a hundred-mile-an-hour wind. And I still haven't managed six words."

Odette set down her mug. She took my face in both hands, the way she used to when we were nineteen and I'd get stuck inside my own head for weeks at a time, and she said, "Cyrus Sterling. That woman drove through a historic gale with a box of spare parts on her passenger seat because your light went dark. She has known for a year. She's been waiting for you to catch up. Stop making her wait in the rain."

"She's not waiting in the rain. She's planting things in my garden."

"Even worse," said Odette. "She's putting down roots. Hurry."

* * *

I told Wren about the book that same evening, or rather I tried to, and it went badly in the best possible way.

She came by after work — she'd taken over the paint counter at Callahan's Hardware, half-days, enough to feel like a person and not so much that Juniper's school pickup became a crisis — and found me pacing the kitchen with my phone in my hand like it was a live grenade.

"You're doing the thing," she said, hanging her coat on the hook that had become hers sometime in November without either of us discussing it. "The pacing thing. What happened?"

"Sit down."

"No." She crossed her arms, but she was smiling. "Tell me standing up. Standing-up news is either terrible or wonderful and I want to watch your face."

So I watched her face instead. "They want the novel," I said. "Harborlight Books. Spring list. Real publication, real cover, the whole — Wren, they said *honored.*"

She made a sound I'd never heard from her before, somewhere between a laugh and a sob, and then she crossed the kitchen and grabbed me by both shoulders and shook me, once, hard, the way you'd shake a jar of paint to mix it. "Of course they did. Of course they did, you impossible woman, it was never in question, I told you in the third letter—"

"You told me the heroine was passive and my prose was 'competent at best.'"

"And I was being generous, because I liked you." Her eyes were shining. She didn't wipe them. "A book. Cyrus. An actual book with your name on it, sitting in stores, in libraries, where strangers—" She stopped. Something moved across her face, quick as a shadow of a bird. "Where strangers will read it."

"Strangers read books. That's generally the arrangement."

"Yes," she said faintly. "Yes, of course. It's wonderful. I'm thrilled." And she was — I could see that she was, genuinely, underneath whatever else was moving through her. But there was a flinch in it too, a small animal startle, and I recognized it because I'd seen it before: at the hardware store, the first week; whenever anyone mentioned the reveal event; every time the past reached for her and she wasn't sure whether it meant to hold on or to shake her.

"Wren." I said it gently. "Whatever you're afraid of — people knowing, people connecting it back to you, to Daniel, to all of it — the book isn't about that. Not really. It's about a keeper who learns to stop hiding behind duty. Nobody in it is named anybody."

"I know." She exhaled. "I know that. It's just — being visible again. After so long being careful." She laughed at herself, wet-eyed. "Nine years of keeping my head down and now the woman I love writes a book. Of course she does. Of course."

I heard it then. Maybe she heard it too — the way the sentence had arrived without permission, without ceremony, without the six-word speech I'd been drafting for weeks. *The woman I love.* Just set down on the kitchen floor between us like a dropped key.

Neither of us picked it up. Not yet. But neither of us stepped around it, either. We stood in the kitchen with the truth lying there, gleaming, and Wren turned pink to her ears and said, "I'll make dinner," and I said, "You absolutely will not, we're getting lobster rolls to celebrate," and we went, and the truth rode in the truck with us the whole way, quiet as a passenger, patient as a keeper.

* * *

The ribbon came later. The ribbon came, in fact, on the worst possible evening, which is how the important things usually arrive.

It was mid-December, the night of the first hard freeze, and I'd gone down to the boathouse to dig out storm shutters and found instead a cardboard box I hadn't opened since I moved in — my grandfather's, marked in his blocky carpenter's hand: *MISC. KEEP.* Inside, under a coil of rope and a brass barometer, was a photograph I'd never seen: my grandfather, younger than I'd ever known him, standing on the lighthouse steps beside a young woman I took a moment to place, because she was laughing in it, and I had only ever known that laugh in its present tense.

Wren. Twenty-three, maybe twenty-four. One hand on the railing, one hand shading her eyes against a sun nine years gone.

I carried the photograph upstairs and sat with it at the kitchen table until headlights swept the window and Wren's truck crunched into the drive. She came in stamping snow off her boots, calling something about the roads, and stopped mid-word when she saw my face.

"What is it?"

I turned the photograph around.

She went still in the doorway, snow melting on her shoulders, and for a long moment the only sound was the radiator ticking and the wind testing the east windows. Then she crossed the room slowly, sat down across from me, and took the photo in both hands like it was something living.

"He gave me tea," she said finally. "That summer. I used to walk out here when Daniel and I fought, and your grandfather would be on the steps no matter the hour, and he'd say, 'Come sit, girl, the light doesn't need two of us watching it.' He never asked what was wrong. He just talked about the weather and the boats and the names of stars, and somehow I'd leave feeling like a person again." Her thumb traced the edge of the photo. "He knew. I think he always knew everything and said nothing. Keepers."

"There's something on the back," I said, because there was, in pencil, faded: *W.C., Aug. Kind girl. Tell her the door's open.*

Wren read it twice. Then she put the photograph face-down on the table, very carefully, and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes, and cried — not the controlled crying she did at funerals and school plays, but the other kind, the kind with noise in it, nine years' worth.

I went around the table and held her. It was awkward. Chairs were involved. Neither of us cared.

When she could speak again, she pulled back and wiped her face with her sleeve and said, "There's something I've never shown you. I was going to save it for — I don't know. A better moment. There are no better moments. There's just this one." She dug into her bag, the canvas one she carried everywhere, and brought out a bundle wrapped in a dish towel.

Inside was a stack of letters tied with ribbon. Green ribbon, frayed at the bow, the kind you'd buy at Callahan's for ninety cents. Every letter I had ever sent her — the stiff first one, the hostile-adjacent ones, the harvest letter, the storm-night letter with the coffee ring on the corner — all of them, in order, kept.

"I wrote to you for a season," she said. "You wrote back like you were answering a summons. And then somewhere around the fourth letter I realized I was checking the mailbox the way other people check the weather. So I started keeping them. All of them. Even the mean one about my soil advice."

"That letter was fair."

"That letter was *cruel,* and I read it maybe sixty times." She untied the ribbon with fingers that weren't quite steady and spread the letters across the kitchen table, and I saw that some were soft at the folds from rereading, and one had a water stain shaped like a small island, and the last one — the most recent, from two weeks ago — still smelled faintly of the woodstove smoke it had traveled past.

"It's a songbook," she said simply. "That's what I've been calling it, in my head, since the beginning. Every letter a verse. Not music, exactly. But the same shape — something repeated until it becomes true, something you carry, something you can't stop humming even when the words hurt." She looked up at me, and her eyes were red and clear at once. "You asked me once, in a letter, why I stayed in the program when I suspected who you were. I never answered honestly. Here's the honest answer: because you were the first person in nine years who wrote to me like I was worth arguing with. Because every envelope from that lighthouse felt like a door opening. Because I loved you by the sixth letter and I was too much of a coward to say it, so I kept the evidence instead."

The kitchen was very warm. The wind had given up on the east windows and settled into something almost gentle. Somewhere below us the lamp turned, steady as a pulse, sweeping its long arm across the black water, and I understood suddenly that I had been drafting a six-word speech for weeks when the words had never needed to be six, or short, or clever. They only needed to be true.

"I love you," I said.

No fanfare. No tower, no gale, no grand gesture. Just a woman in a kitchen, holding a green ribbon, telling another woman the plainest sentence in the language.

Wren closed her eyes. When she opened them she was smiling the smile from the photograph — the one I'd only ever known in its present tense, and now, finally, in all its tenses at once.

"I know," she said. "I've known since the dedication page. *Who edited my life.* Cyrus, do you have any idea what it's like to open a submission email your girlfriend forwarded you and find yourself on the dedication page of a novel?" She laughed, wet and bright. "I cried in the break room at Callahan's. Gus thought I was having a medical event."

"You read it already?"

"I've read everything you've ever sent me at least twice." She gathered the letters back into their stack, unhurried, reverent, and held them against her chest. "This is the first draft of us. Someday — not yet, but someday — I want Juniper to read these. I want her to know that people can be wrong about each other for nine years and still find their way to the same kitchen table. I want her to know love isn't a second chance. It's a first honest one, if you're brave enough to write it down."

Outside, the snow had thickened, fat flakes turning slow in the beam of the lamp. Inside, Wren stood up, took my hand, and led me to the window where we could see the light sweep — dark, bright, dark, bright — the oldest rhythm either of us knew.

"One more verse," she said softly, and kissed me, and the songbook on the table waited, patient as a keeper, for whatever we wrote next.

We were still standing there, wrapped in each other and the lamplight, when the phone rang — Odette, breathless, with news that would turn our quiet winter inside out: the library wanted to host the launch. The whole town. In April. With a reading.

And Odette had already, she informed me, volunteered us both.
