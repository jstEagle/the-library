## 39. Revisited

The paint was called Harbor White, which Wren considered a small act of fraud, because harbor white was really just gray with ambitions. She said as much to Cyrus, who was up the ladder with a brush in her teeth and her hair tied back with what appeared to be a length of twine from the garden shed.

"It's called Harbor White because it's the color of the railings," Cyrus said around the brush. "Not because it's the color of the harbor."

"That's circular reasoning."

"That's branding." She took the brush out of her mouth and pointed it at Wren like a conductor's baton. "My grandfather bought this exact paint for thirty years. If I switch brands now, he'll haunt me, and he was a stubborn enough man alive that I don't care to test him dead."

Wren dipped her own brush and laid a stripe of gray-with-ambitions along the top rail. The morning was cool and bright, the kind of early autumn day that Port Merrow did better than anywhere she'd ever lived — air like rinsed glass, the water flat enough to hold the lighthouse's reflection whole. One year. Almost exactly one year since the gale, since the lamp room, since she'd shouted the worst and truest things about herself into the wind and been answered not with a slap but with a boat saved and a silence she'd deserved.

She had learned to live inside that year the way you learn to live in a house: where the floor creaked, which windows stuck, where the light came in at four o'clock and made everything look forgiven even when it wasn't quite.

"Mum." Juniper was sitting on the bottom step with her legs straight out in front of her, wearing overalls two sizes too big because she'd declared jeans "too grabby" and Cyrus had produced the overalls from a box of her grandfather's things without a flicker of hesitation. Juniper now wore them constantly. There was a paint smudge on one knee already, and they'd been on her body for eleven minutes. "Mum, why does the light turn at night but not in the day?"

"Because ships can see the land in the daytime," Wren said. "They only need help when it's dark."

"But how do they know when it's dark? The light doesn't have eyes."

Cyrus snorted from the top of the ladder.

"The keeper decides," Wren said. "Or nowadays the timer decides. But there's always somebody who could decide if they needed to."

"Is Cy the somebody?"

"I'm the somebody," Cyrus confirmed. "It's a terrible job. You have to know things."

"What things?"

"Things like why the fog signal used to sound different when your grandfather was in a bad mood," Cyrus said, coming down a rung. "True story. He'd hit the manual override when the automatic horn annoyed him, and it had this little hitch in it, like a cough. Fishermen could tell his mood from shore by the horn. Your great-great— " she caught herself, recalibrated without stumbling, "—Mr. Sterling, the old keeper, he was famous for it."

Juniper absorbed this with the gravity of a person taking minutes at a meeting. "Was he sad a lot?"

"No," Cyrus said, and Wren watched her choose the honest answer over the easy one, the way she'd learned to do, the way they'd both learned to do, slowly, expensively, over twelve months. "He was particular. It's different. Sad is heavy. Particular is just — knowing exactly how you like things and being unwilling to pretend otherwise."

"Like Mum with the mugs."

"Exactly like Mum with the mugs."

"The chipped one goes in the back of the cupboard," Juniper recited, "because a chip is a wound and wounds face the wall."

Wren nearly dropped her brush. "I never said *wounds face the wall*."

"You say it with your hands," Juniper said serenely, and went back to drawing a lighthouse on the step with her finger in spilled paint.

* * *

They worked through the morning. Cyrus took the seaward rail, Wren the landward, and Juniper rotated between them carrying the smaller brush and applying paint mostly to herself. The rhythm of it was so ordinary it kept catching in Wren's chest like a fishbone — this, this ordinary thing, three people and a railing and a bucket with a rusted handle, and none of it performance, none of it provisional. A year ago she would not have believed she got to keep mornings like this. A year ago she'd been packing boxes with a child's drawing of a lighthouse tucked between the plates, certain that love was a country she'd been deported from.

She'd been wrong about the deportation. That was the thing nobody told you: sometimes you weren't banished. Sometimes you'd just been standing on the wrong side of your own door.

"Cyrus," she said, because the thought was too big to hold silently and too important to save for later, "do you ever think about how we met twice?"

Cyrus didn't stop painting. "Constantly. It's the best story I have. Better than anything I've written."

"We met before we met."

"And then spent nine years getting it right." Cyrus looked down at her from the ladder, and the sun was behind her so Wren had to squint, which felt accurate somehow. "Odette says we did it backwards. Met as enemies, fell in love through paper, introduced ourselves last."

"Odette says a lot of things."

"Odette is usually right, which is infuriating." Cyrus climbed down and set the ladder aside, surveying their work. Half the railing gleamed wet and new; half still showed the salt-scoured gray of the winter just past. "People talk about second chances like they're consolation prizes. Like the first attempt burned down and you get to rebuild with cheaper lumber."

"What would you call it instead?"

Cyrus considered. She was a woman who considered, Wren had learned. Even now, even after a year, even when the answer was simple, she gave words room to arrive properly. It had driven Wren half-mad in the letters. It made her weak at the knees in person.

"A first chance," Cyrus said. "Honestly taken. Everything else was rehearsal."

* * *

At noon they ate sandwiches on the steps, facing the water, because Cyrus had rules about eating with your back to the sea — her grandfather's rule, inherited without discussion, like the paint. Juniper sat between them, swinging her feet, her heels drumming the stone in an arrhythmic code.

"Why did the boat almost crash last year?" Juniper asked. This was a question she asked periodically, in different forms, and for a long time Wren had flinched at each version. Now she let it land.

"Because of the storm," Cyrus said. "And because the lamp failed."

"And you fixed it."

"Wren fixed it. I just carried parts and shouted."

"You carried parts *up a tower* in a *storm*," Juniper said, with the emphasis of someone who understood storms to be roughly equivalent to dragons. "Mum tells it different. Mum says you were very brave and also extremely rude."

"I was not rude."

"You said a word that made Mum laugh and then Mum said 'don't repeat that.'"

Cyrus looked at Wren over Juniper's head. Wren looked back with the serene expression of a mother who had absolutely repeated it to Odette within the week.

"There were extenuating circumstances," Cyrus said gravely. "There was a boat. And the part wouldn't fit. And I had been awake for twenty hours."

"So the word was because of the part."

"The word was entirely because of the part."

Juniper nodded, satisfied, and bit into her sandwich. Wren watched the two of them — the keeper and the keeper's accidental family — and felt the old ache arrive and pass through her without stopping, the way weather passes through a place that has finally built proper shelter. For nine years she had believed the story of herself was simple: she had broken a thing and run. It wasn't false. But it had been incomplete, and incompleteness, she'd learned, was its own kind of lie. She hadn't known Daniel had told her to go. She hadn't known Cyrus had frozen rather than been jilted. She hadn't known that everyone in that old story had been lying to themselves and calling it kindness.

The truth had come out in pieces, over months, in kitchens and gardens and one memorable evening in the lamp room with tea going cold between them. Each piece had hurt less than she feared and healed more than she hoped. That was the arithmetic of honesty, she'd decided. Terrible exchange rate going in. Miraculous one coming out.

"Mum," Juniper said, mouth full, "when I grow up, can I be a keeper?"

"You can be anything," Wren started, and Cyrus, gently, put a hand on her arm.

"What do you think a keeper does?" Cyrus asked Juniper.

"Keeps the light. Keeps the garden. Keeps the letters in the ribbon." Juniper counted these off on paint-stained fingers. "Keeps us."

The sea made its long sound against the rocks below. Wren found she couldn't speak for a moment, and Cyrus, looking straight ahead at the horizon, said quietly, "Then yes. You're already one. You've been one since you drew the lighthouse on the suitcase."

"You knew about the suitcase?" Juniper gasped, betrayed and delighted.

"I read your mum's letters. All of them. It's sort of my job now."

* * *

In the afternoon the wind picked up from the southeast, soft but insistent, and Cyrus went up to check the lamp housing out of pure habit, and Wren stood in the garden — *their* garden now, though the apple rows still argued about ownership in the way that grafted things do, old wood and new wood making one tree whether the tree likes it or not — and let herself do the accounting she only allowed on days like this.

One year ago: a house with a for-sale sign, boxes in the car, a daughter's drawing smuggled into the luggage like contraband hope.

Now: the sign long gone, the house painted a color Juniper had chosen ("Sunrise Yellow," aggressively orange), a row of beans and a row of something Cyrus insisted was kale and Wren insisted was a weed with a publicist. A drawer in the lighthouse kitchen that held Juniper's crayons. A hook by the door that held Wren's coat, because Cyrus had installed it without comment one Tuesday and never mentioned it again, and that, more than any speech, had been the moment Wren knew she was home.

She had not gotten a second chance. She wanted to write that somewhere, carve it into something, say it aloud at some town event until people rolled their eyes. A second chance implied the first one had been real and squandered. But her first chance had been a forgery — a life built on leaving before she was left, loving at a safe distance, telling herself that disappearance was a form of mercy. What she had now was the first attempt at the actual thing. First chances were supposed to be clumsy. Nobody warned you they might come at thirty-four, with a child and a history and a paintbrush in your hand, and feel like the beginning of everything.

"Mum! Mum, look!"

Juniper stood at the base of the tower, pointing up. Cyrus leaned out of the lamp room, waving, silhouetted against the bright sky, and Juniper waved back with her whole body, both arms, total commitment, the way children wave and adults forget to.

"She says lunch is ready!" Juniper translated, though no such message had visibly been conveyed. "She always says lunch is ready when she waves like that!"

"Does she," Wren said, walking over, taking her daughter's paint-crusted hand.

"It's her code. She has codes for everything. The horn means grumpy, the wave means lunch, and when she touches her nose it means she loves you but she's not going to say it in front of people."

Wren stopped walking. "Does she now."

"I'm not supposed to tell you that part."

"Too late. It's in my head forever."

Juniper considered the magnitude of what she'd done, shrugged with philosophical acceptance, and pulled her mother toward the sandwiches.

* * *

Evening came the way it did in September, gold going to rose going to that brief blue hour when the lighthouse's beam woke and began its patient turning. They stayed on the steps past bedtime, all three, wrapped in one blanket that was technically Cyrus's grandfather's and functionally communal property. Juniper fell asleep against Wren's shoulder mid-sentence, mid-question — something about whether fish knew about the light, whether they thought it was a star that fell in love with the water — and her breathing went slow and salt-sweet.

Cyrus reached over and adjusted the blanket across the girl's knees. Her hand lingered a moment, then withdrew, and she looked out at the darkening water.

"She asked me yesterday if she could call the tower 'ours,'" Cyrus said. "I said it already was."

"When did she ask you that?"

"Tuesday. When we were thinning the carrots. She asks you things and me things, you know. Splits her questions between us like she's keeping both banks funded." Cyrus smiled faintly. "Today's question for me was whether you'd ever leave again."

Wren's breath caught. "What did you tell her?"

"The truth. That you tried to, once, and it's the great regret of your life. And that regret is a good teacher but a bad home, and you don't live there anymore." Cyrus turned to look at her, and in the blue dusk her face was half shadow, half the reflected pulse of the rotating lamp. "That's the answer I believe. Not because you promised it. Because you unpacked."

Wren laughed, wet-eyed. "You romantic. Unpacked."

"One box at a time. It's the most honest love story I know." She paused. "I put it in the book, you know. The unpacking. Odette cried. She claims it was allergies."

"It's never allergies with Odette."

"No. It's never allergies with anyone. We just prefer the fiction because it lets us cry in public without explaining ourselves." Cyrus settled back, her shoulder finding Wren's, the contact easy and worn-in as the blanket. Above them the beam swept, swept, swept, counting out the dark like a heart that had never once considered stopping.

Wren watched the light go around and thought about the girl she'd been at twenty-five, standing in this same town with a secret under her ribs and a train ticket in her pocket, believing that love was a thing you survived by leaving early. That girl had been so tired. So sure that staying was a trap other people were building for her. She wanted to go back and sit beside her on the harbor wall and tell her: you're not running from a punishment, you're running toward a life, and it takes nine years and one storm and forty-odd letters and a six-year-old with paint on her knee, but you get there. You actually get there.

Not a second chance. A truer first one. Earned the hard way, kept the harder way, renewed daily like a tide chart.

"Mum," Juniper murmured, surfacing halfway from sleep, "is the light going to stay on all night?"

"All night," Wren said. "Every night."

"Who keeps the keeper?"

Wren looked at Cyrus. Cyrus looked back, and touched her nose.

"Everybody takes turns," Wren said.

Juniper sighed, satisfied, and slept. Below them the garden held its breath in the dark, the grafted trees standing in their rows, old wood and new wood, blossoms waiting in next spring like letters not yet opened. And far out on the black water, the beam turned, and turned, and turned — steady, unhurried, keeping its faithful count of everything the light touched, and everything it had finally, after all these years, been allowed to reach.
