## 40. The Light Keeps Turning

*Cyrus*

The ledger smelled the way it always smelled in October — of salt-softened paper and lamp oil and the faint ghost of my grandfather's pipe, though the pipe had been gone eleven years and the tobacco with it. I opened the book flat on the watch desk, the way he had taught me to open it, pressing the spine gently with both palms so it would lie honest instead of fighting me.

*October 14th. Wind SW, freshening toward evening. Barometer steady, then falling after four. Sea: moderate, long swell from the southeast. Lamp: lit at 6:41.*

My handwriting. His columns. The same book he had kept by hand for thirty years, and that I had kept by hand for eleven more, long after the automation made it unnecessary, because some rituals are not about necessity. Because a record is a kind of promise. Because someone, someday, might want to know what the weather did on an ordinary Tuesday in the fall, and I wanted them to be able to find out.

I had written the entry early, before the light came on, the way I always did — logging the day as I understood it, leaving the lamp line to be filled in at the end, when the work was actually done. It was a habit Wren had teased me about once, in the spring, standing in the garden with dirt to her elbows.

"You write your own weather report before the weather's finished," she'd said. "That's very like you."

"How do you mean, very like me?"

"You decide the shape of the day and then you live into it." She'd shrugged, smiling, not meaning it as criticism. "I used to do the opposite. Let the day decide me. Look how that went."

I had thought about that a great deal since. About the two of us, and the two methods, and how neither one had made us happy alone.

Now I sat in the watch room with the ledger open and the evening coming down over the water, and I waited for the light to come on — 6:41, give or take a minute, the way it had come on every night of my life whether I watched it or not — and I thought about handwriting.

Because that was the thing I'd been circling all week, the thing I wanted to get right.

The lamp came on. I felt it before I saw it, the way you feel a hearth light in a cold room — a small change in the quality of the air, a warmth with no temperature. Through the watch room glass the beam swung out and swept the water, and I did what I had done ten thousand nights and would do ten thousand more: I checked it. Sweep, dark, sweep. The rhythm of it. My grandfather's rhythm, and his father's before him, and now mine.

Then I picked up the pen.

Not for the ledger. Not yet. For the other thing — the small stack of paper I'd started keeping beside it a month ago, after the night Wren had come up to the lamp room with me during the equinox gale and stood at the glass with her hands flat against it, watching the beam fight the rain, and said, quietly, "She's beautiful when she's working."

*She.* The lighthouse. Wren talked about the lighthouse the way other people talked about ships, or horses, or children — with that particular tenderness people reserve for things that work hard without complaint. She'd been here nearly every evening that month. Juniper did her homework in the watch room chair, feet swinging, and Wren and I sat on the gallery or in the garden or, when it rained, at this desk, and the days had taken on a shape I hadn't decided in advance and hadn't let decide me either. A third method. We were inventing it as we went.

I wrote, in the margin of a scrap page, the way I drafted everything — fast, unguarded, trusting the trash bin later:

*The light doesn't choose what it shines on. It turns, and everything in its path gets lit, and then it turns again. My grandfather kept it by hand for thirty years and never once, in all those pages, wrote about being lonely. I used to think that was stoicism. Now I think it was company. You don't get lonely keeping a light when you understand that the light is a form of speech. It says the same thing every night to everyone: you are seen, you are seen, you are seen.*

I looked at it for a while. It wasn't the novel. The novel was done, was at my editor's, was — God help me — scheduled. This was something else. The other book I hadn't known I was writing: the one made of ledgers and letters and evenings, the one with no plot except continuation.

The watch room door opened below, and I heard the two sounds that had become, without my ever deciding it, the sound of my evenings: the slap of the screen door, and Juniper's voice going up the spiral stair ahead of her mother's, breathless with whatever had happened at school that day.

"Cyrus! Cyrus, we got the eggs, and one of them has *two* yolks, and Mum says that's lucky but I think it's just a chicken who got confused—"

"Both can be true," I called down. "That's usually how luck works."

* * *

*Wren*

I let Juniper go up first because I needed a minute in the garden, and because I had learned — was still learning, would probably always be learning — that the minute was worth taking.

The garden in October was going down gracefully. The beans were finished and the stalks turned to straw in their rows; the squash had gone from ambitious to enormous to harvested, and the last of them sat on the kitchen windowsill downstairs, orange as small suns. But the grafted trees — my trees now too, I supposed, though I still felt a small astonished jolt every time I thought of them that way — the grafts had taken. All four of them. In the spring they would blossom, and in some future October they would fruit, apples from Cyrus's grandfather's tree growing on new roots in soil I had taught her to feed and she had taught me to love.

I stood in the dark among the beds and did the thing I did now instead of the thing I used to do. The thing I used to do was rehearse my leaving: what to pack, what to abandon, which version of the story I'd tell. I had done it in Port Merrow nine years ago and in two towns since, and I could do it in my sleep, and for a while this fall I had caught myself doing it here — the old reflex, the suitcase mind.

The thing I did now was stand still and look at what was actually here. Dirt under my nails. Juniper's laugh going up the stairwell like a struck bell. The beam of the lighthouse passing over the garden every few seconds, laying a stripe of light across the bean rows and taking it away again, patient as breathing.

You are seen, you are seen, you are seen. Cyrus had told me once that was what the light said. She'd said it in the lamp room with the wind screaming and the truth just out between us, and I had thought it was the most frightening sentence I'd ever heard, because I had built my whole life on not being seen.

I wasn't built that way anymore. Building took longer than breaking; that was the whole unfair joke of it. But the new structure held. I checked it the way Cyrus checked the lamp — nightly, without drama, the way you maintain anything you intend to keep.

I went inside and up the stairs.

The watch room was yellow-lit and warm, and it was full — it had been full all autumn, this room built for one keeper and now holding three, and nobody had ever said anything about it, it had just happened, the way the garden had happened, the way the family had happened. Juniper was at the desk with her math homework spread over the weather ledger's spare pages, because Cyrus had ruled that the ledger was sacred but the scrap paper was communal, and Juniper had taken the ruling as a license and colonized the entire desk. Cyrus stood at the glass with her arms crossed, watching the water, and when I came in she didn't turn around, but she shifted — a small movement, six inches to the left, making room at the window without looking, without needing to look.

I stood beside her. The beam went out, swept, came home.

"Two-yolk egg," I said. "Juniper's declared it a miracle and a clerical error simultaneously."

"She gets that from your side."

"She gets everything from your side. The stubbornness, the seasickness, the—"

"I don't get seasick."

"You got seasick in the harbor in July. In the *harbor*, Cyrus. On a boat that never left the mooring."

"That was the herring sandwich."

"Whatever you need to believe." I leaned my shoulder against hers, and she leaned back, and we watched the light work.

After a while she said, "I'm doing the entry," and something in her voice made me look at her. She had the ledger open to tonight's page, and she was holding her pen the way she held it when she was about to do something that mattered and wanted to pretend it didn't.

"I know," I said. "You do it every night."

"I know I do it every night." She turned the book slightly toward me. "I want you to do it with me."

The room went very quiet. Even Juniper looked up from her math, sensing — the way children sense — that something was happening above her pay grade.

"The weather's half yours now," Cyrus said. She was looking at the page, not at me, and her ears had gone faintly pink, and I loved her so much in that moment that it was less a feeling than a fact, like the barometer falling. "You read the sky better than I do. You knew that squall was coming off the water three days before the forecast did. My grandfather would have hired you on the spot." A breath. "And it's not just the weather. It's — the year. I want the year recorded properly. Both hands."

*Both hands.* I looked at the ledger, at the columns in his handwriting and then hers, thirty years and eleven years of one woman's family keeping faith with the sea, and I understood what she was asking. Not to be added. To be *entered*. Written in. Made part of the record.

"Wren," Juniper whispered, tugging my sleeve. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face you did when we got the house."

I laughed, which ruined the dignity of the moment entirely, and Cyrus laughed too, and then she handed me the pen, and I took it, and my hand was steady.

* * *

*Cyrus*

She wrote small. That was the first thing I learned about her handwriting in the ledger, though I'd known her handwriting for a year by then — had known it before I knew I knew it, back when it was only a stranger's neat, slanted hand arriving in my mailbox and making me feel, inexplicably, like home. She wrote small and slightly forward-leaning, as if every word were leaning into a wind she'd decided to walk through anyway.

She took the weather from me — wind, barometer, sea state — and read it over, and added one thing I hadn't written: *First frost expected within the week. Garden beds mulched and put to bed.*

Then she paused, pen hovering, and looked at me.

"Go on," I said. "Whatever it is."

She wrote, in the margin, beneath the day's record, where my grandfather had once logged *Lamp: lit at 6:41* in his careful hand, and where I had logged the same thing in mine for eleven years:

*Lamp: lit at 6:41. Keepers: two.*

I read it and my throat did something I decided not to interfere with.

"That's not standard notation," I managed.

"No," Wren said. "It's better than standard."

Below us, the beam swept the water. Out past the point, the fishing fleet rode at anchor with their riding lights on, a little scattered constellation, and somewhere beyond them the dark held the open sea, and the light went out to meet it and came back, went out and came back, the way it had every night of my life.

My grandfather had believed — he'd written it once, in the front of the very first ledger, in ink gone brown with age — that a lighthouse was not a building. *It is a sentence,* he'd written, *spoken to the water, over and over, forever, and the work of a keeper is simply not to stop speaking.* I'd read that as a boy and thought it was about duty. I'd spent most of my life thinking duty and love were opposites, the two roads, the choice you had to make — the lighthouse or the life, the staying or the going, and I had chosen staying and called it sacrifice and let it make me small.

I had been wrong about the sentence. It was never only duty. It was never only anything. The light doesn't choose between the rocks it guards and the water it loves. It turns. It shines on all of it. The staying and the shining were the same act, and I had only ever needed someone to stand beside me at the glass to see it.

Juniper had finished her math and fallen asleep in the watch chair, the way she did, her pencil still in her hand and her notebook sliding. Wren went and lifted her — six years old and getting heavy, the good kind of heavy, the kind that means growing — and Juniper murmured something about two yolks and put her face into her mother's neck, and I turned down the watch room lamp so the stair would be lit gently for them.

At the door, Wren looked back at me over our daughter's head.

"Coming down?"

"In a minute. I want to finish the entry."

She nodded. She understood. She was the only person alive who understood that the last lines of the day's record were a kind of prayer, and that you said them alone, and that you were not lonely saying them.

I sat back down at the desk. The ledger lay open, his hand and mine and now hers, three keepers in one book, and outside the glass the light swept the dark water — out, and home, and out again — and I picked up the pen, and I wrote the last line of the day, and it was the truest thing I had ever put in the book:

*Lamp: lit at 6:41. All hands home. The light keeps turning.*

* * *

*Wren*

Down in the garden, on my way to hang Juniper's jacket by the door of the keeper's cottage — our door now; the keys had been quietly, permanently transferred sometime in September without ceremony, the way all the truest things in Port Merrow happened — I stopped once more and looked up.

The lamp room glowed. Behind the glass, a small figure bent over the great desk, writing by lamplight the record of an ordinary Tuesday: the wind, the swell, the frost to come, the two of us. In a few minutes she'd come down the spiral stair, and we'd eat the two-yolk egg boiled and split three ways, because Juniper had declared it must be shared to work, and then we'd read, and then we'd sleep, and in the morning the light would go off at dawn the way it always did, and the day would come up unscripted, and we'd live into it together, both hands.

Nine years ago I had left this town in the dark, driving through the night with everything I owned and a secret I hadn't yet told anyone, and I had looked in the mirror once at a rest stop and seen a person I didn't want to be, and I had thought: *that's it, then, that's who I am.* I had been wrong. That's the thing I'd tell her, someday, when she was old enough to need it — that's what I'd tell Juniper, and what I'd whisper to Cyrus on the nights she got quiet and started rereading her own old guilt like it was scripture. You are not the worst thing you did. You are not even the story you tell about the worst thing you did. You are what you do next, and next, and next, the light going around, and around, and coming home.

I looked up at the lamp room one more time. The beam swept out over the garden, over the mulched beds and the sleeping grafts and the four small trees that would blossom in the spring, and it touched everything, and it left nothing out.

You are seen, you are seen, you are seen.

I went inside, and closed the door softly behind me, and the light kept turning.
