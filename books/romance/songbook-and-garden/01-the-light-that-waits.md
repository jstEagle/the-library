## 1. The Light That Waits

The solstice came in on a grey tide, the way it always did, and Vesper Winterbourne met it the way she met every December: alone, with a kettle on and her grandfather's inventory ledger open to a page she had been pretending not to read for three days.

*Item 41. Brass oil measure, dented. Item 42. Barometer, glass cracked, still honest.*

She wrote in his hand when she catalogued — small, upright letters, no flourish he hadn't earned — because it felt less like trespass that way. The lamp room above her turned its slow rotation whether or not anyone watched it, which was the whole point of a lighthouse, her grandfather used to say. *A light doesn't need an audience, Vesper. It needs only to be lit.* She had taken this as wisdom at nine years old. At thirty-two she suspected it had also been a warning.

Outside, the Cornish sea worked at the rocks below with the patience of something that had all of eternity and intended to use it. The wind had teeth today. It found the gaps in the window frames and whistled through them in a thin, complaining note, almost musical, almost like someone trying to remember a tune.

Vesper poured tea she wouldn't finish and turned to the shelf where the unopened letter lived.

It had arrived eleven days ago, cream-coloured and expensive, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than her last royalty statement earned per quarter. *VANE DEVELOPMENT GROUP* embossed in the corner in silver-grey, tasteful as a headstone. She knew what it said. She had known since the first phone call in October, since the man with the pleasant voice explained about heritage exemptions being reviewed, about coastal erosion liabilities, about *an opportunity for a dignified resolution*. The Vane people never said *demolition*. They said *decommission*, they said *site clearance*, they said *the inevitable arithmetic of the coastline*. As if the lighthouse were a sum that refused to come out right.

She picked up the letter now. Held it. Put it down again by the kettle, face-down, the way you'd turn over a photograph of someone you weren't ready to grieve properly.

There. Filed. She was very good at filing things.

The truth — and she permitted herself this truth only in the hour before dusk, when the light had to be lit and her hands had work to do — was that the lighthouse was the one promise she had never broken. Everything else, she had broken beautifully. Thoroughly. With style, even, if style counted for anything, which at two in the morning it did not.

She climbed the ninety-one steps to the lamp room, counting them as she always did, because her grandfather had counted them, because somewhere in the family mythology there had been a great-aunt who'd counted them during the war, and so the number was less a measurement than a rosary. Ninety-one steps between Vesper Winterbourne and the rest of her life.

At the top, the great lens waited in its housing, brass and crystal, patient as scripture. She trimmed the wick out of habit though the lamp had been electric for decades; she checked the rotation mechanism; she wiped salt haze from the glass with a cloth kept solely for that purpose. Then she stood while the sun went down over the Atlantic in its usual extravagant fashion — bruised gold, then copper, then a red like something bleeding out slowly and without complaint — and she threw the switch, and the beam swung out over the darkening water, sweeping, sweeping, sweeping.

*You're welcome,* she said to the empty horizon, because there was no one else to say it to, and because irony was cheaper than honesty.

* * *

Ten years ago, almost to the day, she had walked out of Julian Ashcombe's life in a rented room in Camden that smelled of dust sheets and other people's cigarettes.

She could still reconstruct it with terrible fidelity. The bare floorboards. His piano against the wall, secondhand, one key in the upper register that stuck and made everything sound briefly haunted. The score paper everywhere, covered in his handwriting — that quick, slanted script that looked like weather coming in. And Julian himself, twenty-four and luminous with the certainty of a man who has just written something true, holding the pages of a piece he'd named for her without asking, because asking would have required admitting what it was.

*Vesper's Nocturne,* he'd called it. He played it for her once, all seven minutes, and she sat on the edge of his bed with her coat already on and understood that if she stayed even one more evening, she would become a person who lived inside someone else's music. A muse. A marginal note. *Dedicated to V.W.*

She had wanted, with a ferocity that frightened her, to be the composer. Not the dedication.

"You're leaving," Julian had said. Not a question. He had that gift, then, of hearing what wasn't played.

"I'm going home," she'd said. "Grandad needs me."

"Your grandfather," Julian said gently, "has a housekeeper, a doctor, and a better support network than either of us. You're going home because I scared you."

And the unforgivable thing — the thing she had spent ten years refusing to think about directly, approaching it only sideways, the way you approach a dog you've bitten — was that he had been right. She had packed that night. She had kissed him once, on the mouth, tasting of goodbye, and said, "This isn't a door closing, it's just—" and he had finished it for her, because he always could:

"—a rest. Yes. Composers know how rests work, Vesper."

But she had never come back to resolve the phrase. Ten years of silence, ten years of novels nobody bought under a name nobody connected to the girl who'd left London, and the rest stretched on until the music itself had surely forgotten there was ever a melody waiting after it.

She had heard him, of course. Everyone had heard him. Julian Ashcombe, whose film scores filled cinemas, whose name appeared on programmes in fonts usually reserved for cathedrals. Once, in a supermarket in Penzance, she'd heard four bars of *Vesper's Nocturne* — reworked, orchestrated, famous now — drifting from the checkout radio, and she had stood clutching a basket of oranges while a stranger's arrangement of her own worst decision played her out of the store.

That was the night she'd started sleeping with the radio unplugged.

* * *

Now she went back down the ninety-one steps and made herself useful, because usefulness was the anesthetic she preferred. Her grandfather's effects still waited in the sitting room, boxed but not sorted, six months after the funeral. She had managed the funeral itself flawlessly — had delivered a eulogy that made the harbourmaster cry — and had not managed to open a single box since.

Tonight, apparently, she would open one.

It was the small box, the one with *MUSIC* in his handwriting on the lid, and she told herself it was practical, that the contents needed climate-appropriate storage, that sentiment had nothing to do with it. Inside: tuning forks wrapped in flannel. A pitch pipe. And beneath them, flat and leather-bound, a songbook.

Her breath did something complicated.

She knew this book. She had grown up with it living in his jacket pocket, fat with loose papers, bristling with pencil stubs. Folk tunes — some old as the cliffs, some his own — collected across sixty years from fishermen and farmwives and one memorable blind fiddler who'd stayed a week in 1989. But this copy was different. This was clean, bound, deliberate. A fair copy. Made late, by the look of the ink.

She opened it on her knees.

His hand, yes — the small upright letters — but interleaved among them, filling margins, completing fragments, harmonizing where her grandfather had left single lines, was a second handwriting. Quick, slanted, looking like weather coming in.

Vesper stared at it for a long time. The fire ticked. The beam swept overhead, around and around, patient as ever.

She knew that handwriting. You didn't forget a handwriting like that any more than you forgot a voice saying your name like a chord resolving. She had watched it race across score paper in a Camden bedsit while a stuck key made everything sound haunted.

*Julian.*

Julian Ashcombe's hand, in her grandfather's songbook, dated — she turned pages with suddenly cold fingers — dated the summer she was twelve. The summer a gangly boy had supposedly stayed with the vicar's family and taught her to skim stones. The summer before everything. A summer Julian had never once mentioned in three years of loving her, not once, not in Camden, not ever.

The wind rose outside, finding new notes in the window frames, and far below, where the cliff road vanished into the first white smother of snow, Vesper thought she heard — faint, impossible, or perhaps only the sea arranging itself — the sound of tyres on gravel, and then nothing at all.
