## 2. Two Cars in the Snow

The storm arrived the way bad news always did in Cornwall — first as a rumor on the wind, then all at once, without apology.

Vesper had been in the lamp room when she saw it coming: a grey wall of weather shouldering up out of the Atlantic like something with intent. By three o'clock the horizon was gone. By four, the world had narrowed to the lighthouse and a hundred yards of screaming white, and by five she could no longer see the cliff path she'd walked every day for ten years.

She lit the lamp early. Not because any ship needed it — no sane captain would be out in this — but because her grandfather had taught her that a light kept out of habit was still a light, and habits were the bones of a life. *You keep the routine, Vesper-girl,* he used to say, hands wrapped around his tea mug, *and the routine keeps you.*

She'd been keeping routines for a decade now. She was beginning to suspect they were keeping her hostage.

Down in the kitchen, she made soup she didn't want and ate it anyway, listening to the wind try every seam of the old tower like a burglar working a lock. The letter from the Vane Development Group sat on the dresser where she'd left it that morning, face down, its cream envelope smug as a closed door. *Final Offer.* She'd read enough of it to know the shape: generous sum, swift completion, demolition of "the obsolete structure" come spring. They'd spelled obsolete in a font that looked expensive.

*Come spring.* As if the lighthouse were a weed to be pulled before planting season.

Outside, the whiteout thickened until the window showed nothing but the lamp's own glow thrown back at her, ghostly, doubled. And it was into that doubled glow — just as she was rinsing her bowl — that she heard it. Faint, absurd, impossible.

A horn. Two notes, patient, repeated. Coming from below the cliff road.

Nobody drove the cliff road in this. Nobody drove the cliff road at all after dark; the council had let it crumble to a single rutted lane years ago. But there it was again — two notes, then silence, then two more — thin as a memory under the wind.

Vesper stood very still, bowl dripping in her hands, and felt the strange cold certainty that the day had been waiting all along to become this one.

* * *

She dressed for war: oilskins over two jumpers, boots, a rope coiled across her chest like a bandolier, a hurricane lantern in one fist and a spade in the other. The moment she opened the door the storm took her breath and gave her ice in trade. The wind came off the sea sideways, carrying snow that wasn't soft but granular, stinging, a sandstorm of winter. She leaned into it and descended the cliff steps one at a time, counting them the way she always counted them — forty-one — because counting was how you stayed brave when you were alone.

Except she wasn't alone anymore. Below, through gaps in the whirling white, she caught the gleam of headlights. Two sets. One car slewed across the narrow track at an angle that made her wince; the other sat behind it, nose to the drift, hazards pulsing like a failing heart.

"Hello!" she shouted, and the wind took the word and shredded it.

The driver's door of the first car opened and a figure emerged — tall, coatless, immediately swallowed to the knees in snow. He turned toward her lantern, and even at forty feet, even through the blur, Vesper's whole body recognized him before her mind consented to.

Julian Ashcombe.

Ten years, and she knew him by the way he stood — weight slightly forward, head tilted, as if listening for music under everything. His hair was longer than it had been in London, silvered at the temples where it had been merely dark, and the wind was doing violence to both. He squinted into her light. Then he said her name.

Not shouted. Said — carried somehow intact through the gale, the way certain notes carry through noise because they're pitched to the room itself.

"Vesper."

It landed in her chest like a chord resolving. All those years of half-finished sentences, and he began with her name, and it sounded exactly the way it had sounded at twenty-two, and that was unbearable, so she did what she always did with unbearable things: she got practical.

"What in God's name are you doing on the cliff road?" she yelled, closing the distance, spade first.

"Sat nav," he called back, and there was something helpless and almost funny in it. "It said — it said coastal route, scenic—"

"It's December!"

"I noticed!" Snow was in his eyelashes. Up close he looked older in the ways ten years are supposed to make people older, and younger in the eyes, which hadn't aged at all. "The car's not going anywhere. I've been out here twenty minutes and I think my hands have resigned."

Behind him, the second car's door opened, and a woman stepped out with a composure so total it seemed like a special effect. She wore a wool coat worth more than Vesper's annual royalties and she wore it like armor. Dark hair pinned back. Eyes the grey of the sea before it decides to be cruel.

"You must be the keeper," the woman said. Her voice cut the wind better than Julian's had. "Marguerite Vane."

Vesper felt the name go through her like a fishhook. *Vane.* Of course. Of course the storm would deliver the demolition itself to her door, half-frozen and immaculate.

"You picked a fine evening to close on a lighthouse," Vesper said.

"I picked a fine evening to arrive early." Marguerite glanced at the buried car, then at the churning dark above them, and something flickered across her face — brief, quickly filed away — that might have been recognition. Might have been memory. "My meeting can wait. My hypothermia cannot. Is there shelter?"

"There's a lighthouse," Vesper said. "Forty-one steps up. It's not for sale yet."

"No," Marguerite agreed, and held her gaze a beat too long. "Not yet."

* * *

Getting them up the cliff was the hardest hour of Vesper's year.

Julian insisted on helping and was worse than useless, gallant and clumsy, sinking to his hip twice in drifts so that Vesper had to haul him out by the collar while he laughed — actually laughed, breathless and white-lipped, a sound she remembered from a rain-soaked night outside a closed pub in Camden and had spent ten years pretending not to remember. Marguerite climbed like a woman crossing a boardroom, measured and uncomplaining, though Veper saw her hand tremble on the rope and saw, too, the moment Marguerite realized she'd been seen, and the chin came up, and the trembling stopped by force of will.

At step thirty, Marguerite paused, breathing hard, and looked back down at the vanished road.

"My mother used to walk this path," she said, so quietly Vesper almost lost it to the wind. "Before the railings went. Before everything."

Then she climbed on, and said nothing else, and Vesper filed the sentence away next to the fishhook feeling, in the drawer marked *later.*

They reached the top as full dark fell, if dark was a word for a world made entirely of moving white. Vesper got them through the door, bolted it against the wind's fury, and stood a moment in the sudden ringing quiet of the round stone entrance hall, three strangers dripping on her grandfather's flagstones, the lamp turning overhead with its slow, patient heartbeat.

Julian looked around the tower the way other men looked at cathedrals. His gaze climbed the spiral stair, touched the great lens glowing above, and came back to her, and softened into something she refused to name.

"It's smaller than I imagined," he said.

"You never imagined it at all," Vesper said. "That was rather the point."

He flinched. Good, some small cruel voice in her said. Then the voice added, less helpfully: *he's shivering, Vesper, put the kettle on.*

She put the kettle on.

* * *

By eight o'clock the storm had graduated from violence to siege. The radio spoke only static. The phone line was dead — it was always dead in weather, a fact the Vane contract had noted under *infrastructure deficiencies*, item eleven. There would be no cars, no rescue, no departure. Whatever was going to happen in this lighthouse would happen between the three of them, with nowhere for anyone to hide except inside their own histories.

Vesper found dry clothes — her grandfather's jumpers, which fit Julian as if the old man had knitted them for a future he'd foreseen, and which Marguerite accepted with a raised eyebrow and, eventually, genuine gratitude. She fed them soup and bread and the last of the damson jam. Julian ate like a man who'd forgotten meals existed. Marguerite ate precisely, tasting everything, and when she thought no one was watching she looked out the kitchen window at the lamp's rotating beam and her expression went somewhere private and young.

"So," Vesper said, because silence with Julian Ashcombe in it was a kind of drowning. "What brings the composer to Cornwall in the dead of winter? Signing papers, I heard you say. What papers?"

His spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

"A commission," he said carefully. "In Falmouth. A film score." A pause, a hair too long. "And you? Still writing?"

"Still writing. Still nobody buying. Some things hold their pitch."

"That was never—" He set the spoon down. "Vesper, about London—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended, sharp enough that Marguerite looked up from her soup with the alertness of a hawk hearing a vole. Vesper made herself breathe. Made herself unclench her jaw. "It's ten years ago. It's snowing. We're trapped. Let's not spend the avalanche relitigating the flood."

Julian held her eyes, and there was hurt in his, and patience, and underneath both something steady and warm that she wanted to lean into the way a freezing man wants to lean into fire — knowing it will cost her.

"All right," he said softly. "But I'm going to say it eventually. You should know that. I've been composing toward saying it for a decade."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. The lamp turned. Marguerite Vane watched them both over the rim of her bowl with the expression of a woman taking minutes.

"Well," she said. "This is going to be an interesting storm."

Later — much later, when Julian had been installed in the spare room beneath the eaves and Marguerite in her grandfather's old bed, when the house had gone quiet except for wind and the great slow sweep of light — Vesper climbed to the lamp room and stood with her palms flat on the cold glass, looking down into the white chaos where two cars sat buried like fossils of the day's bad luck.

*Composing toward saying it.* Ten years, and he'd learned nothing about how to talk to her, and everything about how to make her want to listen.

She pressed her forehead to the glass. Below, in the guest room, she heard — faintly, through old stone, or perhaps only in her head — Julian humming. Four bars. Unfinished. A tune she'd never heard before and somehow already knew.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow they'd dig out the cars, or fail to, and Marguerite would produce her contracts, and the letter on the dresser would stop being avoidable.

But tomorrow was twelve hours away, and tonight the light was burning, and somewhere below her a man was humming her name in melody.

She hated how much that sounded like hope.
