## 3. Three at Supper

The storm arrived the way an orchestra tunes before a symphony — scattered, discordant, then suddenly everywhere at once. Julian Ashcombe stood at the lighthouse kitchen window and watched the sea erase itself, white into grey into nothing, and thought: *I have written this weather. I have put exactly this into the second movement of something, once, and called it original.*

Behind him, Vesper Winterbourne was lighting the stove.

He had forgotten how she moved in her own kitchen — or perhaps he had never known, and his memory had invented it: the economy of her gestures, the way she fed kindling to the flame with the tenderness other people reserved for sleeping children. Ten years, and she still wore her sleeves pushed to the elbow as if the world were a workshop and she its foreman.

"You'll want to sit," she said without turning around. "Both of you. The road won't reopen tonight, and I refuse to serve supper to people standing up like mourners at a wake."

Marguerite Vane sat at the scrubbed pine table with the composure of a woman who had been stranded in worse places by worse men. She had removed her coat — cashmere, Julian noted, ruined now beyond any dry cleaner's redemption — and folded it over the back of her chair with the precision of someone filing a document. Her phone lay face-down beside her plate. She had checked it eleven times since four o'clock. He knew because he'd counted; counting was what he did instead of praying.

"Your generator's holding," Marguerite said to Vesper. "Impressive, for infrastructure this old."

"It's not a generator. It's my grandfather's stubbornness, converted to diesel." Vesper set down a pot of stew that smelled of thyme and barley and something darker — ale, maybe, or memory. "Sit. Eat. Complain about the wine if you like; there isn't any."

Julian sat. The chair creaked under him like a note held too long.

* * *

They ate mostly in silence at first, the good kind and then the bad kind, and Julian catalogued the difference the way he catalogued everything: the good silence was the wind against the glass and the tick of the stove and Vesper's spoon against her bowl. The bad silence was Marguerite looking at the lamp room overhead as though measuring it for demolition, and Vesper pretending not to notice, and Julian caught between them like a bridge between two cliffs that did not want to be joined.

It was Marguerite who broke it, which surprised him. He had expected her to be all clause and leverage tonight, all terms and conditions. Instead she set down her spoon and said, quietly, "I knew this light."

Vesper looked up. "What?"

"The lighthouse." Marguerite turned her water glass a quarter-turn, a habit Julian recognized from boardrooms — the gesture of a woman buying herself three seconds. "I grew up in Porthmerra. The village below the cliff. Until I was nine."

Julian stopped eating. In fifteen years of rivalry he had learned every fact about Marguerite Vane worth knowing — her schools, her acquisitions, the name of her yacht — and not one of them had included a childhood in a Cornish fishing village.

"My mother walked this cliff path every evening when the boats came in," Marguerite went on, her voice level in the way voices are level when they are carrying something heavy across ice. "She'd point up at the lamp and tell me, *that's how you find your way home — somebody keeps a fire burning even when they can't see you yet.*" A pause. The wind filled it. "She died when I was ten. My father sold everything and took me to London, and I have spent thirty years making certain no one can ever take anything from me again."

"And so you buy things," Vesper said. Not cruelly. Wonderingly.

"I acquire assets." But Marguerite's mouth twisted on the word, as if she could taste its falseness. "This is the last building left from that summer. The chandlery's gone. The chapel's a holiday let. When your lawyers sent me the survey photographs—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and Julian watched a woman decide, in real time, not to say the true thing. "Demolition is efficient," she finished. "Efficiency is a virtue."

"It's a substitute," Vesper said softly, "for grief."

The silence after that was enormous. Julian found he was holding his breath, and let it go slowly through his nose, the way he'd been taught to breathe before conducting.

* * *

Later — dishes washed, Marguerite installed in the guest room with a hot water bottle and a dignity that almost concealed her exhaustion — Julian stood in the sitting room while Vesper built up the fire. The lamp above them turned its slow patient circle, sweeping light across the ceiling like a searchlight interrogating the past.

"She's not what I expected," Julian said.

"No one ever is. That's rather the point of people." Vesper knelt at the hearth, and the firelight made copper of her hair, and Julian felt the old music move in him — the music that had gone silent, or underground, or somewhere he couldn't follow, sometime in the decade between her leaving and now.

He had composed four symphonies without her. He had won things. His name was printed on programmes in cities where it snowed differently than it snowed here. And every one of those works, if he were honest — and the storm outside seemed determined to make him honest — had been written *away from* something. Away from a door closing. Away from a platform at Paddington. Away from a woman with a suitcase and a voice like low strings saying, *I can't be the reason you become small, Julian, and I can't watch you become large without me.*

He had told himself for ten years that she had chosen fear. Tonight, watching her feed the fire, he understood for the first time that she might have chosen *him* — some version of him she couldn't bear to watch happen.

"You're staring," she said, without turning.

"You laughed. At dinner. When Marguerite said the word 'efficiency.'" He came closer, drawn along the floorboards like a bow along a string. "You laughed, and I realized—" He stopped. Started again. "I've been writing for you for ten years, Vesper. Every slow movement. Every melody I couldn't explain to critics. They'd ask what the second theme *means*, and I'd say longing, and they'd nod, and none of them knew I meant a kitchen in Cornwall and a woman pushing her sleeves up."

Vesper rose. She didn't come toward him and she didn't step away, and the space between them hummed like a struck string left ringing.

"Don't," she said. But gently. Gently was worse. "Don't do this tonight, Julian. The storm makes everything sound like a confession."

"Maybe it is one."

"Then save it." Her eyes were bright and terrible. "Confessions need daylight. That's what my grandfather used to say. Anything said in a storm belongs to the storm."

Above them the lamp swept round — dark, bright, dark, bright — and Julian thought of Marguerite's mother on the cliff path, and of all the people who had ever navigated by somebody else's stubborn fire, and of how he had spent a decade mistaking success for arrival when really he had only been circling this tower like a ship that refused to admit it was lost.

"Then I'll wait for daylight," he said.

She almost smiled. It wrecked him more thoroughly than a full smile would have.

* * *

He woke at three in the morning to the sound of the piano.

Not the piano — there wasn't one. It was the wind, finding the gap in the window frame of the room Vesper had given him, and playing it: a single keening tone that rose and fell, rose and fell, and underneath it, faintly, another note, a fifth above, from some other breach in the old house. Two notes. Sustained. Moving against each other.

An interval.

Julian lay in the dark and listened, and his hands shaped chords against the blanket, and he understood that the house itself was humming — that his grandfather-in-law-to-never-be, that old man he'd met only once, had built or mended or simply *failed to fix* a window in such a way that the storm made music here, and that Vesper had slept beneath this music her whole life, and that he had spent ten years searching conservatories and concert halls and his own ambitious heart for a sound that had been waiting in a wall.

He got up. He followed the sound down the corridor on bare feet, meaning only to stand outside her door and listen to the interval resolve, and it was there — passing the narrow door to the storage stair, the one wedged shut with a chair — that he heard something else.

Voices. Below. Muffled, urgent, rising through the floor like bubbles through dark water.

Vesper's voice, tight with anger he had never heard from her. And beneath it, half-swallowed by the wind, a man's voice — low, pleading, familiar in a way that made Julian's blood stop and restart.

A voice he knew.

A voice that had no business being inside a lighthouse at three in the morning in the teeth of a solstice storm — unless it had climbed the cliff path in the dark, unless it had known these stairs, unless it had been here *before*.

Julian pressed his ear to the cold stone of the storage-stair door, and the voice below said his own name.
