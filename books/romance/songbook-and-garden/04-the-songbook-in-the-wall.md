## 4. The Songbook in the Wall

The storm had left its signature everywhere.

Vesper stood in the kitchen at dawn with a bucket catching drips from a ceiling that had never leaked before, watching the water ring against the tin like a slow metronome. The gale had spent itself sometime after midnight, and the silence it left behind was the heavy, ringing kind — the silence of a house that had been shouted at for two days and was now sulking.

Julian was still asleep on the settle in the front room, one arm flung over his eyes, his coat bunched beneath his head as a pillow. Marguerite had taken the guest room and, judging by the immaculate state of her folded blanket when Vesper had peeked in, had probably been awake since five, doing whatever ruthless developers did at five in the morning in other people's houses. Pressing suits. Drafting clauses. Being terrifying in a quiet way.

Vesper filled the kettle and let herself have one minute of looking at Julian asleep before she looked away from it. Ten years since she'd seen him unconscious and unguarded, and he still slept like a man braced for someone to take the music away. She remembered that about him from London — how he'd doze off mid-score, pencil still in hand, and wake furious at himself for the lost hour. She had loved that fury once. She had mistaken it for ambition's engine. Later she'd understood it was just fear wearing ambition's coat.

The kettle sang. She made tea, carried hers up the spiral stair to check the lamp room, and found what she'd feared: a damp bloom spreading across the ceiling of the gallery below the lantern, brown as old tea, and along the north wall — the wall that faced the worst of the wind — a long crack where the plaster had lifted from the stone.

Her grandfather would have known exactly what to do. He'd patched this building through forty winters with lime mortar and stubbornness and whatever materials the sea threw at him. Vesper had inherited the light but not, apparently, the knack. Still, plaster didn't wait, and neither did damp. She fetched the toolbox from under the stairs, a bucket of filler, the wide blade scraper, and went to work while the house slept.

* * *

Stripping loose plaster is an archaeology you don't choose. The blade found the soft places and the soft places gave, and by nine o'clock Vesper had opened a wound in the north wall three feet long, down to the granite beneath, and was hauling out the crumbled lath with her bare hands, dust in her hair, tea gone cold on the sill.

That was when the blade struck something that wasn't wall.

A hollow knock. She knocked back, curious, and heard it again — not the dead thud of stone but the papery echo of a cavity. She widened the hole carefully now, picking rather than scraping, until a section of plaster came free in a sheet and revealed a gap between the inner wall and the outer stone, a space perhaps four inches deep, running up behind the lath like a secret throat.

Something was wedged inside it. Something wrapped in oilcloth.

She drew it out with both hands, the way you'd draw a splinter from a child's foot — slowly, gently, already apologizing. The oilcloth was stiff with age and smelled of paraffin and decades. It was tied with tarred twine, and her grandfather's knots were unmistakable: he'd taught her them when she was eight, standing on this very gallery, saying, *A knot is a promise you make to your own hands, Vesper-girl. Don't tie ones you can't untie.*

She untied it.

Inside was a book. Not a logbook — she knew every logbook in this tower, had catalogued them all last month in the slow, dutiful grief of sorting his effects. This was smaller, squarer, bound in grey board gone soft at the corners, and when she opened it the smell rose up out of the pages like a voice: pencil shavings, salt, lamp oil, time.

It was a songbook.

Folk tunes, handwritten. *The Maid on the Cliff Road.* *Three Ships to Falmouth.* *The Lighthouse Keeper's Lament*, which she knew, because he used to hum it while trimming wicks, though he'd always claimed he didn't know where he'd learned it. The notation was her grandfather's hand — she'd know those blunt, patient note-heads anywhere; he wrote music the way he mended nets, functionally, without ornament. But around the tunes, in the margins, above the staves, between the lines, there was a second handwriting.

Slanted. Quick. Beautiful in the way that handwriting is beautiful when the person writing it is thinking faster than the pen can go. Little annotations curled around the melodies like gulls around a mast: *this phrase wants a suspension here — try the seventh.* *Grantham says the fishermen sing verse two a tone lower; they're right.* *He calls this tune "nameless" but it is not nameless, it is waiting.*

Vesper sat down on the floor of the gallery with the book open in her lap and the cold coming through the stripped wall against her shoulder blades, and she read the second handwriting again, and again, because the first time her eyes had refused to deliver the message.

She knew that hand. Of course she knew that hand. She had watched it move across manuscript paper for two years in a flat above a piano shop in Camden, had kissed the fingers that held the pencil, had once traced a whole melody onto Julian Ashcombe's forearm in the bath just to feel him laugh at the tickle of it.

*J.A.*, said the inside cover, in that same slanting script, followed by a date.

June. Nineteen years ago. Julian had been nineteen years old. Vesper had been twelve.

* * *

She did not cry. That surprised her. She had expected, opening something hidden in her grandfather's walls, to weep — grief does that, ambushes you in doorways. But what came instead was colder and more precise, a kind of forensic stillness, as if she'd found a stranger's toothbrush in her own bathroom.

He never mentioned it.

Ten years together — no. Two years together, eight years apart, ten years total of knowing Julian Ashcombe, first as the brilliant angry composer she'd loved at twenty-two, then as the ghost she'd made of him. And in all that time he had never once said: *I knew your grandfather. I knew him before you did. I sat in this kitchen. I learned these tunes. I wrote in his margins.*

She turned pages with hands that wanted to shake and wouldn't be permitted to. The summer of that date, she'd been twelve, spending July at the lighthouse as she always did — crabbing off the low rocks, reading in the lamp room, trailing her grandfather on his rounds. Had there been a young man? She searched the memory and found only fog. A boy who helped haul the boat, maybe. Someone her grandfather had called... what? She couldn't retrieve a name. Twelve-year-olds don't inventory strangers.

But the margins inventoried everything. As the pages turned, the two hands began a conversation across the years — her grandfather posing problems, the boy answering. *Why does this tune end unresolved?* — *Because the singer isn't home yet. You'll understand when you've waited for someone.* And later, in the boy's hand, pressed hard enough to emboss the next page: *He says I play like I'm fleeing. He says the sea doesn't flee, it returns. I hate that he's right.*

Vesper closed the book. Opened it again. Checked the date on the flyleaf against the dates scattered through the margins. Six weeks. The boy had been here six weeks, June into August, and had written his initials and nothing else, and had apparently walked out of her childhood and into her adulthood without ever connecting the two — or without ever telling her he had.

Unless he hadn't known. Unless the coincidence was real, the way the sea is real, circling back on itself. Unless Julian had come to her London flat at twenty-two genuinely not knowing that the woman who played his unfinished nocturne badly on a rented upright was the granddaughter of the old man who'd taught him that folk tunes were architecture.

But no. No, because she remembered — suddenly, horribly, with perfect clarity — the night he'd asked her where she grew up, and she'd said *a lighthouse in Cornwall, you'll laugh,* and he hadn't laughed. He'd gone very still, the way he went still before difficult passages, and said, *Which light?* And she'd told him, and he'd looked at her for a long moment with something moving behind his face like weather, and then he'd kissed her and changed the subject.

She had thought, all these years, that the stillness was desire. Now she recognized it. It was recognition.

* * *

The floorboard on the stair creaked at half past eleven, and Vesper had the songbook shut and under her sweater before she'd decided to hide it — a decision her body made without consulting her, which told her something about what her body thought she'd found.

Julian came up into the gallery carrying two mugs of tea, hair flattened on one side, wearing yesterday's shirt with the particular shamelessness of a man who has no luggage. He looked at the stripped wall, the tools, the dust sheet she'd thrown over the debris.

"Storm damage?" he said.

"Plaster failed." She took the tea. Her hand was steady. She'd rehearsed it. "North face took the worst of it."

"Your grandfather built things to last."

"He built things to last longer than most things last." She sipped. Over the rim of the mug she watched him look at the wall, at the exposed granite, at the dark seam of the cavity — empty now, thank God — and she watched his face do absolutely nothing, and understood that a man who could keep his face that blank had practiced.

"You're limping," she said, to say something true.

"I'm fifty-one floors of hotel stairs out of practice." He smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had undone her at twenty-two, warm and self-deprecating and precisely calibrated. "Marguerite's already working. I could hear her typing from the kitchen. Does she sleep?"

"In shifts. Like a shark." The joke came out level. Good. Keep it level. Because if she opened her mouth and let out what was actually in it — *you were here, you were HERE, you wrote in his margins, you knew his hands before you ever held mine* — she would not be able to stop, and she was not going to hand him her hurt in a room with no door.

He drifted to the window instead, tea in hand, and looked out at the sea, which lay flat and glittering and innocent as a liar after church. "I used to love storms," he said quietly. "When I was young. Everything loud outside meant you could finally hear yourself think inside."

"When were you young?"

The question slipped out before she could weigh it — too casual, or not casual enough. He turned. For a moment something crossed his face, fast, like a bird past a window: not guilt exactly. Recognition of a trap he couldn't see the shape of yet.

"Nineteen," he said. "Nineteen was the year everything broke and got rebuilt wrong." He smiled again, smaller. "Long story. Bad summer."

"Bad summers make good composers," Vesper said, and heard her grandfather's cadence come out of her mouth, and saw Julian flinch — actually flinch, a small involuntary thing, as if she'd played a chord he knew in a key he didn't expect.

"Yes," he said. "They do."

He drank his tea. She drank hers. Below them Marguerite's typing resumed, quick and slanting and relentless, like handwriting thinking faster than the pen can go.

Vesper stood with the songbook burning against her ribs under her sweater, sixty pages of a summer Julian Ashcombe had buried in her grandfather's wall, and thought: *the past refuses to stay buried — fine. Then I'll dig. And when I ask him, I'll watch his hands.*
