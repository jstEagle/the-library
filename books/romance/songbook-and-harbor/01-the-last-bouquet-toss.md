## 1. The Last Bouquet Toss

The bride was crying again, and Quentin Okafor knew the difference.

Twenty years in the business taught you to read tears the way sommeliers read vintages. There were joy-tears, which came fast and bright and spilled sideways when a bride laughed at something her mother said. There were relief-tears, which arrived after the vows, when the terrifying part was over and the body finally believed it. There were even performance-tears, which Quentin forgave, because every wedding needed a little theater and she'd rather supply it herself than leave it to chance.

But fear-tears were different. Fear-tears pooled low on the lash line and refused to fall, because falling meant admitting what they were. Fear-tears made a bride hold her bouquet like a life raft. Fear-tears turned a woman's smile into something load-bearing.

The bride in question—Danielle Marsh-Kowalski, three hundred guests, $412,000 budget, ballroom of the Whitmore Grand—was standing at the top of the aisle beside a man who loved her, being photographed by a man who cost Quentin more per hour than her first car, and she was crying fear-tears with the discipline of a professional.

Quentin stood at the back of the room with her tablet against her hip and watched it happen, the way she watched everything: from the perimeter, where nothing could touch her.

"Flawless," murmured Priya Nair at her elbow, scrolling the run-of-show on her own device. "Catering hit every mark. The string quartet cued within four seconds of the doors. Your florist outdid herself—the arch looks like it grew there. If I didn't know better, I'd say you sold your soul for this one."

"I don't have a soul, Priya. I have vendor contracts."

"See, that's the kind of joke that would worry me if you weren't so good at your job."

Quentin didn't answer. On the dance floor, Danielle's new husband dipped her, and the crowd sighed as one organism, and Danielle's smile held its shape like wet paper holding water.

Quentin had seen the fear-tears start during the vows. Right at *to have and to hold*, right where the language got specific, right where the promise stopped being poetry and became architecture. She'd seen Danielle's fingers tighten on the bouquet stems until the peonies blanched. And she had done what she always did: adjusted the lighting cue to soften the moment, signaled the quartet into their next phrase thirty seconds early, and let beauty do what beauty did best—cover the seams.

That was the job, really. Nobody said it out loud, but that was the job. Not love. Coverage.

* * *

An hour later, during the dinner service, Quentin stationed herself at the service corridor between the kitchen and the ballroom, because that was where weddings actually lived—in the corridors, behind the walls, among the people carrying trays. The ballroom was the dream. The corridor was the machine. Quentin Okafor had built her reputation on knowing which one you were looking at.

Priya found her there, as expected, holding two espresso cups and wearing the expression she wore when she was about to commit insubordination.

"You haven't sat down all night."

"I sat down at the tasting in March. It went fine."

"That was a chair, not sitting down." Priya handed her an espresso anyway, and Quentin took it because refusing would require energy she was saving for the cake cutting. "Also, the mother of the groom asked if we could move the sweetheart table six inches to the left."

"It's centered under the chandelier."

"She knows. That's why she wants it moved."

Quentin closed her eyes briefly. "Tell her the chandelier is a metaphor for marriage. Everything looks balanced until someone insists on moving."

"I'm going to tell her the table is bolted down, which is a lie, but a kinder one." Priya sipped her coffee. Then, casually, in the tone of someone mentioning weather: "So. Did you check the mail today?"

"No."

"Because I checked it for you. Because I'm devoted. And there was an envelope in it."

"There are always envelopes."

"This envelope," Priya said, "had a wax seal. A *wax seal*, Quentin. In this economy. Who uses wax seals? People in period dramas and—" she paused for effect, and Quentin hated that she could see the pause coming, "—the Correspondence Circle."

The espresso cup paused halfway to Quentin's mouth.

"The what?"

"Don't do that. Don't do the thing where you pretend you don't know things you absolutely know. You signed up for it. Two months ago. At Marisol's birthday. After the third negroni."

"I signed up for no such thing."

"You filled out the questionnaire on my phone. I watched you do it. You answered forty questions about your emotional availability while eating your weight in churros, and then you laughed like a Bond villain and said, and I quote, 'It's anonymous, it means nothing, worst case scenario I get a pen pal and best case scenario I get material.'"

Quentin set down the cup with great precision. "I was drunk."

"You were tipsy. Drunk people can't spell 'correspondence,' and you spelled it twice, once correctly and once with a flourish."

The corridor smelled of rosemary and warm bread and the faint industrial hum of a kitchen running at capacity, and Quentin looked down the length of it—at the trays waiting, the timeline pinned inside her head, the whole gorgeous apparatus of other people's happiness humming along on schedule—and felt the particular loneliness that only comes in rooms full of people who assume you're fine.

"I don't need a pen pal," she said.

"Nobody needs a pen pal. That's the entire point of them." Priya leaned against the wall. "When's the last time you talked to someone who wasn't a client, a vendor, or me?"

"I talk to my sister."

"Your sister lives in Lisbon and thinks you're happy because you send her photos of centerpieces."

"They're very good centerpieces."

"Quentin." Priya's voice did the thing it rarely did, which was drop the teasing entirely. "You just spent eleven months building the most beautiful wedding this city has ever seen, and you spent the whole ceremony watching the bride like she was a structural failure waiting to happen. What were you looking for?"

*Fear,* Quentin thought. *I was looking for fear, so I'd know I wasn't the only one.*

What she said was: "Bouquet toss is in nine minutes. The band runs long if we let them, and Danielle wants the toss on the terrace before sunset. Go find the DJ."

Priya held her gaze for a moment longer, then pushed off the wall. "The envelope's on your desk," she said. "Wax seal and everything. It'll keep. Unlike the sunset."

She went, and Quentin stayed in the corridor a moment longer, alone with the machinery.

* * *

The terrace at the Whitmore Grand faced west, and at 7:42 in the evening in early October it did exactly what it was designed to do: it caught fire with light. The harbor glittered beyond the balustrade, freighters and sailboats and the old lighthouse hall on the far point, small and gray and stubborn, the way it had been Quentin's whole life. She noticed it without meaning to. She always noticed it without meaning to.

Danielle stood at the edge of the terrace with her back to three hundred guests, bouquet raised, veil catching wind, and Quentin watched her from the doorway the way she watched everything—from the perimeter.

The count started. Three hundred voices counting down like a launch. Ten. Nine. Eight. And in the space of those numbers, Quentin saw Danielle's shoulders rise, saw her grip the stems, saw her glance back—just once, quickly, over her shoulder at the man she'd married four hours ago—and Quentin read the look fluently, because she'd written that look herself once, twenty years ago, in a different city, in a different dress, holding a different bouquet she'd never thrown.

*What am I doing. What if I'm wrong. What if everyone is watching and I'm wrong.*

Three. Two. One.

The bouquet sailed backward in a high silver arc, petals scattering like sparks, and a bridesmaid in sage green leapt for it with both hands and caught it against her chest to a roar of applause, and the band crashed into something triumphant, and the photographer's flash strobed white across the terrace, and everyone was happy, or performing happiness, or performing performing happiness, and Quentin stood in the doorway with her tablet against her hip and felt the old cynicism settle over her like a coat she'd owned so long she'd forgotten it wasn't her skin.

*Promises kept in public.* Her mother's phrase. Her mother had believed it the way some people believe in tides. Quentin had inherited the business of promises and lost the belief in them somewhere around year zero, and here she was, two decades later, the best in the business, manufacturing public promises by the dozen and believing in none.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Priya: *Envelope. Desk. Wax seal. Just saying.*

Then another buzz, seconds later: *Also the groom's uncle is trying to give a speech. Intercepting. You're welcome.*

Quentin almost smiled. Almost.

She looked out past the terrace rail, past the glittering water, to the far point where the old hall stood against the darkening sky—her mother's hall, technically nobody's hall now, slated for demolition in the spring, its windows dark for years. Her mother had managed that place for thirty years. Had walked brides down its warped wooden aisles. Had kept a songbook behind the front desk and told anyone who'd listen that a wedding was just a promise that agreed to be witnessed.

Quentin hadn't been inside it since the funeral.

Below her, the party roared on, flawless, expensive, covered at every seam. Behind her, somewhere in the building, an uncle was being gently disarmed. Ahead of her lay teardown at midnight, vendor settlement, a Monday site visit, and a desk with an envelope on it bearing a wax seal from a program whose entire premise was that two strangers could say true things to each other as long as they never learned each other's names.

Anonymous. Unaccountable. Safe.

"It means nothing," Quentin said quietly, to the harbor, to the lighthouse hall, to no one.

The harbor, being a harbor, kept its opinion to itself.

She turned back toward the light and the noise and the next hour of the flawless evening she had built, and she did not know yet that the envelope on her desk would be answered—that she would answer it, carefully, guardedly, telling herself the whole time it meant nothing—or that the stranger on the other end of that thin thread of ink was already, at this exact moment, sitting in his own office across town, writing his own first letter, complaining about her.

But that was tomorrow's problem.

Tonight there was only the terrace, and the dying sun, and the bride who had thrown her bouquet like a woman throwing a rope off a sinking ship, and Quentin Okafor, the best wedding planner in the city, standing alone at the perimeter of everyone else's love, professionally, flawlessly, completely fine.
