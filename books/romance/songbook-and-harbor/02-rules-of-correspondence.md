## 2. Rules of Correspondence

The office of the Anonymous Correspondence Program occupied the third floor of a converted cannery on Pier Nine, which Quentin found either charming or insulting, depending on how she tilted her head. She had arrived twelve minutes early, because arriving early was a form of control, and control was the only thing she trusted anymore. The harbor outside the tall windows was doing its Tuesday-morning impression of a Monet—gray water, gray sky, gulls stitching the two together—and she stood at those windows for eleven of her twelve spare minutes, telling herself she could still walk out.

She did not walk out. She had signed up for this on a dare, and Hartwell Events did not leave contracts unsigned, even the ones written in wine.

"Ms. Okafor?"

The coordinator's name was Dev, according to the lanyard around his neck, and he had the unhurried warmth of a man who had talked a hundred strangers off a hundred ledges of second thoughts. He was maybe forty, cardigan over a T-shirt that read I READ YOUR LETTER AND ALL I GOT WAS THIS LOUSY EMOTIONAL BREAKTHROUGH, and he shook her hand like he meant it.

"Thanks for coming in," he said. "Most people do the intake online."

"I like to know what I'm signing." She sat in the chair across from his desk—a good chair, she noted automatically; even her cynicism had opinions about furniture. "And I'd like to be clear up front that this is not—" she gestured vaguely at herself, at the room, at the entire concept "—a *thing*. It's an experiment. A colleague dared me. I'm mostly here to prove I can follow through on something stupid."

Dev smiled like a man who heard that sentence weekly and had stopped keeping count. "Of course. Let's talk rules."

He slid a single sheet of paper across the desk. One page. Quentin appreciated that; she'd built a career on contracts that ran forty pages, and she distrusted anything that needed more than one to say what it meant.

"Rule one," Dev said. "No names. Not yours, not theirs. Not real ones, not fake ones you'll grow attached to. You'll each have a program number. Yours is 4471. Letters go through us—we open, scan for identifying details, redact if necessary, forward. No addresses, no employers, no 'guess where I work.'"

"That seems paranoid."

"That seems *necessary*," Dev said mildly. "You'd be amazed what people can deduce from a mention of a neighborhood. Rule two: no photos, no descriptions beyond what you'd tell a stranger on a train. No 'I have green eyes and a dimple.' If you want someone to know your face, that's what rule four is for."

"Which is?"

"No meeting until both parties agree. In writing. Both of you, separately, no pressure from either side. And even then, we facilitate. Neutral ground, no last names until you're both standing in the same room and have decided it's worth it." He leaned back. "The anonymity isn't a gimmick, Ms. Okafor. It's the whole point. People lie differently to faces. They perform for faces. On paper, with no name attached, people tend to say the true thing first and regret it later, instead of saying the safe thing first and regretting it forever."

Quentin looked out at the harbor. A tugboat was dragging something across the bay, patient as grief.

"And if I never agree to meet?" she asked. "If I write letters for six months and then just—stop?"

"Then you stop. No penalty. We send one final letter letting your correspondent know the correspondence has ended by choice, no reason given. That's rule five: anyone can end it, anytime, no explanation owed." Dev folded his hands. "We're not trying to make matches. We're not a dating service. We're trying to give people a place where they can be honest without consequence. Whatever grows there grows."

*Whatever grows there grows.* Quentin turned the phrase over and found she disliked how much she liked it.

"One more question," she said. "How do you pair people? Randomly?"

"Algorithmically. Interests, values, communication style, a few things we won't disclose because disclosure ruins the instrument." He said it lightly, but she caught the flicker of something underneath—the same flicker she got when a client asked how she managed to make every wedding feel inevitable. Trade secrets. "The questionnaire you filled out was long for a reason."

"The questionnaire was forty minutes of my life I'll never get back," she said. "Including a question about whether I believe love is a decision or a weather pattern."

"And what did you answer?"

"*Weather,*" she said. "Obviously."

Dev wrote nothing down, which somehow felt more ominous than writing it down. "Mm. Your correspondent answered the same way."

Something small and unwelcome moved in Quentin's chest. She identified it, named it, and evicted it promptly. "That doesn't mean anything. Half the world thinks love is weather. It's why half the world's marriages fail—they wait for a forecast instead of bringing an umbrella."

"There it is," Dev said, sounding pleased.

"There what is?"

"The thing the algorithm found. You'll see." He slid a pen across the desk. "First letter goes out today if you want. Handwritten, if possible. We transcribe and forward, so your handwriting stays private too—but there's something about writing by hand that changes what people admit. Our data says so."

"Your data," Quentin repeated. "You run a romance pen-pal program and you have *data*."

"We've been running nine years. We have a lot of data." He said it without pride, which was somehow worse. "One request, before you write. Don't plan this letter."

She blinked. "I'm sorry?"

"You're a professional planner—I did look you up, it's allowed, public information only. You plan things for a living. Beautifully, I'd guess. But a first letter that's been planned is a performance, and performances are how people hide." He tapped the blank page. "Write it like you're talking to someone who will never find out who you are. Because they won't. Unless you decide otherwise, someday, maybe. Write the true thing."

Quentin took the pen because refusing would have required explaining herself, and she was already spending more words in this room than she'd budgeted.

* * *

She wrote the letter that night at her kitchen table, after two glasses of the Malbec she kept for clients she didn't like and emergencies she did. The apartment was quiet in the particular way expensive places are quiet—all soft surfaces absorbing sound, nothing left over to echo. Twenty years ago she'd chosen it because Daniel had hated it. That had seemed like a reasonable criterion at the time. It still did, most days.

The stationery was good stock, cream, heavy—the kind she used for client proposals—and she caught herself starting three times.

*Dear 4-something-or-other,* — too flippant, scratch.

*To whoever you are,* — too much like the opening of a eulogy, scratch.

On the fourth attempt she wrote what came, unplanned, unedited, the true thing, and only recognized afterward how dangerous it was:

---

**4471**

I'm told I'm supposed to introduce myself, but the rules forbid the interesting parts, so here's what's left: I am very good at my job, which means I am very good at other people's happiness, and lately I've begun to wonder if that's the same as having none of my own.

I plan weddings. I won't say where or for whom—the rules again—but imagine the most flawless wedding you've ever attended. The candles were the exact color of the light at 7 p.m. The bride's grandmother's ring was sewn into the bouquet wrap so it wouldn't catch the light wrong in photographs. Every moment landed on time to the minute. I built all of it, and standing in the back during the vows, watching, I felt nothing except a kind of professional satisfaction, the way an architect feels about a roof that doesn't leak.

Here is my confession, since we're being honest with strangers: I used to believe weddings were promises made in public. Now I think they're promises made in front of witnesses, which is different. Witnesses can be managed. Promises can't. I've watched enough of them break to know the difference, and I've gotten very skilled at building beautiful containers for things that don't last.

I'm not writing this to be fixed. I'm not writing this to fall in anything—love, lust, the harbor. My colleague dared me to sign up and I don't back out of dares; it's a character flaw I've decided to call integrity.

But I'll admit one thing, since you're a stranger and strangers are safe: sometimes, at these weddings, when the couple does something unplanned—he laughs into her veil, she mouths the vow wrong and they both crack up—something in me leans toward it like a plant toward a window. And then I remember what I know, professionally, about how those stories end, and I straighten back up.

Tell me something true. That's my price for this letter. One true thing, no names, no context. I'll know if it's performed.

—4471

---

She read it twice, hating how exposed it looked in ink, and reached for the envelope with the intention of not sending it. Her hand disagreed with her intention. By midnight it was in the drop box on Pier Nine, and by the time she fell asleep she had constructed three separate arguments for why it meant nothing: it was a dare honored, an experiment conducted, a line item in the ongoing research project of Quentin Okafor's well-managed life.

All three arguments held up beautifully under review.

None of them explained why she checked the mail twice the next morning.

* * *

The week that followed was the kind of week that reminded her why she'd stopped believing in happy endings in the first place. The Whitmore wedding—her flagship account, eighteen months of cultivation, a client she'd personally talked out of eloping because "eloping is what people do when they haven't met a good planner"—was suddenly asking pointed questions about "other firms." On Thursday, the questions acquired a name, and the name was Reyes.

August Reyes. Even the syllables annoyed her, all that soft vowel weight, like a man who'd never had to fight for a consonant in his life. Reyes & Co. had opened downtown three years ago with the aesthetic of a Scandinavian spa and the aggression of a private equity fund, and in three years he had taken exactly what he wanted from the market: the modern couples, the big budgets, the press. At the last industry gala he'd given a keynote called "Weddings as Experience Design" and Quentin had sat in the third row with her arms crossed, radiating disagreement hard enough that two colleagues changed seats.

"He cited *you*," her assistant Priya had said afterward, delighted. "In the Q&A. He said your tablescapes were 'structurally perfect but emotionally conservative,' and then everyone looked at you."

"They looked at me because I was the only person in that room who knew he'd stolen the Delgado florist contract out from under me."

"Or because you were glaring like a gargoyle."

"Gargoyles are load-bearing, Priya. Someone has to hold up the standards of this industry."

Now Reyes wanted the Whitmores. Of course he did. Quentin spent Friday afternoon drafting a counter-proposal with the cold precision of a general, and if her pen pressed a little harder than necessary on certain letters, well, ink was cheap and grudges were free.

It was Saturday, sorting through the program's forwarded mail between a site visit and a tasting, that she found the reply.

Program envelope, cream, her number handwritten in a stranger's hand—slanted, quick, confident, the handwriting of someone who wrote fast because he thought faster. She told herself she'd read it later. She read it standing in her own lobby, coat still on.

---

**4471**

You asked for one true thing, no context, and said you'd know if it was performed. Fair warning: everything I write is slightly performed. It's an occupational hazard. But here's the least performed thing I own:

I am terrified of becoming my father, and I am becoming him anyway, one careful day at a time.

There. Now you know the worst of me, and you don't even know my name, which is exactly how I like my confessions served.

As for your letter: you say you build beautiful containers for things that don't last. I'd push back on that, if pushing back is allowed. Maybe the containers aren't the problem. Maybe the problem is that you keep building them for other people and living in a rented room yourself.

I also plan events—not weddings, before you ask, though I have opinions about them, all of them correct. And I noticed something in your letter you may not have noticed yourself. You described the bride's grandmother's ring sewn into the bouquet wrap. You described the candles matching the 7 p.m. light. Those aren't the details of a cynic. Cynics describe crowds and costs. Those are the details of someone paying extremely close attention to beauty and calling it professionalism so nobody asks why she cares.

Straighten back up all you want. I saw you lean.

—your correspondent

---

Quentin read it twice, standing in her lobby while the doorman pretended not to watch her face do whatever it was doing.

*Straighten back up all you want. I saw you lean.*

She had a sudden, irrational urge to check whether the program's envelopes were actually anonymous, whether the cream paper betrayed her, whether this stranger—this presumptuous, perceptive, insufferable stranger—had some channel into her life. Then she made herself laugh at the thought. Paranoia. The man was simply good with words, the way she was good with floor plans, and good with words was its own kind of danger.

She wrote back that night. Carefully. Guardedly. She dismantled his argument about rented rooms with three paragraphs of clean logic, conceded nothing, admitted less, and then—at the bottom, in a postscript she told herself was purely tactical—

*P.S. What do you mean, "not weddings"? Everyone has opinions about weddings. State yours. I'll be grading.*

Because a rival's weakness, she reasoned, was useful intelligence. Because knowing how a stranger thought was a skill, and skills were assets. Because it meant nothing.

The letter went out Monday. On Wednesday, the Whitmores canceled their meeting with her and booked a consultation with Reyes & Co., and Quentin declared war in the privacy of her office with a single, quiet sentence that Priya pretended not to hear.

And on Thursday, walking past the mail cart, she found herself checking for a cream envelope—and finding one—and carrying it upstairs unopened, saving it, the way you save the last bite of something you refuse to admit you love.

Telling herself, the whole elevator ride, that it meant nothing at all.
