## 3. Enemy of the State of Matrimony

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the day August Reyes had designated for losing his mind in a controlled and professional manner.

It sat on his desk between a florist's invoice and a contract for the Delgado-Whitmore wedding—*the* Delgado-Whitmore wedding, four hundred guests, a budget with more zeros than his first car had miles, the client he'd spent eleven months and one very expensive dinner cultivating away from Okafor Events. The envelope was plain cream stock, hand-addressed, no return address. Just a number: **Correspondent #4471**.

He'd signed up for the program six weeks ago, at his sister Marisol's kitchen table, after his third glass of her terrible boxed wine and her fourth iteration of the same speech: *You build other people's happiest days for a living, Augusto, and then you go home to an apartment where the only thing that knows your name is the espresso machine.* He'd laughed. She'd slid the application across the table. He'd filled it out to make her stop looking at him like that.

The rules had come by email: **No names. No photographs. No identifying details of residence or profession. No meeting until both parties agree, in writing. Correspondence continues at the pace you set. If either party withdraws, the match dissolves permanently.**

Anonymity as architecture. A room with no windows where you could say anything and be no one. He'd told himself it was harmless. A curiosity. A social experiment he could abandon whenever he liked.

Then he opened the envelope, and the first line read:

*Dear 4471 — I'm told I'm supposed to start with something true, so here is mine: I don't believe in this program, and I suspect you don't either, which may be the only honest thing two strangers can offer each other on day one.*

August sat back in his chair and laughed out loud, alone in his office at nine in the morning, which was not something he did.

*All right,* he thought. *You have my attention.*

---

He should have been working. There was a seating chart bleeding red ink across his monitor, a mother-of-the-bride who wanted to relocate a string quartet "somewhere less visible," and a venue walkthrough at two o'clock. Instead he pulled a legal pad toward him—the good paper, the heavy cream stock he saved for apology notes to clients—and began drafting a reply, because apparently he was the kind of man who answered a stranger's honesty before he answered his own accountant.

The problem was what to say.

He wrote: *I don't believe in it either.* Crossed it out. Too agreeable. He wasn't going to open with capitulation; this person had drawn first blood with wit, and August Reyes did not lose exchanges.

He wrote: *What exactly don't you believe in—the program, or the premise underneath it?* Stared at it. Too much like a deposition. He interrogated vendors all day; he refused to become the kind of man who interrogated pen pals.

He wrote: *Tell me why you signed up.* Crossed that out too, because she—he'd decided it was a she within thirty seconds, based on nothing but the pressure of the pen strokes and the way the letters leaned slightly right, like they were hurrying somewhere interesting—she hadn't asked him anything yet. She'd offered a truth instead. The correct move, strategically and otherwise, was to answer in kind.

Which left him staring at a blank page with the uncomfortable realization that he didn't actually know what his truth was.

Marisol would have said: *Your truth is that you're lonely, Augusto, and you dress it up in eighty-hour weeks and call it ambition.* Marisol said a lot of things after three glasses of boxed wine.

His truth, as far as he could locate it, was more annoying than loneliness. His truth was that he'd spent the last decade building a firm that manufactured perfect days—aisles and arches and candlelight calibrated to the milliliter—and that somewhere in the last year the whole enterprise had started to feel like watching a film through soundproof glass. Beautiful. Silent. Not his.

And beneath that, closer to the bone: Elena. The engagement he'd ended seven years ago with a speech so rehearsed it still made him flinch when he remembered how calm he'd sounded. *I don't think I'm capable of what you deserve.* Noble words. Coward's words. He'd wrapped retreat in virtue and called it kindness, and Elena had looked at him with something worse than anger—recognition—and said, "You'll regret the way you did this, if not the fact of it."

She'd been right about the way. He thought about it every time he watched a groom's hands shake while fastening a boutonnière, every time a bride's father mouthed the vows along with his daughter from the second row. All that trembling, public hope. August stood at the edge of it professionally, taking notes, adjusting lighting, and privately he envied those people the way a man locked out of his own house envies the party inside.

That was the truth. That was the thing he could put on heavy cream paper and send to a stranger numbered 4471, who couldn't look at him afterward.

He picked up his pen.

---

Before he could write a word of it, though, his office door opened without a knock, which meant Priya, his lead coordinator, who knocked for clients and never for him.

"You're smiling at a piece of paper," she said.

"I'm reviewing correspondence."

"Nobody smiles at invoices." She dropped a folder on his desk. "Okafor found out about Delgado-Whitmore."

The smile died on schedule. "How?"

"How do you think? Lucia Delgado posted a photo from our tasting. Tagged the venue, tagged us. Quentin Okafor has eyes everywhere and a memory like a collections agency." Priya perched on the edge of his desk, which was her way of saying she planned to stay until the conversation concluded to her satisfaction. "She called the Delgados' assistant. Didn't try to win them back—Quentin doesn't beg—but she left a message that Lucia reportedly described as 'chillingly polite.' Something about being available should any questions arise."

"That's not a message. That's a landmine with a bow on it."

"It's Quentin Okafor," Priya said. "Same thing."

He knew the name the way sailors know the name of a storm season. Quentin Okafor had been the undisputed queen of the city's high-end wedding circuit for fifteen years—immaculate, untouchable, famous for a composure that people described as elegance and August privately described as armor. They'd orbited the same galas for a decade. They'd exchanged the same forty words a hundred times: congratulations on the Whitfield wedding, lovely work at the Ashford benefit, isn't the weather remarkable. Her handshake was precise. Her smile arrived exactly on time and left exactly on time. Talking to her felt like being served by a very beautiful machine that might, under certain conditions, invoice you.

And now he'd taken her biggest client, and she knew it, and the industry—which ran on gossip the way engines run on oil—was already placing bets.

"She'll retaliate," Priya said.

"Of course she will."

"The Kwan engagement is still unassigned. She's going to move on it."

"Then we move faster." He heard his own voice go flat and tactical and recognized it as the voice he used for war, which was most voices he used. "Get me everything on the Kwans. Guest count, aesthetic history, the grandmother's dietary restrictions, whatever exists."

Priya didn't move. She was studying him with the expression she reserved for grooms who insisted on writing their own vows. "You know what's strange? You've been in a good mood for two days. Since the letter came, actually—I saw the envelope on your desk Tuesday. And now you get Okafor news and you're not even properly angry. You're—" she waved a hand, "—elsewhere."

"I'm focused."

"You're composing." She tapped the legal pad, the crossed-out lines, the half-formed sentences. "This isn't a seating chart, August."

"It's personal correspondence."

"On company time."

"Bill me." He turned back to the pad, and after a moment she went, but he felt her diagnosis hanging in the air like perfume: elsewhere. Accurate. He hated accurate people.

Here was the thing he would not have admitted under torture: he'd been thinking about Quentin Okafor too much lately, and not only as a competitor. It had started at the Meridian Gala in March, when a waiter dropped a tray near the head table and the entire room flinched except Okafor, who caught a falling champagne flute mid-air without breaking her sentence, set it down, and kept talking as though physics were a staff member she'd trained personally. August had watched her do it and felt something he refused to name, because the candidates were admiration and irritation and both were unacceptable.

Or it had started earlier—at the awards thing two years ago, when a drunk vendor had cornered her by the coat check and August had inserted himself into the conversation for reasons he still couldn't reconstruct, and she'd looked at him with genuine surprise, one human second of it, before the composure reassembled. He'd thought about that second for weeks. The crack in the porcelain. What lived behind it.

Now he'd stolen her flagship client, and instead of feeling triumphant he felt like a boy who'd thrown a rock through a window and then couldn't stop wondering about the room inside.

*Irritation,* he told himself firmly. *It's irritation. She's your rival. Rivals are supposed to occupy your thoughts; that's what makes them rivals.*

The legal pad disagreed. The legal pad contained three crossed-out opening lines and, in the margin, in handwriting he didn't remember producing, the words *catches champagne flutes* with no referent whatsoever.

---

He wrote the letter that night, at his kitchen counter, after the espresso machine had done its single duty and gone silent. The apartment was quiet in the way Marisol despised. Rain against the window. The city humming below.

*Dear 4471,* he wrote. *You're right that I don't believe in the premise. But I'll confess the real reason, since you led with truth: I plan weddings for a living. Professionally, I am fluent in love. Personally, I'm a man who once ended an engagement with a speech so well-rehearsed it should have won an award. So when I say I don't believe in strangers-on-paper, understand that my disbelief is not philosophical. It's autobiographical.*

He stopped. Read it back. It was more than he'd meant to give—Marisol would frame it—but there was a freedom in the number, in the absence of a face. 4471 could not look at him with recognition. 4471 could not say *you're the man who does everything beautifully and feels nothing*. 4471 was a blank page that had already proven itself sharper than most people he knew.

He kept going.

*As for what I don't believe in: I don't believe in the version of love where everything is certain beforehand. Certainty is just fear wearing a tuxedo. I believe in the trembling hands and the father mouthing the vows and the bride who laughs in the middle of the ceremony because the officiant mispronounced her new surname. I build those moments for strangers and I stand outside them like a man looking through glass. Your turn. Tell me what you're afraid of. Everyone who signs up for a program like this is afraid of something specific. I'll trade you mine for yours.*

He read it twice, decided it revealed exactly enough and no more—a line he walked professionally every day—and sealed it.

Then, because the night was long and his discipline was a finite resource, he let himself think about her. Not 4471. The other her. Quentin Okafor, across a ballroom, in charcoal gray, telling some vendor that peonies were "a cliché with a fragrance" while everyone laughed a half-beat too late because they weren't sure it was a joke. He tried to summon irritation and got, instead, a vivid image of her catching the champagne flute, the flicker of triumph she'd smothered before anyone could see it.

*She's going to come after the Kwan account,* he thought. *And when she does, I'll be ready, and it will feel like winning, and I will think about her the entire time.*

He went to bed annoyed at himself, which was, at least, a familiar emotion.

---

Three days later, the reply came, and it undid him a little.

*Dear 4471 — You want my specific fear. Fine. Here it is: I'm afraid that I've gotten so good at protecting myself from the worst day of my life that I've accidentally prevented all the best ones. I keep waiting for the feeling to be safe before I act on it. It never is. It never will be. I know this and I do it anyway.*

*Also, you're wrong about certainty. Some of us aren't afraid of certainty. We're afraid of wanting things we can't control, and pretending that's the same as wisdom.*

*Your turn again. Tell me about the speech. What did you say, and what did you mean to say? Those are never the same speech.*

August read it standing in his hallway, coat still on, mail fanned forgotten across the floor.

*Afraid of wanting things I can't control.* He read that line four times. Somewhere in the city was a person he had never met who had reached directly into the machinery of his chest and adjusted a setting no one else had ever found—not Elena, not Marisol, not the army of therapists and friends and one memorable tarot reader his sister had ambushed him with.

And the worst part—the genuinely destabilizing part—was that the voice on the page reminded him of someone. Not the words themselves; the cadence. The precision of it. The way every sentence seemed to arrive exactly on time and leave exactly on time, and yet underneath, if you pressed your ear to the paper, something was hurrying somewhere interesting.

He shook it off. Coincidence. Projection. He spent his life around articulate, guarded people; of course they blurred together.

He sat down at the counter and began to write:

*Dear 4471 — What I said was: "I don't think I'm capable of what you deserve." What I meant was: "I'm terrified, and terror is embarrassing, so I've dressed it up as a gift to you." She saw through it instantly, which was the cruelest part. People forgive you for leaving. They don't forgive you for lying about why.*

*You said you wait for the feeling to be safe. I have a professional observation for you, free of charge: in fifteen years of weddings, I have never once seen a safe feeling walk down an aisle. Only brave ones.*

*So here's my proposal, correspondent: we tell each other the truth, since neither of us seems capable of it anywhere else. One rule. No advice. Advice is what cowards give instead of company.*

He sealed it, addressed it, and set it by the door for morning pickup.

Then he checked his phone, because Priya had texted twice, and the texts said what he already knew: **Kwans just toured Okafor's studio. She's pitching Thursday.**

Thursday. Two days. He pictured Quentin Okafor in her immaculate studio, presenting the Kwan family with a vision so polished it would make their current ideas feel like finger painting, and he felt the old competitive fire light up on schedule—and underneath it, quieter, the other thing. The thing that had no business being there. The image of her catching the flute. The crack in the porcelain. The suspicion, growing despite himself, that the woman across the battlefield and the voice in his mailbox were built from the same material: people who had gotten so good at protecting themselves from the worst day that they'd accidentally prevented all the best ones.

*No,* he told himself. *4471 signs letters with a fountain pen and quotes nobody. Okafor wouldn't be caught dead baring her throat to a stranger.*

He turned off the light. On the counter, the outgoing letter waited by the door, and beside it, the incoming one, reread twice more before bed, its final line circling him like a bird that refused to land:

*Those are never the same speech.*
