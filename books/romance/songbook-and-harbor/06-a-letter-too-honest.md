## 6. A Letter Too Honest

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which was the day August Reyes had privately designated as the worst day of the week, and it sat on his desk between a florist's invoice and a cease-and-desist from Okafor Events that he had no intention of honoring.

He almost didn't open it. He had drafts to send, a bride in Napa who wanted peonies in December, and a rival whose name he said out loud more often than he'd like to admit. But the envelope was heavy paper, cream-colored, with his post-office-box number written in that handwriting—the one that slanted left like it was leaning away from something—and he found himself opening it before he'd decided to.

*Dear Wren,*

That was him. Wren. The program coordinator had assigned the bird names in week one—his correspondent was Lark, and he was Wren, small birds both, and August had rolled his eyes at the sentimentality of it for a full day before writing back anyway.

*I've read your last letter four times now. You asked me what I'm most afraid of, and I gave you a joke. You deserved better than a joke. So here is the true answer, and I'm going to ask you not to write back right away, because I need you to know it without softening it.*

*I was engaged once. Eleven years ago. Her name was Marisol, and she was better than I deserved, which is a thing cowards say to make their leaving sound like humility.*

August stopped reading. He was standing in his office at Reyes & Co., the harbor gray outside the window, and his own words were coming back at him from the page, and he had not known until this moment how they would look in ink.

He had written it at two in the morning, after the third glass of the good scotch he saved for closing deals, and he remembered thinking: *she'll never know who I am. She can't use this. It's safe here.* That was the whole architecture of the program, wasn't it? No names, no photos, no faces. A confessional with the grille drawn shut.

He kept reading because apparently some part of him needed to be punished.

*We had been engaged fourteen months. I had the venue booked—a beautiful place, a converted cannery up north, string lights, the whole dream. And every time we sat down to plan, I found a reason to postpone. The caterer wasn't right. The date conflicted with my sister's schedule. The invitations felt premature. I told myself I was being thorough. I told myself a wedding planner's own wedding should be perfect.*

*Here is what actually happened. My mother pulled me aside three weeks before the date and said, "Mijo, you look like a man standing on a ledge." And instead of saying yes, I am terrified but I want this, I said nothing. I let her worry. I let Marisol carry the whole weight of my doubt alone because I couldn't bear to hold it in front of her.*

*Two weeks before the wedding, I called the caterer and canceled. Not postponed. Canceled. Then I called Marisol and blamed the venue. I let her believe for six months that it was logistics, that it was bad luck, that it was anything except a man who wanted the idea of being loved more than he wanted the work of loving someone.*

*I sabotaged it. Me. Deliberately, in the coward's way—slowly, deniably, so that when it collapsed I could stand in the wreckage and say, well, these things happen.*

*She married someone else two years later. I sent a card. I didn't go.*

*You asked why I do this work if I don't believe in it. Here's the ugly truth: I plan weddings because it's the closest I can get to the fire without burning. I build other people's courage by the pound and sell it back to them at market rate. And every time a groom cries at the altar—every time, without exception—I think: he doesn't know yet what it costs. He hasn't stood on the ledge.*

*I envy people who risk everything. Genuinely. There's a woman I know professionally—not a friend, God, no—who bets her whole firm on every contract like it's a knife fight, and I hate her and I envy her in equal measure, and some nights I can't tell the difference. She has never once hedged. She has never once canceled the caterer.*

*I don't know why I'm telling you this. Except that you wrote me last month about your mother, and about the hall, and you didn't flinch, and I thought: this stranger owes me nothing and I owe her the same currency she paid in.*

*Don't write back right away. Or do. I have no right to instruct you. That's rather the point of this letter.*

*—Wren*

August set the letter down on his desk with hands that were not entirely steady, which was absurd, because he had written every word of it. But reading it was different from writing it. Writing it had felt like confession; reading it felt like exposure. Somewhere across the city, a person whose face he had never seen now knew the single worst fact of his life, and the sky had not fallen, and his firm still stood, and Marisol was still married and fine, and he was still here, slightly lighter and slightly more naked than before.

His assistant buzzed. "Mr. Reyes, the Okafor camp returned our call about the Delgado contract."

"Tell them nothing," August said. "Tell them we're reviewing."

He hung up and looked at the letter again. *She has never once canceled the caterer.* He hadn't named Quentin Okafor—he'd disguised her as "a woman I know professionally"—but even disguised, even drunk at two a.m., he had confessed that his enemy lived rent-free in his head, and that the hatred had a suspicious amount of admiration load-bearing underneath it.

He filed the letter in the drawer where he kept all of them. Nine letters now. He knew the drawer's contents better than he knew most of his living friends.

* * *

Quentin Okafor read the letter twice.

The first time was on the ferry, standing at the rail because her apartment felt too small for whatever was coming, with the wind pulling her hair across her face and the harbor doing its slow gray breathing below her. She read it straight through and then stood there for eleven minutes watching the water, because the letter had done something to her chest that she did not have vocabulary for.

The second time was that night, at her kitchen table, under the lamp, slowly, the way you reread testimony.

*I sabotaged it. Me.*

She had spent twenty years building a career on the conviction that men like this existed—men who smiled at brides while quietly planning their exits, men who treated vows as marketing copy. She had built an entire philosophy on the ruins of her own engagement, and the philosophy required villains, and here was a stranger handing her one, gift-wrapped: *I canceled the caterer and lied about it.*

And instead of feeling vindicated, she felt—

Seen. That was the horrible word for it. Because she recognized the mechanism. The slow, deniable sabotage. The wreckage you could stand in and call fate. She had done her own version: she had never again planned a wedding where she knew the groom's doubts, never again asked the questions that might crack the facade, because asking meant caring and caring meant the ledge. Twenty years of professional excellence built on the coward's foundation: *never get close enough to be responsible for the fall.*

She thought about her mother. Her mother, who had managed the harborfront hall until it ate her health and gave nothing back, who used to say weddings were promises kept in public—as if publicity were the whole sacrament. Quentin had spent two decades proving her wrong. Promises kept in private, promises broken in private, promises never made at all—it all ended the same, so why build the theater?

But this stranger, this Wren, had stood on the ledge and jumped *backward*, and then spent eleven years telling strangers the truth about it in anonymous letters, and there was something in that honesty that her ex-fiancé Daniel had never once managed. Daniel, who had left with such gentle, reasonable sentences. Daniel, who was calling her office again this month, she'd heard through the grapevine, wanting lunch.

She got out her stationery. Cream, heavy, the good stuff she saved for apology notes to clients, which meant it was rarely used.

*Dear Wren,*

*You told me not to write back right away. I waited four days, which for me is roughly a geological era, so consider that my act of courage for the quarter.*

*Here is what I should say: that what you did was unforgivable. Here is what I actually think: that what you did was human, and that the unforgivable part isn't the canceling—it's the eleven years since, the way you've turned your guilt into a business model. You sell courage you don't have. So do I. Maybe that's the job description.*

*You want to know something true? Fine. Here's mine, and I'm only saying it because you went first and I refuse to be out-honest by a man who signs himself after a small brown bird.*

*I was engaged too. Twenty years ago. He was kind and reasonable and everyone approved, including me, right up until the week of the wedding, when I found myself standing in my mother's kitchen unable to remember a single specific reason I loved him. Not one. Just a general glow, like remembering a room you liked as a child. I gave the ring back. I told everyone it was cold feet. It wasn't cold feet. Cold feet is fear of the wrong thing. This was clarity about the wrong thing, and I have spent twenty years dressing it up as wisdom.*

*Except here's the part I've never written down: sometimes I can't tell anymore whether I saw clearly or whether I just got scared and called it sight. Twenty years is long enough to lose the evidence. And I have built a whole life on a verdict I can no longer verify, and some nights that feels less like freedom than like a very well-decorated prison.*

*You said you envy people who risk everything. I am one of those people, professionally, and I'm going to tell you a secret about us: half the time the risk-taking is just retreat at speed. I charge into contracts and bidding wars and knife fights because a fight is a place where I know exactly who I am. It's the quiet rooms that terrify me. The kitchens. The ledges.*

*So we're a pair, aren't we? You, who burned the bridge and called it weather. Me, who never crossed the bridge and called it judgment. Between us we might make one whole honest person, though God knows neither half would admit it out loud.*

*Your mother was right to worry, by the way. About the ledge. Mothers usually are. Mine managed a wedding hall on the harbor until it killed her, and she died believing I'd inherited her faith, and I have spent every year since letting her down in the most competent way possible.*

*Write back whenever you like. Or don't. Apparently we're both experts at not showing up.*

*—Lark*

She sealed the envelope before she could reconsider, which was the only way she ever did anything brave: fast, before the reasonable voice woke up.

Then she sat at her kitchen table with her hands flat on the wood and realized what she had done. She had told a stranger things she had never told Daniel, never told her therapist, never told Priya, who had been her assistant for six years and knew her coffee order and her blood type. Twenty years of armor, and she'd mailed a panel of it to a post-office box because a man she'd never seen had gone first.

"It means nothing," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen, in the tone of a woman rehearsing.

The kitchen declined to agree.

* * *

Three days later, the war resumed on schedule. Quentin's team discovered that Reyes & Co. had quietly signed the Chen-Reyes wedding—no relation, the universe had a sense of humor—out from under them, and Quentin spent forty minutes on the phone dismantling a vendor agreement August had spent months cultivating, and by five o'clock both firms were bleeding and both principals were magnificent and merciless.

At 6:40 that evening, August opened his mail.

There was a note from the Chens' lawyer. There was an invoice. And there was a cream envelope in the left-slanting hand, and he stood in his foyer holding it and felt the strangest sensation: that the woman who had gutted his vendor roster that afternoon and the woman who had told him about the ring and the kitchen and the verdict she couldn't verify were, impossibly, the same species. That somewhere in this city, his enemy had gone home to her kitchen table and written him the truest thing anyone had said to him in eleven years.

*It means nothing,* he reminded himself, filing the letter in the drawer with the others.

The drawer was getting full. He noticed, distantly, that he had begun organizing his weeks around its arrival—that Tuesday had stopped being the worst day of the week sometime around letter seven, and he had not examined why, because examining it would require admitting that the best hour of his life currently belonged to a stranger named after a songbird.

He took out fresh paper. He had a vendor to mourn, a rival to destroy, and a letter to write, and for the first time in years, he knew exactly which of the three he was looking forward to.

Across town, Quentin was also at her desk, also holding a pen, also telling herself the same lie at the same volume.

Neither of them heard the other's silence. Neither of them noticed that the war and the letters had begun keeping the same hours—that every act of daylight cruelty was followed, within days, by an act of midnight honesty, as if two people were apologizing to each other in a language they didn't know they shared.

The harbor kept its counsel. It was very old, and it had watched people mistake each other for enemies before.
