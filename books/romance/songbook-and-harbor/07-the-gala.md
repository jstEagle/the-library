## 7. The Gala

The Meridian Hotel's ballroom had been dressed by someone who understood that money and taste were not the same thing, and Quentin Okafor spent her first ten minutes cataloguing the failures the way other people counted sheep. The centerpieces were too tall—you couldn't see across a table, which killed conversation, which killed the evening. The uplighting washed everyone the color of old champagne. And the string quartet was playing an arrangement of "Canon in D" that had been butchered so thoroughly it should have been reported to the authorities.

"Stop it," Marisol said, appearing at her elbow with two glasses of something sparkling and aggressively local. "You're doing the thing."

"I'm not doing the thing."

"You're doing the thing where you redecorate the room in your head and then get sad about it. You're at a party, Q. Drink the wine. Judge people instead."

Quentin took the glass. "That's what I'm doing."

"You're judging the *chandeliers*."

"They deserve it."

Marisol grinned and drifted off toward the silent auction, where she would bid on things she didn't want just to watch rich men flinch, and Quentin was alone with her reflection in a window the size of a cathedral door. She'd worn the emerald dress. She knew what that meant even if she refused to say it out loud—she'd stood in front of her closet for twenty minutes that morning, which was nineteen minutes longer than usual, and had pulled the emerald dress out from the back where it lived between events, and had told herself it was because the gala photographs went to the trade press and the trade press remembered.

The trade press remembered. That was the story she was going with.

Her phone buzzed. Not a text—she'd know the difference by the vibration, the way you know footsteps in your own house. It was the program's notification: *You have a new letter waiting.*

She didn't read it. That was the rule she'd made for herself after the third week, when she'd caught herself checking the app in a bathroom stall during the Delacroix wedding: no letters at work, no letters before eight a.m., no letters when she was wearing armor. Letters were for the apartment, for the good lamp, for the version of herself that existed after the day was over and the doors were locked. She'd broken the rule exactly once, during a hurricane-delayed reception in Providence, and the letter had been so tender it had made her cry in a coat closet, and she'd decided the rule existed for a reason.

So she pocketed the phone, and she turned around, and there he was.

August Reyes stood across the ballroom like a man who had never once worried about a room's opinion of him. He wore midnight blue—of course he did, the man had the ego to wear midnight blue—and he was laughing at something a vendor was saying, and his laugh carried across two hundred strangers and landed on Quentin like a thrown dart. She hated that. She hated that she could pick his laugh out of a crowd the way you'd pick out a fire alarm.

Three months ago, Reyes & Co. had taken the Whitfield account out from under her. Two months ago, the Ashford wedding. Last week, she'd learned, the Marchetti engagement—her Marchetti engagement, a couple she'd been cultivating since their first date at a restaurant she'd personally recommended. August Reyes was building his firm out of the wreckage of hers, brick by stolen brick, and he did it all with that laugh and that smile and that infuriating habit of sending her handwritten notes after every poaching that read like condolence cards. *Sorry we won't be working together on this one. The couple spoke very warmly of you.* She'd framed one, once, out of spite. It was in a drawer. She looked at it sometimes the way you'd look at a scar.

He saw her. Of course he saw her. The whole room had a way of rearranging itself around the two of them at these events, the way iron filings find a magnet, and she watched the smile he'd given the vendor cool by exactly one degree as their eyes met across the floor. He lifted his glass. She lifted hers. Neither drank. Around them, two hundred people kept talking, unaware that a border had just been redrawn.

"There she is." Dev Anand, the guild president, materialized at her shoulder with the breathless energy of a man who'd had three coffees and one idea. "Quentin. Quentin, Quentin. You've heard about the harborfront, yes?"

"The lighthouse hall." She kept her voice level. "The city's finally moving on it."

"The committee is opening bids. Full restoration, exclusive operating contract, ten-year term." Dev's eyes gleamed. "It's the story of the season, Q. Everyone's watching. And I thought—" he leaned in, dropping his voice to a conspirator's register, "—wouldn't it be wonderful if the announcement came from this room? Tonight? A little theater. The guild loves theater."

Quentin's stomach did something complicated. "The bids aren't due for a month."

"We're accelerating the timeline. The council found matching funds." Dev spread his hands. "Letters of intent, tonight. Just a statement of interest, nothing binding. And—" he glanced across the room, and Quentin followed his glance, and understood everything, "—we thought it would be *lovely* if both of you submitted one. Publicly. A little ceremony. The press is here."

"You want us to declare war in front of the *Coastal Living* stringer."

"I want you to declare war in front of everyone," Dev said cheerfully. "It's good for the guild. Conflict sells tables at next year's gala."

* * *

They staged it at nine o'clock, under the worst of the chandeliers, with the quartet mercifully silenced and the room arranged in a horseshoe that made Quentin feel like a gladiator. Dev made his announcement from the steps of the dais, his voice doing the thing that voices do when they've been trained for exactly this, and then he called the two names into the microphone like a man introducing title fighters.

"Quentin Okafor, of Okafor Events. And August Reyes, of Reyes & Co."

The applause had an edge to it. She felt it on her skin.

She walked to the dais from the left; he came from the right, and they met in the middle the way they always did at these things—close enough to shake hands, far enough to draw blood. Up close, midnight blue was worse. Up close he smelled like cedar and something warm, and up close his eyes did a thing she refused to name where they seemed to actually *look* at her, which was a tactical advantage no one should be allowed.

"Ms. Okafor." He offered his hand.

"Mr. Reyes." She took it. His grip was warm and brief and entirely professional, and she hated how her hand remembered it afterward, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

Dev handed them each a card—a letter of intent, pre-printed, with a blank line for a signature—and a pen. The room went quiet in the specific way rooms do when they smell spectacle.

"Before we sign," August said, and his voice carried without a microphone, easy and unhurried, "I'd like to say something."

"August," Dev began, "we're on a schedule—"

"Thirty seconds." He turned to the room, but Quentin had the sudden, awful sense that he was speaking to her. "The lighthouse hall is the last building on this harbor that remembers what this industry used to be. Before the ballroom packages and the per-plate pricing, weddings happened in places like that—imperfect places, honest places, places that had already survived a hundred years of weather. Whoever wins this contract shouldn't just restore the building. They should restore the *idea* of it." He paused. "Some people think a wedding is a production. I think it's a promise. And promises deserve better than a production."

The room made the sound rooms make—warm, approving, a little star-struck—and Quentin felt the trap close around her with a click she could hear. Because it was a good speech. It was a *beautiful* speech. It was her speech, the one she gave clients, the one she'd been building her whole career on, and he had walked into her house and set it on the table like a gift.

She could give a better one. She had to.

"He's right," Quentin said, and the room laughed at the surprise of it, and she let them laugh, because surprise was a weapon and she'd never once left it holstered. "August is right, and I hate that he's right, and I'd like the committee to note that this is the first and last time I'll say so publicly." Another laugh, warmer. She turned to the room. "That hall matters because of who kept it. My mother managed that building for eleven years. She booked every wedding in it, swept every floor, fixed every leak with her own hands. She believed a wedding was a promise kept in public—said it so often I got tired of hearing it, and then she died, and I never got tired of it again." She held up the card. "So yes. I'm bidding. Not because I want the contract. Because that building is the only inheritance I have that isn't in a box, and I don't lose inheritances to men in midnight blue."

The applause this time was hers, and she took it, and she signed the card with a flourish she'd practiced on nothing and meant completely, and beside her August signed his, and Dev raised both their hands like a referee, and somewhere in the room a camera flash went off and would appear by Thursday under a headline about rivals.

It was perfect. It was exactly the theater Dev wanted.

It made her feel like she'd swallowed a stone.

* * *

Later, at the bar, she ordered the local sparkling again because the wine list was an insult, and August Reyes appeared beside her the way weather appears—not suddenly, but inevitably, and all at once.

"Your mother managed the lighthouse hall," he said. Not a question.

"Eleven years." She didn't look at him. "You knew that."

"Everyone in this industry knows your origin story, Quentin. You've told it at three award ceremonies." He accepted his own glass from the bartender. "I just didn't know you'd deploy it at a hostage negotiation."

"That wasn't a hostage negotiation. That was a land grab, and you brought a speech."

"I brought a *true* speech." He turned to face her, and she was forced by the geometry of the bar to do the same, and now they were standing the way enemies stand in movies, close and angled, the whole ballroom reflected in the mirror behind the bottles. "You looked like you wanted to object."

"I object to the whole enterprise. I object to you." She sipped. "You stand up there talking about promises like you've never broken one."

Something moved across his face—fast, gone before she could name it, a door closing in a house she couldn't see into. "And you talk about them like you've never been afraid of one."

The words landed harder than they should have. She covered it with the glass, with a slow sip, with the expression she used when a bride's mother suggested a chocolate fountain. "Careful, Reyes."

"I'm always careful." He smiled, and it was the gala smile, the public one, but there was something underneath it tonight, something almost tired. "That's my whole problem, if you want to know."

"I don't."

"No," he said. "You wouldn't." He lifted his glass a fraction, a toast to nothing, and Quentin had the strange, vertiginous thought—arriving from nowhere, arriving from *everywhere*—that her pen pal would have liked that line. *That's my whole problem.* Her pen pal, who wrote about carefulness like a disease he was trying to catch his way out of. Her pen pal, who would have said something about armor, about how the careful ones are the ones who've already been burned and are guarding the scar.

She put the thought down like a hot pan.

"Your speech was good," she said, because the silence was doing something to her chest. "The promise thing. You stole it."

"I stole the *framing*," August said. "The sentiment's been in the water supply for years. You just bottled it first."

"Flattery from my mortal enemy. The evening's complete."

"We're not mortal enemies, Quentin." He said it lightly, but he said it looking at her, and the lightness didn't quite hold. "That would require taking each other seriously."

"I take you very seriously. You're the most serious problem I have."

"Then we have something in common," he said, "because so are you," and for one unguarded second—she would replay this later, in the apartment, with the good lamp, dissecting it like a specimen—his face did something that wasn't rivalry at all. It was almost relief. Like a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying, just for a moment, in front of the only person who'd understand why it was heavy.

Then the moment closed, and he was August Reyes again, and he wished her luck with the bid in a voice like a handshake, and he walked back into the crowd.

* * *

She found Marisol at the silent auction table, losing gracefully to a florist over a weekend in Napa that neither of them wanted.

"You and Reyes just had a ten-minute conversation at the bar," Marisol said, not looking up from the bid sheet, "and I watched three separate people take photos of it."

"We were discussing the terms of our mutual destruction."

"Mm." Marisol raised the bid by fifty dollars, purely out of spite. "You wore the emerald dress."

"The trade press remembers."

"The trade press remembers, sure." Marisol finally looked at her, and her expression was the one she saved for brides who insisted they weren't crying. "Q. Can I say something annoying?"

"You always do."

"You've been different lately. Since the pen-pal thing." She said it quietly, under the quartet's return, under the noise of the room. "Lighter. You laughed at the Chen meeting. You *laughed*. I wrote down the date." She capped her pen. "And tonight you stood next to August Reyes and looked like a person instead of a brand, and I'm not saying those things are connected, because that would be insane, because you don't even know who he is—"

"Marisol."

"—but I'm saying the universe has a sense of humor, and you should be careful about what you write to strangers."

"He's not a stranger," Quentin said, before she could stop herself. "That's the entire point. He's the only person who isn't."

Marisol looked at her for a long moment, and then, mercifully, let it go, and raised her bid another fifty dollars, and Quentin stood in the beautiful, over-lit ballroom with her phone in her clutch like a coal she was carrying in her hands.

* * *

The apartment. The good lamp. Eleven forty.

She opened the letter standing up, then sat down, then took her shoes off, because the letter was long, and his long ones were the dangerous ones.

*You said once that you watch people at weddings the way lifeguards watch water,* he'd written, in that handwriting the app rendered in his chosen font, a serif she'd started to find more familiar than her own. *I've been thinking about what you're watching for. I think you're watching for the moment it goes wrong. And I think—I could be wrong, tell me if I'm wrong—that you're not watching because you're cruel. You're watching because someone once promised you something in front of a crowd, and the crowd watched it break, and you've been on lifeguard duty ever since. Standing in the chair. Waiting for the drowning.*

Quentin read that paragraph three times.

Then she read the rest, which was about his week, which was about a rival—*the same one, the one I told you about, the one I can't stop circling*—and how they'd stood in a room together tonight, or a room like the rooms they always stood in together, and how the rival had given a speech about her dead mother and a harbor building and promises kept in public, and how it had been, in his words, *a good speech, a true speech, and I hated how much I wanted her to be saying it about something we were on the same side of.*

*Her,* he'd written. *She. I keep saying she, and you've never asked me why, and I keep not telling you.*

Quentin set the phone down on the table, very carefully, the way you'd set down a glass you suspected was already cracked.

The room was quiet. The good lamp made its small gold circle. Outside, the harbor did what the harbor always did, which was hold the city's light on its back and give nothing away.

He'd said *she*. He'd said it twice, and she had read the letter twice, and her heart was doing something she refused to narrate. It was nothing. It was a pronoun. It was a man writing about a rival in an industry that was—she thought, with a laugh that came out wrong—*full* of women, it was practically a census of women, it meant nothing.

But her pen pal had written: *I wanted her to be saying it about something we were on the same side of.*

And August Reyes had stood on a dais tonight and said: *Some people think a wedding is a production. I think it's a promise.*

The same words. The same idea, the same architecture, the same soul underneath the different clothes. But that was the industry talking, she told herself. That was the water supply. Everyone said it. Everyone had been saying it for years.

She picked the phone back up and typed: *Tell me about her. Your rival. What does she look like, when she's not looking?*

She stared at the message for a long time. Then she deleted it, character by character, and typed instead the safe thing, the armored thing, the thing that kept the door closed and the lifeguard in the chair:

*Tell me why you said "she."*

And she sat in the gold circle of the lamp, and waited, and did not sleep, while across the city a man in midnight blue read her question and understood, all at once, how close the water had come to his door.
