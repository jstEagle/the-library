## 9. Correspondence in Wartime

**QUENTIN**

The email arrived at 6:47 on a Tuesday morning, which told Quentin everything she needed to know about the kind of person Marcus Delacroix had become since leaving her firm.

*Quentin — regret to inform you we're going in another direction for the Whitmore wedding. Wishing you all the best. — M.*

No phone call. No meeting. A form letter sent before most of the city was awake, the way you'd cancel a dentist appointment. She read it twice at her kitchen counter with coffee going cold beside her, and what stung wasn't the loss—Whitmore was a mid-size account, two hundred guests, a bride who'd changed her color palette four times—but the *direction*. She knew exactly which direction. It pointed across the harbor, toward a glass office on Pier Street where August Reyes kept a wall of client testimonials and, apparently, no conscience.

She called Marcus anyway. Voicemail. She emailed his mother, who had hired her personally and once cried into Quentin's shoulder about seating charts. Nothing.

By nine, she'd drafted three responses: one professional, one scathing, one that began *I hope your new planner enjoys working for someone who learned everything he knows from watching me.* She deleted all three. Instead she opened the drawer where she kept the program's stationery—heavy cream paper, the kind that made even an apology feel like an event—and pulled out a pen.

She hadn't written to her correspondent in eleven days. The silence had started as busyness and hardened into something else, something she didn't want to name: the fear that if she wrote honestly about how much this week hurt, she'd have to admit how much the letters themselves had come to matter.

*Dear Blue,* she wrote, because they'd settled on colors instead of names months ago, hers a deep harbor blue, his—she'd decided—a tarnished gold.

*I've had a bad week. I don't say that lightly; I'm not someone who says it at all, usually. But you're the only person I know who doesn't need anything from me, so I'll tell you the truth: someone I mentored left my firm and took a client with him, and he did it by email, before dawn, like a coward. And I'm furious, but underneath the fury I'm embarrassed, because I trained him. I taught him every trick he used against me. There's a special kind of stupid in building the weapon that gets used on you.*

She stopped. Read it back. In any other hand, this would be a confession of weakness. To Blue, it felt almost safe—which frightened her more than the sentence itself.

*Tell me something ordinary,* she added. *Tell me what you ate for dinner. I need proof the world still does small things correctly.*

She sealed the envelope, walked it to the corner box in the rain rather than trusting it to the office mailroom, and told herself, as she always told herself, that this meant nothing.

That night, Marcus Delacroix posted a photo from Reyes's office. Champagne. A signed contract framed on the wall behind him. The caption: *Home.*

Quentin looked at August Reyes's smiling face for a long time, then went upstairs and checked the mailbox twice, though she knew the schedule by heart.

* * *

**AUGUST**

August Reyes had won the week, and winning felt like chewing foil.

Delacroix was a good hire—hungry, talented, loyal enough for now—and the Whitmore account was a clean steal. His team toasted him at a bar on Pier Street while he smiled and made the right noises and thought, the entire time, about a letter he'd received that morning on heavy cream paper.

*Someone I mentored left my firm and took a client with him, and he did it by email, before dawn, like a coward.*

He'd read it standing in his kitchen, still in his running clothes, and felt the strange vertigo of holding a mirror at the wrong angle. Because he *had* taken clients that week. Three of them, counting Whitmore. He'd done it cleanly, legally, with better bids and better coffee, and he'd told himself it was business, and it was business, and yet here was a stranger on cream paper describing the exact wound he'd spent the week inflicting on someone else—and the stranger sounded less angry than *grieving*, and grief was not something he knew how to fight.

He wrote back that night.

*Dear Blue,*

*Dinner was leftover risotto eaten standing over the sink, which I mention because you asked for ordinary and because I suspect you never eat standing over sinks. You strike me as someone who sets the table properly even when alone. Am I wrong?*

*As for your week: I'm sorry. Not in the polite way—in the specific way. Being betrayed by someone you built is its own category of pain, worse than being betrayed by a stranger, because you can't even hate them cleanly. Part of you keeps being proud of them. That part is the worst part.*

He paused, pen hovering. He thought about the champagne photo, the caption, the way his own smile had looked in the reflection of his laptop screen afterward: correct and hollow.

*I'll tell you something I don't tell anyone. Sometimes I win things and feel nothing. This week I won three accounts and stood in my office afterward feeling like a man who'd robbed a house and found nothing inside worth taking. I keep waiting for the winning to feel like arriving somewhere. It never does. Is that ambition, or is it just noise? You'd know. You seem like someone who arrived a long time ago and has opinions about whether the trip was worth it.*

*Don't answer that last part if it's too honest. I find I'm only brave on paper. Isn't that pathetic?*

He sealed it, addressed it, and set it by the door for morning pickup. Then he opened his laptop, pulled up Okafor Events' website—the firm of his rival, the woman whose voice at galas could strip paint, whose firm he'd been dismantling account by account all season—and read her bio again for no reason he cared to examine.

*Every wedding is a promise made public,* the bio said. *My job is to make sure the promise survives the party.*

"Sentimental," he said aloud to the empty office, and closed the laptop, and found himself smiling at nothing, which annoyed him considerably.

**QUENTIN**

The vendor war turned ugly in the second week of October.

It started with the florists. Lumen & Vine, who had done every Okafor wedding for six years, called to "pause" their retainer—code for *Reyes offered us a floor contract and we took it.* Then the string quartet. Then, unforgivably, Marguerite Osei, the cake designer whose work Quentin had championed back when Marguerite was selling cupcakes out of a church basement. Marguerite at least had the decency to cry on the phone.

"He offered me double," she said. "Quentin, I have a lease now. I have employees."

"I know," Quentin said, and meant it, and hated that she meant it, because understanding your enemy's logic was the first step toward becoming him.

Her assistant Dev put up a whiteboard in the conference room titled *ATTRITION* and started tracking losses like war casualties. By Thursday there were nine names on it. By Friday, ten—and the tenth was the Harborview Hotel ballroom, their anchor venue, gone without so much as a phone call.

"They're not even hiding it anymore," Dev said. "This isn't competition. This is a siege."

Quentin stood at the whiteboard with a dry-erase marker and realized her hand was shaking—not with rage, exactly. With something adjacent to rage. Something that had been building for weeks and had nothing to do with vendors.

Because here was the thing she couldn't explain to Dev, couldn't explain to anyone: every time August Reyes struck, she went home and wrote to Blue about it, and every time she wrote to Blue, the writing got softer. The first letter after Lumen & Vine defected had been all fire. The second had been fire wrapped in wit. The third—

The third she'd written last night, and she'd caught herself doing it: softening the story as she told it. Making the rival planner in her letter into a figure almost tragic. *He must be very afraid,* she'd written, *to need to win this badly.* And she'd sat back and stared at those words and thought: where did that come from? Since when did she grant August Reyes interiority? Since when did she look at the man across a bidding table and see anything but the knife?

Since the letters. That was when. Some part of her had started extending Blue's tenderness backward through time, applying it to everyone, including the enemy. It was dangerous. It was, she suspected, exactly how good people lost wars.

She wrote the day's letter at her kitchen table, rain on the window, and this time she didn't soften anything except herself.

*Dear Gold—I've decided that's your color, you never confirmed—*

*Today I lost the tenth thing. At some point I'll stop counting, or start counting what I have left instead, whichever comes first. Here's what I want to confess: on the drive home I passed the man responsible walking out of a restaurant, and I slowed the car. Not to run him down. God. Just to look at him. And he was laughing at something on his phone, this easy unguarded laugh, and I thought—that's the laugh of a man who doesn't know he's at war. Or the laugh of a man who's already won. And I hated him, I did, but underneath the hating there was this awful flicker of—recognition? Like looking at a photograph of myself from twenty years ago, before I learned that caring about things is just pre-registering the grief.*

*I'm sorry. That's a strange thing to send a stranger. Forgive me. Tomorrow I'll be sharp again.*

*P.S. You never told me what you ate. I asked for ordinary and you owe me ordinary.*

**AUGUST**

The bid leak happened on a Wednesday.

August knew it the moment he saw the numbers: Okafor's sealed proposal for the Ashford estate job, sitting in his inbox via a "concerned vendor" who was, in fact, one of his own people playing both sides. Her pricing. Her staffing plan. Her entire strategy, laid out like a patient on a table.

He should have been thrilled. Instead he sat in his office for a long moment feeling sick, because he knew exactly what she'd assume—that he'd stolen it, that he'd do anything, that the gloves were off now and forever. And the worst part, the part he'd have denied under torture, was that a small traitorous voice whispered: *use it.* Undercut her. Take Ashford. Win the week.

He didn't use it. He deleted the file, reported the vendor to the industry association, and then sat with his hands flat on his desk wondering why protecting his rival's secrets felt more like himself than winning ever did.

That night he wrote:

*Dear Blue,*

*Someone tried to hand me a weapon today. Your enemy's secrets, free of charge. And I want you to understand what I did with it, because I need to say it out loud somewhere, even to paper: I burned it. Unread—or mostly unread, I'll be honest, I saw the first page before I understood what it was—and I reported the person who brought it. And I've been asking myself all evening why, because the old me, the me from five years ago, would have used it without losing sleep.*

*I think the answer is you. Which sounds insane. We've never met. I don't know your face or your name or what you sound like when you're tired. But you write to me like a person who believes certain things are load-bearing—that promises are real, that how you win matters as much as whether you win—and somewhere along the way I started checking my days against that standard. Would Blue flinch at this? Apparently yes. Flinch achieved.*

*So thank you for being the kind of stranger who makes a man want to be better than his worst quarter. Don't let it go to your head.*

*P.S. Risotto. Standing over the sink. I answered weeks ago—you never wrote back about it, and I've decided to be wounded about it in a small dignified way.*

He read her reply four days later, and it undid him a little.

*You answered about the risotto and I missed it, and I'm sorry, truly—I've been reading your letters the way I used to read my mother's, slowly, saving them, and I think I reread the wrong ones. Tonight I reread the right one and found your sink and your risotto and your question about whether I set the table properly when alone. The answer is no. I eat over the counter, standing, like a woman being chased. My mother would be appalled. She believed a table was a promise too—set it well and the day has to behave.*

*About the weapon you burned: I don't know why, but I read that paragraph twice and had to put the letter down. There's a version of me that would have used it. There's a version of me that DID use things like that, years ago, and paid for it in ways I'll tell you about someday when I'm braver. So when you ask whether Blue would flinch—yes. Blue flinches. Blue has flinch scars. Keep checking your days against that standard. It's the best thing anyone's built in me in twenty years.*

*Twenty years. God. I've been armored for twenty years and I'm telling a stranger about it on cream paper. What are you doing to me, Gold?*

He read that line—"What are you doing to me"—and felt it land somewhere below his ribs, and had to get up and walk to the window and look at the harbor for a while like a man in a novel he'd have mocked last year.

**QUENTIN**

November came in gray, and the war entered its strangest phase: the phase where both sides started apologizing.

It made no sense. On Monday, August Reyes's firm undercut her bid on the Calloway wedding by a margin so precise it could only have been calculated to humiliate. On Tuesday, a letter arrived on heavy cream paper—no, wait. That was backwards. Let her get the sequence right, because the sequence was the whole madness of it.

Monday: the undercut. Monday night, furious, she wrote Blue three vicious paragraphs about men who mistake conquest for character.

Wednesday: Blue's letter arrived, and it was gentle. *Whatever happened to you today, I'm sorry. Whatever cruelty the world did, I'd undo it if ink could reach that far.*

Thursday: she discovered Reyes had quietly returned Marguerite Osei's contract—released her from exclusivity, no penalty, terms generous to the point of charity. Marguerite called Quentin crying again, different tears, and came back.

Friday: Quentin wrote Blue about the returned contract, and found herself defending the gesture. *Whoever this rival is—and I know I've painted him as a monster—he did a decent thing this week. Maybe the war is making us both strange.*

Saturday: a letter from Blue arrived saying, nearly verbatim, *Maybe the war is making us both strange.*

She sat with that coincidence at her kitchen table for a long time. Everyone in her industry was fighting someone; the season was thick with feuds; of course the timing rhymed sometimes. Of course. Cities were full of parallel sorrows.

Still, she noticed that she'd started structuring her days around the letters. Morning fury, evening confession. She'd snap at a vendor at noon and apologize to Blue by post at midnight, and the apology to Blue somehow counted as the apology to the vendor, which was probably not psychologically healthy, and was definitely working.

Dev caught her smiling at an envelope on Thursday and narrowed his eyes. "Good news?"

"Personal," she said.

"You have personal news?" He said it the way one might report a sighting of a rare bird. "Boss. You laughed at your mail. I've worked for you three years. I've seen you open invoices with more emotion."

"It's nothing," she said, and slid the letter into her bag, and carried it around all day like contraband, and read it twice more in the stairwell where nobody could see her face.

* * *

**AUGUST**

In December, the letters crossed in the mail for the first time—both written the same night, both about the same gala invitation.

*They're seating us at the same table,* Blue wrote. *(Not us—my rival and me. The industry gala. The committee thinks putting enemies in close proximity is comedy.) I don't want to go. I used to love these rooms. Now every room feels like a battlefield I didn't choose. Tell me something kind about enemies. Do you have one? Everyone has one. What would you say to yours if you could say one true thing?*

Quentin's letter, written the same night, unaware:

*The gala invitations came today. I'm seated next to my rival. The universe has a sense of humor and it's cruel. Twenty years ago I'd have treated it as sport. Now I just feel tired, and worse—curious. When did I get curious about him? When did the hate start requiring effort? If you could say one true thing to your enemy, what would it be? I've been drafting mine all evening and every draft scares me.*

August read her letter at his kitchen counter and felt the floor tilt slightly, the way it does when two maps turn out to describe the same territory.

Same night. Same gala. Same question. He told himself it was coincidence—industry-wide events produced industry-wide moods, obviously, obviously—and the explanation held, technically, the way a dam holds, technically.

He wrote back:

*One true thing to my enemy? Here it is, and I've never admitted it anywhere: I think about her more than I think about anyone else in my life. Not the way you're thinking—stop smirking, I can hear you smirking through the paper. I mean she's the standard. Every decision I make, I make it aware of where she stands. She's the fixed point my whole compass is set to. I don't know when that happened. I don't know what to call it. Rivalry feels too small a word lately and I refuse to examine what the bigger word might be.*

*Your turn. One true thing. And Blue—if your draft scares you, send that one. The scary drafts are the true ones. I've learned that from you.*

Quentin received it on a Sunday, read it standing in her hallway with her coat still on, and read the line about the compass three times.

Then she sat down at her kitchen table, took out the cream paper, and began the scariest draft of her life—one true thing about her enemy, the man whose voice she loathed, whose laugh she'd watched through a restaurant window, whose returned contracts and precise undercuts had begun to feel less like attacks and more like a language she was, horrifyingly, starting to translate.

She wrote until 2 a.m. She did not send it. She put it in the drawer with the unsent letter from October, the one about the dance she hadn't danced and the kiss she hadn't let happen, and she told herself she'd send it tomorrow, or next week, or whenever she was braver.

Across town, August Reyes opened his laptop to check the pen-pal program's portal for scheduling updates—and paused on a notice he'd never clicked before, buried in the member dashboard, in type so small it was practically an apology:

*Pairing Methodology & Program Fine Print — click to review.*

His cursor hovered.

He clicked.
