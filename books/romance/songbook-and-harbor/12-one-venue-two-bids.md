## 12. One Venue, Two Bids

**Quentin**

The committee room smelled like burnt coffee and municipal carpet, and Quentin Okafor had dressed for war.

Her bid for the Meridian Harbor Hall restoration sat in a navy leather folio on the table before her—forty pages of vision, budget lines, and a rendering so beautiful the architect who'd drawn it had wept a little. Across the table, August Reyes sat with his own folio in charcoal gray, because of course he'd chosen charcoal. Charcoal was the color of men who wanted you to know they were serious.

"Ms. Okafor," said Councilwoman Adeyemi, chairing the review panel with the weary patience of a woman who had heard too many pitches about adaptive reuse. "Your firm proposes full restoration at one point four million."

"One point three eight," Quentin corrected. "We found savings in the millwork."

"And Mr. Reyes, your proposal comes in at one point five two, but includes an expanded event calendar and a community programming wing."

"A hall that only hosts weddings is a hall that dies in February," August said. His voice did that thing it always did—low, unhurried, reasonable in a way that made her want to throw something. "Weddings are the heart. Programming is the pulse."

"The heart works fine without your pulse," Quentin said sweetly.

Adeyemi closed both folders. "Here's what's going to happen, and I want you to hear me out before either of you starts negotiating against yourselves. The historical trust has earmarked funds for exactly one restoration on this waterfront this fiscal year. The hall is structurally compromised—we're not talking cosmetic work, we're talking joists, we're talking the east foundation wall. Either one of your bids alone stretches the money thin and leaves us praying nothing goes wrong mid-construction." She let that land. "So the committee is proposing a joint venture. Both firms. Shared contract, shared credit, shared liability."

The word *joint* hit the room like a dropped glass.

"Absolutely not," Quentin said.

"I'd like to formally associate myself with absolutely not," August said.

"You'd like to *what*?"

"It's how boardrooms work, Quentin. You'd know if you'd ever sat on one."

"Gentlemen. Ladies. Persons." Adeyemi pinched the bridge of her nose. "You have until Friday to tell me which of you is withdrawing. Because if neither withdraws, I'm awarding it jointly, and you can hate each other on your own time."

* * *

**August**

He told his pen pal about it that night, because he told her everything now—or nearly everything—and because writing to her had become the strange soft hour of his day, the way other men went to the gym or watched garbage television.

*Dear Wren,* (she'd never given him a name either; they'd traded letters under program-assigned numbers until she'd started signing herself Wren, and he had started looking forward to the word itself) *today the city tried to punish me for ambition by chaining me to my nemesis. Imagine the person you find most insufferable on earth. Now imagine sharing a desk with them for eighteen months.*

He paused, pen hovering. He almost wrote more. He almost wrote: *the terrible thing is that when she argues with me, some animal part of my brain lights up like a struck match.* He crossed it out. Some confessions were too true even for paper.

Instead he wrote: *I keep thinking about what you said—that the things that infuriate us most in others are usually the things we're most afraid of in ourselves. What does it mean that what infuriates me most is that she's right? About the hall. About the joists. About all of it.*

He sealed the envelope and stood in his kitchen under the single yellow light, and thought about Friday, and about the fact that he had no intention of withdrawing, and no intention of letting her withdraw either, though he'd have cut off his own hand before saying why.

* * *

**Quentin**

Friday came, and neither of them withdrew, and Councilwoman Adeyemi looked personally delighted about it, which Quentin felt was a betrayal of the basic sisterhood of exhausted public servants.

The joint contract was signed in a conference room with bad lighting. Their attorneys hashed out the governance structure while Quentin and August sat across from each other not speaking, radiating enough hostility that the paralegal asked twice if they needed a moment.

The terms were simple and humiliating: co-principal designers, alternating site visits, a shared project email account they would both monitor, and weekly coordination meetings at the hall itself, because—"for insurance reasons," Adeyemi said, with a smile that suggested she knew exactly what she was doing—the committee required on-site collaboration.

Their first meeting was on a Tuesday in October, in the gutted great room of Meridian Harbor Hall, where dust hung in the light from the boarded windows like something the building was exhaling after holding its breath for years.

Quentin had been here before—last month, alone, in the blue hour, just to stand where her mother had once stood. She hadn't told anyone. She especially hadn't told August Reyes, who was currently standing in the middle of the floor with his hands on his hips, surveying her mother's hall like a man appraising a horse.

"The load-bearing wall comes down here," he said, tapping the air. "Opens the whole eastern view to the water."

"That wall is original 1920s millwork."

"That wall is rotting, Quentin. I can smell it from here."

"You can't smell rot through a drawing."

"I'm not looking at a drawing. I'm looking at the wall." He turned, and the late light caught him sideways, and she hated—hated—that he had the kind of face that light cooperated with. "Come here. Look at the baseboard."

"I'm not coming over there."

"Quentin." And then, quieter, in a tone she'd never heard him use outside of—outside of anywhere, actually, a tone with no audience in it: "Please."

She came over there. She told herself it was professional diligence.

The baseboard was rotted. She could see it now, the soft give of wood gone to sponge, and beneath it, when August knelt and pried gently with a pocketknife, the ghost of something older: a strip of wallpaper, faded to the color of weak tea, printed with tiny harbor scenes.

They both went quiet.

"My mother used to point at wallpaper like this," Quentin heard herself say, before her brain could stop her mouth. "In houses we'd visit. She'd say, *someone chose that pattern for someone else's wedding day, and now it's just wallpaper.*"

August looked up at her from one knee, knife still in hand, and for a second the war between them simply wasn't there. "That's the saddest beautiful thing anyone's said to me in this building."

"Don't get sentimental. It's a business relationship."

"Obviously." He rose, brushing dust from his trousers. "Site meeting Thursdays. You take mornings, I'll take afternoons, and we overlap for one hour to fight about the load-bearing wall."

"One hour," she agreed. "And Reyes? If you poach so much as a napkin vendor during this project, I will end you."

"Noted. And Okafor—if you undercut my structural engineer again, I'll bury you in change orders."

"Wonderful. See you Thursday."

* * *

**August**

The trouble with Thursdays was that Thursdays kept happening.

Week one, they fought about the staircase. Week two, the staircase was settled and they fought about lighting. Week three, the lighting was settled and they stood shoulder to shoulder at the eastern window—now unboarded, glass still missing, the harbor laid out gray and glittering beyond the empty frame—and argued about whether to preserve the original window casings, and Quentin said, "My mother managed this hall, you know," and then went very still, as if she'd handed him a live grenade.

"She did?" he said carefully.

"For eleven years. Before she got sick." Quentin's jaw set. "Forget I said that. It's not relevant to the casings."

"It's completely relevant to the casings. It might be the most relevant thing about the casings." He watched her decide whether to be furious. She decided, visibly, not to be—not today. "Tell me about her."

"Why do you want to know?"

Because you sound like someone I know, he didn't say. Because when you talk about her, your voice does the thing my pen pal's voice does on paper—the thing where the armor opens one inch and something warm walks out. Because I've started checking the mail like a teenager and I refuse to examine why.

"Because," he said instead, "a building restored without its story is just a renovation. And I don't do renovations."

She studied him for a long moment, wind coming through the empty window frame and moving the loose hair at her temple. Then she told him. Not everything—but the shape of it. A woman who believed weddings were promises kept in public. A daughter who learned the trade at her elbow. A songbook.

"What kind of songbook?" he asked.

"Hymns. Wedding music. She annotated it." Quentin looked out at the water. "There's a song in it she promised would play at mine someday. Which is a stupid thing to promise a seven-year-old."

"It's not stupid," August said. "It's the least stupid thing I've ever heard."

And she turned and looked at him then—really looked, the way you look at a stranger who has suddenly stopped being one—and the hour they were allotted ran ten minutes over, and neither of them mentioned it.

* * *

**Quentin**

The problem, Quentin decided in week six, was proximity.

Not the hostile kind. The other kind. The kind where you learn a man takes his coffee black but pretends to like it with cream because his grandmother said milk built character. The kind where he starts leaving the good ladder on your side of the site because he noticed you don't like asking for help. The kind where you catch yourself, on a Thursday afternoon, laughing at something he said—a real laugh, ugly and loud—and seeing him go quiet with an expression you couldn't name and didn't want to.

She went home that night and wrote to Wren—her number, her stranger, the one safe inbox left in her life.

*Dear Wren, I am in trouble. There is a man. You already know there's a man—I've complained about him for months. But the complaining has developed a complication, which is that lately I look forward to the complaining. Today he fixed the site radio without being asked and hummed along to a song my mother used to play, and I had to leave the room. I am forty-six years old. I have planned four hundred weddings. I know exactly what falling feels like, professionally speaking, and I can confirm that I am falling, and I can also confirm that the ground below me is made entirely of reasons this is a catastrophe.*

She hesitated. Then added:

*Tell me what to do. Or better—tell me you've done something reckless lately, so I feel less alone in the dark.*

*—Q*

She mailed it Tuesday. His letter arrived Wednesday—his timing had gotten uncanny lately, their letters crossing in transit like ships passing in a channel they both kept denying they sailed.

*Dear Q,* he wrote. *I've done something reckless. I've agreed to spend a year building something beautiful with the last person on earth I should build it with, and the worst part—the genuinely unforgivable part—is that some days I forget to hate her. Some days I catch myself wanting her to see the thing I just fixed. Wanting her approval like it's oxygen. If that isn't recklessness, I don't know the word for it.*

Quentin read it twice, sitting on her kitchen floor with her coat still on.

Poor Wren, she thought. Poor anonymous, unlucky Wren, falling for someone unavailable. Falling the way I'm falling—for a man I can't stand, which is to say, can't stand to be without.

She wrote back: *Your person sounds like an idiot. Forgive them anyway. Life is short and grudges are heavy luggage.*

And across town, August read her reply at his kitchen table and felt the words land somewhere under his ribs like a key turning in a lock he hadn't known was locked, and thought: *whoever you are, Wren, you understand me better than anyone alive—and God help me, I think I know exactly who you are.*

But that suspicion—he tucked it away, unexamined, the way you tuck a lit match into a pocket, telling yourself it's fine, it's out, it's nothing.

The match was not out.

Thursday was coming.
