## 13. The Almost

The harborfront hall at nine p.m. was a different animal than it was at noon.

By day it was a construction site—dust sheets, sawhorses, the committee's project manager barking about load-bearing joists and permit timelines. But at night, when the crew went home and Quentin stayed behind with her clipboard and her cold coffee, the old building remembered what it was. Moonlight came through the east windows in long silver panes and lay across the restored floorboards like table runners set for a wedding that hadn't happened yet. The scaffolding along the north wall threw shadows like rigging. Somewhere above, the lighthouse beam swept past on its ancient rotation, filling the room with light and then taking it away again, patient as a metronome.

Quentin stood in the middle of the floor with her acoustic consultant's report open on her tablet and did not read a word of it.

She had been avoiding this for three days. The report said what she'd already suspected: the hall's acoustics were extraordinary—too extraordinary to trust to a spec sheet. Sound carried here in strange ways. A whisper at the far wall could be heard at the center of the room. The consultant recommended a live test. Speech, ideally. Music, if possible.

*Movement*, he'd written, in the kind of language consultants used when they wanted someone else to make the call. *The room responds to bodies.*

Which meant what she was about to do required two people, and there was only one other person on the joint venture who would understand why it mattered, and that person was August Reyes, and Quentin would rather have eaten the clipboard.

Her phone buzzed against the sawhorse. She looked down.

**AUGUST:** Still at the hall?

She typed *no* and then deleted it, because he could probably see her car through the window, because the man noticed everything, which was one of the four hundred things wrong with him.

**QUENTIN:** Obviously.

**AUGUST:** Don't move. I'm ten minutes out. I have something for the acoustics test.

**QUENTIN:** We are not doing the acoustics test tonight.

**AUGUST:** Too late. Already driving.

She stared at the screen, composing three separate replies that all began with some variation of *you don't get to just decide*, and sent none of them, because the truth—the humiliating, inconvenient truth—was that she'd been standing in the middle of this beautiful ruined room for an hour hoping he'd show up.

That was new. That was very new. And she was not going to think about it.

* * *

He arrived in eleven minutes with his sleeves rolled up and a small portable speaker under one arm and a paper bag from the taqueria on Delancey in the other hand, and Quentin felt her whole body do something traitorous and warm before she clamped down on it.

"You brought dinner," she said.

"I brought *tacos*. Dinner implies vegetables." He set the bag on a sawhorse and looked up—at the ceiling beams they'd sanded back to their original fir, at the chandelier frame waiting for its glass, at the sweep of moonlight across the floor. He went quiet in a way that still surprised her every time. By day he was all motion and argument, quick-tongued and quick to claim territory, but give him a room like this and he went still, reverent, like a man walking into a church he didn't believe in but respected anyway.

"It's going to work," he said finally. "This room. It's actually going to work."

"Don't jinx it."

"Planners don't believe in jinxes. We believe in contingency plans."

"We believe in *insurance*, August. Jinxes are for amateurs."

He smiled—that crooked, infuriating smile that had launched a thousand client defections—and held up the speaker. "So. The test."

"The test is speech first. Read something. A toast, a vow, whatever."

"I have a better idea." He was already setting the speaker on the floorboards, center of the room, where the consultant's diagram said the resonance point lived. "The report says the room responds to movement. Bodies. Weight."

"The report says *speech*, August."

"The report also says the floor's resonance changes under distributed load versus point load, which is planner-speak for: we should know how it sounds when people are dancing on it." He pulled out his phone, thumbed through it with maddening calm. "Every wedding we ever book here will have dancing. Probably within the first hour. If we can't hear a string quartet over forty couples moving, we've wasted six months and both our reputations."

It was sound reasoning. That was the worst part. Everything dangerous he'd ever done to her had arrived dressed as sound reasoning.

"And your solution to testing this is—" She stopped. "No."

"A waltz is just distributed load with a tempo."

"No."

"Quentin." He said her name differently than anyone else did. Not *Ms. Okafor*, not the clipped professional address he used in meetings with witnesses. Just her name, plain, like it was a fact he'd accepted a long time ago. "It's nine-thirty at night. There's no one here. No clients, no committee, no cameras. It's data collection."

She should have said no again. She catalogued, rapidly, all the reasons to say no: the war between their firms, only recently converted into an uneasy ceasefire by the joint contract; the gala; the leaked bid she still half-suspected him of; twenty years of disciplined distance; the fact that she could not currently look at his forearms without losing her train of thought, which was a problem she intended to take to her grave.

Instead she heard herself say: "One song. For science."

"For science," he agreed solemnly, and put on the music.

* * *

It wasn't a waltz. Of course it wasn't. It was something slow and old—a jazz standard, brushed drums and a piano that kept stepping outside the melody and coming back apologetic—and Quentin realized, as August crossed the moonlit floor toward her, that he'd chosen it deliberately, that *for science* had been a lie wrapped around an intention, and that she had known it and let him proceed anyway.

"Hand here," he said, placing her hand on his shoulder like he was demonstrating a fold in fabric. His other hand found her waist—not grabbing, just resting, a question asked in advance. "And we walk. Basically. Walking with opinions."

"I know how to dance, August."

"I know you do. I've watched you work a reception floor. You dance like you're proofreading."

"That's called *leading*."

"That's called choreography." He started them moving, a slow turn through the moonlight, and the floor answered beneath them—a low, warm hum rising through their shoes, the old wood singing under weight the way the consultant had promised. "There. Feel that?"

"I feel it."

"The room likes us."

"The room is structurally compromised and so are you."

But she was smiling. She could feel herself smiling and couldn't stop it, and the lighthouse beam swept through and lit them silver and then released them into shadow, and somewhere in the second turn her hand slid from his shoulder to the back of his neck without consulting her, and his hand spread slightly at her waist, and the distance between them—which had been a professional arm's length, defensible, documented—became something else entirely.

They stopped dancing. Or rather, the dancing stopped happening to them, and they were just standing close in the middle of a hundred-year-old hall with music playing to no one, and August looked down at her, and everything in his face was unguarded in a way she had never once seen in daylight.

"Quentin."

"Don't," she said.

"You don't even know what I was going to say."

"I know exactly what you were going to say, and the answer is that we have a contract with a morals clause and a committee that already thinks this partnership is a mistake, and I am not giving the *Harbor Light Journal* a headline."

"I wasn't going to say anything about the contract." His voice had gone low. Honest. Worse. "I was going to say that I've spent four months looking for reasons to be in rooms with you, and I'm running out of plausible ones."

The words landed somewhere beneath her ribs and detonated quietly.

Because here was the thing—the thing she had been not-thinking about for weeks now, through site visits and vendor negotiations and long email threads that ran too late and got too funny: she had been doing the same thing. Every reason to come to the hall early or stay late had been transparent to her the moment she invented it. They were two people conducting an elaborate fraud on themselves, jointly, with permits.

"August." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved credit for. "We can't."

"Why not? Give me one real reason. Not the committee. Not the clause. One real one."

And the real one rose in her throat like floodwater—*because the last time I bet on a marriage I lost twenty years to the wreckage; because I don't believe in the thing I sell; because if you kiss me I will want it to mean everything and I cannot survive it meaning nothing*—and she said none of it, because saying it would be handing him a knife and a map to her throat simultaneously.

He leaned in. Slowly. Giving her the whole length of the hallway to say stop, telegraphing every inch, and she stood absolutely still and let the distance close to almost nothing—to the warmth of him, to breath, to the moment balanced on its head like a champagne tower—

And she stepped back.

Not gracefully. Not with dignity. She stepped back like the floor had shocked her, one full stride, her heart slamming, and the music played on into the empty space between them, oblivious and cruel.

"Quentin—"

"I have to go." She was already reaching for her tablet, her bag, her coat, assembling her exit with shaking hands. "It's late. The readings are—we have what we need. The floor works. Note it down."

"Quentin, wait—"

"*Don't.*" It came out sharper than she meant, sharp enough that he actually stopped, and she hated the look on his face—not wounded, worse, *understanding*, like he'd seen exactly what had just happened inside her and filed it away gently. "I'll see you at the site meeting. Nine a.m. Bring the revised lighting plan."

She walked out of the hall without looking back, because she knew—if she looked back, if she saw him standing alone in the moonlight where she'd left him, she would go back to him, and she was not ready to become a person who did that.

Outside, the night air off the harbor was cold enough to hurt. She sat in her car in the empty lot for a long time, hands on the wheel, engine off, watching the lighthouse sweep its light across the water.

Then she drove home, and did not cry, and did not sleep, and wrote a letter.

* * *

The letter took four drafts.

The first draft she burned in the sink—literally, a match and the kitchen stovetop, an old habit from her mother—because it was a confession, and confessions were not allowed. The rules were clear. No names, no photos, no meeting until both parties agree. Nothing in the rules said *no falling in love*, but nothing needed to; some prohibitions were structural.

The second draft was a lie. She wrote about the weather and the restoration timeline and a funny thing a vendor had said, and she got three paragraphs in before she understood that she was insulting him—her pen pal, the one person on earth who had never once been lied to by her—with banality. He wrote to her like she was a person. He deserved better than a press release.

The third draft was true, and it terrified her:

*Something happened tonight that I can't talk about directly, so I'm going to talk around it, which you'll recognize as my native language.*

*I have spent twenty years being very good at wanting things I don't let myself have. I built a whole career on it—I give other people the day I don't believe in anymore, and I'm excellent at it, and I go home to an empty apartment and call it discipline. And lately there's someone. Someone impossible, which is to say: someone with every practical reason in the world to be wrong for me, and every impractical reason to feel like the only honest conversation I've had in years.*

*Tonight I walked away from him. Mid-moment. Like pulling a fire alarm on my own life.*

*And now I'm sitting here wondering when retreat started feeling like my natural state instead of a choice I keep making. My ex-fiancé used to say I loved the idea of love the way architects love buildings they'd never live in. I told myself he was bitter. Twenty years is a long time to keep telling yourself someone was bitter.*

*I don't expect advice. I don't even expect a reply. I think I just needed to say it somewhere it counts, and you're the only place left where things count.*

*Tell me something true back. That's all. Tell me something true.*

She read it four times. Her thumb hovered over the program's submission portal—the anonymous relay, the coordinator's careful machinery, the whole apparatus built to let two strangers be honest at a safe remove.

And then she thought of August's face in the moonlight. *I've spent four months looking for reasons to be in rooms with you.* The way he'd stepped back when she did, hands raised slightly, like a man declining a gift he'd been promised. The way he'd said her name. Just her name. Plain. Like a fact.

If she sent this letter, she was doing it to hide. To give the truth to a stranger because she couldn't give it to the man standing three feet away from her. To keep loving safely, at a distance, through a slot in a wall—exactly the architecture-of-cowardice her pen pal had once confessed to building around his own broken engagement, the confession that had cracked something open in her the first time she read it.

*You deserve to be chosen knowingly,* he'd written. *Not despite the risk. With the risk standing right there in the room.*

She deleted the third draft.

The fourth draft said only:

*Not yet. Ask me again soon. I'm working up to something brave.*

She saved it to drafts, unsent, and closed the laptop, and lay awake in the dark listening to the foghorn out past the breakwater, counting the seconds between its voice and its echo off the hills.

Four seconds. The harbor had always been honest about distance. You sounded the horn, and the world told you exactly how far away everything was.

Her phone lit on the nightstand. August.

**AUGUST:** Got home fine?

She stared at it. Two little words carrying about forty others.

**QUENTIN:** Fine.

**AUGUST:** Quentin.

**QUENTIN:** Goodnight, August.

Three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again. Then:

**AUGUST:** For the record: the acoustics in that room are perfect. Best I've ever tested. Whatever we do next, the room will hold it.

She read it five times. She did not answer. She turned the phone face-down on the pillow beside her, which was either an ending or a beginning, and she honestly could not tell which—

—and in the morning, when she opened the program portal over coffee, there was a new letter waiting from her correspondent, timestamped 11:47 p.m., written in the same hours she'd been writing hers.

She opened it with her heart already pounding, though she could not have said why.
