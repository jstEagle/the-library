## 21. The Wedding Planner's Wedding

Forty-eight hours.

I have planned weddings in eleven days. I once pulled off a vineyard ceremony in six when a hurricane ate the original date. I have rebuilt collapsed arches at midnight and re-seated two hundred guests around a feud between the bride's family and the groom's without a single raised voice reaching the head table.

I have never done this in forty-eight hours.

And I have never done it for me.

"Quentin." Priya stands in the doorway of my office with her tablet hugged to her chest like a shield, and she is wearing the expression she wears when a client has just asked for peonies in December. "The committee called back. The deck passed inspection."

"Of course it did," I say. "August rebuilt it himself. He doesn't build things that fail."

"That's not—" She stops. Recalibrates. Priya has worked for me four years; she knows that when I say something in that voice, I am not talking about lumber. "The *deck*, Quentin. Passed. Inspection. You can get married on it Saturday."

Saturday. Two days from now. Forty-six hours, if we're being precise, and I am always precise, except for every single time it has mattered most.

"Then we'd better start," I say, and pull a legal pad toward me, and my hand is shaking, and I let it shake, because I decided somewhere between the ashes and the dawn that I was finished pretending my hands don't do what they do.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about planning your own wedding: you are the worst client you will ever have.

By nine a.m. I have rejected three color palettes, torn up a seating chart twice, and stood in front of my closet for twenty minutes holding the same navy blazer I wear to negotiate contracts, as though I might conduct my own vows in business casual because at least business casual has never lied to me.

Priya finally takes the pad out of my hands.

"Okay," she says. "New rule. You don't get to be the planner today. Today you're the bride, and the bride's only job is to answer questions and stand where I tell her to stand."

"I'm not a bride, I'm a forty-nine-year-old woman remarrying—"

"You're a bride," Priya says, with the iron calm she usually reserves for caterers who arrive three hours late, "and you're going to let people love you, which I know is physically painful, so we're going to do it in small doses. First dose: flowers. The wholesaler owes us a favor since the Delgado wedding. What do you want?"

What do I want.

I think about my mother standing on the harborfront steps in a cotton dress, salt wind ruining her careful hair, singing hymns off-key while my father laughed so hard he had to sit down on the dock. I think about the songbook, smoke-scuffed at one corner but whole, open on my kitchen table right now to the page she annotated in blue ink: *For my Quentin, someday. Sing it loud.*

"Gardenias," I say. "And whatever else is about to die anyway. Nothing precious. Everything alive."

Priya writes it down. Then she looks up at me, and her eyes are wet, and she pretends they aren't, and I pretend I didn't notice, and this is how we say things in my firm.

"The harbor's going to look beautiful," she says. "It already does."

* * *

At noon I drive down to the hall, because there are things a planner checks herself, and also because I cannot stay away.

They've been working on it for weeks—the joint crew, our crews, the ones who spent a year trying to destroy each other's bids and now pass lumber back and forth like it never happened. The fire took the east wing and the old ballroom floor, took the wiring and the roof over the stairwell, took things that can't be replaced. But the bones held. My mother's bones, in a sense. The stone foundation she walked every morning for nineteen years, checking the tide line, checking the light.

And the deck. August built the new deck himself, salvaged wood from three demolished boathouses, planed and fitted and oiled until it glows like something poured rather than assembled. When I walk out onto it the boards give slightly under my feet, alive, and the harbor opens up beyond the railing all the way to the horizon, gray-blue and patient, the same water that watched my mother promise things in public and keep them in private for thirty years.

August is on his knees near the far end, fitting a rail post, sleeves rolled, sawdust in his hair. He hears me and turns, and the smile he gives me is still new enough to knock the air sideways out of my chest. Eleven days since the dawn in the ashes. Nine days since I said yes—not with a speech, God knows, I said it the way I do everything important now, plainly, with both hands. Six days since I told him: *Saturday. Us. Here. Before I lose my nerve or my calendar fills up, whichever comes first.*

He didn't laugh. He sat down across from me in the ash-gray morning light and said, "Forty-eight hours is plenty. I've seen what you can do with less."

"Quentin Okafor," he says now, rising, dusting his palms on his jeans. "You're supposed to be at home being fussed over."

"I don't get fussed over. I fuss. It's directional."

"Mm." He crosses the deck to me, unhurried, and takes my hands, and looks at them. "You're shaking."

"It's adrenaline. It's logistics. There's a difference between stage fright and—"

"Quentin." He says my name the way he wrote it, once a week for a year, in letters signed with nothing but an initial, back when neither of us knew whose hand was holding the pen. "Look at the water."

I look at the water.

"My mother used to say the harbor keeps everything," I tell him. "Every promise made on it. She thought that was comforting. For twenty years I thought it was a threat."

"And now?"

"Now I want to give it something worth keeping."

He lifts my hands and kisses my knuckles, sawdust and ink and all, and says, "Then let's go make a mess of it. A beautiful mess. The kind nobody photographs perfectly."

I laugh—actually laugh—and for a moment we just stand there on the wood he saved and the stone my mother kept, two people who spent a year at war learning how to stand still together.

Then my phone buzzes, and it's the florist, and the spell breaks, and I'm back in motion, and August watches me go with an expression I decide to believe is admiration and not mild terror.

* * *

The next thirty hours are the strangest of my professional life, because everyone keeps doing things *for* me.

The Delgados send their string quartet, unpaid, unasked. Marcus from the bakery—Marcus, whom I poached back from Reyes Events during the war with a contract so aggressive his lawyer flinched—shows up at the hall with a cake box and says, "No charge. Consider it a peace treaty." The committee chair, a granite-faced woman named Odell who spent months making our joint venture miserable, appears on the deck Friday afternoon with folding chairs and begins setting them in rows without asking anyone's permission, and when I thank her she says gruffly, "Your mother would have hated folding chairs. But she'd have loved the nerve of this."

People I wronged. People who wronged me. Vendors I fought over like territory. They keep arriving, carrying things.

By Friday night the deck holds sixty chairs that don't match, an arch built from the fire-salvaged beams wrapped in gardenias and wild roses already shedding petals onto the boards, lanterns strung on rope because we couldn't rig proper lighting in time, and a aisle runner that isn't a runner at all—it's the sailcloth August pulled from a derelict sloop behind the boathouses, hemmed by Priya at two a.m. with visible resentment and invisible care.

There is no rain contingency, because the forecast is clear and because I have decided, deliberately, against contingencies. That sentence would have been physically impossible for me to utter a year ago. I built my entire career on contingencies. I built my entire *life* on them—backup plans for backup plans, exit strategies dressed as professionalism, a heart so well-rehearsed in retreat that I mistook the choreography for character.

No contingencies tomorrow. If it rains, we get wet. If the quartet misses a note, the harbor will cover for us. If I cry—I will cry—we'll call it sea spray.

* * *

Friday, 11 p.m. The hall is dark except for the lanterns. Everyone's gone home to sleep before the day. I should be asleep. Instead I'm standing at the top of the aisle in bare feet, because I kicked my shoes off an hour ago and nobody stopped me, running the walk in my head.

Forty feet of salvaged wood. Sixty mismatched chairs. One man at the end of it who read my worst fears in anonymous ink and chose me anyway, twice, three times, as many times as I gave him the chance to choose.

My mother's songbook is in my hands. I've carried it everywhere these two days, like a talisman, like a contract. Tomorrow it sits on the signing table beside the rings—his grandmother's rings, smoke-stained and re-polished, that we pulled from the wreckage together at three in the morning while the hall groaned around us.

I open it to the blue-ink page. *For my Quentin, someday.* Beneath it, the hymn. And beneath that, smaller, in handwriting gone faint with decades:

*Promise it out loud, sweetheart. That's the whole trick. Love kept private is just a wish. Love said in front of witnesses becomes a fact the world has to reckon with.*

Twenty years ago I stopped believing in marriage because my own engagement collapsed, and I told myself it was disillusionment, sophistication, professional objectivity. It wasn't. It was fear. I had promised out loud once, and the promise broke, and I decided the harbor was full enough.

But the harbor kept my mother's promises. All of them. Thirty years, and not one fell through the water.

"Okay, Mom," I say to the empty deck, to the dark water, to the woman who believed weddings were promises kept in public. "Out loud, then."

* * *

Saturday arrives the way Saturdays do—too fast, badly lit, smelling of coffee and panic.

Things go wrong immediately and gloriously. The gardenias arrive heat-shocked and half the blooms brown overnight, so Priya and I spend the morning stripping them and rebuilding arrangements around the survivors, and the result is lopsided and strange and more beautiful than anything I've ever spec'd, because it looks like what it is: rescued. The quartet's cellist gets a flat tire and arrives twelve minutes before processional, tuning in the doorway. The wind picks up at two o'clock and starts a slow campaign against the lanterns, and we surrender two of them to the harbor and move the rest inside the arch.

My dress is not a wedding dress. It's ivory silk I bought Thursday afternoon off a rack at a shop in town because the idea of a fitting, of being measured and pinned and pronounced ready, made me feel like a lie. It fits imperfectly. The hem will drag on the sailcloth. I find I don't care even slightly.

At 3:40, Priya finds me in the hall's back room, which survived the fire, which still smells faintly of my mother's era—salt and old varnish—and she takes one look at me and says, "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The checklist face. You're mentally running the timeline. Quentin. Stop. Look at me." She grips my shoulders. "Everything is handled. Everything has been handled for hours. There is nothing left to plan. Do you know what that means?"

"That I've lost control."

"That you did it. All of it. In forty-eight hours, with burned flowers and borrowed chairs and a sailcloth aisle, you built the thing you spent twenty years telling yourself was a fairy tale adults were too smart to believe in." Her voice cracks and she lets it. "You're not the planner today. You're the proof."

I hug her, hard, longer than I hug anyone, and then someone knocks—the coordinator from the pen-pal program, of all people, Ms. Adeyemi herself, in a bright yellow dress, holding a small envelope.

"A delivery," she says, eyes twinkling. "Hand-carried. He insisted. Said you'd understand."

Inside, in August's handwriting—the handwriting I stared at for a year without recognizing, God help me—one line:

*Last letter. Come find out how it ends. —A.R.*

I laugh until I cry, and then I fix my makeup, because I am still me.

* * *

The quartet plays. The wind carries the sound out over the water instead of down the aisle, which means the music arrives soft and sideways, like something remembered.

I walk.

Forty feet of salvaged wood, and I take it slowly, slower than any timeline I ever wrote, because my mother's hymn is being sung—sung, actually sung, by the whole room, because when the quartet started the melody Odell stood up first and began the words in a cracked alto and everyone followed, guests and vendors and rivals-turned-crew, sixty voices none of them trained, all of them loud, exactly as instructed by a dead woman's blue ink: *sing it loud.*

Gardenias in my hands, brown-edged and alive. Sailcloth under my feet. The harbor wide open to my left, keeping everything.

August waits under the arch of fire-saved beams, and he is crying, openly, unashamedly, and I love him so much it feels less like a feeling than a fact—a fact the world now has to reckon with.

When I reach him he takes my hands and says, quietly, just for me: "Dear Q. It was always you. Every letter. Even the ones I mailed to the wrong address."

"Dear A," I answer. "You're late. You were supposed to meet me a year ago."

"I'm here now."

"You're here now," I agree, and turn to face our witnesses, and say my vows out loud, plainly, with both hands, no contingencies, no exits, no armor—just a promise, made in public, on a harbor that remembers everything, and keeps what matters.

We sign the register on my mother's songbook. August signs first. Then I take the pen, and above my name I write, for the first time in twenty years, without hesitation, without a single crossed-out draft:

*Quentin Okafor-Reyes.*

* * *

Later—after the dancing on the deck that gives and gives under our feet, after the cake with its lopsided peace-treaty frosting, after Priya catches the bouquet with the reflexes of a woman who has dodged them professionally for years—August finds me at the railing, past the last lantern, where the harbor goes black and endless.

"Tired?" he asks.

"Exhilarated. Terrified. Planning our anniversary in my head already, don't look at me like that, I can't help it."

"I'd be worried if you weren't." He leans on the rail beside me. "You know what I keep thinking? A year ago I wrote to a stranger because I couldn't say any of this out loud to anyone real. And now—" he gestures at the deck, the hall, the lights, the whole improbable evening, "—now the stranger's my wife, and I still write her letters."

"So write one," I say. "Right now. Tell me something you haven't told me."

He thinks. The water breathes against the pilings below us.

"Alright," he says finally. "Here's your letter. *Dear Q—today I watched you walk forty feet of wood we saved from a fire, singing your mother's song, and I understood that everything I tore down in my life, I tore down because I never saw anyone build anything brave enough to live in. Now I have. Yours, entirely, A.*"

I press the letter into the songbook, between the blue-ink pages, where it belongs.

And that's when Priya comes running down the deck, phone in hand, breathless, half laughing, half alarmed, in a way I recognize from a decade of crises—which means either disaster or opportunity, and lately I've learned they often arrive wearing each other's clothes.

"Quentin," she says. "You need to see this. Someone just emailed the firm. About the lighthouse on the north shore—the one everyone says is beyond saving. They want a quote." She pauses, grinning. "They heard about the wedding. They said—they said they heard two planners brought a dead venue back to life, and they want to know if it's true."

August looks at me. I look at the harbor, which is keeping everything.

"Well," I say, taking the phone. "Tell them yes."
