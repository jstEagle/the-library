## 1. The Last Train Rule

The rule was simple, and Marguerite Eastwick had kept it for ten years without exception: no one stayed past the last train.

Not customers lingering over sheet music. Not Odette, who had a gift for turning a five-minute visit into an evening. Not even Bram, who sometimes fell asleep in the armchair by the window with a varnish rag still in his hand and had to be woken gently, like a child, and sent home before the 11:47 rattled through the station four blocks east. The rule wasn't cruel. It wasn't even, strictly speaking, about other people. It was about the shape of an evening — the way a day needed to end cleanly, all its instruments back in their cases, so that the next morning could begin on time.

Marguerite believed in beginning on time. She opened the shop at nine and not at 8:58, because 8:58 invited 8:45, which invited chaos. She wound the shop's ancient wall clock every Sunday because it lost two minutes a week and she preferred correcting it herself to being corrected by it. She drank her coffee from the same chipped blue cup, ate lunch at 12:30 standing behind the counter with a book propped against the register, and closed at six sharp with a ritual so fixed she could perform it blind: lights off in the back room first, then the front, then the sign flipped, then the key turned twice — once for the lock, once out of habit, though the lock only needed one.

People called her precise. Precise was the polite word. Sam, the fourteen-year-old violin prodigy who came in Tuesdays and Thursdays to argue about rosin, called her "a robot with excellent taste," which Marguerite pretended to find insulting and privately considered the kindest review of her life.

She had been many things before she was precise. That was the part nobody in the shop knew, or if they knew, they had the grace never to say. Ten years ago she had been Marguerite Eastwick, cellist, the kind of name that got printed in programs with a small biography underneath. She had played halls where the air conditioning hummed over two thousand held breaths. She had owned a cello worth more than the building she now rented. And then there had been one night — one performance that came apart in her hands like wet paper — and one review the next morning, three columns wide, written by a critic whose name she could still spell without thinking, the way you can spell a word that's been carved somewhere painful.

She did not think about it. This too was a discipline, and she was very good at disciplines.

* * *

On the first Tuesday of October, Odette came into the shop carrying a clipboard, which was never a good sign.

Odette ran the community center the way other people run small countries: efficiently, affectionately, and with total disregard for personal boundaries. She was sixty-one, built like a fireplug, and wore cardigans the color of emergency. Today's was orange.

"No," said Marguerite, before Odette said anything.

"You don't know what I'm going to say."

"You're holding a clipboard. Last time you held a clipboard I spent a weekend judging a pie contest."

"And you were magnificent at it." Odette set the clipboard on the counter, directly on top of a stack of invoices, which Marguerite noted and forgave. "It's the pen-pal program. For isolated adults. Winter's coming, people get lonely, we match them up anonymously and they write letters. Real letters. We provide the stationery, the post office box, the whole apparatus. It's very dignified."

"I'm not lonely."

"I didn't say you were."

"You were about to."

Odette looked around the shop with the unhurried patience of a woman who had known Marguerite for eleven years and had learned that the trick was simply to remain in the room longer than resistance lasted. She picked up a bow, examined it, put it down. She straightened a display of metronomes that did not need straightening.

"Say what you came to say," Marguerite said finally.

"Six names. That's all I need. Six volunteers and the program runs. I have five." She tapped the clipboard. "The sixth slot has been empty for three weeks and I refuse to cancel a program over one stubborn woman who alphabetizes her greeting cards."

"They're easier to find that way."

"Marguerite." Odette's voice changed — not softer exactly, but closer. "You talk to maybe eight people a week, and half of them are children buying strings. You're allowed to want more than that. Nobody would even know. It's anonymous. You write to a stranger. They write back. That's the entire thing."

A stranger. Marguerite looked at the clock above the door — 4:12, correct to the minute — and felt the familiar architecture of her days stand up around her, load-bearing and safe. A stranger meant unpredictability. A stranger meant a letter arriving whenever it pleased, asking whatever it pleased, occupying space she hadn't cleared for it.

And yet there was a version of the thought that arrived sideways, uninvited: *no one here knows anything about me.* Eight people a week, and every conversation was rosin and prices and the weather. There was no one — she performed the inventory honestly, because she was honest with herself the way she was honest about the clock — there was no one who knew what she'd lost. No one she told things to. The rule kept evenings clean; it did nothing for the long middle hours after closing, when the shop went quiet and the silence had a specific texture she had stopped naming years ago.

"One letter a week," Marguerite said. "I'll decide when it ends."

"Done." Odette wrote her name on the clipboard with triumphant speed. "You won't regret it."

"I already do."

But when Odette left, bell chiming, orange cardigan receding down the street, Marguerite stood at the counter a while, looking at the form beneath the glass. *Name (optional):* she left blank. *Interests:* she wrote *music,* then crossed it out, then wrote it again, smaller. It seemed dishonest to pretend otherwise. It seemed worse to lead with it.

She sealed the envelope at 5:50, ten minutes before close, and dropped it in the box on the corner as if disposing of evidence.

* * *

That night she broke her own routine by eleven minutes, and hated how much it cost her.

It happened because of the letter — not a reply, obviously; it had been four hours — but because signing up made her think of letters generally, and thinking of letters led her, without permission, to the bottom drawer of the desk in her apartment above the shop. The drawer she never opened. Inside was a folder of clippings she had promised herself she'd thrown away and had instead kept, organized chronologically, because destruction required a decisiveness she reserved for other things.

She took out only the last one. Three columns. She read the first paragraph, which she could have recited, and which began: *What we witnessed Tuesday night was not a performance but a slow-motion accident...*

Julian Voss. Even the name had a shape she disliked — two hard consonants with nothing between them. He had written it from the third row, she'd learned later, and filed it before midnight, and it had run above the fold. Other reviews followed, kinder ones, but his was the one that got quoted, the one conductors referenced, the one that traveled. Careers are strange organisms; they can survive a bad night. What they cannot always survive is a sentence, perfectly constructed, that everyone else decides to agree with.

She put the clipping back. She locked the drawer. She stood at her window looking down at the dark shop, at the street, at the distant red signal where the trains passed, and she thought about a stranger somewhere in this city writing to her, wanting something true.

Fine, she thought. They could have true. Just not everything. Just not the drawer.

At 11:47 the last train went through, and she heard it, faint and punctual, and felt the day officially end. She was asleep by midnight, which was late, and she woke at six feeling like she'd committed a small crime and gotten away with it.

* * *

The reply came nine days later, which Marguerite decided was either rudeness or restraint, and which she found herself checking the mail for anyway, twice, then pretending she hadn't.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, addressed in handwriting so controlled it looked typeset. Inside, a single page:

*Dear Pen Pal,*

*I'm told this program is for the lonely, which flatters neither of us. I'll skip the pleasantries since I suspect you skipped reading them. One question to begin: what is the last thing you gave up that you actually miss?*

*Mine, if you're wondering, is applause. Which I realize sounds vain. It isn't vanity. It's the sound a room makes when it agrees with you, and I haven't heard it in some time.*

*Yours,*
*A Stranger*

Marguerite read it three times at the counter between customers. On the fourth read she caught herself doing something she hadn't done in years: composing a reply in her head while ringing up a sale, the sentences arranging themselves with an ease that alarmed her.

He was prickly. He was proud. He was, unmistakably, hiding something — she recognized the architecture of a deflection, having built so many herself. *What is the last thing you gave up that you actually miss?* Who asked that question except someone standing in the wreckage of an answer?

She waited until closing. Lights off in the back room first, then the front, sign flipped, key turned twice. Then she sat down at the counter under the one lamp she'd left on, took the good stationery — the cream stock she saved for nothing, apparently saving it for this — and wrote:

*Dear Stranger,*

*You ask what I miss. Careful. Questions like that get honest answers.*

*I gave up performing. Don't ask why. Ask instead whether I miss the stage or the person I was on it, because those turn out to be different things, and I'm no longer sure the second one existed.*

*You miss applause. I understand it better than you'd want me to. Applause isn't agreement — it's forgiveness, granted in advance, for whoever you had to be to earn it.*

*One rule, since you seem like a man who appreciates rules: we don't ask each other's names. Names ruin things. Everything true can be said to a stranger, and almost nothing can be said to a friend.*

*Write back if you can live with that.*

*M.*

She read it over once. It was more than she'd said aloud in a year. It frightened her slightly, how easily it came, like a muscle remembering motion.

At 11:47 the train went through, right on time. Marguerite sealed the letter, set it by her keys for morning, and turned off the lamp — and in the dark, for the first time in ten years, she caught herself waiting for something.
