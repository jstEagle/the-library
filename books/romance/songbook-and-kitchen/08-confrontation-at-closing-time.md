## 8. Confrontation at Closing Time

He came in at ten minutes to six, the way he always did on Thursdays, and I decided before the bell finished ringing that tonight was the night.

I'd been deciding it for three weeks, which is its own kind of cowardice. Every Thursday he appeared in aisle three with his thin pretext — a set of strings he didn't need, a metronome he never bought, a question about rosin that no grown man asks four times — and every Thursday I rang him up and smiled my shopkeeper's smile and lied to his face by omission. And every Sunday an envelope arrived at the community center addressed to "M," containing sentences I had never said aloud to another living person. He wrote them to me believing I was a stranger. I read them knowing exactly whose hands had once held the pen that ended me.

"Two sets of Evah Pirazzi," Julian Voss said, setting them on the counter like evidence. "And I was wondering if you'd ever carried the Larsen Il Cannona. For cello."

"For cello," I repeated.

"You play?"

The question hung there, absurd and enormous. Ten years of this town politely not asking me that question, and here was the one man alive with no right to ask it, asking it.

"No," I said. "Not anymore."

Something moved behind his eyes — not recognition, nothing so clean as that. Just the flicker of a man hearing a door close somewhere in a house he thought was empty.

"I'm sorry," he said. "That was intrusive."

"It's fine." I slid the strings across the counter. "Sixty-four fifty."

He counted out cash. He always paid cash. A man building a life out of transactions that couldn't be traced back to anything.

"Marguerite."

My name. My actual name, in his mouth, for the first time since the review. I had given it to him three weeks ago at this same counter, watching for the flinch that never came, and I still didn't know if that meant he hadn't recognized me or that he had, and was better at hiding than I would ever be.

"Yes?"

"The dinner last week. Odette's paella. I've been meaning to say—" He stopped. Started again. "I haven't sat at a table like that in a long time. Where nobody wanted anything from me."

*Where nobody knew what you took,* I thought.

"It's just paella," I said.

"It isn't, though. You know it isn't."

And that was when I understood that I couldn't do this anymore — not the Thursdays, not the letters, not the careful architecture of almosts I'd built around this man. The shop was empty except for us. Bram had left at five. Sam had recital rehearsal. Outside, the streetlights were coming on the way they did in October, early and apologetic, and somewhere down the block the 11:40 train would eventually come through whether anyone boarded it or not.

"Julian." I locked the register drawer harder than I needed to. "Do you know why I hired on to the pen-pal program?"

He blinked. "Odette. She mentioned—"

"Odette asked. I said yes because saying yes was easier than explaining why the answer should be no. And then the coordinator made a mistake, and instead of pairing me with some retired teacher in the next town over who likes gardening, she paired me—" My voice did something I hadn't authorized. It shook. "She paired me with you."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight. It pressed down on the strings in their cases along the walls until I could have sworn the whole shop was humming.

"I don't understand," he said, and I watched him understand it anyway, watch the sentence assemble itself behind his face letter by letter: *the handwriting, the phrase about trains, the way she said* not anymore *like a woman reading her own obituary.*

"M," he said.

"Marguerite Eastwick," I said. "Cellist. Formerly. You reviewed me once. October fourteenth, eleven years ago. You called my Shostakovich 'technically immaculate and emotionally illiterate.' You said I played 'like a woman performing the idea of grief rather than grief itself.' You said—" and here my voice finally broke open, all the way, ten years of dammed water coming through at once, "—you said the second movement lacked *the courage of genuine despair.*"

"I know what I said."

"Do you?" I came around the counter because staying behind it felt like armor and I was done with armor. "Because I've had eleven years to memorize it, Julian. Four hundred words. I can recite it. Do you know what happened after? Marcus Hale dropped me from the festival roster by phone, ninety seconds after the review posted. Ninety seconds. My agent stopped returning calls within the month. The recording contract I'd signed — the one that was supposed to be announced in the spring — quietly evaporated. Nobody ever cited your review. Nobody had to. Everyone in that world reads four hundred words and does the arithmetic. You didn't end my career. You just told everyone it was already over, and they believed you, because you were *Julian Voss*, and your sentence was law."

"I know," he said.

"And the worst part—" I was crying now, furiously, hatefully, the way I hadn't let myself cry in a decade, "—the worst part is that you were *right*. That's the filth of it. I went home that night and I listened to the tape and there it was. The idea of grief. Performed. Because I was so busy being flawless that I forgot to be human, and you saw it, and instead of writing it like a man who loved music, you wrote it like a man swinging an axe, and I have spent eleven years trying to figure out which one of those men you actually were, and now you're standing in my shop buying strings you don't need, writing me letters about loneliness signed with a fake name, and I don't—"

I stopped. Breathed. The clock above the door ticked through six full seconds.

"I don't know which man I've been writing to," I finished, quietly. "That's the truth. That's the whole ugly truth. I knew it was you by the second letter — the way you write about orchestras, the way you flinch around the word *mentor*. And I kept writing anyway. I kept letting you tell me things. So whatever judgment lands tonight, it lands on both of us."

* * *

He didn't defend himself. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the ledger of my own head. In all the years I'd imagined this confrontation — in the shower, on the train, at 3 a.m. with the shop dark around me — he had always defended himself. He'd explained context, pressure, the economics of criticism. In the flesh, standing among my cellos under the bad fluorescent light, he did none of it.

"Sit down," he said. "Please. This will take longer than you want it to standing up."

"I'll stand."

"All right." He folded his hands, and I recognized the gesture — I'd seen conductors do it a thousand times, the stillness before the downbeat. "You asked which man wrote the review. Here's the answer. Neither. A third man wrote it. A man so terrified of his own teacher that he used your body to prove something to a corpse."

"Marcus Hale wasn't dead."

"Hale was dead to me in every way that mattered, and I was still auditioning for him. Every review I wrote, I heard his voice first. *Too soft, Julian. Sentiment is what critics reach for when they have no authority.* So when I sat down after your concert — and it was a good concert, Marguerite, that's the part I've never said to anyone, the playing was real, the grief was real, I heard it, and it frightened me how much I heard it — I made a choice. I could write the honest review, the one that said *this woman is more truthful than I will ever be permitted to be*, or I could write the one that would get quoted at the dinner table where Hale held court. I chose the table." His voice didn't waver, but his hands had begun to shake, very slightly, the way mine did for a year after the collapse. "I chose it again and again for nine years. Do you want the list?"

"No."

"You need the list." He said it gently, horribly. "Because you think I destroyed you. I did. But I was a machine, and machines have throughput. There was a soprano, Ana Reyes, whom I buried in 2009 because she refused my casting suggestions and I had column inches and she didn't. She teaches middle school choir in Sacramento now. She was better than everyone I ever praised. There was a composer, Dmitri Osei, whose opera I killed in two hundred words because its conductor had once embarrassed me publicly. He stopped composing. Not slowed down. Stopped. There was an intern at the paper, a girl named Priya, twenty-three years old, who edited one of my reviews without authorization because I'd been cruel about a friend of hers, and I had her fired, and I told myself it was about editorial integrity. It was about teaching the building to fear me." He looked at his hands. "There are others. I keep the list. I've kept it for four years, since the thing with the young violinist surfaced and everything collapsed. I add names when I remember them. It's the only honest work I do."

The radiator clicked. Somewhere in the back room, Bram's repair bench sat under its sheet like furniture in a house where someone died.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "Why now?"

"Because you gave me eleven years of your life to carry, and you deserve to know the full weight of it, not the excerpt." He looked up, and his eyes were dry in a way that scared me more than tears would have. Dry the way things are dry after fire. "And because you lied to me for months, and I am not going to stand in your shop and pretend we're keeping score honestly. We're not. I lied to the world for a decade; you lied to me for a season. If we're settling accounts, start with mine."

"I didn't lie to hurt you."

"No. You lied because you were afraid. So was I, every time I picked up the pen. Fear is the family resemblance. You said it yourself in your letters — you just didn't know you were describing me too."

"Don't quote my letters at me."

"Then hear this instead." He stepped forward, one step, into the space between the counter and the door, and stopped there, leaving the exit path between us unobstructed, and I understood that even now, especially now, he was careful about doors. "I am not asking for forgiveness. I'm telling you what I did so that you never have to wonder, at three in the morning, whether the man who ended you was also the man who wrote you that he was afraid of dying unknown. Both men are real. They live in the same body. I have to house them. You don't. You never have to see either of them again after tonight, and I will honor whatever you decide, including silence, including the police, including Odette, including all of it."

Outside, far off, the freight line sounded its horn — not the passenger train, just freight, but my body heard *last train* and flinched on schedule, eleven years of rule firing like a nerve.

"That's not the passenger line," Julian said quietly. "You have time."

"How would you know what time I have?"

"Because I've read forty of your letters," he said. "And in every single one, you count the minutes until you're allowed to be alone. I just never knew I was the reason you learned to."

The clock above the door read 6:41. The shop was dark except for the lamp over the register. My rage was still there — I could feel it banked in my chest like coals, not extinguished, not forgivable, maybe never — but underneath it, unbearably, was the other thing. The thing I'd felt reading his letters before I knew. The recognition.

"Thursday," I said. "You come in Thursdays."

"If you want me to stop—"

"I didn't say stop." I turned the lock on the front door, the small brass sound that has ended a thousand days of my life, and did not look at him while I did it. "I said Thursday. Come at six. And this time buy the damn metronome."

He stood very still, the way men stand when they're not sure the ground will hold. Then he nodded once, picked up his string packets, and left through the door I unlocked for him, and I stood in the dark of my own shop listening to his footsteps fade toward the station, counting the minutes until the last train — and for the first time in eleven years, hating myself for hoping it would be late.
