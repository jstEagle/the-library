## 10. Encore, Unwritten

The folding chair wobbles when I sit down in the wings of the community center auditorium, and I grip the seat edge and laugh — a small, startled sound that comes out of me like a hiccup. Ten years ago I would have demanded a new chair. Ten years ago I would have demanded everything: the right temperature, the right light, the right silence. Tonight there is a wobbly chair, a radiator clanking like a man digging his way out of prison, and through the curtain gap I can see Odette in the third row wearing a sequined cardigan she absolutely bought for this occasion.

I am not nervous. That's the strange part. Or rather — I am nervous in the place where nerves live now, which is somewhere behind my ribs instead of in my throat, and it feels less like terror and more like standing at the edge of cold water on a hot day. You know it will hurt for one second. You know you'll be glad after.

Sam finds me backstage, violin case under one arm, bowing to everyone he passes including the janitor. He's sixteen now and taller than me by four inches, which he uses exclusively for looking down at people while asking for advice.

"You're going to play," he says. Not a question. He's been saying it all week like a spell.

"I'm going to try."

"No." He sets down the case and looks at me with the terrible directness of teenagers and cellists. "You're going to *play*. There's a difference. Trying is what you do when you're still negotiating with yourself."

"When did you get wise?"

"Tuesday. It was very sudden." He hugs me, quick and hard, then pulls back embarrassed, as if the hug had escaped without authorization. "Bram saved you a spot in the back row. He said if you chicken out he's going to tell everyone about the time you cried at the Vivaldi."

"That was allergies."

"Sure it was."

He goes out to tune up, and I'm alone with the wobbly chair and the radiator and the sound of an audience assembling — chairs scraping, Odette's laugh carrying like a foghorn, someone dropping a program. And somewhere in that gathering noise, near the very back, a voice I would know through concrete, through fire, through ten closed years: Julian, low, answering something Bram has said. I don't look through the curtain again. If I look, I'll spend the whole evening performing for him instead of for myself, and tonight is not a performance. Tonight is the opposite of a performance. Tonight is the first honest thing my hands have done in a decade.

* * *

The fundraiser is Odette's doing, as all catastrophes and miracles at the community center are. She framed it as a benefit for the music program — new stands, repaired instruments, scholarships for kids like Sam once was, hungry and broke and ferocious. She asked me three months ago if I'd "maybe consider saying a few words," and I said yes because I owed her for the pen-pal program, which turned out to be either the worst clerical error in municipal history or the best thing that ever happened to me, depending on the hour.

Then, two weeks ago, sitting in the shop after closing with tea going cold between us, she said: "You should play. One piece. Your terms, your hands, your choice. Nobody's asking you to come back. We're asking you to show up."

And I heard myself say, before the fear could get its hand over my mouth: "Yes."

The word sat in the air between us like a bird that had flown into the room and couldn't find the window.

So here we are. Sam opens with Bach — the Chaconne, because he has no fear and no sense of scale, and he plays it beautifully and slightly too fast, the way youth plays everything. Bram follows with a luthier's demonstration that is somehow both a lecture and a love letter to spruce and varnish. The audience is small and warm and half of them are my regulars, the people who buy strings and rosin and gossip from me every week, who have spent ten years treating me not as a fallen artist but as the woman who knows exactly which shoulder rest will stop your neck hurting.

They never asked me why I stopped. That's the gift I only understood recently. They let the silence be mine until I was ready to break it.

Odette introduces me with a speech I only half hear, because my blood has gone loud. Something about second acts, something about the shop being "the warmest room in this neighborhood," something that ends with her looking straight at me and saying, "She's going to hate me for this, but here she is anyway," which is so precisely Odette that I almost laugh walking out.

* * *

The stage lights are cheap and yellow. The piano bench is where a chair should be; I carry out my own folding chair, the twin of my wobbly one in the wings, and set it center stage, and the audience makes a soft sound — surprise, or recognition. A folding chair. No throne. That's the point, though they can't know it yet.

I sit. I adjust the endpin. My cello is the good one, the one I kept through everything because selling it would have been admitting the whole decade was real. Bram serviced it last month and left a note inside the case: *She sounds ready. Are you?*

I look out. Third row: Odette, sequins blazing, hands already clasped like prayer. Fourth row: Sam, still flushed from the Bach, watching me with his whole face. Back row, against the wall where the light doesn't reach: Julian Voss, alone, hands in his lap like a man at a hearing. He came early. Bram told me later he helped stack chairs. Julian Voss, stacking chairs, and nobody recognizing him, or worse — everybody recognizing him and letting him do it anyway, which is its own kind of mercy.

I don't play anything anyone expects. Not the Elgar. Not the piece from that night, the one that broke. I chose weeks ago, lying awake, testing pieces against my chest like fabric: not Dvořák, too much history; not Bach, too much church. I chose the Fauré Élégie — no. Even that felt like mourning, and I'm done holding my own funeral.

I chose something small. A piece almost nobody programs. Fauré's Sicilienne — no, everyone plays that. All right. The truth: I chose an arrangement of a folk song Bram taught me, a Swedish tune called *Polska efter Byss-Calle*, which he used to hum while gluing seams, and which I transcribed one slow afternoon in the shop because it wouldn't leave me alone. Three minutes long. Modest. Written for dancing, not for proving anything. When I told Sam, he frowned and said, "That's not a comeback piece." And I said, "It's not a comeback. It's a first piece."

Because that's what I finally understand, sitting here with the rosin dust hanging in the yellow light: I am not reclaiming the career. The career belonged to a woman who played to survive her teacher's expectations and then a critic's pen, a woman whose whole body was an argument she was always losing. That woman drowned on a stage ten years ago and I have spent a decade pretending I wasn't the one who walked away from the water. I'm not her. I'm the one who ran a shop, who learned names, who wrote letters to a stranger and meant every reckless word. This piece belongs to *her* — to me, the current model, the final edition.

I put bow to string.

The first note comes out rough. I let it be rough. In the old days a rough note would have detonated a chain reaction — panic, compensation, collapse. Now I just play the next note, and the next, and the tune gathers itself the way tunes do, and somewhere in the second phrase my breathing syncs with the bow and I stop being aware of my hands at all, which is the whole secret nobody tells you: mastery isn't control, it's permission. The radiator clanks. Someone coughs. A baby gurgles in the back and instead of flinching I lean into the lilt of the dance, because the baby is right — this is music for moving, for living rooms, for people who came to be together and not to judge.

I think, absurdly, of letters. Of ink smudged on my thumb. Of the sentence that changed everything — *I know who you are, and I've known since aisle three* — and how writing it cost me him for six weeks and gave me back myself forever. Both things true. Most important things come in pairs like that.

The last note decays into the radiator's clank and the small stunned quiet of a room that expected a resurrection and got something better: a person, playing.

I stand. The applause starts careful and then stops being careful, Odette leading the charge like cavalry, Sam whistling through his fingers badly. I bow — one bow, shallow, honest — and I say into the microphone I don't need:

"Thank you. New stands for the kids start Monday."

Which gets a bigger laugh than the speech would have.

* * *

Afterward the lobby smells of coffee and supermarket flowers. I work the crowd the way I work the shop — easy, unhurried, hugging Bram, letting Odette squeeze me until my ribs file a complaint, promising Sam masterclass listening sessions all summer. And through all of it I feel the back of the room, the gravity of one particular person waiting without waiting, patient as furniture, present as weather.

When the room thins, I find him by the coat rack, holding a coat he hasn't put on, wearing the expression of a man rehearsing a speech he'll never deliver.

"Don't," I say.

"Don't what?"

"Apologize again. Don't review the performance. Don't tell me the intonation in bar twelve was brave." I step closer. He smells like he always does now — cedar shavings from helping Bram, coffee, cold night air. "Tell me what you actually thought."

He looks at me for a long moment, and whatever he was going to say falls away, and what's left is just true: "It sounded like you. Whoever that is now. I'd like to keep finding out."

"The last train's at 11:42," I say.

His face does something complicated — hope and fear fighting over the same ground. "Marguerite—"

"It's 11:15." I take his hand. His fingers are cold; mine aren't. "My rule was never really about trains, Julian. It was about making sure nothing could stay long enough to hurt me. You already stayed. You're in the letters, in the shop, in the margin notes of every score I've touched for a year. So here's my terms, my hands, my choice:" I hold his eyes. "Stay past the last train. Stay past tomorrow's. I'm not asking for a review. I'm asking for a duet."

Outside, faintly, a train sounds — leaving, not arriving, carrying nobody we know.

Julian closes his hand around mine and says, "I'll need a chair."

"There's one in aisle three," I tell him, and turn off the lights.
