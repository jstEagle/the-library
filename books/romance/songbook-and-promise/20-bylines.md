## 20. Bylines

The pen was heavier than I expected.

That sounds like a metaphor, and I suppose it was one too, but mostly it was literal: Julian had sent me a fountain pen for the signing, because Julian did not understand that some of us had spent a decade writing in whatever was nearest—a hotel pen, a grocery receipt stub, the back of a boarding pass at 30,000 feet while a client's voice poured out of us like water from a cracked jug. The pen was black lacquer with gold trim and it cost more than my first car, and when I uncapped it over the title page of *Harvest Light: A Memoir*, my hand shook so badly I had to set it down and laugh at myself.

Ten years. Ten years of bylines that weren't mine, of acknowledgments that thanked "our collaborator" in language vague enough to survive a legal review, of watching strangers fall in love with sentences I'd written at 3 a.m. in someone else's grief. And now here was my name—*Noor Fairweather*—printed on a spine, in a font, in the world, where anyone could find it.

I signed. The ink bled slightly into the paper, a small dark bloom, like something taking root.

"There," I said to the empty room. "That's the whole trick."

My phone buzzed. My agent, Diana, who had spent ten years telling me anonymity was a career asset and then, the moment the storm story broke—the ghostwriter unmasked, the rival vintners reconciled, the memoir rewritten as co-authored truth—had called me weeping about how proud she was, as though she hadn't been the architect of my invisibility all along.

**DIANA:** NYT review drops Sunday. They're going to say your name. Are you sitting down?

**ME:** I'm standing in a vineyard cellar holding a very expensive pen. Everything is already absurd.

**DIANA:** Good. Absurd is your natural habitat. Don't let Elias read the review before you do. Authors get weird about reviews.

*I'm not an author,* I almost typed, out of pure habit. Then I looked at the page again—at my name on it—and deleted the sentence, and felt something in my chest unlock that I hadn't known was locked.

* * *

Elias read the acknowledgment page standing up.

I want to be clear that this was not a dramatic choice. He was in the cellar because he was always in the cellar; the man treated that room the way other people treated churches or therapists' offices. He'd come down to check barrel temperatures and found the advance copy propped against a case of his own pinot noir, and he simply started reading, standing there in his barn coat with a hydrometer still in his pocket, and I came down the stairs and stopped halfway because I recognized what was happening and didn't want to interrupt it.

He read slowly. His lips moved a little, which he would deny to his grave.

The acknowledgment page had taken me eleven drafts. Not because it was hard to write but because every version I tried was either too much or not enough, and the true thing kept trying to dress itself up in poetry. In the end I'd written it plain:

*To E.V., who taught me that a voice is a thing you grow, not a thing you find—and who never once, in ten years of silence, pretended he'd forgotten mine.*

He got to the last line and stood there. The cellar was cold and smelled of oak and wet stone and the particular mineral sweetness of wine sleeping in barrels, and above us the late-winter light came through the little window in a slant, catching dust motes, making everything look briefly consecrated.

"You put me in a book," he said finally, without turning around.

"I put an initial in a book. Very discreet. Nobody will ever know."

"Fairweather." He turned then, and his eyes were doing something complicated—wet and amused at once, which was, I had learned over these past months, his most dangerous setting. "You dedicated four pages of the final chapter to my pruning technique."

"It's *good* pruning technique. It's practically erotic pruning technique. The editor wanted more."

He laughed—that low, surprised laugh he still seemed to produce against his own will, as if humor were a crop that kept coming in despite his best efforts at austerity—and crossed the cellar floor and took my face in both hands, careful of the pen still in my fist, and kissed me the way he did everything now: without hurry, without performance, like a man who had learned exactly what rushing cost and refused to pay it again.

When we broke apart, he rested his forehead against mine.

"I read it twice," he said. "Last night. Before you even gave it to me. I found the box on the porch."

"You *broke into* my advance copies?"

"I'm a winemaker. We're famous for patience." He stepped back, and there it was—the thing underneath the joke, surfacing the way things did between us now, gently, because we'd agreed to stop drowning them. "It's good, Noor. It's better than good. And it's *yours*. Every sentence. I kept checking, you know. Looking for the seams. Where you disappear into someone else." He shook his head. "There aren't any. You didn't hide anywhere in it."

"That's the terrifying part," I admitted. "I kept reaching for the mask and it wasn't there. I wrote a whole chapter about my mother and thought, well, that'll get cut, nobody wants the ghostwriter's mother. And the editor called me and said it was the heart of the book." I looked down at the pen. "Turns out I had a voice this whole time. Extremely inconvenient information to receive at thirty-eight."

"What are you going to do with it?"

"God, don't ask me big questions in a cellar. The acoustics make them worse."

But he wasn't laughing now, and neither was I, and this was the thing I was still learning about him—about us—that we had gotten very good, in the wreckage and rebuild of the past year, at standing in the uncomfortable quiet together instead of filling it with something easier.

Because the truth was, the book being done had changed the weather. For months we'd had the manuscript between us like a shared project, a legitimate reason to be in the same valley, the same kitchen, the same bed on weekends when the roads allowed. Now the book was finished, printed, real. Now there was no deadline left to hide behind. And Elias Vance, who had waited ten years and then another year after that, was not going to ask me for anything—I'd learned that much. He would stand in cellars reading acknowledgment pages and wait for me to speak first, forever, if I let him.

So I spoke first.

"We should talk about terms," I said.

His eyebrows went up. "Terms."

"Terms. Like adults. Like two people who have already done the grand-gesture version and know how it ends." I hopped up to sit on the tasting bench, the pen balanced across my knee. "The last time we did this, it was all feeling and no structure. You were about to be famous, I was about to be terrified, and neither of us said a single concrete thing to each other until I was gone. So. Concrete things." I held up a finger. "One: I am not moving to the valley full-time. Not yet. My life is half in Brooklyn—Diana, my sister, a plant I've somehow kept alive for six years and feel morally bound to continue keeping alive."

"Two?" he said, and I loved him a little desperately for not arguing, for treating my list like a contract worth reading rather than a wall worth scaling.

"Two: you will tell me when I scare you. When it feels like I'm vanishing into work, or into someone else's story, or just into my own head. You don't get to swallow it for a decade and present me with the bill later. That nearly killed us both."

"Agreed." A pause. "And you tell me when I start treating the vineyard like it matters more than—"

"It won't be hard. You sulk visibly. It's one of your best qualities."

"Three?"

"Three." I took a breath. This was the hard one, the one I'd rehearsed in the shower and immediately garbled aloud. "Three: we go slow enough that I can feel my feet. I spent ten years being a voice in other people's mouths. I need a little while to get used to being a person who says things out loud and has them count. Which means—" and here my voice did wobble, damn it, "—which means some days I might be strange about it. About being seen. And I need you to not interpret strange as leaving."

Elias was quiet for a moment. Then he sat down next to me on the bench, close enough that our shoulders touched, and looked at the rows of barrels as if consulting them.

"My turn," he said. "Terms from my side."

"Proceed."

"One: I will never ask you to be smaller. Not for the vineyard, not for me, not for the story people tell about us—which, by the way, the local paper has already started telling, and it's insufferable, they've given us a portmanteau."

"Oh no."

"'Vanceweather.'"

"*Oh no.*"

"Diana called me. She thinks it's marketing gold. I told her if she put it in print I'd stop selling her cases for the restaurant trade."

"She'd survive. She's ruthless." I leaned into his shoulder. "Keep going. Two?"

"Two: I'm building the new block on the north slope next spring, the one I told you about, the experimental varietals." He said it lightly, but I heard the weight under it. "I want you there for the first planting. Not living here. Just—there. There's a tradition, sort of. You plant the vines with the people you expect to drink them with, someday, years from now. It's a long promise. It's the longest promise we make." He turned to look at me. "No pressure. But I wanted it said out loud, concretely, per your rules."

I looked at him—this man who had once been a boy in a shared apartment playing me half-finished songs on a guitar with a missing string, who had become a stranger, who had become a name I couldn't say, who had become, through blizzards and betrayals and one genuinely catastrophic distributor dinner, this: someone who negotiated love like a man drafting a contract he actually intended to honor.

"Yes," I said. "To the planting. To the long promise. Yes."

"Yeah?"

"Don't make it weird, Vance."

"I would never." He was smiling like sunrise over the north slope, which was frankly unfair. "Three: the songbook. I'm still working on it. The years apart—it's taking longer than I thought, because some of those years I only have your books for, the ones you ghostwrote, and I keep finding you in them, and it's—" He stopped. Started again. "It turns out I can't finish it until I figure out how to forgive that decade, and I'm close, but I'm not there, and I won't give you a half-finished thing."

"Elias. You don't have to forgive the decade. You just have to stop living in it. Those are different jobs."

He considered that the way he considered everything—thoroughly, seriously, like a man tasting something with his whole attention.

"Amend the term," he said. "I'll work on stopping living in it."

"Accepted." I picked up the pen and, on impulse, reached for the advance copy and opened the flyleaf. "Hold out your hand."

"Why?"

"Because I'm about to deface a first edition and I need a witness."

He held out his hand, palm up, puzzled, and I ignored it entirely and wrote in the front of the book instead, right under the title page, in my own handwriting, in ink that would never be attributed to anyone else:

*For Elias—who read the drafts before anyone, including me, believed in them. First edition of everything.*

Then I signed it. *Noor Fairweather.* Full name. Both names. All of me.

He read it upside down, since I was still holding it, and then he took the book from my hands with a care that undid me completely, the way you'd handle something living.

"I'm putting this in the cellar," he said. "Locked case. Behind the '09s."

"The '09s are your best vintage."

"Exactly."

* * *

We went up for lunch, and I made soup, because I had discovered that Elias's kitchen contained three bottles of olive oil and a single sad lemon and otherwise nothing a human could eat, and feeding him had become one of the quiet pleasures of my weeks in the valley. He sat at the counter and chopped bread for croutons with the intense focus he brought to all manual tasks, and we argued amiably about whether the review would mention the co-authorship or fixate on the scandal, and the afternoon light came through the windows all gold and unhurried, and I thought: *this is what it's supposed to feel like.* Not the falling. The staying. The ordinary miracle of a Tuesday.

The phone rang at four.

I almost didn't answer—an unknown number, probably a journalist, the press cycle around the book had been relentless—but something made me pick up, and the voice on the other end was crisp, professional, and faintly familiar in a way I couldn't place.

"Ms. Fairweather? My name is Catherine Osei. I'm an editor at Meridian House." A beat, the kind editors use when they're about to say something they know is valuable. "I've read *Harvest Light*. Twice. And I'll be direct, because I understand you appreciate that: we'd like to talk to you about your next book. Your own. Under your own name. Whatever it is—we'd like to see it."

I stood in Elias's inadequate kitchen, holding a wooden spoon, and looked out the window at the bare vines in their long winter rows, thousands of them, all pruned back to nothing, all waiting, all of it—every drop of the coming summer—already stored in those stripped brown branches, invisible, patient, certain.

"Ms. Fairweather?"

"Yes," I said. "Sorry. Yes, I'm here." I laughed, and it came out steady. "Tell me everything."
