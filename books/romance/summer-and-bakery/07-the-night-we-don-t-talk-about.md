## 7. The Night We Don't Talk About

The storm had been talking all night—a low, grinding conversation between wind and window that neither August nor Theo acknowledged, the way you don't acknowledge a third person at a table who knows too much about you.

They had managed, somehow, to get through the evening. Dinner had been soup the innkeeper's wife left warming before she and her husband drove down to her sister's in the valley, with instructions to help themselves and a look that said she understood exactly what kind of situation she was leaving behind. They'd eaten at opposite ends of a table built for twelve. They'd washed up without speaking, and the silence had been almost companionable, which was the most dangerous thing that had happened all day.

Now August stood in her room on the second floor, dressed in the spare clothes she kept in her expo kit—a flannel shirt, wool socks, her grandmother's cardigan—and listened to the wind try the shutters like a burglar with all the time in the world.

She should sleep. She had a café to save, a supplier contract that depended on surviving this weekend with her dignity intact, and a body that had been running on four hours of sleep since the foreclosure notice. Instead she kept replaying the moment in the kitchen that afternoon, when Theo had reached past her for the copper bowl and his sleeve had brushed her wrist, and she had felt it all the way up her arm like a current finding its old circuit.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of building a life on the assumption that Theo Marchetti was a closed door in a city she never had to visit.

And now he was downstairs, probably, in the kitchen, because of course he was. Where else would either of them go when the world ended?

* * *

She told herself she was going down for tea. That was the story she told her feet as they carried her along the corridor, past the portraits of dead innkeepers, down the stairs with their runner worn to the weave. The kitchen was dark except for the banked fire in the old hearth at the far end, and Theo was standing at the worktable with his sleeves rolled, staring at the innkeeper's flour bin like it had personally wronged him.

"I'm not stealing anything," he said, without turning around. "I'm inventorying."

"At eleven o'clock at night."

"Old habit." He turned. The firelight caught the gray at his temples now—new, or maybe she'd just never let herself look long enough to notice. "You want tea? There's a kettle. It's not a tempering machine, but it has its charms."

She should have said no. She should have said goodnight and climbed back into her narrow bed and stared at the ceiling until the wind wore itself out. Instead she filled the kettle and set it on the hob, and when she turned around he was still watching her with that expression she remembered too well—the one that meant he was assembling a question he wasn't sure he was allowed to ask.

"You didn't come to the expo to network," he said.

"No."

"You came because Marta Voss threatened your contract."

"How did you—"

"Because she did the same thing to me in 2019. It's her move. She plays the long game with suppliers." He leaned against the worktable, arms folded. "August. What's happened to you? And don't say nothing. I've seen your hands."

She looked down at her hands as if they might testify against her. There was flour in the creases still, from the 3 a.m. session she'd told herself he'd slept through. Her knuckles were cracked from winter and from washing up in a café whose water heater had been dying since October.

"Nothing happened," she said. "I have a bakery. I have a life. It's small, but it's mine."

"It was always going to be small," he said, and then winced, as if the words had come out with edges he hadn't intended. "That's not—I didn't mean—"

"No, you did." She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "You did mean it. That's the honest thing you've said all day."

"August."

"Theo." She turned to the kettle because it was easier than his face. "Why are we doing this? We've managed fifteen years without—"

"Have we?" His voice was quiet, but it carried in the empty kitchen the way sound carries in a snowfield. "Because I've managed it badly. I've managed it with a different pastry chef in every kitchen I've worked, none of whom could make a cardamom bun, and I've managed it with awards on shelves I don't look at, and I have managed it, August, by never once going back to that hotel ballroom in my memory, because if I go back there I have to ask the question I've been asking for fifteen years, which is—"

The kettle screamed.

She turned it off with a hand that wasn't quite steady, and the silence that followed was worse than the wind.

"Which is what?" she asked the kettle.

"Which is why you left."

* * *

They ended up at the hearth, because the kitchen table felt like an interrogation room and the hearth at least had the decency to be warm. He sat in the innkeeper's chair; she sat on the floor with her back against the stone, knees up, tea cradled in both hands, because sitting on the floor was a thing she did when she was twenty-three and couldn't be a grown-up about anything, and apparently some part of her had never left.

"You know the official version," she said. "Everyone knows the official version. You froze. You panicked. You served a broken sponge and walked out, and the judges were kind about it because you were young, and then you disappeared for two years and came back and built an empire."

"I know the official version," Theo said. "I've spent fifteen years not correcting it."

"Why?"

"Because correcting it would have required saying what actually happened. And what actually happened required—" He stopped. Started again. "August. I stood in that ballroom and watched you walk out during the plating hour and I told myself you'd be back. You were always back. You went out for air before every service, you came back with your sleeves down and your jaw set and you were the best cook I've ever stood next to. So I plated your components because that's what we did, we covered each other, that was the whole— that was the entire point of us. And then the judges called time and you weren't there, and my sponge was on the bench because I'd been too busy watching the door to bake mine properly, and the whole thing came down on me like a building."

She had known this. She had known it in her body the way you know a scar is there, and hearing it said aloud, plainly, without accusation, was worse than any accusation could have been.

"Theo—"

"I'm not finished." He set his tea down. His hands were steady. His voice wasn't, quite. "I'm not finished because I've rehearsed this for fifteen years and I never thought I'd get to say it. I lost everything that night. Not the competition—I didn't care about the competition. I lost the assumption that you'd come back. And then the press got hold of it, and my mentor at the time, Chef Aubert, he told me the smart thing was to take the fall quietly, because a young man who chokes is a story that fades, but a young man who blames a woman who left is a story that follows her. So I let them say I choked. I let them say it for fifteen years because the alternative was saying, *she left, and I don't know why, and I've been standing in that ballroom ever since.*"

The fire shifted. A log collapsed into embers and a small storm of sparks went up.

"I didn't choke," August said.

It came out before she could decide to say it. Theo went very still.

"I didn't choke, and you didn't choke, and the sponge wasn't broken because you were watching the door." Her voice sounded strange to her, flat and far away, like someone reading a report. "The sponge was broken because I broke it. I came back. Twenty minutes before time. I came back through the loading corridor, and I was going to walk in and finish, I swear I was, I was going to fix it, and instead I—" Her hands were shaking now, properly, and she set the tea down before she scalded herself. "I took the sponge out of the oven. Your sponge. The one you'd built all morning, the good one, the one you'd been babying because you knew I was rattled. And I—"

"August."

"—I pressed my thumb into the middle of it. While it was still hot. And I walked out the loading door and I got in a cab and I went to King's Cross and I took the first train north and I didn't stop until the tracks ran out."

The silence was absolute. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

"Why," Theo said. It wasn't a question. It was the word itself, stripped of everything.

"Because I was going to win," she said. "Do you understand? We were going to win, or I was going to win, and the prize was a stage at the Ritz program and a write-up and everything we'd said we wanted since we were nineteen and stealing flour from Aubert's stores. And I stood there in the last hour and I realized I couldn't do it. Not the cooking. The *winning*. Winning meant leaving. Winning meant London, and the program, and a life where I was someone's chef instead of—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "Instead of yours. Because that's what we were, wasn't it? We were each other's, and everyone knew it, and the prize was only ever going to go to one of us, and I couldn't stand in that ballroom and become the person who left you behind for a job. So I made sure I couldn't win. I destroyed our entry. I destroyed *your* work. And then I ran, and I let you stand there and take it, because the story of a man who choked was kinder than the story of a woman who sabotaged her partner and fled, and I told myself it was mercy. I told myself I was protecting you. Fifteen years, Theo. I have told myself that for fifteen years."

Theo hadn't moved. The fire had burned down to a red glow, and in that light his face was unreadable, and August understood, finally, the full weight of what she had done—not the sabotage, which was a young woman's panic made flesh, but the fifteen years of silence that had let him carry a story about himself that was a lie.

"Say something," she whispered.

"I don't—" He stood up. Sat back down. Stood up again, and this time he crossed to the window, where the snow was coming down so thick it looked like the world had been erased. "I spent two years after that thinking I was broken," he said to the glass. "I thought something in me had failed under pressure, and I rebuilt myself around that. I built the whole— the company, the discipline, the whole architecture of Theo Marchetti, on the foundation that I was a man who could not be trusted in the final hour. And you're telling me it was never true. It was never true, and I could have—"

"I know."

"—I could have been *free*—"

"I know, Theo."

He turned around. His eyes were wet, and he wasn't bothering to hide it, and that undid her more than shouting would have.

"Why are you telling me this now?" he asked. "Why tonight?"

"Because you asked why I left. And because I'm so tired." Her voice cracked on the last word. "I'm so tired of carrying it. I've carried it through every café I've worked in, every 4 a.m. start, every cardamom bun I've baked with her recipe, and I thought if I never said it out loud it would stay small enough to hide, but it didn't, it got bigger, it got into the foundations, and then you walked into that expo hall with your Pâtissier of the Year face on holding a dessert I invented when I was twenty-three, and something in me just—" She gestured helplessly. "Broke open."

"The cardamom thing in the magazine," Theo said slowly. "The *Delice Ashgrove*. That was yours."

"Ours. I made it for you the night before the competition. It was supposed to be—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It was supposed to be our signature. For the restaurant we were going to open. The one we drew on napkins."

Theo sat down on the hearthstone beside her. Not touching. The space between them was maybe four inches, and it was the loudest four inches in the world.

"You let me take the fall," he said. Not cruel. Just true.

"Yes."

"And I let you."

She looked at him.

"You think I didn't wonder?" he said. "You think in fifteen years I never once thought, *August Ashgrove does not freeze. August Ashgrove once talked a health inspector out of closing a charity bake sale through sheer force of will. Something else happened that night.* But wondering was easier than knowing, because knowing meant you'd done it on purpose, and if you'd done it on purpose, then you'd chosen to hurt me, and I would rather have been a man who choked than a man who wasn't worth staying for."

"Oh, Theo."

"So." He dragged a hand down his face. When he looked at her again, something had shifted—not forgiveness, not yet, but the ground clearing after an avalanche, the terrible quiet where you take stock of what's buried. "We're both liars. Fifteen years, and we built lives on top of the lie, and now we're sitting in a snowed-in kitchen and the lie is out, and I don't—" He laughed, short and unsteady. "I genuinely don't know what you do next. There's no protocol for this. I've read every leadership manual ever printed and none of them cover *my former partner confessed to sabotaging our competition entry and I feel relieved, which is its own kind of horrifying.*"

"You feel relieved?"

"I feel like a man who's been told the tumor was benign fifteen years after the surgery." He shook his head. "It doesn't undo the surgery, August. I still lived it. I still built everything on it. But God help me, some part of me is *dancing*."

A sound came out of her that was half laugh, half sob. "That's the most Theo thing you've ever said."

"I'm very consistent." He was quiet for a moment. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a shutter banged and settled. "The sponge," he said. "You pressed your thumb into it."

"I did."

"Was it—" He stopped. Started over, and this time his voice was very careful, the way it went careful when he was handling something that could go off. "Was it because of what I said that morning? In the prep kitchen. Before service."

August's breath caught. Because yes. There had been a morning. There had been a prep kitchen that smelled of yeast and nerves, and Theo, pale with focus, tying his apron, saying, *Whatever happens today, don't let it change us. Promise me. Whatever they offer you, whatever they take—don't let it change us.*

And she had promised. And then she had stood in the ballroom with the offer in her hands and understood that keeping the promise and taking the prize were two doors in the same wall, and she had been twenty-three, and she had chosen the wall.

"You said don't let it change us," she whispered.

"And you heard that as—"

"As a choice. You made it a choice. Win and lose you, or lose and keep you. And I couldn't— I couldn't pick, Theo, so I picked neither, and I ran, and I've been running ever since, and the worst part—" The tears came now, finally, the ones she'd been holding since the expo hall, since the foreclosure notice, since the morning fifteen years ago when she'd watched the north coast slide past a train window and told herself she felt nothing. "The worst part is it didn't work. I lost you anyway. I lost you for fifteen years and I didn't even get the prize."

Theo reached out then. Not for her hand, not for her face—his hand landed on the stone between them, palm up, an offer rather than a taking. She looked at it for a long time. The fire had burned so low that his hand was mostly shadow.

She didn't take it. Not yet. Some part of her understood that taking it now would be a way of ending the conversation, and the conversation wasn't finished—it had fifteen years left to say.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me," she said. "I know what I did. I know what it cost you. I'm not going to stand here and ask for absolution because I had a bad night in a snowstorm."

"I know." His voice was rough. "I'm not offering it. Not yet. I want to be honest about that too. I look at you and I'm furious and I'm relieved and I want to shake you and I want—" He stopped himself. "It's a lot. It's going to take more than one night."

"It should."

"But August." He said her name the way he used to say it across a hot pass, when the kitchen was screaming and her station was on fire and his voice was the one thing that cut through. "You should know that in fifteen years, I never once stopped—"

The lights went out.

Both of them froze. The storm had finally taken the power line, and the kitchen dropped into firelight and shadow, the wind suddenly enormous, and the moment—the sentence, whatever the sentence had been going to be—hung in the dark between them, unfinished.

"The generator's in the barn," Theo said after a moment, into the dark.

"Is it."

"It's a long walk. Through the snow. In this."

"So we'll be dark."

"So we'll be dark." She could hear him settling back against the hearthstone beside her, close enough now that she could feel the warmth of him along her whole left side. "The innkeeper said the fire holds till morning."

"The fire holds," August agreed, and did not move, and did not want to.

They sat there while the storm did its work, and neither of them finished the sentence, and neither of them slept, and somewhere in the small hours August realized that for the first time in fifteen years, the thing she was most afraid of was not the past.

It was the morning. And what Theo Marchetti would look like in the daylight, after a night like this—whether he would be a man who had heard her confession and opened his hands, or a man who had heard it and understood, at last, that some doors, once you walk through them, don't open again from the other side.

The fire held. The wind screamed. And August Ashgrove, who had run from every hard thing for fifteen years, sat very still in the dark and, for once in her life, stayed.
