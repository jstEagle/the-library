## 15. The Ethics Hearing

The boardroom of Marchetti Patisserie Group occupied the top floor of a glass tower in the financial district, and it had never once smelled like pastry.

Theo noticed that on the morning of the ethics hearing, standing at the window with his hands in his pockets while the board filed in behind him. Fifteen floors below, the city went about its business—buses sighing at curbs, a man selling roasted chestnuts from a cart despite the season, steam rising off grates like the whole street was proofing. Up here: filtered air, leather chairs, a long table of walnut veneer that had cost more than August's annual flour budget. Not one gram of butter in the building. He'd built an empire with no smell of bread in it and called that success for fifteen years.

"Théodore." Marguerite Chen, chair of the governance committee, took her seat at the center. She was sixty-one, silver-haired, and had once fired a CFO mid-sentence for using the word *synergy*. "Shall we begin?"

"We may as well."

He'd known this was coming since the moment his lawyer called three weeks ago. *Anonymous creditor, related-party transaction, potential conflict of interest.* The words had arrived in his inbox formatted like any other memo, but reading them, Theo had felt something crack quietly behind his sternum—the sound of a man realizing he's been caught doing the one honest thing he's done in years.

Marguerite opened the folder in front of her. "For the record, this session concerns your acquisition of the outstanding debt on Ashgrove Café, owned by one August Ashgrove. You did not disclose the acquisition to this board."

"I didn't consider it a company matter."

"You purchased it through a holding entity."

"To keep my name out of it."

"And why," said Dev Okafor, the youngest member, not unkindly, "would you want your name out of it?"

Because she would have thrown it back at me, Theo thought. Because she'd rather burn the café down than owe me anything. Because I know her better than I know myself and I still couldn't predict what she'd do with the truth except flinch.

"Because the debt is personal," he said aloud. "It predates any question of competition."

"It doesn't," Marguerite said. "That's precisely the problem, Théodore. You are the most visible pâtissier in the country. Your name sits on forty-two locations, a product line, a television contract pending renewal. If you forgive—or appear to forgive—a direct competitor's commercial debt, every rival chain in the sector will allege market manipulation. Our regulators will be obliged to look. And they will find, sitting underneath it all, a fifteen-year-old scandal involving the same woman that this company has spent a decade and considerable money burying."

The word *burying* landed in the room and stayed there.

Theo walked to the head of the table but didn't sit. Sitting felt like surrender. "I'm listening to the options."

"Two options," Marguerite said. "One: you recuse yourself entirely from any matter touching Ashgrove Café. The debt reverts to standard collections. The café's fate returns to the bank's normal process. You have no further involvement, publicly or privately, until the review clears you—which it will, if there's nothing to find."

"And two?"

Dev looked down at his papers. Marguerite didn't.

"Two," she said, "isn't on the table yet. It comes after. Henri Delacroix has requested a meeting with you this afternoon. I suggest you hear him out before deciding how much of your life you're prepared to spend on a bakery in a town whose name I've already forgotten."

Theo had forgotten nothing about the town. He could draw its high street from memory now—the crooked lamppost outside the hardware shop, the frozen fountain, the way the light came down the valley at four in the afternoon and turned the snow the color of custard. He knew which window of the café caught that light. He'd stood across the street from it often enough, in the dark, holding notes he'd written like a coward and left with flour deliveries like a ghost.

"The review," he said. "If I recuse. How long?"

"Ninety days. Possibly less."

Ninety days. Her loan terms gave her until spring. It might just work—it might strangle her slowly instead of quickly, which was somehow worse—and all of it depended on him agreeing to become, officially, no one. No involvement. Publicly or privately.

Privately. The word was doing heavy lifting, and everyone at the table knew it.

"I'll need the day," Theo said.

"You have until five o'clock," Marguerite said, closing the folder. "And Théodore—for whatever it's worth, and I suspect it isn't much—I've read the file on what happened fifteen years ago. All of it. Including the parts your lawyers made sure were never filed anywhere." She held his gaze without cruelty. "You were twenty-four years old and you took the blame for something that wasn't yours to take. This board has benefited from that silence for a decade. Don't let us benefit from it again."

* * *

Henri Delacroix received him in a private dining room at Le Ciel, the restaurant where Theo had eaten exactly twice in his life and both times felt like he was being weighed.

His old mentor was seventy-three now, smaller than memory kept him, but the hands were the same—huge, scarred, a cook's hands in a financier's world. Henri poured two glasses of white wine without asking and pushed one across the table.

"You look terrible," Henri said.

"I've been sleeping badly."

"You've been sleeping in your office. Your assistant told me when I called to check." Henri drank. "Sit down, boy. You make me feel like a doorman."

Theo sat. Outside the window, the river moved gray and patient between its banks.

"Henri. You asked for this meeting, so I'll spare us the part where we discuss my health."

"Direct. Good. You learned one useful thing from me." Henri set down his glass. "I'll be plain, because you haven't got time and neither have I. There's a project in Lyon. A school—not a franchise, a real school. Classical technique, modern kitchen science, and a mandate to train kids who'd never get near this industry otherwise. Kids like you were. Kids like her was, if half of what I hear is true."

Theo's hand stilled around the stem of his glass.

"They want a founding director. Someone with a name, a reputation, and—" Henri's eyes were shrewd and gentle at once, an unbearable combination, "—someone who's finished with whatever it is that's been eating him alive for fifteen years. The salary is modest. The work is enormous. You'd have to divest Marchetti entirely. Walk away clean, walk away now, and build something that matters more than any dessert you've ever plated."

"And if I don't walk away clean? If I stay tangled in it?"

"Then you stay tangled in it." Henri shrugged, massive shoulders barely moving. "Théo. I taught you lamination when you were nineteen and furious at the world. I know how you work. You will stay tangled in it. You'll manage it, and lawyer it, and keep it in a drawer the way you've kept everything in a drawer, and in thirty years you'll be the richest sad man in Europe." He leaned forward. "Or you burn the drawer. One chance. That's all I can offer, and frankly it's all I'd respect."

Theo looked at the wine. At the river. At the old man who had once told him, in a scalding kitchen in Lyon, that talent was common and character was rare and Theo should hurry up and acquire some.

"Why me?" he asked. "For the school. There are a hundred chefs with cleaner stories."

"Because of the story." Henri smiled without much humor. "A man who lost everything at twenty-four because he loved someone more than his own reputation—that man knows exactly what it costs to hide. Who better to teach young cooks not to do it?" He drained his glass and stood, joints cracking. "I need an answer by Friday. Think about the girl, if you must. But think about the boy you were, too. He's still in there. I've seen him looking out of your face for fifteen years, waiting for someone to open the door."

* * *

That night Theo went back to his apartment—a beautiful, silent, magazine-featured apartment that photographed like a dream and lived like a hotel—and stood in his kitchen for a long time without turning on a single light.

He cooked, when he couldn't sleep. Everyone assumed that. The truth was worse: he almost never cooked anymore. There was a test kitchen downstairs staffed by six brilliant people who executed his concepts, and a home kitchen worth more than a car that he used to boil water for tea. Somewhere in the last decade, cooking had stopped being the thing he did and become the thing he supervised. He'd told himself that was growth. Scaling. Maturity.

Standing in the dark, he understood it for what it was. Hiding. The same hiding, just with better lighting. He hadn't left the kitchen the night August vanished—he'd left himself. He'd taken the blame publicly, yes, worn the scandal like armor, and then he'd spent fifteen years building a life so large and so armored that no one could ever get close enough to ask him a real question. *Why did you let her go? Why didn't you follow her? Why did you believe, for even one hour, that she'd cheat?*

He knew the answers now. They'd come to him at 3 a.m. in a mountain inn kitchen, watching August's hands move through dough like they were greeting an old friend. He'd believed it because believing it hurt less than believing she'd run. He'd let her go because chasing her meant admitting how much he needed her, and needing people was the one thing his childhood had trained out of him. He'd taken the blame because guilt was a shape he could hold. Love wasn't.

His phone buzzed on the counter. Marguerite, sending the formal recap. *Decision required by 17:00 tomorrow.*

Below it, a second notification. An alert from a news service he subscribed to mostly out of vanity: *Local Chef August Ashgrove Fails Surprise Health Inspection; Sources Cite Anonymous Complaint.*

Theo read it three times. Then he sat down on the floor of his own beautiful kitchen, back against the cabinets, phone in his hand, and laughed—one short, broken sound with nothing in it but recognition. Of course. Of course it was escalating. The universe had watched him hesitate for three weeks and decided to stop being polite.

She'd nearly signed the surrender papers once before; her lawyer had mentioned it, in the careful way lawyers mention things. A failed inspection on top of everything else—

He thought of her grandmother's notebook, which she'd shown him once, years ago, in another lifetime: flour-stained pages, handwriting going soft with age. He thought of the cardamom buns. He thought of her face in the firelight saying *I froze, Theo. I froze and I ran and I let them hang it on you,* and how forgiving her had been the easiest thing he'd ever done, and how forgiving himself remained the one recipe he'd never cracked.

Marguerite wanted an answer by five. Henri wanted one by Friday. The board wanted recusal or divestment. The regulators wanted cleanliness. Everyone wanted a version of Theo Marchetti that fit neatly in their filing systems.

But there was a third option none of them had written down, because none of them had stood in a snowbound kitchen at 3 a.m. and remembered who they used to be. He could resign. Not recuse—resign. Hand back the empire, eat the scandal, stand in front of the cameras and say *here is what actually happened fifteen years ago, and here is what I did about it, and here is what I'm doing next.* Dirty, expensive, irreversible. The kind of choice that couldn't be managed or lawyered or put in a drawer.

The kind of choice a man makes when he finally forgives himself enough to stop hiding.

Theo got up off the floor. He opened his laptop, and instead of drafting a statement, he opened the document where he'd been keeping something else entirely—a speech, half-written, that he'd started the night after the inn and couldn't finish. He read it once. Then he picked up his phone and dialed the number he'd sworn he wouldn't use unless it was an emergency.

It rang four times.

"Ashgrove Café," said a voice he'd know through concrete, through fire, through fifteen years of glass towers. Tired. Guarded. Alive.

"August," Theo said. "Don't sign anything. Whatever they've put in front of you today—don't sign it. I'm coming, and I'm bringing the truth with me, and it's going to be loud."

A pause. Down the line, faintly, he could hear the tick of an oven cooling, the small domestic music of her kitchen, and beneath it—barely—the sound of a woman deciding whether to trust him one more time.

"Theo," she said slowly, "what did you do?"
