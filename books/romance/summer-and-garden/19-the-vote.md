## 19. The Vote

The dining room of Eastwick House had been built for exactly this kind of occasion, which was to say: for the performance of family. The long table with its water-stained mahogany, the portraits of dead Eastwicks glowering from the walls, the chandelier that Odile's grandfather had shipped from Prague and that had never once, in Oscar's memory, been fully lit—every element arranged to remind the people seated beneath them that they were temporary and the house was not.

Odile had lit the chandelier tonight.

All twelve bulbs burned above the table where the family gathered on the last Saturday of September, and Oscar thought, watching the light tremble in the good crystal Odile had insisted Camille set out, that his grandmother understood theater better than any conductor he'd ever played under. She wanted them to see each other clearly. That was the point of all this light. You could not hide in a room this bright.

Theo sat at the far end, linen shirt pressed, hair damp from a shower, looking like a man who had already won something. He had a leather folder beside his plate—the way he always had a folder, always a document, always a version of events that favored him. Camille sat across from him with her ledger open and her pen capped, which meant she had finished her arithmetic and was waiting for the rest of them to catch up. Marisol sat at Oscar's right hand, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, wearing the blue dress she'd worn the first night, and when Oscar glanced at her she gave him a small nod—not encouragement, exactly. Acknowledgment. *I'm here. Do what you came to do.*

And Odile sat at the head of the table like weather.

She was smaller than she had been even three months ago. The summer had spent her, Oscar realized—or rather, she had spent herself, deliberately, the way you spend the last of a savings account on one final purchase. Her hands were folded on the white damask, and those hands, which had once rapped knuckles and turned pages of sheet music and gripped Oscar's father's arm hard enough to leave marks, were now thin as winter branches. But her eyes were the same. They had always been the same. They moved around the table now, counting her descendants the way a general counts troops before a battle she intends to win.

"Thank you all for coming," she said, though none of them had been given a choice. "The lawyers have drawn up the final papers. Before I sign anything, the house will be decided. Tonight. By vote."

"There are four of us," Theo said. "You, me, Camille, Oscar. Simple majority."

"Nothing about this family has ever been simple, Theodore." Odile let the silence do its work. "But yes. A vote. And before it, statements. Each of you will say what you believe this house is for. Then we vote, and whatever is decided, it is decided, and I will hear no appeals, no revisions, no *revisitings*. I am eighty-one years old and I am tired of being the only adult at this table."

Camille uncapped her pen.

* * *

Oscar had not slept. He had spent the night in his childhood bedroom with Odile's unsent letter open on his chest, reading it until the words wore grooves in him, until he could recite the passages the way he'd once recited the Bach suites—*your mother deserved better than our silence; you deserved better than our fear; the house was never your father's to lose.* He had read it so many times that the paper had gone soft at the folds, and somewhere around four in the morning he had understood that the letter wasn't really an apology. It was instructions. It was Odile telling him, in the only language she trusted—writing, formality, distance—what she would never say aloud: *Take it. It was always yours. Stop asking permission from people who never gave it.*

He had gotten up in the dark and stood at the window overlooking the garden, and the garden had looked different to him now. Not a wound. A bed, lying fallow. Waiting to be asked what it wanted to grow.

Marisol found him at dawn, or he found her—they had stopped keeping track of who went to whom. They sat on the boathouse dock with coffee going cold between them, and he told her what he intended to do, and she listened the way she listened, wholly, without rehearsing her reply while he spoke.

"You're sure?" she said.

"No," he said. "But I'm done letting *sure* be the price of doing things."

Something moved through her face then—something that might have been pride, or grief, or both wearing the same coat—and she said, "Then say it like you mean it. Not like an apology. Like a man who owns the room."

"I don't own the room yet."

"You will," Marisol said. "That's the whole point of tonight."

* * *

Theo went first, because Theo always went first; waiting felt to him like losing.

He opened the folder. Of course he opened the folder. "The facts," he began, and Oscar watched Camille's mouth flatten at the word, because there were no facts in Theo's folder, only interpretations wearing facts' clothing. "The house costs eleven thousand a month to maintain. The roof needs replacing within five years. The market is strong *now*, and sentiment is not a financial instrument. I've secured an offer from the Halvorsen Group—forty-two acres, waterfront, they want it for a resort development. The number is generous. The number is *final*. We split it four ways, we invest wisely, and we stop pretending that a building can hold a family together when the family couldn't hold itself together."

He closed the folder and spread his hands, the picture of reasonableness. "Dad would have sold. Let's not pretend otherwise."

It was a calculated blow, aimed at Oscar, and Oscar felt it land and noted, with a kind of clinical interest, that it landed in a place that had already healed over. His father was a scar now, not a wound. Two years ago that sentence would have ended him. Tonight it just made him tired, and a little sad, because Theo still believed their father was a fixed point to argue from, when the truth was that their father had been a storm system all along, and everyone in this room had been weather.

"Camille," Odile said.

Camille did not stand. She never performed when sitting would do. "The numbers support a sale," she said, and Theo smiled, and she continued, "if the only thing you value is liquidity. I ran three scenarios." She turned her ledger around. "Sale and split. Retention as private residence. And retention as—" she paused, and looked at Oscar, and something passed between them that had never passed between them before, some acknowledgment that she had known more than she'd said for longer than she'd admitted, "—as an operating entity. A school generates modest revenue, qualifies for the historic preservation trust, and reduces carrying costs by sixty percent. It doesn't make anyone rich. It keeps the house alive." She capped her pen. "For what it's worth, I priced the garden restoration separately. It's the cheapest line item on the page and the only one that appreciates."

"Sentiment," Theo said.

"Arboriculture," Camille replied, deadpan, and for one absurd second Oscar almost laughed, and saw Marisol almost laugh too, and understood that his sister had just chosen a side in the only language she spoke.

"Oscar," Odile said.

* * *

He stood. His chair made a sound against the floor, small and final, like the first note of a piece after the tuning stops.

There was a moment—he knew it intimately, had lived inside it for two years—when the body decides whether it will hold. Onstage it had happened between the downbeat and the first phrase: the hands present, the mind present, and then the third thing, the traitor thing, arriving uninvited. He waited for it now. He stood in the lit dining room of Eastwick House with his family watching and he waited for the shaking to begin.

It didn't come.

What came instead was the memory of a pier, two nights ago, and the sound of a ferry horn fading down the channel, and his own hands steady on the fingerboard for the first time since the collapse—steady not because the fear was gone but because he had finally been playing *for* someone instead of *against* himself. Marisol had stood at the end of the dock with her arms crossed against the wind, and she hadn't applauded. She had just watched him the way you watch a boat come safely into harbor. Like it was expected. Like it was home.

"Theo's right about one thing," Oscar said. "A building can't hold a family together." He let that sit. "But this isn't about holding us together. We're past that. This is about what the house is *for*."

He walked to the window—the long window overlooking the terraced garden, gone to seed, wild with late goldenrod and the skeletal stalks of Marisol's first plantings, which had come up anyway, stubborn, unsupervised, alive.

"When I was nine years old, I practiced in the music room while my mother gardened. I could see her through this window. She'd look up when I got a passage right—I could feel it, even with the glass between us. When she died, the garden died with her. Nobody said so. Everybody knew it." He turned back to the table. "My father knew it most of all. And everything he did after—to me, to my career, to this family—he did in a house that was quietly grieving, and he made all of us keep the grief a secret, and secrets are expensive. Camille could tell you the exact figure."

"Oscar," Theo warned.

"I'm not finished." And he heard it himself, the steadiness in his own voice, low and unhurried, and marveled faintly that it belonged to him. "Grandmother held this house in trust against him. Against exactly what he became. She protected me the only way she could, by silence, and silence cost her twenty years, and I'm not going to honor that by selling the protection off to a resort developer so we can each buy a nicer apartment in cities where we never have to see each other." He looked at Theo. "I refuse your deal. All of it. There's nothing you can expose that matters anymore. Yes, Marisol came here as my paid companion. She stayed as something else. If you'd ever stayed anywhere long enough to learn the difference, you'd know it's not for sale either."

The silence rang. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked against its frame in the wind, once, twice, like applause from an empty gallery.

"So here is my vote, and my proposal." Oscar laid his hands flat on the table—his hands, the ruined ones, the healed ones, scarred at the fingertips where the tendons had been repaired, steady now, visibly steady, and he let everyone look. "Eastwick House becomes a summer music school. For kids from the island, for kids from the mainland who can't afford conservatories, for anyone whose hands or hearts need teaching how to hold again. The music room, the boathouse, the grounds. Camille runs the books—she's the only one of us who won't steal, including from herself. And the garden gets restored, properly, under the person who knows soil better than any of us know music." He glanced at Marisol, and did not look away quickly, and did not care who saw. "It becomes the heart of the school. My mother's garden. Growing things again."

"You can't be serious," Theo said. "A *school*. You'll burn through the trust in a decade and be begging the Halvorsen Group for scraps."

"Probably," Oscar agreed pleasantly. "Or it outlives all of us. Either way, it will have been *used*, Theo. Which is more than you're offering it. You want to convert it into money so it can stop meaning anything. I'm voting to let it mean something."

"Grandmother," Theo said, turning, appealing, and for the first time all evening he sounded young. "Tell him. Tell him what this family owes—"

"What this family owes," Odile said, and her voice, though thin, cut the room like wire, "is exactly what Oscar has just paid." She rose. It took her a moment, and Camille half-rose too, and Odile waved her down with imperial irritation and stood alone at the head of the table with the chandelier blazing above her. "I have listened to forty years of arguments in this room. Your father's. Your grandfather's. Mine. Every one of them was about control dressed up as duty. This is the first argument I have ever heard in this house that was about *giving*." She looked at Oscar for a long moment, and her eyes were bright and terrible and proud. "I vote with Oscar. The house goes to him. The trust transfers. Camille?"

"With Oscar," Camille said. "Obviously. I told you the numbers."

"Two to one," Odile said. "Motion carries. Theodore, you may appeal to the lawyers, and the lawyers will bill you handsomely to tell you what I'm telling you now: it's done."

Theo sat very still. Then he closed his empty folder, and something in his face shifted—not surrender, not yet, but the dazed expression of a man discovering the map he'd been using was of a different country. "You'll fail," he said quietly.

"Almost certainly, at first," Oscar said. "That's how playing works. You miss the note, you play it again."

* * *

Later—much later, after Camille had gone to her room with the ledger hugged to her chest like a rescued child, after Theo had left without goodbye, the front door closing behind him with a sound that was not quite a slam and not quite anything else—Oscar and Marisol walked out into the garden under a sky rinsed clean by wind.

The stars were out in force, the cold-season stars, sharp and numerous. Marisol stopped at the edge of the first terrace, where her autumn plantings stood in ragged rows, and put her hands on her hips, surveying her scruffy kingdom.

"A summer music school," she said. "With my garden as its heart."

"Your words. I stole them shamelessly."

"You'll need a name for it."

"It has one." He said it simply. "Summer and Garden. My mother used to call it that—this season, this place. She said the whole year was just waiting for it."

Marisol was quiet for a moment. Then she reached over and took his hand—his right hand, the ruined one, the steady one—and laced her fingers through his, careful of the scars, uncareful of everything else.

"Then you'd better write it down," she said, "before you forget it like everything else you were supposed to remember."

And Oscar laughed—really laughed, the sound surprising him, ringing out over the dark terraces—and pulled her close, and neither of them heard, until it was nearly upon them, the crunch of gravel on the drive: a car arriving, headlights sweeping the facade of Eastwick House, and a figure stepping out into the starlight who made Marisol go rigid against his side.

Because Marisol recognized the walk before she recognized the face.

"Oscar," she said, very quietly. "That's my brother."
