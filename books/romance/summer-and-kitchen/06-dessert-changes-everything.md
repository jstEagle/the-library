## 6. Dessert Changes Everything

**Coral**

The dessert course arrived at four o'clock sharp, because Aunt Bev ran the reunion like a naval operation and had announced the schedule twice before lunch.

Coral watched the servers—his cousins' kids, drafted into aprons two sizes too big—carry out trays of lemon posset in little glass jars, each one crowned with a torched meringue that had gone slightly brown at the peaks. Bev's signature. She'd been making it since Coral was nine, back when he'd still believed family gatherings were something to look forward to instead of survive.

Forty Okafor relatives filled the community hall, and every single one of them had, at some point during the afternoon, looked at Juniper Vale with open, cheerful curiosity. She'd been asked where they met (a cooking class, the lie they'd built together on a napkin at the dockside café), how long they'd been together (seven months, which Juniper had delivered with such specific weariness—"long enough that he snores"—that three separate aunts had laughed until they cried), and whether he was as stubborn at home as he'd been as a boy (yes, Juniper said, worse; he alphabetizes his spices and then pretends he doesn't).

She was magnificent. That was the problem.

Coral sat beside her at the long table under the paper lanterns Bev had strung from the rafters, and he was aware of her the way you're aware of weather—a pressure change, a shift in the air. Her knee was six inches from his under the tablecloth. He kept cataloguing the distance. Six inches. Then five, when she turned to answer a question from Cousin Denise. Then back to six. He hated himself for measuring it.

"You're doing great," she murmured, not looking at him, smiling brilliantly at something Uncle Ray was saying about boat engines.

"I'm doing nothing," Coral murmured back. "You're doing everything."

"Team effort." She speared a strawberry from the centerpiece and set it on his plate like it was nothing, like it wasn't the kind of small domestic gesture that made three separate relatives exchange glances of pure delight. "Eat something. You've been pushing rice around your plate for an hour."

"I'm pacing myself for dessert."

"Liar. You're hiding." But she said it kindly, and low enough that only he could hear, and there it was again—that strange, disorienting experience of being seen by someone who was supposed to be pretending not to know him.

Across the hall, his father stood near the window with a beer he wasn't drinking, talking to Marcus Okafor Sr.'s old fishing partners. Marcus hadn't come over to their end of the table all afternoon. Coral didn't know if that was better or worse than the alternative. The not-coming had a weight to it, a held breath. His father was watching. Coral could feel it the way you feel sun through a window—no direct heat, but you knew.

Then Aunt Bev tapped her glass with a spoon, and the hall went quiet in stages, laughter folding down into attention.

"If everyone could raise a glass," Bev called out, "I want to say a few words before dessert—and yes, Dennis, I will be brief, don't start."

Ripples of laughter. Coral's stomach performed a slow, cold roll. He knew this ritual. Every reunion, Bev toasted the milestones: the new babies, the graduations, the anniversaries. The safe, legible happinesses. He'd spent twelve years positioned carefully outside the blast radius of that toast, close enough to the family, far enough from the spotlight.

"And last," Bev said, her eyes finding their end of the table, warm and terrible, "I want to toast something we haven't had at an Okafor reunion in a very long time." She raised her glass higher. "To Coral—and to the lovely Juniper, who finally dragged our boy out of that lighthouse long enough to bring her home to meet us all. To the happy couple!"

*The happy couple.*

Forty glasses rose. Forty faces turned toward them. Somewhere in the back, someone whooped. Coral felt the moment arrive like a wave he couldn't dodge—all that warmth, all that belief, aimed directly at a thing that did not exist.

He reached for his glass. His hand wasn't quite steady.

And Juniper moved.

It happened fast, smooth as anything she'd done all day—she leaned in, one hand coming up to rest against his jaw, and pressed her lips to his cheek. Selling it. Closing the deal. Exactly what they'd rehearsed in theory, if never in practice, and Coral braced for the performance of it, the stagecraft, the pleasant hollow theater of a fake kiss for a fake couple.

That is not what happened.

Her lips were soft and slightly cool from the lemon water she'd been drinking, and they landed just below his cheekbone, close enough to the corner of his mouth that his whole nervous system reorganized itself around the point of contact. And then—this was the part, this was the part he would think about later, in bed, staring at the ceiling—the kiss *stayed*. A half-second past sellable. A half-second past performance. Long enough that he felt her exhale through her nose, small and unsteady. Long enough that his hand came up without his permission and settled against her back, between her shoulder blades, and he felt her go still under his palm like a struck bell.

One second. Maybe less. It lasted a geological age.

When she pulled back, her face was composed, her smile intact, her eyes bright with something that was absolutely not part of the act. She raised her glass toward Bev with the other hand. "To the happy couple," she echoed, voice steady, and the hall drank, and cheered, and moved on to the babies and the boats and the price of crab pots.

Coral drank too. The wine tasted like nothing. His cheek burned where her mouth had been, a brand he could feel when he talked, when he swallowed, when he turned to accept Cousin Denise's delighted shoulder-squeeze.

Under the table, her knee touched his. Neither of them moved away.

* * *

**Juniper**

The kiss was a mistake. Juniper identified it as such approximately one-tenth of a second after initiating it, which was, she reflected grimly, about nine-tenths of a second too late.

Here was what she'd intended: a quick, professional peck. Cheek, corner, done. Two seconds of theater, forty satisfied customers, contract fulfilled. She'd kissed strangers on cheeks before—in her old life, in London, pressing her lips to the damp cheeks of investors and food critics and her business partner's friends at launch parties, and every one of those kisses had been exactly as empty as they were designed to be. Kissing was a currency. She'd thought she still had some.

But Coral had turned into it. Just slightly—two degrees, maybe three, an angle so small it might have been accidental, except that nothing about this careful, watchful man was accidental. He'd turned into the kiss the way a plant turns toward light, instinctive and helpless, and her hand was already on his jaw, and she could smell salt and cedar and whatever soap he used, and her mouth had landed soft instead of quick, and somewhere in the middle of it her chest had done something complicated and terrifying that she refused to name in a room full of strangers.

Half a second. She'd given him half a second too much, and she'd felt it land—felt him go still beneath her hand, felt the exact moment the performance stopped being a performance for both of them simultaneously, like a pilot light catching.

Now she sat with her knee against his under the tablecloth, eating lemon posset she couldn't taste, while around them the Okafor family absorbed her into its bloodstream with alarming efficiency. An aunt had already asked if she'd cater. A teenage cousin had asked for her café's Instagram. Someone's grandmother had gripped her wrist with surprising strength and told her she had "good hands, a cook's hands," and Juniper had felt the compliment land somewhere deeper than compliments usually reached.

This was the danger of the Okafor family, she was beginning to understand. They didn't ask polite questions and move on. They *kept* you. They remembered your name and your favorite pie and the fact that you'd gone back for seconds of the stuffing, and next year they'd ask about it, and the year after that. Belonging here wasn't a door you walked through. It was a current.

And Coral sat beside her, quiet and watchful, deflecting questions with a smile she now recognized as armor, and she wanted—with a want that had an expiry date stamped on it, three weeks minus two days—to know what was underneath the armor. What he pushed around his plate instead of eating. Why his father stood across the room like a lighthouse running dark. Why, when Bev toasted them, Coral's face had gone through joy and grief in the space of a single breath, so fast she almost missed it.

"To the happy couple," someone repeated down the table, and there was scattered laughter, and Coral laughed too, and it was a good laugh, warm and real, and Juniper realized with a lurch that she couldn't remember which parts of today had been the lie.

That was the trouble with honesty-about-everything-except-each-other. The rule had seemed so clean in her inbox, so elegant. But rules assumed you could keep the categories separate. Fake date, real life. Performance, person. Contract, heart.

Her knee was still touching his. She still hadn't moved it.

"So," Coral said quietly, turning to her while Denise interrogated a nephew about his exams. Up close, his eyes were darker than she'd clocked at the café, flecked amber at the center, and he was looking at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere—gratitude and warning and question all at once. "About that kiss."

"What about it?" Casual. Professional. Her pulse was doing something unprofessional.

"That wasn't in the rehearsal."

"There wasn't a rehearsal. That was the problem." She held his gaze, refusing to blink first. "You turned into it."

"You lingered."

"You counted?"

A beat. His ears went faintly red, and Juniper felt something in her chest crack open sideways, warm and disastrous. *Oh no,* she thought, with the clarity of a chef watching a soufflé begin to fall. *Oh, this is bad.*

"For the record," Coral said, "I'm filing that under 'committed performance.'"

"File it however you want." She picked up her spoon, pointed it at his posset. "Your meringue's getting soggy."

"Coral!" Bev bore down on them with the unstoppable momentum of a woman who had fed forty people and deserved adoration. "Juniper, sweetheart, tell me honestly—is that recipe a keeper? I've been tinkering with the vanilla."

"It's perfect," Juniper said, and meant it, and felt Coral watching her mean it. "Don't change a thing."

Bev glowed, squeezed both their shoulders, and swept off toward the kitchen, and the hall roared back into noise around them—babies, boats, crab pots, the whole loud living machinery of a family that had decided, unanimously and without consultation, that Juniper Vale belonged to it now.

Coral leaned closer, his shoulder nearly touching hers, and said, so only she could hear: "Same time next year?"

It was a joke. It was obviously a joke. It was also, she suspected, a test fired casually across the table to see what she'd do with it, and she had three weeks minus two days, and a rule she'd written herself, and a half-second of kiss she could still feel on her mouth like sunburn.

"We'll see," she said.

His smile flickered—just barely, just at the edge—like a light across water, there and gone, and Juniper understood suddenly that she'd failed the test, or passed it, or that the test had never been passable at all.

Down the hall, his father set down his untouched beer and finally, finally started walking toward their end of the table.
