## 12. Pruning Season

The shears were older than Lior was. Her father had bought them the year the first Chardonnay went in, and their handles had been worn to the shape of two different grips since then—his broad palm, then Dev's narrower one, and now hers, which fit neither and both. She tested the blade against her thumb and felt the fine serration of rust where the oil had worn away.

"Left side first," Dev called from three rows over, his breath pluming white in the gray morning. "We take the west block before the wind comes up off the water."

"I know how to prune, Dev."

"You know how to *cut*. There's a difference." He appeared at the end of the row, wool cap pulled low, cheeks already ruddy from cold. "Dad used to say the vine tells you what it wants to keep. You just have to shut up long enough to hear it."

Their father had been dead six years, and still he moved through this vineyard like weather—every instruction arriving secondhand through Dev's mouth, every row a conversation they were all still having without him. Lior bent to the first vine and let the shears decide.

The work was good in the way that only repetitive physical labor could be good: it asked nothing of her mind except rhythm. Snip, twist, drop. The canes came away pale at the wound, weeping a little sap that would freeze by afternoon. Around her the rows ran long and bare toward the shoreline, where the tanks sat silvered with frost, and beyond them the sea lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of dishwater. Three weeks since the storm. Three weeks since candles and confessions and a night in the boathouse she had not yet found a language for. Three weeks of Theo Vance's absence, which was somehow louder than his presence had been.

She cut harder than she needed to. A cane dropped, and she didn't pick it up.

* * *

By midmorning her shoulders had settled into the ache that meant the work was working, and Dev drifted into her row with two thermoses and the particular carefulness he wore when he was about to say something she wouldn't like.

"Break," he said, and handed her the cup before she could argue.

They sat on the overturned crate at the row's end, shoulder to shoulder, steam rising between them. Below, the cove held its winter color, and one of the resident gulls stood on a tank lid like a small judgmental auditor.

"Your hands are shaking," Dev observed.

"It's cold."

"It's forty degrees." He sipped. "You've pruned in worse."

Lior looked at her own hands. They were steady enough holding the cup; it was only when they were empty that they trembled, some residue of everything they'd done and failed to do. She wrapped them around the warmth and said nothing.

Dev waited. He had gotten better at waiting in the years since their parents died—the vineyard had taught him that, or grief had, or both. Finally he said, quietly, "You want to tell me what happened out there? During the storm? Because Mara left looking like she'd been flayed, and Theo sends you postcards now, which is—" he made a gesture with his cup, "—a thing people do in novels, not real life. And you won't talk about any of it."

"Nothing happened."

"Lior."

"*Nothing happened,*" she repeated, and then, because the lie sat so badly in her chest, "Everything happened. All of it, all at once, in one house with no electricity. That's what happened."

He nodded slowly, absorbing this. "And now?"

"Now I prune vines." She drank. The coffee was terrible—Dev had never learned proportions—but it was hot, and heat was its own honesty. "Now I count turtles and prune vines and try not to think about the fact that my entire field knows my name only as a cautionary tale."

"That's not true."

"It's *mostly* true."

"It's not true at all, actually." Dev turned to face her, and she saw that beneath the cap and the farm-worn patience there was something sharper today, something he'd been carrying longer than a morning. "You know what I did last week? The co-op meeting in town. Hollis from the north ridge stands up and says, *Halloran's sister, the scientist—she's been running those shoreline counts for three years, hasn't missed a season.* People know, Lior. Not the scandal version. The version where you're out on that beach at five in the morning in February because nobody else will be."

Lior stared at him. Something moved behind her ribs, unwelcome and warm.

"That's not science," she managed. "That's inventory."

"That's *data*," Dev said. "Collected rigorously, by someone who trained for a decade. You think the university would turn its nose up at eight years of longitudinal nesting data on a coastline nobody monitors?" He shook his head. "You keep talking about your career like it's a room you got locked out of. But you're standing in a lab every single day. You just haven't noticed because it doesn't have your name on the door."

The words landed somewhere deep, and Lior looked away, down the rows, at the disciplined bareness of the vines. Each one reduced to its essential structure—one trunk, two arms, a handful of chosen buds. Everything else cut away so that what remained could put everything it had into fruit.

She thought about the notebooks in the mirror room upstairs, the ones she'd reread until two in the morning some nights, the margins full of her own twenty-six-year-old handwriting, ambitious and terrified in equal measure. She thought about how she had signed the attribution agreement without reading past the first page—not because she hadn't understood it, but because understanding it would have required a fight, and fighting required believing she deserved the thing fought for.

"I miss it," she said. The admission came out smaller than she intended, almost lost under the wind coming off the water. "Not the conferences. God, not the conferences. Not the grant dinners or the department politics or having a title. I miss—" She stopped. Started again. "I miss the moment when the data starts arguing with you. When you've got a hypothesis you love and the numbers just look at you and say no, and you have to sit there and be wrong on purpose, in public, because that's the job. I miss caring more about being right than being comfortable. I miss being *good* at something that mattered."

Dev was very still beside her.

"I don't miss the status," she said. "I never wanted the status. I wanted the work. And I let them convince me those were the same thing, and when it all burned down I told myself I'd lost the status, because that was easier than admitting I'd lost the thing I actually loved." She laughed, short and joyless. "Three years of hiding in a turtle census, telling myself I'd retired. I didn't retire. I *grieved*. There's a difference, and I'm only figuring it out now, in a vineyard, holding bad coffee."

Her brother set down his cup and pulled off one glove so he could press his bare hand briefly against her shoulder—a gesture their father used to make, wordless, heavy, brief.

"Took you long enough," he said, and his voice cracked slightly on the last word, and neither of them acknowledged it.

* * *

They worked through the early afternoon, and the light did that thin winter thing where it brightened without warming, laying long blue shadows from the vines across the frost-stiff grass. Lior lost herself in the rhythm again, but differently now—the confession had loosened something, and the cutting felt less like avoidance and more like tending. She found herself reading each vine the way her father would have wanted: where the wood was honest, where it was spent, what deserved another year.

It was nearly three when Dev stopped mid-row, shears hanging open in his hand, and didn't start again.

Lior knew before he spoke. She'd grown up watching their mother read Dev's silences the way sailors read cloud banks.

"What," she said.

He didn't answer right away. He walked to the end of the row and back, which was what he did instead of pacing, and when he finally faced her his jaw was set in the shape of a man who had rehearsed this too many times.

"The bank called Tuesday."

Lior's stomach dropped through the frozen ground. "How much?"

"Not a call about money we owe. A call about money other people want to give us." He said it flatly, the way you'd report a death. "Meridian Agricultural Partners. They've been circling coastal properties for two years—they bought the Aldous place up the peninsula in the fall. They made a formal offer on Halloran Vineyards. A good one." His mouth twisted around the word. "*Good.* Enough to clear every debt, enough for me to walk away whole, enough that Dad's insurance would finally be done doing the job it was never meant to do."

The wind came down the rows and Lior felt it go through her coat, through her sweater, straight to the skin.

"And if we don't sell?"

Dev looked at her, and she watched him decide, visibly, to stop protecting her. It was a kind of respect, and it hurt worse than kindness would have.

"Then we need harvest revenue to double," he said. "This year. Not eventually, not with a few good seasons—this coming harvest, or we refinance on terms that eat us alive within eighteen months anyway. The numbers don't lie, Lior. I've run them every way they can be run. Our yield per acre is decent, our quality is decent, but decent doesn't service this debt. We're a boutique operation with boutique prices and boutique volume, and the world has decided boutique means *disposable*." He kicked softly at a fallen cane. "Double the revenue. One season. Or Meridian flattens the old blocks and puts in whatever monoculture pencils out, and the last thing Dad built becomes a line item in somebody's portfolio."

Lior stood among the vines with her shears going cold in her hand and felt the two halves of her life slam together like doors.

One front: her name. The stolen proposal, the dormant paper, Mara's bargain sitting unanswered in her desk drawer, the journalist's email drafted and deleted four times, Theo's unsigned postcards accumulating in a shoebox like evidence of a crime or a courtship—she couldn't tell which. Justice, in every form she could imagine it, cost something she wasn't sure she could pay.

Second front: the vines. Her father's vines. Double the harvest or lose the ground itself—and the ground was the only place she'd been able to stand for three years.

"You're telling me this now," she said, "in the middle of pruning season."

"I'm telling you now because pruning season is *when you decide*," Dev said. "Every cut you make today is a bet on next fall. You choose which buds get to become fruit. You choose what the vine spends its strength on." He gestured at the whole bare hillside, the thousands of small decisions already made and thousands still waiting. "I've been making these choices alone for six years because you were busy surviving, and I don't regret that, and I'd do it again. But I can't carry this one alone anymore. If we're going to try for double—if we're insane enough to try—I need all of us deciding it. Together. Today."

Lior looked down the row. The vines stood in their winter discipline, stripped to intention, every one of them a question about the future written in wood. *What do you keep? What do you let go? What are you willing to bleed for?*

Her father had believed the vine told you what it wanted. She was beginning to suspect that was only half true. The other half was that you had to be brave enough to answer.

"We'd need a second crush line," she heard herself say, and her voice sounded strange—awake, engaged, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic instead of avoiding it. "And a plan for direct sales, we can't just hope volume doubles on wholesale margins. And the late-harvest block—Dad always said it could carry a reserve label if we ever had the nerve." She was walking as she talked now, moving down the row, touching canes, counting. "And I'd need the shoreline data. All eight years. Written up properly."

Dev blinked. "The turtle data?"

"Coastal ecosystem health correlates with watershed management practices, and our watershed is this valley." She was already three steps ahead of herself, the old engine turning over after three years of rust, coughing, catching. "If we're asking restaurants and distributors to pay premium prices, we give them a story that's true: a vineyard that keeps a living coastline. Peer-reviewed, even. It's not vanity, Dev, it's *marketing that happens to be science*, which is the only kind I trust."

Her brother stared at her, and then a slow grin broke across his cold-flushed face—the first unguarded expression she'd seen on him in weeks.

"There she is," he said softly.

"Don't." But she was smiling too, helplessly, the cold air sharp in her lungs.

They worked until the light failed, and the pile of cut canes grew at the row ends like kindling for a fire they hadn't lit yet, and Lior felt—for the first time in three years—that she was cutting toward something instead of away.

It was nearly dark when she climbed the porch steps and found the mail still lying on the hall table where Dev had left it that morning. Bills, a seed catalog, and one postcard.

No return address. No signature. Just her name in careful block letters, and on the reverse, in that handwriting she knew too well, a single line:

*"Attribution is not a courtesy extended after the fact; it is the architecture of the work itself."*

Page nine of her proposal. Her own words, stolen and returned, one sentence at a time, like a man paying off a debt in installments he knew would never be accepted at face value.

Lior stood in the dark hallway with the card in her hand, the vineyard's future and her own pressing against her from both sides, and understood that the thaw was coming whether she was ready or not—and that this time, everything would be decided in the open.

She went inside, and she did not throw the postcard away.
