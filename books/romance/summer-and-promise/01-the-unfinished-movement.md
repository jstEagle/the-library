## 1. The Unfinished Movement

There is a particular silence that lives inside a cello when nobody is playing it. I have spent eleven years learning to inhabit it.

People imagine that a musician's silence is empty. It isn't. It is the most crowded room in the world. Every phrase I never finished, every note I abandoned mid-flight on a Tuesday evening in March, eleven years ago, in front of nine hundred people — they all live in there, pressed against the walls of the instrument like pressed flowers, perfectly preserved and perfectly dead.

I teach now. Three days a week at a private academy where the students are polite and the parents are punctual and nobody has ever once asked me to perform. This is by design. I teach the mechanics of the instrument — bow hold, posture, the geometry of the left hand — and I am very good at it, because mechanics are the one part of music that can be trusted. Mechanics don't betray you. Mechanics don't walk off a stage in the middle of the Elgar Concerto's first movement and leave an orchestra floundering like a body without a spine.

My hands, for the record, are fine. That is the cruelest joke of it. I could pick up the cello tomorrow and play the Elgar beautifully, and everyone would say, *see, there was nothing wrong with him after all,* and they would be right, and they would also be entirely wrong.

What was wrong with me was not in my hands.

Here is what I have come to believe, in the long apprenticeship of my own cowardice: the heart is a muscle. This is not a metaphor; it is cardiology. And like any muscle, it responds to training. You can train it to lift more or you can train it to lift less. For eleven years I have trained mine to lift less. I have put it on a regimen of small weights — a pleasant student, a good meal, a Sunday crossword completed in pen — and I have carefully, methodically, taught it not to strain. No big feelings. No big asks. No music that might crack me open in public.

It works. I want to be clear about that. It works the way anesthesia works. You are still there, underneath, but you have arranged not to be present for the procedure.

* * *

On the first of June, a letter arrived that did not fit my regimen.

I recognized the handwriting on the envelope before I recognized the name, which is to say I recognized it in my body before my mind consented to the information. That slanted, impatient script, the *W* in Whitmore like a bird mid-takeoff. Theodore Ambrose Whitmore had been dead for six weeks, and his handwriting had no business being alive on my kitchen table, but there it was, unmistakable as a voice through a wall.

*Mr. Wendell Eastwick,* it began, in the solicitor's typewritten text beneath the handwritten address. *You are requested to attend the reading of the will of the late Theodore Ambrose Whitmore, on the fourteenth of June, at eleven o'clock.*

I made tea. This is what I do instead of feeling things at speed; I make tea, and by the time the water boils I have usually negotiated myself down to something manageable. I put the kettle on and stood at the counter and read the letter four more times, and by the fourth reading I had located the sentence that mattered, the one I had skimmed past in my panic:

*The deceased has left a specific bequest for you, to be delivered in person at the reading. He was insistent.*

Specific bequest. Delivered in person. Insistent.

Teddy Whitmore had been my teacher for nine years, from the age of fourteen, and he had a genius for insistency. He was the sort of man who could make you believe a thing was your own idea by the sheer force of having suggested it eleven times in a row while looking out the window. He taught me that the cello is held between the knees like a secret, and that technique is only the price of admission, and that the music itself — the actual thing, the reason anyone bothers — is a conversation you agree to have in public with parts of yourself you'd rather keep private.

I walked off his student's stage eleven years ago, and I never called him, and he never called me, and we let the silence stand between us like a fence both of us pretended was a river.

Now he was dead, and he had left me something he was *insistent* about delivering in person, and I stood in my kitchen with the kettle screaming and did not move to take it off the heat until the window had fogged completely.

* * *

I did not sleep much in the fortnight that followed, which is not accurate, since I slept exactly as much as always — six hours, punctual as a metronome — but the sleep had a different texture. Thin. The sleep of a man lying on top of his own life rather than inside it.

I kept trying to imagine what the bequest might be. A bow, I decided first. He'd leave me his bow, the French one, the one he let me hold exactly once and then took back with a laugh and the words *not yet, Eastwick.* That would be Teddy all over — a gift that was also a sentence, an object that was also a rebuke. *Not yet.* Even from beyond, still grading.

Or the watch. Or the metronome, the ugly brass one that sat on his piano like a small judgmental god. Or — and here my imagination, which I keep on a short leash, slipped it anyway — a letter. A letter in that slanted hand, saying something I would have to read standing up, because I would not trust my knees.

I told myself it would be the bow. I am very good at telling myself things. It is, if I am honest, my principal artistic achievement.

The days passed the way they always pass: students, scales, the small weights. A girl named Priya whose vibrato was finally loosening, whose face when it happened was the closest thing to music I permit myself anymore. A boy whose mother wanted to know if cello would "look good on applications." The kettle. The crossword. The regimen.

And underneath it, patient as a held note, the date approaching. The fourteenth of June.

* * *

The solicitor's office was in a part of town where the buildings wear their age like medals. I arrived eleven minutes early, because arriving early is a form of control, and stood outside the door rehearsing nothing, which is my other principal achievement.

There were perhaps a dozen people in the room. Teddy had outlived most of his contemporaries and out-argued the rest, and the ones who remained sat in the solicitor's leather chairs looking like an orchestra between movements — attentive, a little stiff, waiting to see what the conductor would do. I recognized a violinist I'd once subbed beside, a woman who had studied with Teddy in the sixties and still played with her chin too high. Nobody recognized me. Eleven years is long enough to be forgotten in a city, and I had made myself easy to forget: no concerts, no recordings, no presence. A man who teaches children where to put their fingers.

The solicitor was a dry, precise man named Mr. Halloway, who read the will in a voice like someone reading a grocery list in a cathedral. Small bequests went around the room — the violinist got the metronome, and I felt a strange stab of envy for a piece of brass. The estate's modest funds were divided among three conservatories. And then Halloway removed his glasses, which I have since learned is what he does when the grocery list is over, and said:

"To Wendell Eastwick, the deceased has left the following, in his own words."

He took out an envelope.

Not a bow. Not a watch. An envelope, sealed, yellowed at the edges in a way that told me it had been sitting in a drawer for a very long time — years, not months. The handwriting on the front was Teddy's. The address, though. The address was not mine.

It was a name I had not seen written down in eleven years, and my body knew it before my mind did, the way it had known the handwriting on the summons. *Ida Marchetti.* And beneath the name, an address in the river district, in Teddy's slanted, takeoff-bird script.

"This is to be delivered to the addressee," Halloway said, "or — the deceased's instruction — to be opened by Mr. Eastwick should delivery prove impossible. He was, as I say, insistent."

I took the envelope and it weighed nothing and it weighed everything, and I said thank you in a voice that sounded like it belonged to a man standing one room over from his own life.

But the envelope was not the bequest. Halloway cleared his throat.

"There is one further matter. The envelope is sealed, but the deceased left a note regarding its provenance, which he asked be read to you privately." He paused, and the pause had a shape to it, the shape of a man who had read the note already and was deciding how much of it to hand me. "Perhaps in the other room."

The other room was smaller, and had a window looking out on a courtyard where a plane tree was doing its indifferent summer work. Halloway read from a card in Teddy's handwriting, and I will set it down here exactly, because I have had eleven years of practice at setting things down exactly, at a safe distance:

*"The enclosed was written by Mr. Eastwick eleven years ago and entrusted to the post. It did not reach its destination. I take full responsibility for that. Whether I also take responsibility for keeping it — that, I leave to Mr. Eastwick's conscience to determine. He will know what he wrote. He will know why it matters. And he will know, if he is honest, why I kept it."*

The plane tree went on being indifferent. Somewhere in the building a telephone rang twice and stopped.

"Mr. Eastwick?" Halloway said. "Are you quite well?"

I was quite well. I was extremely well. I was a man whose heart, after eleven years of careful training, had just picked up a weight it was in no condition to lift, and I said, in a voice I barely recognized as trained at all:

"Who is the co-beneficiary?"

Halloway looked at me over his glasses. "I beg your pardon?"

"The address. Ida Marchetti. Was she — is she — in the will?"

He consulted a folder with the unhurried precision of a man paid by the hour to be unhurried. "Miss Marchetti is named, yes. In a separate provision. Concerning a property." He closed the folder. "I'm afraid I can say no more until the provision is activated. Those are the terms."

I walked out of the building with the envelope in my inside pocket, over the heart, which was beating in a way I had not authorized. Eleven years of regimen, of small weights, of tea instead of feeling — and one slanted line of Teddy's handwriting had undone the whole training program in under a minute.

The address was in the river district. I knew the district the way you know a place you've avoided: by its reputation, by the fact that it was never on my way to anywhere.

I stood on the pavement outside the solicitor's office and told myself I would take the envelope home, put it in a drawer, and decide calmly, over tea, over the crossword, over the small weights, what a man in my condition ought to do.

I told myself that.

And then I found, without quite deciding it, that my feet were already pointed toward the river, carrying me — the whole trained, tuned, silenced apparatus of me — toward the one address I had spent eleven years not knowing I was memorizing.
