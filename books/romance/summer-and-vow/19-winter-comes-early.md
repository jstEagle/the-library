## 19. Winter Comes Early

The first thing to go was the word *lighthouse*.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left — a man counting his coins on the way to ruin. It wasn't that I forgot the word. I knew there was a word. I could feel its shape in my mouth the way you can feel a stair that isn't there in the dark, that sickening lurch of reaching for something solid and finding air. The tower stood outside my window every morning, white as bone against the grey water, and I would look at it and think: *that is a — that is a —* and the sentence would simply end, like a wave dying on sand.

Paloma told me the word, gently, without making it strange. "Lighthouse," she said, touching my hand where it rested on the sill. "It's been there since before either of us was born."

"Lighthouse," I repeated, and it came back then, the whole architecture of it — the lamp room, the keeper's logbook, her grandfather's hands on the rail. But I had seen the shape of the hole now. And once you've seen a hole in yourself, you cannot stop looking for the others.

They came faster after that. October went out like a tide pulling everything with it.

I lost a Tuesday entirely. Not an hour, not an afternoon — a Tuesday, from waking to waking, gone as if someone had lifted it out of the book with a blade. I knew it because Paloma mentioned the bread we'd baked together, laughing about how I'd floured my own eyebrows, and I remembered nothing. Nothing. I stood in her kitchen staring at the flour tin and felt the floor tilt beneath me. She saw my face change and stopped talking mid-sentence, and we looked at each other across the warm room like two people on opposite banks of a river that hadn't existed yesterday.

"Jonah." Her voice very quiet. "What day did you think it was?"

"I don't know," I said, which was the truth, and the most frightening sentence I have ever spoken.

She crossed the room and held me, and I let her, and over her shoulder I watched the sea through the window doing what the sea does — erasing things, patiently, one grain at a time.

* * *

In November I called her by the wrong name.

Not another woman's name. That would almost have been kinder — a ghost of some old love, some girl from university with a name I could apologize to. No. I woke on her sofa in Gull Cottage, where I'd fallen asleep during an afternoon of rain, and she came in from the kitchen with two mugs of tea, her hair loose, backlit by the window, and I looked up at this stranger leaning over me with such kindness in her face and said:

"You're very good to me. I don't know your name."

I said it the way you'd say it to a nurse. Politely. Grateful. A gentleman to the last.

And I watched it land on her — I watched the exact moment the words reached her, because her face did something I will never forget as long as I am able to remember anything at all. It didn't crumple. That's what people expect grief to do, to fold like paper. Hers went still. Utterly still, the way the sea goes still before weather, and behind the stillness something enormous moved, deep down, like a whale turning under ice.

"It's Paloma," she said. Her voice was steady. Steadier than mine has ever been. "Paloma Brightwater. I live next door. I read you letters every evening."

"Letters," I said, because the word meant something, some warmth attached to it like a tag on luggage.

"Our letters," she said. "Yours and mine."

"I'm sorry," I said. "You're very kind. I'm sorry — I don't—"

"I know," she said. "It's all right. Drink your tea while it's hot."

And she sat beside me and drank her own tea and talked to me about the weather and the boats and the price of mackerel, ordinary things, anchoring things, and not once did her voice shake. When I finally slept again I heard her in the kitchen, washing up, and under the sound of the water I heard something else — very small, quickly mastered, the sound a person makes when they break quietly so as not to wake the house.

I lay in the dark and understood that I had done the cruelest thing I would ever do to another human being, and that I would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, because the illness does not ask permission. It takes and takes and hands you the bill afterward.

That was the night I decided to send her away.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I did, alone, in the small hours, with the rain ticking against the glass. Every day I stayed was a day she spent loving a man who was leaving in installments. Every evening she read our letters aloud, she was building a house on ground that was being sold from under her. She was forty-one years old. She had given up her publisher, her tour, the career that had finally arrived after ten years of work — she had set it all down like a heavy bag to take my hand, and I could not even keep hold of her name.

Love, I had always believed, was staying. I had built my whole second life on that belief — staying at the letter-writing after my father died, staying through ten years of ink, staying when I came to Cornwall knowing what I was carrying inside my skull like a lit fuse. Staying had been my vow.

But there are two kinds of staying. There is the kind that holds, and there is the kind that drowns both people. And I could see, with the terrible clarity the disease sometimes grants you between losses — lucidity as a cruel gift, a flash of lightning showing you exactly where you stand — that my staying was becoming the second kind.

So I did what cowards do. I made it look like mercy.

* * *

I began with distance. Small things. I stopped meeting her at the gate in the mornings. I told her I needed more rest, that the doctor wanted me keeping regular hours, that solitude helped. Each lie was small enough to pass for true, and she — trusting me, loving me, reading my face the way she'd once read my handwriting — believed them, or pretended to, or filed them away with the other evidence she was too brave to look at directly.

Then I sharpened it. One grey morning she came over with eggs from the farm shop and found me cold and formal at my own table, a stranger in my own manner.

"I've been thinking," I said. "About winter."

"What about it?"

"My sister has a place near Bath. Quiet. Good care nearby." I kept my eyes on the table. "I think I should go before the roads get bad."

Silence. I made myself look up. She was standing very straight, the egg box held against her chest like a shield, and her eyes were dry and bright and fixed on me with an attention that made my skin prickle.

"When did you decide this?" she asked.

"A while ago."

"A while ago." She set the eggs down on the counter, carefully, precisely, the way she did everything — as though gentleness were a discipline rather than a temperament. "Jonah. Look at me."

I looked at her.

"Is this what you want? Or is this what you think I should want?"

And here is where I should tell you I lied well. I had rehearsed it — God knows I'd rehearsed it, pacing this cottage at three in the morning, building the case the way I'd once built letters, clause by clause. *You deserve a life. You gave up too much. I won't watch you bury me twice.* I had the whole speech ready, polished, persuasive.

Instead what came out was: "I called you a stranger yesterday. In your own kitchen. You were carrying tea and I asked who you were."

"I know," she said. "I was there."

"And I'll do worse. It gets worse, Paloma. You know it gets worse. You've read the same leaflets I have." My voice cracked and I hated it, hated the weakness leaking through the seams of my resolve. "I will forget you while you're standing in front of me. I already have. And you'll sit there every evening reading our letters to a man who doesn't know what he's listening to, and you'll call it love, and maybe it is, but it's also a slow drowning and I refuse — I *refuse* — to be the sea that takes you."

The wind pressed at the windows. Somewhere outside a gull screamed once and quit.

"You don't get to decide that," she said.

"It's my life."

"And this is mine." She came around the table and stood over me, close enough that I could see the salt-grey threads in her hair, the fine lines at her eyes that I had memorized and would someday un-memorize, and her voice dropped low and level, the voice I imagined she used on editors and storms. "You think you're sparing me. Let me tell you what you're actually doing. You're taking the only choice I've made in ten years that I've never once regretted, and you're taking it away from me because it frightens *you*. Because watching me choose you feels like a debt you can't pay. Well, Jonah Vane, it isn't a debt. It was never a debt. I am not here to settle accounts."

"The publisher—" I started.

"Don't." One word, sharp as a gaff hook. "Don't you dare put my book between us. I sent that manuscript because it was true, not because it cost me you. Those are not the same sacrifice and you know it."

I looked away first. Ten years ago I had written to a grieving stranger because I couldn't bear the silence where her grandfather's voice had been; I had known even then that she was braver than me, that courage lived in her like the light lived in the tower — automatic, relentless, needing no one to tend it. Nothing had changed. I was still the man hiding behind half-truths. She was still the woman walking straight into the wind.

"Bath," she said softly. "Your sister in Bath." A pause. "There is no sister in Bath, is there. You told me once you hadn't spoken to her since the funeral."

The trap closed so gently I barely felt it until it had shut. I stared at the table. "No," I admitted. "There's no sister."

"No," she agreed. "There's just you, trying to disappear politely, and me, refusing to let you." She knelt then, so her face was level with mine, and took my hands — my traitor hands, the ones that trembled now when I was tired, the ones that had written all those letters — and held them still against her knees. "Listen to me. I have thought about this every single day since I found that letter in the drawer. Every day. I know exactly what's coming. I have done the arithmetic you've done, and mine comes out differently, because mine includes the evenings. Yours only counts the losses."

"The losses are coming regardless."

"Yes. So is the light." She smiled, and it broke my heart clean through, because it wasn't a sad smile at all. It was the smile of a woman who has decided something. "Every evening, Jonah. You fall asleep inside our story. Do you know what that means to me? It means that whatever the illness takes, it has to take it fresh every single night, and every single night I give it back. You don't remember? Fine. I'll remember. I'll remember for both of us, I'll remember out loud, I'll make remembering a *thing we do*, like lighting a lamp. You taught me that. Your father taught me that, through you. Love isn't a memory. Memory is just one of its rooms."

I tried to speak and couldn't. The tears came instead, hot and humiliating, and she gathered me in the way she gathered everything — steadily, without drama, as if holding me were simply the day's next task and she was glad of the work.

"I hate this," I whispered into her hair. "I hate what it does to you."

"I know," she said. "Stay anyway."

* * *

He stayed, of course. He stayed because she refused to let him go, and because some stubborn keel-deep part of him — the part that had written those later letters, steadier and funnier and more afraid — knew that leaving her would be the one forgetting he chose himself, and he could not bear to add his own cowardice to the pile the disease was already building.

But winter did not wait for their truce. It came down off the Atlantic in the last week of November, three days of screaming southwesterly that rattled the cottage panes and threw spray clear over the lantern room, and on the third night Jonah Vane walked out of Gull Cottage into the teeth of it wearing only a jumper, and did not come home.

Paloma found the door open at midnight. His coat still on its hook. His shoes by the fire.

And out there, past the harbour wall, the dark heaving water, and somewhere in it — or perhaps not in it, perhaps wandering the cliff path, perhaps miles gone already, following some map only his failing mind could read — the man she loved, who had promised to stay, was disappearing into the storm.

She pulled on her boots and her oilskins and went out after him, into the wind, calling his name into the dark.
