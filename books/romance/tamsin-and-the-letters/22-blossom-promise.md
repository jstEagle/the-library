## 22. Blossom Promise

### Ulyana

The first blossoms opened on a Tuesday, though Ulyana only learned that later, because Tamsin had been up before dawn and had come back to the kitchen with her sleeves full of petals and her voice carefully, deliberately casual.

"Row three's starting," she said, setting down a basket of windfall branches she'd pruned to open the canopy. "The Merton Russets. You'll want to see it before the bees get organized."

He had seen it. He had seen it every morning for a week, actually—the slow unfurling along the rows like the orchard remembering itself, white and faintly pink, the whole hillside going soft at the edges. But he said, "Show me anyway," because he had learned, over one long winter and one longer decade, that Tamsin Vale did not ask to be shown things twice, and that the asking was its own kind of gift.

They walked the rows together in the blue-gold light of early April, their boots dark with dew, and Tamsin narrated the orchard the way she always did—not like an owner, he'd realized months ago, but like a historian. *This tree your grandmother planted. This one came back from the storm of '09, look at the scar. This one I grafted myself the year I thought you were never coming back, and I'm not telling you which year that was.*

She told him that last part with a crooked smile, and he caught her hand, and she let him, and neither of them said anything, because they had spent the winter learning that not everything needed to be said out loud to be true.

The town had begun arriving by nine.

It had been Tamsin's idea, or possibly Mrs. Okonkwo's idea that Tamsin had failed to argue with, which amounted to the same thing. *Blossom Day*, the hand-painted sign at the gate read, the letters wobbling with the particular enthusiasm of the Harvest Festival committee, who had apparently decided that one festival a year was not enough for a town that had spent a decade learning how to gather. *All Welcome. Bring a Chair. Bring a Neighbor. Bring the Good Scissors, Delia, We Know You Have Them.*

By ten there were forty people on the hillside. By eleven there were eighty, and someone had set up a folding table with cider that was technically last year's pressing and technically still excellent, and children were running the rows with paper nets, trying to catch petals the way they'd once caught falling leaves in October. Ulyana stood at the edge of it with a cup of cider he wasn't drinking and watched Tamsin move through the crowd—laughing with Delia, crouching to admire a toddler's mud-stained knees, pointing out the grafts to a teenager who'd asked an actual intelligent question about rootstock—and felt the thing he still hadn't gotten used to, the thing that arrived in his chest every time and still startled him:

*This is mine. I am allowed to be here.*

He had spent so many years believing belonging was something you forfeited. That there were doors, once closed, that stayed closed. That a man who ran could not come back to the same ground and be received by it. And here was the proof against all of it, blooming on a hillside in Ashgrove Hollow, in the form of an old woman pressing a plate of lemon bars into his hands and saying, "You've put on weight, good, you were all elbows when you were a boy," as if the intervening decade had been an inconvenient weather pattern and nothing more.

"Ulyana." Mrs. Ashcombe, from the bakery, small and sharp-eyed under a sun hat. "You'll do the lantern count again in autumn? Tamsin says you're good at counting."

"Tamsin says a lot of things."

"Tamsin says you counted turtle nests for a living. Which is either very boring or very romantic, and I've decided it's romantic, so don't correct me." She patted his arm and moved off, and Ulyana stood there holding his lemon bars and his cider and his uncorrected romantic reputation, and laughed—actually laughed, out loud, alone in a crowd, which was not something the Ulyana of two years ago could have done.

He found Tamsin an hour later, at the far end of row three, where the crowd thinned and the Merton Russets stood in full white riot. She was alone, which was rare enough to be suspicious, and she was looking up into the branches with an expression he'd learned to read over the winter: the one that meant she was talking to her aunt. Not out loud. Just—talking.

He didn't interrupt. He leaned against the trunk beside her and waited, and eventually she said, "She'd have hated this crowd. She'd have loved it too. Both at once."

"Sounds about right."

"She'd have liked you better now than you were at seventeen."

"I like me better now than I was at seventeen."

Tamsin turned her head, and the light through the blossoms came down green-white on her face, and she said, "That's the first honest thing you've said all spring," and kissed him, briefly, with the whole orchard in bloom around them and the town's laughter drifting up the hill, and then pulled back and said, briskly, before the moment could get any ideas, "Right. I promised Delia we'd judge the grafting demonstration, and if we don't, she'll do it herself, and last year she gave first prize to a stick."

"A stick?"

"A stick with potential. Her words." She was already walking. He followed. He would follow her down these rows for the rest of his life, he thought, if she'd let him, and the thought no longer terrified him. It just sat there, warm and enormous, like a stone in the sun.

But there was one thing he had to do first. And he'd been waiting for today to do it.

* * *

### Tamsin

She noticed he was gone an hour before she noticed he was gone.

That was the strange arithmetic of it—she'd been standing three feet from him at the grafting demonstration, then Delia had commandeered him to hold a ladder, then the cider table had needed reinforcements, and the afternoon had folded itself around the absence of him before her mind caught up to the fact. She found herself scanning the crowd with an unease that was old, older than this spring, older than the reconciliation—a muscle memory from the years when scanning a crowd for Ulyana Coldharbour had been an exercise in futility.

He wasn't at the cider table. He wasn't at the gate. He wasn't—

The orchard house.

She knew it the way you know weather coming. She set down her cup, told Delia she'd be ten minutes, ignored Delia's entirely transparent smile, and walked up through the rows to the old house where they'd lived all winter—where the storm damage from two autumns ago had finally, fully been repaired, new clapboard silvering gently into the old, the wall on the north side patched and painted and pretending it had never held anything but insulation.

Except it had held the tin. And the tin had held a decade of his unsent letters, every one of them cut off mid-sentence, and she had read them all now, every fragment, and he knew she'd read them, and they had never once spoken about it directly, because some conversations you have entirely in glances and in the way a person makes your tea in the morning.

He was crouched at the north wall when she came around the corner, a trowel in one hand and a folded paper in the other, and he looked up at her with an expression she'd never seen on him before—not guilt, not deflection, none of the old architecture of escape. He looked, she thought, like a man about to do something he had decided to do, and there was a whole world of difference between that and everything he'd ever been before.

"You're supposed to be judging grafts," he said.

"Delia's judging grafts. Delia's judging everything. I got demoted." She crossed the grass. "What are you doing?"

He stood. Brushed the dirt from his knee with the trowel-hand, which accomplished nothing. "You found the tin," he said. "Two autumns ago. In this wall."

"Yes."

"You never asked me about them. The letters."

"You never offered."

"No." He looked down at the folded paper. "I couldn't. Every one of those letters, I got so far and then I stopped, because finishing them meant deciding something, and I wasn't brave enough to decide anything. Not about you. Not about my father. Not about anything. I was a boy who could count ten thousand turtle nests but couldn't finish one sentence." He held out the paper. "So I finished one."

She didn't take it. "Read it to me," she said, because she wanted his voice on it, wanted it in his voice the way she'd wanted it in his voice for twenty years, and because she had earned the right to be a little greedy about this.

He unfolded the paper. The blossoms of the nearest tree had begun to drop, a few petals settling on his shoulders like the orchard itself was leaning in, and he read:

"*Dear Tamsin. I've started this letter more times than there are trees in the orchard, and every time I stopped because the next part was a question, and questions can be refused. But I'm not seventeen anymore, and you've taught me—God, you've taught me—that staying is a thing you do with your hands, every day, not a thing you feel once. So here is the question, finished, out loud, in the ground where the last ones hid: build a life here with me. Not a bargain, not a co-ownership, not a harvest season. A life. The orchard, the town, the winters, all of it. I'm not asking you to trust me. I'm asking to spend as many years as it takes earning what I already have and don't deserve. I love you. I left because I loved you more than I trusted myself, and I'm staying because you trusted me more than I deserved, and I want to grow old counting blossoms instead of regrets. Say yes, or say nothing, and I'll still be here tomorrow, and the day after, and every day after that, for as long as you'll have me. —Ulyana.*"

The orchard was very quiet. Somewhere down the hill the festival murmured on, a fiddle tuning up, a child shrieking with joy over nothing. A petal came loose and landed on the paper in his hands, and he didn't brush it away.

"You wrote that," Tamsin said, and her voice was not entirely steady, "before you asked me."

"I wrote it last night. I've been writing versions of it since I was seventeen." He knelt, worked loose the board they'd never quite nailed back flush—the one flaw in the repair, the seam they'd both pretended not to notice all winter—and slid the letter into the wall, into the dark where the tin had been, and pressed the board home. "The tin's empty. I checked. Whatever you kept, you keep."

"I kept them," she said. "All of them. Even the half-sentences."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're you." He stood up, and there was dirt on his knee and a petal on his shoulder and his eyes were wet and he wasn't hiding it, and Tamsin thought: *there he is. The boy from the wall. The man who stayed.* "You keep things. You kept the orchard alive for a decade on the chance I'd come back. You kept a place for me at every table in this town. You kept my letters in a tin in a wall like a promise you were holding in trust." He took her hands. "I'm not asking you to stop keeping things. I'm asking you to keep me."

Tamsin Vale had spent ten years learning not to hope, and one winter learning to hope again, and she found that the hoping had not made her soft. It had made her precise. She looked at this man who had left her twice and chosen her three times—once in the storm, once under the lanterns, once every ordinary morning all winter when he made the tea and split the wood and learned the names of her aunt's trees—and she gave him the answer the way she gave all her answers now: plainly, with her whole self.

"Yes," she said. "Obviously yes. You enormous idiot. I've been waiting since I was twelve years old for you to finish a sentence."

He laughed, and she kissed him, and above them the Merton Russets went on blooming as if nothing in the world had ever been in question—as if the orchard had known all along what the two of them had taken twenty years to figure out: that nothing planted in good faith is ever truly lost. That roots hold. That what you tend, tends you back.

* * *

They walked down to the festival together, hand in hand, and Delia took one look at them and rang the bell on the cider table, which was how the whole town came to know within the space of four minutes, and Mrs. Ashcombe cried, and Mrs. Okonkwo said she'd known since the storm, obviously, and someone's grandmother pressed a lemon bar into Tamsin's hand and said, "About time, and you'll want a June wedding, before the cherries," and Tamsin opened her mouth to say they hadn't discussed—

And found that they had, somehow, in the way of people who have spent a winter learning each other's silences. Because Ulyana said, calmly, into the gathering cheer, "June works," and squeezed her hand, and that was that.

Later, when the light went long and gold and the crowd thinned to stragglers and the fiddle gave way to someone's phone playing something old, Ulyana walked her back up through the rows one last time. At the north wall of the orchard house he stopped, and put his palm flat against the new board, over the place where his finished letter now lay in the dark beside the ghost of a tin.

"What are you doing?" she asked.

"Checking," he said. "Making sure it stays buried."

"And if it doesn't? If someone finds it in fifty years?"

He considered the wall, the blossoms, the woman beside him with petals in her hair and cider on her breath and twenty years of patience finally, finally spent.

"Then I hope," he said, "they learn what I did. That you can run from everything except the thing you're made of. And that home isn't the place you left." He turned to her, and the last of the sun came through the blossoms and lit them both, and the orchard, in full and generous flower, went on quietly keeping its oldest promise. "It's the place that keeps you."

Above them, a branch let go of its petals all at once, a small white snowfall, and Tamsin laughed and Ulyana laughed, and the letter in the wall held its finished sentence in the dark—*for as long as you'll have me*—which was, as it turned out, forever, and had been from the very beginning.
