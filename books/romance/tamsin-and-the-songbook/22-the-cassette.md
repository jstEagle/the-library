## 22. The Cassette

The storm had passed two days ago and left the valley rinsed clean, the light coming down the hillside like something poured. Tamsin stood in the doorway of Flour & Salt with a roll of packing paper under her arm and watched Dominic wrap wine glasses in newsprint with the tenderness of a man dressing wounds.

"You don't have to help," he said, not looking up. "I know you've got the tasting room inventory."

"The tasting room inventory can wait." She came in and let the door swing shut behind her, the little bell giving its tired jangle. "You've got thirty days and four hundred pieces of stemware. I've got a father who naps through the afternoons and an aunt who thinks she's running the vineyard now anyway." She pulled on the work gloves he kept by the sink—too big for her, flour-ghosted at the fingertips—and picked up the first crate. "Besides, you fed half this town last night. Consider it interest."

He made a sound that might have been thanks if sounds could be embarrassed, and they worked.

The café was quieter than she'd ever seen it. After the benefit—after the pastry case emptied in ninety minutes and old Marta Reyes bought six tarts she openly admitted she'd freeze and never eat, just to put money in the jar—the place had felt like a held breath. Now the breath was going out of it. Crates labeled GLASSWARE and CHINA and BOOKS stacked along the wall where the second table used to be. The chalkboard menu wiped blank except for one line Dominic hadn't been able to bring himself to erase: *Sourdough — ask.*

It hurt to look at. So she didn't. She wrapped glasses instead, and let the rhythm of the work settle her: paper, glass, fold, nestle. Paper, glass, fold. Dominic worked across the counter from her, their hands occasionally meeting over the same crate, each contact brief and charged and immediately pretended away.

They had gotten good at pretending away, these last weeks. Since the cellar. Since the kiss that tasted like rain and cabernet and the end of every careful thing between them—and since the morning after, when she'd said *Marcus* out loud like setting down a stone, and watched something close behind his eyes.

She still hadn't told him about the letters. That was her own pretense, her own stone she carried. She'd narrowed The Songbook to this valley, maybe this town; she'd baited half the high street with casual mentions of harvest weather and bakery hours and watched for the flicker of recognition. And once—only once—she'd caught it, on Dominic's face, there and gone so fast she'd told herself she imagined it. Because wanting him and suspecting him couldn't live in the same chest at the same time. She'd chosen, for now, to want him.

The morning light moved across the floor. They filled crate after crate.

It was the back shelf that undid everything.

* * *

"Careful with that one," Dominic said, when she reached for the box wedged behind the flour bins, up where the shelf met the wall. "That's—old stock. Fragile."

"What is it?"

"Nothing. Junk, really. I'll deal with it."

Which was, of course, precisely the wrong thing to say to a woman who had spent three weeks training herself to notice what people did instead of what they said. He crossed the kitchen too quickly. His ears had gone pink. And Tamsin Vale, who had grown up reading her father's face across boardroom tables and barrel tastings, who had learned from Marcus that men hid things best when they were busiest, smiled pleasantly and said, "Fine," and waited until he went to answer the phone in the office.

Then she opened the box.

It wasn't much. A tin of recipe cards gone soft at the edges. A child's drawing of a lopsided croissant, signed *Dominic age 7* in pencil. A photograph of a young woman with dark hair and a tired smile holding a boy on her hip outside a bakery whose sign read FAIRWEATHER'S. And beneath all of it, wrapped in a dish towel like something sacred or something dangerous—

A cassette tape.

She knew it before she turned it over. She knew it the way you know your own name shouted across a field. The label was handwritten, the ink faded to brown, but the letters were still legible, still hers, still *theirs*: **TAMSIN & DOM — THE SONGBOOK**. Side A. Her own seventeen-year-old handwriting, the *S* looping extravagantly because she'd been showing off for a boy who laughed at her loops.

The world went very quiet, the way it does before hail.

Her hands were shaking. She set the cassette down on the counter with exaggerated care, as though it might detonate, and stared at it. It couldn't be. It was a coincidence—a common brand, a common era, thousands of mixtapes called the songbook, surely, somewhere in the world. But even as she thought it, her eyes went to the handwriting again. The way the crossbar of the *T* hooked left. The ink. The specific blue-black ink of the pen she'd stolen from her father's study the summer she was seventeen, the summer of the crush press and the power cut and the boy from the bakery who'd kissed her under the string lights and then, when her family's world tilted, simply—wasn't there anymore.

Ten years of letters sat in a shoebox in her cottage forty feet away. Ten years of a voice that knew her better than anyone alive, signed only *The Songbook*. And here was the songbook itself, physical, real, dust-furred, hidden behind flour bins in the back of a café owned by a man who had kissed her in a cellar five nights ago and never mentioned it.

The office phone was still going. She could hear the low murmur of his voice, the particular patience he used with suppliers.

She shouldn't. She knew she shouldn't. There was a version of her—the version raised by Marcus Hale, the version that believed love was a contract and privacy was a clause—that would put the cassette back, close the box, wait for him to volunteer the truth. She'd already decided once, in her kitchen over a shared recipe, to give him that chance. To wait.

But her hands weren't listening. Her hands were turning the cassette over. Side B. And on Side B, where there should have been nothing, where the label should have been blank—

There was a second line of writing. Not hers. His. Small, cramped, pressed hard enough to emboss:

**FOR TAMSIN — READ FIRST.**

Read first. Read what? Her thumb found the edge of the cassette shell, the seam, and it took her a moment to understand why the case was thicker than it should be, why it sat wrong in her palm, why the weight of it was off by grams. Then she saw it: the case had been opened and resealed, the screws' heads scuffed bright. And inside, folded small and tight against the spools, protected by plastic the way a heart is protected by ribs—

Paper.

* * *

Later, she would not remember deciding to open it. She would remember the sound the cassette shell made coming apart, a small dry click like a knuckle cracking, and the smell of the paper when it unfolded—dust and ink and, faintly, impossibly, vanilla. She would remember standing in the empty café with the light going long and gold across the floorboards, and unfolding a letter written in a hand she had loved for ten years without knowing its owner, and reading the first line:

*Tamsin —*

*If you're reading this, then either I finally found my courage, or you found it for me. Given our history, I'm betting on you.*

*I have started this letter a hundred times. In ten years I have written you a hundred and twenty-two letters, and every single one of them was a rehearsal for this one, and every single one of them failed, because the truth doesn't fit through a postbox. The truth is this:*

*I am The Songbook. I have always been The Songbook. I was The Songbook the night you left, standing in the road watching your taillights, too poor and too proud and too much of a coward to run after the car. I was The Songbook the day your father's lawyer told me, in words I still hear, that a baker's son with debts and no prospects had nothing to offer a Vale, and that if I cared for you at all I'd let you go. I believed him. God help me, I believed him, because believing him hurt less than finding out I was right.*

*So I wrote instead. One letter the first year, because the silence was drowning me. Then two. Then more. I told myself it was closure. I told myself it was charity, almost—a kindness to us both. It was none of those things. It was the only honest room I had, and I furnished it with you.*

*Every detail you ever wondered about—the bakery hours, the frost dates, the tart you described once and I learned to bake until it came out right—I gave you those things because they were the truest things I had. You fell in love with a ghost assembled out of my best hours. I'm sorry. I'm sorry most of all that the man standing in front of you—the one with flour on his hands and a foreclosure notice in the drawer and a decade of cowardice in his ledger—is smaller than the ghost, and clumsier, and afraid of nearly everything except you.*

*I am not asking for anything. That's the point of this letter, the thing I could never manage in the others: I'm asking for nothing. If you hate me, keep hating me and I'll stop. If you love the ghost, I understand; so do I. But if there is any part of you that could forgive a man who loved you the only way he knew how—from hiding, badly, for ten years—then tear this up and tell me, and I will spend whatever's left of my life being worth the postage.*

*Yours, entirely, and at last signing it—*

*Dominic Fairweather*

The letter slipped from her fingers.

It didn't fall so much as drift, settling onto the counter beside the cassette, and Tamsin stood very still in the middle of the empty café while the whole architecture of her last three weeks quietly collapsed and rebuilt itself around a new center of gravity.

Him. It had been him. All of it—the letters that arrived every few months like weather, the ones she'd read on London park benches with her coat buttoned to her chin, the ones she'd answered in a PO box she'd told Marcus was for bills. The man who knew about the frost dates. The man who baked the almond tart she'd described exactly once, in ink, to a stranger with a pseudonym. The man who had kissed her in the cellar with rain still in his hair, who had held her hand over a crate of merlot grapes, who had looked at her across a fence with an awkwardness she'd found endearing—it had all been him, ten years deep, standing forty feet from her cottage the entire time.

And underneath the wonder, rising through it like gas through water, something darker.

He'd known. He'd known since the day she moved in. He'd known when he offered her bread for fruit, when he worked her harvest, when he kissed her—he had known exactly who she was to him, and she had known nothing. Every tender, halting, honest-seeming moment between them had been conducted on uneven ground, him carrying the whole map and her navigating blind. Ten years of devotion, yes. Ten years of love, yes. And also ten years of watching her grieve a ghost he could have released her from with a signature. Ten years of letting her build a life—letting her get engaged, letting her plan a wedding to another man—while he posted anonymous devotion into her mailbox like a man feeding a fire he refused to warm his hands at.

*Cowardice dressed as devotion.* Where had she heard that phrase? Nowhere. She'd invented it just now, and it fit like a key.

The office door opened. "Sorry, that was the—" Dominic stopped.

He saw the open box. He saw the cassette, gutted, its shell in two pieces. He saw the letter on the counter, unfolded, and he went the color of uncooked dough.

"Tamsin." Her name came out cracked down the middle. "That's—that's nothing, it's—"

"Don't." The word came out low and level and surprised her with its own calm. "Please don't. Not after all of it. Don't lie to me in this room."

He stood there with the phone still in his hand, and slowly, like a man disarming himself, he set it down on the shelf. "How much did you read?"

"All of it."

The silence stretched between them, taut as wire. Outside, a tractor changed gears on the hill road; somewhere a dog barked twice and gave up. Dominic Fairweather looked at the letter—his letter, the one he'd sealed away years ago because he couldn't send it and couldn't burn it—and Tamsin watched ten years arrive on his face all at once: relief, terror, shame, and beneath them all, naked and undefended, hope.

"When?" she asked. Her voice shook now; she let it. "When were you going to give me this? Or was it going to stay behind your flour bins forever? Was I supposed to leave at the end of harvest, marry Marcus, and get letter one hundred and twenty-three next spring, signed by a ghost, while you waved at me over the fence?"

"Tamsin—"

"One hundred and twenty-two letters." She picked up the page and held it up between them like evidence, which is what it was. "I have read every word you ever wrote me a hundred times. I have *slept* with them, Dominic. When my father had his stroke, the only thing that got me through the night was a letter from a man who wouldn't sign his name. And it was you. It was you the whole time, and you watched me pack my bags for London, and you said nothing."

"I was going to tell you." His voice was barely there. "After the harvest. When the café was—I wanted to be someone who had something to offer before I—"

"You had ten *years*."

"I know."

"You had a cellar full of barrels and a night full of rain and you kissed me, and you knew, and you said nothing."

"I know." He stepped toward her and she stepped back, and the distance that opened between them was the width of the whole decade. "I know what I am," he said. "I've known longer than you have. I wrote that letter five years ago and I couldn't send it, because sending it meant you'd look at me the way you're looking at me now, and I couldn't—"

"Bear it?" She laughed, and it came out ugly. "I have borne it for ten years without knowing what I was bearing. I loved a ghost, Dominic. I defended a ghost to myself. I compared every living man to a piece of paper and found them all wanting, and the paper was *you*, sitting forty feet away, baking my childhood back at me in tarts, and you never—you never once—"

"I was afraid."

"So am I! Every day! Fear isn't a vault, it doesn't make it noble, it just means you made *me* carry all the risk alone!" The tears came then, furious, unwelcome, and she swiped at them with the back of a gloved hand. "Do you know what the worst part is? The worst part is that it's the most beautiful thing anyone has ever done for me. Ten years. Nobody has ever—no one will ever—" Her voice broke entirely. "And it's also the cruelest. Both at once. How am I supposed to hold both of those in the same hands?"

He had no answer. She hadn't expected one. He stood among his packed crates with his hands hanging empty at his sides, the man from the letters and the man from the cellar and the man from the fence all present at once in his face, and none of them equal to the moment.

She set the letter down. Gently. That was the terrible part—how gently she did it, how her hands refused to slam anything, how even her fury handled him like something breakable.

"I need you to not follow me," she said.

"Tamsin, please—"

"No." She pulled off the gloves and laid them on the counter beside the gutted cassette, beside the photograph of the dark-haired woman and the boy, and walked to the door. Her hand was on the latch when she stopped, because there was one more thing, one question that had been forming since the first line of the letter, and it came out of her quiet and cold:

"The gag clause. In Marcus's buyout offer. The one about 'correspondence.'" She turned. "Did he know? Does he have proof?"

Something moved behind Dominic's eyes—fear of a different shape entirely—and that was answer enough.

Tamsin pushed through the bell-door into the gold afternoon light, leaving the songbook open on the counter behind her, and started walking—not home, not yet, nowhere, just walking, fast, down the middle of the street with the whole valley watching and ten years of letters burning in her chest like a lit fuse.
