## 38. Harvest Festival, Again

### Dominic

The tarts came out of the oven at four in the morning, and by six he had made them again, because the first batch—perfect as they were—hadn't been perfect enough for what tonight had to carry.

Rosa found him on his third tray, sleeves rolled to the elbow, flour to the wrists, the kitchen hot enough to fog the windows despite the October chill pressing at the glass. She didn't say anything at first. She just leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed and watched him pipe the last rosettes of crème pâtissière onto a grape-and-almond tart, her expression doing that thing it did when she was deciding whether to scold him or feed him.

"You've made two hundred," she said finally.

"Two hundred and forty."

"For a town of nine hundred people."

"Some of them will want seconds." He slid the tray onto the cooling rack and stood there a moment, hands braced on the counter, letting the heat of the ovens work its way out of his shoulders. "Some of them might not come at all."

Rosa crossed the kitchen and took his chin in her hand, the way she'd done when he was twenty-two and burning his first croissants, turning his face toward hers. Her eyes were sharp and brown and entirely unfooled.

"They'll come," she said.

"You don't know that."

"I know this town better than you do, and you've lived here your whole life. You think people whisper for a week and then stay home? No. People whisper, and then they get curious, and then they show up to see for themselves." She released his chin and flicked flour off his shoulder with two brisk swats. "And then they taste your tarts, and they remember that the man who wrote those letters is the same man who fed half of them through the worst winter any of us can remember, free, no questions asked, and they feel like fools. And feeling like fools makes people generous."

"That's cynical."

"That's Tuesday." She was already moving toward the door, pausing with her hand on the frame. "Wear the good shirt. Not the one with the burn hole. Tonight you're not hiding behind an apron."

He looked down at the apron in question, stained and soft and older than his café, and after she'd gone he hung it on its hook with something like ceremony. Like retiring a flag.

* * *

By five-thirty the square had transformed.

Dominic stood at the Flour & Salt stall—he'd insisted on building it himself, hauling planks from the cellar, sanding them until midnight—and watched the valley assemble itself into something it hadn't been in years. The vineyard's long tables ran the length of the square, draped in white cloth that Tamsin's aunt had dug out of some attic, ironed within an inch of their lives. Barrels stood on end as standing tables, their heads chalked with the evening's pours. The old bandstand, which he'd honestly believed was structural decoration, had been swept and strung with lights, and a trio of musicians was tuning fiddles with the cheerful incompetence of men who hadn't played together since the last festival, four years ago, before the bad harvests and the worse bookkeeping and everything else that had hollowed the town out from the inside.

String lights everywhere. Someone—Mrs. Okonkwo from the post office, he suspected—had run them along every awning on the square, so that the whole street wore a net of small gold lights, doubled in the dark windows, reflected in the wet cobbles from the afternoon's rain.

He arranged his tarts with hands that wouldn't quite steady. Grape and almond, the ones Tamsin had described in a letter eleven years ago—the ones he'd reverse-engineered from three sentences about her grandmother's kitchen and spent a decade refining. Fig and honey. The plum galette Rosa had taught him, back when he'd had nothing but a borrowed oven and a mother who'd already bought her ticket out. And at the center of the display, on a raised stand he'd built for exactly this purpose: the harvest tarts, the ones he'd baked through the night on day twenty-nine, betting the café's last dignity on a single public act. They'd sold out that day. They were here again now, and this time nobody was calling him a stalker under their breath. This time, mostly, they weren't saying anything at all—which was its own kind of weather, and he kept checking the sky of it, waiting for it to turn.

The first hour was the hardest.

People came. That was the thing. They came in twos and threes, drawn by the lights and the music and the simple gravitational fact of a festival, and they approached his stall the way you approach a dog of uncertain temperament—sideways, smiling, one hand extended. A farmer named Aldous whose land bordered the vineyard bought two fig tarts and ate one standing right there, deliberately, holding Dominic's eyes while he chewed, and said, "Better than the ones at the church fête," which from Aldous was practically a love letter. The schoolteacher brought her whole class of leftover children, still in wellies, and let each of them point at what they wanted while Dominic knelt on the cobblestones and answered questions about why the cream was yellow (eggs, real ones) and whether he was the man from the newspaper (yes) and whether he was sorry (more than they could know) and whether he'd write *them* letters sometime (only if they promised to write back).

That last one got a laugh from the parents hovering behind, and the laugh broke something loose—not all at once, but the way ice breaks, in long cracks that spread faster than you expect.

By seven the queue at his stall was ten deep, and Mrs. Okonkwo herself was at the front of it, buying a dozen assorted and announcing, loudly enough for the square to hear, "I delivered those letters, you know. Ten years. Never once a return address, never once a name. I used to stand at my sorting frame and wonder about you, Dominic Fairweather. I decided whoever you were, you were either a poet or a coward." She counted out coins with great precision. "I'm pleased to report you were both."

"It's not poetry," he managed.

"No," she agreed, tucking the box under her arm. "It's better. It's specific." And she walked off into the lights, leaving him blinking.

Tamsin appeared at eight, and the square rearranged itself around her the way water rearranges around a keel. She was wearing the green dress—the one from the night harvest, dried and pressed and mended at the hem where a barrel hoop had caught it—and she carried two glasses of the new vintage, and she moved through the crowd accepting apologies without ever once making anyone feel forgiven too cheaply. He watched her stop at the bandstand and say something to the fiddlers that made all three laugh. He watched her take old Mr. Ferreira's arm and walk him slowly past the barrels, translating the vintage notes into plain speech, her hand light on his sleeve. He watched her father, upright in a wheeled chair for the first time in public since the stroke, wrapped in a blanket against the chill, surveying the square with the expression of a man taking inventory of everything he'd nearly lost and finding most of it accounted for.

Then she arrived at his stall, set both glasses down on the plank counter, and looked at the tarts—at two hundred and forty tarts reduced to perhaps thirty survivors—and then at him.

"We're going to run out," she said.

"I know."

"Good." She pushed one of the glasses toward him. The wine caught the string lights and held them, small gold points suspended in dark red. "First pour of the cuvée. Before anyone else. That's not sentiment, that's protocol—I checked the charter."

He lifted the glass. Their fingers didn't touch, and somehow that was more deliberate than if they had. Across the rim, over the top of the wine, she watched him with an expression he was only just learning to read without flinching—open, unhurried, a woman who had read a decade of a man's soul in ink and was now patiently teaching herself to read the living version, typos and all.

"To the correspondent," she said quietly.

"To the editor," he said, and they drank.

The wine tasted like the night harvest tasted: rain and exhaustion and the strange bright joy of work shared at two in the morning. It tasted like forgiveness wasn't a single act but a practice, like showing up, like showing up again tomorrow. Around them the square filled and filled, the music found its feet, someone started dancing badly and bravely in front of the bandstand, and Dominic Fairweather—who had spent ten years believing that love was something you did alone, in ink, in secret, where it couldn't be taken from you—stood in the middle of nine hundred people eating his pastries and understood that he had never once been alone, he had simply refused the evidence.

### Tamsin

Her father danced.

Not well—the stroke had taken the smoothness from his left side, and the waltz they attempted in front of the bandstand was less a dance than a negotiated settlement between a man and gravity—but he danced, one hand gripping hers, the other braced on her shoulder, his face turned up toward the string lights with an expression she hadn't seen on it since her mother was alive. Halfway through the second song he stopped trying to steer and just laughed, breathless, and said, "Your grandmother is watching from somewhere and taking notes, and we are both failing," and Tamsin laughed too, and held him up, and thought: *this*. This was what the vineyard was for. Not the margins Marcus had penciled, not the valuation reports, not the tidy arithmetic of debt and sale. This. A bad waltz under borrowed lights, in a town that had shown up.

Because that was the thing she kept circling back to, all evening, the thing that sat in her chest like a warm stone: they had shown up.

She had stood at the tasting table for two hours pouring the cuvée, and she had watched the town perform its apology in the only language it knew. Nobody had made a speech. Nobody had cornered her to formally disavow the whispers that had run through the square like frost for a week after Marcus's revelation—the *stalker*, the *letters*, the *decade of it*, served up with just enough truth to curdle. Instead: Aldous, who had reportedly said some unforgivable things outside the pub, buying three of Dominic's tarts and carrying them around the square like a peace offering looking for its recipient. The schoolteacher, who had pulled her own children from the festival committee in protest, now volunteering at the raffle with the fierce cheer of a woman making amends. Mrs. Okonkwo, who had sorted ten years of anonymous mail and said nothing to anyone, delivering a public verdict from the post office's unofficial bench of judgment: *poet and coward both*, and then, devastatingly, *it's better than poetry, it's specific*.

They apologized by eating. By dancing. By queueing forty deep at a pastry stall run by a man they'd side-eyed in the lane, and by paying full price, and by coming back for seconds. By asking him, over and over, in a hundred small ways, *tell us about the tarts, tell us about the bread, tell us who you are*—the question they should have asked before the whispers, offered now as penance.

At half past nine she escaped the tasting table and went looking for him, and found him not at the stall but at the edge of the square, standing alone beneath the big chestnut tree with an empty crate under one arm, watching the dancers with an expression she recognized from Letter Forty-Seven—the one where he'd written, *I have always been best at loving things from slightly outside them; it's the window seat I was assigned early and never traded up.*

She took the crate out of his hand and set it on the ground.

"Window seat's closed," she said.

"Is it."

"The whole building's closed. Demolished. I've filed the paperwork." She held out her hand. "Dance with me. Badly. My father set the standard and you cannot possibly do worse."

He looked at her hand. She watched him decide—watched the old reflex move through him, the one that calculated exits, the one that had written a hundred letters instead of knocking on a hundred doors—and then watched it lose. He took her hand, and it was warm and flour-roughened and slightly trembling, and she loved it more than she could reasonably account for.

The band had moved on to something slow and slightly out of tune, a song she didn't know until the fiddler reached the chorus and she did know it, of course she knew it, because Mrs. Okonkwo had requested it—she saw the old woman across the square, watching them over the top of her cider with unconcealed satisfaction—and the opening line of it was the line from the mixtape, the line from the label of the cassette that had named a decade of letters, and Tamsin felt her throat go tight.

"He requested it," Dominic said into her hair. His voice was rough. "She planned this."

"Everyone planned this. The entire town has been conspiring all week. I'm fairly sure the vicar has a spreadsheet."

He laughed—a real one, startled out of him—and pulled her closer, and they danced badly under the chestnut tree, out of step, her cheek against his shoulder, the string lights smearing gold above them, and around them the town went on apologizing in the best way it knew: by being present, by being loud, by refusing, tonight, to let either of them sit at any window.

Later—much later, when the fiddlers had packed up and the lights were being unstrung by volunteers moving with the unhurried contentment of people whose evening had gone right—she found him stacking crates beside the dead bandstand, and she took his hand and led him away from the square, up the lane, to the fence between the cottage and the café. Their fence. The fence of the confrontation, the fence of the rupture, the fence she had once gripped hard enough to splinter.

She stopped in front of it and put both palms flat on the top rail.

"Do you know what I keep thinking?" she said. "Marcus had photographs. Proof. A whole file. And the story he told with it almost worked, because it was *clean*—a stalker, a decade, a warning sign, very tidy, very legible. But the town met the actual version tonight. The messy one. The man who fed them through the winter and hid in his letters and showed up to pick grapes with blistered hands. And the messy version won." She turned to look at him. The last of the string lights glowed down the lane behind her, and his face was half-lit, watchful, waiting the way he always waited—as though good news were a trick with a cost hidden in the fine print. "So here's mine. No envelope. No postmark. No signature at the bottom, because you're standing right here."

"Tamsin—"

"Letter one hundred and twenty-four," she said, "is due soon. And I've decided how I want it delivered."

His breath went out of him slowly. Under the half-light she watched him understand—not the words yet, but the shape of what was coming, the way you understand weather—and his hand came up and covered hers on the fence rail, flour and all, holding on.

"Tomorrow," he said. "I'll need until tomorrow."

She smiled, and squeezed his fingers, and left him standing at the fence with the whole night ahead of him and nothing left to hide behind.
