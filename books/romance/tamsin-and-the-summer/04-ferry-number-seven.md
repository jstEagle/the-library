## 4. Ferry Number Seven

The diesel engine had a voice, and after eleven years Tamsin Vey knew all of its moods. This morning it was grumbling—low, congested, a rattle in the lower register that meant she'd be under the boat with a wrench before the first crossing, elbow-deep in the bilge while the tourists on the pier took photographs of her like she was part of the scenery. Which, in a way, she was. The *Merrilee Ann* was the last independent ferry running out of Marrow Bay, and Tamsin was the last Vey to run her, and the county had filed papers in March that said both of those things were temporary.

She stood at the wheel now with one hand resting on the throttle and let the engine's complaint wash over her. Six-forty in the morning. Fog still sitting on the water like a held breath. On the mainland side of the channel, the Halloran Group's survey stakes glowed orange in the early light, planted along the shoreline in a neat, proprietary row, each one topped with a little fluorescent flag that fluttered like it was celebrating something.

Her father had taught her to read an engine by sound before he taught her to read words off a page. *A boat tells you the truth,* he used to say, wiping his hands on a rag that was more grease than cloth. *People lie. Engines don't.* He'd been dead nine years, and his tools still hung on the pegboard in the engine room in the exact order he'd left them, and every morning when she came down the ladder and saw that crescent wrench hanging third from the left, she said good morning to him out loud, quietly, the way you'd greet someone sleeping.

The dock was another matter. The dock didn't speak. It just stood there in the gray water on pilings her grandfather had driven in 1954, holding up the fuel shed where her mother's handwriting still filled the logbook columns—*diesel, 40 gal., R. Pruitt, paid*—in a slanted, patient script that looked like it had all the time in the world. The Halloran Group wanted the dock for a marina expansion. They had maps with colored zones on them, and lawyers with soft voices, and a filing that called the Veys' operation "a nonconforming waterfront use." Four words to describe sixty years of her family's hands.

Tamsin had read the eminent-domain notice so many times she could recite it. She kept a copy folded in the breast pocket of her work jacket, not because she needed it but because touching the fold of it in the morning was a kind of prayer—the angry kind, the kind where you dare God to try something.

The foghorn sounded up the channel, distant and mournful, and she checked her watch. First run in twenty minutes. She killed the engine's idle, listened to it settle into silence, and went down to open the ticket window.

* * *

The terminal building had been built in the seventies and never apologized for it: wood-paneled walls gone amber with age, a linoleum floor worn pale in the shape of foot traffic, a bulletin board crowded with notices for lost dogs, oyster suppers, and the island's ongoing war with the mainland over everything. Tamsin ran the ticket window herself most mornings because hiring help meant payroll, and payroll meant money, and money was currently being eaten alive by legal fees. Her lawyer, Deb Okafor, billed in six-minute increments, and Tamsin had taken to doing mental math during their calls—*that sentence cost me eighteen dollars*—until she'd stopped talking altogether except to answer questions.

She opened the window at seven sharp. The morning crowd was the usual: three commuters who worked at the mainland clinic, a man hauling coolers of seed oysters, a pair of birders with telescopes in padded cases who would stand at the rail the whole crossing and point at nothing. She sold tickets, made change, stamped the day sheet. Her hands did the work while her mind ran through the morning's list: call Deb about the surveyor who'd come onto the dock unannounced Tuesday; get the quote back from the welder on the cleat plates; write the letter to the harbormaster that she'd been drafting in her head for four days and hadn't managed to put on paper without it sounding either desperate or unhinged.

The line thinned. The birders bought their tickets and argued softly about whether they'd seen a harrier or just wanted to have seen one. And then the terminal door opened again, letting in a slab of cold light and the smell of the mainland—exhaust and coffee—and Tamsin looked up out of habit, already reaching for a ticket, and stopped.

Ansel Sandoval stood in the doorway of the Marrow Bay ferry terminal.

For a moment nothing in Tamsin moved—not her hand, not her breath, not the thought forming behind her eyes. Eleven years is a long time, but it turns out it is not long enough. The body keeps its own ledger. Hers recorded, instantly and without permission: the way Ansel still stood slightly on the left foot, weight cocked, like someone perpetually about to step into a room or out of one. The hair, shorter than she remembered, cut close and practical. The coat—expensive, city-expensive, the kind of coat that had never been rained on honestly. And the face. The face was the same face, which felt like a personal insult. Time was supposed to do something. Time was supposed to take a side.

Tamsin had imagined this moment maybe a thousand times, and in none of those versions was she sitting behind a ticket window selling passage across the very water they'd failed to cross together. In some versions she turned away. In one memorable version she threw a cup of cold coffee with impressive accuracy. In most of them she said something devastating, something she'd spent years polishing in the dark like a stone in her pocket—a whole collection of devastating things, sorted by occasion.

What she actually did was this: she reached for the ticket book, tore off a ticket, and said, "Round trip's fourteen."

Her voice came out level. Flat as the harbor at slack tide. She was almost proud of it.

Ansel stepped forward. Up close there were new lines around their eyes, and something else—something watchful, a person who had learned to scan rooms for exits. They set a card on the counter instead of cash, and their fingers, when they moved, had the particular precision of someone whose hands were trained instruments. Of course. Tamsin had heard, the way news travels on islands—slowly, then all at once—that Ansel Sandoval planned weddings now. Big ones. Famous ones, if the mainland gossip that drifted over on the afternoon boats was to be believed. Someone had told her at the church supper two winters ago, watching her face carefully as they said it, and Tamsin had laughed and said, *good for them,* and then gone home and sat in her truck in the driveway for forty minutes with the engine off.

"Actually," Ansel said, and their voice was different too—smoother, sanded down, a voice trained for ballrooms, "I'll need the ferry for a wedding party. Late September. Maybe eighty guests, plus—" They stopped. Something crossed their face, quick as a fish turning under the surface. "You're running the service now."

"I'm running the service."

"The Vey line."

"There's no other line." Tamsin held out her hand for the card. "That's rather the point of the lawsuit."

She hadn't meant to say that last part aloud. Or she had, a little. Spite was a fuel she'd learned to run clean.

Ansel blinked. "Lawsuit?"

"Eminent domain." Tamsin swiped the card with more force than the machine required. "Halloran Group wants our dock for their marina. So if your wedding guests are planning to arrive by water, you might want to check whether there'll be a dock here in late September, or just a construction barge and an apology."

Something moved through Ansel's expression then—something Tamsin couldn't name and refused to try to name, because naming it would mean caring what it was. Ansel looked at the bulletin board, at the faded notice about the county hearing, at the orange survey flags visible through the window, and then back at her, and said, quietly, "I'm sorry. I didn't know."

"You wouldn't." Tamsin slid the ticket across the counter. "That'll be twenty-eight."

Ansel looked down at the ticket. "I thought round trip was fourteen."

"It is." Tamsin met their eyes, and let eleven years sit there between them, plain as a mooring post. "Yours is double. Island surcharge."

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then—God help her—something flickered at the corner of Ansel's mouth. Not quite a smile. A recognition, maybe, of the girl who used to charge them a sand dollar and a secret for rides on the skiff, who once made them swim to the mooring because they'd teased her about her sunburn. Some old animal memory of who they'd been to each other, before.

Ansel paid without argument. Tapped the card reader when it chirped. Took the ticket like it might be warm.

"Gate's at the end of the pier," Tamsin said. "Boarding in fifteen."

"Tamsin." Ansel said her name the way you'd set something fragile down on a table, careful, watching to see if it held. "I'm not here to—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she intended, and she watched it land, watched Ansel absorb it the way a hull absorbs a knock against a piling—one dull blow, no visible damage, everything transferred inward. She lowered her voice. The birders were pretending to study the tide chart ten feet away, and the terminal had that quality of small rooms, where every word carried. "Whatever you're here to say, I've had eleven years to imagine it, and I promise you my version is better written. Save it."

Ansel nodded slowly. Picked up their bag. And then, at the door, paused with one hand on the frame—an exit staged so perfectly it could only be instinct, the body remembering how to leave—and said, "It's good to see you, Tamsin."

She didn't answer. She watched them go down the pier, coat collar up against the fog, walking the same boards her father had laid, past the survey flags, toward the gate at the end where the *Merrilee Ann* waited with her engine ticking as she cooled, patient as a dog.

* * *

The crossing took twenty-two minutes. Tamsin ran it on autopilot—channel markers, tide rip off Gull Point, the long slow bend around the shoal where the water went from brown to green—and stood at the wheel with Ansel Sandoval somewhere behind her on the deck, and did not look back once. That was its own kind of labor. It took actual muscle, not looking. By the time they rounded the marker at the halfway point her shoulders ached from the effort of keeping her eyes forward.

The birders stood at the bow rail. The oyster man napped against the wheelhouse. And Ansel—she caught them once, only once, in the reflection in the brass compass housing, a small warped mirror of a figure standing alone at the stern, facing the wake, facing the mainland they'd just left, or facing something else entirely. Watching them in the reflection felt like spying and she made herself stop.

Eleven years. No note. No call. One August day the Sandovals' house was empty, the car gone from the drive, and Mrs. Pruitt said she'd seen the moving truck at dawn, and Tamsin had walked down to their house and stood in the empty yard until the mosquitoes drove her inside. Seventeen years old. She'd had a plan for that summer—she'd had it since June, written in her head, rehearsed in the dark the way you rehearse anything that matters too much to say casually. And then the house was empty and the plan was a stone in her chest for a decade, and everyone on the island had been kind to her in that awful, gentle way people are kind to the visibly wounded, and she had learned, eventually, to build a life around the hole instead of waiting for it to fill.

Mostly learned. She was honest enough with herself, alone on the water, to admit the *mostly.*

The dock came into view through the thinning fog, her dock, the family dock, with the fuel shed and the pegboard and her mother's handwriting, and beyond it the orange flags marching down the shoreline like an infection. She brought the *Merrilee Ann* in slow, feathering the throttle the way her father had shown her when she was twelve, and thought: *they will not have it. Whatever else happens, whatever this wedding is and whoever that person on my deck has become—they will not have this.*

Ansel came up to the wheelhouse door as they tied off. Stood there a moment, bag over shoulder, framed in the doorway with the fog burning off behind them into a pale, washed-out morning.

"Nice crossing," Ansel said.

"Fares go up in October," Tamsin said, and turned away to log the time in the day sheet, in her own handwriting, in the book her mother started, and did not watch them walk down the gangway onto the dock her great-grandfather built, carrying a ticket stub for twice what anyone should pay to cross open water.

Behind her, the engine ticked as it cooled. Down the pier, Ansel stopped once—just once, half-turned, as if the morning had a question in it—and then kept walking, up the ramp, toward town, toward whatever had finally, after eleven years, brought them back across the water.

Tamsin wrote *8:42, arrived* in the log, closed the book, and allowed herself exactly one long breath before the next boatload of the world came down the pier.
