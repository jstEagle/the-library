## 15. Halloran Makes an Offer

The invitation arrived the way all of Halloran Group's communications arrived: beautifully, and slightly too early.

Ansel found it slipped under the door of their room at the Wrenfield Guesthouse on a Tuesday morning—cream cardstock, letterpress, the kind of paper that cost more per sheet than most people spent on a week of groceries. *Mr. Marcus Vane requests the pleasure of your company for dinner. The Boathouse Room, Seven o'clock. Dress is whatever you like; the view does the work.*

Ansel held it by one corner, the way they'd been trained to hold anything that might be a trap or a gift, since those were so often the same object.

They knew what this was. They had known since the tasting dinner, when Vane had worked the room in his linen jacket with the ease of a man who had never once been told no by anyone whose opinion mattered to him. Ansel had watched him that night the way they watched all powerful men—at first with professional admiration, cataloguing the technique, and then with a slow, cold recognition, because Vane's technique was not so different from Ansel's own. Warmth deployed strategically. Attention paid exactly where it bought something. The handshake calibrated to feel like friendship.

The difference was that Ansel had spent eleven years doing it to weddings, and Vane did it to towns.

*You could say no,* Ansel told themselves, setting the card on the desk beside the ledger—the ledger, still wrapped in its cloth, still sitting there like a coal that hadn't decided whether to burn the house down. *You could just not go.*

But they went. Of course they went. That was the thing about muscle, Ansel thought as they dressed—dark trousers, the blue shirt Tamsin had once said made them look like weather coming in over the water, a compliment Ansel had carried around for eleven years like a stone in the shoe. Muscle didn't ask whether you wanted to lift. It simply lifted.

* * *

The Boathouse Room sat at the end of the new pier—the developer's pier, the one that had gone up in eight months where the Vey dock had stood for a hundred years, though technically the Vey dock still stood too, wounded and storm-battered, awaiting the county's verdict. The restaurant was all glass and pale wood, designed to look effortless the way everything Halloran built looked effortless, which was to say it had cost a fortune to make it look like it hadn't.

Vane was already seated at the best table, the one facing the water, and he rose when Ansel came in—not all the way, just enough, a man who understood that standing fully made you a supplicant.

"Ansel Sandoval." He said the name like he'd practiced it. He had. "Sit. I ordered you the scallops before you arrived—I hope that's not presumptuous. I asked around. You strike me as a scallops person."

"And what makes someone a scallops person?"

"Restraint," Vane said, smiling. "People who order scallops are telling the kitchen they trust precision over abundance."

Ansel sat. "And what are you?"

"Me?" Vane spread his hands. "I'm a steak person, obviously. I have the palate of a fourteen-year-old boy from Hartford and I've stopped apologizing for it." He laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and self-deprecating, and Ansel felt the old familiar pull of it—the pull of competence, of charm aimed directly at them, the feeling of being *seen* by someone who saw everyone this well. They had built a career on manufacturing that feeling for other people. It was disorienting to sit inside it.

The scallops arrived. They were perfect. Ansel ate them and hated how much they enjoyed them.

"I'll come to the point," Vane said, when the plates were cleared and the second bottle of wine—a Burgundy, of course, chosen to signal that he knew Ansel would know why it mattered—was open between them. "I want to hire you."

"You already hired me. Indirectly. Through June's family."

"That's a wedding." Vane waved a hand, dismissing an entire category of human celebration with the gesture. "Weddings end. I'm talking about a relationship. I've been watching you work, Ansel. The Priya-and-June event—it's going to be extraordinary. I've seen the plans. The tide-timed ceremony, the lanterns on the water, the way you've routed the guest flow so nobody ever sees the kitchen chaos. You don't plan events. You design experiences." He leaned forward. "I want that mind. Not for one Saturday in August. I want it permanently. A division. Halloran Celebrations. Your name on the door, equity in the division, a salary that will make you briefly furious at every year you spent working for less."

Ansel kept their face very still. This was the discipline, the oldest one: nothing shows, nothing costs. But underneath the stillness their heart was doing something embarrassing, something hopeful and greedy, because the numbers Vane named were real, and the vision was real, and some starved part of Ansel—the part that had eaten ramen through two failed startups before wedding planning found them, the part that remembered the lean years in Boston when a client's drunk uncle had called them "the help"—that part lit up like a harbor at night.

"Permanently," Ansel repeated.

"Marquee properties. Private islands. I'm buying three more before the market corrects. Every one of them will need someone who can make a place feel like—" Vane paused, searching, and found the word with visible satisfaction, "—like it belongs to you. Like it was always yours. That's your gift, I think. You make borrowed things feel inherited."

The words landed somewhere they shouldn't have. Ansel reached for the wine.

"There's one condition," Vane said.

And there it was. Ansel had been waiting for it since the scallops, since the letterpress card, since the moment Vane had risen halfway out of his chair. There was always a condition. Love had conditions; so did everything else. At least Vane was honest about it, which put him ahead of most of the people Ansel had ever worked for.

"The ledger," Vane said.

The word hung in the air between them, small and enormous.

"I don't know what you mean," Ansel said.

"Come now." Vane's voice stayed pleasant, but something under it shifted, the way water shifts over a dropped anchor. "My people tell me you've been in the county records office three times this month. My people tell me you requested the original parcel surveys for the east waterfront, the ones from 1890-something. My people tell me—" and here he smiled again, and the smile was different now, thinner, more honest, "—that you checked out a shipping ledger with a Halloran name in it. Well. A *Sandoval* name. Which used to be the same thing, back when your family ran boats out of this town instead of selling it."

Ansel set down the glass. Their hands were steady. Eleven years of practice, and here, finally, was the dividend.

"What do you want?"

"I want to know what it says. And then I want you to let it stay in the past, where it's been doing no harm to anybody for a hundred and thirty years." Vane refilled both glasses himself, unhurried. "Here's my read of the situation. That ledger probably shows the Vey family's dock predates every claim we've filed. Probably shows your own grandfather paying mooring fees to a Vey before the county even existed in its current form. And if it surfaces at the hearing next month, my lawyers will handle it—they always do—but it gets ugly, and ugly is expensive, and frankly, Ansel, I'd rather not be ugly to this town. I'm trying to be its benefactor. Can't you see? Everything I build here, I build for the islanders too. The jobs. The restaurants. The ferry contract alone—"

"The ferry contract you're suing her out of."

"A consolidation," Vane said smoothly. "Progress requires consolidation. But that's not the point. The point is you. You're standing at a fork, and one road has a beautiful house on it, and the other road has a woman who charges you double for a boat ticket and a courthouse full of people who will forget your name the week after the hearing." He leaned back, letting the view do the work, the dark water and the lights of the town strung along the shore like a necklace someone had laid down carelessly. "Take the house, Ansel. Take the career. Hand me the ledger—or better, tell me where it is and forget you ever saw it—and I'll make you the most celebrated wedding designer on the Eastern Seaboard within five years. You'll never chase a client again. You'll never count a season's bookings and wonder if it's the beginning of the end. Security, Ansel. Real security. I know you've never had it. I can smell it on people—the ones who learned young that nothing is safe. It's why you're good. It's also why you're tired."

Ansel sat very still and let him talk, because talking was what Vane did best, and while he talked he revealed himself, the way all salesmen reveal themselves, in the architecture of the pitch.

*I know you've never had it.* That was the knife hidden in the silk. Vane had done his research—not just the records office, but the shape of Ansel's life, the absence at the center of it. Somewhere in Boston or in the county archives or in the mouth of some gossipy islander, Vane had learned about the Sandovals who left Marrow Bay in a hurry one summer and never came back, and he had understood, with a predator's efficiency, that the child of that departure had grown into an adult who mistook control for peace and contracts for love.

He wasn't wrong. That was the humiliating part. He was describing Ansel's life with the accuracy of a portrait painted from behind.

"I'll need time," Ansel said.

Vane's eyebrows rose, genuinely surprised, and for a half-second Ansel saw the real man underneath—the one who was not accustomed to the word *no*, or even to the word *wait*. Then the surprise smoothed away and the warmth returned, banked and patient.

"Of course. Big decisions deserve deliberation. The hearing isn't until next month. I'll have my assistant send over the draft agreement tomorrow—you can look at the numbers, sleep on them, show them to whoever you show things to." He stood, extending his hand. "For what it's worth, Ansel, I hope you take it. I think you and I understand each other. We're both in the business of making people feel safe. You just do it for one day at a time. I do it forever."

Ansel shook the hand. It was dry and firm and perfectly calibrated, and walking out along the glass corridor above the black water, Ansel realized their palm was sweating and wiped it on their trousers like a teenager.

* * *

The walk back to the Wrenfield took twenty minutes along the shore road, and Ansel took it slowly, because the night was warm and because they did not want to arrive anywhere, least of all at their own thoughts.

The draft agreement was waiting when they got back, of course. Vane's assistant had emailed it within the hour. Ansel read it standing up, coat still on, the screen's light blue on their face. It was generous. That was the terrible thing—it was genuinely, insultingly generous, the kind of money that reorganized a life. Page four contained the relevant clause, written in lawyer's silk: *Consultant agrees that any materials, documents, or information relating to historical property claims shall remain confidential and shall not be disclosed to any third party, including but not limited to any party to ongoing litigation involving Halloran Group affiliates.*

*Including but not limited to.* Ansel read the phrase twice. Lawyers loved that construction. It meant Tamsin. It meant the hearing. It meant that if Ansel signed, and then handed Tamsin the ledger anyway, they wouldn't just lose the job—they'd be sued into the seabed, blacklisted across an industry that ran on reputation, finished at thirty-nine in the only profession they'd ever been good at.

And if they signed and kept quiet—if they did what the clause actually asked—then Tamsin would stand in that hearing room with her father's tools and her mother's fuel log and nothing else, and she would lose, and she would know, afterward, that Ansel had known. Had held the proof in their hands and chosen a salary.

Ansel closed the laptop and sat on the edge of the narrow guesthouse bed in the dark.

Here was the arithmetic they couldn't stop running. Eleven years ago, they had left Marrow Bay to protect something—their secret, their family's flight, the shame of a father who'd taken money he shouldn't have and a mother who'd packed the car before dawn rather than face the neighbors. Leaving had cost Ansel everything that mattered, and they had told themselves for a decade that it had been necessary, that safety was worth the price, that the heart was trainable and grief was temporary and a person could build a whole life out of flawless Saturdays for other people.

And now here was the same offer again, wearing better clothes. Leave, and be safe. Stay silent, and be secure. Sign here, and never have to find out whether you could have survived being honest.

The muscle wanted to sign. That was the truth Ansel sat with in the dark, and it was a shameful truth: some large and cowardly portion of them wanted to take the money, take the title, take the beautiful house on the fork in the road, and tell themselves—as they had told themselves for eleven years—that it was wisdom, that it was maturity, that a person couldn't set themselves on fire to keep someone else warm.

Except.

Except that last week, on the low-tide flats, Tamsin had asked the question that had cracked something open in Ansel's chest and never quite sealed again: *Why couldn't you just tell me you were scared?* And Ansel had stood there in the wet sand with no polished answer, none, for the first time in their adult life, and Tamsin had waited, and then hadn't waited, and had walked back toward the tideline alone, and Ansel had understood that the silence was the answer, and that the silence was the same silence they were being paid, right now, handsomely, to repeat.

The ledger sat on the desk under its cloth. Ansel didn't look at it. Looking at it felt like looking at the sun.

They thought about June instead—June at the rehearsal dinner, flinching under her family's expectations, asking later in a voice smaller than her usual voice: *Do you think a wedding can make people love you the right way?* And Ansel, hired to sell the dream, had heard themselves say: *No. It can't manufacture belonging. It can only witness it, if it exists.* Words that had inverted their entire creed, spoken before they'd understood they believed them.

If they signed Vane's contract, they would spend the rest of the summer building a beautiful wedding inside a lie, and they would be very well compensated, and they would be, at last and completely, the help.

Ansel lay back on the bed without undressing and stared at the ceiling, where the guesthouse's old fan turned in slow, creaking circles, the same fan that had turned there the summer they were seventeen, the summer of the letter they still hadn't answered properly, the summer that had never actually ended.

*Sleep on it,* Vane had said, magnanimous, certain.

Ansel slept badly. In the gray hour before dawn they got up, made coffee they didn't drink, and opened the laptop again. The cursor blinked in the reply field of Vane's email. Ansel typed: *Thank you for dinner. I need a few days.*

It was true. It was also a stall, and Ansel knew it was a stall, and knew what they were stalling toward, the way you know the direction of a current even with your eyes shut. There were only two endings to this. One of them had a beautiful house on it.

Outside, the fog was lifting off the bay, and somewhere down at the harbor, Ansel knew, Ferry Number Seven was warming its engine for the morning run, and Tamsin Vey was checking her lines in the dark, and neither of them knew yet that a piece of paper in a guesthouse room had become the most dangerous object on the island.

Ansel hit send, closed the laptop, and reached for the ledger after all.
