## 14. Meeting the Mother

The knock came at six-forty in the morning, which Ansel knew from experience was the hour reserved for two categories of visitor: emergency plumbers and mothers.

She opened the door of the guest room she'd been occupying for eleven days — eleven days past her original departure date, a fact she was carefully not thinking about — and found Mama Adeyemi standing in the hallway wearing a headwrap the color of ripe mangoes and an expression of serene, absolute purpose.

"You're awake," Mama said. It was not a question. It was a verdict.

"I was, as of about four seconds ago."

"Good. Wear something you can sweat in. We're going to the market." She turned and walked away, her slippers making soft, confident sounds against the floorboards, a woman who had never once in her life waited to see whether her instructions would be followed.

Ansel closed the door, leaned her forehead against it, and exhaled. In eleven days at the inn, she had survived two family dinners, one impromptu church service, a cousin's engagement party where she'd been asked to judge a jollof rice competition (she'd judged diplomatically; there had been no diplomatic option), and no fewer than six variations of the question "So how did you two meet?" — each answer a slightly different work of fiction, each one polished a little more than the last.

But she had not yet survived Mama Adeyemi alone. Tamsin had been there for the dinners, running interference with the skill of a woman who'd spent thirty years dodging maternal artillery. This morning, apparently, the interference had been deliberately scheduled out.

She dressed quickly — linen trousers, a shirt that would forgive the humidity — and found Mama waiting by the front door, holding two woven market bags and the keys to an ancient Toyota whose paint color could best be described as "regret."

"You didn't have to wait for me," Ansel said.

"I know." Mama handed her a bag. "But you would have taken too long deciding what to wear, and the fish man leaves at seven."

* * *

The drive into town took twenty minutes, during which Mama Adeyemi asked nothing and said very little, humming along to the radio in a way that Ansel recognized, professionally, as the calm at the center of an interrogation. Ansel had planned two hundred weddings. She knew what it looked like when someone was being maneuvered into position. She had done the maneuvering herself, gently, a hundred times — steering a nervous mother of the bride away from a seating chart catastrophe, guiding a groom's best man past his third cocktail. She knew the feeling of being the one in control of the room.

She was not in control of this car.

The market opened up before them like a riot — stalls under corrugated roofs and striped awnings, pyramids of tomatoes and peppers, the smell of frying plantain and diesel and fish and something sweet she couldn't name, a hundred conversations happening at a volume that suggested every one of them was urgent. Mama moved through it like a queen through her own coronation, greeting vendors by name, touching babies, sampling things she had no intention of buying, and Ansel trailed in her wake carrying the bags, realizing with dawning horror that she had been conscripted not as a guest but as a *porter*.

"You know," Mama said, selecting a pineapple with the scrutiny of a jeweler, "Tamsin was a strange child."

Ansel blinked. "Was she?"

"Stubborn. Quiet in a house where nobody was quiet. She would sit under the table at family gatherings — under it, Ansel — with a book, and refuse to come out until someone coaxed her with puff-puff." Mama handed the pineapple to the vendor, who bagged it without being asked. "Ngozi was the loud one. The one who performed for everyone." A small pause, no longer than a knife's edge. "You knew Ngozi, I think. When you were girls."

Ansel's stomach performed a small, controlled drop. "A little. We all ran around together in the summers, didn't we? Tamsin and me, and — " She let the sentence trail off with the artful vagueness of a woman who had genuinely forgotten, which required artfulness, because she had not forgotten anything.

"Mm," said Mama Adeyemi, and moved on to the peppers.

* * *

The thing about being a wedding planner, Ansel thought, as she carried a bag now containing pineapple, peppers, three kinds of greens, and a live catfish that kept giving her accusatory looks through its bag, was that you developed a very specific skill set. You could read a room in four seconds. You could defuse a drunken uncle with a joke. You could smile through a toast that was secretly an insult. And above all, you could tell when someone was building toward something — when the pleasant small talk was scaffolding around a structure that hadn't revealed itself yet.

Mama Adeyemi had been building for forty minutes.

They stopped at a fabric stall where Mama examined wax prints she clearly had no need of, and it was there, with her fingers resting on a bolt of indigo cloth, that she finally turned and looked at Ansel directly — a look that was warm, and kind, and had all the softness of a well-lit operating theater.

"My daughter tells me you two met again in April," Mama said. "At a wedding you planned. In Ibadan."

"That's right." The story, version seven, fully polished. "A mutual friend's wedding. We hadn't seen each other in years. It was — lucky."

"Lucky," Mama repeated, tasting the word. "And by June you were together. And by July you were here, staying in my guest room, eating my food, letting my sister-in-law's husband teach you to play ayo badly." She smiled. "That is very fast, Ansel. Even for young people. Even for love."

The market roared on around them. Somewhere a woman was arguing joyfully about the price of stockfish. The catfish shifted in its bag. Ansel felt the particular cold that professionals feel when the trap they've been watching being built turns out to have been built around *them*.

"People do move quickly," Ansel said, "when they know."

"Mm." Mama picked up the indigo cloth and held it against Ansel's shoulder, considering her the way she'd considered the pineapple. "Do you know what I did before I married their father? I was a schoolteacher. Thirty-one years. And in thirty-one years, I learned that you can always tell when a child has studied for a test, and you can always tell when a child has studied the *teacher*." She lowered the cloth. "You, my dear, have studied my daughter. You know her coffee order before she says it. You know she goes quiet when she's frightened and funny when she's cornered. You know she sleeps with the window open even in rain." A gentle tilt of the head. "These are not things you learn in two months. These are things you learn in years. Or things you never forgot."

The market noise seemed to recede, as if the whole sprawling chaos of it had leaned in to listen.

Ansel had prepared for this. Not this exact conversation, but this *species* of conversation — she'd been preparing versions of it since the day she agreed to stay past Sunday, and she had a portfolio of answers, each one plausible, each one rehearsed. She opened her mouth to deploy one.

And then she looked at Mama Adeyemi's face — patient, unthreatening, absolutely certain — and understood that every prepared answer had already been priced and found counterfeit.

"I knew her when we were children," Ansel said. It wasn't the prepared answer. It was smaller and truer. "We were close. Then we weren't. For a long time. And when I saw her again in April, it was like — " She stopped, because the prepared answer would have been *like no time had passed*, which was a lie, because all the time had passed, all ten years of it, sitting between them like the sealed room upstairs.

"Like the time had passed," Mama supplied quietly, "but mattered."

"Yes."

Mama nodded slowly, and for a moment she looked out over the market — at the women haggling, the children darting between stalls, the whole loud, surviving, ongoing business of it — and when she spoke again her voice had changed register, softer, the interrogation giving way to something more dangerous: sincerity.

"I am going to tell you something, and you will not interrupt me, because I am old and I have earned it." She turned back. "I have buried a mother, a husband, and a marriage. I have watched one daughter leave this family and another daughter let her go. I have watched Tamsin hold this inn like a woman holding a door shut against a flood. So I do not miss much, and I am not easily frightened." She stepped closer, and the mango-colored headwrap caught the light. "But I will tell you what frightens me. It is not that my daughter's relationship began strangely. Strangely is how most true things begin. It is that she looks at you the way she looked at that inn when the lawyers first came — like something she is afraid of losing, and willing to hold too tightly."

Ansel's throat went dry. "Mama — "

"I have not asked you what this is," Mama said, holding up one hand, rings flashing. "I have not asked, and I am not asking now. But I am telling you that I have watched you for eleven days, and I have watched her for thirty years, and whatever the two of you are building, one of you is building it out of guilt and one of you is building it out of hope, and I have not yet decided which is which." She picked up her own bag, lighter than Ansel's, and began walking. "You have until her birthday to help me decide."

* * *

They bought fish. They bought more peppers, because Mama declared the first ones "for show, not for eating." They bought a bag of gari and a jar of honey from a man who tried to charge them tourist prices until Mama looked at him and he remembered his own mother. Ansel carried everything. At one point the catfish's bag leaked slightly down her side and she did not react, because she was busy replaying *one of you is building it out of guilt* on a loop, and because she suspected that flinching in front of this woman was a data point that would be collected and used.

It was at the honey stall, waiting for change, that Mama said, conversationally, as if remarking on the weather:

"Ngozi is coming for the birthday. Did Tamsin tell you?"

The market tilted, very slightly. "I — no. She didn't mention it."

"Mm. She doesn't know yet. Her sister has not spoken to me directly in nine years, but last week a letter came, and in the letter, a yes." Mama took her change, counted it, and tucked it away with unhurried precision. "I have invited her to stay in the house. In the room next to yours, as it happens. Ngozi always liked that room."

Ansel thought of the sealed room upstairs, of the framed vow behind glass, of Tamsin's face whenever her sister's name surfaced and went carefully, deliberately flat.

"She said yes," Ansel managed. "After nine years."

"People say yes to birthdays," Mama said. "They come home for other reasons." She glanced at Ansel then, one last time, and there was something in it that was almost — almost — kindness, or warning, or both braided together. "You should ask my daughter what happened between the sisters, Ansel. Before Ngozi arrives and tells you herself. It is always better when the story comes from someone who loves you."

She turned and walked toward the car, slippers slapping the pavement, mango headwrap blazing, a woman entirely at peace with the wreckage she'd just made.

Ansel stood in the market with a leaking catfish, a bag of peppers, and the sudden, ringing understanding that the next chapter of this lie was going to arrive in the room next to hers — and that Mama Adeyemi hadn't just dismantled her in a fabric stall.

She'd handed her a countdown.
