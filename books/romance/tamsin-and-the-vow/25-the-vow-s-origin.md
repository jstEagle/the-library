## 25. The Vow's Origin

Tamsin didn't sleep.

She lay in her old bedroom at the inn — the one with the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a leaping fish that she'd named Gerald when she was nine — and stared at the dark while Ngozi's words turned over and over like stones in a tumbler, wearing down all their sharp edges until what remained was something smooth and terrible and simple.

*Grandma Ruthie wrote the vow after I left. Ask yourself why.*

She got up twice to go knock on Ngozi's door and got as far as the hallway both times before turning back, because what would she even say? *Excuse me, sister of mine, exiled these ten years, please explain my dead grandmother's legal documents at two in the morning*? No. Some conversations needed daylight. Some needed coffee. Some needed, apparently, six weeks of dread and a near-miss with a development consortium before they could finally be had.

At four in the morning she gave up on sleep entirely, wrapped herself in the quilt Mama had made her for college — the one with the crooked stars because Mama refused to use a pattern — and padded downstairs through the sleeping inn.

The kitchen was empty, which was unusual enough to feel like a gift. Usually by five Aunt Bisi was up frying something aggressive, and Cousin Dele slept on the parlor sofa like a Victorian ghost with opinions about thermostat settings. But now the house held its breath around her, and Tamsin put the kettle on and sat at the big table where every important Adeyemi conversation had ever happened, and tried to remember.

Not the vow itself. She knew the vow by heart; she'd had it read to her so many times it had worn a groove in her memory like a favorite song she'd come to hate. She meant she tried to remember the night it was made.

And found, to her horror, that she couldn't. Not really. Not past the fog of grief and hospital corridors and the particular exhaustion of being twenty-nine and losing the person who loved you most unconditionally. She remembered fragments: the antiseptic smell, the squeak of her shoes on linoleum, the way Grandma Ruthie's hands had felt — papery and strong — gripping hers. She remembered crying. She remembered promising things. What things? She had promised everything, that last week. People did that. They promised dying women anything, because it cost nothing and it seemed to help.

Had she actually listened?

The kettle clicked off. She poured the tea and didn't drink it, and somewhere between the first steam and the first gray light coming up over the lake, she made a decision that frightened her more than the consortium papers had.

She went upstairs, took the spare key from the hook in the linen closet — the one nobody used, the one Ansel had noticed within a day because Ansel noticed everything — and let herself into Grandma Ruthie's room.

* * *

The room smelled the way it always smelled: lavender, old paper, and something faintly medicinal that no amount of airing out had ever removed. Dust motes hung in the morning light like suspended weather. The bed was made with hospital corners Ruthie herself would have approved of. Her reading glasses sat folded on the nightstand beside a half-finished crossword, frozen mid-puzzle for ten years, forty-seven across forever blank.

Tamsin had been in here exactly three times since the funeral. Once with Mama, once alone and weeping too hard to see anything, and once to retrieve the framed vow from the wall above the desk when the lawyer required it. Each time she'd come for a specific thing and taken only that thing, the way you might visit a grave.

This time she came to listen.

She sat down in Ruthie's reading chair — the wingback by the window, faded green, still holding the impression of a woman long gone — and looked at the room properly for the first time in a decade.

It was full of paper. That was the thing she'd never let herself see. Not tidy paper, not archived paper, but *working* paper: notebooks stacked in drifts, letters bundled with rubber bands gone brittle, recipe cards in a shoebox labeled in Ruthie's slanted hand — *STOLEN RECIPES, DO NOT SHOW BISI*. There were photographs everywhere, pinned and propped and tucked into mirror frames, and Tamsin understood with a slow, cold drop in her stomach that she had spent ten years treating this room as a shrine when it was actually an office. A desk. A woman's working desk, abandoned mid-thought.

On the desk blotter, weighted under a smooth gray stone from the lakeshore, lay a notebook with a cracked spine. Tamsin recognized it instantly — Ruthie had bought them by the dozen from the stationery shop in town, cheap spiral-bound things, and filled them with everything: inn accounts, garden plans, complaints about the church choir, and, famously, letters she never sent.

Tamsin opened it to the last written page and found the date. Three weeks before Ruthie died.

*My hands are bad now,* the entry began. *The doctor says arthritis, I say betrayal. Either way I can't write long, so I will be brief, which anyone who knows me will find hilarious.*

Tamsin laughed. It came out wet and startled her, laughing alone in a dead woman's room at dawn, but there it was. Ruthie could still do that. Ten years gone and she could still make her granddaughter laugh in the middle of the worst morning of her life.

*I have been watching this family fail at the same lesson for thirty years,* the entry went on, *and I am tired, and I am running out of time, and so I am going to cheat.*

Tamsin's tea went cold on the windowsill behind her. She didn't notice. She turned the page.

*Ngozi is gone and everyone is pretending that is fine, which is the family disease if there ever was one. We are generous with everything except honesty. We feed people until they can't move and we love them until they suffocate and we forgive them — oh, how we forgive — but ask us to say one true hard sentence out loud and watch us scatter like chickens.*

*Bisi forgives by feeding. Mama forgives by forgetting. And Tamsin—*

Tamsin stopped breathing.

*Tamsin forgives by pretending nothing happened, which is not forgiveness at all, it is just a prettier kind of exile. She has been doing it since she was small. Break her heart and she'll hand you a plate of jollof and change the subject. I love her more than my own heartbeat and I am telling you plainly: my Tamsin is a coward about the people she loves best.*

The word *coward* should have stung. Instead it landed like a key fitting a lock she hadn't known was there. Because Ansel had said almost the same thing to her, weeks ago, standing in the boathouse after the storm, before everything went wrong: *You do this. You organize instead of feel.* And Tamsin had responded by building a spreadsheet.

*So here is what I have done,* Ruthie wrote. *I have called Mr. Okafor the lawyer — no relation to any Okafor worth mentioning — and I have made him write a clause. The family will call it the marriage vow, because the family sees weddings the way other people see sunsets, and because I need them looking at the wrong thing.*

Tamsin's hands were shaking. She set the notebook on her knees to steady it.

*If I tell Tamsin plainly — "bring your sister home" — she will manage it like a project. She'll send a car. She'll send money. She'll arrange a reconciliation the way she arranges a buffet, beautifully, efficiently, and with nothing true in it. Ngozi will smell the arrangement from a hundred miles away, because Ngozi is the honest one, God help her, and honesty always smells an arrangement.*

*No. If Tamsin is going to learn truth, she has to be cornered into it. So I have given her a deadline and a condition she cannot fake her way through. Marriage. A household built on truth — I made the lawyer put those exact words in, and I dare anyone to read them carefully, because they mean exactly what they say. You cannot build a household on truth while your sister stands outside it. You cannot stand at an altar having told one true sentence your whole adult life.*

*Either she figures it out and comes to me — well, to my memory — with the real problem, or she loses the inn. And I know, I know, everyone will say, Ruthie, you wouldn't take the inn from her, you loved that child. I do love that child. That is why I trust her with an impossible thing. She is the only one stubborn enough.*

*And if she fails — if she chooses pride over truth — then perhaps the inn is better off with strangers who will at least be honest about wanting it.*

There was one more line, written smaller, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn:

*Forgive me, Tamsin. I have set you a riddle instead of a request because I know you, and I know you would refuse a request and die for a riddle. Bring her home. That was always the whole of it. Bring her home.*

* * *

Tamsin didn't remember walking out of the room. She had a vague sense of replacing the notebook exactly as she'd found it, stone and all, some deep-trained obedience to Ruthie's orderings surviving even catastrophe. Then she was sitting on the top stair with the door closed behind her and the notebook's contents ringing in her skull like a struck bell.

*A household built on truth.*

She had read those words a hundred times. In the framed document, in the lawyer's letters, in her own frantic annotations. She had skimmed them every single time — filed them under *sentimentality*, under *Ruthie being Ruthie*, the little flourish of an old woman who liked her legalese garnished with poetry. She'd built spreadsheets around the word *marriage*. She'd negotiated deadlines. She'd hired — well, recruited — a fake girlfriend with the efficiency of a woman booking catering.

And the whole time, the answer had been sitting in the room down the hall, in a dead woman's handwriting, patient as a seed.

*Bring her home.*

Ngozi had been home for two days. Ngozi had delivered an ultimatum and a revelation and had then, in the family way, retreated to the east wing guest room to be miserable in private, and Tamsin had let her retreat, because retreating was the family dance, because pretending nothing had happened was the inheritance none of them had needed a lawyer to receive.

Tamsin thought about the night Ngozi left. She was nineteen. She had stood in the driveway with one suitcase while the family watched from the porch like a jury, and Mama had said *we don't speak of this again*, and Tamsin — nineteen, terrified, guilty in a way she had spent a decade refusing to look at directly — had said nothing. Had handed her sister a container of food for the road. Had watched the taillights go and turned around and helped clear the dinner table.

*You cannot build a household on truth while your sister stands outside it.*

She had solved the wrong problem. For ten years — no, for six weeks, but the six weeks had contained all ten years inside them like a nest of boxes — she had been solving the wrong problem with tremendous skill. She had planned a wedding the way she planned everything, flawlessly, and the flawlessness had been the point, had been the hiding place. Nobody questions a perfect plan. Nobody looks behind a flawless spreadsheet for a coward.

Ansel had looked, though. That was the thing about Ansel. Ansel, who had fled this town ten years ago carrying her own locked room; Ansel, who had agreed to lie for money and then stayed for free; Ansel, who had stood in the kitchen at midnight counting linens and asked the one question nobody else in this family would ask. Ansel would understand this. Ansel, of all people, knew what it was to discover that the story you'd been telling yourself had a different ending than the one you'd memorized.

The floorboard creaked behind her and Tamsin looked up.

Mama stood in the hallway in her robe, silver hair unbraided, holding two cups of tea. She looked at Tamsin sitting on the stairs, at the closed door of Ruthie's room, and something moved across her face — recognition, maybe, or resignation, the expression of a woman watching history walk through her house in slippers.

"You've been in her room," Mama said. Not a question.

"Yes."

"And?"

Tamsin opened her mouth to do the family thing. To deflect, to soften, to hand her mother a plate of jollof and change the subject. The instinct rose in her throat, fluent and old.

She swallowed it.

"She wasn't asking me to get married," Tamsin said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she let it crack, which was possibly the hardest thing she'd done in ten years. "Mama. She was asking me to bring Ngozi home. It was never about a wedding. I've been — I've spent six weeks planning a wedding, and she wanted—" Her breath hitched. "I failed her test. I solved the wrong problem. I'm good at the wrong problem."

Mama came and sat beside her on the stair, robes pooling, and pressed one of the teacups into Tamsin's hands. For a long moment neither of them drank. Downstairs, faintly, someone was up already — the clatter of a pan, probably Bisi, frying her feelings like always — and the lake outside the window had gone pink at the edges.

"I knew," Mama said quietly.

Tamsin turned. "You *knew*?"

"Your grandmother told me, near the end. Not the clause — she kept that from everyone, even me, said I talked in my sleep." Almost a smile. "But she told me what she wanted. She said, *Adaeze, one of my granddaughters will have to be brave and the other will have to be forgiven, and I don't get to choose which is which.*" Mama looked down into her tea. "I hoped it would be you. The brave part. And then Ngozi arrived and you froze her out at the door, and I thought — ah. So it's the other way."

"Mama—"

"Don't apologize yet." Mama's voice was gentle and immovable, a force Tamsin had been navigating her whole life. "Apologies are cheap in this house. We season everything else, why not sorrow?" She set her cup down. "Go talk to your sister. Not with food. Not with arrangements. With the true sentence. Whatever it is — and I think you know what it is, Tamsin, I think you've known since you were nineteen and you handed her that container like it could pay a debt."

Tamsin's eyes burned. "What if she won't hear it?"

"Then you'll have told the truth," Mama said, rising, gathering her cups, "and the truth does not require a signature. That was always your grandmother's whole theology, though she dressed it up in lawyers' language to make us listen." At the hallway's end she paused, silhouetted against the morning light. "Also — that girl of yours. Ansel. She's been awake since five, pacing the dock like a woman rehearsing something. Whatever you're about to do, do it soon. This family can only hold its breath so long."

She went downstairs. Tamsin sat alone on the landing with cold tea and a decade of misreading, and below her, through the window at the turn of the stairs, she could see the dock — and Ansel on it, small in the early light, arms wrapped around herself, staring at the water like it owed her an answer.

Two truths, then. One for her sister, waiting behind a guest-room door. One for the woman on the dock, who had carried her own silence for ten years and was, right now, visibly deciding whether to keep carrying it.

Tamsin stood. Her legs shook. She went toward the guest room first, because Ruthie's riddle had an order to it, and because she was, whatever else was true, her grandmother's granddaughter: stubborn enough for impossible things.

Behind Ngozi's door, she raised her hand to knock — and heard, from inside, the low sound of her sister's voice, on the phone, saying words that stopped Tamsin's blood:

"No. Don't send the letter yet. I haven't told her about the fire."
