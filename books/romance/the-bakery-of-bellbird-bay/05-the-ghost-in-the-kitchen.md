## 5. The Ghost in the Kitchen

The money problem announced itself the way most of Zephyrine's problems did: politely at first, then all at once.

He discovered it on a Tuesday morning, standing barefoot in the cottage kitchen with his phone pressed to his ear and the sound of his own bank balance being read to him by a woman with the soothing cadence of a hospice nurse.

"Your available balance is—" she paused, as if giving him a moment to compose himself, "—forty-one dollars and sixty cents."

"Right," Zephyrine said. "And the—there was supposed to be a final payment. From Hale's people."

"The account shows that payment was frozen pending legal review, Mr. Vasquez."

Legal review. Of course. The memoir had been recalled from shelves across three countries; somewhere in Sydney, lawyers were dividing Edmund Hale's reputation into salvageable and unsalvageable piles like fishmongers sorting the morning catch. Zephyrine's ghostwritten sentences were in neither pile. They were simply gone — pulped, allegedly, along with forty thousand hardcovers.

He thanked the woman, hung up, and stood for a while in the grey light watching a spider rebuild its web in the cottage window with more industry than he'd shown in a decade.

Forty-one dollars and sixty cents. He thought about it in practical terms. Bread was apparently free here — Marlow kept pressing loaves on him like a man offloading contraband — but bread alone did not make a life. There was rent on the cottage, due in eleven days. There was the small matter of food that wasn't sourdough. And there was, though he hated to admit it, a train ticket back to Sydney sitting in his coat pocket like a loaded gun, purchased the first night out of pure cowardice and never quite thrown away.

He could go back. He could find another chef, another memoirist, another voice to wear. His agent — ex-agent now, presumably; he hadn't had the courage to check — used to say Zephyrine could write anyone. That was the whole tragedy of him, really. Anyone. Just never himself.

Outside, the bakery window glowed its familiar gold against the pre-dawn dark. Four-fifteen. Marlow would be inside already, sleeves rolled, forearms dusted white, doing whatever alchemy turned flour and water into the only thing this town seemed to agree on.

Zephyrine put the kettle on, watched it fail to boil with the enthusiasm of a kettle that had seen better decades, and made a decision that felt less like a plan and more like a surrender to gravity.

* * *

"You want to work," Marlow said, "for free."

"For exposure," Zephyrine corrected. "It's the currency of my people."

Marlow looked at him across the counter with an expression that suggested he was running the phrase *the currency of my people* through several internal translations and finding none of them sensible. Flour clung to the hair at his temple. There was a smear of something — apricot jam, maybe, or the ghost of yesterday's custard — on his apron pocket. He looked, as always, like a man who had been interrupted mid-thought by the arrival of the world.

"It's not free," Zephyrine said. "I need money. I'm terrible at asking for it directly, so I've constructed this elaborate fiction where I'm helping you."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me all week."

"I have others. They're mostly worse."

Marlow wiped his hands on his apron, which accomplished nothing except redistributing the flour, and leaned against the display case. Behind him, the morning's first trays cooled on racks, filling the shop with that smell Zephyrine had already become embarrassingly dependent on — warm crust, dark caramel, the faint yeasty sweetness of patience.

"The campaign," Marlow said slowly. "You mean the lease thing."

"I mean the lease thing." Zephyrine had heard about it secondhand within three days of arriving, because Bellbird Bay processed information the way gills process water — constantly, and through everyone. Ninety days. Triple the rent. A developer named Corrigan who smiled in photographs the way sharks smile in documentaries. "Everyone in town knows. Everyone in town is furious. Everyone in town is also, if today's conversation at the post office is representative, planning to do absolutely nothing about it except be furious in a slightly louder register."

"Furious is our main export."

"So let's give them something else to do." Zephyrine pulled a napkin from the dispenser and clicked his pen — a real pen, fountain, the one indulgence he'd carried out of Sydney along with the train ticket. "Posters. A letterbox drop. A page on the internet — do you have a page on the internet?"

"I have a nephew who mentioned once that I should."

"A nephew is not a strategy, Marlow. Words are a strategy. I happen to—" he caught himself, one syllable from the cliff edge, and swerved, "—I used to do some writing. Corporate stuff. Menus, brochures, that kind of thing."

It came out smoothly. That was the horror of it. Ten years of professional lying had left him with a tongue so well-oiled it barely needed him anymore.

Marlow studied him. Not suspiciously — that was the thing Zephyrine was learning about him — but with the careful attention of a man reading a recipe for the first time, checking every measurement twice before committing.

"Menus," Marlow repeated.

"And brochures."

"Huh." Something almost like a smile moved under the flour. "Okay. But I'm paying you. Actual money. Bakery rates."

"Bakery rates being?"

"Day-old everything, plus twenty dollars a week until the campaign either works or dies."

Zephyrine considered pointing out that this was less than he'd once been paid per word. Then he considered the look on Marlow's face — guarded hope, held at arm's length like something that had been dropped before — and found that the arithmetic had changed.

"Deal," he said.

* * *

They worked in the bakery's back room, at a table scarred by thirty years of tray racks and knife nicks, beneath a clock that ran four minutes fast and had apparently done so since the Howard government. Marlow baked while Zephyrine wrote, which meant Zephyrine spent his days in a warm room full of rising dough, drafting slogans on the backs of invoice paper while a man hummed tunelessly six feet away.

It was, Zephyrine realized on the third morning, the happiest he had been in years. This was deeply annoying.

"Read that again," Marlow said, peering over his shoulder at the poster draft. His breath stirred the hair near Zephyrine's ear, close enough that Zephyrine could smell yeast and salt and something underneath that was just Marlow, warm skin and clean cotton.

"*Ninety days is enough time to save a bakery,*" Zephyrine read. "*It's not enough time to build another one.*"

Marlow was quiet for a moment. "That's good."

"It's true. True is easier to write."

"Is it?" Marlow went back to his bench and began folding dough with quick, practiced turns. "Seems like it'd be harder. Truth doesn't let you take anything out."

Zephyrine's pen hovered. Somewhere in the walls, the fast clock ticked its stubborn four-minute lie.

"You'd be surprised," he said lightly, "what you can take out of the truth and still call it true."

Marlow glanced at him — one of those looks, brief and level, that made Zephyrine feel like a window someone was checking for cracks. Then he shrugged and turned back to the dough, and the moment passed, the way moments did, leaving only a faint bruise.

* * *

It happened on the fifth day, and it happened because of a shoebox.

Marlow had gone next door to settle a dispute with the butcher — something about who was entitled to the shared alleyway, a feud Zephyrine suspected had been running longer than either man had been alive — and he'd asked Zephyrine to grab the order book from the office shelf. "Green cover, under the tin," he'd called over his shoulder. "Don't judge me by the state of that office."

The office was a closet with ambitions. Shelves bowed under ledgers going back decades, their spines labelled in fading marker: 1998–2001, 2002–2006, DAD'S OLD ONES — DON'T TOSS. Zephyrine found the green order book wedged beneath a biscuit tin, and when he pulled it free, the tin shifted, and the box behind it slid forward, and the lid came off, and letters spilled across the floor like startled birds.

He knelt to gather them up. He was halfway through stacking them when his eye caught the top envelope, and the world stopped.

He knew that handwriting.

Of course he knew it. You don't spend ten years forging a correspondence without learning your partner's hand better than your own — except this wasn't forged. This wasn't his work. This was the incoming mail, the letters Marlow had *sent*, the ones Zephyrine had read and reread and answered late at night in Edmund Hale's borrowed voice, and Marlow's writing was as unmistakable to him now as a fingerprint. The narrow, upright letters. The way the *f* crossed low, hurried. The little habit of pressing so hard on full stops that they dented through to the next page.

*Dear Chef Hale,* the letter began. *My dad died in March. I'm writing because you answered the last one, and I don't really have anyone else who does.*

Zephyrine sat down on the office floor amid thirty years of bakery ledgers and a decade of a stranger's grief, and did not move.

He remembered this letter. God help him, he remembered it. It had arrived in his second year of answering the fan mail — a task Edmund Hale himself had abandoned after roughly four months, handing the stack to his ghostwriter with a shrug and the words *"you're better at the sad ones."* Zephyrine had read this letter at his desk in Potts Point, rain on the window, and had felt something crack open in his chest, and had written back for two hours — longer than the fee justified, longer than any professional courtesy required — signing a dead man's warmth to a living man's wound.

Ten years. Birthday letters. Letters after floods, after bad Christmases, after the year the council tried to rezone the foreshore. He knew this man's life in a way he had never known any lover's. He knew that Marlow Finch took his coffee black but liked the smell of the vanilla syrup. He knew about his father's hands, the arthritis, the way the old man had still kneaded dough every Sunday long after he couldn't feel his fingertips. Zephyrine had written *"your father sounds like he taught you everything that matters"* and signed it E. Hale, and somewhere in Bellbird Bay a grieving man had pinned those words above his bench, believing a celebrity chef had taken time out of his famous life to care.

The footsteps came back down the hall. Zephyrine had perhaps four seconds.

He did the only thing his body knew how to do, the thing ten years of concealment had trained into his reflexes: he swept the letters together, shoved them toward the box, and stood up too fast, holding the green order book like a shield.

Marlow appeared in the doorway. His eyes went to Zephyrine. To the floor. To the shoebox, lid askew, one envelope still lying face-up where Zephyrine had missed it.

Something crossed Marlow's face — not anger, worse, a kind of involuntary softening, the look of a man caught holding something tender in public. He crossed the room and picked up the envelope, and Zephyrine watched him decide, visibly, whether to explain.

"My dad's," Marlow said finally, tucking the box back on the shelf. "Sort of. Letters. Old ones."

"Letters," Zephyrine said. His voice came out half a tone wrong, and he heard it, and Marlow heard it too.

"You okay? You've gone a bit—"

"Fine. Fine. The tin — it fell, I knocked the tin, clumsy." He laughed, and the laugh was the worst thing he'd ever produced, bright and hollow as a struck pan. "Just startled me. Boxes falling. Classic hazard of the, ah, archive industry."

"The what?"

"Closets. Small rooms. You know." He handed over the order book with the smoothness of a magician completing a trick he despised. "Green book. Retrieved. No letters harmed."

Marlow took it. For a moment he didn't move, just stood there in the doorway with the light behind him, looking at Zephyrine with that same careful, measuring attention — and then past him, at the shoebox, and something in his expression folded closed like a hand around a coin.

"Thanks," he said, and went back to the kitchen.

Zephyrine stayed in the office a moment longer, heart slamming, and understood with perfect clarity that he had just lied beautifully and completely to the one person whose letters he had answered for ten years, and that the lie had landed, and that landing it was the loneliest thing that had ever happened to him.

* * *

That evening he sat in the cottage with the lamp on and tried to draft the campaign flyer, and got exactly one line down — *Some things are worth keeping warm* — before the pen stopped moving.

Because here was the shape of it, the trap he'd built without noticing: he was writing copy to save the bakery of a man whose darkest hours he had secretly witnessed, whose father's death he had answered in another man's name, who had told Zephyrine things over coffee and flour-dusted counters that he'd never told the person he thought he was telling. Every day the lie got heavier. Every day it also got warmer. Marlow laughed at his jokes now. Marlow saved him the end piece of the olive loaf, the one with the most crust, without being asked. This morning Marlow had touched his shoulder reaching past him for the proving baskets, and Zephyrine had thought about it for four hours afterward like a schoolboy.

And all of it stood on a foundation of paper and ink and a signature that wasn't his.

He looked at the flyer draft. *Some things are worth keeping warm.* Even his honesty was plagiarized from the situation.

There was a knock. Soft, apologetic, two knuckles' worth. Zephyrine's stomach dropped through the floorboards before his brain caught up — *he knows, he knows, the envelope, the voice, the laugh* — and he opened the door with what he hoped was the face of an innocent man.

Marlow stood on the step in the blue dusk, holding two mugs and a plate covered in a tea towel. He looked awkward. He looked, Zephyrine thought with a lurch, nervous.

"Closing-time coffee," Marlow said. "It's a tradition. Well. It's a thing I do. Alone, usually." He lifted the mugs slightly, an offering. "Thought maybe — you'd earned the end of the olive loaf. And I wanted to talk about something."

Zephyrine's mouth was dry. "About something."

"Yeah." Marlow's eyes held his, steady and unreadable in the porch light. "About the letters."

Behind him, the sea kept its slow time against the rocks, and the last light went off in the bakery window, and Zephyrine stepped aside to let the truth — or whatever he was about to do instead of it — walk in.
