## 2. The Baker at Stall Nine

There are three kinds of people who come to Stall Nine on a Saturday morning in Junebug Creek.

The first kind know what they want. They say "two peach hand pies and the lemon tart if it's still there," and they pay, and they leave, and I love them with a quiet, professional devotion that will never require anything of me.

The second kind don't know what they want, but they want to talk about it. These are my bread and butter — literally, in the case of Mrs. Hollis from the hardware store, who has been circling the cinnamon rolls since opening and will buy one only after she's told me everything wrong with her son-in-law's new truck. The town of Junebug Creek does not have a newspaper anymore. It has my stall. I am, functionally, the press. I print nothing. I simply let the dough rise while people confess.

The third kind are tourists, and God bless them, they tip.

I'm telling you this taxonomy so you'll understand that by 9:40 on the morning of the Founders' Day market — the first of the season, the one where the whole town shakes off winter like a wet dog — I had already served all three kinds, sold out of the apricot galettes, and heard that Dale Pruitt's divorce was back on, which was news to everyone including Dale. I was in a good mood. A flour-dusted, sun-warmed, cash-box-heavy good mood.

Which is exactly when the storm cloud in linen walked past my stall.

I noticed him the way you notice weather — not because he was doing anything dramatic, but because the air pressure changed. He moved through the market crowd like a man who had been handed a map of Junebug Creek and found it personally offensive. Linen shirt, sleeves rolled with the kind of precision that suggested rolling them had been a decision rather than an accident. Jaw set. Shoulders carrying something heavier than the leather satchel slung across his chest. He looked, frankly, like money that had recently learned bad news.

"Thackeray," said Mrs. Hollis, in the tone other towns reserve for *storm's coming*. "That's the Thackeray boy. Lior. Back for the will reading."

"The vineyard Thackerays?" I asked, because I'd been in town three years and knew the name mostly as the label on the bottles at the diner and as the answer to the trivia question *who owns half the creek frontage*.

"The *only* Thackerays." She lowered her voice to the register Junebug Creek uses for both gossip and prayer. "Old Edmund passed in March. Whole family was up at the house yesterday for the lawyers. You could hear Marisol hollering from the road."

Marisol I knew — or knew of. She bought macarons in August and paid in exact change and once informed me that my lavender shortbread was "interesting," which in her mouth was a felony.

The storm cloud — Lior, apparently — had stopped three stalls down at the honey stand, where he was being talked at by Earl Wexler, who could talk at a fence post until the fence post filed for a restraining order. Lior's face did something complicated. Not rude. Just... elsewhere. Like every word Earl said was landing on a desk already buried under paper.

And here is the thing about me, the thing my grandmother put in me along with the recipe cards and the fear of wasting butter: I cannot stand to see someone go hungry who doesn't have to be. It's not kindness. Kindness implies I'd do it for anyone. It's more like a defect. A hunger-shaped hole in my otherwise excellent judgment.

Lior Thackeray had the look of a person who hadn't eaten since Tuesday, out of either grief or spite, and both options made me itch.

So when he turned away from Earl — visibly escaping, moving fast now, cutting between stalls with the single-mindedness of a man fleeing a burning building — and his path took him past Stall Nine, I did the thing I probably shouldn't have done.

I held out a hand pie.

"Peach," I said. "You look like you're losing an argument with the whole county."

He stopped. That was my first mistake — noticing that when he stopped, he stopped completely, like the rest of the world ran on a different clock. His eyes came to me and I got the full force of them, dark and tired and sharp enough to slice, and I understood why the Thackerays were famous for more than wine. Some families breed charm. This one bred intensity, and intensity is just charm with the safety off.

"I'm not hungry," he said.

"You're lying," I said. "Nobody walks past a hand pie at a walking pace unless they're hungry. People who aren't hungry speed up. You slowed down. That's science."

"That's not science."

"It's bakery science. Different discipline. Very rigorous." I wiggled the pie. Somewhere behind me, Mrs. Hollis had gone silent, which meant I now had an audience, which meant there was no graceful exit, so I committed. "It's free. Consider it a public service. You were making Earl Wexler nervous, and when Earl gets nervous he talks faster, and when Earl talks faster the whole market loses ten minutes of productivity."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. Not a smile. The *site* of a future smile, maybe, cleared and graded but unbuilt. "That's a lot of words to give away a pie."

"It's a lot of pie to give away a lecture. We're even." I set it on the edge of my counter, within reach, and went back to boxing tarts for the Delgado twins, because the second rule of Stall Nine is that nobody — not even a beautiful disaster in linen — gets to make me hover.

He didn't take it right away. I felt him standing there, feeling the weight of it, deciding whether accepting food from a stranger constituted a debt, and I thought: *there it is.* I'd seen that look before, in people who grew up in houses where everything was entered into a ledger. My grandmother used to say some folks can't take a gift without checking the fine print. She said it sadly, over her recipe box, usually about her own sister.

He took the pie.

"For the record," he said, "I wasn't losing an argument."

"No?"

"I wasn't having one. Nobody's argued with me yet today. They've all been too busy telling me things I didn't ask to know." He bit into the pie, and I watched him have a small, involuntary crisis about how good it was, and I pretended not to watch, which is a skill, and I failed at it, which is honesty.

"Welcome to Junebug Creek," I said. "Consent isn't required for conversation here. It's a feature."

"Mm." He chewed. Swallowed. Looked at the pie like it had betrayed him by being delicious. "What's in this besides peach?"

"Butter, sugar, a little cardamom, and spite."

"Spite?"

"My grandmother's word for the last tablespoon of something you add when you're angry. It improves everything. Anger is very high in acid." I shrugged. "I was angry when I developed the recipe. Best batch I ever made. Now I have to stay mildly annoyed to keep the quality consistent, which is exhausting."

This time the corner of his mouth actually built something. Small. Load-bearing, maybe. "What were you angry about, three years ago?"

"Lease negotiation. Landlord wanted to triple the rent on my kitchen because he found out the festival named my stall a 'town treasure,' which apparently means I can afford extortion." I wiped my hands on my apron. "I stayed anyway. Stubbornness is also an ingredient."

"Why stay? If he squeezed you once, he'll squeeze you again."

And there it was — the question underneath the question, the one strangers always ask bakers and never ask bankers. Why does a person choose flour over leverage. I could have given him the polite answer, the one about community and craft and loving what you do. Instead I heard myself say, "Because everybody told me not to. And because I'm good at it, and being good at something nobody thinks will survive is its own kind of revenge." I nodded at him. "You'd know. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The *everybody-told-me-not-to* face. You're wearing it like cologne."

He looked at me for a long moment, and I had the distinct sensation of being inventoried — assessed, appraised, entered into some internal ledger with a value assigned. Most people find that uncomfortable. I found it interesting, which should have been my warning sign. Interesting is the gateway drug. Interesting is how self-sufficient people end up entangled.

"You run this whole stall alone?" he asked.

"Every Saturday. Every festival. Four o'clock starts, seven days before a big market." I gestured at the operation — the striped awning, the tiered baskets, the chalkboard where I'd written TODAY'S GOSSIP: DALE (AGAIN) in erasable marker, because I commit to the bit. "No staff. No partner. No help I didn't build myself."

"Why?"

"Same reason you didn't eat until someone forced you. Habit." I tilted my head. "Why does a man with a satchel full of lawyer paper walk through a farmers' market looking like the market owes him money?"

His jaw did the thing again. Set. Reset. "How do you know there's lawyer paper in the satchel?"

"Because you keep touching it like it might bite, and because this is the week of the Thackeray will reading, and because you flinched when Mrs. Hollis said the word 'lawyers' from six feet away. Also you have ink on your thumb. Signing ink. Blue-black. The bank uses it; the school uses red." I smiled, sweet as glaze. "Bakery science. Observation is a different discipline. Very rigorous."

For a second I thought he'd be angry. I braced for it — the rich-boy flash, the *who do you think you're talking to*, the retreat. I'd earned it. I'd pried, and we both knew it.

Instead he exhaled, long and slow, like a valve releasing, and said, "My father died in March. The will was read Thursday. It turns out he left me a vineyard, a mortgage, and a homework assignment." His voice stayed level, but level the way a table is level — flat, and holding up something heavy. "'Settle the family account.' Those were his words. In writing. To me. From beyond the grave, still assigning chores."

I should have said *I'm sorry*. Any decent person would have. But Wren Okafor has never once in her life taken the decent exit when the true one was available, and what I said was:

"He sounds delightful."

"He sounds like a man who treated love like bookkeeping." Lior looked down at the hand pie, half-eaten, and something crossed his face — grief, yes, but grief that had curdled into something with more teeth. "Everything was a balance owed. Affection was a line item. You performed well, you got credited. You stepped out of line—" He stopped himself. Closed the ledger, whatever ledger lived behind his eyes. "Anyway. Thank you for the pie."

"You're welcome for the pie. The advice is extra."

"Advice?"

"You look like a man about to make a deal with somebody out of desperation." I leaned on the counter. "Don't. Desperate deals are how landlords get you and uncles get vineyards. Whatever corner you think you're in, eat first. Sleep first. Nobody signs anything hungry. That's not bakery science, that's just survival."

"I'm not—"

"Signing anything *today*, maybe. But you've got the look. I've seen it on brides, on defendants, on Mrs. Hollis's son-in-law the day he bought that truck. It's the look of a person about to solve a feeling with a transaction." I held his gaze, because I figured someone in this town should, and because — fine, because his eyes were unfair and I was already in trouble. "Whatever it is, it'll still be there tomorrow. Eat something tonight that isn't a contract."

He stared at me. Then he laughed — one syllable, surprised out of him, rusty as a gate hinge — and the sound did something to my chest cavity that I chose not to examine, because examination leads to diagnosis and diagnosis leads to feelings and I had a strict no-feelings policy that had kept my life clean and mine for thirty-one years.

"You always like this?" he asked.

"Worse. This is the customer-service version."

"Lucky me." He pulled out his wallet, and I waved it off.

"It was free. Free means free. That's the whole point of free — no entry in anybody's ledger." I said it lightly. I watched it land heavily. He went still, the way people go still when a stranger says something true about them without meaning to, and I thought: *there's a story there, and it's none of my business,* and then I thought, immediately and against my will: *but I'd like to hear it.*

He put the wallet away. Stood there a moment longer than the transaction required — than the non-transaction required — and I got the sense he was deciding something, though what, I couldn't have said.

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Wren. Okafor. Stall Nine. Open Saturdays and whenever the mood strikes."

"Lior." He said it like an apology. "Thackeray. Vineyard. Apparently."

"I know. Mrs. Hollis briefed me before you arrived. You're famous. Well — infamous. Well — discussed."

"That's worse than either."

"Much." I slid a lemon tart across the counter before my brain could file an objection. "On the house. For the road. You look like you're about to go be brave somewhere, and bravery runs on citrus."

He looked at the tart. At me. At the tart again, with the expression of a man recalculating a figure that refused to balance.

"Why are you being nice to me?"

"Who says I'm nice? I'm strategic. Sad-looking customers depress the other customers. You're bad for ambiance. Fixing you is good for business."

"That's a lie."

"It's bakery-grade truth. Ninety percent accurate, structurally sound, and nobody needs to know what's in it." I turned to the next customer — Mr. Delgado, two tarts, one opinion about the price of eggs — and by the time I looked up, Lior Thackeray was walking away down the row of stalls, slower than he'd come, eating the lemon tart like a man who'd forgotten food existed and was being reintroduced to it.

Mrs. Hollis materialized at my elbow like a vulture with opinions.

"Well," she said.

"Well what."

"*Well.*"

"I gave a grieving man a pie, Mrs. Hollis. It's called charity. It's in the Bible."

"That was not charity. Charity doesn't look like that. Charity doesn't lean on the counter." She selected a cinnamon roll with the air of a woman collecting evidence. "That's the Thackeray heir, Wrennie. That family eats people. Old Edmund bought up half this creek by being charming to exactly the ones who couldn't afford to say no. You want to end up a line item in somebody's ledger, you go right ahead."

"I don't want anything," I said, and it was mostly true, and the part that wasn't true I fed to the compost bucket along with the day's scraps. "I want to sell out of galettes by noon and I want Dale Pruitt's divorce to hold this time. That's the complete list."

She hummed the hum of a woman filing something away for later, paid, and drifted off to distribute her findings across the market like a bee cross-pollinating rumors.

I worked. I sold. I refilled the peach basket twice, which never happens, and blamed the weather. And all morning, in the gaps between customers, my traitor brain kept returning to the same image: a man in linen slowing down past a hand pie, hungry and refusing to know it, taking the first bite like it cost him something.

Self-sufficient people recognize each other. It's like a signal only we can see. And the signal Lior Thackeray was broadcasting, under all that linen and ledger-keeping, was simple: *I don't need anyone, and it's working great, and please don't look too closely.*

I'd been sending that same broadcast for years. I knew exactly how well it worked.

By closing, the till was fat, the gossip had been distributed, and the chalkboard needed wiping. I erased DALE (AGAIN) and stood a moment with the rag in my hand, looking down the emptying lane toward the parking area, where a familiar figure was loading a leather satchel into a truck older and more dented than the linen shirt had led me to expect.

He caught me looking. I didn't pretend I hadn't. He raised the last corner of the lemon tart in something that was almost a salute, and drove off toward the vineyard road, and I stood there in the flour-dust and the late light thinking about what I'd told him — *nobody signs anything hungry* — and wondering, with a small cold prickle I couldn't source, why I had the sudden, certain feeling that whatever deal Lior Thackeray was going to make out of desperation...

...was somehow, impossibly, going to involve me.

* * *

That night I locked up the stall, carried my cash box to the truck, and told myself the prickle was indigestion.

It was not indigestion. Two days later, on Monday, when I came back from the farmer co-op to find a message on my answering machine from a Lior Thackeray — formal, stiff, rehearsed, and audibly desperate around the edges — asking if I might be available to discuss "a business arrangement," I stood in my little kitchen with flour on my hands and laughed until I cried.

Then I played it again.

Then I started making a list of terms.
