## 5. Rehearsal Dinner, Unrehearsed

### Lior

There are rehearsals for things that matter — weddings, eulogies, the occasional hostage negotiation — and then there is a Thackeray family dinner, which requires neither rehearsal nor honesty but a kind of improvisational theater training you can only acquire through childhood trauma.

I had prepared Wren anyway. In the truck on the drive over, I gave her the briefing like a general handing over maps: Aunt Bev would ask about intentions within ten minutes. Cousin Marisol would smile at her the way a cat smiles at a bird it has already decided to eat. Uncle Ambrose would be charming in the specific way of men who have already counted your money as theirs. And my mother—

My mother I couldn't brief. There was no map for Odette Thackeray. She was less a person than a climate.

"Got it," Wren said, watching the vineyard rows slide past the window. "Hostile weather system. Do I get hazard pay?"

"You're getting paid an obscene amount to eat free food and hold my hand."

"The hand-holding is the hazardous part," she said, and I hated how much I noticed that she didn't pull her hand off the gearshift when our knuckles brushed.

The house had been decorated for the reunion's opening weekend by someone with more budget than taste: white lights strung through the pergola, tables dressed in linen the color of expensive apology. The whole extended family was there — thirty-odd Thackerays and their satellites, all of them performing warmth at a volume just short of sincerity.

Wren walked in beside me and the noise dipped. Not stopped — dipped, the way water does before it decides whether to boil. Thirty pairs of eyes doing arithmetic: *who is this, what does she mean, can we use this.*

Then Aunt Bev solved it for everyone by crossing the terrace like a heat-seeking missile with a wine glass.

"You must be *her*," Bev announced, seizing both of Wren's hands. "Lior never brings anyone. Lior brought you. So you're either very brave or very foolish, and either way, sit next to me."

"She's a baker," I said, because Wren had instructed me to lead with the profession — people underestimate bakers, she said, which is exactly why we let them.

"A baker!" Bev's delight was genuine and therefore terrifying. "Oh, thank God, someone at this table who makes something instead of *money*. Sit. Sit. Tell me everything."

And here is the thing about Wren Okafor that I should have anticipated and did not: she doesn't perform. I'd spent two weeks coaching her on our story — how we met (the festival stall, the tart, the unsolicited advice), how long (eight months), how serious (ask her, not me) — and she delivered all of it flawlessly, but she delivered it *herself*, with her own laugh underneath it, her own hands moving when she talked about laminated dough the way other people talk about religion. She wasn't playing a role. She was being herself in a fictional frame, and the frame disappeared so completely that halfway through dinner I caught myself leaning toward her answer to a question I hadn't scripted, genuinely wanting to know.

"So how did you two actually happen?" Marisol asked from across the table, smiling her accounting smile. "Lior's been so *private* since coming back."

"The tart," Wren said.

"The tart," I confirmed.

"That's not a story," Marisol pressed.

"It's absolutely a story," Wren said. "Your cousin stormed past my stall looking like a storm cloud in linen —"

"Linen," Marisol repeated, delighted.

"— and I told them they looked like they needed fruit, because sour people need sour fruit, it's science. They bought a peach hand pie out of spite and came back the next Saturday out of something else."

"What else?" Bev demanded.

Wren looked at me. Just looked, one beat too long, and whatever crossed her face wasn't in the script.

"Peaches," she said, and the table laughed, and I laughed, and somewhere under my ribs something shifted its weight and settled in for a long stay.

Ambrose held court at the far end, telling a story about the harvest of '09 that made him the hero of a year everyone present knew had nearly bankrupted the vineyard. He caught my eye across the table and raised his glass a half-inch. *Later*, the gesture said. *We'll talk terms later.* I lifted my own glass a quarter-inch back, which in Thackeray meant *over my dead body*, and he smiled like a man who owned shovels.

Dessert came out — catered, because my mother did not cook, my mother *curated* — and that was when Odette finally approached.

She'd been watching all evening. I'd felt it the way you feel weather change. Now she drifted to Wren's side of the table with her glass of untouched white wine and her face arranged into gracious interest.

"So," Odette said. "You make the pies at the festival. Stall nine."

"Stall nine," Wren agreed. "Best corner on the green. I fought a woman for it in 2019 and I'd do it again."

And Wren laughed — that real one, the unguarded one, head tipped slightly back, the sound that comes out of her like she's surprised by her own amusement —

Odette's glass stopped halfway to her mouth.

It was small. Anyone else would have missed it. But I've spent thirty years reading that woman's micro-expressions the way sailors read clouds, and I saw it: the flicker, the recalculation, the moment where recognition snagged on something and couldn't name it.

"You have a wonderful laugh," Odette said slowly.

"Thank you. It's load-bearing."

"My husband used to say—" Odette stopped. Set the glass down with a small precise click. "Excuse me."

She left. Wren watched her go, then leaned close, her shoulder warm against mine. "Did I do something wrong?"

"No," I said, which was true, which was the problem. Whatever Odette had heard in that laugh belonged to a story I hadn't given Wren yet. A story with a boy in it, and a summer, and a sentence I'd been drafting for ten years.

Bev stood up with her glass and demanded a toast. "To Lior! Back where they belong, and clearly, *clearly* in love — look at them, Marisol, look at their faces, I called this in April—"

"To Lior," the table echoed, glasses rising.

"And to many years," Bev continued, merciless, "and to hearing wedding bells before the reunion's out, mark my words—"

"Mama," Marisol said sweetly, "let them breathe. Though honestly, Lior, after everything, it's lovely to see you happy again. After *the accident*." Her eyes were on Wren now, gentle as a trap. "You know about the accident, don't you? Of course you do. Eight months. Lior must have told you everything."

The table waited. Thirty faces, warm candlelight, Ambrose at the end of the table suddenly very still and very interested, and my hand under the tablecloth going cold, because *the accident* was not a thing anyone had ever said aloud in front of me, and certainly not a thing I had ever told Wren, because there was no accident. There was only a departure, and a silence, and a sentence.

I opened my mouth and found nothing in it.

"There was a fire," Wren said.

Every head turned.

"At the bakery where Lior worked, before Junebug Creek. Years ago." Wren's voice was steady, warm, terrible. "They don't talk about it. So I don't either. But I know enough to know that when they're ready to tell you, they will, and until then the most loving thing any of us can do is pass the potatoes."

Silence. Then Bev, wet-eyed, "Pass the potatoes," and the moment broke apart into clatter and murmuring, and under the cover of it Wren's hand found my knee and squeezed once.

*I invented that,* her eyes said. *Improvise with me or kill me later.*

I passed the potatoes. My hand shook only a little.

Later, on the terrace, away from the light, she stood beside me at the railing overlooking the dark rows of vines.

"I lied for you in there," she said quietly. "Rule two. No lies about the past. I broke it inside of four hours."

"You saved me."

"I invented a fire, Lior." She turned to look at me, and the string lights caught the edge of her jaw. "Which means someday you're going to have to tell me what actually happened. Because I just built us a history out of nothing, and histories like that—" she exhaled, "—they don't stay empty. They fill up. With true things."

Below us, the vines moved in the dark like something breathing.

"Wren," I started, and didn't know how the sentence ended, and she waited, patient, while I stood there holding ten years of drafts and none of them usable.

From inside, my mother's voice rose — laughing, at something, bright and brittle — and Wren glanced toward the sound, and I watched her hear it again, that laugh, and watch her wonder.

### Wren

Here's what nobody tells you about lying professionally: it's easy. What's hard is the part after, standing in a stranger's bathroom splashing water on your face, realizing you improvised a house fire into a man's life because his cousin cornered him and you could not — *could not* — watch him stand there with nothing in his mouth.

I'd met Lior Thackeray eight months ago over a hand pie and a piece of unsolicited advice. I'd known them three weeks as a paid companion when I invented a tragedy at a dinner table. The math of that should have frightened me. It did frighten me. I washed my face anyway and went back out, because rule two was broken and the only way through a broken rule is forward.

The party was thinning. Bev had moved on to interrogating a nephew about his fertility. Ambrose had cornered Lior near the bar, and I watched from across the room as he talked — smooth, unhurried, one hand on Lior's shoulder like ownership — and watched Lior's spine go rigid in a way I was starting to catalog. There was a whole taxonomy of Lior's tension by now. This one was new. This one had teeth.

Ambrose left. Lior stood alone with two glasses of wine they hadn't drunk.

I went over. "So," I said. "Fire."

"Not here." Their voice was low and scraped. "Please."

"Saturday," I said. "At the bakery. Before open. You bring the truth, I bring the coffee."

Something moved through their face — relief, fear, gratitude, some alloy of all three. "Deal."

"Also, your mother thinks I'm haunting her."

Lior's laugh came out wrong. "She thinks you're familiar."

"People say that. I have one of those faces."

"It's not your face." Lior looked at me then, really looked, and I felt it land somewhere below the professional arrangement, below the contract, in the territory we'd agreed not to enter. "It's your laugh. She heard something in it."

"Well," I said lightly, because lightness was the tool I had, "when she figures out what, let me know. I'd hate to be haunted by a stranger's nostalgia without context."

Odette appeared at my elbow like a cold draft. Up close she was beautiful the way winter is beautiful — precise, silvered, no wasted warmth. "Wren. May I steal you for a moment? Lior won't mind." It wasn't a question, and the glance she gave her child was long and unreadable.

She led me to the garden, past the roses nobody tended anymore, and stopped under the old pecan tree.

"You bake," she said.

"I bake."

"For a living. For strangers. You feed people who pay you and leave."

"That's generally how bakeries work, yes."

Odette studied me with an expression I couldn't parse — grief wearing a mask of courtesy, maybe, or courtesy wearing a mask of grief. "My late husband used to sneak out to the festival every June. Every year. Told me he was meeting suppliers." Her voice stayed level; her hands didn't. "He'd come home smelling like cinnamon and lie about it beautifully. I let him. That's what love looks like, sometimes. Letting someone keep their small fires."

The word landed between us like a dropped glass.

"Mrs. Thackeray—"

"He loved stall nine," she said. "He always bought from stall nine. And tonight, when you laughed—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. When she spoke again the mask was back, flawless. "You have a lovely evening, Wren. Take care of my child. Whoever they are now."

She walked back toward the light, straight-backed, leaving me under the pecan tree with my heart doing something complicated.

Because here's what I knew, standing in the dark of a stranger's garden: Lior had never mentioned a father who loved festivals. Had barely mentioned a father at all. And I had just been handed a piece of a puzzle by a woman who thought she was giving me a warning, and every piece fit a shape I hadn't been told existed.

I found Lior on the terrace, still holding the untouched wine, watching the door their mother had gone through.

"Your turn," I said. "Saturday. Bring everything."

They nodded. And above us, faint through the window, Odette laughed at something Bev said — and Lior flinched, just slightly, like a person hearing an echo from a room they'd sealed shut years ago.

Neither of us said anything else on the drive home. But at the bakery door, before I got out, Lior caught my wrist — gently, like asking permission — and said, "Whatever you heard in that garden—"

"I'll wait for Saturday," I said.

They let go. I watched the taillights go, and stood a long time in the dark outside my own shop, thinking about fires — the ones people survive, the ones people start, and the one I'd invented at a dinner table that was already, steadily, filling itself with true things.
