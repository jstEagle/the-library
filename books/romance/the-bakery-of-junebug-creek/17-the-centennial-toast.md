## 17. The Centennial Toast

There is a particular kind of silence that falls over a crowd of eight hundred people when the mayor says "and now, a word from the Thackeray family," and every single one of them turns to look at you. I'd describe it as the sound of a noose being measured, but that's uncharitable to ropes, which have never done anything to me.

I stood at the edge of the bandstand in my good blazer — the one with the elbow patches Wren had laughed at and then quietly stopped laughing at because she realized I was serious about them — and I watched my uncle Ambrose climb the steps like a man ascending a pulpit he'd built himself.

He looked wonderful. That was the thing about Ambrose that had taken me thirty years and one wine cellar to understand: he always looked wonderful. Silver at the temples, artfully rumpled linen, a smile calibrated to suggest he knew something charming about you. The town loved him. The town had *always* loved him. He was the one who remembered names, who sent flowers when your mother died, who showed up at the volunteer fire department fundraiser with a check so large it made the local paper. Generosity was his camouflage. It worked on everyone.

It had almost worked on me, right up until I found six years of ledgers in a cellar that smelled like dust and my father's ghost, documenting monthly payments to a consulting firm that existed nowhere except on paper and in Ambrose's imagination.

Now I stood in the front row of the centennial festival's opening ceremony with those same ledger pages folded against my ribs like a letter bomb, and Ambrose raised a glass of Thackeray Reserve — our best vintage, the one we could least afford to pour — and began to speak.

* * *

"One hundred years," he said, and his voice carried the way voices do when they've been practicing. "One hundred years ago, Elias Thackeray planted the first vines on this hillside with nothing but faith, a mule named Clementine, and soil he swore was better than anything in France."

A ripple of laughter. Clementine the mule was a piece of family lore Ambrose trotted out at every public occasion, and the town ate it up every time, because a mule named Clementine is funnier than any truth.

"Elias didn't live to see the first real harvest." Ambrose's voice dipped, went tender. "But his son did. And his granddaughter. And now his great-nephew stands among us, returned home after too long away, carrying the family forward."

Home. He said it like a gift he was handing me in front of everyone, and I felt the trap teeth of it. Because that was Ambrose's genius: everything he did was technically a kindness. The toast was a kindness. The deadline was a kindness. The knife, when it came, would be presented handle-first, and he'd expect me to thank him for the ergonomics.

I found Wren in the crowd.

She was easy to find, actually, because she'd refused to stand anywhere sensible. The Okafor stall — stall nine, the beating heart of this whole sugar-dusted circus — had been relocated for the festival to the row nearest the bandstand, and Wren stood beside it in her flour-ghosted apron, arms crossed, watching Ambrose with the expression she usually reserved for customers who asked if the hand pies were gluten-free. Which is to say: patient, skeptical, and armed.

When my eyes found hers, she didn't smile. She didn't nod or wink or do any of the things we'd rehearsed — God, we'd actually rehearsed, standing in her kitchen at midnight while she coached me through "looking natural," which is a sentence that should appear in no healthy relationship. She just looked at me. Steady. Like an anchor point on a map. Like someone saying, without moving her mouth: *You know where north is.*

My mouth was dry enough to qualify as a drought condition.

Because here's what nobody tells you about holding evidence of a decade-long fraud committed by your own flesh and blood: the paper doesn't get lighter. Sixteen pages of ledgers, photocopied twice and hidden in three places including Wren's recipe box — her idea, and I still maintain there's poetry in hiding the proof of my family's sins beneath her grandmother's cardamom bun recipe — and they weighed approximately four hundred pounds against my sternum.

Ambrose was still talking. "...and I know that if Elias could see us today, he'd be proud. Proud of what this family has endured. Proud of what we've *protected.*" A pause, calibrated. His eyes swept the crowd and landed on me with the gentle weight of a hand on a shoulder. "Family is the vine, friends. Everything else is just weather."

The crowd applauded. Somewhere near the back, Aunt Bev blew her nose with the force of a small foghorn. Marisol stood off to the side in sunglasses despite the overcast sky, watching me the way a chess player watches a board she thinks she's already solved.

And Ambrose came down the steps, shaking hands, kissing cheeks, working the crowd like the harvest was his personal parade — until he reached me, and his hand closed around mine, and his voice dropped into the register he used only indoors, only for family, only for business.

"You look pale, Lior." Warm. Concerned. A man worried about his nephew's health. "Nerves before the big weekend?"

"I'm fine."

"Mm." He turned to face the bandstand with me, shoulder to shoulder, two Thackerays admiring the bunting. Out here, we were a portrait. "I spoke with Odette this morning. She's feeling much better about things. About *your* decision." A beat. "Midnight, by the way. I mentioned that, didn't I? Midnight tonight. The papers will be at the house. Sign them, and by tomorrow morning all of this—" he gestured at the festival, the town, the hundred years of it, "—stops being your problem. Debt gone. Marisol gone. Your mother's name stays exactly where it belongs: nowhere near the mess. You can go back to whatever city you crawled out of with a clean conscience and a generous settlement, and Junebug Creek gets its vineyard back in responsible hands."

"Responsible hands," I repeated.

"I've been consulting on this property for years, Lior. Nobody knows it better." He smiled at the crowd, waved at the Hendersons, kept talking through the smile. "You could make trouble instead, of course. You could stand up at some point this weekend and start reading numbers aloud. And then everyone learns where the money went, and *who signed for it.*" His eyes flicked toward the VIP seating, where my mother sat in her good pearls, serene as a portrait of serenity. "Your call. You have until midnight. After that, my generosity expires, and I stop being the only person protecting Odette."

He squeezed my hand once more — the cameras from the *Creek Gazette* were clicking — and moved on into the crowd, collecting adoration like it owed him money.

I stood there with my heart going like a drumline and sixteen pages of truth pressed against my ribs, and I thought: *midnight*. He'd given me until midnight on the opening night of the centennial festival, which meant he wanted this finished before the weekend gathered momentum, before anyone had time to think, before I had time to grow a spine in public. Every hour I waited was an hour he spent wondering what I'd do, and Ambrose did not tolerate wonder.

The band struck up something brass-heavy and celebratory. The crowd began to drift toward the stalls, the games, the pie-eating contest that Wren had won three years running and defended with the ferocity of a warlord.

I stayed at the edge of the bandstand, and I did the math I'd been doing for two weeks, and it came out the same way every time: there was no version of midnight that didn't cost someone everything.

Sign, and Ambrose wins, and my mother's complicity stays buried under his ownership of everything my father built — and Wren lives in a town owned, eventually, by the man who'd already called her landlord.

Don't sign, and Ambrose burns it all down, starting with Odette, and possibly with Wren, because a man who manufactures a debt will manufacture anything.

Or — the third option. The one I'd been circling for days like a moth around a bulb. The one Wren had demanded on festival eve, flour on her forearms, chin up, no negotiation: *"No more rehearsed sentences, Lior. Whatever you're going to say, say it spoken, not memorized. Say it like it costs you."*

The pages against my ribs felt less like a bomb now and more like a key. Still heavy. But keys are supposed to be.

* * *

The morning had started badly, which in retrospect felt like thematic consistency.

I'd woken at five in the bakery — yes, the bakery; rule one had been dead for weeks and neither of us attended its funeral — on the cot behind the kitchen with the smell of Wren's first proofing batch already filling the building like a benevolent gas leak. She'd been up since four-thirty, because festival mornings apparently begin before God does, and she'd handed me coffee and said, "Say it again."

"The ledger thing?"

"No. The other thing. The sentence."

I'd held the mug and looked at the floor. "Wren—"

"You've been rehearsing some version of it for ten years. I read the notebook, I know the drafts, I know how many times you've rewritten nine words." She'd wiped her hands on her apron, businesslike, terrifying. "So today, when it matters, you don't get the notebook. You don't get the draft. You get your actual mouth, saying your actual thing, in front of whoever needs to hear it. If you can't do that, don't start. Because half a confession is just gossip with better intentions."

"That's a terrible aphorism."

"It's mine. I'm keeping it." She'd kissed me then — quick, floury, decisive — and said, "Whatever happens at midnight, happen it out loud."

And now it was barely noon, and Ambrose had set his clock ticking in front of the whole town, and Marisol was cutting across the fairground toward me with her lawyer's envelope in her bag and her sunglasses finally off, which meant she wanted me to see her eyes, which meant she wanted something.

"Lior." She stopped close, voice pitched under the band. "Uncle Ambrose looked very pleased with himself just now."

"He usually does."

"He told Mother the sale papers are being drawn for midnight." Her jaw tightened. "He told *me* you'd already agreed. So one of you is lying, and I've known Uncle Ambrose longer than I've known you."

"What do you want, Marisol?"

Her mouth did something complicated. For a second — one strange, human second — she looked less like a schemer and more like a tired woman in her forties who'd spent a year being the only person willing to ask inconvenient questions at family dinners. "I want to know if you found the ledgers."

The band played. A child ran past chasing a runaway balloon. My lungs performed their duties on autopilot.

"Because I found them first," she said quietly. "Eight months ago. I took copies to a lawyer in Ashford, and he told me the case was airtight and unwinnable, because the payments were co-signed." Her eyes went, briefly, to the VIP seats. To our mother. Our mothers. "He said the only person who could blow it open was also the only person who'd be destroyed by it. And then you came home, and I thought — maybe the heir with nothing left to lose would do what I couldn't."

"Marisol—"

"I'm not withdrawing the contest," she said. "Not for Ambrose's deal, not for silence, not for anything. I should have said that weeks ago instead of playing games with lawyers' letters. I was scared, and I handled it badly, and I'm telling you now because in about six hours one of us is going to do something irreversible, and I'd rather it be coordinated." She slid her sunglasses back on. "Midnight, Lior. Whatever you're going to do — do it before he does."

She walked away toward the wine tent, spine straight, and I stood in the middle of the centennial festival holding a secret that was now shared by three people and a folder, and I thought: *well. The conspiracy has a quorum.*

* * *

By late afternoon the fairground had reached full riot. Kids with painted faces. The fire department's dunk tank, featuring, inexplicably, the vice-principal. The cakewalk. Somebody's grandfather winning the pie contest and weeping about it. And stall nine, doing land-office business, Wren working the counter with peach hand pies and festival gossip dispensed in equal measure, because that was always the arrangement: pastry for news, news for pastry, the town's economy running on butter and information.

I bought a pie I didn't eat. She rang me up like a stranger, which was our private joke and also, today, camouflage.

"Midnight," she said, low, sliding my change across the counter.

"Midnight."

"Ambrose gave his little speech."

"He did. Very moving. Clementine the mule made an appearance."

"Every year with the mule." She leaned on the counter, close enough that the flour on her sleeve dusted my cuff, and her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Lior. Look at me."

I looked at her. Brown eyes, steady, the anchor point, north.

"You've got the pages. Marisol's with you — I saw her walk off looking like she'd just defected. Your mother's sitting over there in pearls waiting to find out which of her children survives the night." A breath. "You don't have to decide right now whether you can say it. You just have to decide that when the moment comes, you won't reach for the draft."

"And if my mouth stops working?"

"Then I'll be in the crowd," she said, "looking at you like I'm looking at you now. And you'll remember that somebody down here already knows the worst of it and stayed anyway."

The line behind me cleared its throat, collectively. Wren straightened, snapped back into vendor mode, and called, "Next!" with a smile that could power the ferris wheel.

I walked back into the festival with my pie going cold in my hand and the sun leaning west, and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives when it's least convenient, that the day had stopped being a countdown and become a fuse.

* * *

Dusk came down over Junebug Creek like a curtain between acts.

They strung the lanterns along Main Street at seven — hundreds of them, a tradition dating back, according to the historical society placard, to the town's founding, though probably the founding generation hadn't bought theirs in bulk from a party supply outlet in Ashford. The whole street glowed amber. The band played slower songs now. Couples drifted. Old men argued gently on benches. The air smelled like frying dough and cut grass and, faintly, from the direction of the vineyard on the hill, like grapes getting ready.

At eight, the mayor took the bandstand again for the centennial toast proper — the official one, the one that would go in the newspaper, the one with the commemorative glasses everybody got to keep. The crowd packed in tight. I ended up near the front, because Ambrose had arranged it that way; he'd steered me there with a hand between my shoulder blades, friendly as a cattle chute.

"To Junebug Creek," the mayor said, "one hundred years young!"

"One hundred years!" the crowd roared, glasses up, lanterns swinging, and for one suspended moment the whole town was a single bright organism, and I stood inside it with the truth folded against my chest and thought: *this is what he's buying. Not the vineyard. This. All of it, wholesale.*

Ambrose gave the reply toast, of course. He was standing beside the mayor, glass raised, silver catching the lantern light, and he looked out over the town he'd been quietly draining for a decade and said, "To legacy. To family. To another hundred years — with the right people at the helm."

His eyes found mine on the last word. Held. Smiled.

Then, as the crowd drank, he leaned fractionally toward me, and under the roar of eight hundred voices and a brass band, he said, so quietly it might have been the wind:

"Four hours, Lior. Then I stop asking nicely."

The crowd cheered. The band swelled. Somewhere a child screamed with joy on the tilt-a-whirl.

I raised my glass with everyone else. I even drank. And over the rim of the commemorative cup, past the lanterns, past the banners, past the entire glittering surface of the town that raised me — I found stall nine.

Wren stood at the counter, mid-sale, handing a bag of hand pies to old Mrs. Pruitt, but she wasn't looking at Mrs. Pruitt. She was looking at me. She didn't raise her glass. She didn't nod. She just held my gaze across eighty feet of festival light, steady as a lighthouse, and mouthed four words I had no trouble reading:

*Out loud. No drafts.*

The band finished. The crowd began to move, to eat, to dance, to live the last night of the centennial like it would never end.

I touched the pages through my jacket. Four hours. One sentence. Eight hundred witnesses.

Somewhere up the hill, the papers waited on Ambrose's desk, and somewhere in the crowd, my mother waited in her pearls, and somewhere in my chest, ten years of rehearsal finally, mercifully, began to burn.

I started walking toward the bandstand.
