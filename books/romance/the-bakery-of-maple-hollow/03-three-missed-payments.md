## 3. Three Missed Payments

The bank opened at nine, and Ines Oyelaran arrived at eight-forty with flour still ghosting the creases of her knuckles and a folder of documents she had organized three times in the parking lot of her own bakery.

She had not slept well. She never slept well on the nights before she had to ask anyone for anything—a habit inherited from her mother, who could stretch a pot of stew across four days but would sooner walk into traffic than request an extension on anything, from a bill to a bus fare. *We do not beg,* her mother used to say, ladling soup with the gravity of a woman administering sacraments. *We make do.* Ines had spent thirty-one years making do, and for most of them it had worked. Making do was just another kind of training regimen: discipline applied to want until want grew small enough to fit inside a life.

But making do did not cover three missed mortgage payments, and this morning the folder in her hands contained the arithmetic of failure, tabulated in a font designed by someone who hated joy.

She pushed through the glass doors of the First Kettle Savings & Loan and was immediately enveloped by the smell of carpet cleaner and toner, a scent so aggressively neutral it seemed engineered to remind visitors that nothing here belonged to them. The lobby was all beige and brass. A clock ticked somewhere above the teller windows with the patience of a creature that had never once been hungry.

"Ms. Oyelaran?" The loan officer's name was Douglas Petrie, and he said her name the way people say the names of small towns they've driven through—accurately, without curiosity. He was younger than her, probably, though his haircut made him seem like a man who had been forty since birth. He shook her hand with two pumps of exactly calibrated pressure and led her back to his office, where a motivational poster of a rowing team hung slightly crooked above his desk.

*TEAMWORK,* the poster said. *Coming together is a beginning.*

Ines sat, arranged her folder, and folded her hands over it like a woman presenting evidence at her own trial.

"So," Douglas said, opening her file on his monitor. "Maple Street property, commercial mortgage, originated—" he scrolled, "—six years ago. You're aware you've missed three payments."

"I'm aware." Her voice came out level. She had rehearsed it in the car, in the shower, in the dark at 3 a.m. while proofing tomorrow's dough and doing the math again even though the math never changed. "I'm here to discuss a modification. I have documentation of revenue recovery after the road construction last spring. Foot traffic dropped forty percent during the detour months, but it's rebounding. I have the sales figures."

Douglas clicked through something. Somewhere down the hall a printer groaned to life, laboring over some other person's bad news.

"The figures are impressive," he said, and she believed him, which was worse than if he'd lied. "Honestly. For a single-operator operation in a market this size, your margins are—" He stopped himself, as if the compliment were unprofessional. "But Ms. Oyelaran, I have to be transparent with you. The arrears total eleven thousand four hundred dollars. Even with a modification, we'd need to see demonstrated capacity to absorb that plus the resumed payments. And there's the matter of the balloon clause."

"There's no balloon clause."

"There is if we restructure. That's how modifications work now. We extend the term, lower the monthly, and the remainder comes due at year ten." He turned his monitor toward her with the gentle apologetic motion of a man showing a child a drawing of a dog that has died. A number sat at the bottom of the amortization table. It had five digits before the decimal point. "Or," he said, "we proceed toward default proceedings, which nobody wants."

Ines looked at the number. She thought about the ovens—the big deck oven she'd bought secondhand from a restaurant closing in Harwick, the one that ran hot on the left side so she'd learned to rotate every tray like a dance step. She thought about the espresso machine she'd named Brunhilde. The counter her father had helped her sand and seal the week before he got sick. Eleven thousand dollars. Four hundred dollars a month. She had survived worse numbers; she simply hadn't survived them *yet*.

"What about the letter?" she asked.

Douglas blinked. "What letter?"

"Marchetti Development. They sent me a purchase offer two weeks ago. I assume they've contacted the bank directly. I assume everyone contacts the bank directly except me."

Something shifted in Douglas's face—not guilt exactly, but the professional discomfort of a man whose institution had been having conversations about her behind her back, which, she understood now, was simply what banks did. Conversations about her were happening in conference rooms right now, in cities she'd never visited, between people who had never tasted her cardamom buns.

"They've made an offer on the block," Douglas admitted. "The whole block. Your parcel, the hardware store, the laundromat, the vacant lot by the corner. It's a substantial offer. If the sale closed, the outstanding balance on your mortgage would be satisfied in full at closing." He said it carefully, watching her. "You'd walk away clear, Ms. Oyelaran. No debt. No balloon payment. Possibly money left over."

The word *clear* landed in her chest like a stone dropped into a well. She waited to hear it splash. It kept falling.

"How much left over?"

He told her. It was more money than the bakery had netted in any year of its existence, including the good years, including the festival years when tourists came up from the valley and bought loaves by the armful because the town was pretty and the bread was honest.

"And in exchange," Ines said slowly, "I stop existing on Maple Street."

"In exchange you get liquidity. You get out from under a leveraged asset in a rural market with declining—" He caught her expression and softened. "Look. I'm not supposed to editorialize. But between us? This offer doesn't come along twice. Somebody wants that block badly, and buyers who want badly pay well. You could open anywhere. You're talented. You could—"

"I know what I could do." She heard the edge come into her voice and didn't file it down. "I built what I could do. On Maple Street. With my hands."

Douglas nodded, chastened, and slid a form across the desk. "Take some time. Nobody's forcing anything today. But I'd be negligent if I didn't tell you the default timeline is real. Sixty days from the third missed notice, we initiate formal proceedings. After that it stops being a conversation and starts being a process."

A process. Like weather. Like erosion. Things that happened to landscapes regardless of what the landscape wanted.

Ines took the form. Her signature line waited at the bottom, blank and patient as a held breath.

* * *

She did not go straight back to the bakery. She drove instead to the overlook above the hollow—the gravel pull-off past the water tower where teenagers went to make bad decisions and she went to make calculations—and sat with the engine off and the folder on the passenger seat like a sleeping animal she didn't dare disturb.

From up here Maple Hollow arranged itself into its best self: the church steeple, the green with its bandstand, Main Street curving along the creek like a sentence someone had written with care. And there, third building from the corner, the bakery's chimney putting out its thin gray breath into the cold. From this distance it looked so small. From this distance it looked like something a person could lose.

*Clear,* Douglas had said. *You'd walk away clear.*

She tried it on, the way she'd once tried on a coat in a shop window in the city, back when she was twenty-four and Amara was laughing beside her saying *that's so you, that's so completely you*, and Ines had touched the price tag and put it back on the rack and said *someday*. The coat had been forest green wool, knee-length, absurdly expensive. She had thought about it for a year afterward. She had never gone back for it, because someday was a country you could only reach by walking through every day between here and there, and walking required keeping your pockets empty and your eyes forward.

If she sold, she could buy the coat. She could buy ten coats. She could pay off her mother's roof, the one that leaked over the kitchen where she'd learned to bake. She could sleep through the night. She could stop doing the math.

God, the math. She did it everywhere—in the shower, on the bus of her own skull, standing in line at the co-op. Flour cost X. Butter cost Y. Winter was coming and winter meant gas bills like ransom notes. If she sold the fourth Saturday of every month to festival prep, if she raised prices nine percent, if she cut the afternoon hours—

It was always *if*. Her whole life had become a conditional tense.

And underneath the ifs, quiet as a pilot light, the other voice. The one that sounded, annoyingly, like Amara.

*Ines. Baby. You know what your problem is? You think suffering is a business plan.*

That was what Amara had said to her once, sophomore year of culinary school, sprawled on the floor of their shared apartment surrounded by cost-accounting homework, both of them eating cold rice out of the same container because neither of them had done dishes in a week. Ines had been explaining—for the third time—her plan to graduate debt-free by working doubles at a bistro in addition to classes, and Amara had looked at her with that mixture of love and exasperation that was, in retrospect, the truest thing anyone ever felt for her.

*You think if you just suffer correctly, the universe will owe you something. It won't. It'll just take the suffering.*

"You don't know that," Ines had said. "Discipline works. Discipline is the only thing that works."

"For croissants, sure," Amara said. "For hearts, no."

Amara had been wrong about a lot of things. She'd been wrong about leaving—about the night of their finals, about the job in Singapore, about the way she'd stood in the doorway of their apartment with her suitcase already packed and said *this is huge for me, you understand, you of all people understand*—and Ines had understood, that was the humiliating part, she had understood so completely that she hadn't even fought, just helped carry the suitcase down three flights of stairs and stood waving on the sidewalk like a woman seeing off a soldier, smiling so hard her face ached, and then walked back up to an apartment that suddenly had too much air in it.

But maybe Amara had been right about the suffering.

Ines gripped the wheel. Below her, the bakery breathed its chimney smoke. She had put everything into that building—every dollar, every 4 a.m., every version of herself she'd had to kill to become the woman who could run it alone. Selling wouldn't just erase a debt. It would erase the evidence. It would say: *the discipline didn't work, the dream wasn't load-bearing, the heart was a muscle and you trained it for nothing.*

No. She started the engine. Not yet. There were sixty days. There was a winter festival coming, the biggest foot-traffic weekend of the year. There was a plan to be made, because plans were the only prayer she knew how to say.

* * *

The office at the back of the bakery was really a converted pantry, six feet wide, with a desk her father had built and shelves of binders organized by color because organization was the one luxury she allowed herself. She hadn't deep-cleaned it since spring. Now, with the afternoon lull settling over the front of house—Brunhilde hissing, old Mrs. Halloran doing the crossword in the window seat, the radiator ticking—Ines pulled everything off the shelves and began sorting.

Receipts. Recipes. Insurance papers. A tin of pens that all wrote in different stages of dying. And then, at the back of the lowest shelf, wedged behind the fire-safety binder she'd never needed and always feared, a shoebox.

She knew what it was before she opened it. Her hands knew. They went still on the lid the way hands go still on a door handle outside a room where something is being decided.

Photographs. From culinary school, mostly. Printed in the era when people still printed photographs, back when documenting a moment meant choosing it twice—once to live it, once to keep it. Ines sat down on the floor of her office, in the flour-dusted light, and let herself look.

There she was at twenty-two, in chef whites too big for her, hair braided tight for service, holding up a plated dish with the solemnity of a priest raising the host. Beside her, arm slung around her shoulders, grinning with her whole face: Amara. Amara mid-laugh, Amara stealing a strawberry off the garnish station, Amara asleep on a textbook in the library with a pen still in her hand, a smear of chocolate at the corner of her mouth that Ines remembered licking off with her thumb, and the memory arrived so whole and warm that Ines had to set the photo face-down on her knee for a moment and breathe.

They had been inseparable the way double yolks are inseparable—one shell, two of everything. Amara was the loud one, the brave one, the one who talked their way into kitchens they weren't qualified for and talked their way out of trouble they absolutely deserved. Ines was the quiet engine underneath. People assumed Amara led. People were wrong. Amara flew; Ines aimed her.

And then Singapore. And then the suitcase. And then ten years of Christmas cards that got shorter each year—*Hope all's well! A.*—until even those stopped, and the silence between them hardened into architecture, load-bearing, impossible to remove without bringing down the house.

At the bottom of the box was the last photo. Taken the night before finals, in their apartment, both of them flour-faced at 1 a.m. after a practice bake that had gone gloriously wrong—their laminated dough had shattered, their crème pâtissière had split, and they had eaten the failures standing over the sink, laughing until they cried, and someone—Amara, obviously—had insisted on a selfie to commemorate the disaster. In the photo, Amara was kissing Ines's floury cheek and Ines was caught mid-protest, and written on the back in Amara's looping hand:

*Whatever happens tomorrow — this was the good part. You + me + butter. Always, A.*

Ines held the photograph for a long time. The radiator ticked. Out front, Mrs. Halloran chuckled at her crossword. Somewhere on Maple Street, a truck rumbled past carrying materials for a world that intended to keep going.

*This was the good part.*

Had it been? Or was the good part now—this exact minute, this small room, this failing, fighting, stubborn life that Amara had never seen? Ines found that she couldn't answer, and the not-knowing frightened her more than the bank had.

She put the photos back in the box. She did not throw them away. She put the box back on the shelf, behind the fire-safety binder, where things go that aren't finished with you.

Then she washed her hands, tied her apron, and walked out front to check on the proofing trays for tomorrow—and stopped.

Through the window, across the street, a man was standing in front of the vacant lot by the corner. Good coat. City shoes despite the mud. He had a tablet in one hand and was turning slowly, surveying the block the way a buyer surveys a horse—with appreciation, yes, but also with arithmetic. Even at this distance, even through glass, Ines recognized the posture. It was the posture of the letter. It was the posture of the number Douglas had turned his monitor to show her.

The man lowered the tablet and looked directly at the bakery.

Directly, Ines would have sworn, at her.

He smiled—polite, brief, unreadable—and began walking toward her door.
