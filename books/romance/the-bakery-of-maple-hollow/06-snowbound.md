## 6. Snowbound

The storm was supposed to arrive at midnight.

Ines had planned around it the way she planned around everything—precisely, with margins built in for disaster. The winter festival was Saturday; the bakery's ovens would run all Friday night into Saturday morning, producing four hundred cardamom buns, sixty mince pies, and the honey wreath breads that sold out before nine every year. She'd prepped doughs through the afternoon, stacked sheet pans like ammunition, and taped a hand-lettered sign to the door: *OPEN UNTIL THE SNOW TAKES US.*

By three o'clock, the sky over Maple Hollow had turned the color of dishwater and the light had gone strange and yellow, the way it did before weather decided to stop being polite. By four, the first flakes came down fat and sideways, not falling so much as being thrown.

"Early," said Dot Prewitt from her seat by the window, where she'd been nursing the same cup of coffee since noon. Dot was eighty-one and claimed she could smell a storm in her hip. "Told you. My hip doesn't lie."

"Your hip also predicted rain on my wedding day," Ines said, sliding a fresh bun onto her plate.

"You don't have a wedding day."

"Exactly."

Dot laughed, a dry rustle, and went back to watching the snow erase the street. Outside, Main Street was emptying with the particular speed of a small town that knew when to quit. The hardware store's lights blinked off. Someone dragged the festival's wooden reindeer indoors, all six of them, lashed together like prisoners.

Ines worked. That was the answer to everything, always: work. Knead when your hands wanted to shake. Proof when your chest got tight. There was a discipline to baking that she had built her whole life around—the heart is a muscle, trainable, containable—and the kitchen was its gymnasium. Flour, water, salt, time. Nothing in here could ambush her if she kept moving.

At five-forty, Theo Marchetti came in shaking snow off his good coat.

She'd been expecting him, which annoyed her. He'd been coming every evening for a week now, always near closing, always ordering something small—a coffee, a roll, once just a spoonful of the orange-blossom honey she kept by the register—and lingering with questions about the block that were too careful to be casual. *How long has the building on the corner been vacant? Does the bank own much of this street? Who do the shopkeepers trust?* Questions with ledgers underneath them. She knew what he was. Marchetti Development. The letter was still pinned under the blade of her paper cutter upstairs, face-down, because she couldn't decide whether to frame it or burn it.

Tonight he didn't order. He stood dripping just inside the door, looking out at the white roar, and said, "I think I've made an error."

"What kind?"

"The rental car kind." He held up his phone, screen glowing uselessly against the window light. "I was going to drive back to the inn in Fairview before the roads closed. My GPS says the pass road is already shut. And my phone says—" he turned it toward her, and she saw the battery icon, red, at four percent, "—that it's about to die and take my navigation, my maps, and my ability to call anyone with it."

"So walk," Dot said without turning around. "It's two miles. Builds character."

"It's two miles *uphill into a blizzard*," Theo said.

"That's the character part."

Ines looked at him—at the good coat beaded with meltwater, at the man who represented everything trying to buy her block out from under her—and felt the old reflex rise, the one that said *keep him outside, keep everything outside.* But the wind hit the windows then, a long flat shove of it, and the lights flickered, and Dot's hip, apparently, did not lie.

"The couch in the office pulls out," Ines heard herself say. "It's ugly and it smells like vanilla. You can have it until the plows come through."

Theo looked at her with an expression she couldn't file anywhere—relief, maybe, or something more dangerous than relief. "You're sure?"

"No," she said. "But I'm not heartless."

*Careful,* said some quiet part of her. *That's exactly what you are. It's trained.*

* * *

The snow took the town in layers. First the sidewalks, then the parked cars, then the shapes of things, until Main Street became a suggestion. By seven, the plows had given up; by eight, the power had blinked twice and steadied, and Ines learned that the bakery sat on the same grid as the water treatment plant, which meant they'd keep light as long as the pumps needed it. Small mercies. She put them to work: ovens on, radio low, the festival bake proceeding on schedule because the festival might still happen and because stopping would mean thinking.

Theo turned out to be useful, which was worse than if he'd been useless. He hauled flour sacks without being asked. He learned the word *lame* and used it correctly when she handed him the scoring knife. He stood at her elbow while she shaped buns and watched her hands with an attention that made her skin feel too tight.

"My grandmother baked," he said, somewhere around the third tray. "Not like this. But Sundays, there was bread. She'd let me tear off a piece of dough and make a little snake with it and bake it alongside the loaf, and it was always terrible, and she always ate it like it was the best thing she'd ever tasted."

"Where?"

"Bologna. Until I was six. Then Cleveland, which is not the same thing at all." He scored a bun, crookedly. "This tastes like hers. The cardamom thing. First day I came in. I didn't tell you because it felt like—it felt like saying something true in a room where I'm not supposed to be honest."

"And what are you supposed to be here?" Ines asked, before she could stop herself.

He was quiet long enough that she heard the oven tick. "Consulting," he said finally, and the word had a wall built into it, and she hated that she noticed.

The knock came at nine-fifteen.

Three knocks, frantic, half-swallowed by wind. Ines opened the door to a swirl of white and a figure in it, hooded, gloved, stamping—and even before the hood came down, even before the voice said, "Please tell me this is open, please tell me—" Ines's whole body went cold in a way the storm had nothing to do with.

Amara Osei pulled her hood back. Ten years older than the girl who'd left, hair cropped short, cheeks scoured red, eyes furious and frightened at once.

"My car died," Amara said. "Half a mile out. Walked here because it's the only light on the whole road. Ines. Please."

For a moment neither of them moved. Snow blew in across the threshold and melted instantly on the warm tile, vanishing, like it had never happened.

"You walked," Ines said.

"I knocked," Amara said. "That was harder."

Behind her, Ines heard Theo set down the scoring knife. Heard Dot, gathering her coat by the fire, pause and look between the two women with the unerring radar of an old woman who has watched enough human weather in her life to know a front when she sees one.

"Come in," Ines said, because the alternative was letting the heat out, and the heat was the only thing she had left that was working properly. "Before you freeze. Come in."

Amara stepped inside, and the trap, which had been assembling itself all day—storm, dead phones, closed roads, one lit building on a dark street—snapped shut with a sound only Ines could hear.

* * *

They thawed Amara in stages. Hot towel. Soup from the pot Ines kept going for herself. A chair near the oven, where the radiant warmth made the air shimmer. Amara held her hands around a mug of ginger tea and shook, fine tremors, the body's delayed accounting, and Ines found herself standing too far away and wanting, absurdly, to stand close. Ten years of distance and her arms still remembered the geography.

"I was coming for Mom's surgery," Amara said. "Monday. I left early to beat the storm, which—" she gestured at the window, where the world had ceased to exist, "—clearly worked great. My phone's dead. I don't even know if the hospital's still reachable."

"Landline works," Ines said. "We're on copper. One of the perks of a building the bank considers a liability."

Amara laughed, wet and short. "Still doing that. Jokes like little locks."

"Somebody taught me that."

"Yes," Amara said quietly. "Somebody did."

Theo, to his credit, made himself scarce—back to the ovens, washing trays, giving the two of them a radius of privacy that was itself a kind of eavesdropping. Dot had gone home at seven with her grandson, who'd come for her in a truck with chains, and had squeezed Ines's arm on the way out and murmured, "Interesting evening ahead," with the tone of a woman settling in to watch from memory later.

"So," Amara said, when her hands had stopped shaking. "You're still here. Still—this." She looked around at the bakery with an expression Ines couldn't read: pride, grief, appraisal. "It's beautiful, Ines. It's really—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than intended. "Don't do the tour. Don't admire it from over there. You've been reading about this place for years, apparently. Everyone knows my bakery but nobody knows me."

"That's not fair."

"None of it was fair!" It came up out of her like steam from a proofing box, ten years of it. "You left the night of finals. Not after—during. I stood in that kitchen with our practical in forty minutes and you sent a *text*, Amara. A text. And then you were in Singapore, and then Dubai, and then everywhere I wasn't, and now you're standing in my bakery telling me it's beautiful, and I don't know what you want me to do with that."

Amara set down the mug. Her eyes were bright and steady. "I want you to know I've regretted it every single day. And I want you to tell me why it broke you the way it did, because I've had ten years to build theories and none of them survive contact with how angry you still are."

"Because I loved you," Ines said, and the room went very still, and somewhere behind her a timer began to ring, shrill and stupid, and no one moved to stop it. "Because you were the one person who saw all of it, the whole mess, and you chose the job. And I decided right then that nobody would ever again get close enough to choose away from me. So yes. Little locks. You built the door I live behind. Congratulations on the craftsmanship."

The timer rang on. Theo turned it off. The silence afterward was enormous.

"I didn't know," Amara whispered. "I thought—I thought it was ambition. Yours and mine. We were going to *conquer*—"

"One of us conquered," Ines said. "One of us stayed."

"Ines—"

"Save it." She wiped her hands on her apron, a gesture that meant *work now, feeling later*, her oldest sacrament. "The roads are closed. You're staying. Both of you are staying. There's wine in the back and dough that needs shaping at midnight, and we are going to get through this night like adults, and tomorrow the plows will come and everyone will go back to their lives. Agreed?"

Amara nodded, jaw tight, eyes shining.

From the ovens, Theo said, "Agreed," and his voice was careful in a way that told Ines he understood far more of that conversation than she'd wanted anyone to understand.

* * *

Midnight came and went inside a pocket of impossible coziness. The storm raged; the bakery glowed. They baked the festival bake because there was nothing else to do with their hands. Amara fell into rhythm beside Ines as if no time had passed at all—folding, shaping, knowing where the bench scraper lived without asking—and every effortless synchrony was a small knife and a small comfort at once. Theo fetched, carried, asked questions about fermentation with genuine hunger, and laughed—really laughed—when Amara told the story of the culinary school fire drill that Ines had caused by torching a crêpe suzette.

"Were you two actually good?" he asked. "Together? As a team?"

"We were going to be legendary," Amara said.

"We were going to be *something*," Ines said, and reached for the wine.

They drank it out of mismatched mugs at the worktable, surrounded by rising dough and the smell of cardamom and orange, while the wind threw itself against the building like something that wanted in. Theo's phone died for good at one. Amara's followed at two. The landline hummed its dial tone like a held breath. Three people, one island of light, no exits until morning—or later.

"We should sleep," Theo said eventually. "Shifts. Someone should watch the ovens."

"I'll take first watch," Ines said.

Neither of them moved to leave, though. The three of them sat in the warm yellow light with the storm sealing the doors, and Ines felt the strange, terrible peace of it—the containment failing, the muscle she'd spent five years training pulling against its own regimen, wanting things it wasn't cleared to want. Theo's shoulder a foot from hers. Amara's laugh, rusty, coming easier now. The best and worst night of the decade, arriving all at once, uninvited, snowbound.

Outside, the drift against the front window had climbed past the sill. Inside, Amara poured the last of the wine and said, "Since we're trapped: I think we should tell the truth. All of it. Tonight. While nobody can leave."

Theo looked at Ines.

Ines looked at her hands, flour to the wrists, heart hammering like a mixer on high.

"Start talking," she said.
