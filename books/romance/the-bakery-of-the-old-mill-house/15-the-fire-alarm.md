## 15. The Fire Alarm

Rowan Ashcombe had not slept well in eleven years, but the night the bakery cellar burned, he was awake for all the wrong reasons.

He'd been sitting at his kitchen table with the tin box's surviving contents spread before him—photocopies Tamsin had made, the originals locked in the bookshop safe since the night they'd read them aloud by lamplight—when the smell reached him. Not smoke, not yet. Something sweeter and wrong, like burnt sugar with a chemical edge underneath, drifting through the cracked window on a wind that came from the direction of the mill house.

He was out the door before his boots were fully on.

The town was dark the way small towns are dark at two in the morning—streetlamps spaced like afterthoughts, the river a black ribbon beyond the mill. He ran the length of Bridge Street with his heart hammering in a rhythm he recognized from another life, from the years when sirens meant something and his badge meant he was supposed to be first through the door. The old instinct rose in him clean and sharp, and for one terrible, clarifying moment he didn't feel disgraced at all. He felt like a lawman.

Then he saw the glow behind the mill house's lower windows and stopped feeling anything but fear.

The fire wasn't large. That was the first thing he registered—the wrongness of it. A cellar fire that had caught the old shelving, the paper, the dust of eighty years, and had burned hot and low and *contained*, as if it had been fed just enough to do its work and smothered just before it could spread to the joists above, to the bakery, to the rooms where Tamsin and Juniper slept. The flames were already dying back when Rowan came around the side of the building, and he found the cellar door standing open, orange light pulsing up the stone steps like a slow heartbeat.

"TAMSIN!"

A crash above him. Footsteps. Then Tamsin was in the doorway in his undershirt, hair wild, eyes huge and white in the firelight, and behind him Juniper's small voice, frightened and sleep-thick: *"Papa? What's burning?"*

"Stay with her," Rowan said, and it came out in the voice he'd used a thousand times on doorsteps and dark roads, the voice that made people move. "Stay upstairs. Lock the door. Don't come down."

Tamsin hesitated—one heartbeat, two, his eyes on the cellar steps as if he might bolt past Rowan and into the fire for the tin box, for the letters, for his grandmother's handwriting—and then he nodded once and was gone. Rowan heard the bolt slide home above him, and Juniper's voice muffled through the floor, and only then did he go down into the smoke.

* * *

The cellar was a blackened throat. The fire had taken the corner where they'd stacked the mill house records—the boxes Tamsin had carried down from the attic weeks ago, the ledgers, the correspondence, the fragile paper archaeology of two families' intertwined sins. Rowan found the garden hose spigot where Tamsin had shown him it was, doused what was left, and stood in the hissing dark while steam rolled off char and the smell of wet ash filled his lungs.

He didn't touch anything. That was the second thing he registered, and the discipline of it steadied him. Eleven years out of the work and his hands still knew: don't touch, don't disturb, look first, look longer, look at what *isn't* there.

He had a flashlight in his truck. He went and got it.

The beam found the burn pattern immediately, and Rowan felt the hair on his arms lift. Fire moves like water—it flows down and out and follows fuel—and a paper fire in a cluttered cellar burns in a ragged starburst, hungry fingers reaching in every direction. This wasn't that. This was a tight, deliberate pool of destruction, centered on the records, with clean borders where the flames had simply *stopped*—stopped where the fuel stopped, stopped where someone had arranged the fuel. He crouched. The floorboards at the edge of the burn were dark with something that wasn't ash, a slick rainbow sheen where liquid had pooled and run into the grain before it caught.

He knew that sheen. He'd knelt over it in a barn outside Fairview in his third year as a deputy, and in a garage on Delmore Road, and in the back room of a bait shop that had burned down with a man's debts inside it.

Accelerant. Poured, not splashed. Poured by someone who'd taken their time, who'd known the cellar, who'd known *exactly* which boxes held the letters.

Rowan sat back on his heels in the wet char and let the cold move through him, slow and total, like floodwater filling a cellar of its own. Because there was only one person in this town who would know what those letters meant. Only one person with the reach to have heard that two men were digging through eighty-year-old land records. Only one person who'd sat across a desk from Rowan three weeks ago with a smile like a closed door and said, *Some stones aren't worth turning, Ashcombe. This town likes its history where it lies.*

Councilman Alder Voss.

The name settled into Rowan's mind with the ugly rightness of a key turning a lock. Voss's grandfather had been the county clerk in 1941. Voss's family had held parcels along the river for three generations—parcels that, according to the letters in the tin box, had been carved out of the Ashcombe orchard through a forged survey and a witness paid to disappear. Voss had the motive. Voss had the standing to hear gossip. Voss had looked Rowan dead in the eye and told him, in the language of friendly advice, to stop.

And Rowan had thought, at the time, that it was bluster. Old man's bluster. Political muscle-flexing from a man who'd never had to do more than lean on a zoning board.

He'd been wrong. Voss hadn't been flexing. He'd been *counting*.

Rowan stood, and his knees cracked in the cold, and he looked at the burned corner for a long moment—the wet black ruin of eighty years of paper, the tin box itself sitting scorched but intact at the edge of the burn, its lid warped, its lock sprung. Someone had moved it. Someone had pulled it clear of the worst of it, or it had been sitting far enough out. He made himself not touch it yet. He made himself photograph the scene with his phone, flash after flash stuttering white across the dark, because whatever else he'd lost, he still knew how to build a record.

Then he climbed the stairs and knocked on the bolted door, gently, the way you knock when there's a child on the other side.

* * *

Tamsin opened it before the second knock. He had a robe on now and Juniper's arms were locked around his leg, her face pressed into the fabric, only her eyes visible—huge, wet, fixed on Rowan with the particular terror children reserve for the moment they learn the world can actually break.

"The fire's out," Rowan said. He crouched down, put himself at her level, kept his voice low and even. "It was small, and it's out, and your papa's bakery upstairs is just fine. Not even a scorch mark. You're safe."

"You're the sheriff," Juniper said. It wasn't a question. She'd been calling him that since the festival preparations, since she'd decided with a child's absolute authority that this was what he was, regardless of what the town said he used to be. Her grip on Tamsin's leg loosened by a degree. "Sheriffs stop fires?"

"Sheriffs find out who started them," Rowan said, and heard the words land in the room like a dropped glass.

Tamsin's face went through three expressions in a second—fear, then a flicker of the old guardedness, then something raw and open that Rowan had never seen on him before, not even at the platform, not even in the orchard at midnight. Because Tamsin understood instantly what Rowan understood. Someone had come into their building. Someone had known about the letters. Someone had meant to erase them, and had come *here*, to the place where Juniper slept, with a can of accelerant and a match.

"Was it—" Tamsin started, and stopped. He couldn't say the name. Rowan watched him decide not to say it in front of his daughter.

"Go back to bed, Juniper-bird," Tamsin said softly. "I'll check on you in a minute."

"I want to stay."

"Ten minutes," Rowan said. "Then straight to sleep. Deal?" She studied him with her mother's skeptical eyes, and Rowan had the sudden disorienting thought that he was negotiating treaties with a nine-year-old at three in the morning in a burned-out cellar's doorway, and that he would not trade this moment for anything. She nodded and retreated, walking backward, watching them both until the hallway swallowed her.

The moment she was gone, Tamsin's composure cracked down the middle.

"The letters." His voice came out scraped. "The originals—we moved them to the safe, but the *copies*, the ledgers, the—"

"The copies are gone. The ledgers are gone. Most of the loose papers are gone." Rowan kept his voice flat and factual because Tamsin was one breath from coming apart and one of them had to hold. "The tin box survived. It's scorched, but the letters inside are intact as far as I can see. And Juniper made copies of her own, didn't she? Her careful little transcriptions. Where are those?"

Tamsin blinked. "Her room. She keeps them under her—she calls it her *evidence folder*, she's been—she's been copying them for weeks, she thought it was a treasure hunt—"

"Then we didn't lose them." Rowan said it firmly, and watched it land, and watched Tamsin's shoulders come down half an inch. "Tamsin. Look at me. We didn't lose them."

But Tamsin wasn't looking at him. He was looking past him, down the cellar stairs, at the black wet ruin, and his face was doing something terrible and quiet. "They were *here*," he said. "In my cellar. Under my daughter's room. Someone walked into my home, Rowan. Someone stood where I stand every morning and poured that stuff and lit it, and my daughter was asleep ten feet above, and I didn't hear a thing. I sleep with one ear open. I *always* sleep with one ear open. I didn't hear—"

"Because they were careful." Rowan stepped closer. "And because they knew the building. Tamsin—"

"Who?" It came out cracked down the center. "Who would do this?"

Rowan held his gaze. This was the moment, and he knew it—the moment where he could soften it, could say *I don't know, could be anyone, kids maybe, an accident*, could buy them both a night of sleep with a lie. He had spent eleven years being a man who didn't lie to people he— he stopped the sentence before it finished itself. He had spent eleven years being a man who kept his mouth shut. That was over. It had been over since the platform, since the train left without either of them, since the word *stay* had stopped being a question.

"Alder Voss," he said. "Or someone Voss paid. I can't prove it yet. But I know it the way I used to know things—down in the bones. The accelerant was poured by someone methodical. The burn was contained by someone who didn't want the building destroyed, just the paper. And three weeks ago Voss told me to stop digging, and I told you, and we kept digging anyway. This is what that costs."

Tamsin sat down. Not gracefully—his legs simply stopped holding him, and he ended up on the top cellar step with his head in his hands, breathing. Rowan lowered himself beside him. The stone was cold through his jeans. The smell of wet ash hung over everything, and somewhere above them Juniper's floor creaked as she got back into bed, obedient to the ten minutes, trusting them.

"My grandmother," Tamsin said finally, into his hands. "Her letters survived. She survived everything else—she outlived them all, she kept that box locked for sixty years, she *knew* what happened and she was too afraid to say it, and now some man with a county sticker on his truck burns what's left of her voice because it's inconvenient." He lifted his head. His eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely furious, and Rowan thought: *there he is. There's the man under all that quiet.* "I spent seven years running from something I didn't even do, Rowan. Seven years being small and careful and invisible. I am *done* being careful."

"I know," Rowan said.

"You knew who it was and you didn't tell me. You told me Voss leaned on you and I—" Tamsin stopped. Rowan watched him take inventory of his own anger and find most of it aimed inward. "No. You told me. I just didn't believe it would come *here*."

"Neither did I." Rowan rubbed his face. "That's on me. I've spent eleven years believing that if you keep your head down, the world leaves you alone. It's the same lesson you learned, just a different classroom. And it's a lie. It was always a lie. Some people don't stop because you stop."

The wind moved through the open cellar door below them, stirring the ash smell, carrying the river sound up from the dark. Tamsin leaned slightly, just his shoulder against Rowan's shoulder, the smallest point of contact, held there like a hand on a rail.

"What do we do?" Tamsin asked.

* * *

What they did, in the next two hours, was the work Rowan's hands remembered.

He called the fire department's non-emergency line at four a.m. and woke Chief Braddock, who'd been a volunteer when Rowan was a deputy and who owed Rowan from a flood year, and got him out to the cellar with a camera and a grudge against being awake. Braddock crouched over the accelerant pour patterns and said, "Somebody knew what they were doing," and looked at Rowan with a question in his face, and Rowan said, "You know what I used to do," and Braddock said, "Yeah," and didn't ask anything else, which was its own kind of loyalty.

They documented everything. The pour patterns. The point of entry—the cellar door's hasp, old and rusted, forced with a flat tool and closed again after, which meant the arsonist had wanted the fire discovered late but not *too* late, had wanted the records gone but the building standing, had wanted it to look like bad wiring, like old paper, like accident. Rowan stood in the doorway and looked at the careful, closed door and felt his blood go cold all over again, because that wasn't a drunk with a grudge. That was a man covering his tracks in advance. That was a man who'd thought about what the sheriff—what the *former* sheriff—would see when he came down the stairs.

Voss knew him. Voss knew exactly what he'd see.

At dawn, Tamsin made bread. Rowan stood in the bakery kitchen watching him do it—watching him slam the dough down with a violence the gentle man had never shown in daylight, watching him work the fury into the gluten until his breathing evened out—and understood that this was how Tamsin prayed, or fought, or both. Juniper came down in her pajamas, took one look at the ash smell on both of them, and asked no questions. She went to her evidence folder instead, pulled out her careful pencil transcriptions—*Letter the Third, Grandmama Ivy to Mr. Edmund Ashcombe, March 1942*—and laid them on the counter between the flour tin and the salt like a small, solemn offering.

"They can't burn these," she said. "I wrote them in my own hand. That makes them mine."

Rowan looked at her—at this fierce, precise, nine-year-old archivist—and something in his chest turned over and resettled. "No," he said. "They can't burn these."

By seven, the plan had shape. Braddock's report would say electrical fault, because Rowan asked him to, because the moment Voss heard the word *arson* he'd know how much they knew and would go deeper underground. The surviving letters would go somewhere Voss couldn't reach—not the bookshop safe, not the bank, somewhere no one would think to look. Rowan had a call to make to a man he hadn't spoken to since his resignation, a state archivist named Okafor who owed him a favor and owed Voss nothing. And then there was the question of the fire itself—because accelerant leaves a purchase trail, and a small town has exactly three hardware stores, and Rowan had spent six years learning how to work a county's worth of receipts.

"Tamsin." He said it quietly, while Juniper was in the back room feeding her evidence folder into a school backpack. "There's something you need to tell me. About the money. About why you were short last month, and why you've been watching the road like you're expecting a car."

Tamsin's hands went still on the dough.

Rowan held up a hand before he could speak. "Not now. Not with her here. But tonight, after she's asleep, you're going to tell me everything—everything you've been carrying alone since before I met you—because someone just burned a fire under your daughter's bedroom, and I can't protect either of you from a man like Voss if I'm working with half a map." He kept his voice gentle, but he didn't look away. "I'm not asking as a partner on a deed, Tamsin. I'm asking as the man who's going to stand between this town's worst ghost and your family. Whatever it is. Whatever you did or didn't do, whoever's been squeezing you—*everything*."

Above the ovens, through the window, the sun was coming up over the orchard, gilding the bare branches, lighting the frost on the grass where a stranger had walked in the night with a can in his hand. Tamsin looked at it, and then at Rowan, and Rowan watched seven years of silence come to the end of their road in the man's face.

"Tonight," Tamsin said.

Rowan nodded once, and went to make the call that would drag a dead man's name out of a state archive and into the light—because this was no longer paperwork, and it had not been for a long time. This was a fight. And Rowan Ashcombe, who had lost everything once by staying silent, intended to win this one out loud.
