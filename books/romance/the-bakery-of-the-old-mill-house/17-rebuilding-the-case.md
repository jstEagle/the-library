## 17. Rebuilding the Case

**Tamsin**

The kitchen table had become a war room.

Tamsin stood at the head of it at seven in the morning, before Juniper woke, before the ovens needed tending, and looked down at what remained of his grandmother's life spread across the scarred pine: eleven letters that had survived the fire, their edges charred to lace; a photograph of two men standing in front of the mill house in 1968, squinting into a sun neither of them could have known would outlast them; and Juniper's school notebook, opened to a page of careful, ruled handwriting.

She had copied everything. Every date, every name, every half-legible line her father read aloud to her by lamplight while the smell of smoke still clung to the cellar stairs. She'd done it without being asked, the way children do the thing adults are too afraid to begin. When he'd found her at the table the morning after the fire, pencil worn to a stub, she'd looked up and said, "If they burn again, we'll still have them."

He hadn't been able to speak for a full minute.

Now he laid the letters in chronological order for the fourth time, because order was the only weapon he had. Elias Ashcombe's hand was sharp and slanted, a man writing fast, writing angry. Margaret Winterbourne's was round and patient, a woman choosing every word like she was setting it in stone. Between them, across four years of correspondence, a story assembled itself the way bread rises—slowly, invisibly, until suddenly it had structure and you couldn't unsee it.

*The parcel north of the creek,* Elias had written in March 1971. *Forty acres. Hollis drew up papers transferring it to the county trust "for maintenance," but there is no trust, Margaret. I've been to the recorder's office twice. There is no record of any trust.*

And Margaret's reply, weeks later, her handwriting tighter than usual: *Councilman Voss came to the house. He asked what Elias has told me and whether I understood how a widow's testimony can be... complicated. He was polite. They are always polite.*

Polite. Tamsin touched the word through the protective plastic sleeve Rowan had insisted on. He knew about polite men. He'd spent six years learning that the people who ruined you rarely raised their voices.

The cellar fire had taken nine letters whole and chewed the edges off these. But it had also left something behind—or rather, whoever set the fire had. Rowan had found it two days after, sifting the ash with gloved hands and a former lawman's patience: a corner of a gasoline can's label, and pressed into the soot near the back wall, a boot heel print with a distinctive crescent-shaped gouge. Rowan had gone very quiet looking at it. Then he'd said, "I know this boot," and driven to the county seat without explaining, and come back at midnight with a face like weather coming.

Today he was due at eight. Tamsin put water on to boil and tried not to count the minutes, which was absurd, because he'd stopped pretending to himself somewhere around the night on the platform. He knew what Rowan's truck sounded like now. He knew the particular way the screen door stuck and the particular sound Rowan made when it gave—a small huff, almost a laugh, as if the door were an old friend being difficult.

At 7:52, Juniper padded down in her socks, hair wild, clutching the notebook against her chest like a shield.

"Is he coming?" she asked.

"He's coming."

"Good." She climbed onto her chair and smoothed the notebook flat. "I finished the cross-reference. The dates where Grandmargaret writes about Voss? I marked them yellow. There's one from every year except 1973."

Tamsin stared at her. "You cross-referenced them."

"You said lawyers like patterns." She shrugged, eight years old and terrifying. "There's a pattern."

There was a pattern. He could see it even before Rowan arrived—the annual pressure, the annual silence, Margaret holding the line alone for years while Elias's letters grew shorter and more desperate, until the last surviving one, dated October 1974, contained only five lines:

*They have taken everything but my name. If anything happens to me, look to the creek parcel and to who profits when the highway comes through. M — tell your granddaughter I'm sorry I couldn't be braver.*

Tamsin had read that line forty times. *Tell your granddaughter.* As if Elias had known, somehow, across the gap of fifty years, exactly who would end up holding these pages.

The screen door stuck. Huffed. Gave.

Rowan came in carrying a cardboard file box under one arm and cold air and sawdust on his jacket, and stopped when he saw the table—the letters ranked like soldiers, the photograph, the notebook with its yellow flags.

"You've been busy," he said.

"We've been busy," Juniper corrected, not looking up.

Rowan's mouth did the thing it did around Juniper—the softening he probably thought no one noticed. "Yes, ma'am. My apologies." He set the box down with a thump that meant weight. "I spent yesterday on the phone with people I swore I'd never call again."

* *

**Rowan**

The box held twenty-two years of a career he'd tried to bury, and Rowan lifted the lid on it feeling like a man opening his own grave.

"Delia Marsh," he said. "Records clerk in the county seat, back when I wore the badge. She kept copies of things. Everything, actually—she was the kind of woman who believed paper was the only honest witness there was." He pulled out a folder, thick, tabbed in Delia's blocky capitals. "When I resigned, she sent me a card that said *the truth keeps*. I thought it was just kindness. Turns out she meant it literally."

Tamsin took the folder with both hands, careful, the way he handled everything that mattered—as if it might be taken from him. Rowan watched him open it and go still.

Inside were certified copies. Deed transfers going back to 1969. The formation papers of the "Mill Creek Land Trust"—filed, Rowan had confirmed yesterday, by a firm whose senior partner had been Aldous Voss's brother-in-law. And beneath all of it, the crown of Delia's hoarding: a handwritten affidavit, signed and notarized in 1975, from a surveyor named Peter Lomax stating that he had been instructed to alter the boundary maps of the creek parcel and that he had refused, and that he had then been removed from the county contract list and never worked in the region again.

"There was a witness," Tamsin said quietly. "A real one. Notarized."

"A silenced one." Rowan sat down heavily. "Lomax died in '89. But his affidavit didn't. It's been sitting in a filing cabinet in Delia's garage for thirty years because nobody ever asked the right question. Nobody connected a dead surveyor to a land trust to—" he gestured at the letters, at the photograph of the two young men in front of the mill house, "—to any of this. Why would they? Everyone who cared is dead."

"Not everyone," Juniper said.

Rowan looked at her. She was watching him with Margaret Winterbourne's level gray eyes, and something in his chest turned over.

"No," he agreed. "Not everyone."

They worked through the morning. It went better than Rowan had any right to expect, given that six months ago he'd stood in this same room accusing this same man of fraud. That was the thing about Tamsin he'd learned and kept relearning: he worked like someone who had practiced being careful his whole life. He read a document twice before commenting. He wrote in pencil. When he disagreed with Rowan's reading of a deed clause, he didn't argue—he laid his copy beside Rowan's and said, "Read the third line again," and let the paper make the argument for him.

By noon they had it shaped into a spine: the theft of the creek parcel, the fake trust, Voss's family fingerprints on the paperwork, Lomax's refusal and ruin, Elias Ashcombe's increasingly desperate letters, and Margaret Winterbourne's long, costly silence—she had kept the evidence safe at the price of her friend's good name, and Rowan could hear in her rounded handwriting how much that price had cost her.

"And then there's you," Rowan said.

Tamsin's hands stopped moving.

"The accusation," Rowan said. "The one you ran from. If we're doing this—if we're standing up in front of the whole town on festival day and saying *the record was wrong*—then yours goes in the same pile. Same disease. Paperwork that says a thing that isn't true."

"I don't have affidavits," Tamsin said. His voice was flat, controlled, the voice of a man holding a door shut. "I have a police report I was never allowed to answer. A fire marshal's finding that contradicted the arson charge. And a lawyer who told me pleading out was cheaper than fighting."

"You have more than that." Rowan reached into the box and brought out the last folder, thinner than the others, and set it on the table between them. "I called Delia about this too. Your case was filed in Harrow County. Delia's sister works records there."

Tamsin looked at the folder the way a man looks at a dog that has bitten him.

"I didn't read it," Rowan said. "I want to be clear about that. Delia's sister pulled it sealed, and I told her to keep it sealed until you say otherwise. It's yours. I'm not going to open a door you haven't chosen to walk through." He leaned back. "But I'll tell you what I know from the outside, because I've spent three days checking, and you should know I checked. The fire at the Meridian Street warehouse—March, six years ago. The arson charge rested entirely on one deputy's timeline, and that deputy was later named in two separate misconduct filings. The fire marshal's own report listed the origin point as electrical. Those two documents have been sitting in different drawers of the same building for six years, contradicting each other, and nobody ever put them side by side."

"Because nobody asked the right question," Tamsin said. His voice had gone rough.

"Because nobody asked," Rowan said. "Same as Lomax. Same as Elias." He tapped the table, once, softly. "It's the same disease everywhere, Tamsin. Paper says what powerful people pay it to say, and it stays wrong until somebody with nothing left to lose stands up and reads it out loud."

Juniper had stopped copying. She was watching them both, pencil poised, and Rowan realized with a jolt how much of this she'd already absorbed—how much children absorb, always, whether or not anyone intends them to.

"Dad," she said carefully. "If we fix Grandpa Elias's name. Can we fix yours too?"

The silence stretched. Rowan watched Tamsin's throat work. Watched him look at his daughter—at the notebook full of names she'd copied in her careful hand, at the yellow flags marking every year a powerful man had pressured a woman into silence—and Rowan watched the exact moment the door inside him swung open.

"Yes," Tamsin said. It came out cracked. He cleared his throat and said it again, steadier, like setting a stone in a wall. "Yes. We can fix mine too."

**Tamsin**

That evening, Tamsin opened the folder.

He did it alone, upstairs, after Juniper was asleep, with Rowan downstairs in the bakery keeping the ovens company—which was to say, keeping Tamsin company from a respectful distance, the way he'd learned to. The folder wasn't heavy. Six years of his life weighed less than a loaf of bread.

He read it straight through in forty minutes, and it was worse and better than he remembered. Worse, because seeing it in cold type stripped away the fog of memory and left only the machinery: the deputy's statement, the district attorney's filing, the speed of it all—charged within nine days, plea offered within twelve. Better, because Rowan was right. The contradictions were right there. The fire marshal's electrical finding. The deputy's timeline that required the fire to start ninety minutes before the building's power was even connected. Any competent reading tore the case apart. He'd never been able to afford a competent reading.

He also found, tucked at the back, something he hadn't known existed: a supplemental incident report, filed by a responding officer whose name he'd never heard, noting that a witness at the scene had described seeing a vehicle leaving the alley behind the warehouse twenty minutes before the alarm—an observation that appeared nowhere else in the file. Someone had written it down and it had gone into a drawer and died there.

Paper was the only honest witness. It just needed someone to call it to the stand.

He carried the folder downstairs. Rowan was at the big oak counter, sleeves rolled, flour to his elbows—not baking, Tamsin realized, just working dough for the sake of his hands, the way Tamsin himself had once done to keep his name quiet. Two men, same remedy.

"It's enough," Tamsin said. He set the folder down. "With Delia's copies and Juniper's transcriptions and the letters—it's enough. For the grandfathers, for the land, and for me. If we present it right. If we present it all at once, publicly, so it can't be quietly buried again."

Rowan wiped his hands. "Voss won't take it lying down."

"No. He'll do what polite men do." Tamsin heard his own voice come out steady and was surprised by it. "But he's been polite for fifty years, Rowan. Politeness only works on people who agree to be quiet."

Rowan looked at him across the counter, flour-dusted lamplight between them, and something passed over his face that Tamsin had learned to read as pride trying to disguise itself as something more casual.

"When did you get brave?" Rowan asked.

"About eleven seconds ago," Tamsin said. "Ask me again tomorrow and see if it holds."

"It'll hold." Rowan said it like a fact, like a deed entry, like something recorded. Then he nodded at the folder. "We'll need it typed clean. Sworn statements from Delia. And we'll need to decide who speaks first on Saturday—because once you stand up in front of this town and say your real name, there's no taking it back."

Tamsin thought of the platform. Of the train pulling away without him. Of Juniper's yellow flags, row on row, a child's patience outlasting a town's silence.

"I've been rehearsing it for six years," he said. "I just didn't know the venue."

Rowan smiled—rare, unguarded, gone quickly—but before either of them could say another word, headlights swept across the kitchen window above them. A car, slowing on the lane. Stopping.

Rowan's smile faded into something older and harder. He moved to the window without hurrying, and Tamsin watched his shoulders change, watched six years fall off him and the sheriff come back.

"That's the councilman's car," Rowan said quietly. "Two days early. He wants to talk before Saturday."

Through the glass, below, a door slammed in the dark.
