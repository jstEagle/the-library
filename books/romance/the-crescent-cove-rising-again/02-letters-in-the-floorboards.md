## 2. Letters in the Floorboards

The cottage had a smell that no amount of sea air could explain — cedar and old varnish and something faintly sweet, like apples left too long in a bowl. Coral stood in the middle of the front room on her second morning with a crowbar in one hand and a cup of black coffee going cold on the windowsill, and she thought: *this is what a life looks like when it stops being lived.*

She hadn't come to Crescent Cove to renovate. That was the story she told people — her agent, her mother, the woman at the ferry office who'd looked at her a half-second too long — but stories were just lies with better posture. She'd come because the cottage was hers now, because Grandma June had left it to her in a will that read like an apology, and because there was nowhere else on earth where nobody knew her name.

Except, of course, everybody knew her name. That was the whole problem with names.

*Three years,* she reminded herself, prying up the first board. *Three years since the stage, since the bow skittered off the string like a thing possessed, since four thousand people watched Coral Thornbury's hands forget how to be hands.* The video still lived somewhere online. She'd never watched it. She never would.

The floorboard came up with a groan and a puff of dust that made her sneeze twice. Underneath: dirt, a dead spider curled like a comma, and nothing else. Fine. This was fine. This was exactly the kind of mindless demolition work she needed — work that asked nothing of her except force, work that couldn't judge her tempo or her tone or the way her left hand trembled when she thought about fingerboards.

She worked through the morning. Board by board along the north wall, where the joists showed water damage from some long-ago winter. Her grandmother had kept the place standing through stubbornness and duct tape, and Coral was discovering that stubbornness only went so deep. By noon her palms were blistered and her hair was full of sawdust and she felt, for the first time in months, almost quiet inside.

It was the eleventh board — third from the window, under where a rocking chair used to sit — that sounded different. Hollow.

She knelt. Ran her palm over the wood. Tapped it. Hollow again, but not rot-hollow. *Constructed*-hollow.

For a long moment she didn't move. Old houses kept things. She knew this. Coins and letters and once, in her childhood, a shoebox of photographs under the porch that had turned out to be pictures of strangers — her grandmother's first life, before the cove, before any of them. Coral had learned early that June Thornbury was a woman with compartments.

But this compartment was under the floor of the room where Coral had slept every summer of her childhood. This was *her* floor.

She got the flat bar under the edge and levered. The board lifted easier than the others, as if it had been waiting to be asked.

Beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a tin. A biscuit tin, blue and gold, the kind that used to sit on her grandmother's counter full of sewing supplies. Except this one was heavy, and when Coral shook it, papers shifted inside with a dry whisper.

She sat back on her heels in the ruined floor and did not open it.

That was the strange part, later — that she didn't open it right away. She carried the tin to the kitchen table, set it down beside the cold coffee, washed her hands, made lunch she didn't eat, and stared at the thing for the better part of two hours while the light moved across the table like a slow hand.

Because she already knew. God help her, she already knew whose handwriting was under that lid. She'd known from the moment she touched the oilcloth, from some animal instinct she couldn't explain and didn't want to examine. Some part of her had been waiting three years for the past to find its way back to her, and here it was, delivered by floorboard, patient as tide.

She opened it at four o'clock, when the light had gone amber and the wind had picked up outside, rattling the loose shingles like applause.

Letters. Dozens of them. Envelopes, mostly unsealed, addressed in her own handwriting.

*To the girl with the broken bow.*

Coral's breath went out of her all at once.

She knew that phrase. Of course she knew it. It was what the newspapers had called her — *the girl with the broken bow* — after the collapse, after the video, after the doctors found nothing wrong with her hands and the critics found everything wrong with her nerve. It had been cruel then, a headline doing what headlines do. But somewhere in the worst year, the phrase had turned over in her chest and become something else. Something private. Something she'd started calling herself in the dark, the way you'd speak to a child who'd fallen down — *there, there, girl with the broken bow, get up, get up.*

She had written letters to herself. She remembered now, in pieces, the way you remember a fever. The winter after the collapse, holed up in a rented apartment in Boston, drinking too much and sleeping at noon. Writing pages she never intended anyone to see — least of all herself. Writing to the girl she'd been on stage, the one frozen mid-phrase with four thousand eyes on her, because someone had to say something kind to that girl and Coral couldn't bear to say it in her own voice, face to face, in the mirror.

So she'd written letters. And apparently — apparently — she'd brought them here. Or some of them. Had she? She couldn't remember bringing them. Couldn't remember hiding them. But her grandmother's house had been the one place she'd fled to, that first awful spring, before she'd retreated fully into silence. Three days here, she remembered. Three days of sleeping in this room, of Grandma June leaving soup outside her door without a word.

And then she'd gone back to Boston and buried the letters under the floor, or maybe June had — no. The handwriting was Coral's. The envelopes were sealed in Coral's careful, slanted script, the one teachers used to praise. *To the girl with the broken bow.* Over and over, a dozen times, like a prayer repeated until it lost meaning and then, deeper down, found it again.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed this with clinical detachment, the way she'd once noticed everything about her hands, back when noticing was her profession.

She took out the top letter. Unsealed. Dated — she checked — fourteen months after the collapse.

*Dear girl,*

*I don't know if you're reading these or writing them or both, and I think that's the point. I think we're the same person having a conversation across a gap I can't close. Here is what I know: your hands are fine. The doctors said so. Everyone keeps saying so, as if it's good news, as if the problem were ever your hands.*

*Here is what I also know: you were afraid before you ever walked on that stage. You were afraid for years and you called it excellence. You called it preparation. You called it being serious about the music. You practiced until the fear had nowhere to live except the moment itself, and then you handed the moment four thousand witnesses.*

*Nobody collapsed. Let's be exact about this, because exactness is all we have. Your body stopped. There's a difference. Bodies stop when they're protecting something. What were we protecting?*

*I keep coming back to the summer I was nine. The recital at the church hall. Do you remember? You played the Swan and forgot the middle section and Mrs. Halloran kept conducting and you just — stopped. Stood there. And afterward Grandma June said, "Well. Now you know the worst thing that can happen, and it happened, and you're still you."*

*You're still you. That was supposed to be the cure. Instead you spent twenty years making sure the worst thing never happened again, which is another way of saying you spent twenty years dying slightly, every day, in advance.*

*I don't write this to hurt you. I write it because no one else will. Everyone else wants you fixed. I'm the only one who wants you true.*

*— C.*

Coral put the letter down very carefully, the way you'd put down something sleeping.

The kitchen was dimming around her. Outside, the wind had risen, and the first spit of rain ticked against the window. Somewhere down the lane a dog barked twice and gave up, and the sound of the cove — that low, constant conversation between water and stone — filled the space the dog left behind.

*Everyone else wants you fixed. I'm the only one who wants you true.*

She had forgotten she was capable of that sentence. That was the cruelest part. Somewhere in the last three years of silence and small safe jobs — teaching a handful of students, playing weddings under a false name once, once only, fleeing the reception when a guest asked her to play something *uplifting* — she had forgotten that the woman who wrote those words was also her. Not a former self. Not a casualty. Her.

She read the next letter. Then the next. She read them in order, and the order mattered, she realized — they traced a descent and then, near the bottom of the tin, a leveling out. The early ones were knives. The middle ones were grief. The last few were quieter, almost tender, the voice of someone who had argued herself to a standstill.

The final letter was different. Shorter. Sealed, unlike the others, though the seal had never been licked — the flap tucked in, waiting.

*Dear girl,*

*I'm putting these away now. Not because we're finished talking but because talking isn't the same as living, and I've been living in this tin long enough. One day you'll find these — I don't know when, I don't know how — and I want you to know that I forgive you. Not for collapsing. There's nothing to forgive there. I forgive you for the twenty years of dying slightly. I forgive you for mistaking fear for discipline. I forgive you for every safe choice you made with your beautiful ruined caution.*

*Come home. Wherever that turns out to be.*

*— C.*

Coral sat in the darkening kitchen with an unsealed letter in her lap and rain beginning in earnest against the glass, and she understood that her past had not been waiting for her. It had been *waiting for her to be ready*, and it had chosen its moment with a cruelty or a kindness she couldn't determine — here, in her grandmother's house, in the town where she'd come to hide, three days after she'd arrived with a crowbar and a plan to fix floors instead of anything that mattered.

Grandma June must have known. Must have seen her tuck them away, or found them after, and put them back — kept them safe under the boards of the room where Coral slept, all those summers, all those years. Kept them the way she'd kept everything: without comment, without judgment, in compartments, waiting.

"You could have just told me," Coral said aloud, to the empty kitchen, to the dead woman whose soup she still remembered on her tongue. Her voice cracked on the second word. "You could have just *said*."

The house said nothing. Houses are fluent in silence; so, lately, was she.

She gathered the letters, squared their edges against the table, and slid them back into the tin — all but the last one, the sealed one, which she held a moment longer. *Come home.* She tucked it into her shirt pocket, over her heart, which was either sentiment or superstition and probably both.

Then she got up, lit the lamp because the light was failing, and stood looking at the ruined floorboards, the exposed joists, the dark gap in the floor where eleven boards used to be.

Tomorrow she'd put the floor back together. That was the practical thought, the grief-disguised-as-practicality thought, and she reached for it gratefully. Nails, boards, hammer. Work that asked nothing of her except force.

But underneath the practical thought, in the hollow place where the letters had been, another thought was stirring — unwelcome, insistent, beating against its boards.

*You're still you.*

And if that was true — if the girl in the letters and the woman in the kitchen were the same person, separated only by three cowardly years — then the cello case upstairs, the one she'd been circling since she arrived, the one under the dust sheet in the locked back room that she had not yet been able to make herself open —

That case wasn't going to stay closed forever either.

Outside, the storm rolled in off the water, and somewhere down the coast, low and steady, a foghorn began to speak.

* * *

She dreamed of the stage that night. She always dreamed of the stage, but tonight the dream was different — tonight, in the dream, when her hands stopped, the hall didn't gasp. A single voice in the dark said, *take your time, girl with the broken bow,* and it was her own voice, and it was also, somehow, her grandmother's, and she woke at dawn with wet eyes and the taste of salt she couldn't blame on the sea air.

The tin sat on the table where she'd left it. The floor gaped. And through the window, over the gray water, she could see the neighboring house — the one that had stood empty for years, the one with lights on now, and smoke rising from a chimney, and a man splitting wood in the yard with slow, even strokes, as if he had all the time in the world and something to prove anyway.

Coral watched him longer than she meant to.

Then she picked up the crowbar, and went back to work.
