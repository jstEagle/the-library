## 5. Salt and Sulfur

*Elodie*

The bank sent a checklist. Of course it did. Banks are incapable of human emotion except in the form of laminated documents, and the one Julian's office had forwarded me read like a love letter written by a machine that hated both of us: *Soil samples, Block A through F. Irrigation assessment. Structural review of outbuildings. Borrower to accompany appraiser at all times.*

Borrower to accompany appraiser at all times.

I read that line four times before the meaning landed like a wasp in a wine glass. They were sending him. Not a junior loan officer with a clipboard and a sympathy face—him. Julian Reyes, regional director, keeper of my foreclosure and my broken heart, would be walking my vineyard rows for six hours, close enough to touch, far enough to pretend neither of us wanted to.

I considered calling in sick to my own life. I considered sending Marco, my one part-time hand, who is seventy-one and would have charmed the appraisal right off the books. But the checklist said *borrower*, and I am, apparently, still a borrower, so on Thursday morning I put on my least pathetic flannel and waited at the tasting room door like a woman meeting a blind date arranged by a vengeful god.

He arrived at eight sharp in a car too clean for this road. He got out wearing khakis and a bank-issued polo, and I want you to understand the violence of that image: Julian Reyes, who once came to dinner smelling of diesel and salt, who used to say he'd rather be gutted by a halibut than wear a collar, standing in my gravel lot dressed like middle management.

"Nice polo," I said.

"It breathes," he said.

"That's what they told you, huh."

Something flickered across his face—the ghost of the boy who used to laugh at my jokes before he decided I wasn't worth laughing with anymore. Then it was gone, filed away behind the professional blankness of a man carrying a tablet.

"We should start with the soil samples," he said. "Block A first."

"After you," I said, and hated how much it sounded like surrender.

* * *

*Julian*

She looked exactly like herself, which was the cruelest possible outcome. I'd spent eleven years editing her in memory—softening her, sharpening her, making her into whatever the current season of my resentment required. The real Elodie Fairweather, standing in her gravel lot in a flannel shirt with a hole near the cuff, ruined all of it. She was thinner. Her hands were wrecked in a way that told me everything about the last decade without her saying a word. And she still looked at me like I was a bill she intended to dispute.

Block A ran along the eastern slope, the old Pinot Noir, planted by her grandfather before either of us existed. She walked it like she owned the ground itself, which technically she didn't anymore—the bank and I had opinions about that—but she walked it anyway, boots sure on the rows, and I followed with my soil probe feeling like exactly what I was: an intruder with credentials.

"Pull from the third row," she said. "Second row drains weird since the culvert washed out in '19. You'll get bad numbers and write something insulting about my drainage."

"I don't write insults. I write findings."

"In my experience those are the same document with different fonts."

I pulled from the third row. She was right about the drainage; she was right about everything, which was another thing I'd forgotten to hold against her and now remembered all at once. We worked down the slope in something adjacent to silence, and the silence had texture—it had salt in it, and sulfur, and the particular mineral weight of two people remembering the same hillside for different reasons.

Because this was where we kissed. First time, the summer we were nineteen, right there at the head of row four, after she'd dared me to eat a grape so sour it rearranged my face. She'd laughed so hard she had to grab my shoulders to stay upright, and then neither of us was laughing, and then—

"You're staring at row four," she said.

"I'm assessing row four."

"Assess it harder, then. It gets nervous."

I drove the probe into the earth and pretended my hands were steady. "The vines look good," I said, because they did, because someone had kept them good through ten years of whatever she'd been surviving, and I needed her to know I'd noticed even if I couldn't say why it mattered to me.

"They should. I've bled on every one of them." She said it lightly, but it landed heavy, and for a second her guard slipped and I saw underneath—not the girl I'd left, but the woman who'd stayed, which was somehow worse. "You wouldn't know anything about that, though. You left before the bleeding started."

There it was. Eleven years of rehearsal on her side too, apparently, delivered casual as weather.

"Elodie—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, dirt under every fingernail. "We're working. Let's work."

So we worked. And God help me, it was almost easy. That was the trap of her, always had been. We fell back into banter like water finding a channel worn into stone—her needling me about the polo, me needling her about the tasting room's "Vineyard Closed, Ask Me Why" sign, her explaining that the sign had reduced awkward tourist questions by forty percent and she stood by it as a business decision. By the time we reached Block C I was laughing. Actually laughing. And somewhere between laughs I realized I'd stopped being angry for a full ninety seconds, and that frightened me more than the foreclosure ever had.

Because if I stopped being angry, I'd have to ask the real question. The one I'd carried across eleven years and three hundred miles: *why didn't you come?* Not why didn't you write—I wrote. I wrote the letter, I left it where I said I'd leave it, I waited at the marina until the tide made the decision for me. But writing was the easy half. Coming was the hard half. Coming meant choosing me over her father, over the vineyard, over the whole gravity well of Crescent Cove, and she hadn't come, and I had built my entire adult life on top of that absence like a house built on a sinkhole.

At the top of Block D, where the rows bend toward the water, I finally asked it.

"Why didn't you come?"

* * *

*Elodie*

Of all the sentences in the world, he chose that one, standing at the exact spot where I once believed love was a place you could live.

The truth was in my mouth already. It had been there for weeks, since the boathouse, since midnight, since his handwriting undid a decade of my grief in four paragraphs. *Meet me at the marina Friday night. Bring nothing. I'll bring everything else.* My father had taken that letter out of the mailbox slot—or however he'd done it, I still couldn't make the mechanics sit straight in my mind—and Julian had stood at the marina believing I'd read it and chosen the vineyard over him.

And here was the sentence I'd rehearsed against the bathroom mirror, burned twice in the woodstove, drafted a third time in ink I couldn't bring myself to strike: *Your letter never reached me. My father made sure of it.*

Three words from that confession away. Three words, and the whole rotten architecture comes down—his anger, my guilt, my father's sainted memory, all of it.

But my father was dead, and dead men can't defend themselves, and some cowardly animal part of me whispered: *let them hate each other across the grave instead of hating you.* If I told him, I wouldn't just be confessing ignorance. I'd be telling Julian Reyes that the man whose bread he'd eaten, whose daughter he'd loved, had bought his departure like a pallet of grapes—and worse, that when I found the ledger entry, when I found the second payment to the harbormaster that I still hadn't fully understood, I'd sat on all of it. That I'd known something was wrong and said nothing, for weeks, while he stood in my tasting room being professional at me.

So I swallowed it. I felt it go down like a fishbone.

"I couldn't," I said. Which was true. Just not the true he deserved.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have today."

"Today." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, just old rust coming off the blade. "You know what I did, Elodie? I stood at that marina until two in the morning. I watched every set of headlights come down the coast road and I gave each one a name, and none of them were yours. And then I got on the boat, and I told myself, *she has her reasons, she'll explain later,* and later never came, and eventually I understood that later was a thing I'd invented because the alternative was unbearable." His voice didn't rise. That was the worst part. It stayed level, directorial, a man reading findings aloud. "You forgot me. That's fine. People do. But don't stand here and tell me you *couldn't* come, when what you mean is you didn't want to enough."

"I thought about you every day." It came out raw, unvetted, and I hated myself for it even as I said it, because it sounded like a plea and I refuse to plead. "Every single day for eleven years. You don't get to tell me what I forgot."

"Then what did you remember?" He stepped closer, up into my space, close enough that I could smell salt on him again—some things survive even bank employment. "Tell me one specific thing. One moment. Because I've got a thousand, and I've spent eleven years wondering if any of them happened to you too."

The harbor lights. The sour grape. His hands cold from the ocean, warming against my jaw. The way he said my name like it was a place he was trying to find on a map.

"The grape," I said quietly. "Row four. You made a face like you'd been personally betrayed by agriculture."

Something moved through him—grief or relief or rage, impossible to say, maybe all three sharing a coat. "Yeah," he said. "That one happened to me too."

And then he turned back to the rows, and clicked open his tablet, and became a bank again, and I stood in the aisle between the vines with the truth still lodged sideways in my throat, thinking: *one more day. One more inspection day. I'll tell him tomorrow.*

I had been telling myself *tomorrow* for eleven years. It had begun to feel less like a plan and more like an address.

* * *

*Julian*

We finished Block F as the light went long and gold, and she was efficient and distant and magnificent, and I catalogued irrigation deficiencies I barely saw. At the truck she shook my hand—shook it, like a vendor at a trade show—and said, "Send your findings," and drove off in a truck older than our history.

Back at the motel, I opened my laptop to draft the report and instead opened the desk drawer, where I keep the one thing I brought to Crescent Cove besides clothes: a letter of my own, written last winter, unsent, addressed in my own hand to a woman I was certain had forgotten me.

I hadn't reread it since I arrived. I took it out now, and the paper trembled slightly, and I told myself it was the motel's cheap fan.

Tomorrow, the inspection continued. Tomorrow, the cellar, the equipment, the financial records—the deep files, the ones the bank required and the borrower dreads. Her father's records. Ten years of Fairweather bookkeeping, going back further than that, back to the season everything ended.

I didn't know yet what was in those ledgers. But I knew Elodie Fairweather had lied to me today—not clumsily, not cruelly, but with the precision of someone protecting something—and I had spent my whole life learning to read water, and I knew disturbed sediment when I saw it.

Whatever she was hiding, it was buried in that cellar. And tomorrow, I was going to be standing in it.
