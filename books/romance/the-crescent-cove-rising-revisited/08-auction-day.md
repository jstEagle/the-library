## 8. Auction Day

The courthouse steps smelled like rain and old stone, the way they always did, and I climbed them with my chin up because if I was going to lose everything my father built, I was going to do it standing straight.

I'd dressed for it, too. Not mourning black—black would have been an admission of defeat, and besides, I'd worn enough black in the last decade to last two lifetimes. Instead I put on the blue dress Julian once said made me look like the sky before a storm, which was either a romantic compliment or a weather warning, and eleven years later I still hadn't decided. Armor, maybe. Or a confession you could wear.

The auction was held in the county assembly room, which had hosted everything from 4-H awards to a wedding that ended in a fistfight over the cake. Today the folding chairs were filled with neighbors who had bought our grapes at the farmers' market, who had drunk our rosé at their daughters' weddings, who had watched me patch the same section of roof three summers running and never once offered a ladder. Crescent Cove was a small town. Small towns show up for auctions the way they show up for funerals: out of obligation, and for the gossip.

Julian sat at the front, at the bank's table, in a charcoal suit that fit him like an apology. He didn't look at me when I came in. That was its own kind of message, delivered in the language we'd been fluent in since we were seventeen: *I am not looking at you* is the loudest way to look at someone.

Auctioneer Delgado took the podium, cleared his throat, and began the recitation of my life in legal terms: parcel numbers, acreage, assessed value, liens. Ninety-two acres. A tasting room with a leaky skylight I'd caulked myself. A boathouse half-gutted by a contractor who'd found more than rot in its walls. Eleven thousand vines, most of them older than me, all of them about to belong to someone else.

"Opening bid," Delgado said, "one point four million."

A hand went up in the third row. Marcus Webb, who owned the marina and had wanted our waterfront parcel since before my father died. Then another hand. Then another. The numbers climbed in increments that felt less like money and more like a countdown.

And here is what I had planned to do: nothing. Sit in the back row, hands folded, dignified, let the gavel fall, sign whatever needed signing, and walk out into the rain with whatever scraps of dignity remained. That was the plan. That was the safe plan, and I had spent ten years being safe, and look where safety had gotten me—alone in a falling-down house, guarding a dead man's secrets like they were heirloom silver.

Delgado called six hundred thousand over asking, and something in my chest stood up and refused to sit back down.

I raised my hand.

Not to bid—I didn't have two nickels to rub together, and everyone in that room knew it. I raised my hand the way you raise it in school, when you have an answer nobody wants to hear.

"Ms. Fairweather?" Delgado blinked at me. "Do you wish to bid?"

"I wish to speak."

Murmurs. Somewhere near the front, Julian's shoulders went rigid under that charcoal suit. He turned then, finally looked at me, and his face did something complicated—the kind of expression you can't fake and can't take back.

"The vineyard you're bidding on," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "was built on stolen ground. Not land—truth." I stood up, because sitting felt like lying. "Eleven years ago, my father paid a man named Julian Reyes to leave town. Paid him to break off with me and disappear. And when money alone didn't work, he bribed the harbormaster to run the Reyes fleet aground on paperwork—to sink their best season so Julian's family would have no choice but to go."

The murmurs stopped. The whole room had gone church-quiet, the kind of quiet that has weight.

"I found the ledger eight months ago," I went on. "I found his letter to me before that—a letter my father intercepted, offering Julian a way out that wasn't really an offer at all. And I knew. For months, I knew, and I said nothing, because the man who did all of this was my father, and I loved him, and loving him felt like the only inheritance I had left." My throat tightened. I let it. "It isn't. It wasn't. The truth is the inheritance. So there it is. Bid accordingly."

I sat down. My hands were shaking so badly I had to press them flat against my knees.

Here's what I expected: pity, or worse, the particular cruelty of small-town kindness, the casseroles and the head-shakes and the whispered *that poor girl*. Here's what I got instead: silence, and then a shifting in the seats, like the room itself had decided to lean away from me. Because I hadn't just confessed my father's sins. I'd confessed my own—the knowing, the silence—and small towns forgive grief but they don't much forgive complicity.

Marcus Webb withdrew his bid with a visible wince. Another bidder followed. The number stalled, then started sliding backward as men who'd come to pick at a carcass decided the carcass might be haunted.

Good, I thought, with a wild, dizzy clarity. Let it be haunted. Let it be honest.

"One million," Delgado said hopefully. "Do I have one million?"

And then Julian Reyes stood up.

He didn't look at the room. He looked at me, and his eyes were doing that thing they used to do across the tasting room bar, that thing where I could never tell if he was about to laugh or say something that would rearrange my whole life. Both, usually. Usually both.

"One point five," he said.

Nobody countered. Of course nobody countered. Half the room had just heard what the Reyes family had suffered to build this vineyard's fortune, and the other half was busy deciding whether to sell their story to the county paper.

"Going once," said Delgado. "Going twice—"

"Gavel," Julian said quietly, like a man closing a door.

The sound cracked through the room, and it was done. Ninety-two acres, eleven thousand vines, one leaking skylight, one half-drowned boathouse, sold to Julian Reyes for one point five million dollars, and every person in that assembly room understood exactly what it meant: the son of the man my father ruined had just bought the ruin.

Revenge, I thought. Clean, patient, public revenge. He'd waited eleven years, taken a job at the bank, learned the loan inside and out, and now he owned the ground my father stole from his family. There were ballads written about less.

I stayed in my chair while the room emptied around me, folding chairs clattering, voices rising into the rain-washed morning outside. Julian signed papers at the bank's table with a pen some clerk handed him, and I watched the back of his head and tried to feel destroyed. That was the appropriate feeling. Destroyed. Humiliated. Ruined.

Instead, underneath the humiliation—underneath the hot, prickling shame of having stood up in front of my entire town and handed them the worst story about my family ever told—there was something else. Something quiet and strange and clean, like the air after lightning.

I had nothing left to hide. Nothing. No letter in a drawer, no entry in a ledger, no rehearsed sentence burning a hole in my chest. Ten years of carrying it, and I'd set it down in front of forty witnesses, and I was still breathing. Still sitting upright in a blue dress. The vineyard was gone and I was lighter than I had been since I was nineteen years old.

Julian finished signing. He gathered his folder, thanked the clerk in a voice too low to catch, and walked toward me down the center aisle, past the emptying chairs, his footsteps slow and deliberate, a man approaching something carefully.

I stood. If I was going to be destroyed, I'd be destroyed standing.

"So," I said, before he could speak, because I'd always been brave in the first three seconds and cowardly after. "Congratulations. You got everything you wanted."

Something flickered across his face—not satisfaction, not triumph. Something that looked almost like pain.

"Elodie," he said, and my name in his mouth after all these years sounded like a key turning in a lock I'd forgotten existed. "We need to talk."

"We just did. In front of the whole county. I'm talked out."

"There's something you need to see." He reached into his jacket, and my stomach dropped, because I knew that gesture, I knew the shape of a man pulling out a letter, and I thought, absurdly, *not again, not another letter, I can't survive another letter.* "Not here," he said, glancing at the clerk still stacking chairs. "The boathouse. Tonight. Six o'clock."

"You own it now," I said, and heard how bitter it came out. "You can tell me anything you want anywhere you want."

His jaw tightened. "Six o'clock, Elodie. Please."

And because I had spent eleven years learning exactly what his *please* cost him, I said yes.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing the only sensible thing a woman does on the day she loses her home: I packed.

Not much. Ten years of invisible labor fits in four boxes and a duffel bag. My mother's recipe tin. A photograph of my father that I couldn't decide whether to keep or burn, so I wrapped it in a dish towel and put it in the bottom, where decisions go to wait. The water-stained letter, of course. That was coming with me no matter where I went, even if where I went was nowhere.

At quarter to six I walked down through the rows one last time—not because anyone asked, but because the vines deserved a goodbye, or I did. The evening light came in low and gold through the leaves, the way it always did in late summer, and the grapes hung heavy and sweet-smelling, and I thought: *someone else will harvest you now. Someone else will complain about your yields and curse your mildew and love you anyway.* It should have broken my heart. Mostly it did. But there was that clean place underneath, still clean, still strange.

The boathouse stood at the bottom of the hill, half-gutted, ribs of new lumber showing where the contractor had torn out the rot. Julian was already there, standing in the doorway with his suit jacket folded over one arm and his sleeves rolled up, and for one disorienting second he was nineteen again, waiting for me on the dock with the whole summer ahead of us.

Then he saw my face, and whatever he read there made him step aside and let me pass.

Inside, the light came through the gaps in the boards in long dusty stripes. He'd set two chairs where the old workbench used to be. On one of them lay an envelope.

Unsealed. Unsent. The paper yellowed differently than mine had—newer, but kept, carried, creased from being opened and refolded many times.

"What is that?" I asked, though I already knew. God help me, I already knew.

"It's dated fourteen months ago," Julian said. His voice wasn't steady anymore; the professionalism had burned off somewhere between the courthouse and the dock, and what was underneath was raw and young and terrified. "I wrote it the week I took the bank job. I never sent it. I was going to—that's why I transferred to this region, why I took your file, why I—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes for a moment, like a man resetting himself. "I need you to understand something before you decide what today meant."

"Today meant you bought my father's sins at a discount," I said. "One point five million. Revenge priced to move."

"Read it," he said. "Please. And then—if you still believe that—tell me to my face, and I'll drive you wherever you want to go, and I won't follow."

My hand shook when I took the envelope. Outside, the tide was coming in against the pilings, that old rhythm underneath everything, patient as gravity.

I unfolded the letter, and the first line hit me like a wave breaking over the seawall:

*Elodie — I've been building a life for two for eleven years, and I'm tired of pretending one of us is dead.*

I looked up. Julian was watching me with the expression of a man standing at the edge of something high, and the light through the board gaps fell across his face in stripes, and somewhere behind us the boathouse—the boathouse that kept one letter for eleven years and had apparently developed a taste for it—creaked in the evening wind.

"Keep reading," he said.
