## 2. Dust and D Major

The inn surrendered its secrets one room at a time, and Sebastian catalogued each surrender on a legal pad with the ruthless efficiency of a woman who had learned that feelings, like orchestras, responded best to a firm downbeat.

*Attic: crates, label. Parlor: drapes, donate. Kitchen: grandmother's recipe box — keep, appraise later.*

She wrote *appraise* and then crossed it out and wrote *decide* and then crossed that out too, because the truth was she hadn't decided anything about this house except to leave it. The Crescent Cove Inn had been in her family for sixty years, and it would be in someone else's family by June. That was the plan. Plans were the only instruments she still trusted.

Three days into packing, she had developed a system. Mornings for inventory, afternoons for boxing, evenings for sitting very still on the porch with a glass of wine and not thinking about the concert — the one in Vienna, eight weeks ago now, where her left hand had simply stopped obeying her mid-Elgar, where four thousand people had watched a cellist forget how to grieve and how to play and which of those failures came first. The reviews had been kind in the way that kindness is cruel. *A performer visibly wrestling with something larger than the music.* She'd read that sentence forty times before she stopped reading anything at all.

So: checklists. Boxes. Tape gun. The small tyrannies of order.

It was on the fourth morning, in the parlor, that she found the cello.

* * *

She should have known it was there. Of course she should have known — she'd spent half her childhood in this room, doing homework on the window seat while her grandmother practiced, the two of them sharing the space the way trees share light. But memory is a poor archivist, and when she pulled back the dust sheet from the corner behind the writing desk, the shock went through her like cold water.

Her grandmother's cello. A 1912 Vincenzo Postiglione, warm amber varnish gone honey-dark with age, the kind of instrument that made conservatory professors go quiet and touch the scroll with both hands. It stood in an open case lined with green velvet, and someone — Jonah, she supposed, the caretaker, the man who existed at the edges of every room in this house like a tide line — had kept it strung and tuned. The strings gleamed. The bridge stood straight.

Sebastian stood over it for a long time without touching it.

Her own cello was in a flight case in her apartment in Boston, in the closet, behind winter coats. She hadn't opened that case in two months. She had told herself this was discipline. Rest. A muscle does not heal while you keep testing it. But standing here, looking at her grandmother's instrument — the instrument that had taught her what sound could be before any teacher ever did — she felt the lie of that word *discipline* the way you feel a cracked rib: only when you breathe wrong.

*Don't,* she told herself. *You're here to pack.*

But her hand was already reaching.

* * *

The first note was terrible.

That was the strange mercy of it — that after all the dread, all the white-knuckled terror of picking up a bow again, the sound that came out was thin and scratchy and human. Not the polished voice of the stage. Just wood and gut and a woman's uncertain arm, waking up.

Sebastian sat on the piano bench with the cello between her knees, the way her grandmother had positioned her at seven years old — *sit tall, sweetheart, let the instrument lean into you like it trusts you* — and played a scale. Then another. Her fingers found their geography by some memory deeper than thought, calluses long softened protesting against the strings, and the notes wobbled but they existed, they came out of her and filled the dusty parlor, and something in her chest that had been clenched since Vienna loosened by a single degree.

She didn't decide to play the piece. It simply arrived, the way weather arrives. D major — her grandmother's favorite key, *the key of open windows*, she used to say — a little air she'd written herself at nineteen, back when she still believed music was something you gave rather than something you owed. It was nothing. Three minutes of melody, unremarkable, unpublished. But her grandmother had loved it, had asked for it every visit, had hummed it while kneading bread, and so Sebastian played it into the sunlit dust of the empty parlor with her eyes closed, and for three minutes she was neither a failed soloist nor a grieving granddaughter nor a woman with a checklist. She was just a body making sound in a room that remembered her.

The last note hung. Faded. Went out through the open window toward the water.

And then, from the doorway, quiet as a change in the wind:

"You kept it."

Sebastian's eyes snapped open. Jonah Reyes stood at the threshold with a toolbox in one hand and a length of copper pipe under his arm, and he was not looking at her face. He was looking at the cello, or at her hands on it, with an expression she couldn't file anywhere — not surprise, not warmth, something older and more carefully maintained than either.

"I didn't know it was here," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. She set the bow down across her knees like setting down a weapon. "Did you tune it?"

"Twice a year." He shifted the pipe. "Strings go false if you don't."

"Of course they do." She laughed, and hated the sound of it — brittle, defensive, a laugh with no floor under it. "You've been keeping a Postiglione in playing condition for eleven years in an empty house."

"It wasn't empty." He said it flatly, without accusation, and somehow that was worse. "Your grandmother lived here until eighteen months ago. And before that—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "There was always someone here."

"Right." Someone. The word sat between them like a third person. Sebastian looked at him properly for the first time since the porch, since he'd taken her suitcase without being asked and said *your room's made up* as if eleven years were a weekend. He'd been twenty-three when she left. He was thirty-four now, and time had done to his face what it does to coastline — pared it, deepened it, left it more itself. There was gray at his temples. His forearms, below the rolled sleeves, were tanned and scarred from decades of salt work, and she caught herself cataloguing these details with an intimacy that felt like trespassing.

"You heard," she said. "All of it."

"The door was open."

"That's not what I asked."

Jonah set the toolbox down. The copper pipe he leaned against the doorframe with exaggerated care, as though arranging props for a scene he'd rehearsed too many times. "I heard enough to know you shouldn't stop," he said. "Playing. You shouldn't have stopped."

Something hot rose in her throat. "That's not— I haven't stopped. I'm between engagements."

"Sure."

"I'm *between engagements*, Jonah. There's a difference."

"Is there?" He asked it gently, which was the unforgivable part. If he'd been cruel she could have met cruelty; she'd built a whole career on meeting cruelty with poise. But he said it the way you'd tell someone their shoelace was undone, and it landed anyway, because he had always been able to see through her — because at nineteen, at twenty-two, at twenty-three, he had been the one person who never once asked her to perform.

"You don't get to have an opinion about my career," she said. "You don't get to stand in doorways and— and assess me. You've been here eleven years and I've been wherever I was, and that's a choice we both made, and I'd appreciate it if we could keep this professional. The roof over the kitchen needs looking at, apparently, and there are boxes in the hall—"

"Sebastian."

"*What?*"

He was quiet a moment. Outside, a gull cried and the tide turned somewhere below the bluff, patient and enormous. When he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone careful, the way voices do around old wounds.

"You sounded like her," he said. "Just now. In the middle of it. There's a phrase — the second one, where it lifts — she used to close her eyes exactly like that. Like she was listening to somebody else play." He picked the pipe back up. "I'll be on the roof if you need me."

And he was gone, boots on the stairs, before she could assemble a single word of reply.

* * *

Sebastian sat in the parlor for a long time afterward with the cello humming faintly against her sternum, every string still faintly alive with vibration, and tried to understand what had just happened to her containment strategy.

She had a system. She had *always* had a system: grief filed under later, love filed under never, music filed under obligation. It had gotten her through Juilliard and through Vienna and through the funeral where she'd shaken hands with two hundred mourners without crying, because crying was a thing that happened to women who hadn't learned control, and control was the only inheritance she'd ever believed in.

But Jonah Reyes had stood in a doorway and said *you sounded like her* and the filing cabinet had tipped over.

Because here was the thing she had not let herself think since the taxi from the station: he had known. On the porch, day one, he had looked at her and there had been no surprise in it — none — and she had told herself that was professionalism, that the town must have announced her arrival, that caretakers were informed of such things. But now, with the dust still turning gold in the light and her grandmother's D major still dissolving off the walls, another explanation presented itself, unwelcome as a debt collector.

He had known because he had been waiting to know. Because some part of him had spent eleven years expecting her to walk back up that path, and had prepared his face for it, the way he prepared the cello — twice a year, whether anyone came or not.

*Tuned. Maintained. Kept in playing condition.*

She looked down at her hands, at the fresh dents the strings had pressed into her fingertips, red as confession. Eleven years ago she had left this town without saying goodbye — had left *him* without saying goodbye, had boarded a train with a scholarship letter in her pocket and convinced herself that ambition required amputation, that you could cut away a person like a bad habit and the wound would close clean. It had closed crooked. Everything since had been played slightly out of tune because of it, and she had blamed the acoustics.

Sebastian lifted the bow. Set it down again. Lifted it.

She played the phrase — just the second phrase, the one that lifts — and closed her eyes, and hated him a little for being right, and hated her grandmother more for leaving her a house full of rooms that refused to stay packed.

When she finally rose to put the instrument away, she noticed, for the first time, the state of the case's inner lid. Beneath the green velvet, where the rosin dust had settled undisturbed for years, there was a rectangle of cleaner fabric — the shape and size of something that had lain there a long time and recently been removed.

An envelope-sized absence.

She stood very still, listening to Jonah's hammer begin its slow rhythm on the roof above her, and thought: *what else has been kept in playing condition in this house?*

The attic ladder waited at the end of the hall. She told herself she was going up for the crates.
