## 2. Advance Copies

The parcel arrived on a Tuesday, which meant it came with the milk delivery and the smell of brine off the harbour, because everything in Bellbird Bay arrived on a Tuesday or not at all.

Oscar Whitlock heard the van before he saw it — the rattle of old suspension coming up Harbour Street like a coughing animal — and he was already unhooking the shop keys from his belt when Dougal Fenn leaned out the window of the delivery van and held up a cardboard box the size of a small dog.

"Advance copies," Dougal said, as though announcing a birth. "Big one, too. City's making noise about it."

"Everything the city makes noise about ends up in the remainder bin by August," Oscar said, but he took the box anyway, and the weight of it settled into his arms with the particular gravity of paper and ambition.

The morning had come in gold and grey, the way autumn mornings did in the Bay — the light slanting low across the water and setting every window on Harbour Street alight for twenty minutes before the cloud came back in to take its turn. Oscar had opened the shop at seven, as he always did, propped the door with the brass whale doorstop his daughter had made in pottery class two years ago — its tail chipped, its eye painted slightly too far to the left, so that it always seemed to be regarding him with mild suspicion — and put the kettle on in the back room while the shop came up to temperature around him.

The shop was called *Whitlock's*, which had been his grandfather's lack of imagination and Oscar's inheritance, and it was the last bookshop in Bellbird Bay, which was less an achievement than a stubbornness. It smelled the way it had always smelled: old paper and wood polish and the faint ghost of pipe tobacco that had soaked into the shelves during his grandfather's tenure and had never, in thirty years, fully left. In the winter it smelled of the paraffin heater; in the summer, of salt through the propped door. It smelled, Oscar had long ago decided, like the inside of a life that had chosen to stay in one place.

Wren came down the stairs at half past seven, hair unbrushed, one sock inside out, carrying her school bag by one strap the way she carried everything — as though she might have to drop it at any moment and run.

"Cocoa?" she said.

"Kettle's on."

She poured the milk into the saucepan herself — she was twelve now and had opinions about the correct ratio, which she defended with the fervour of a barrister — and Oscar sliced the bread and put it under the grill while she stirred, and the two of them performed the small liturgy of their mornings without needing to speak much. This was the hour Oscar guarded most jealously. The shop didn't open until nine. The town didn't wake properly until the bakery bell rang at eight. For ninety minutes every morning, Harbour Street belonged to the gulls and the tide and the two of them, and the cocoa was made in the dented saucepan, and Wren sat on the counter with her feet in the empty biscuit tin, and nothing in the world required anything of him.

"You've got flour on your eyebrow," Wren said.

"It's distinguished."

"It's flour."

She wiped it off with her sleeve, which transferred the flour to her own cheek, and Oscar decided not to mention it, and they drank their cocoa in the gold light with the harbour glittering through the window like something spilled and precious.

"You're coming to the harvest thing on Saturday," Wren said. It wasn't a question. It was a summons.

"Wouldn't miss it."

"Mrs. Calloway's going to be there."

"Ah," Oscar said.

"She's going to ask you about the autumn fair again. She's going to ask if you're *available*."

"Available," Oscar repeated, as though the word were in a foreign language he'd studied once and found ugly.

"Dad. She asked me. At the bakery. She asked me if you were *seeing anyone*, and I said you were married to the shop, and she said that wasn't a real answer, and I said it was the only answer I had."

Oscar turned back to the grill, where the bread was going golden at the edges, and gave himself the three seconds he needed to arrange his face. Ten years. Ten years of Mrs. Calloway and her predecessors, of the whole gentle apparatus of a small town trying to fold him back into the warm shape of a paired life. Ten years of deflections that had worn smooth as sea glass: *I'm busy, I'm content, the shop takes everything, Wren's enough.* All of it true. None of it the truth.

"Married to the shop," he said. "Good answer. Stick with it."

"It's not a real answer, Dad."

"It's the best one I've got."

She looked at him for a moment — she had a way of looking at him sometimes, a long, level, unchildlike look, as though she were reading something over his shoulder — and then she hopped down from the counter, shouldered her bag, and kissed him on the cheek on her way to the door, quick as a bird.

"Saturday," she said. "Don't be weird about Mrs. Calloway. She's lonely."

"Everyone's lonely. That's not a reason to marry them."

"It's a reason to be *nice* to them." And she was gone, out into the gold and grey, her footsteps slapping the cobbles, and Oscar stood in the doorway of his shop and watched her go until she turned the corner by the chandlery, and then he went inside and opened the parcel.

* * *

The box held eleven copies, wrapped in tissue, each one heavier than it should have been — the weight of good paper, sewn bindings, the kind of book a publisher sends out when it expects the book to matter. The cover was deep blue, almost black, with a single embossed image: a lighthouse, small and pale, at the bottom edge of the cover, its beam a thin gold line running up the spine.

*The Salt Hour*, said the title. And beneath it: **N. R. ASHFORD**.

Oscar had heard the name, of course. Everyone had heard the name by now. The literary pages had been full of it for months — the debut novelist of the season, the one the prizes were already circling, the one whose author photo showed a woman with dark hair and a face turned three-quarters away from the camera as though she had somewhere better to be. He'd read the reviews the way he read weather reports for places he had no intention of visiting: with a distant, professional interest. He'd even ordered six copies for the shop, back in the summer, because a bookseller stocked what the season wanted regardless of his private weather.

He set the parcel knife down. He picked up the first copy. He did what he always did with a new book, the ritual older than his own memory of learning it at his grandfather's elbow: he turned to the front matter, and he read the dedication.

Dedications were the truest page in any book. Covers lied, blurbs lied, even first chapters lied a little, setting out the bait. But the dedication was written before anyone knew whether the book would be worth reading, written in the privacy of the thing, when the author still didn't know who would ever see it. It was the one page where the author stood in the room with you.

*For the boy on Harbour Street.*

The book did not fall. Oscar's hands were steady — he was a man who had carried a newborn through a winter of night feeds, who had caught a falling child off the top of a bookshelf, whose hands did not shake, whose hands *never* shook — but the book tilted, and the tilt was the world tilting, and he set it down on the counter very carefully, face up, the way you set down something that might go off.

The shop was very quiet. The paraffin heater ticked. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy rang, once, twice, patient as a heartbeat.

*The boy on Harbour Street.*

There was only one boy who had ever mattered on Harbour Street. There had only ever been one boy at all, in her telling of it — a boy who'd grown tall and serious too young when his father died, who'd taken over a bookshop at twenty-six with a baby on his hip because there was no one else, who'd read her poetry in the stockroom with the door shut and told her once, in the blue hour of a July evening on the headland, that she was going to write books one day whether the world was ready or not.

Nora.

Nora, who had left on a night of rain ten years ago with a suitcase and a sentence he had never understood — *I can't be what you need, Oscar* — and who had not written, not called, not sent so much as a postcard in a decade. Nora, whose name he did not say, whose books he did not stock, whose existence he had filed away in the one drawer of himself that stayed locked, and had stayed locked, and had cost him more than he would ever admit out loud.

N. R. Ashford.

He had known. Some part of him, some cold clear part below the waterline, had known since the reviews began — had known the way you know a storm is coming before the sky says anything. He had read the author's name and thought: *common enough name. Ashfords everywhere. N. R. could be anyone.* He had performed the small daily magic trick of a man who has gotten very good at not looking at things.

But the dedication. The dedication was a hand reaching out of a locked drawer and taking him by the throat.

*For the boy on Harbour Street.*

He sat down on the stool behind the counter, the one with the wobbly leg he kept meaning to fix, and he read the dedication again, and then a third time, as though repetition might make it mean something else. It didn't. Words didn't work that way. He'd been a bookseller long enough to know that words only ever meant more, never less, the longer you looked at them.

The boy on Harbour Street. Not *for my parents*. Not *for J.*, the way novelists did when they wanted privacy. Not the name of an agent or an editor or a dead grandmother who'd handed her her first library card. *The boy.* As if the boy were a figure, a type, a character — as if she had taken the one person she had ever — and turned him into a dedication like a pressed flower, flat and permanent and public.

He opened the book at random. He was a professional; he could do this; he could hold a book by Nora Ashford and read it the way he read any book, coldly, with a bookseller's eye.

He read a paragraph about a woman standing in a kitchen in a coastal town, watching rain come in off the sea, and the prose was good — it was very good, it was the kind of good that made his trade both possible and unbearable, sentences with the tide in them — and every word of it was fine, every word of it was about a stranger, until the woman in the kitchen reached for a tin on the high shelf and couldn't find it, and the paragraph said: *she had never learned where things were kept in other people's houses, only how to leave them.*

Oscar closed the book.

He sat in the gold-and-grey light with the heater ticking and the bell buoy ringing its patient two notes, and he thought about what a man was supposed to do with a thing like this. Stock it, obviously. The book would sell itself; the season had already decided that. A bookseller stocked the book. A bookseller did not stock the *dedication*, could not, there was no shelf for it. He would put the six copies he'd ordered face-out on the new releases table, spine up the rest, and he would write a card — *The novel of the autumn, per the broadsheets* — and he would sell every copy and never once read past page one, and in ten years' time, when the book came up in remainder, he would buy the whole lot and pulp them, or burn them, or line the cat box with them, whichever came first.

That was the plan. That was a good plan. That was the plan of a man with a locked drawer and a steady hand.

The bell over the door rang, and Mrs. Calloway came in on a draught of cold air with her shopping trolley and her prepared remark about the autumn fair, and Oscar stood up and sold her a card and a bar of chocolate and agreed, with a smile he'd been practising for a decade, that yes, the weather was turning, and yes, he'd see her Saturday.

And when she'd gone he stood at the counter and looked at the blue book lying there with its lighthouse and its gold line, and he understood, in the way you understand a tide has turned — not by seeing it turn but by noticing, all at once, that the water is going the other way — that the plan would not hold. That a book with a dedication like that was not a book. It was a door. And doors, in Oscar Whitlock's long experience, did not stay closed just because a man stood in front of them and asked nicely.

* * *

He did not open the shop properly that day. He turned the sign, he made the coffee, he dusted the front table with a cloth that moved over the wood without touching it, and at eleven o'clock, when the post came, he dealt with the invoices in a silence so complete that the clock over the door seemed to be reading them aloud. At noon he ate a cheese sandwich standing up and did not taste it. At one o'clock a customer asked for a book about beekeeping for her grandson and Oscar found it in eleven seconds flat, because his hands knew the shop the way his hands knew Wren's hair, and the customer said, "You're a wonder, Oscar Whitlock," and he said, "Just long practice," and meant it in a way she couldn't have guessed.

At two o'clock he took the blue book off the counter, where it had been lying all day like a patient on a table, and he carried it into the stockroom and set it on the highest shelf, behind the local history and the unsold remainders of books about canal boats, in the one corner of the shop where the light never reached and where he himself had no reason to go.

Then he went back out front, and he stood behind his counter in his shop that smelled of pipe tobacco and old paper and the ten years since, and he waited for the feeling to pass.

It did not pass. It did what the sea does. It went out, and he thought, *good, gone*, and then, an hour later, with the light going long and low across the floorboards, it came back in, and it brought things with it. A blue dress. Rain on a tin roof. The particular way she had of laughing with her whole face, eyes last, so that you spent the whole joke waiting for her eyes. The sentence in the rain. The suitcase. The ten years of nothing.

He had built a life on top of that nothing, the way the town had built its sea wall on top of the old harbour — layer over layer, solid, functional, holding. And he had been, he would say this for himself, he had been *happy*. Not the loud happiness of the young; the quiet kind, the kind you earn. Wren's cocoa in the morning. The shop. The town. A life with its doors and windows all exactly where he wanted them.

And now a parcel had come up Harbour Street on a Tuesday with a dedication in it, and the whole sea wall had a crack in it, thin as a hair, and he could hear — he could almost hear — the water moving behind it.

At four o'clock Wren came in with the wind in her hair and her bag over one shoulder and said, "You look weird."

"I look distinguished."

"You look like you've been arguing with someone and lost." She dropped her bag and went to the cocoa tin. "Mrs. Calloway says there's a big new book everyone's talking about. She said it's by someone from around here, originally. She said you'd know."

The kettle began, far away, to heat toward its whistle.

"Did she," Oscar said.

"She said the author's name is Ashford." Wren got down the saucepan, the dented one, and set it on the hob with the confidence of a girl who had never once doubted where things were kept in her own house. "Dad? Do you know her?"

Oscar stood in the gold-and-grey light of his shop, in the smell of his whole life, and he heard himself say, in the voice he had practised for ten years, smooth as sea glass:

"Never heard of her."

And upstairs, in the dark of the stockroom, on the highest shelf behind the canal boats, the blue book waited with its lighthouse and its gold line and its dedication like a hand reaching out of the past — and somewhere down the coast, though he could not have known it, a woman was getting into a car.
