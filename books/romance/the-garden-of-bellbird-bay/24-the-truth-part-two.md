## 24. The Truth, Part Two

The shop had gone quiet in the way rooms do after something has broken — not silence exactly, but a hush with edges on it, the kind of quiet that listens back. Rain still worked at the windows in long silver threads. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, and Nora flinched as though the house itself had spoken.

Oscar stood by the counter with his hands flat on the wood, as if the counter were the only thing holding him upright. Imogen had sunk into the reading nook chair, her confession letter — the one she'd burned, and then, God help them all, rewritten and carried through the rain — lying open on her knee like a wound she'd finally stopped covering.

*Wren is Nora's biological daughter.*

The words were already out. They hung in the lamplight between the three of them, impossible to take back, impossible to breathe around.

And now it was her turn.

Nora had rehearsed this. That was the terrible irony of it — ten years of rehearsal, in showers and stairwells and rented flats in cities where nobody knew her name before it was on everyone's lips. She'd said it into mirrors. She'd said it into the dark of overnight trains. She had composed versions: the dignified version, the brief version, the version that began with an apology and the version that began with an explanation, as though explanation could ever be mistaken for absolution. She knew this speech the way she knew her own novels — every beat, every pause, every place where a reader's heart was supposed to break.

But standing here, under the bell that had announced her arrival a lifetime ago, looking at the man whose life she had rearranged without his consent — the speech dissolved like sugar in rain.

"Sit down," she said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Please. Both of you. I can't— I can't do this standing up."

Oscar didn't sit. He looked at her the way he'd looked at her across the storm-dark shop weeks ago, when the first flakes fell and the door swung open and ten years ended in a single ring of a bell. But there was something new in his face now, something that frightened her more than anger would have. He looked *unmoored*. He looked like a man doing arithmetic on everything he'd ever believed.

"Nora." Just her name. Her name in his mouth, after all this time, saying *please* without saying it.

"I was twenty-three," she began, because you had to begin somewhere, and beginning at the end would kill her. "When I met Daniel. You never met him. I made sure of that — I kept him away from Bellbird Bay, away from university, away from everything that was mine. He was—" She stopped. Started again. "He was charming the way weather is charming right up until it kills you."

Imogen made a small sound. Nora didn't look at her. If she looked at Imogen she would lose the thread, and the thread was all she had.

"The first year was good. The second year I started making excuses to my friends about why I couldn't see them. By the third year my friends had stopped calling, which was the point, though I didn't understand that yet. He controlled the money. He controlled the phone. He controlled—" Her breath caught. "He controlled what I was allowed to be afraid of. Do you know what that's like? To have even your fear edited?"

Oscar's jaw worked. He said nothing. The rain filled the space where his voice should have been.

"When I found out I was pregnant, I thought — I actually thought it would fix him. I'd read that somewhere, some magazine in a checkout line, some lie women tell themselves because the truth is unbearable. And for about six weeks it was almost true. He was gentle. He bought things. He talked about names." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, thin as paper. "Then I was eight months along and I said the wrong thing at dinner — I don't even remember what it was anymore, isn't that obscene? Eight months of pregnancy and I can't remember the crime — and he put me against the wall and told me what he'd do to the baby if I ever tried to leave. Not to me. To the *baby*. He'd figured out, you see, that hurting me wasn't enough anymore. I'd learned to survive being hurt. So he found the one thing I couldn't survive losing."

The lamp flickered. Outside, the storm leaned its whole weight against the glass.

"I ran that night," Nora said. "I ran with a suitcase and forty pounds and a bruise in the shape of his hand on my arm, and I went to the only person I trusted more than I feared him." Now she did look at Imogen, and Imogen's eyes were closed, tears cutting down her face. "I went to my sister."

"Nora," Imogen whispered.

"Let me finish. Ten years, Imogen. Let me finish." She turned back to Oscar. "Imogen hid me. She had a friend from uni who worked in women's services in Manchester; she got me a room, a doctor, a lawyer who specialized in exactly this kind of escape. And I gave birth in a hospital forty miles from here under a false name, alone except for Imogen, holding a baby girl with your—" Her voice cracked clean through. "With *dark hair*. With a cry that sounded like fury and joy having an argument."

She wrapped her arms around herself. The reading nook, the shelves, the smell of paper and dust and cocoa — all of it pressed in close, the scene of the crime and the scene of the salvation occupying the same four walls.

"I want to be very clear about what I believed," she said. "Because I know how it looks. I know it looks like I threw her away. I need you to hear that in the world I was living in, keeping her was the same as killing her. Daniel had found me twice already — once in Manchester, once in Leeds. He had money and patience and a talent for finding people, and the police were helpful in the way police are helpful until they aren't. Every expert I spoke to said the same thing: disappear completely or don't bother disappearing at all. A mother with an infant leaves a trail. A trail is a target. And a target is a grave."

"So you erased yourself," Oscar said. His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man walking on ice and listening for the crack beneath each step.

"I erased *her*," Nora corrected, and the word cost her everything. "That was the part I couldn't live with, so I did the only arithmetic that made sense to me at twenty-four years old and nine days postpartum and terrified out of my mind. I couldn't keep her safe. But there was a man — there was *you* — who ran a bookshop and fixed the church roof and coached the junior football team and had never in his life raised his voice at anyone, and who loved me enough that when I told him I was leaving, he'd stood in the rain and asked me to stay and hadn't once, not once in ten years, come looking for me to make me pay for it." She was crying now, properly, without grace. "You were the safest person either of us had ever known. You were the *safest place in the world*, Oscar. And I signed her into it."

The silence that followed was total. Even the storm seemed to hold its breath.

"You could have told me," Oscar said at last, and it wasn't a shout. It was worse than a shout. It was bewildered. "You could have knocked on that door and said, 'Here is a child, she is in danger, help me.' I would have helped you. I would have helped *both* of you. You didn't need to make me complicit in my own—"

"I know." It tore out of her. "I know what I took from you. I've known for ten years, every single day, I've done the accounting every day — every birthday I missed, every first day of school, every scraped knee I heard about secondhand through Imogen's carefully worded Christmas letters. Don't you think I know? I built a career writing about mothers and daughters and homesickness because it was the only place I was allowed to feel any of it. The dedication page — 'for the boy on Harbour Street' — I wrote that knowing you might never read it, and hoping you would, and hating myself for both."

"Why not tell me later?" His voice rose at last, cracking on the word. "The danger passed. People leave men like that. Years passed. You were famous, Nora. You had resources I can't imagine. Why did my daughter grow up calling another woman's name for her mother while you were doing *book tours*?"

"Because telling you meant telling *him*," Nora said. "Don't you see? The moment I surfaced in your life, I surfaced in hers. He knew about you. He knew about this town. I checked, Oscar — for years I checked, quietly, through lawyers, through friends of friends. As long as I stayed away, Wren was just a bookshop keeper's daughter in a town too small to appear on anyone's map. The moment I came back, she became the daughter of Nora Ashford, whose face is on buses, whose ex-husband has Google alerts on my name." She pressed her fist to her mouth, then lowered it. "I traded being her mother for being her shield. I told myself it was temporary. I told myself I'd come back when he was gone, when it was safe, when she was old enough to understand. And then the years just — accumulated. Like snow. Like the snow outside. One year becomes two becomes ten, and every year it gets harder to say, and easier to write it in a novel instead, where it's fiction, where it doesn't cost anything."

"It cost everything," Oscar said quietly.

"Yes." She didn't flinch from it. "It cost everything. That was the price, and I paid it, and I'd pay it again, and I will hate myself forever for the fact that I'd pay it again, because she's *alive*, Oscar. She's upstairs asleep, and she's alive, and she laughs like me, and she reads under the covers with a torch, and she is the best thing that ever happened to anyone, and none of it — none of it — makes what I did to you right."

She stepped toward him. He didn't step back, but he didn't step forward either, and she understood that this was the truest measure of where they stood.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight," she said. "I'm not asking you to forgive me this year. I am asking you to believe one thing, and it's the only thing I've never lied about, not in ten years, not in a single sentence of five published novels: I did not leave because I stopped loving you. I left because loving you was the last weapon anyone had left to use against me, and I refused — I *refused* — to let him take you too."

Oscar closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he let them be wet, and somehow that undid her more than anything else in this ruined night.

"She has your laugh," he said. It wasn't a question. It was a surrender to a fact he'd been circling for weeks.

"And your stubbornness," Nora managed. "And your habit of alphabetizing things that don't need alphabetizing. I've watched her shelve returns for three weeks. She does it exactly the way you do. She's yours, Oscar. In every way that matters, she has been yours since the day Imogen carried her up these stairs. I just—" Her voice broke entirely now, and she let it. "I just wanted, for one hour, one hour in ten years, to stand in the same room with her and be allowed to look."

Upstairs, faintly, a floorboard creaked.

All three of them went still.

It was nothing. Children shift in their sleep; houses settle; storms make old buildings talk. But the sound dropped through the ceiling like a stone through water, and Nora saw Oscar's face change — saw him think of Wren asleep above them, twelve years old and dreaming, her whole history rewritten beneath her without her consent, without anyone in this room having asked her permission for any of it.

"We wake her," Oscar said slowly, "and we tell her nothing until she asks. Nothing. Whatever happens between the three of us — however this ends — she hears it from us together, or she doesn't hear it at all. Swear it. Both of you."

"I swear," Imogen said instantly, hoarse.

Nora nodded. Then, because she had spent ten years learning that promises kept in the dark are worth nothing unless they survive daylight, she said it aloud: "I swear it. On her life. On both their lives."

Oscar looked at her for a long moment — looked at her the way he must have looked at her once, before, across a counter stacked with paperbacks, when the future was a country they both assumed they'd reach. Then he picked up the confession letter from Imogen's knee, folded it with the precise, deliberate care of a man shelving a book he wasn't ready to read, and set it facedown on the counter.

"It's late," he said. "There are blankets in the nook. Nobody drives anywhere tonight."

He climbed the stairs. They listened to his footsteps cross the landing, pause outside Wren's door — a long, long pause, the length of a father memorizing something — and continue on.

Nora sank into the reading nook beside her sister. The rain kept on. Neither of them slept.

And neither of them, in the darkest hour of that long night, heard the small bare feet that padded silently back across the landing, or the door that closed without a sound — or the girl who lay awake in the dark with her mother's laugh caught in her throat, piecing together, word by terrible word, the shape of everything she'd overheard.
