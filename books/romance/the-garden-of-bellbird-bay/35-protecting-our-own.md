## 35. Protecting Our Own

The morning of the third Thursday in February arrived the way bad news does — quietly, with a light that seemed ordinary right up until it wasn't.

I knew something was wrong before I understood what. The shop had a rhythm to it, ten years of rhythm: the groan of the floorboards at half past seven when I came down from the flat, the particular rattle of the coffee machine's heating element, Wren's socked feet on the stairs above me as she got ready for school. That morning all of it was in place except one thing. Nora was late.

She'd been staying through the winter now — six weeks since the storm of truths, five since we'd begun the slow, clumsy work of learning each other again. She kept a room at the Bay Inn but she'd colonized the reading nook by the window as her writing spot, and she had fallen into the habit of arriving at eight sharp with two pastries from Ruthie's bakery and an opinion about my shelving system. Eight sharp. It was nearly nine.

"She'll come," Wren said, though I hadn't said anything out loud. Twelve-year-olds hear everything; they simply pretend not to. She was at the counter doing her homework, hair still damp, pencil moving in that furious way she had when she was thinking about something other than fractions. "Mum—" she caught herself, the way she always did now, correcting and not correcting at once, "—Nora said she had a call with her editor this morning. Time zones or whatever."

"Right," I said. "Time zones."

The bell over the door rang and my heart did its stupid hopeful lift before I saw it was only Mrs. Calloway, wanting the second book in a series about a lighthouse keeper's ghost. I wrapped it in brown paper while she told me, at length, about her hip. And then, halfway through the wrapping, she went quiet in a way Mrs. Calloway never went quiet, and I looked up and followed her gaze through the front window to the far side of Harbour Street.

There was a man standing there.

He was standing the way men stand when they are waiting for something they believe is owed to them. Not loitering — loiterers drift. This man was planted, hands in the pockets of a coat too thin for the coastal wind, collar up, watching the shop. Watching, I understood a moment later, not the shop. The door. Waiting for it to open and deliver him someone.

"You know who that is?" Mrs. Calloway asked, low.

"No," I said, and then I did, because Nora had described him once, in the reading nook, in a voice stripped of all her usual music: *He has a way of standing like the world owes him rent.* She hadn't given me his name. She hadn't needed to. Some things you describe so precisely because you've spent years memorizing them against your will.

My hands stopped on the brown paper.

"Mrs. Calloway," I said, "would you mind finishing your purchase another time?"

She looked at me. She looked at the man across the street. Something moved behind her eyes — Bellbird Bay has a long memory for trouble, and every woman over sixty here can smell it coming off the water — and she said, "I'll just be going round to tell Dot," which was not what I'd asked for but was exactly what I wanted.

* * *

What I remember most clearly, afterward, is how slow everything became. The way time does when the body understands danger before the mind consents to name it.

I stepped out onto the pavement. The wind came off the harbour carrying salt and the last grit of melting snow, and it pushed at me like a hand. Across the street the man watched me come, and he didn't move, and that was the first thing that made me afraid — not for myself, but properly afraid, the kind of fear that arrives wearing someone else's face. Because I had seen men like this before, in another life, in the years before Bellbird Bay, and I knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a man conserving himself for the thing he'd actually come to do.

"Nora Ashford inside?" he said, when I reached the middle of the road. His voice was flat. Northern vowels worn down to nothing.

"There's no one here by that name," I said.

It was a stupid thing to say and we both knew it. He almost smiled. Up close he was smaller than the waiting had made him, wiry, with the kind of handsome that has gone slightly wrong at the edges, and a scar through one eyebrow that Nora had never mentioned but that my mind catalogued anyway, furiously, uselessly. His eyes went past me to the shop window, to the shelves, to the warm gold light inside where my daughter sat doing her homework.

"Cute town," he said. "Cute little life." He said *little* the way some men say *woman*, making it small. "She always did land soft. Somebody always builds her a nest."

"Whatever you've driven here to do," I said, "you can turn around and do it somewhere else."

"Or what?" He looked genuinely curious. "You'll what, bookseller?"

And that was when three things happened almost at once, and I have thought about them many times since, because together they amounted to the single most instructive moment of my adult life.

The first thing: Imogen Ashford came around the corner of the chandlery at a walk so fast it was nearly a run, coat unbuttoned, no scarf, hair whipping, and planted herself on my left without a word, without looking at me, her whole body aimed at the man like a drawn bow. She had been watching from somewhere. Of course she had. Imogen had been watching for this man for ten years — longer than any of us had known there was anything to watch for — and when he finally came she had been ready in the way that guilt makes people ready, the way a woman who arranged one escape out of a burning building never stops mapping exits.

"Danny," she said, and her voice didn't shake. Ten years ago it would have. I could see that it would have, the way you can see the fossil of a fear inside a person who has outgrown it. "You're trespassing on a lot of things. My sister's peace. My niece's street. My patience, which ran out on you in 2014."

His jaw worked. "Imogen. Still playing guardian angel."

"Still better at it than you ever were at being human."

The second thing: the town arrived. There is no other word for it. Mrs. Calloway had gone round to tell Dot, and Dot had evidently told everyone, and Harbour Street began, gently, unhurriedly, to fill. Ruthie from the bakery appeared in her apron with flour still on her forearms and stood in the doorway of her shop with her arms folded. Old Ted from the boatyard leaned on the rail outside the chandlery. Two women I knew only by their library cards drifted to the corner near the post office and stopped there, talking to each other while looking nowhere else. Nobody shouted. Nobody formed a line. They simply accumulated, the way weather accumulates, until the man became aware that the street had acquired an audience, and that the audience was not curious. Curiosity looks at you. This was something older. This was a town deciding what it was willing to have happen on its paving stones.

Bellbird Bay closes ranks the way a hand closes around something small and frightened. I had lived here ten years and never seen it done. I saw it now.

The third thing happened inside the shop, and I only learned it later, but I set it down here because it belongs in the order of events. Wren had come to the window. She had seen the man, seen me, seen Imogen arrive like a thunderhead, and instead of hiding — and God knows I would have ordered her to hide if I'd known — she had gone to the telephone list taped inside the counter and called the police station, and then she had stood in the doorway with the door locked behind her, arms crossed, twelve years old and absolutely ferocious, watching through the glass with an expression I recognized with a sick lurch because it was Nora's expression. It was the face Nora made in chapter nine of her own novel, the one I'd read four times now, the heroine refusing to be made small.

Three things. A sister, a town, a daughter. None of them mine by arrangement. All of them mine by choice, and mine by choosing me back.

* * *

The constable's car came up Harbour Street at a speed that suggested someone had rung twice. By then it was nearly over.

Not because anything dramatic happened. Nothing dramatic happened, and I want to be exact about that, because the drama was all in what did *not* happen — in the space between what the man had driven four hours to do and what he found himself able to do with a hundred pairs of eyes on him and a woman named Imogen reciting, in a voice pitched to carry, the precise terms of the restraining order she had helped Nora file six weeks ago, the day after the storm, the paperwork nobody had mentioned to me because it was one more brick in the wall of things these sisters had decided I couldn't yet be trusted to hold.

"You're on camera," Imogen said, nodding at the bakery's new security camera, installed after a spate of gull-related thefts, repurposed now for something worthier. "You're on Ted's camera. You're on the post office camera. You walked up to a family on a public street and you've been asked to leave. Say something else, Danny. Give them all something to write down."

He looked at her. He looked at the crowd. He looked past all of us at the window, and for one second his eyes found Wren's through the glass, and I felt something go through me that I had only ever read descriptions of in other people's books — a heat, a total absence of thought, the body's oldest arithmetic. I stepped between them. Fully between them, shoulder squared, so that the line from his eyes to hers ran through me and broke.

I am not a large man. I want that recorded accurately, because it isn't bravery if you're certain of winning. I am a bookseller of middling height whose hands are good for lifting boxes of stock and turning pages, and I stood in front of a man who had put a woman I love in a hospital once — she told me that much, finally, in January, in eleven words she could barely get out — and I stood there because there was no version of the next thirty seconds in which I stood anywhere else.

"That's enough," I said. I don't think I said it loudly. "That's all of it. You're done."

Something passed over his face then that I recognized, and it frightened me more than the anger had, because it wasn't anger. It was the look of a man doing sums and finding the numbers worse than expected. He had come expecting a woman alone in a small place, easy to find, easy to corner, easy to make feel the old cold. He had found instead a street full of people who knew his name, a sister with paperwork, a constable pulling up at the kerb, and a stranger standing in his sightline like a shelf that wouldn't be moved.

"Tell her," he said, and his voice cracked on it, just slightly, just enough to show the rot underneath, "tell her this isn't finished."

"It is finished," said Ruthie from the bakery doorway, mildly, terribly, in the tone she used for customers who tried to return books with water damage. "Constable Marsh is right behind you, love. Would you like to say that bit again with him listening?"

The constable took his details. Took photographs of him, of the street, of the shop. Told him, in the bored voice police officers reserve for men who are not worth excitement, that Ms. Ashford's order covered harassment by proxy and that driving four hours to stand outside her family's business qualified under section four, and that the next visit would begin with handcuffs rather than conversation. The man listened with his chin down. When it was done he walked back down Harbour Street toward wherever he'd parked, and the crowd parted for him the way water parts for something it doesn't want touching it, and not one person spoke to him, and the silence followed him all the way to the corner like a dog.

Then the street exhaled, and became a street again. Ted went back to his boatyard. The library women resumed their conversation about parking. Mrs. Calloway materialized at my elbow and pressed a thermos cap of tea into my hand, having apparently decided I was the party most in need, and perhaps I was; my hands were not steady, and the tea sloshed, and I drank it anyway.

* * *

Nora came at twenty past nine.

She came running, actually — I heard her before I saw her, boots hammering the cobbles, and then she was through the door and into the shop and her eyes went around the room in a fast, terrible sweep: me, Imogen, the constable leaving, and last, longest, Wren. She crossed the floor and crouched in front of our daughter and took her face in both hands and looked at her the way you check a lamp for cracks.

"Are you hurt?"

"I called the police," Wren said, with enormous dignity. "I used the emergency number. I gave the address first, like you're supposed to."

"I know. Constable Marsh told me. You did it exactly right." Nora's voice held until the end of the sentence and then didn't. She pulled Wren in and Wren let her, and over the top of our daughter's head Nora looked at me, and her face was the face from the hospital corridor I have never seen but somehow know — the face of a woman tallying, again, what her survival has cost the people around her.

"He saw me," she said to me. "Through the window. He looked right at me."

"He looked at you for one second," I said, "and then he looked at a street full of people who'd already chosen, and he left. That's what he saw, Nora. Not you. Us."

She stood up slowly. She was shaking — I could see it in her hands, which she pressed flat against her thighs the way you press down a map in wind. "This is what I was afraid of," she said. "Not him. This. Him walking into the middle of—"

"The middle of what?" Imogen asked, from the doorway. Her voice was gentle, which from Imogen was remarkable, like a wolf asking politely. "Say it. Say what he walked into the middle of."

Nora looked at her sister. Then at Wren. Then at me.

"My family," she said. It came out rough. "He walked into the middle of my family. And they closed ranks around me like I belonged to them." She laughed, one broken note, and wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "Ten years I built my whole life on the idea that keeping people away from me was the same thing as keeping them safe. And your entire town just proved me wrong in under fifteen minutes."

"Not the whole town," I said. "Mrs. Penhale's still convinced you're going to reopen the cinema."

That got the laugh out of Wren, a real one, startled out of her, and the sound of it did something to the air in the room — loosened it, warmed it. Nora laughed too, wetly, and then she crossed the floor and put her arms around me, right there in front of the window with the whole of Harbour Street presumably still watching, and I held her and felt the shaking gradually leave her the way a tide goes out, slowly and then all at once.

"Thank you," she said into my shoulder. "For stepping in front of her."

"It's the only direction I know how to step," I said. "Ask anyone. I've been doing it for ten years."

* * *

We closed early that afternoon. There was no point pretending otherwise; half the town drifted through anyway, ostensibly to buy books, actually to confirm with their own eyes that everyone was upright, and each of them bought something on the way out because that is how this town says a thing it doesn't say aloud. Mrs. Calloway bought the lighthouse ghost sequel after all. Ted bought a book about knots that he almost certainly owned already.

At four o'clock, with the light going copper over the harbour, Wren came downstairs from the flat and stood in the middle of the shop floor with her arms crossed, and announced, in the tone she uses for verdicts:

"We should get hot chocolate. All four of us. Because today was horrible and that's the rule."

"What rule?" Imogen asked.

"The rule that after something horrible, the family gets hot chocolate." She paused, and I watched her weigh something, twelve years old and choosing her words with the care of a bomb technician. "All four of us. That's the family. In case anyone was unclear."

Nobody was unclear. Nobody said anything either, for a moment, because the moment was too large for speech. Then Nora said, "Yes. All four of us," and her voice did the thing it does when she means something completely, dropping all its performance, going quiet and level as deep water.

We locked the till. I turned the sign on the door. And as we filed out into the cold — Imogen first, then Wren dragging Nora by the sleeve toward the bakery, arguing about marshmallows with the passion of a trade negotiation — I stood for a second in the doorway of my shop and looked down the length of Harbour Street, at the lamps coming on one by one, at the water beyond them going dark and patient.

He had said *this isn't finished*. Men like him always say that; it's the last coin they have. But standing there, watching my improbable family argue about whipped cream under a copper sky, I understood something with a certainty that settled into my bones like ballast: he was right that it wasn't finished. It just wasn't finished the way he meant. What had started on this street today wasn't a threat. It was a fact, witnessed by a hundred people and a security camera and a twelve-year-old girl with a telephone: that Nora Ashford was done facing things alone, and that the rest of us were done letting her.

I locked the door and went to join them.

Behind us, in the dark shop, the bell over the door gave one last soft ring, settling, like a promise that had finally stopped waiting to be kept.
