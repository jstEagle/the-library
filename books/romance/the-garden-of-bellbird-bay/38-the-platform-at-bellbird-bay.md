## 38. The Platform at Bellbird Bay

### Oscar

The station at Bellbird Bay had never been built for crowds. It was a single platform of grey timber, a corrugated roof the colour of old pewter, and a bench that generations of waiting lovers and leaving sons had worn smooth as river stone. The timetable board still flipped its cards with a mechanical clatter, though only four trains stopped here each day now — two north, two south — and half of those ran empty.

Oscar arrived forty minutes early because he did not trust himself to arrive on time. On time would mean standing still with nothing to do but think, and thinking was precisely what he'd been doing for three days without relief, like a tongue returning again and again to a cracked tooth.

The two tickets sat in his breast pocket, in an envelope he'd taken from behind the till, where he kept the good paper. He'd bought them at the bakery counter that doubled as the ticket office — Mrs. Calloway selling him rail travel along with his morning loaf, her eyebrows performing an entire silent opera above her spectacles — and he'd carried them home like contraband, set them on the kitchen table, and looked at them until Wren came down for breakfast and said, *Dad, you're staring at those tickets like they're going to bite you.*

*You always say timing is an excuse,* she'd said, pouring her cereal with the terrible confidence of a twelve-year-old who has found the exact sentence that will end the argument before it begins. *You say it about everything. You said it about repainting the shop. You said it about calling the bank. You're saying it right now.*

He had laughed. Then he had not laughed. Then he had gone upstairs and packed a bag he wasn't sure he'd use, because the city was three hours south by rail and Nora's life was there — her publisher, her apartment, the next book already contracted, the whole glittering machinery of the career she'd built out of the wreckage of the one she'd lost. If love meant anything after everything, it meant meeting her halfway instead of standing in Bellbird Bay waiting to be chosen. He had spent ten years being left. He would not spend ten more being passive about it.

So: the platform, the grey morning, the smell of salt and creosote and the cold iron breath of the rails stretching away toward the headland tunnel. Gulls wheeled over the harbour beyond the station wall, crying their harsh news to nobody. The sea was the colour of hammered tin under a sky that couldn't decide between rain and shine.

Oscar stood at the far end of the platform with his hands in his coat pockets, one hand closed around the envelope, and rehearsed what he would say. That was the trouble with being a bookseller — he knew too well how sentences were constructed, where the load-bearing words went, how easily a beautiful structure could collapse if you put the wrong clause first.

*I bought these for me.* No. Too blunt.

*I thought maybe—* No. Too vague. She'd spent ten years in the vagueness he'd mistaken for peace.

*I'm not asking you to come back. I'm telling you I'll come to you.*

That one. That was the one. Simple as a shelf bracket, strong enough to hold weight.

The clatter of the timetable board made him look up. The 10:42 from the north — the train Nora would take if she were leaving, the one Imogen had told him, tight-voiced over the phone last night, that Nora had booked. *She's going back, Oscar. She has a deadline. She has a life. You have to let her have a life.*

He hadn't slept much after that call. He'd lain in the dark listening to the house settle, listening to the sea work at the harbour wall, and somewhere around three in the morning he had understood something so simple it embarrassed him: he could survive her leaving. He had proof; he'd done it once. What he couldn't survive was another decade of wondering whether he'd let the wrong thing happen simply because standing still felt safer than stepping onto a platform.

The rails began to sing — that high thin hum that travels ahead of the train itself, through the steel, through the ground, into the bones of your feet. Down the line, past the signal box with its peeling green paint, a smudge of smoke appeared against the grey hills.

Oscar took a breath. Took out the envelope. Held it.

And then he saw her.

Not coming off the train. Not waiting on the bench. But standing at the *other* end of the platform, half-hidden by the pillar that held up the timetable board, wearing a coat the colour of wet slate and a scarf he recognized — God help him, he recognized it, the blue one with the fraying edge, the one she'd worn the winter they were together, the one he'd once wrapped around her own neck when hers blew off the pier — and she was holding a ticket in her gloved hand, and she was looking at him the way people look at a lighthouse when they've been at sea too long.

Neither of them moved. The train roared in.

It came between them — three carriages of dark windows sliding past, the platform shuddering, the wind of its passage pressing his coat flat against his back — and when the last carriage cleared and the brakes sighed and the doors clunked open onto emptiness, she was still there, and he was still there, and between them lay thirty metres of wet timber platform and ten years of everything.

A single passenger got off: old Mr. Trelawney, back from visiting his sister, dragging a suitcase with a broken wheel that shrieked against the boards. He looked at Oscar. He looked at Nora. He looked at the space between them with the frank appraisal of a man who had lived in Bellbird Bay for seventy years, and then he shuffled off toward the car park, and the conductor leaned out, called something about the delay at the junction, and climbed back aboard.

The train pulled away south.

Nora didn't get on it.

Oscar's legs carried him toward her before his mind gave the order. Halfway across the platform he stopped, because there was something he needed to see first, needed to be sure of, and he lifted the envelope slightly — just slightly — and watched her eyes drop to it, widen, and fill.

"You bought a ticket," she said. Her voice came out rough, unused, as if she'd been standing in this cold a long time.

"So did you," he said.

They looked at each other's tickets. And then — absurdly, impossibly, the way laughter arrives at funerals — they both started laughing. It came up out of them like water finding a crack, helpless and shaking, and Nora pressed the back of her glove to her mouth and Oscar tipped his head back at the grey sky and let it happen, because there was nothing else in the world to do with two useless train tickets and a decade of silence finally breaking.

"I was going to surprise you," she managed. "I was going to come to the shop tonight. I had a whole — I'd written it down, Oscar, I wrote a speech on the train, do you know how humiliating that is for someone who does this professionally—"

"Mine wasn't a speech," he said. "Mine was one sentence. I've been rehearsing it since Tuesday."

"Let me guess." Her eyes were streaming now, from laughter or cold or something else entirely, and she didn't wipe them. "*I'm not asking you to stay. I'm coming with you.*"

The wind moved along the platform, lifting the loose edge of the timetable card so it flapped like a small applause. Oscar looked at her — at the woman who had walked out of his life in the rain and walked back into it through a snowstorm, who had given away a daughter to save her and lost a decade learning the price — and he held out the envelope.

"Open it," he said.

Her fingers shook getting the flap up. Two tickets slid into her palm. Southbound. Today's date. She stared at them, and then at him, and something crossed her face that he would spend the rest of his life trying to describe — grief and gratitude and a kind of astonished tenderness, all arriving at once like weather.

"You were going to give up your shop," she whispered. "Your whole life. For me."

"No." He stepped closer. Close enough now to see the salt dried white on her scarf, the shadows under her eyes, the small scar above her brow he'd forgotten he remembered. "I was going to *share* my life. There's a difference. Wren made me a list of the difference. It's very thorough."

That startled another laugh out of her, wet and bright. "How is she?"

"Terrifying. She hid my suitcase on Monday to see if I'd really go. Found it in the attic behind the Christmas boxes. She says if I'm going to make grand gestures I should at least commit."

"She sounds—" Nora's voice caught. She looked down at the tickets in her hand, and her thumb moved over them the way a person touches a wound to check it's real. "Ten years ago I stood on a platform like this one. Different town. Rain instead of wind. I had a ticket south and a bag with everything I owned, and I told myself I was doing the brave thing." She looked up. "I wasn't. This is the brave thing. Staying is the brave thing. It took me ten years and a twelve-year-old to learn it."

"Nora—"

"I'm not finished." She held up a hand, and he saw her gather herself the way she must gather herself before readings, before interviews — except this time there was no audience, no craft, nothing between her and the saying of it. "I bought this ticket yesterday afternoon. After Wren's birthday. After I watched her blow out twelve candles in a room full of people who love her, and I realized I could stand in that room forever and it wouldn't be mine unless I chose it out loud. So I went to the inn and I booked a seat on this train — the *staying* kind, the open return, the one that doesn't go anywhere — and I sat up all night knowing that when you found out, you might tell me it was too late. That ten years is ten years. That some doors—" her mouth twisted, "—some doors stay closed."

"I wrote that rule down once," Oscar said quietly. "In a notebook. I lived by it for a decade." He reached out and took her gloved hand, the one holding the tickets, and closed both his hands around it. "Then a woman walked into my shop during a snowstorm and made the rule look like exactly what it was. A door I was hiding behind."

Above them the gulls turned. Out past the harbour wall the tide was coming in, filling the bay the way light fills a room when you finally open the curtains. Somewhere behind the station, faintly, the bakery bell rang the hour, and Mrs. Calloway — Oscar would swear to this later, and later he would swear it was true — paused in her sweeping of the front step to watch two figures on the platform lean toward each other like sentences completing.

"Nora Ashford," he said. "Stay."

"I bought the ticket," she said. "I'm staying."

"Not the ticket." He touched the envelope still in her other hand, the one with his two southbound trains inside, going nowhere now, worth every penny. "Me. Stay with *me*. Not for a season. Not for distribution meetings. Not until the next deadline pulls you south. Stay the ordinary way. School runs and stocktakes and Sunday dinners with your terrifying sister. Stay badly. Stay tired. Stay."

She was crying properly now, and laughing through it, and her forehead came to rest against his chest the way it used to on slow mornings in a smaller flat above a different street, and when she spoke her voice was muffled against his coat.

"You should know," she said, "that I am extremely difficult to live with in deadline season. I talk to characters who aren't real and ignore people who are."

"I've raised a twelve-year-old. Nothing frightens me."

"And I will write about you. Eventually. In a book. Everyone will know."

"They already know," Oscar said. "The dedication page sold out twice."

### Nora

Later — much later, after the walk back along Harbour Street with their shoulders bumping, after Mrs. Calloway's triumphant wave from the bakery window, after Oscar had made coffee in the machine behind the till with the particular ceremony he reserved for occasions (two cups, the good beans, the ones he hid from tourists) — Nora stood in the middle of the bookshop and understood that she was memorizing it.

She'd done this before, in other rooms: her first apartment, the hospital corridor, Imogen's kitchen the night everything ended. The mind, she had learned, takes photographs without permission. But this was the first room she'd ever stood inside wanting the photograph, choosing it, saying *yes, this, keep this.*

Rain had begun again outside — soft, unhurried, the polite coastal rain that meant nothing and threatened nothing — and it ticked against the shop windows while the lamps made small gold pools between the shelves. Upstairs, faintly, Wren's music played, and beneath it her footsteps crossed and recrossed the floor of her room, the restless orbit of a girl who had heard the front door and was pretending, magnificently, not to care.

"She knows you're here," Oscar said, following her gaze to the ceiling.

"She's been watching from the window since we turned the corner. I saw the curtain move."

"She's been watching from the window since Tuesday. I think she suspects we're having feelings. She finds it deeply embarrassing."

"Good," Nora said. "Something should embarrass her. It builds character."

He laughed, and the sound of it did something to the room, loosened it, made the shelves seem less like walls. She drifted along the fiction bays with her coffee, trailing her fingers over spines, and stopped where a face-out display stood on the oak table by the window: her own novel, *The Winter Harbour*, stacked in a careful pyramid beside a handwritten card in Oscar's even, bookish script — *Local author. Ask us why we waited ten years to stock it.*

"You can't put that out," she said.

"It's the best marketing copy I've ever written. People keep buying it just to ask."

"Oscar."

"Wren wrote the second line." He nodded at the card, and there, beneath his neat hand, in a twelve-year-old's looping scrawl: *Ask the tall one. He tells it better.*

Nora laughed until she had to set her coffee down. And then, because the room was warm and the rain was gentle and the man she loved was watching her with an expression she had spent ten years drawing from memory and was now, at last, seeing live, she took the pen from the till cup, pulled her own novel from the stack, and opened it to the dedication page.

*For the boy on Harbour Street.*

Beneath it, in ink that feathered slightly on the soft paper, she wrote:

*And for the girl who taught him that timing is an excuse. — N.A., who stayed.*

"There," she said, capping the pen. "Now it's true in two places."

Oscar came around the table and read it, and read it again, and when he looked up his eyes were bright and his jaw was doing the thing it did when he refused to feel something in public. "Wren's going to be insufferable about the second line."

"Wren's going to frame it."

"Wren's going to quote it at school."

"Wren," said a voice from the top of the stairs, "is standing right here and has been for four minutes, and yes, I'm framing it, and also Dad, your phone's been ringing."

Oscar fished the mobile from his pocket, glanced at the screen, and Nora watched his face change — a flicker, quickly smoothed, the way water smooths over a stone.

"Imogen," he said.

"Tell her," Nora said, and her heart was suddenly loud in the quiet shop, louder than the rain, louder than the gulls. "Tell her I'm staying. Tell her—" She stopped. Started again. Ten years of rehearsals, and still the true sentences came hard. "Tell her I want Sunday dinner. If the invitation still stands. If she can bear it."

Oscar looked at her for a long moment — measuring, she thought, not doubting — and then he raised the phone.

"Imogen. Yes. Yes, she's here." A pause. His eyes came up to Nora's and stayed there, steady as a mooring. "No — she's not leaving." Another pause, longer, and Nora heard the tinny thread of her sister's voice, too low to catch the words, and braced herself for fury or tears or both.

But when Oscar spoke again, there was something in his tone she hadn't heard before — something almost like wonder.

"She says," he reported slowly, lowering the phone, "that she'll bring the good plates. And that she's been keeping a box for you. Since the beginning. She says—" he frowned slightly, translating, "—she says you'll understand when you see it. She's bringing it Sunday."

Nora went still.

A box. Kept since the beginning. Ten years of Sundays, ten years of dinners she hadn't been invited to, and all that time Imogen — practical, infuriating, loyal-to-the-bone Imogen — had kept something aside, something she'd guarded through a decade of guilt and distance, waiting for a day she perhaps never believed would come.

"What's in it?" Wren asked from the stairs, with the directness of her years.

"I don't know," Nora said truthfully. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. She looked at Oscar, at the rain silvering the windows, at the small gold pools of lamplight and the pyramid of her own books and the girl on the stairs with her mother's laugh and her father's stubbornness — *her* father, in every way that counted, and hers now too, if she earned it.

Outside, the storm-light was fading toward evening, and the bell over the door stirred in a draft though no one had entered, ringing once, softly, like a promise kept.

"Sunday," Nora said. "Then we find out."
