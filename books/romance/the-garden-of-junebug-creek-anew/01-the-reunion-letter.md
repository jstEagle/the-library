## 1. The Reunion Letter

The envelope was cream, which should have been my first warning. Nobody in my family sends cream. The Zhous communicate by text message, by shouted fragments across parking lots, by the passive-aggressive medium of forwarded chain emails with subject lines like FW: FW: FW: SO TRUE. Cream stationery means my mother has been to the stationery store in Millbrook, which means she has planned something, which means I am already in trouble.

I found it on a Tuesday morning, wedged between the screen door and a flyer for a gutter-cleaning service, addressed in her handwriting—that upright, unyielding script of a woman who learned penmanship under circumstances she refuses to discuss and considers cursive a moral obligation. The return address was her own house, four blocks away, because my mother does not believe in the postal system so much as she believes in demonstrating that the postal system works.

*Wendell Zhou,* it said. Not *Wendell.* Not *Mr. Zhou.* The full formal treatment, like a summons.

I stood on the top step of the bookshop's back stairs with the envelope in one hand and a mug of coffee going cold in the other, and I did what any reasonable man does when he senses an ambush: I stood there long enough for the coffee to die completely before opening it.

Inside, two things. An invitation card—cream again, embossed this time, someone at the print shop had earned their week's wages—and a note on lined paper torn from a legal pad, because my mother believes formality is a coat you wear only as long as necessary.

The card read:

**THE ZHOU FAMILY REUNION — FORTY YEARS OF GATHERING**
*Saturday, June 14th · Harmony Pavilion, Junebug Creek*
*Potluck · Games · Family Portrait*

Forty years. I did the math standing there, coffee mug dangling from two fingers. Forty years of reunions, and I had attended twenty-nine of them, first as a child dragged along under threat, then as a teenager pretending to be elsewhere, then as a young man showing off a fiancée, then as a husband showing off a wife, and then—

Then three years of nothing. Three years of my mother attending alone and coming back with leftovers I hadn't asked for and reports I hadn't requested: *Your aunt Rosemary's hip is better. The Lees brought that noodle thing you like. Everyone asks about you.*

Everyone asks about you. In Zhou family language, that sentence is less a comfort than an invoice.

And now, the note. My mother's handwriting got smaller here, more careful, the way it does when she is being delicate, which is to say dangerous:

*Wendell — It has been too long. Junie needs to know her family. You need to know yours. This year we are doing the portrait properly, all of us, no excuses. I have told everyone you will be there.*

*I have also told them you will be bringing someone.*

*Do not argue with me on paper. Come to dinner Sunday.*

*Mama*

I read it twice. Then a third time, holding it closer to the light as if the words might rearrange themselves into something survivable.

*I have also told them you will be bringing someone.*

There it was. Not a question. Not even a suggestion dressed as a question. A fact, announced in advance, printed into the permanent record of the family gossip network before I'd agreed to anything, before there was anything to agree *to*, before there was a single candidate human being on earth I could produce for the occasion.

My mother had invented a person. She had conjured a companion out of maternal stubbornness and mailed the fiction to me in cream stationery, and now somewhere across Junebug Creek, forty-some Zhous and their assorted satellites were already speculating. By Wednesday it would be at the nail salon. By Friday, Auntie Rosemary would have opinions.

I went inside. I put the kettle on, because the situation called for tea, and tea requires waiting, and waiting is how I think.

* * *

The shop below was quiet in the way it is quiet every weekday morning before ten—not empty, never empty, but hushed, the particular silence of a room full of people who aren't there yet. Morning light came through the front windows in long dusty panels and lay across the poetry section like it owned the place. Maybe it did. Poetry doesn't sell. Poetry just sits there being beautiful and unsold, which is more than I can say for most things.

I opened the register without turning on the computer. I watered the fern by the door, which Lian had bought the summer Junie was born and which I have kept alive through what I can only describe as spite—mine or its, unclear. I straightened the display table, where somebody had shelved a cookbook face-out next to a memoir about grief, an accidental cruelty I corrected immediately.

At eight-fifteen, feet hit the floor overhead in the pattern I know better than my own heartbeat: the thump of small legs off the bed, the pause at the doorway (checking the hallway, checking for monsters, checking whether the day was safe yet), then the drumroll down the stairs.

Junie arrived in the kitchen nook behind the register wearing pajamas with dinosaurs on them and an expression of tremendous purpose.

"Papa. I need to tell you something important."

"Good morning to you too."

"It's really important." She climbed onto the stool behind the counter—the stool that used to be Lian's reading spot, though Junie doesn't remember that and I don't say it—and folded her hands like a tiny lawyer. "Miss Okafor says at school that families come in all different shapes."

"That's true."

"And some families are a mama and a papa and some are just a papa." She watched me carefully. "Or just a grandma. Or two papas. Or a papa and a dog."

"Also true."

"So ours is a papa-and-Junie family. Which is a real shape."

"The realest shape," I said, and meant it, and felt the letter in my pocket get heavier anyway.

She nodded, satisfied, mission accomplished, and held out her hand for the toast I'd already started making, because eight-year-olds deliver philosophy only in exchange for carbohydrates.

While she ate, I made her lunch and thought about the word *someone*. My mother hadn't written *a friend.* Hadn't written *a guest.* She'd written *someone*, lowercase, singular, loaded. And the worst part—the part I kept circling back to while cutting the crusts off a sandwich I'd cut crusts off a thousand times—wasn't the ambush itself. It was that some tired, lonely, unguarded corner of me had read those words and felt, for half a second, something adjacent to hope.

That corner of me could go jump in the creek.

* * *

After drop-off—I walk Junie the six blocks to school every morning, past the hardware store, past the creek footbridge, past the Ashford Community Garden sign that always makes something in my chest turn over quietly and look away—we came back to the ordinary machinery of the day. Story hour at eleven. A delivery of damaged boxes I had to dispute over the phone with a distributor named Gary who has never once been sorry. An elderly customer, Mrs. Pruitt, who comes in Tuesdays to buy romance novels and complain about them, and who today complained that the heroine forgave the hero too easily.

"Some things you don't forgive easy," she said, thumping the paperback on the counter. "Some things sit in you like a stone."

"What do you do with the stone, then?" I asked, because Mrs. Pruitt appreciates being engaged with.

"You carry it," she said, "until your back gets used to the weight," and she left with her novel and I stood there holding a scanner gun feeling like I'd been handed a fortune cookie with teeth.

In the afternoon lull, I took out the legal pad. If my mother could conduct family business on legal pad paper, so could I. I was going to write her a refusal. A firm, dignified, final refusal. I was going to write several, actually, because I knew myself, and I knew that the first would be too angry, and the second too sad, and the third would probably be the one I almost sent.

**Draft One.**

*Mama — No.*

I looked at it. It was honest. It was also the kind of thing that would result in my mother appearing at the bookshop within the hour, sitting down in the customer chair, and not leaving until she had extracted from me either compliance or a complete emotional inventory, possibly both, likely with dumplings involved as leverage.

I crossed it out and tried again.

**Draft Two.**

*Mama — I appreciate the invitation, and I know you mean well. But I cannot bring 'someone' because there is no someone. There is me, and there is Junie, and we are a complete unit, and I need you to tell your sisters to stop asking. I am not incomplete. I am not a project. I am a father raising his daughter and running a bookstore and I am fine.*

I read it back and heard my own voice cracking through the middle of it like ice on the creek in March. *I am fine.* Nobody writes *I am fine* four times in one paragraph unless they are performing it. My mother would read that paragraph and hear exactly what I heard: a man arguing with a mirror.

Also—and this was the humiliating part—there was a stone in my back that had gotten very heavy all of a sudden.

I set Draft Two aside. Outside, rain had started, soft and unhurried, the kind that makes the whole street smell like wet asphalt and possibility. A woman came in shaking out an umbrella, browsed the history section for forty minutes, bought nothing, and left. That's retail. That's life. Things come in, things go out, most of them leave nothing behind.

**Draft Three.**

*Mama —*

I stopped after the dash. Because here is the thing I have never said out loud, not to her, not to anyone, not even to myself at three in the morning when the house above the shop is so quiet I can hear the fern photosynthesizing: the reason I stopped going to the reunions wasn't grief. Or it wasn't only grief. It was the portrait.

Every reunion ends with the family portrait. Forty years of portraits hang in my mother's stairwell, and I grew up looking at them, and in each one the family is arranged by generation and importance, and there is a space that has always been mine. For years that space held me and Lian, her hand in the crook of my elbow, laughing at something my uncle said right before the shutter clicked. The last portrait I appear in, she's in it too. After that, blank years. My mother swears there isn't a gap in the wall. My mother has hung a plant in front of the gap. My mother thinks I don't know about the plant.

If I go to this reunion, I stand in that space again, and whatever shape they photograph will be a shape with Lian cut out of it. And if I bring *someone*—if I bring anyone—a stranger will be standing where she stood, and the camera will record it, and the portrait will hang on that stairwell for another forty years, and some future child of the Zhou clan will point at it and ask, and the answer will be a story I haven't figured out how to tell.

You cannot refuse a portrait. You can only refuse to be in it, and I have done my three years of refusing, and all it got me was a plant in front of a gap in a wall.

I wrote: *Mama — I'll come. I'll bring Junie. I can't promise anyone else.*

Then I sat there for a long time, listening to the rain, knowing it was a lie in the sense that all my letters to my mother were lies: technically accurate, spiritually fraudulent. Because I wasn't going to show up alone. I knew my mother, and I knew the machine she'd started, and I knew that showing up alone would be worse than not showing up at all—the pity, the whispers, Auntie Rosemary's head tilting like a concerned cockatoo. Showing up alone was not an option the universe was offering.

Which meant I needed a someone. A fake, rented, temporary someone, the way you rent a tuxedo: worn for one evening, returned undamaged, no feelings exchanged.

Junebug Creek has maybe nine hundred residents. I know all of them by their book-buying habits. The dating pool, such as it was, consisted of Mrs. Pruitt (seventy-nine), the new vet (engaged), and whoever wandered in off the highway looking for a map.

As if summoned by the sheer desperation of the thought, the door chime rang.

* * *

She came in dripping, shaking rain off a dark coat, and I want to say I noticed her eyes first, or her smile, or some other detail that would make a better story later. What I actually noticed was that she tracked the room like she was casing it—door, window, counter, exits—and that she had the particular posture of someone returning to a place she once knew and finding it changed. People who've been away carry themselves differently. They hold maps in their spines.

"Do you have—" she began, and stopped, and started over. "I'm looking for a map. Of the town. Which I realize is ridiculous, because it's a small town, but it's—" She gestured vaguely at the rain, the shelves, the whole concept of Junebug Creek. "It's not the town I remember."

"We have one map," I said. "It's from 2009, and it still shows the Dairy Freeze, which burned down in 2014, so adjust your expectations accordingly."

Something happened to her face then—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile was deciding whether to live. "Honest advertising. I'm impressed."

"I find it builds trust." I pulled the map from the drawer behind the register, the one nobody had bought in years, and spread it on the counter between us. Her finger came down on it and moved along streets she clearly once knew, pausing at corners, tapping twice at the creek.

"Everything's smaller," she said, mostly to herself.

"Or you're bigger."

She glanced up at that, quick and assessing, the way people look at you when they're deciding how much of themselves to spend. Rain dripped off the hem of her coat onto my floorboards. Somewhere upstairs, Junie dropped something and yelled "I'M OKAY" preemptively, which is the family motto.

"Vega," the woman said suddenly, offering her hand across the counter and the map. "Marisol Vega. I used to live here. Eleven years ago."

"Wendell Zhou." I shook it. Her grip was brief and firm, a handshake conducted by someone with places to be. "This is my shop. Well—it's the town's shop. I just sweep it."

"Modest bookseller."

"Accurate bookseller."

She looked at the map a moment longer, and then at me, and I had the strange, dislocating sensation of being seen by someone who was also busy not being seen—a mutual arrangement of disguises, two people each carrying something heavy and pretending the weight was normal.

"Well," she said, folding the map. "How much?"

"For you? Free. It's wrong anyway."

She laughed—one short syllable, surprised out of her—and paid for a paperback of poems she picked up on impulse from the table by the window, and the bell chimed her out into the rain, and I stood there thinking about cream envelopes and rented tuxedos and the fact that I had just met the first person in three years whose handshake felt like it belonged to someone with secrets of her own.

Upstairs, Junie thundered down half the stairs and shouted through the banister: "Papa! Grandma called! She wants to know if your someone likes noodles!"

I closed my eyes.

Nine hundred residents. One map. Nine days until the reunion.

Somewhere out there in the rain, apparently, was a someone. I just hadn't negotiated the terms yet.
