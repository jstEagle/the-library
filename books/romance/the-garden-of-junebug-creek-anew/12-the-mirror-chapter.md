## 12. The Mirror Chapter

The bathroom mirror at two in the morning is a cruel invention. Whoever decided that houses should come equipped with a piece of glass designed to show you exactly what you look like when you have failed to sleep—flourescent-lit, jaw stubbled, eyes the color of old dishwater—that person was not your friend.

I stood in front of it because I had given up on the bed. The bed had become a negotiation table where I offered my body to sleep and sleep declined, politely, for the fourth hour running. Junie's nightlight glowed under her door down the hall, a small amber promise that she was breathing, that the house was holding, that the world had not yet found another way to take something from me.

In the mirror, a man looked back who I recognized the way you recognize a stranger from your hometown: familiar features, wrong context.

"Okay," I said to him. "Let's do this."

He didn't answer. He never does. But he stayed, which is more than most people manage at two in the morning, so I pulled the stool from the corner—the one I use to reach the top shelf where Lian's good teacups live, untouched, gathering dust like a museum exhibit titled *Before*—and I sat down in front of myself.

Here is what nobody tells you about guilt: it doesn't announce itself. It moves into your house gradually, the way dust does, the way a smell does, until one day you can't remember what the room was like without it. For six years I had lived with mine so comfortably that I'd mistaken it for furniture. I called it grief. Grief is respectable. Grief gets casseroles and knowing nods and a pass from the aunties at every reunion. Guilt, if you name it correctly, gets questions. And questions have answers, and answers have consequences, and I had built an entire life—a bookshop, a daughter, a careful, curated, dust-free existence—on not having to say any of them out loud.

But Marisol Vega had come back to town with a stolen case file and a lawyer's eyes, and she had stood in my shop three days ago looking at me like I was a document she was still reading, and something in me had started to shake loose. Not because of anything she said. Because of the way she waited. Lawyers wait. They know the truth is a animal that eventually has to come out of the brush for water, and they are very, very patient by the water.

I wasn't ready to be water. But apparently I was ready to sit in front of a mirror at two in the morning and start negotiating with the man in it.

"All right," I said again. "The night Lian died."

My voice sounded strange in the tiled room. Flat. Like a deposition.

"You were there."

* * *

Here is the version of that night I told people, the version I told Junie when she got old enough to ask, the version I told myself so many times it wore smooth as a river stone: I was driving home separately. Lian had stayed late at the Hendersons' dinner party—I'd left early with a headache—and somewhere on Miller's Bend, a car came around the curve too fast and took her from the world. I arrived after. I saw the lights. I held what there was left to hold. A tragedy. A stranger's cruelty. Nobody's fault but the driver's, whoever he was, whoever he would turn out to be.

And here is the version that lives underneath, the one I have never said aloud, the one that has been sitting in my chest for six years like a stone swallowed whole:

I left the Hendersons' party angry.

Not big-angry. Small-angry. Married-angry. We had argued in the kitchen over nothing—over whether we should tell the Hendersons we couldn't make their summer trip because money was tight, or go and quietly suffer; over whether I was too proud; over whether she was too generous with other people's opinions of our finances. It lasted four minutes. It was the kind of argument that dissolves by the time you've driven ten minutes, the kind you laugh about later over dishes. She kissed my cheek when I left. She said, "Drive slow, you maniac," which was her way of saying *I love you, come home*, and I said something back—I don't even remember what, some dry little deflection, some Zhou-family specialty of affection delivered sideways—and I drove off fast, because I was annoyed, because I wanted the road to myself, because I was thirty-one years old and immortal and stupid.

I was on Miller's Bend maybe ninety seconds ahead of her. Maybe less.

I heard the impact before I understood it. That's the part that lives in my body now, the part that wakes me at 2 a.m. sharp, punctual as a debt collector: the sound. Metal doing something metal should never do. And then the silence after, which was worse, which was the loudest thing I have ever heard.

I stopped. Of course I stopped. I want to be clear about that, mirror, about you, about whoever else ends up hearing this confession in whatever form it finally escapes me: I stopped the car. I ran back up the road. I was the first one there—or near enough to first that it doesn't matter, because what matters is what I saw, and what I saw was Lian's car folded against the guardrail like paper, and another car already pulling away, taillights shrinking, going, going, gone around the far curve while I stood in the road screaming a name.

I never saw the driver. I never saw the car clearly—it was dark, it was rain-slicked, it was *fast*. What I knew, in the animal part of my brain, was only this: someone hit my wife and did not stop, and I was ninety seconds behind her, and if I had not been angry, if I had not driven off fast, if I had let her kiss my cheek and then waited, waited, waited—

Then I would have been in the car with her. Then either we both would have died, or—this is the thought, the true thought, the one I could never look at straight on—we both might have been fine. Slower. Together. Two cars taking the bend at a civilized speed instead of one wife alone in the path of a drunk or a coward or whoever it was.

Or maybe none of that. Maybe the math doesn't work that way. I'm a bookseller, not a physicist. But grief doesn't do physics. Grief does arithmetic of the cruelest kind: *if only*, minus *if only*, equals *you*.

So here is what I did, in the days after, when the deputy came around with his clipboard and his tired eyes: I told him I hadn't seen anything. I told him I'd been home. I told him I arrived after, that I didn't hear anything, that I didn't see taillights, that I had no information, Officer, I'm sorry, I wish I did.

Why?

That's the question, isn't it. That's the one I sat on this stool to answer. Six years of not answering it, and here is the answer, and God help me it's small and ugly and human:

Because I was afraid they'd ask me why I was ninety seconds behind her. Because I was afraid they'd ask about the argument. Because I was afraid that if I told the truth—if I said *I was there, I heard it, I saw the taillights*—then someone, someday, would do the arithmetic, and the sum would be me. Because it was easier to be the widower than the witness. Easier to be the man tragedy happened *to* than the man who might have been able to describe the car, the direction, the speed—the man whose testimony might have caught a killer inside a week instead of letting him drive home to his family and his charity galas and his sainted reputation while I sold paperbacks above a creek and taught my daughter that Mommy's garden died of neglect.

I told myself I was protecting Junie. She was two. She wouldn't have remembered a deposition. I told myself I was protecting Lian's memory from being picked apart by investigators, from becoming a case file instead of a person. Noble reasons. Reasonable reasons. Reasons I repeated until they almost believed themselves.

But the truth, mirror, the truth I am saying to your smudged glass at two in the morning while my daughter sleeps forty feet away:

I protected myself. I chose silence because silence kept me blameless. Silence made me the widower instead of the husband who'd stormed off ahead of his wife on a wet road. Silence meant no one would ever stand in a courtroom and say, *Mr. Zhou, how fast were you driving?* No one would ever suggest—not even wrongly, not even cruelly—that the accident was somehow, in some fractional, unforgivable-to-myself way, connected to me. I couldn't survive that suggestion. So I made sure it could never be spoken, and the price was justice for Lian, and the price was six years of a lie so quiet I mistook it for peace.

There. Said. The stone is out. It doesn't feel like relief. It feels like the moment after you set down something heavy and your arms don't know what to do.

* * *

I went downstairs after that, because sitting in a bathroom talking to yourself past three is the kind of thing that ends with the aunties organizing an intervention. The shop below was dark, that particular darkness of books at night—all those spines standing at attention like a congregation waiting for a sermon nobody's giving. I put the kettle on because my hands needed a job, and while it heated I took down the pad of cream stationery Lian bought the year we opened the shop. She'd ordered it special, embossed with a little sprig of jasmine, because she said a love letter deserved better than loose-leaf. She wrote me exactly one letter on it, hidden in my suitcase before a conference, three sentences long, and I still have it in the drawer of my nightstand where I keep things I can't explain to insurance companies.

I sat at the children's table in the reading corner—Junie's table, knee-high, painted with sunflowers—and I wrote to my dead wife on jasmine stationery with my knees up around my ears, and I am telling you honestly: it was the most honest posture I have managed in six years.

*Lian.*

*I don't know where letters go when the addressee is everywhere, so I'll just write and trust the mail system of whatever comes next.*

*You kissed my cheek and told me to drive slow. I didn't. You know that now, wherever you are—you've probably known all along, which must have been its own kind of loneliness for you, watching me build a cathedral out of a lie. I was behind you on Miller's Bend. I heard it. I saw the taillights go. And I told the deputy I'd been home, and I have been telling everyone, including myself, that same lie ever since, and I am done.*

*I'm not writing this to ask forgiveness. I think forgiveness is a door you walk through yourself; nobody can carry you. I'm writing it because Marisol Vega is back in town asking questions I should have asked six years ago, and I have realized that the person I was protecting with my silence was never Junie. It was me. And Junie deserves better than a father who models self-protection as a virtue. She deserves a father who shows her what it looks like when a man tells the truth with his whole chest, even late, even ugly, even when the truth makes him smaller instead of bigger.*

*Here's what I remember about the car, now that I'm finally letting myself remember: dark, maybe gray or black, newer than most cars in this town, moving fast enough that the sound arrived before the sight of it. Taillights, low and wide. Going toward the Ashford side of the valley. I know what that means. I've known since Marisol said the word "file" in my shop and went pale as newsprint.*

*I'm sorry I left fast. I'm sorry I was angry about money, of all the stupid, small, mortal things. I'm sorry I let them close your case in an afternoon because closing it closed mine too. I'm sorry I tended your grave more faithfully than your memory. I'm sorry I told Junie the garden died of neglect when really it died because I couldn't stand to look at soil you'd knelt in.*

*Junie is eight. She reads under the covers with a flashlight and argues with characters in books like they can hear her. She has your stubbornness and my terrible poker face. She asked me last week whether new people are allowed to grow in your garden, and I gave her a bedtime story instead of an answer, and I owe her that answer, and I owe you this letter, and I owe the truth to whoever is patient enough to wait for it.*

*Drive slow, wherever you are. Or don't. Fly, if you want. You always said I drove like a maniac. You were right. About that and, I suspect, about everything.*

*Yours — W.*

The kettle screamed halfway through and I ignored it, which Lian would have found hilarious, since ignoring kettles was her signature move and I was constitutionally incapable of it. The tea, when I finally made it, tasted like pencil shavings and apology. I drank it anyway, at the tiny table, in the dark shop, surrounded by ten thousand stories about people who confessed too late, and I thought: not this one. This one confesses on time. Late by six years, yes—but on time relative to the alternative, which was never.

* * *

Dawn came the way it does in Junebug Creek in autumn: gray, apologetic, smelling of woodsmoke and creek damp. I hadn't slept. I'd moved past needing to. There's a clarity on the far side of a sleepless night that feels almost chemical—everything stripped down to its load-bearing walls.

At seven, feet on the stairs: Junie, in her dinosaur pajamas, hair like a dandelion that lost an argument with the wind. She climbed onto the chair across from me at the sunflower table, surveyed the stationery, the cold tea, my face.

"You look like Grandpa's dog after the bath," she said.

"Thank you. I worked hard on it."

She squinted at me the way she does when a character in a book does something she finds suspicious. "Did you stay up all night?"

"I wrote a letter."

"To who?"

And here it was, the first test of the new administration, arriving before I'd even had coffee. The old me would have said *nobody, sweetheart* and ruffled her hair and changed the subject to pancakes. The new me—the one with the stone out of his chest, the one whose arms didn't know what to do yet—took a breath.

"A grown-up letter," I said. "To Mommy. About some things I need to fix."

Junie considered this with the gravity of a supreme court. Then she nodded once, satisfied, and delivered her ruling: "Good. You should also fix the garden. Marisol says the soil's still good under all the weeds. She checked." She paused, and added, with devastating casualness, "We're starting Saturday."

"We?"

"The garden team." She slid off the chair and padded toward the kitchen. "Pancakes, please. With the blue faces."

Blueberries. Blue faces. I sat there a moment longer, letter in hand, listening to my daughter rummage for the pan she wasn't tall enough to reach, and I understood that the reckoning wasn't finished—it had only changed venues. The mirror had gotten its confession. Now the town would get its questions, and Marisol Vega would get her answers, and somewhere out there a man with sainted taillights was waking up in a warm house believing himself safe.

I folded the letter. I didn't seal it. Some letters you send; some you carry.

Saturday, then. The garden. And before that—today, this morning, as soon as the shop opened—one retired deputy to find, and one question I should have asked six years ago:

*What did they close the file without?*
