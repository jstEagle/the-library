## 16. Empty Rows

Grief, I have learned, looks a lot like productivity.

By six a.m. the morning after I threw Delaney Voss off my property — off my property, off my *life*, off the trellis row where they'd kissed me like a promise they had no intention of keeping — I was already in the east block with a pair of shears and something to prove to absolutely no one. The light hadn't fully committed yet. The valley was still gray and soft-edged, the way it gets before the sun burns the romance out of everything, and I moved down the rows cutting fruit with the mechanical fury of a woman who had decided that if her hands were busy enough, her heart would get bored and give up.

It didn't work. For the record. In case you were wondering.

The grapes didn't care about my personal catastrophe. That's one of the things I love about them and one of the things I resent most deeply. A cluster of Syrah does not ask whether your fake boyfriend turned out to be the corporate assassin sent to dismantle your entire inheritance. It just hangs there, heavy and purple and indifferent, waiting to be picked. I picked it. I picked all of it. Row after row after row, until my shoulders burned and my fingertips were stained black and the sun climbed high enough to make me sweat through my shirt, and still the ache in my chest sat there like an unpaid bill.

"August." Pru's voice came from the end of the row, careful in the way people are careful around a lit match. "Honey. You've been out here since dawn."

"I'm aware."

"You missed breakfast."

"I ate a grape."

"That's not breakfast, that's a garnish." She came closer, arms crossed over her apron, deviled-egg diplomacy face firmly in place. "The crew's asking questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind where Marisol wants to know why the sommelier's truck is gone and Denny from the co-op is telling everyone he saw them loading up at five a.m. looking like a funeral." She paused. "Also someone said they saw them at the gas station buying a road atlas, which, honestly, who uses a road atlas? Very suspicious behavior either way."

"Pru."

"I'm just reporting the news. I'm not editorializing."

"You're always editorializing."

"Well, yes," she admitted. "But today I'm doing it gently." She looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her drop the act. Her voice went softer. "August, sweetheart. Do you want to talk about it?"

There was a version of me — the version from four months ago, before a stranger bought me a beer and lied beautifully on my behalf — who would have said *no* and meant it and built a wall so fast you'd hear the mortar dry. But the thing about letting someone in is that afterward there's a door-shaped hole in you, and cold air comes through it whether you want it to or not.

"No," I said. And then, because the door was already open: "They work for Meridian. They've been working for Meridian since before we met. The whole thing was an assignment. I was — the vineyard was the target. I was just the person standing in front of it."

Pru absorbed this the way she absorbed most shocks, which was to reach into her apron pocket, produce a peppermint, and begin unwrapping it with great deliberation.

"Well," she said finally. "That little snake."

"Yeah."

"And the kissing? Was that also in the report?"

"Pru."

"I'm asking for tactical reasons."

"It wasn't in the report," I said, and my voice did something humiliating on the last word, so I turned back to the vines and cut another cluster harder than necessary. "I don't know what was real. That's the whole problem. I don't know how much of it was cover and how much was—" I gestured vaguely at my own chest, at the wreckage therein. "This."

Pru put the peppermint in her mouth, considered, and then did the kindest thing anyone had done for me all week: she picked up a second pair of shears and started working the row beside me without saying anything else at all.

We harvested in silence for two hours. It was almost bearable.

* * *

By noon the gossip mill had achieved full operational capacity, which in Junebug Creek means the speed of sound multiplied by boredom.

I discovered this when I ran into town for supplies — diesel for the tractor, yeast for the crush, the basic chemistry of pretending life continues — and the errand took forty minutes and cost me approximately eleven conversations I did not consent to.

Denny at the feed store: *"Heard your fella cleared out. Real shame. Good palate on that one."*

Marisol at the co-op: *"August, honey, if you need somebody to key his rental car, I know a guy."*

Mrs. Halloran, who technically doesn't work anywhere but occupies the post office like a barnacle with opinions: *"My cousin works at the county clerk's office and she says there's been a corporate inquiry about parcel lines out your way. Take that however you like."*

I took it exactly how I liked, which was badly.

Because that was the other thing, wasn't it. The thing underneath the thing. Delaney hadn't just broken my heart; they'd broken it *usefully*. Every lie they told me was also intelligence. Every Sunday dinner, every walk down the rows, every time I'd talked with wine-stained hands about the debt and the tasting room and Odette's failing health and Randall circling like a well-dressed vulture — I'd handed them the whole playbook, annotated, with footnotes. Somewhere in a glass tower in the city there was probably a document with my grandmother's mortgage terms in it, sourced to *primary contact, A. Brightwater.*

I bought my diesel. I paid cash. I did not cry in the feed store, though I want credit for how close it came.

Driving back, the valley did its usual trick of being beautiful at me — gold light on the hills, the creek flashing silver through the willows, all of it looking exactly like the place I'd described to Delaney on our third night, when I'd told them it was the only place I'd ever figured out how to stand. They'd listened like it mattered. That was the cruelest part. Not the lying itself — liars lie, fine, water is wet — but the *listening*. Nobody had ever listened to me like that. Turns out it was a job requirement.

* * *

Odette found me at dusk in the crush pad, hosing down equipment I had already hosed down twice.

She stood at the edge of the concrete with her cane and her cardigan and her seventy-nine years of not being fooled by anybody, and watched me scrub a press that gleamed like a wedding ring.

"You're going to wear the paint off that," she said.

"It needs cleaning."

"It needed cleaning yesterday. Now it needs therapy." She lowered herself onto the crate we keep by the door, the good crate, the one reserved for sitting-down conversations, which meant I was in trouble. "Come sit."

"I'm almost done."

"August."

I turned off the hose. The sudden quiet rang in my ears. I sat on the far end of the crate, as far as the crate allowed, which was not far enough.

For a while she just looked out at the vineyard, the way she does, like she's reading a letter from an old friend. The last light was coming off the hills and the rows went blue in the shadow.

"I knew," she said.

I looked at her. "You knew what?"

"About the boy. The girl. Whatever Delaney was." She waved a hand, encompassing the whole impossible pronoun situation. "I knew from the first weekend. From the reunion."

The words landed wrong, like a stair that isn't where your foot expects. "What do you mean, you knew?"

"I mean I've been running a blind tasting in this house for thirty years, child. I've watched sommeliers and wine reps and one unfortunate man who claimed to be a cheese importer come through my dining room. Delaney Voss could name the vintage of a Finger Lakes Riesling by smell alone. Then suddenly they can't tell Pinot from Gamay?" She snorted, elegant even in derision. "They threw that tasting on purpose. To protect your story. You think I raised fools?"

"But then why—"

"Why did I let it go on?" She folded her hands on her cane. "Because I wanted to see what you'd do when you thought nobody was checking your homework. And because—" she paused, and for a moment the sharpness in her face went somewhere else, somewhere older and more tired, "—because I wanted to watch you be happy, even if it started as a lie. Especially if it started as a lie. Most true things do."

I stared at her. The hose dripped behind us. Somewhere up the hill a quail was making its idiot noise.

"You let me humiliate myself," I said. "You let me fall in love with a spy in my own kitchen."

"I let you fall in love," she corrected. "The spy part was their poor judgment."

"This isn't funny, Grandma."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. But listen to me now, because I'm only going to say this once and I'm old and I reserve the right to nap through your rebuttal." She turned to face me fully, and her eyes — Odette's eyes, the ones that had measured every harvest of my childhood, that could judge ripeness from a golf cart at forty yards — fixed on mine. "You're acting like the fact that it began as a lie makes the rest of it worthless. Like there's a clean version of love that arrives pre-approved, paperwork in order, and anything else is counterfeit. That's not how it works. That has never once been how it works. Your grandfather courted me to spite his mother. Your parents met because your father rear-ended your mother's car and was too broke to admit whose fault it was. Being loved imperfectly is still being loved, August. Sometimes it's the only way anybody manages it."

"They signed a contract," I said, and my voice came out flat and hard, anger doing its excellent impression of composure. "They wrote a report about us. About *you*. They sat at your table and ate your food and priced your life for a spreadsheet. That's not imperfect, Grandma. That's not a rough draft of love. That's surveillance with a nice smile."

"And yet they stayed," she said quietly. "Weeks past the reunion. Harvesting. Fixing the irrigation line at two in the morning. Why would a spy do that?"

"Because it's good cover."

"For eight weeks?"

"Yes!"

"Child." She reached over and took my chin in her hand, firm, the way she used to when I was small and sulking, turning my face toward hers. "I have buried a husband, outlived both my children, and held this land through three floods, two recessions, and the year Randall got a business degree. I know what it looks like when someone stays because they're getting something out of it. And I know what it looks like when someone stays because they can't make themselves leave. Delaney Voss was the second thing. I'd bet the vineyard on it." A beat. "Which, given current circumstances, is a substantial bet."

I pulled my chin free. Something hot was climbing my throat, and I hated it, because it wasn't anger, it was the thing under the anger, wearing anger's clothes, trying to get out.

"You want me to forgive them," I said. "You want me to just — what, call them up? Say, hey, no hard feelings about the corporate liquidation plot, welcome back to dinner?"

"I want you to stop confusing being hurt with being right."

"They *lied to me.*"

"People lie to you constantly," Odette said. "Randall lies to you. The bank lies to you. The state of California lies to you about water rights. The difference is those liars never once made you laugh until you cried over a burnt casserole. You're not angry because they deceived you. You're angry because for the first time in your adult life, somebody chose you first — and you found out about it second. That's a different wound, and you keep dressing it in the wrong bandages."

I stood up. My legs felt strange, too tall, borrowed.

"Don't," I said. "Don't do the wise-grandmother thing. Don't turn this into a lesson. They walked off this property with everything they needed to destroy us and I let them in the door and poured them wine and you *knew*, and you said nothing, and now you want me to feel grateful for the education." I grabbed the hose, more for something to hold than any practical purpose. "Being loved imperfectly is still being loved. Fine. Beautiful. Put it on a tea towel. But some imperfections aren't flaws in the love, Grandma. Some of them are the whole point. Some people don't love you *imperfectly*. They just love the asset."

She looked up at me for a long moment. I braced for the rebuttal, the scalpel, the Odette treatment.

Instead she sighed, gathered her cane, and rose slowly from the crate.

"All right," she said. "Refuse the lesson. You're entitled. I refused several myself at your age, and look how well-adjusted I turned out." She patted my arm once, briskly, on her way past. "But I'll say one more thing, and then I'll nap through your rebuttal as promised."

"Grandma—"

"There's a car coming up the drive," she said. "Black sedan. Been idling at the gate for ten minutes. And the man inside it has been on his phone the whole time, taking pictures of the north fence line." She glanced back at me, and her mouth was grim. "So whatever you decide about love, child, decide it quickly. Because I don't think it's Delaney Voss in that car — and I think whoever it is, they're not here for the view."

I turned toward the gate.

Headlights swept the drive as the sedan rolled through it, slow and deliberate, eating up the distance between the road and everything I had left.
