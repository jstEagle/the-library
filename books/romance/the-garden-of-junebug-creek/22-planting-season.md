## 22. Planting Season

Spring came to Junebug Creek the way forgiveness does — slowly, and all at once, and mostly when you weren't looking.

One morning in late March I walked out to check the frost damage on the Chardonnay and found the whole hillside wearing a green haze so faint I thought my eyes were going. By the following Tuesday the valley had committed to the bit entirely: mustard blooming between the rows in reckless yellow, the creek running loud and showoffy, the air smelling like wet earth and possibility, which is a smell I had spent three years training myself not to trust.

I still didn't entirely trust it. But I'd learned to stand in it.

Delaney was already up on the south hill when I carried the coffee out — two mugs, because some rituals you build without noticing, the way a vine builds itself around a wire. They were kneeling in the dirt with the survey map spread out beside them, weighing it down with rocks, looking for all the world like someone who had always belonged to this hill instead of someone who had once, in a bar in Junebug Creek, lied their way into my life with the smooth confidence of a professional and the terrible aim of an amateur.

They'd gotten better at lying, incidentally. They just didn't do it anymore. Which I supposed was growth, or love, or both — in my experience the two were hard to tell apart at this range.

"You're staring," Delaney said, without turning around.

"I'm supervising."

"You're staring at me over the rim of a coffee mug like I'm a problem you haven't solved yet."

"I solved you in October," I said. "Now I'm just enjoying the view."

They turned then, and the morning light caught them the way it catches everything on that hill — generously, like the land itself had decided to flatter its residents. Dirt on their knees. A smudge of it along one jaw where they'd pushed their hair back. Delaney Voss, former rising star of the Meridian Group, current employee of Junebug Creek Vineyard at a wage Odette had set personally and described, in front of the whole family, as "generous for a person with your limitations."

They'd never once complained about the wage. Or the title. Or the fact that "employee" was doing a lot of load-bearing work in the sentence describing what they actually were to me.

"Row's marked," they said, tapping the map. "Forty vines. Enough to make a barrel, if we're lucky, and a memory, if we're not."

"And if we're both?"

Delaney smiled — that crooked, unguarded smile I'd only started seeing after the festival, after the truth, after everything it cost them. "Then we make a toast we can't drink for five years."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about planting a vine: it is an act of staggering optimism conducted on your knees in cold mud.

The varietal was Delaney's idea, which had surprised everyone, including Delaney. Tannat — a grape from the foothills of the Pyrenees, stubborn and late-ripening, a grape that needed patience the way some people needed oxygen. It wouldn't bear a real crop for five years. Five years. In the wine business that's practically a marriage vow.

"Everyone else in the valley plants what sells," Odette had said when Delaney pitched it at Sunday dinner, arms crossed, eyes narrowed to their sharpest configuration. "Why should we plant what doesn't?"

"Because what sells is what everybody has," Delaney said. "And Junebug Creek was never everybody."

The table had gone quiet — the particular quiet of the Brightwater-Voss extended experiment in family, which by then included Pru and her deviled eggs, Pru's daughter Cassie and her tractor opinions, the two hired hands who'd stayed through the winter, and, on alternating Sundays, a chastened and surprisingly useful Randall, who had discovered that abstaining from one vote buys you a surprising amount of moral credit and that moral credit, spent carefully, could purchase a second chance.

Odette had looked at Delaney for a long moment. Then she'd looked at me — a look I have spent my whole life being the object of and still cannot fully translate — and said, "Fine. But if it fails, you're both eating the vines."

It was, from Odette, a benediction.

* * *

The hillside where we planted was the hillside where we'd rehearsed.

I don't know if Delaney thought about it that day in June — the fake first kiss, staged in the trellis shadows, the one that had gone wrong in all the right ways. I thought about it constantly that first winter, back when Delaney was still earning back trust row by row, showing up at dawn without being asked, fixing the irrigation line alone in the freezing gray light while I watched from the kitchen window and pretended I wasn't watching.

I'd understood it finally, watching them that morning — the thing Odette had tried to teach me in the empty rows, the lesson I'd refused like a child refusing medicine. Love isn't the grand gesture. Love is the irrigation line fixed at six a.m. when nobody's keeping score. Love is the person who stays after the applause, after the accusation, after the part where they have every reason to leave, and asks for nothing except the chance to keep showing up.

So when the planting map went up, and the best south-facing slope was available, I didn't say anything. I just moved the X.

Delaney noticed. Of course they noticed. They noticed everything; it was their curse and their profession. They looked at the map, then at me, and something moved across their face like weather.

"This is where—"

"I know where it is."

"You want to plant five years of patience on top of one bad kiss in a trellis."

"It wasn't a bad kiss," I said. "It was a fake kiss that forgot to stay fake. There's a difference. Ask any lawyer."

"Ask any sommelier," they said, "and they'll tell you the best wines come from the worst years. Stress in the vine. Struggle in the roots. You get depth from difficulty or you don't get it at all."

"That's either very romantic or a justification for everything you did last summer."

Delaney had learned, in our months of careful reconstruction, when to laugh and when to stand still. They stood still. "Both," they said. "It can be both. That's kind of the whole point of us."

* * *

The family gathered on planting day the way families gather for weddings and funerals — which is to say, with food, opinions, and at least one person crying who insisted they weren't.

Pru arrived with a basket of deviled eggs, because in our family deviled eggs are not food but sacrament, and she arranged them on the tailgate of Cassie's truck with the gravity of a woman laying a foundation stone. Cassie brought the auger and a running commentary on soil compaction that nobody had requested and everyone received. The hired hands came with shovels and the particular good humor of people who get paid whether the boss's love story succeeds or not. Randall showed up in boots that had clearly been purchased for the occasion, price sticker still ghosting the sole, and stood at the edge of the gathering like a man at a party where he'd once set the curtains on fire.

And Odette came last, as Odette always came last, because Odette understood that the person who arrives last arrives as the point.

She was eighty-one years old and she walked that hillside like she owned it, which she did, which I did, which — after the cooperative buyout closed in November, after every signature but one had been pried loose and the one holdout had finally, in the words of our lawyer, "gotten religion" — we all did, in the way that mattered.

"Well," she said, surveying the marked rows, the auger, the stack of bare-root vines looking like a bundle of sticks that had been promised a future. "Tannat."

"Tannat," Delaney confirmed.

"Five years."

"Five years."

"I'll be eighty-six." Odette's eyes glittered. "I intend to be insufferable about the first bottle. I want it stated for the record that I called the vintage."

"You'll call the vintage," Delaney said. "You'll call everything. You always do."

Odette smiled the smile she saved for people she'd decided to keep — a smile I'd seen her give maybe four times in my life, and twice of them had been at Delaney, which told me everything about how completely my grandmother had been won over by a person who had once, on her own property, deceived her entire family.

Odette had known, of course. She'd known from the first deviled egg. She told me that in the empty rows, back when I was grieving in the costume of anger, and I hadn't wanted to hear it. I heard it now. Being loved imperfectly is still being loved. The vine doesn't refuse the rain because the rain was late.

"August," Odette said, and I braced, because when my grandmother used my full name outdoors it was usually followed by either a blessing or an audit. "Your parents planted the block below this one the spring before you were born. Did you know that?"

I hadn't. Or I had, in the way you know things as children — as furniture, as background, as facts without weight.

"Your mother wanted Pinot Noir. Your father said this hill was too hot for Pinot and she said he was too cautious for her, and they compromised by planting what she wanted and watching it struggle for six years before they pulled it out." Odette looked down the slope, at the rows I'd grown up running through, the rows that held my parents the way amber holds a fly. "They laughed about it every harvest. Your father used to say the only thing that vineyard ever produced was arguments, and your mother used to say arguments were a crop too, if you fermented them right."

I hadn't known that either. There was so much I hadn't known, back when knowing felt like a luxury I couldn't afford — back when I'd cataloged my life in losses, forty acres and a debt and a heart I kept meaning to fence off. Hope as an expense. I'd run that ledger for years. It had nearly cost me everything worth counting.

"So plant your stubborn grape," Odette said. "Plant it right where you want to. And when it struggles — and it will, everything worth keeping struggles — you remember that this family has never once produced anything easy." She picked up a shovel and handed it to me, handle first, the way you hand someone a key. "Now stop standing around. The holes won't dig themselves, and I'm not getting younger, and neither are you, though you have the decency to hide it better."

* * *

We planted forty vines that day, and it took most of it.

Delaney dug; I set the roots. That division of labor was not discussed and did not need to be. There is a thing that happens when two people have worked a harvest together, when they've stood shoulder to shoulder in the crush pad at midnight with juice to the elbows — a rhythm establishes itself below language, below thought, in the place where the body keeps its own promises. Delaney's shovel bit the earth; I followed with the vine, spreading the roots out in the hole like fingers unclenching, like a hand finally opening after years of holding itself shut.

"Am I doing this right?" Delaney asked, on the ninth vine. "The depth. You keep looking at me like—"

"You're doing it right."

"You keep looking at me like I'm doing it wrong."

"I keep looking at you," I said, "because I'm allowed to now. It's one of the terms and conditions."

They grinned and drove the shovel in. "I never read the terms and conditions. That's how I got into this."

"That's how everybody gets into everything worth being in."

Cassie worked the auger down the row, hollering soil reports like a war correspondent. Pru circulated with eggs and commentary — "Delaney, you've got dirt on your face, no, other side, honestly, you'd think a sommelier would have better presentation" — and Delaney took it all with the easy grace of a person who had learned that being fussed over by this family was not a burden but a rite, and that the fastest way into the Brightwater heart was to let Pru fix your collar in public without flinching.

Even Randall dug two holes. Badly. Nobody said anything, which was its own kind of grace, and Randall seemed to understand that the silence was the gift, and dug a third hole slightly less badly, and by the end of it he was standing next to Odette looking out over the rows with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself for years: the look of a person who has just realized the thing they were trying to escape was the thing that would have saved them.

By late afternoon the hillside held forty new vines in forty new holes, staked and tied and watered, a field of sticks with delusions of grandeur. They looked like nothing. They looked like a bet placed against every reasonable odd. They looked like a promise you couldn't cash for five years, made by two people who had already learned — the hard way, the expensive way, the way that leaves scars you end up fond of — that the only promises worth making are the ones you can't keep quickly.

Delaney stood at the top of the row and looked down it, at the little line of green-tinged roots and bare wood marching toward the creek, and I watched their face do the thing it did when they were moved and trying to file it under something more professional.

"Five years," they said quietly.

"You can leave, you know," I said. "Any time. That's still true. I want it to stay true. I don't want you here because the gate closed behind you."

Delaney turned to me. The light was doing the golden thing it does on that hill in late afternoon, the thing that had made me fall for the valley in the first place and had made me fight for it, tooth and nail, ledger be damned.

"I spent ten years traveling," they said. "Ten years of tasting rooms and cellars and hotel bars, being excellent everywhere and home nowhere. I told myself it was freedom." They shook their head. "It was just rootlessness with a wine list. And then I came to a town called Junebug Creek to help tear it down, and a woman lied to me about being her boyfriend, and somewhere between the deviled eggs and the blind tasting I started lying to myself instead, which was worse, and then—" they stopped, and started again, because Delaney Voss, for all their polish, had never once been able to make a grand speech without tripping over the true part. "And then you ordered me off your property, and I stood in a meeting in a glass tower holding a pen over the best contract of my life, and I read your text, and I walked out. And everyone said I'd lost my mind. And they were right. That's what it was. I lost the mind I had, the one that thought five-year plans were for cowards and staying was for people without ambition." They took my hand, dirt and all. "I'm not here because the gate closed. I'm here because I finally found the one place I want to be when it opens."

I looked at them for a long time. The creek ran loud below us. The mustard burned yellow between the old rows. Somewhere down the hill Odette was telling Cassie, at volume, that the irrigation schedule was a suggestion only for people who hated wine.

"Five years," I said. "That's a long time to wait for a glass of wine."

"We've waited longer for worse."

"We have absolutely waited longer for worse."

Delaney laughed, and pulled me in, and kissed me on the hillside where we'd once rehearsed a lie — and this kiss, unlike that one, did not land wrong, did not go too long, did not curdle into want and retreat. It landed exactly right. It took exactly as long as it took. There was nothing to rehearse anymore and nothing to perform and no one watching except a family that had already made up its mind about us and a hillside that had been waiting, I think, longer than either of us.

When we broke apart, Odette was watching from the tailgate with her arms crossed and her eyes suspiciously bright, pretending to inspect the deviled eggs.

"Five years," she called up. "And if that Tannat isn't ready by my eighty-sixth birthday, I'm haunting both of you."

"You can haunt the vines," Delaney called back. "They'll be used to stubborn women by then."

Odette's laugh carried up the hill like something planted.

* * *

That night, after the family had gone and the dishes were done and the tasting room was dark, I found Delaney out on the porch with the survey map spread across their knees, marking the rows in pencil, planning a harvest we wouldn't live to see for years.

I sat down next to them. The spring air smelled like wet earth and possibility, and I let it.

"Whose handwriting is that?" I asked, nodding at the margin, where someone had written, in a strong slanted script: *First bottling — Odette's 86th. Do not fail.*

"Your grandmother's. She snuck it on there while Pru was distracting me with eggs. I've decided not to ask how she does that."

"Nobody asks. It's one of the mysteries that holds the valley together."

Delaney set down the pencil and looked out at the dark hillside, where forty vines stood in the blackness with their whole future ahead of them. "You know what I keep thinking about?"

"Terroir?"

"Terroir," they admitted. "But also — five years ago I was in a different valley, in a different country, drinking something extraordinary, and I remember thinking, this is the best thing I will ever taste, and feeling nothing. Absolutely nothing. And I couldn't figure out why the best thing felt like nothing." They turned to me. "And then I came here, and everything was hard, and I lied, and I lost things I thought I couldn't live without, and I gained things I didn't know existed. And now I plant a grape that takes five years to bear fruit on a hillside where I once kissed a stranger for practice, and I can't stop smiling, and I think—" they paused, and the pencil turned over in their fingers, once, twice. "I think I finally understand what I was tasting for. It was never in the glass."

I leaned my head against their shoulder, and we sat there in the dark like two people with nowhere better to be, which was the whole point, which had always been the whole point.

"Five years," I said.

"Five years," Delaney said. "And then forever."

Down the hill, in the black rows, forty vines stood in the spring dirt and began, quietly, without ceremony, the long patient work of keeping a promise — and for the first time in as long as I could remember, Junebug Creek smelled like a beginning, and I believed it.
