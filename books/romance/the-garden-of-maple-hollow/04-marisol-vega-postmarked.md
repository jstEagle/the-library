## 4. Marisol Vega, Postmarked

The letters sat on the counter between the register and the espresso machine for two hours before Quentin touched them again.

Not because they weren't the only thing in the room. Because they were. The morning rush came and went—four customers, all regulars, all ordering the usual—and Quentin made their drinks and rang them up and smiled the smile that cost nothing and meant nothing, and the whole time the bundle sat there in its faded ribbon like a heart someone had left on the counter by mistake.

Dot was the last to leave. She paused at the door, looked back at Quentin, looked at the letters, and said nothing at all, which from Dot was a speech.

When the bell over the door stopped chiming, Quentin locked it. Flipped the sign. Stood in the sudden hush of an empty café at eleven on a Wednesday, listening to the refrigerator hum and the radiator tick and their own pulse, which had picked up somewhere around the second hour and hadn't slowed down.

*Marisol Vega.*

Even thinking the name felt like pressing a bruise to see if it still hurt.

It did.

Quentin untied the ribbon—slowly, absurdly carefully, as if the letters might spook—and carried the bundle to the corner booth, Theo's booth, the one with the window and the good light. Sat down. The wax paper crackled. There were nine letters inside, each one soft-edged from eleven years of dark, each one addressed in handwriting Quentin would have known blindfolded, upside down, underwater: *Quentin Sandoval, 14 Alder Street, Maple Hollow.* The loops of the M like birds taking off. The V in Vega sharp enough to cut.

Postmarked June through October, eleven years ago.

Nine letters over five months. And then nothing. Quentin counted them twice, as if a tenth might appear if they wished hard enough, and then unfolded the first one with hands that didn't feel entirely like their own.

---

*June 3rd*

*Q.*

*I've written this four times. I keep starting with "I don't understand" and that's not a letter, that's a complaint, so let me start over.*

*I don't understand.*

*No. Okay. Third try.*

*You said Tuesday. You said we'd load the car Tuesday night and drive until the sun came up and figure out the rest after that. I packed my bags Monday. My mom cried and then she hugged me and then she cried again, which is very her, and I sat in my room last night with everything I own in two duffel bags and you didn't come.*

*I called your house. Your mother said you were asleep. At nine o'clock. In June. Q, I stood on your porch and watched your bedroom window and the light never came on, and either you were asleep—which you are not, you have never once in your life been asleep before midnight—or you were lying there awake listening to me knock, and I honestly don't know which one would be worse.*

*So here's what I need you to do. Call me. Come over. Leave a note under my door, I don't care. Just tell me what happened, because I know something happened, and I can't fix it if I don't know what it is.*

*You're my best thing, Q. You know that? Out of everything, you're the best thing. Don't make me find out what the world is like without you in it.*

*M.*

---

Quentin read it three times. The café blurred. The window light went watery.

Age twenty-four lived in a different body than this one—younger, thinner in the face, heavier everywhere else, carrying the specific arrogance of a person who believes the future is a place they've already been. Twenty-four-year-old Quentin had worked the morning shift at a bakery in Corvallis and come home smelling like yeast and ambition, and Marisol had been there, always, cross-legged on the kitchen floor of the apartment above the garage, grading papers or not grading papers, talking about Spain, talking about teaching, talking about the plan.

The plan had been simple, the way plans are when you're twenty-four and broke and certain. Save for a year. Drive west until the maples ran out. Find some coastal town where rent was a rumor and open a place together—a café, obviously, but hers too, a bookshop in the front maybe, or a classroom in the back, Marisol tutoring kids whose schools had given up on them while Quentin baked in the mornings and wrote bad poetry in the afternoons. They'd had a name for it already. They'd had a name for everything. That was the trouble with being young and in love: you named things, and naming made them real, and real things could be lost.

*The Garden,* they'd called it. Because Marisol said every town needed one place where people could grow.

And then—

---

The night itself arrived the way it always did when Quentin let it: sideways, uninvited, in pieces.

A Tuesday in early June. Heat lightning over the ridge. Quentin's mother in the kitchen of the house on Alder Street, smaller than she'd been even a year before, the illness already drawing lines on her that Quentin hadn't learned to read yet—or had learned, and refused to. She'd been stirring tea she wasn't drinking. She'd said, *Sit down, mijo. We need to talk about your girl.*

*My girl.* As if Marisol were a problem to be managed.

What exactly had been said in that kitchen, Quentin could no longer reconstruct. Memory had done what memory does to scenes we can't bear whole—it had kept the fragments and burned the connective tissue. His mother's voice, level and tired: *She'll leave you. People like that always leave. Her family, they move every few years, you've seen it. You'll build a life and she'll be gone, and you'll be thirty with nothing.* Something about how Quentin had the café now—the lease, the loan, the dream his parents had co-signed into existence—and how you couldn't run a business and chase a girl across the country at the same time. Something about sacrifice. About how love that asks you to abandon everything isn't love, it's a test, and failing it early is cheaper than failing it late.

And Quentin—twenty-four, exhausted, terrified in a way that had no name yet—had sat at that table and let the words in. Had let them rearrange the furniture of the future. Had thought, in the small coward's voice that lives behind everyone's sternum: *maybe she would leave first. Maybe it's better if I leave first.*

So Quentin hadn't gone. Hadn't called. Had turned off the phone and lain in the dark listening to Marisol knock on the front door—three times, patient, then not patient—and had done the hardest thing Quentin had ever done, which was nothing.

In the morning, the Vega family's station wagon was gone from their spot on Delgado Lane. Mrs. Vega had taken a job in another state; everyone said so at the hardware store, at the church, at the diner. Gone. Just like that. Just like his mother said people like that always did.

And Quentin had told himself a story to survive it: *she left. She got tired of waiting. It was always going to end.* Told it so many times it wore smooth as river stone, and you could hold it without cutting yourself, and for eleven years Quentin had held it every single day.

The letter in Quentin's hands said otherwise.

The letter said *you said Tuesday.* The letter said *I packed my bags Monday.* The letter said *your mother said you were asleep.*

Quentin set it down on the table, very gently, the way you set down something that might go off.

---

* * *

---

The second letter was dated June 10th, a week later, and the handwriting had changed—not messier exactly, but faster, like someone writing against a clock.

*Q. The car's packed. We leave Saturday for real this time, Mom signed the paperwork, there's no staying now. I'm leaving this at the post office myself because I don't trust anything anymore, including your mail slot, which I know sounds crazy, but I knocked on your door six times this week and yesterday a man was mowing your lawn and he said nobody had been home in days, and Q, I drove past Alder Street tonight and your car was there and your lights were on and you didn't answer, and I stood on the sidewalk and I want you to know what that felt like, because I think you do know, and I think that's why you won't open the door.*

*If you're doing this to spare me, stop. If you're doing this to punish me, tell me what I did. If you're doing this because you're scared—I was scared too. I'm scared of everything. I'm scared of the ocean, Q, did you know that? I never told you because you loved it so much and I didn't want you to love it less. But I was going anyway. I was going to get in that car and drive toward the thing that scares me most because it was going to be next to you.*

*Saturday. If you come to the house Saturday morning, I will wait until noon. After that I'm gone, and I won't hate you, but I'll never understand you, and I think those are different wounds.*

*M.*

---

They had left on a Saturday. Quentin knew that. Knew it the way you know the date of a death. Had woken that morning with the plan fully formed—*go to her, explain, beg, follow the station wagon to the edge of the earth if that's what it took*—and had gotten as far as the porch steps before his mother appeared in the doorway behind him, gray-faced, hand pressed to her side, saying *not today, please, not today, I need you today,* and what kind of monster walks out on that?

So Quentin had stayed. Had held his mother's hand through the worst summer of the illness. Had told himself the story again—*she left, she left, she left*—because the alternative was unbearable, and the alternative was true.

The third letter was from July. Shorter. Colder around the edges, like a house with the heat turned off room by room.

*They gave me your mother's number at the pharmacy. I called it. A woman answered who wasn't your mother—her sister, maybe? She said your family was dealing with a lot right now and it would be best if I didn't call again. Q, I am trying so hard to give you the dignity of a reason. Every day I write down a new theory. You got cold feet. You met someone. You died. You never existed and I invented you, which at this point feels the most likely.*

*I'm in Ashford now. I got a job. Fourth grade. They handed me a classroom and thirty kids and a lanyard and I thought, he would laugh at this lanyard. He'd say I look like a substitute teacher in a movie. I laughed alone in the parking lot and then I cried in the parking lot and then I went inside and taught fourth grade, because that's the job. That's the whole job. You do the thing with the hole in you.*

*This is the last one I'm sending to Maple Hollow. If you get it, and you read it, and you still don't write back—then I'll finally believe you, Q. Whatever you're telling me with your silence, I'll believe it. That's the cruelest thing you could have taught me. That silence counts as a voice.*

---

Quentin read all nine letters that afternoon, sitting in Theo's booth while the light moved across the floorboards and the café stayed pointedly, mercifully empty. By the fifth letter Marisol had stopped asking questions. By the seventh she was writing about other things—a student who'd finally learned to read, a recipe for flan that reminded her of someone, a stray dog she'd half-adopted—and signing off with just *M,* no plea, no *Q,* the salutation amputated like something that had rotted. The ninth letter was four sentences long. It said she hoped he was well. It said she'd heard about his mother, and that she was sorry, truly, and that she knew he must be grieving. It said, *I hope the café is everything you wanted. I hope you're happy. I mean that. I've decided to mean it.*

Dated October 22nd. Eleven years ago almost to the week.

After that, nothing. Not because she'd stopped writing—Quentin understood this suddenly, completely, the way you understand a thing you've spent a decade refusing to understand—but because the letters had stopped arriving. Because somewhere between the Ashford post office and 14 Alder Street, nine letters had become zero, and the silence Quentin had blamed on Marisol had been manufactured in a kitchen on Alder Street by a dying woman stirring tea she wasn't drinking.

*Your mother said you were asleep.*

Quentin's own mother had stood in the doorway of this very building—had she? Had Quentin ever mentioned the letters to her, once, drunk on cheap wine a year after the funeral, crying about the girl who'd left?—and said, in a voice like a closing door: *Some people show you who they are, mijo. Believe them.*

Believe them. As if Marisol had been the one who vanished.

Quentin put both hands flat on the table and breathed, and the breath came out wrong, a sound halfway between a laugh and something else, and then the something else won, and Quentin Sandoval—who had not cried at their mother's funeral, who had not cried when the foreclosure notice came, who had built an entire architecture of calm over a lifetime of falling—sat alone in an empty café and wept over nine letters and a faded ribbon.

It lasted maybe four minutes. Long enough to feel like drowning. Not long enough to finish, because grief that old doesn't drain in one sitting; it comes up in buckets, and there are always more buckets.

---

* * *

---

By evening Quentin had done the only thing that made sense, which was bake.

The ovens roared. The kitchen filled with the smell of browning butter and caramelizing sugar, and Quentin moved through it in the old trance—weigh, fold, turn, rest—hands working while the mind circled the same burning question: *where is she now? Is she in Ashford? Did she ever teach that classroom? Does she still laugh the way she laughed, the full-body, head-back, snorting, undignified laugh that made strangers in restaurants turn around and smile without knowing why?*

Eleven years. She could be anywhere. She could be married. She could have children, a house, a life with no Maple Hollow-shaped hole in it at all. She could be happy, and what then—what does a person do with nine letters and an apology eleven years past its expiration date?

But she could also be reachable. She could be findable. Quentin dried their hands, walked to the office, and sat down in front of the ancient laptop with its dead pixel in the corner of the screen, and typed two words into the search bar:

*Marisol Vega.*

The results scrolled past—professors, realtors, a marathon runner, a woman arrested in Tucson for something involving emus—none of them her, none of them the right her, and Quentin realized they didn't even know enough anymore. Didn't know her middle name. Didn't know her mother's maiden name. Eleven years ago Quentin had known everything, had known the sound of her footsteps on stairs, and now knew nothing, and the nothing was enormous.

Then, on the second page of results, a school district website. *Ashford Elementary — Staff Directory.* And there she was, third row, second from the left in a grid of faculty photos.

Mrs. Marisol Vega-Reyes.

Fourth grade. Room 12.

Quentin stared at the photograph for a long time. She was older—of course she was older, they both were—but the smile was the same, that crooked, whole-hearted, slightly embarrassed smile, as if she'd been caught mid-laugh by the photographer and couldn't quite apologize for it. Beside her name was a line of text, small and administrative and devastating:

*"Mrs. Vega-Reyes joins us with her daughter, Lucía."*

Daughter.

Quentin closed the laptop. Opened it. Closed it again. The kitchen timer went off in the other room and Quentin didn't move, sat in the dark office with the dead pixel glowing like a distant star, understanding that the next thing they did—drive, call, write, hide—would decide everything, and feeling the old familiar gravity of the hiding pulling at them like a tide.

On the counter outside, the nine letters lay in their open bundle, and beneath them, still wrapped in wax paper at the bottom, Quentin noticed for the first time a tenth envelope—thicker than the others, sealed, addressed in a different hand.

Their mother's hand.

It was marked, in her careful cursive, with a single word: *For Quentin. When you're ready.*

Quentin was not ready.

Quentin reached for it anyway.
