## 12. Rufus Repairs More Than Walls

Rufus Delgado had been fixing things in Maple Hollow for thirty-one years, and in all that time he'd learned one truth that held across plumbing, carpentry, and the human heart: most damage wasn't caused by what happened. It was caused by what people did afterward—what they patched, what they painted over, what they pretended they couldn't hear dripping.

The wall behind Sandoval's ovens was a case study.

He stood in the kitchen at seven a.m., coffee from Quentin's machine going cold in his hand, studying the hole he'd cut two days ago. The studs were sound. That was a mercy. But the pipe joint had wept for years before it burst—a slow leak, invisible behind drywall, rotting things quietly until one Tuesday morning it gave up all at once and flooded the kitchen floor with an inch of gray water and eleven years of somebody's letters.

Quentin came out of the back room carrying a box of new copper fittings and set them down without looking at Rufus. That was normal. Quentin didn't look at people much. Not directly, anyway. There was always something else to check—the oven thermometer, the invoice pile, the espresso machine's descaling light. Rufus had watched this for years: a person who moved through rooms like someone trying not to disturb the furniture of other people's lives.

"Morning," Rufus said.

"Morning." Quentin tied on the apron. "I can get the drywall today if you want to just supervise."

"I want you to hand me things and not touch the saw. Last time you nearly took off a thumb."

"That was a box cutter and I was opening a delivery."

"You were opening it like it owed you money." Rufus set his coffee down and picked up the level. "We do this right or we don't do it. Insulation first, vapor barrier, then board. Pipe's already re-soldered?"

"Soldered, pressure-tested, wrapped." A pause. "Twice."

"Good." Rufus nodded at the open wall. "You want to talk about it yet?"

"Nope."

"Okay." He turned back to the studs. Fair enough. He hadn't come to pry. He'd come because Dot had called him last night—Dot called him about everything—and said, *That boy is walking around like a man reading his own obituary, and he's holding letters while he does it. Go be useful.* Dot's instructions were always like that. Specific, bossy, and impossible to refuse without feeling like you'd kicked a dog.

So here he was. Useful.

He fed the first batt of insulation into the cavity between studs, pressing it flat with the heel of his hand. Behind him, Quentin worked the espresso machine through its morning ritual—portafilter, tamp, lock, pull—and the smell of coffee began to fill the room, covering the smell of old plaster and wet wood. Regulars would start drifting in soon. Theo had finals coming up; he'd claimed the corner booth by eight-thirty sharp. Dot arrived whenever she pleased, which was to say early, which was to say when she wanted the first look at whoever else walked through the door.

The work was good. Rufus liked work where you could see your progress. Studs, insulation, barrier, board. A wall went back together in an order. You couldn't say that about much else in life.

They worked most of the morning in a rhythm that didn't need words. Quentin handed him screws, held the board steady, ran the drill when Rufus's shoulder complained—which it did now, reliably, every time he raised his right arm above his chest. Sixty-two years old and the arm kept its own ledger of everything he'd ever lifted.

It was during the second sheet of drywall, when Quentin was cutting holes for the outlet boxes, that the kid finally cracked.

"She wrote forty-one letters," Quentin said, to the wall more than to him. "Forty-one. And I got none of them. My mother—" The sentence stopped. Quentin's jaw worked around whatever came next and swallowed it. "She hid them. All of them. Marisol thought I read them and didn't care. Eleven years she thought that."

Rufus let the silence sit for a moment. He'd learned that from his father, who could fix an engine by listening to it. You didn't rush the diagnosis.

"And what do you think?" he asked.

"I think I'm forty-one letters late."

"That's not what I asked."

Quentin set down the drywall saw. For a second, something raw crossed their face—something younger than forty, something scared. "I think if my mother hadn't hidden them, I still wouldn't have gone after her. Because I didn't go after her when she was *right there*. She lived four towns over her whole childhood. I knew where she worked. I knew her schedule. I chose—" The voice caught. "I chose not to look. The hiding just gave me permission."

There it was. Rufus had suspected as much since the day he'd torn open the wall and seen Quentin's face—not shock at finding the letters, but recognition. Like meeting an old debt.

He wiped his hands on his rag and sat down on the overturned five-gallon bucket he used as a stool. His knees announced the seating arrangement loudly. Quentin glanced over, wary, the way people get when an older person sits down like they're planning to stay.

"I was married once," Rufus said.

Quentin blinked. "You were?"

"Twenty-two years. Elena. You never met her. She moved to Tucson before you bought this place." He turned his coffee mug a quarter turn on the floor beside him, an old habit, aligning the handle toward himself. "People in town know me as the guy who fixes stuff. They don't ask why I live alone above the hardware store with a cat named after a carburetor."

"What happened?"

"The boring kind of tragedy. Nothing dramatic. No affair, no screaming. We just—" He searched for it, wanting to get it exactly right, because getting it wrong would make it a lie, and Elena deserved better than a tidy lie. "We stopped saying things. That's all. It started small. She'd tell me about her day at the clinic and I'd nod at the wrong moment because I was thinking about a job quote. Little stuff. Then bigger stuff. Her sister got sick and she needed me to drive her to Burlington every Thursday for three months, and I said yes but I made sure she knew it cost me something. Made sure she knew I was sacrificing. Kept score out loud instead of just doing it."

Quentin had gone very still. Stillness was Quentin's native language, but this was different. This was the stillness of someone listening hard.

"And then there was the night," Rufus went on, "when she asked me the question. We were at the kitchen table. I remember the table—I built it myself, oak, still have it upstairs, use it as a workbench now, which tells you something about how well I process grief. She looked at me and she said, 'Do you still want this? Us?' And I—" He laughed, short and humorless. "I said, 'What kind of question is that?' Deflected it. Made her feel foolish for asking. Because answering honestly meant admitting I was scared, and I'd rather let her bleed out than admit I was scared."

The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere out front, the door chimed—Dot, probably, arriving to conduct surveillance under cover of ordering a scone—but neither of them moved.

"What did she want you to say?" Quentin asked quietly.

"She wanted me to say 'yes, I'm terrified, and yes, I'm still here.' That's all. Ten words. Maybe twelve." Rufus shook his head slowly. "Instead I said nothing true, and she sat across from me filling up with silence, and six months later she was gone. Left a note on that same table. One line. *You're a good man, Rufus. I just can't keep being the only one talking.*"

He heard Quentin exhale, slow, like air leaving something.

"For years afterward I told myself the story where she left because she stopped loving me. Cleaner that way. But the truth is she left because I made loving me into a solo project. She was carrying the whole conversation for twenty-two years, and one day she put it down." He looked up at Quentin. "I'm not telling you this to scare you straight. I don't do sermons. I'm telling you because when I heard about those letters, I thought: there but for the grace of a nosy landlady go I. Elena never wrote me letters. She talked. I just didn't listen. Same crime, different weapon."

"I ran away," Quentin said. "Literally. Got on a bus at six a.m. so I wouldn't have to see her face when she realized I was gone."

"Yeah?"

"And then I stayed gone. Eleven years. Built a whole life shaped like a locked door." Quentin's hands had found each other in their lap, fingers laced tight, knuckles white. "And now I know she waited. She *waited*, Rufus. She wrote and wrote and I was three hours away thinking she'd forgotten me by June." The voice frayed. "How do you come back from that? What do you even say? 'Sorry, my mom intercepted your mail, also I'm a coward'?"

"You say exactly that," Rufus said. "Word for word. Minus maybe 'coward.'"

"It's not enough."

"No, it's not. Nothing you say will be enough. That's the bad news." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the motion cost his back something he ignored. "Here's the thing nobody tells you about words you didn't say. They don't expire. They just accrue interest. Every year you don't say them, they get heavier. Twenty-two years of unsaid things—that's why I can't lift my right arm over my head anymore. Metaphorically speaking."

A ghost of a smile crossed Quentin's face and vanished. "Metaphorically."

"Mostly metaphorically. The arm's a real problem too." He stood, joints filing their complaints, and picked up the drill. "But here's the other thing. The good news. Unsaid words are the only debt I know of that gets forgiven the moment you finally pay it. Not reduced. Forgiven. Whole balance. Elena's been gone nine years and I still haven't paid mine—she's in Tucson, remarried to a nice man named Gary who plays golf, and I am genuinely happy for her, and I still owe her ten words from a Tuesday night in 2009. Some debts you can't pay the original creditor. So you pay forward. You learn the lesson and you don't repeat it with the next person who matters."

* * *

They finished the insulation before lunch and broke for sandwiches at the counter. Dot was holding court in her usual spot, dispensing opinions on the school board with the confidence of a woman who had attended every meeting since 1987. Theo was in the corner booth, headphones on, textbook propped against the napkin dispenser, occasionally surfacing to accept refills.

Rufus watched Quentin move through the room. Watched them refill Theo's water without being asked. Watched them slide Dot's scone onto her plate with the exact amount of powdered sugar she pretended not to require. Watched them do all of it silently, generously, invisibly—the way they did everything.

And he thought: *this kid has been paying interest on silence their whole life.*

After lunch, the drywall went up. Quentin held sheets while Rufus screwed them home, and the wall closed over the cavity where the letters had slept for eleven years. Before the final panel, Quentin had stood looking into the empty space for a long moment—Rufus saw it, said nothing—and then helped lift the board into place. Whatever ceremony Quentin performed in there was private. Rufus believed in private ceremonies. He'd had one himself, the day he mailed Elena's sister a check for the medical bills and told no one.

By mid-afternoon the wall was boarded, taped, and ready for mud. Rufus ran his palm along the seam, checking the alignment, and found Quentin standing beside him doing the same, unconsciously mirroring him, two sets of hands testing whether a thing would hold.

"It'll hold," Rufus said. "Walls are easy. You build them square and they stay square."

"Is that a metaphor?"

"Everything's a metaphor if you're tired enough." He started coiling the extension cord, giving his hands something to do while he said the rest, because some things were easier said to a power tool than to a face. "One more thing, and then I'll shut up. When you write to her—and you're going to write to her, I've seen you staring at that letter stack like it's a final exam—don't write the version where you explain yourself until you sound reasonable. Write the version where you just tell the truth and let it be ugly. Reasonable is what I gave Elena. Ugly and honest is what she deserved."

Quentin was quiet for a long moment. Out front, Dot laughed at something—her big unguarded laugh, the one she saved for genuine surprises—and the sound drifted back through the pass-through window like weather from a kinder country.

"Rufus," Quentin said. "Why'd you really come today? Dot sent you, obviously. But why'd you say yes?"

Rufus considered lying, decided against it, and settled for the truth at seventy percent strength. "Because I've spent nine years being the guy who almost said something. And I figured if I could help one person not end up like me, that's ten words paid forward." He snapped the cord coil tight. "Also your pipe work was sloppy and I couldn't sleep knowing that joint was behind a wall."

"It was *not* sloppy."

"It was adequate. In my house, adequate is an insult." He shouldered his bag and headed for the door, then stopped, hand on the frame, and looked back. Quentin stood in the middle of the kitchen, flour-dusted and drywall-flecked, lit by the afternoon slanting through the front windows—a person visibly bracing for something, the way they always were, the way they'd been bracing for eleven years.

"One question," Rufus said. "And you don't have to answer it today. But I want you sitting with it."

"Okay."

"When she walks through that door—and she will, because Dot's already scheming, I can see it in the way she keeps checking the parking lot—what are you going to do?" He held Quentin's gaze. "Not say. *Do.* Because I'll tell you what I did when Elena asked her question. I froze. Froze so long she answered it herself, and her answer was goodbye. Don't freeze, kid. Whatever you do—don't freeze."

He let himself out into the cold. Behind him, through the glass, he could see Quentin standing motionless in the empty kitchen, one hand pressed flat against the new wall, as if checking whether it—or anything—would hold.

Rufus climbed into his truck and sat for a minute with the engine off. On the seat beside him was his phone, and on the phone was a contact he'd never deleted: *Elena V.*, Tucson. Nine years of never calling. He looked at it the way you look at a scar.

Then he started the engine, and drove home, and did not call.

But that night, alone above the hardware store, he took a legal pad from the drawer and wrote ten words at the top of the page. He stared at them a long time. He didn't mail anything—he wasn't a miracle worker, just a handyman—but he left the pad out on the oak table where he'd have to look at it every morning.

Some walls, he figured, you tear open yourself.

Two days later, the bell over the café door rang, and a woman with dark hair and a little girl walked in—and Rufus, watching from his truck across the street, saw Quentin freeze.

*Don't freeze,* he thought, gripping the wheel. *Kid. Please.*
