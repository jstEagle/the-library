## 15. Lucía's Birthday

Marisol Vega had planned this party the way she planned everything now: with lists, with contingencies, with a quiet accounting of everything that could go wrong and a private vow to survive it anyway.

Twelve children. Two folding tables borrowed from the church basement. A piñata that had cost eleven dollars and looked like it might disintegrate before anyone got to swing at it. Balloons Lucía had blown up herself until her cheeks went pink, then abandoned in drifts around the living room like colorful weather.

And a cake from Sandoval's.

She had told herself it was practical. Quentin made the best cake within forty miles—this was simply a fact of Maple Hollow, like the way the maples turned all at once in October or how the diner ran out of pie by seven on Fridays. She had called the café on Tuesday, kept her voice level, ordered a vanilla cake with strawberry filling because Lucía had changed her mind four times and finally landed there. Quentin had said *yes, of course* in that careful voice they both used now, the voice of two people walking on a frozen lake, testing each step.

Then Dot happened. Dot always happened.

"You'll want Quentin there," Dot had said at the market, not looking up from inspecting a basket of apples as if she hadn't just detonated something. "Delivery, setup, whatever. Kid's turning eight. You shouldn't be wrestling a cake stand alone."

"It's fine," Marisol said.

"I didn't ask if it was fine." Dot finally looked up, and her eyes were kind and merciless at once. "I've watched you carry everything alone for three years, Marisol Vega. It was admirable for about two of them."

So that was how Quentin Sandoval came to be standing in Marisol's backyard at two o'clock on a Saturday in late September, holding a cake box tied with butcher's twine, wearing a clean shirt that Marisol suspected was an apology for every other shirt, while twelve children screamed over a piñata.

*This is a mistake,* she thought, watching them set the box down on the folding table with the concentration of someone defusing ordnance.

*This is exactly right,* thought some traitorous part of her, the part she'd been starving for three years, and she hated them both—the caution and the hunger—for making her feel like a house divided against itself.

* * *

Lucía spotted the cake before Quentin finished untying the twine.

"Is that—" Her daughter materialized at the table the way only a child can, without crossing the intervening space, simply *arriving*. "Is that the one with the strawberries INSIDE?"

"Inside," Quentin confirmed. "Not just on top. Inside where it counts."

Lucía looked up at Quentin with the frank, total attention children give to people who interest them, and Marisol felt her stomach drop, because she knew that look. She'd worn it herself once, at seventeen, across the counter of a café that smelled like cinnamon, when a pastry chef with flour on their forearms had slid her a burnt croissant and said, *the ugly ones taste better, I promise.*

"Did you make it?" Lucía demanded.

"Every part. Even the strawberries are local—Mr. Abernathy's farm, past the water tower."

"Can I see inside? Right now?"

"Lucía," Marisol started, but Quentin laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of them—and said, "You know what? It's your birthday. But we have to do it properly, with everyone watching, or the cake gets its feelings hurt."

"The cake has feelings?"

"Obviously. Why else would we sing to it?"

Marisol stood by the back door with a pitcher of lemonade going warm in her hands and watched her daughter fall, instantly and completely, under the spell of Quentin Sandoval. And the thing—the unbearable thing—was that it wasn't even a performance. She'd braced for charm, for the practiced warmth of an adult managing a child at a party. Instead she got Quentin crouched to Lucía's height, dead serious, explaining that the frosting roses were made with a trick their mother taught them, and did Lucía want to learn it someday, because it took years and you dropped a lot of roses on the floor first.

"How many years?" Lucía asked.

"Eleven," Quentin said, and something crossed their face like a cloud crossing a field, there and gone, and Marisol gripped the pitcher tighter.

*Don't,* she told herself. *Don't read into it. Don't start keeping a ledger of their wounds. You know what happens when you keep ledgers.*

But she was already writing in it. She couldn't help it. Eleven years. The number sat between them at every encounter now, unspoken, enormous, taking up chairs.

* * *

The party found its rhythm. Children tore through the yard. Theo—sixteen, sullen, secretly devoted—showed up to help and pretended he'd been passing by, which fooled no one, least of all Lucía, who adored him and told him so with the cruelty of the young ("You're basically family, Theo, you don't have to pretend you have somewhere else to be"). Rufus arrived with a repaired swing set bracket and stayed leaning against the fence, drinking lemonade, watching everything with the mild satisfaction of a man whose work was mostly invisible and therefore done.

And Quentin stayed.

That was the thing Marisol kept circling back to, the fact she couldn't put down: they stayed. They refilled juice cups without being asked. They judged the piñata swings with grave impartiality. When a boy named Marcus cried because he didn't get a turn, Quentin knelt beside him and produced, from somewhere, a small extra cupcake—"the tester cake, every baker makes one, it's a rule"—and Marcus's tears stopped mid-fall as if a switch had been thrown.

Marisol watched all of it from the periphery, arms crossed, doing what she did best now: observing, assessing, protecting.

Daniel had been like that with Lucía. That was the thought that ambushed her at the worst moment, while Quentin was teaching six children to play a clapping game Daniel used to play, badly, laughing at his own failure. Daniel had been terrible at games and magnificent at showing up. Three years gone and she still caught herself turning to tell him things—a joke, a grievance, the way the light hit the maples—and finding the space beside her empty, and having to swallow the sentence whole.

*I buried him,* she reminded herself, standing in her own backyard on her daughter's eighth birthday. *I did the work. Grief group for eighteen months. The counselor. The nights I let myself cry instead of cleaning. I did it all correctly.*

And yet here was Quentin Sandoval, laughing with flour-dusted knuckles—no, no flour today, just that clean shirt, sleeves rolled, forearms bare—and her heart was doing something it had no business doing, cracking open along a seam she'd sealed with such care, letting light in, letting light out, being *reckless*, and she was furious.

Furious at Quentin, for being easy with her daughter, for making it look effortless, for not understanding what it cost her to watch.

Furious at Dot, obviously, for engineering this whole afternoon with the subtlety of a marching band.

Furious at Daniel, absurdly, for dying, for leaving her with a heart that had learned to be open and then closed again, both times not by her choice.

And most of all furious at herself, because underneath all of it, plain as a signature, was the truth she could not argue with: she wanted this. She wanted the person at the juice table. She wanted the sound of that laugh in her yard on ordinary Saturdays. She wanted, God help her, to be looked at the way Quentin had looked at her last week over the cake order, when their eyes met for half a second too long and both of them pretended it hadn't happened.

Desire, she thought bitterly, pouring herself lemonade she wouldn't drink. It doesn't check your grief for ID. It just walks in and starts rearranging the furniture.

* * *

The cake, when they cut it, was perfect. Of course it was. The strawberry filling ran red and bright through the white crumb, and Lucía ate her slice with the solemnity of a food critic and pronounced it "the best cake in the history of cakes, better than Mom's, sorry Mom," and Marisol said "noted" and everyone laughed, and Quentin went faintly pink with pleasure and tried to hide it and failed completely.

Later, when the guests thinned to stragglers and the yard was a battlefield of cups and crepe paper, Marisol found Quentin at the folding table, carefully boxing leftover cake slices for the families who'd want them tomorrow.

"You didn't have to stay the whole time," she said. It came out more like an accusation than she intended.

Quentin looked up. In the late-afternoon light, with the noise of children receding, their face was suddenly unguarded, and Marisol saw how tired they were—not party-tired, something older, structural—and how hard they were working to be here, in this yard, in her life's orbit, after everything.

"I wanted to," Quentin said simply. "She's a great kid. You're—" They stopped. Recalibrated. "The cake held up okay? Sometimes the filling shifts in transit."

"The cake is perfect."

"Good." A pause. Quentin's hands stilled on the box. "Marisol, I—"

"Don't." The word came out sharper than she meant, and she watched it land, watched Quentin flinch and absorb it the way people absorb blows they think they deserve. She softened despite herself. "I'm sorry. I just—I can't do a speech in my backyard with piñata debris everywhere. Whatever you're about to say, it deserves better than that."

"Okay," Quentin said quietly. And then, because they were apparently incapable of leaving well enough alone: "For what it's worth, she asked me if I'd come back next year. For her ninth birthday. I told her I'd have to ask her mom."

Marisol closed her eyes briefly. *Traitor child. Wonderful, traitor child.*

"And what am I supposed to say to that?"

"You're supposed to say nothing today," Quentin said. "Today is hers. I just—you should know she asked. That's all."

They gathered their boxes, thanked Rufus, ruffled Theo's hair to Theo's loud protest, and paused at the gate. For one moment they looked back—not at Marisol, at the whole yard, the balloons and the tables and Lucía chasing the dog with a streamer trailing from her shoulder—as if memorizing it, or measuring it, or saying goodbye to something they feared they'd never have.

Then they were gone, and the yard was quieter than it had any right to be.

* * *

That night, after Lucía was asleep and the living room was restored to order, Marisol sat at the kitchen table with a glass of wine she wasn't tasting and let herself do the thing she never let herself do: want, openly, without supervision.

She thought about the handshake eleven days ago, in the café, that had lasted three seconds too long. She thought about the way Quentin listened—not waiting to talk, actually listening, the rarest skill in any room. She thought about the cloud that crossed their face when they said *eleven years*, and the letter-shaped hole in the story she still didn't understand, the silence that had swallowed her twenties, the question she'd carried so long it had grown roots: *why did you leave me without even a goodbye?*

She was angry. She was so angry, and the anger was legitimate, and she intended to keep it.

But she also knew—sitting alone at her table, in the house she'd learned to live in, with her daughter's laughter still hanging in the air like the smell of cake—that anger was not the strongest thing in her anymore. That was the terrifying discovery of the day. Not desire, exactly. Something quieter and worse: recognition. Watching Quentin with Lucía, she'd seen someone trying, clumsily and sincerely, to be worthy of a child's trust. She'd seen someone show up and stay.

And she had felt her guarded heart crack open, and she had felt furious about it, because she had buried a husband and done the work and built the walls correctly, and none of it, none of it, had accounted for Quentin Sandoval walking back into Maple Hollow with a cake and those careful hands and that look they gave her when they thought she wasn't watching.

Her phone buzzed on the table. A text from Dot:

*Saw the yard from the road. Looked like a good party. Ask him about the letters, mija. All of them. Then decide.*

Marisol stared at the screen for a long moment.

*Ask him about the letters.* Dot knew something. Dot, who had known her mother, who had run the town's memory for forty years, who never said anything without three reasons.

Marisol set down the phone. Outside, the first leaves were coming down, and somewhere across town Quentin Sandoval was probably scrubbing cake pans alone in a failing café, and she had a question she'd carried for eleven years, and Dot had just told her it was time to ask it.

She picked up her wine. She drank it all the way down this time.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would ask why he left.

She just hoped she'd survive the answer.
