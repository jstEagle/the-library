## 38. The Question Under the Maples

The first snow came on a Thursday, the kind of snow that doesn't announce itself—just arrives while you're proofing dough and suddenly the window over the sink is white and the whole town has gone quiet in that way Maple Hollow does, like it's holding its breath to see if you'll notice.

I noticed.

I'd been noticing things for months now. That was the strange inheritance of almost losing everything: I paid attention now. To the way Theo's backpack had a new strap because Rufus had sewn the old one. To the way Priya took her coffee—black, but she'd started letting me add a splash of cream without being asked, which from Priya was practically a hug. To the way Marisol stood at the register on Saturdays with her coat still on, like part of her was always ready to leave, and how lately the coat came off earlier and earlier until last week she'd hung it on the hook by the door before she'd even said hello.

Lucía had claimed the corner booth as hers. She did homework there now, colored there, taught herself to fold napkins into swans by watching me and getting them wrong in ways that were better than right. The swans looked like angry ducks. I told her they were a rare breed. She believed me for exactly four seconds and then laughed so hard she knocked over the sugar.

That was the other thing I noticed: how easy it was, now, to be wrong in front of people.

* * *

The market cart had gone home for the season two weeks ago, folded up in the back of Rufus's truck with the awning rolled tight against the cold. Winter market started Saturday—the indoor one, in the grange hall, where Dot had already assigned me a table near the door because, she said, "People need to smell butter the moment they walk in, Quentin, it's not complicated." The line at the debut had wrapped the square. I kept waiting for that to feel real. It still didn't. Maybe it never would. Maybe that was fine.

Thursday night I closed the café late. The renovation was done—new paint, the wall behind the ovens rebuilt proper, a letter sealed inside it addressed to whoever came next—and the place smelled of cedar and vanilla and the ghost of all the years I'd spent here alone, back when alone was the only language I spoke fluently.

Marisol was supposed to come by at seven. She texted at six-forty: *Lucía's at Dot's. Dot insists. I think we've been set up.*

I stared at the message for a long time. Then I typed back: *Come anyway.*

Then I put my coat on, walked out the back door, crossed the yard past the garden beds sleeping under their first blanket of white, and stopped beneath the oldest maple in the hollow.

It stood at the edge of the property, older than the café, older than the town charter if you believed Dot, who believed everything with total conviction and was usually right. In summer its canopy covered half the yard. In winter it went bare and honest, every branch visible, nothing hidden—which was maybe why I'd always avoided standing under it, back when hiding was my whole personality. Tonight the snow came down through its branches in slow spirals, catching the light from the kitchen window, and I stood there with my hands in my pockets and my heart going like a stand mixer on high.

I had a plan. That was the funny part. Quentin Sandoval, who once drove two hours to Ashford and sat in a parking lot for ninety minutes and drove home, had a *plan*. I'd written it down, actually, on the back of an order slip, because apparently I was someone who wrote things down now instead of baking them into cakes and hoping. The slip was in my pocket. It said:

1. Don't say forever.
2. Say next.
3. Mean it.

Three items. Even I couldn't burn that.

* * *

She came around the side of the building at seven-oh-four, flashlight beam bouncing, scarf up to her chin, and when she saw me standing under the tree in the falling snow she stopped about ten feet away and just looked at me.

"You're standing in the snow," she said.

"I am."

"On purpose."

"It's looking more and more that way."

She laughed—that laugh, the one I'd lost for eleven years and found again in a handshake that lasted too long—and came closer, her boots crunching, until we were both under the maple and the snow was coming down between us like the world's slowest confetti.

"Dot has Lucía making cocoa," Marisol said. "And interrogating her about whether she likes you. Lucía told her you make the best croissants in the state and also that you let her crack eggs, so. I'd say the interrogation went well."

"She's a tough room."

"She's eight. She thinks everyone leaves." Marisol said it lightly, but I heard the weight underneath, the same weight I'd heard the day Lucía asked me if I was going to stay, and I'd told her the truth: *I can't promise perfect, but I promise to tell you the truth.* She'd considered this with the gravity of a supreme court, then handed me a crayon drawing of what appeared to be a bear wearing an apron. I still had it taped inside the register drawer.

"Quentin." Marisol's breath made a small cloud. "Why are we standing under a tree in a snowstorm?"

"It's not a storm. It's barely a flurry."

"You have flour on your sleeve and snow in your hair and you're stalling, which is new for you, actually—you used to be terrible at stalling, you used to just bolt—"

"I'm not bolting."

"No," she said softly. "You're really not. That's the thing I keep noticing."

The snow kept falling. Somewhere across town a dog barked twice and gave up. And I reached into my pocket—not for the order slip, it turned out; I didn't need it, I'd known item three since October—and took out what I'd actually brought.

Not a ring. Not yet, not tonight, not something that big. A key. The spare key to the café, on a plain brass fob, the kind Rufus had cut for himself and Theo and Dot and Priya over the past year, one by one, until the hook by the back door looked like a wind chime of trust. Mine had been the only one for eleven years. Now there were six. This would be the seventh.

"What's this?" she asked, though I think she knew.

"A key," I said. My voice did something embarrassing in the middle of the word and I let it. "But before you take it, I want to say the thing, so let me say the thing."

"Okay," she whispered.

"I'm not going to ask you for forever." Snow landed on her eyelashes and didn't melt, or maybe it did and more came. "I thought about it. I drafted it, even—forever, always, the whole vocabulary. And then I threw it out, because forever is a word I used to hide behind. Forever is easy to promise when you don't intend to be held to any specific Tuesday. What I want to promise you is Tuesday."

She made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "Tuesday."

"Next," I said. "I'm asking for next. Next Saturday at the winter market, when you work the register and Lucía hands out samples and Theo takes payments and complains about it. Next Sunday, when you come to the café after church and steal the ends of the baguette. Next month. Next season. Next spring, when the garden comes up where the wall used to be and Lucía learns to knead dough without wearing half of it. I'm asking for the next thing, and then the next one after that. One kept promise at a time. That's all I've got. But I've learned—I finally learned—that's everything. That's the whole thing. Love isn't a forever you declare. It's a next you keep choosing."

The maple creaked above us, shifting its snow like an old man settling into a chair.

"Eleven years ago I left," I said, "because staying felt impossible. Six months ago I packed a bag at three in the morning and got as far as the town line, and you found me there, and you didn't ask me for anything. You just sat beside me in the dark until staying felt possible again. So I'm not asking you to believe in my forever. I'm asking you to hold me to my next. And I'll do the same for yours. Deal?"

Marisol looked at the key in my open palm for a long moment. Snow gathered on her shoulders. Her eyes were bright and wet and furious with feeling in the way I loved most about her, the way that meant she was fighting it and losing.

"You know what I was doing eleven years ago?" she said. "Writing letters to a person who wasn't writing back. You know what I was doing three years ago? Burying a man who promised me forever and kept every single day of it until he couldn't. Daniel gave me forever. It was real and it was beautiful and it ended. So no—" she took a breath— "I don't want your forever either, Quentin. Forever is God's business. But *next*—" her hand closed over mine, over the key, warm despite the cold— "*next* I can do. Next is a promise a person can keep."

"Yes," I said.

"Yes to next," she said. "And yes to the one after that. And the one after *that*, you ridiculous man, standing under a tree in December with flour on your sleeve—"

I kissed her then, or she kissed me, the order of operations lost to history, and the snow came down through the bare branches of the oldest maple in Maple Hollow and landed in our hair and on our joined hands and neither of us moved to brush it off. Somewhere in the middle of it she was laughing against my mouth and I was laughing too, and it occurred to me that this—this exact feeling, joy arriving shyly and then arriving anyway—was what I'd spent eleven years and one burned batch of morning buns trying to bake into bread and never quite could.

Some things you can't put in the dough. Some things you have to stand under a tree and say out loud.

* * *

We walked back to the café hand in hand, leaving one set of footprints each, side by side. Inside, the kitchen light turned the snow at the windows gold. Marisol hung her coat on the hook by the door—first thing, before hello, before anything—and I put the kettle on because that was our liturgy now, tea and the end-of-day report, her school stories, my market numbers, the small currency of a shared life.

My phone buzzed on the counter. A text from Dot:

*Cocoa consumed. Interrogation complete. The girl says yes. Also tell Marisol she left her gloves. Also tell yourself I told you so. —D*

I showed Marisol. She groaned. "She's insufferable."

"She's a prophet," I said. "Forty years running the town's memory. She probably knew before we did."

"She absolutely knew before we did. She knew before *you* did. She knew in 1998."

I laughed and slid her tea across the counter, and she wrapped both hands around the mug, and outside the snow kept falling on the garden beds and the rebuilt wall and the old maple holding its bare branches up like arms opened wide after a long, long time of being closed.

Later—much later, after she'd gone home to Lucía and I'd locked up and stood a minute in the dark kitchen listening to the building settle around me like a living thing—I sat down at the little table by the window with a pen and a sheet of the good stationery Priya had given me as a joke last Christmas. *For a man who owes so many letters,* she'd said.

Because that was the thing about next. Next wasn't just Saturdays and Sundays and springs. Next was a practice. And ours had begun, long ago, with paper.

I wrote the date at the top. Then, beneath the old maple's shadow printed faint on the snow outside the window, I began:

*Dear Marisol —*

*You said yes to next. So here is the first one, kept in writing, the medium that failed us once and gets another chance...*

Outside, the snow went on falling, patient as forgiveness, covering the hollow in white, and somewhere across town a girl slept with a drawing of a bear in an apron pinned above her bed, and a woman named Dot smiled in her sleep for reasons the whole town would be speculating about by morning.

I wrote until the ink ran low and the kettle went cold and the first snow of the year turned, sometime in the small hours, into the second.
