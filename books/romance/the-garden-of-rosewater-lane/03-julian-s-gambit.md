## 3. Julian's Gambit

Julian Voss had always believed that charm was less a gift than a discipline—something practiced before mirrors, honed on waiters and widows, deployed with the precision of a surgeon who had studied anatomy but never once felt a pulse. He arrived in Rosewater Falls on a Tuesday afternoon in early December, driving a rented silver sedan he would later describe to the town as "borrowed from a friend," because *rented* suggested impermanence and *silver* suggested money, and he wanted them to hold both ideas at once without examining either.

The town was smaller than he remembered. That was the trouble with childhoods spent summers elsewhere: places shrank in the memory until returning to them felt like stepping into a dollhouse. Main Street ran four blocks and ended at a green strung already with festival lights, their bulbs dark by day, waiting like an audience. At the far end of the third block, wedged between a bakery that smelled of cardamom and a shuttered tailor's, stood the shop.

*Galloway & Daughter, Books.*

He sat in the car for eleven minutes—he counted, because counting calmed him the way prayer calmed other men—and watched through the frost-rimed glass as a man moved behind the counter with the unhurried economy of someone who had made a religion of routine. Wendell Galloway. Ten years older, ten years grayer at the temples, ten years careful. Julian could see it from here: the way the man shelved books as though each one might detonate, the way his eyes flicked to the door every time the bell chimed and then away again, disappointed or relieved, impossible to say.

*You took everything from my sister,* Julian thought, testing the sentence against his heart and finding nothing there, no weight, no wound. It was Marguerite's sentence, not his. His own was simpler and more useful: *You are standing on property that should have been mine.*

He got out of the car. He smiled at himself in the rearview mirror first—the smile that opened at the corners of the eyes, the one that made strangers feel they'd been let in on something—and went inside.

* * *

The bell over the door announced him, and Julian took his time crossing the threshold, letting the warmth of the shop fold around him. It smelled of paper and cedar and, faintly, of woodsmoke from a small iron stove in the corner. Shelves rose to the ceiling on every wall, ladders on brass rails, and in the window a hand-lettered sign read: WINTER FESTIVAL STORIES — HALF PRICE FOR CHILDREN WHO CAN PROVE THEY'VE BEEN GOOD (PROOF NOT REQUIRED).

"Welcome," said Wendell Galloway, and then he saw who had entered, and Julian watched the color leave the man's face with the satisfaction of a man watching a lock turn.

"Mr. Galloway." Julian extended his hand across the counter, warm and firm and entirely unbothered. "Julian Voss. I don't imagine you remember me—I was fourteen the last time we met, all elbows and bad posture. You were—" he glanced around, gesturing at the shelves with something like reverence, "—beginning all this, I suppose."

"I remember you," Wendell said. He did not take the hand. After a moment Julian withdrew it, converting the motion into a shrug so fluid it looked as though shaking hands had never been the point.

"And yet you didn't come to the door when I knocked at the estate yesterday. I asked after you." He let that sit. Let the man hear what it implied: *I know you've been avoiding us. I know you knew we were coming.* "Marguerite sends her regards," he added, and watched the name land like a stone dropped down a well—a long time falling, no sound at the bottom.

"She's here, then. In town."

"Surveying. There's a great deal of paper to sort through before Friday." Julian drifted along the nearest shelf, trailing one finger along the spines—not reading titles, marking territory. "Grandmother's will is a labyrinth. She did love her conditions, our grandmother. Riddles within riddles. The lawyers are calling parts of it *unusual*. Unprecedented, even." This was true, more or less. What he did not say—that he'd spent three weeks in Boston with a copy of the document and a highlighter, excavating its clauses like a tomb robber—was also true, and better left unsaid.

"I'm sorry to hear it," Wendell said flatly. "I mean that. Nobody enjoys a labyrinth."

"No," Julian agreed pleasantly. "But some of us are rather good at mazes."

A sound from the back of the shop—a small cough, quickly stifled—and Julian turned to find a girl of perhaps nine crouched behind the biography section, holding a book upside down and watching him with frank, unguarded curiosity. She had Wendell's eyes and none of his caution.

"That's Poppy," Wendell said, and something in his voice changed—warmed, defended, human. "My daughter."

"Poppy," Julian repeated, crouching down to her level with the ease of a man who had charmed children at a hundred dull family functions. "What are you reading?"

"It's upside down," she said.

"So it is. That means you're either very clever or very sneaky, and I admire both." She laughed—quick, surprised, delighted—and Julian felt the small cold click of a plan seating itself into place, smooth as a key in oiled tumblers. Children trusted easily. Children talked. And a father who saw his daughter laughing with a stranger would lower his guard in ways no lawyer ever could.

"Julian," he said, offering his hand with mock formality. "I'm going to be in town all week, and I don't know a soul. Would you be my official guide? I'm told there's a festival coming, and I intend to win the gingerbread contest through sheer bribery."

"You can't bribe judges," Poppy said sternly.

"Then I'll need a consultant. Someone local. Someone ruthless." He looked up at Wendell over the girl's head, holding his gaze just long enough. "With your permission, Mr. Galloway?"

Whatever war was being waged behind those tired eyes, it lost. "She has school until three."

"Then I'll return at three," Julian said cheerfully, "every day, if she'll have me," and bought three books he had no intention of reading, paying with cash counted out slowly, deliberately, so they would both remember he was generous.

* * *

He learned the town the way he learned everything: by asking questions that sounded like small talk and writing nothing down. The baker told him about the disputed parcel while selling him cardamom buns—how the Voss estate's eastern field and the lane itself had been tangled in the old woman's will, how half the town believed the shop stood on land that legally belonged to the estate, how the other half believed the old woman had promised Galloway the ground under his feet years ago. The barber told him about the scandal, eventually, after two visits and a tip about his nephew's manuscript: how the engagement had ended not quietly but publicly, how Wendell Galloway had been the one blamed, how he'd never once defended himself, how Marguerite Voss had married no one and gone away and come back thin and armored and alone.

*She let him carry it,* the barber said, snipping, unwatched. *Whole town knows it. Whole town says nothing.*

Julian filed this away where he kept everything—in the ledger behind his smile. Because here was the arithmetic he'd been doing since Boston: Grandmother's will, that labyrinth, had a clause. He'd found it on the second night, highlighted in yellow, read aloud to his own reflection until he could recite it in his sleep. *The stewardship of the contested parcel shall pass to the claimant who demonstrates, before the town, the greatest good faith toward its keeping.* Good faith. Toward its keeping. Which meant the will did not favor blood; it favored narrative. It favored whoever could stand before Rosewater Falls on festival night and tell the story the town most wanted to believe.

And the story the town most wanted to believe, Julian understood now, walking the lane at dusk with Poppy chattering at his elbow about lanterns and gingerbread and whether snow counted as proof of goodness, was not his sister's. Marguerite's story was marble—cold, expensive, closed. Wendell's story was a wound, and wounds made people uncomfortable, and uncomfortable people looked away.

But a story could be sharpened. A wound could be reopened, timed, aimed.

He needed only one thing: all three of them in one room, before Friday, with the temperature high enough that someone said something true.

* * *

On Thursday evening the sky came down gray and low, and the radio in the bakery murmured about a storm moving in off the mountains, early this year, heavier than expected. Julian stood at the shop counter at closing time, buying a clothbound atlas he did not want, and let his voice drop into the register he saved for confidences.

"They're saying the roads may close by nightfall," he said. "I confess I don't know the town well enough to know if that's serious."

"It's serious," Wendell said. His eyes went to the window, where the first flakes were coming down fat and slow, theatrical, like snow in a play about snow.

"Then here's a thought." Julian set the atlas down and leaned in, warm, reasonable, a man thinking aloud. "Marguerite and I are at the Hartfield Inn, and the innkeeper says she's shutting the doors at six. But there are matters we must settle before tomorrow's reading—all three of us, honestly, it can't wait, the lawyers are beside themselves. If the roads close..." He spread his hands, helpless, boyish. "Well. We'd be neighbors, wouldn't we? Trapped together. We might as well make a night of it. Wine, candles, an old grievance aired properly instead of gnawed at in silence for another decade." He smiled. "I'll bring the wine. I bring excellent wine. It's my single virtue."

Wendell said nothing for a long moment. Outside, the snow thickened, erasing the lane streetlight by streetlight, and Julian watched the man weigh it—saw him understand, dimly, that refusal was its own kind of trap, that sending them back out into the storm would look like cruelty, that the shop's whole identity was refuge.

"Poppy's here," Wendell said finally. "She's having supper in the back."

"Then she can help us settle a ten-year-old feud," Julian said lightly. "Children are wonderful referees. They have no patience whatsoever for adults lying." He was already pulling out his phone, thumbing a message to Marguerite—*Come to the shop. Now. Before the roads go. Trust me*—and pressing send before Wendell could formulate an objection, because objection required time and the storm was taking all of it.

Marguerite arrived twenty minutes later, headlights sweeping the darkening glass, cheeks pink with cold and fury, and Julian greeted her at the door with the wine held aloft like a trophy and his smile turned all the way up.

"Sister," he said. "Look what the weather brought us."

Behind him, at the counter, Wendell Galloway stood very still among his books, and outside the snow came down and down, sealing the lane, sealing the exits, sealing the three of them in with every word they had never said—and Julian, pouring the first glass, thought: *Now we find out who breaks.*
