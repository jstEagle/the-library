## 14. The Festival Committee

The town hall smelled like floor wax and old coffee, the way municipal buildings always did — as if the two smells had been arguing for decades and neither had won. Sable stood in the corridor outside the meeting room with her folder of printed menus pressed against her chest like a shield, listening to the muffled sound of chairs scraping and a man's voice saying something about *liability insurance* in the tone people used when they'd already decided.

She had dressed carefully. Not festival-bright, not desperate-dark. A linen dress the color of sea glass, hair pinned back so it couldn't be read as either careless or fussy. Armor made of neutrality. She'd learned that trick in the city, in conference rooms where men twice her age decided whether her work was worth anything. You didn't come in looking hungry. Hungry got eaten.

Through the doorway's narrow window she could see the committee arranged at their folding tables: five members under a banner from last year's festival that someone hadn't bothered to take down, its painted sandpipers faded to ghosts. Mrs. Okonkwo from the library, who nodded at everyone equally. Tom Brannigan, who ran the hardware store and fell asleep during budget items. Two summer residents whose names Sable had never managed to learn. And at the end, closest to the window, Marcus Pell.

He wore a pale blue shirt today, sleeves rolled to the elbow, forearms tanned from his boat. He looked like an advertisement for the town itself — the kind of photograph they'd put on a chamber-of-commerce brochure, *Sandpiper Point: Where Life Slows Down.* He caught her eye through the glass and smiled, warm and easy, and Sable felt the smile land somewhere cold in her stomach because she had spent enough years around charming men to know that the warmth was real. That was the terrible part. It wasn't performance. Marcus Pell genuinely liked being liked, and he genuinely liked winning, and those two appetites had never once conflicted for him.

"Ms. Halloran?" Mrs. Okonkwo leaned out the door. "We're ready for you."

* * *

They gave her four minutes. She knew because Tom Brannigan had a kitchen timer he set with great ceremony, a relic from some long-ago attempt at procedural fairness, and it sat on the table between them ticking like a small judgmental heart.

"Thank you for seeing me again." She laid out her menus along the table, one per member, each one printed on card stock she'd paid for herself. "I know the committee declined my application last month, and I'm not here to relitigate that. I'm here to ask you to look at what you'd actually be turning down."

She opened the folder. Photographs, this time. She'd spent two nights styling and shooting the proposed festival menu on the café's back counter with a sheet of white foam board and the morning light off the water: the salted honey madeleines glazed until they shone like wet stones; the black-sesame shortbread stamped with the shape of a sandpiper; little tartlets of roasted plum and bay leaf that looked, if she was honest, like something out of a magazine she couldn't afford to buy.

"The Garden Café has been part of this town for eleven years," she said. "Ruthie Vane built it into the place where every festival started — half the town still tells me they measure summers by her lemon bars. When Ruthie sold to me, she told me the café wasn't mine, exactly. It was the town's, and I was just the one holding the keys." She let that sit. "This menu is me honoring that. Festival weekend is the biggest foot traffic Sandpiper Point sees all year. Last year the vendors on the square did forty percent of their annual business in three days. I'm asking for a table. One table. Six feet of it."

Tom Brannigan picked up a photograph of the plum tartlets and held it at arm's length, which was how he seemed to read everything. "These look expensive."

"They're priced for the festival. Three dollars each."

"Huh." He put it down, which Sable had learned, watching Dee negotiate with suppliers, was how men said *impressed* when they didn't want anyone to hear them say it.

Mrs. Okonkwo had turned her menu over, checking the back as though the argument might be written there. "Sable, dear, it wasn't a quality question last time. The concern was space allocation. The square only fits so many vendors, and we have returning ones with seniority—"

"I understand." Sable kept her voice level. "But I checked the final layout map posted at the harbor office. There are two unassigned slots by the north archway. Both are marked reserved, but no vendor name is attached to either. I called the harbor office Monday and they said reservation status was at the committee's discretion." She paused. "So the space exists. The question is just who gets to stand in it."

A small silence. Somewhere behind the wall, the town clock ticked through its quarter hour.

Marcus had been quiet through all of it, turning her menu over in his long fingers, reading it with the unhurried attention of a man appraising property. Now he looked up, and his expression was open, reasonable, almost pained.

"Sable. Nobody here doubts your pastries. Honestly, I've had the croissants — they're excellent." He spread his hands. "But you have to see how this looks. Your café has some... financial questions outstanding. The committee has a responsibility to the town. If a vendor folds mid-festival, it reflects on all of us. It's not personal. It's risk management."

There it was. Delivered softly, wrapped in civic duty, but there — the word *folds* placed with precision, like a scalpel laid on sterile cloth. He wanted the committee to hear it. He wanted *her* to hear it. And the worst part, the part that made heat crawl up her throat, was that it was even true. She was one missed payment from the edge. He knew it because he'd delivered the notice himself, standing in her doorway with that same gentle smile, explaining terms like a man describing weather.

"The café's finances are current as of this month," she said. Each word placed separately, like stones across a stream.

"For now," Marcus said, and smiled apologetically, as if he hadn't meant to say it aloud.

Mrs. Okonkwo cleared her throat. "Perhaps we could vote on the north archway slot. Provisionally. Pending—" she glanced at Sable with genuine, useless kindness, "—pending everything remaining stable."

They voted. Tom Brannigan raised his hand first, then one of the summer residents, then, after a visible internal struggle, Mrs. Okonkwo. Three to two. The kitchen timer went off while hands were still in the air, shrilling absurdly, and Tom smacked it silent and said, "Motion carries. Congratulations, Ms. Halloran. Bring your own table."

* * *

The corridor after was cool and dim, and Sable walked it slowly because her legs felt strange, too light, adrenaline draining out through her shoes. She'd won. Narrowly, provisionally, six feet of it — but she'd stood in front of five people with her debt hanging over her like weather and she had not flinched, and they had given her the slot anyway.

She was halfway to the doors when Marcus caught up.

"Hey — hold on." His footsteps were easy on the waxed floor. "Got a second?"

She turned. He stopped a polite distance away, hands in his pockets, and for a moment he just looked at her with something that was almost respect.

"That was well done in there," he said. "Genuinely. The photographs, the layout research — you came prepared. Most people who appeal just come angry."

"Most people who appeal have already been told no once."

"Fair." He laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm, and Sable hated how good it was. "Look, I want to say something, and I'd rather say it straight than have you hear it sideways. Man to — well. Neighbor to neighbor."

"Say it."

"The vote went your way. Great. But I chair the finance subcommittee, which means the paperwork on your building crosses my desk, and I'd rather you weren't blindsided by anything." He tilted his head, and his voice dropped into something gentler, almost kind. "Your next payment comes due the week after the festival. The eleventh. And Sable — the forbearance agreement your previous lender signed, the one that's been letting you make partial payments? It expires at the end of this month. After that, the full balance accelerates. All of it, at once."

The corridor was very quiet. Sable could hear the town clock, could hear gulls somewhere outside, could hear her own pulse in her ears.

"You're telling me this as a courtesy."

"I'm telling you because I don't think you realize how close to the line you're walking." He shrugged, and the gesture was so casual, so boyish, that it took her a moment to understand it as cruelty — cruelty wearing the face of helpfulness. "Festival weekend, you'll do well. Maybe even great. But one good weekend doesn't restructure a debt, Sable. You know that better than anyone. So maybe start thinking about options. The ground lease, for instance. There are buyers who'd take that off your hands and let you keep operating. Everybody wins. No drama, no notice taped to your door."

"You mean you'd take it."

Something flickered across his face — annoyance, quickly smoothed. "I mean there are options. I'm offering to be one of them instead of the alternative." He stepped back, and his smile returned, sunny and unbothered. "Congratulations on the slot. Really. Make it a good weekend. You've earned it."

He pushed through the doors into the bright afternoon, and Sable stood alone in the dim corridor with her folder of menus against her chest, feeling the shape of the thing he'd done. He hadn't threatened her. He'd *scheduled* her. The eleventh. The week after the festival, when whatever she earned would already be spoken for, when the acceleration would hit like a wave she couldn't see over. He'd waited until she'd won something — until hope was back in her hands — to tell her the floor was about to give way.

* * *

Outside, the light was gold and slanting, late-afternoon sun laying long shadows across the square. The festival banners were going up along the lampposts; a man on a ladder worked a knot loose, and below him a woman held the ladder and argued cheerfully with a child about how many churros constituted too many. Ordinary life, assembling itself for summer.

Sable sat down on the low stone wall by the steps and opened her folder and looked at her own photographs. The plum tartlets. The madeleines. Six feet of table by the north archway, hers, provisionally, pending everything remaining stable.

*Pending everything remaining stable.*

Her phone buzzed. Dee: **Well??**

**Got the slot,** she typed. **3–2.**

Three dots appeared, vanished, appeared. Then: **And? What's his angle. There's always an angle.**

Sable looked up. Across the square, Marcus had joined a group near the harbor office, and even at this distance she could see him laughing at something, hand resting easily on a railing, a man entirely at home in the world he was quietly buying. The eleventh. The week after the festival. He'd handed her the date like a gift, like a courtesy, and the generosity of it was the most frightening part — because it meant he was certain. Certain enough to be kind about it.

**Nothing yet,** she typed, and pocketed the phone before Dee could call her a liar.

She gathered her folder and stood. Above the square the first banner caught the wind and filled, sandpipers wheeling across faded cloth, and Sable thought about the north archway, six feet of table, three days in which everything she had left would be on display — and about the calendar page waiting behind it, the eleventh circled in someone else's ink.

One good weekend. He'd said it like a fact.

She would have to make it a lie.
