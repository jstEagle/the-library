## 2. The Girl Who Said Yes to Strangers

The walk-in freezer hummed like a contented cat, and Marisol Vega—who was not, at the moment, Marisol Vega—counted her cash inside it because it was the only room in the whole venue with a lock that worked and no cameras.

Two hundred and eleven dollars. She thumbed through the bills again, slower this time, as if arithmetic might improve under scrutiny. It didn't. Two hundred and eleven, plus the forty-three cents of tips that had come in coins, which she kept in a mint tin because a woman carrying loose change looked like a woman who wasn't going anywhere.

She was going somewhere. That was the problem.

Three weeks, maybe four, if she stretched it. Then she'd need to be someone else in some other town with some other name stitched into her uniform. She'd done it twice before. The first time had been terrifying. The second time had been mostly logistics. She was hoping the third time would be boring, though hope, in her experience, was just fear wearing a nicer dress.

She heard the freezer door before she saw anyone—the particular careful click of a man who treated other people's hinges like they were made of spun sugar—and she had the tin closed and the cash vanished into her apron pocket before the light spilled in.

August Nystrom stood in the doorway holding a clipboard against his chest like a shield, his sleeves rolled to identical heights, his expression caught somewhere between apology and audit.

"You're in my freezer," he said.

"Your freezer," Marisol agreed. "It's very nice. Cold, though."

"It's thirty-eight degrees. It's supposed to be." He frowned at her, then at the shelves of plated desserts behind her, then back at her, as if trying to locate the irregularity on an otherwise flawless spreadsheet. "You're the new girl. Tuesday's hire."

"Rosa," she said, because Rosa was this month's name, and she gave him the smile she'd practiced in bathroom mirrors until it stopped looking like a threat: warm, slightly tired, the smile of a woman who worked hard and wanted nothing from anybody.

The smile never worked on people who were themselves hiding something. August Nystrom looked at it the way a locksmith looks at a key that doesn't fit—politely, professionally, already reaching for another one.

"Rosa," he repeated. "Right. I need—" He consulted the clipboard, then seemed to lose his place in it. "Actually, what I need is not on here."

* * *

Here is what Marisol knew about August Nystrom by the end of her first week at the Whitmore Estate, where she waited tables at weddings for people whose flower budgets exceeded her annual rent:

He alphabetized the place cards. Not sorted—alphabetized, and then checked them twice, and once she'd watched him turn a card ninety degrees because its calligraphy leaned the wrong way.

He apologized to chairs when he moved them.

And he never, not once, looked at the brides without an expression she could only describe as grief doing paperwork.

The other waitresses had theories. Jilted, said one. Gay and in denial, said another, less charitably. Marisol's theory was simpler and sadder: he was a man who loved weddings the way a doctor loves medicine, which is to say professionally, thoroughly, and at a great personal distance. You don't stand that close to a thing you believe will break you.

So when he found her three nights later folding napkins alone in the empty ballroom—she'd volunteered for close because the ballroom had one exit and good sightlines, habits dying harder than she did—and he stood there shifting his weight like a man about to ask for directions, she set down the napkin and waited.

"I have a proposal," he said.

"Wrong department," she said. "You want catering. We do canapés, not contracts."

"No—I mean—" He exhaled through his nose, precise even in frustration. "I have a business proposition. A strange one. Possibly an insulting one. I've rehearsed it four times and each version sounds worse than the last, so I'm simply going to say it."

"Say it," she said, and picked up another napkin, because watching him squirm was more fun if her hands were busy.

"My family has a reunion every summer. Ten years now. At Crescent Cove—it's a house on the water, my mother's pride and joy, the whole production." His jaw tightened around the word *production*, and Marisol, connoisseur of tight jaws, took note. "This year my sister Claire is hosting, and her husband will be there, and various aunts who consider my marital status a group project they've never been able to complete."

"And you want me to tell them I'm your girlfriend," Marisol said.

His head came up sharply. "How did you—"

"August." She gestured at him with the napkin. "Look at yourself. You rehearsed four versions of asking a waitress you barely know to lie to your family. Nobody rehearses that for anything else."

For a moment he looked genuinely stumped, and she had the odd, unwelcome feeling of having surprised him into being real. Then he rallied, because men like him always rallied; order was their native language.

"There's compensation, obviously. Generous compensation. Room and board for the weekend, plus—" he named a figure, and Marisol's hands went still on the napkin.

That was two months of disappearing. That was a bus ticket and a first-last deposit and a name that didn't have to be Rosa or Marisol or any of the girls she'd been. That was breathing room measured in actual breaths.

"How long?" she asked, keeping her voice bored, because interested women got negotiated down.

"Friday evening to Sunday afternoon. Two nights. There are dinners, a boat thing my brother-in-law insists on, a final toast Sunday." He said *brother-in-law* the way you'd say *weather front*. "You'd need to be convincing. My Aunt Bettina once interviewed a cousin's fiancée for forty minutes and reduced her to tears. The engagement survived. Barely."

"And what's the story? How we met?"

"That's—" He paused, and she watched him decide whether to lie to her. He decided against it, which was either honesty or exhaustion. "I hadn't gotten that far. I got as far as the compensation and the dates and then I ran out of nerve and started alphabetizing something."

Despite herself, despite everything, Marisol laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate that hadn't swung in years.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Here's how it works. We met at a wedding here. I dropped a tray near you—or you dropped one, we'll decide—and you were funny about it, which surprised me, because you look like a man who alphabetizes his feelings."

"I don't alphabetize my feelings," he said, wounded.

"You file them cross-referenced. Anyway. We talked. You made me laugh. I said yes to dinner. Six months ago. Long enough to be serious, short enough that nobody asks why there's no ring."

"Six months," he repeated, writing nothing down, though his eyes had the look of a man filing it away forever. "Why six months?"

"Because at three months your aunt asks if it's serious and you get to say 'we'll see,' and at six months she starts planning the wedding herself and you get to look overwhelmed, which you're good at. You look overwhelmed right now."

"I am overwhelmed," he admitted. "I expected more resistance."

"From a waitress?"

"From anyone." He looked at her then—really looked, the way he'd looked at the leaning place card, hunting the flaw—and whatever he saw made him go quiet. "Most people say yes to me slowly. Or never."

Marisol folded the last napkin into a crisp triangle and thought about doors. About how for three years every door had been an exit, every stranger a possible set of eyes, every kindness a bill that would come due. And here was a strange fastidious man offering her a weekend inside a family, in plain sight, on a cliff by the sea, where nobody looking for a waitress named Rosa would ever think to search the girlfriend of the wedding planner.

People hide in crowds. People hide better in families.

"When do we leave?" she said.

"Friday. There's a car." He hesitated at the ballroom door, hand on the frame, and turned back with the expression of a man adding a footnote he hoped wouldn't be read aloud. "One condition. No real names. Yours or mine. If this goes wrong, I don't want—" He stopped, recalibrated. "Clean edges. That's all. Clean edges on both sides."

*No real names.* Marisol smiled her real smile then, the one she never spent on customers, and watched him blink at it like a man who'd opened the wrong door and found sunlight.

"August," she said, tasting the name. "It suits you. Very organized. Very... hemmed."

"It's a normal name."

"It's a name with creases in it." She untied her apron. "Fine. No real names. You get fake-girlfriend Rosa, and I get a weekend where nobody asks me a single true question."

"That's the idea," he said. "Nobody asks anything true."

Later—much later, folding napkins that were already folded, because her hands needed work when her mind got loud—she would think about how easily she'd said yes. How a stranger's lie had felt safer than her own truth. How the figure he'd named was exactly enough to run on, and how some small stubborn part of her, the part that had testified once before and paid for it, had whispered: *or it's enough to stop running.*

But that was later. For now there was only the ballroom, the folded napkins, and Friday coming toward her like a tide.

She just had to survive Thursday.
