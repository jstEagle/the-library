## 7. Festival Preparations

The committee arrived on a Tuesday, which I should have taken as an omen, because nothing good in this village has ever happened on a Tuesday.

I was elbow-deep in the herb bed when three of them came through the garden gate without knocking — Mrs. Aldous from the bakery, Mr. Prewitt who ran the hardware shop and had opinions about everything sold in it, and young Tamsin Cole, who organized things the way weather organizes itself: inevitably, and with no regard for what stood in the way.

"Ivo," Tamsin said, with the bright, terrible smile of a woman about to ask for something enormous, "we need you to play at the festival."

I kept my hands in the soil. It gave me something to look at that wasn't her face.

"The festival has a program," I said. "A lovely program. Full of people who still play."

"The program has a hole in it." She crouched down to my level, which felt like a tactical decision. "Forty minutes of it. The string quartet from Ashcombe cancelled — the violist's having a baby, which we all congratulated her for, very inconveniently timed."

"Congratulations to her."

"Ivo."

"Mrs. Aldous," I said, appealing over Tamsin's head to higher authority, "tell her."

Mrs. Aldous folded her arms. She had known me since I was nine years old and had once paid me a pound to stop playing scales outside her shop window. "I'll tell you what I told your grandmother, love. A gift sitting in a shed is just furniture."

"It's not a gift anymore. It's a cello."

"A cello is a gift," she said, with the immovable certainty of a woman who had been right about parish politics for forty years, "and you're being wasteful."

Mr. Prewitt, mercifully, said nothing. He only looked at me the way men look at other men when they understand something they'd rather not discuss: a brief, sympathetic grimace, like passing a car accident you've survived yourself.

I pulled my hands out of the soil and stood, brushing off my knees as if the matter were settled by dirt alone.

"I'll think about it," I said.

Which is what cowards say, and what liars say, and what I said, and all three of us knew it.

* * *

They came back Thursday. And Friday. By Sunday, Tamsin had escalated to leaving notes pinned to my gate, each one more shameless than the last. *The hole in the program grows.* *Mrs. Aldous says you owe her a pound anyway.* *We will accept any instrument. Recorder. Kazoo. We are not proud.*

I read them all standing in the rose rows, where Marlowe's flowers were going over — the last blooms browning at their edges, hips forming fat and orange where petals used to be. October does that. Everything insists on becoming something else.

I did not answer the notes. But I didn't take them down either, which Tamsin correctly interpreted as encouragement, because by Monday there were four more.

* * *

My mother arrived on Wednesday, which meant someone had told her about the festival, which meant someone had told her about the hole in the program, which meant the entire village had conspired against me with the efficiency of people who have nothing better to do between harvest and Advent.

She came up the drive in her good coat, carrying a basket of apples she hadn't grown, and looked at the mill house the way she always did — as though assessing whether I was maintaining it to a standard that would reflect well on her.

"You look thin," she said, kissing the air near my cheek.

"Hello, Mother."

"They want you to play." Not a question. She set the apples on the kitchen table and began unpacking them, rearranging my counter to suit herself, a habit of hers since 1994. "Tamsin Cole rang me. Very sensible girl."

"She's relentless."

"She's *right*." My mother turned, and there it was — the look. The one I'd spent seven years learning to survive. Hope and judgment braided so tightly together I couldn't tell which was holding which up. "You were the best cellist in the county before you were twenty. You don't get to be the best and then simply—"

"Simply?" I said. "Is that what we're calling it?"

Her mouth tightened. "I'm not doing this with you again, Ivo."

"No," I agreed. "You never do. You do the other thing. The thing where you stand in my kitchen and talk about what I *was*."

The apples sat between us, red and blameless. Somewhere outside, a crow was making an argument about the bird feeder.

"I brought you apples," she said finally, quietly, which was as close to an apology as she had come in seven years, and which was not one.

"I can see that."

"I'll help with the festival," she announced, chin up, retreating into usefulness the way I retreated into gardens. "The committee needs volunteers. Someone has to run the cake stall properly; last year Mrs. Fenwick let the W.I. use margarine."

And that was how my mother came to 'help' — meaning: to occupy the same festival grounds as me, armed with sponge cakes and every expectation she'd ever had, aimed directly at the stage where I was not scheduled to appear.

* * *

Marlowe took the decorating job on a Friday, and I only found out because Tamsin told me, gleefully, in the post office queue.

"Marlowe Finch! Doing the flowers and garlands for the whole hall. Isn't that wonderful? They said yes before I'd finished asking."

"Did they," I said.

Something in my face must have shown, because Tamsin's glee dimmed into curiosity, and I paid for my stamps and left before she could aim it at anything vital.

Marlowe and I had not spoken properly in eleven days. Eleven days since the reunion, since dessert, since Marlowe stood up in front of my entire family and said *he didn't fail, he survived* — words that went through me like light through a cracked window, illuminating everything and making the whole structure feel suddenly unsound. Since I bolted. Since Marlowe found me in the rose rows two days later, furious and hurt and honest, and I answered honesty with the whole ugly story of the concert, the frozen hands, the thousand faces, and then could not say the one true sentence that mattered, which was: *and I have wanted you since long before you owed me anything.*

So we existed now in the particular silence of two people who have said too much and not enough, both at once.

And Marlowe — hurt, proud, magnificent Marlowe — had said yes to decorating the festival. The festival where my mother would be running the cake stall. Where the committee would be begging me to play. Where forty minutes of empty program waited like an open grave.

Marlowe hadn't done it for me. That was the part I had to sit with. Marlowe had done it because the village needed flowers and Marlowe was good with flowers, and because being near me apparently cost less than being far from me, and I couldn't decide which reading wounded worse.

* * *

I saw them Thursday, up a ladder in the village hall, wiring eucalyptus to the beams.

I had come to return a cake tin my mother had left at the mill house — an errand I had accepted specifically because I thought the hall would be empty, which tells you everything about my current state of cowardice.

Marlowe saw me first. Of course they did. They always did.

"Well," they said, from the top of the ladder, not climbing down. "The ghost walks."

"I brought a tin."

"Thrilling. Leave it by the piano."

There was a piano against the wall, draped in bunting already, half-decorated like everything else in the room. I set the tin down. I should have left. Every sensible instinct I possessed voted for leaving.

Instead I heard myself say, "The garlands are crooked."

Marlowe looked down at me with an expression I knew too well — the florist's expression, the one that said *you have opinions about my work?*

"They're asymmetrical," they said. "Deliberately. Symmetry is for funerals."

"This isn't a funeral."

"No," Marlowe said, and climbed down slowly, wiping their hands on their apron, and looked at me across the half-dressed hall with the lights strung overhead like held breath. "It isn't. That's rather the problem, isn't it, Ivo."

"Marlowe—"

"Don't." They held up a hand, gentle but final. "Not here. Not with Tamsin due back in ten minutes with a van full of pumpkins. I'm working. You're visiting. We are both being extremely civil, and I would like credit for how extremely civil I am being."

"You have it. All of it."

"Do I?" Something flickered under the wryness then — the hurt, the real thing, surfacing like a fish turning in dark water. "Because here's what I have, Ivo. I have a job decorating a festival I only said yes to because saying no would have meant admitting why it hurt. I have your mother asking me, twice this week, whether I play an instrument, as though interviewing me for a position. And I have forty minutes of empty program that everyone in this village seems to think belongs to you, and I don't know what I'm supposed to do with any of it."

The hall smelled of eucalyptus and dust and apples. Somewhere a door banged; neither of us flinched toward it.

"What do you want me to say?" I asked, and hated how small it sounded.

"Nothing." Marlowe picked up their wire cutters. Their voice softened by exactly one degree, which from Marlowe was a flood. "That's the trouble. I don't want you to say anything. I want you to *do* something. And I've learned you won't. Not yet. Maybe not ever. So." They turned back to the ladder. "Leave the tin. Mind the garlands on your way out."

I left the tin. On my way out, I straightened one garland — just slightly, just enough — and heard behind me, dry as autumn leaves:

"That doesn't count as doing something, Blackwood."

But there was something underneath it. Almost warmth. Almost hope.

Almost.

* * *

That night I dreamed of the concert again — the first time in months. The hall, the heat, the opening bars going perfectly, perfectly, perfectly, and then my left hand simply declining to continue, fingers curling like leaves in frost, the note dying mid-phrase while a thousand people leaned forward to listen to the silence where the music should have been.

I woke at four with my heart hammering and lay in the dark listening to the mill house settle around me.

On the kitchen table, Tamsin's latest note sat where I'd carried it in from the gate, unable to throw it away. *The hole in the program grows,* it said. Beneath it, in different ink, someone had added: *So does the audience's hope. — M.F.*

Marlowe had been to my gate.

I sat in the dark kitchen for a long time, the note in my hands, the shed at the bottom of the garden holding its breath around a cello case I had not opened in seven years.

Three days until the festival. Forty minutes of silence. My mother arriving with sponge cakes and history. Marlowe up a ladder, waiting for me to do something instead of say something.

I got up, put the kettle on, and did not go to the shed.

But I stood at the window looking at it for a long, long time.
