## 9. Eight Minutes of Imperfect Music

*Ivo*

The stage was a hay wagon with a rug on it.

I want that noted for posterity: seven years of dread, one hundred and eleven sleepless negotiations with my own hands, a mother flown in from the continent of her disappointment — all of it aimed at a hay wagon with a rug on it, parked in the churchyard field between the cider stall and the tombola. The village autumn festival did not do things by halves; it did them by quarters, badly, with bunting.

Marlowe had done the flowers, of course. Marigolds and late dahlias in jam jars along the wagon's edge, sunflowers leaning against the wheel like drunk ushers. I had watched him arrange them at six that morning, sleeves rolled, arguing cheerfully with the festival committee's chairwoman about whether chrysanthemums were "funeral flowers or festival flowers," and I had thought: *he is not even looking at me, and he has made this whole field beautiful anyway.* That was rather the shape of him.

My cello sat in its case at the back of the wagon like a guest who wasn't sure it was invited.

The set was eight minutes. The committee had asked for forty-five and I had laughed until they realized I wasn't joking. Eight minutes: a Bach sarabande, a folk air Mrs. Aldous from the bakery had hummed to me over a loaf, and — because Marlowe had once traded me roses for lessons and neither of us had ever said so out loud — the little piece I'd written at nineteen and never played for anyone, which I had privately named *Peonies* and publicly named nothing at all.

I tuned. My hands shook. Not the old shaking — not the freeze, not that white-noise paralysis — just ordinary human fright, which was almost a luxury. Ordinary fright you can walk through. The other kind walks through you.

I looked up once, before I began, and found Marlowe in the front row.

He was sitting on a folding chair with his knees crossed and his hands folded in his lap like a man at a recital he'd paid good money for, and when our eyes met he didn't smile the way he smiled at customers, or the way he'd smiled across the reunion table while defending my life to my own family. He just looked at me, steady, as if to say: *I'm here. That's all. Get on with it.*

So I got on with it.

* * *

*Marlowe*

Here is what nobody tells you about watching someone you love do the bravest thing they've ever done: you can't help them. You are furniture. You are a folding chair. Your entire job is to exist in their eyeline and radiate the message *the ground will hold.*

Ivo drew the bow across the string and the first note came out — round, low, slightly rough at the edge, like a voice on waking — and the whole field went quiet in that particular English way, which is to say two hundred people pretended very hard to be interested in their cider.

He played the Bach. It was not perfect. There was a passage in the middle where his left hand hesitated, a half-second stumble, and I saw his shoulders start to climb toward his ears — I knew that climb, I'd seen it in the shop doorway when the busker played, I'd catalogued every flinch like a botanist cataloguing blight — but then he did something I will remember until I die, which is nothing. He did nothing. He let the hesitation be part of the music, leaned into the next phrase, and carried on, and the sarabande opened up like something breathing.

Beside me, Mrs. Aldous whispered, "That's my tune coming after," with the proprietary pride of a woman who had hummed eight bars at a florist's counter once and considered the matter settled.

Then it happened.

Halfway through the folk air — her air — Ivo stopped. Not froze. Stopped. The bow lifted clean off the string, deliberate, and the silence stood up in the middle of the field like a guest. I felt the whole crowd lean forward. Somebody's dog whined. Ivo closed his eyes, and I watched him take one breath — I counted it, one long breath, the kind you take before telling the truth — and then he looked out at all of us, and he said, conversationally, into the microphone he hadn't needed until now:

"Sorry. Lost my place. Happens to gardens too."

And the field *laughed* — warm, unembarrassed, the laugh of people who had also lost their places in various things — and Mrs. Aldous called out "Third bar, love!" and Ivo grinned, actually grinned, and picked the air back up from the third bar as if losing your way and being shown it again were simply what music was for.

I had to look at the marigolds for a moment. Jam jars are very moving at certain junctures.

* * *

*Ivo*

Here is what nobody tells you about playing again: it doesn't feel like coming home. It feels like being a stranger in your own house and finding someone left the lights on.

The last piece was mine. Nineteen-year-old me had written it in a practice room in February, thinking about a boy at the flower market who'd handed me an armful of peonies and said, "Teach me the cello and these are free forever." I never taught him. He never charged me. We let the debt sit there between us for years like a seed nobody planted, and then I'd gone and hired him, which was either the stupidest or truest thing I've ever done, and I still hadn't decided.

I played *Peonies* and my hands remembered it better than I did. Somewhere in the middle there was a chord I'd always rushed, and I rushed it again, and this time I heard myself rushing it and thought, with a clarity that felt suspiciously like peace: *that's mine too. The flaw is mine. Keep it.*

Eight minutes. I finished, and lowered the bow, and the silence afterward lasted three full seconds — an eternity, a held breath — and then the field came apart. Not politely. Mrs. Aldous was on her feet. The dog barked. The tombola lady abandoned her tombola entirely. And Marlowe stood in the front row clapping with his whole arms, his whole ridiculous generous body, looking at me like I'd hung the marigolds myself.

I bowed to the field. Then I stepped down off the hay wagon and walked through two hundred people toward one of them, and my heart was doing something unsophisticated and enormous.

* * *

*Marlowe*

He smelled of rosin and cold air and, faintly, of the mill house garden, which I suppose had soaked into him over seven years the way rain soaks into everything.

"Eight minutes," I said, because someone had to be wry about it or we'd both have started crying in front of the tombola.

"Seven and a half. I rushed the chord."

"I noticed."

"You noticed?"

"Marlowe, I've been noticing." He stopped. Looked at his hands — those hands, those traitors, those survivors — and then looked back up at me, and whatever he'd rehearsed on the walk over, I could see him throwing it away. Good. Rehearsed answers had never been our problem. Rehearsed answers had been the entire architecture of the lie.

"Not a favor," he said. "This time. I'm not asking for a favor. I spent seven years building a life with no music in it because I thought a smaller life couldn't break. And then you turned up with peonies and a contract and ruined the whole design." His voice cracked on *design*, which I chose to find charming rather than devastating, though it was both. "I don't want a partner for reunions. I want — I want the real thing, whatever the real thing costs. I want to plant things with you and get them wrong. I want you to teach the busker's daughter on Saturdays and sit in the garden afterwards and complain about her fingering. I want to stop owing each other favors and start just — owing each other. Which is what people do. When they stay."

The field was emptying around us, lanterns coming on, the committee already dismantling the stage with terrifying efficiency. Somewhere behind him my sunflowers were being loaded into a van by teenagers.

"You fled after dessert," I said. Not cruelly. Just checking the ledger.

"I fled after dessert."

"And the apology neither of us drafted?"

"I drafted it about nine hundred times. It always sounded like a business deal." He took a breath — one long breath, the truth-telling kind; I was learning his repertoire. "So. No drafts. Marlowe Finch, will you have dinner with me tomorrow, at the mill house, cooked by me, badly, followed by absolutely nothing contractual whatsoever?"

I made him wait four seconds. For symmetry.

"Yes," I said. "But I'm bringing flowers."

"You always bring flowers."

"That's the point, Ivo. Some debts you keep paying on purpose."

He laughed — the real one, the one from the third bar — and put his hand out, and instead of shaking it, because we were past shaking things, I laced my fingers through his, right there between the cider stall and the tombola, in front of Mrs. Aldous and God and the dog.

Behind us, the committee chairwoman called out that they'd need the wagon back by Sunday, and Ivo said, without letting go of my hand, "Keep it. I'll be needing a stage again."

Which was, I understood later, the closest thing to a promise about next spring that either of us knew how to make yet.
