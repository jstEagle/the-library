## 10. Planting for Next Spring

The first frost came on a Tuesday, and Marlowe took it personally.

"It's not *dead*," I said, watching them glare at the rose beds from the kitchen window, a mug of tea going cold in their hands. "It's dormant. There's a difference."

"They look dead."

"That's the point of dormancy. It looks like death. It isn't. You wait, and then—" I gestured vaguely at the window, at the grey garden, at the whole stubborn machinery of the world. "Then it comes back anyway. Without asking anyone's permission."

Marlowe looked at me over the rim of the mug. They had that expression they got when they were deciding whether to say the true thing or the funny thing — and with Marlowe, the funny thing was usually just the true thing wearing a hat.

"You know," they said, "seven years ago you'd have made that same speech about yourself and meant it as an apology. Now it sounds like a threat."

"I've been practicing threats."

"With your sister?"

"Mostly with my mother. She sends me recordings now. Of herself playing. She wants notes." I shook my head. "My mother wants *notes* from me. On her Chopin."

"And do you give them?"

I thought about it. Since the festival — since eight minutes of imperfect music in front of half the village, since Marlowe's hand finding mine in the dark of the front row like it had always lived there — my mother and I had been conducting a slow, careful correspondence of phone calls and, lately, recordings. She played. I listened. Sometimes I told her where she rushed, because rushing was what she did when she was afraid of being caught feeling something. She never argued. Last week she'd ended a call by saying, "Your grandmother would have liked how you sound now," and neither of us had pretended she meant the cello only.

"Sometimes," I said. "It turns out I have opinions. Who knew."

* * *

Saturday mornings belonged to Wren.

She was eleven, the busker's daughter — the busker himself being Callum, who played outside the bakery and had once, without knowing it, nearly ended me with four bars of Bach on a street corner. He'd apologized so many times since that I'd finally told him to stop, or better, to pay me back by letting me teach his daughter properly instead of letting her learn vibrato off YouTube videos.

So: Saturdays, ten o'clock, the mill house music room. My grandmother's piano pushed against one wall, gathering dust with dignity. The cello — my cello, the one I hadn't opened for seven years and now opened every day like a door into a house I still lived in — sat on its stand by the window.

Wren arrived at 9:58 every week, breathless, hair escaping its plait, and announced some variation of "I broke nothing this time" before she'd gotten her coat off.

"This time," I said, tuning while she wrestled the smaller of the two cellos — a rental, half-size, with a scratch down its side shaped like a river. "What are we working on?"

"The middle bit. Where it goes sad but you don't want it to be sad, just — heavy."

"Heavy but not sad," I said. "That's most of music. That's most of everything."

I put my hand over hers on the bow. Her grip was too tight; mine used to be too tight; somewhere in the last seven years I had learned that the bow is not something you hold, it's something you carry, the way you carry water across a room, carefully, aware that spilling is possible and survivable.

"Loosen here," I said. "If you squeeze it, the sound gets scared."

"Sounds get scared?"

"All sounds get scared. That's why people clap. To reassure them."

She laughed, and played, and the phrase came out heavy and not sad, and I felt something in my chest turn over — not pain, not even ache. Just the quiet astonishment of a man who had assumed his hands were ruined discovering they could build something instead.

At noon Marlowe appeared with sandwiches, as they always did, and sat on the piano bench pretending to read while actually listening, which fooled no one, least of all Wren, who had taken to calling them "the manager."

"I'm not the manager," Marlowe said.

"You manage him," Wren said, pointing her bow at me. "You tell him when he's being dramatic."

"She's not wrong," Marlowe said.

"Traitor," I said, and handed out sandwiches, and the music room was warm and full and ordinary, which was the whole miracle of it, that it had become ordinary.

* * *

The bare-root roses arrived the first week of December, in a box that smelled like cold earth and possibility. Marlowe unpacked them onto the potting bench with the reverence other people reserved for infants or jewelry.

"'Desdemona,'" they read. "'Munstead Wood.' 'Lady Emma Hamilton.' And this one—" they held up a bundle of thorny sticks tied with twine, unimpressive as anything alive has ever looked, "—'Gentle Hermione.' For the bed by the shed."

"The shed bed gets morning light," I agreed. "Good drainage. Grandmother grew 'Albertine' there until it ate the trellis and half the gutter."

"Your grandmother sounds like she treated gardening as a contact sport."

"She treated everything as a contact sport. She once got into a public argument with a judge at the county flower show. Won, incidentally."

We worked through the afternoon, the two of us, kneeling in the cold beds with a spade and a bucket of mycorrhizal powder and our gloves stiffening in the damp. Bare-root planting is an act of faith conducted in mud: you dig a hole wide enough for the roots to spread, you mound the soil in the center, you drape the plant over it like a person settling into a chair after a long journey, and then you bury it up to the graft and walk away and trust.

"Soak first," Marlowe recited, dunking roots in the bucket. "Spread the roots. Don't bury the bud union. Firm the soil but don't compact it—"

"You've been reading my books."

"I've been reading *your* books, yes, because someone in this house annotates them in pencil like a scholar and I wanted to know what all the arguing was about." They glanced up, cheeks pink with cold, hair stuck to their forehead. "For the record, you argue with Rosemary Verey a lot."

"She was wrong about underplanting roses with clematis. It's not a partnership, it's a hostage situation."

"There he is," Marlowe said softly. "There's the man who has opinions about things."

I knelt there in the frozen mud with a bare-root rose in my hands and understood, suddenly and completely, what my grandmother's letters had been trying to tell me from the bottom of that seed drawer for seven years. *I stopped playing, darling, and I told myself it was prudence. But a life spent only keeping safe is not kept. It is only spent.*

She'd regretted the silence more than the failure. I had inherited her garden and her regret both, and for a long time I'd tended one and starved the other. Now there was a cello open in the music room and a girl with a river-scratch on her rental cello learning that sounds get scared, and there was Marlowe Finch on their knees in my dirt, and none of it was safe. All of it was alive.

"Marlowe," I said.

"Hm?" They were firming soil around the Gentle Hermione with both palms, thorough as a surgeon.

"When spring comes. When these bloom. I want you here for it. Not as a favor. Not as a guest. Here." I turned the rose stick over in my hands, absurd, thorny, full of next June. "I'm asking for the long version. The perennial version. I've done annuals. I don't recommend them."

Marlowe sat back on their heels. For a moment they didn't say anything, and the garden held its breath around us, frost silvering the mulch, the mill house windows catching the low sun.

"You're proposing," they said finally, "with a dead-looking stick."

"It's not dead. It's dormant. There's a difference."

And they laughed — that laugh, the one that had started all of this in a flower shop over peonies and an old debt neither of us ever called a debt again — and leaned across the hole we'd dug together and kissed me, tasting of cold air and tea and the particular courage of people who plant things in December.

"Yes," they said against my mouth. "Obviously yes. But I'm choosing the varieties from now on. Your taste runs sentimental."

"My taste runs *classic*—"

"*Sentimental.*"

* * *

That evening, after Marlowe had gone home to their flat above the shop — though "home" was becoming a word we used loosely, a word with a lot of traffic between two locations — I walked the garden in the blue hour before full dark.

The beds looked like nothing. Rows of mounded earth, cut-back canes, mulch like a blanket pulled up to the chin. Anyone passing would see a dead garden, a closed house, a man who had once broken in public and gone quiet.

But I knew what was under the ground. Every one of those bare roots was awake down there in the dark, drinking, waiting, doing the slow invisible work of return. Come April there would be leaf-break. Come June, if we were lucky and the winter was kind, the Desdemonas would open their pale buttons and the whole bed by the shed would smell like myrrh and lemons, and Wren would wrinkle her nose and ask why roses couldn't smell like normal flowers, and Marlowe would explain terpenes, and I would stand in the middle of it with dirt under my nails and a cello waiting indoors, and it would be ordinary. Ordinary. The word I'd spent seven years mistaking for surrender.

In the shed, before I locked up, I took out my grandmother's letters one more time. I didn't need to read them anymore; I knew them the way I knew the garden paths, by feel, in the dark. But I liked holding the paper. I liked the loop of her handwriting, the way she pressed hard enough to dent the page, a woman who never did anything lightly, including stopping.

*I stopped playing, darling, and I told myself it was prudence.*

I had stopped too. And then, one Saturday at a time, one imperfect concert at a time, one bare root at a time, I had started.

I set the letters back in the drawer, next to the packets of seeds Marlowe had ordered for February — sweet peas, nasturtiums, a row of sunflowers along the south wall, because Marlowe believed gardens should be slightly ridiculous — and I went inside, where the kettle was still warm and the cello waited by the window, patient as anything planted.

Outside, the frost settled over the beds like a held breath.

Inside, I tuned, and played — badly, then less badly, then, for eight bars, beautifully — and the old mill house listened the way it always had, keeping what it was given, saving it for spring.

Because that was the thing my grandmother had known, and written down, and left for me to find among the seed packets and the dust:

The garden is not a monument to what survived.

It is a promise of what returns.
