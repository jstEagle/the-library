## 1. The Last Train Schedule

The 8:14 arrived on time, which was how Gideon knew it was Tuesday.

They stood at the crossing gate with a paper cup of tea going lukewarm in one hand and Wren's lunchbox in the other, watching the two-car train sigh into Bellbird Bay station the way it had every weekday morning for as long as anyone could remember. Three years now, and Gideon still counted things this way—by trains, by tides, by the small reliable machinery of a life built to hold still. The 8:14 in. The 3:40 school run. Story hour at the shop on Saturdays at ten, when the light came through the front window at an angle that made the dust motes look like something holy. Closing at six, always six, the brass bell over the door giving its one small note as Gideon turned the key.

Safe increments. That was what they called it in the privacy of their own head, though they'd never said it aloud to anyone, least of all to Dr. Iyer, who kept trying to get them to say *spontaneous* like it was a vitamin deficiency.

"Dad. The gate."

Wren tugged their sleeve, seven years old and already the family's chief officer of schedules. She had her mother's patience and Theo's eyebrows, which meant she could communicate entire paragraphs without speaking, and right now her eyebrows were saying *you're doing the thing where you stare at the train like it owes you money.*

"Sorry, bean." Gideon handed her the lunchbox. "Go on. I'll watch you cross."

They watched her cross. They always watched her cross. It was the one increment Gideon had never managed to make feel safe—the thirty seconds between Wren's small hand letting go of theirs and her small hand waving from the schoolyard gate—and they stood at the crossing until the wave came, and only then let themselves breathe.

The harbor was flat and silver behind the station, the pier reaching out into it like a sentence someone had started and never finished. Gideon didn't look at the pier. That was another increment rule: the pier was for low tide and bad nights and the anniversary, and the rest of the time it stayed in the corner of their vision where it couldn't do any damage.

* * *

The shop opened at nine. Harbor Books had been a Zhou institution since Gideon's grandmother bought the building in 1974 with money she'd won at mahjong and refused to apologize for, and it had survived two recessions, one fire (minor, the poetry section, suspiciously), and one Gideon, who had inherited it along with a locked cabinet of ledgers older than the town charter and a daughter they were raising alone.

"Morning, Gideon." Mrs. Okonkwo from the bakery, collecting her reserved copy of the new Amor Towles. "You look tired."

"I look *rested,*" Gideon said. "This is my rested face."

"It's your *widow* face, dear. Same thing in this town."

Gideon rang her up and did not argue, because Mrs. Okonkwo had been bringing bread to the shop every Friday since the funeral, unasked, unexplained, and you didn't argue with a woman who had fed you through the worst year of your life.

The morning went the way mornings went. A tourist asked for a book about lighthouses and left with three. The story-hour puppet—Otter, felt, one eye missing—needed surgery with a hot glue gun. At eleven, Gideon climbed the stepladder to reshelve the maritime history section and found, wedged behind a 1987 tide almanac, the envelope.

Cream paper. Their aunt Mei's handwriting, which was less handwriting than a declaration of war rendered in fountain pen.

*Gideon. Reunion, last Saturday of the month, the hall, six o'clock. Non-negotiable. Your mother says you've been "keeping to yourself," which in this family is a diagnosis. Wear something that isn't flannel. — Aunt Mei*

Gideon stood on the ladder and read it twice, the way you reread a storm warning.

The Zhou family reunion happened every two years, and it was less a gathering than a tribunal with dumplings. There would be the long tables, the aunties presiding like a bench of judges, the cousins' children measured against one another in height and piano proficiency. And there would be Aunt Mei, who had appointed herself the family's minister of unfinished business, and whose current unfinished business—stated openly, at the last three reunions, in front of God and everyone—was Gideon's heart.

"Three years is enough grieving," Mei had announced at the last one, over the sound of Gideon's chopsticks stopping midair. "Even the harbor lets new boats in."

The table had gone silent. Wren, then five, had asked what was wrong with the harbor, and the subject had mercifully changed.

Gideon put the letter in their apron pocket and finished the shelving. They told themselves they simply wouldn't go. It was a free country. They were a grown adult with a shop and a child and a grief they had, through considerable effort, arranged into something load-bearing. They did not need a reunion. They did not need Aunt Mei's roster of eligible widowers and divorced accountants, each one pre-vetted and rated for stability like a used car.

But they knew how it would go. The letter would be followed by a phone call, and the phone call by Mei appearing at the shop "just passing by" (nothing in Bellbird Bay was a passing distance from anything else), and by Saturday there would be a chair reserved for them at the reunion with their name card in Mei's calligraphy and a kind-eyed stranger beside it, and the whole family watching with the tender, unbearable patience of people who loved you and had decided you were a renovation project.

Gideon had survived the worst thing that could happen to a person. They had done it by building a life out of increments—the 8:14, the school run, story hour, six o'clock—and the increments held, most days. But they knew, standing there in the dust-warm light of the maritime section, that the increments were also a wall, and that Aunt Mei, who had known them since before they could read, was coming at the wall with a ladder and a smile.

* * *

Friday, Mei called.

"I'm just checking you received my letter."

"I received your letter."

"And?"

"And I'm considering my options."

"Your options are Saturday at six or I tell your mother you hung up on me." A pause, the sound of tea being poured, the specific clink of Mei's good cup, which she used for interrogations. "There's someone I want you to meet. Daniel. He restores boats. He's steady. He has a beard you can trust."

"A beard I can *trust.*"

"You'll know what I mean when you see it." Mei's voice softened by one degree, which for her was a landslide. "Gideon. I'm not trying to make you forget Theo. Nobody could forget Theo. I'm trying to make sure there's someone to sit with you when the shop's closed and the kid's asleep and the house is too quiet. That's all any of us want."

And that was the cruelty of it, Gideon thought later. She was right. That was exactly the hour the increments didn't cover—the hour after six, after Wren's bedtime story, when the shop was dark and the ledger cabinet was locked and the house held the shape of a family that had lost its third member. Gideon could manage every hour except that one.

"I'll come to the reunion," they said. "But Mei—no ambush. No Daniel. I'm telling you now."

"Of course, of course." The sound of her smile was audible across the phone line, and it should have frightened Gideon more than it did.

* * *

Saturday came gray and mild, the harbor holding its breath under low cloud. Gideon closed the shop early for the first time in three years—5:40, an act of rebellion so small it barely qualified—and drove Wren to the hall with a pot of their good noodles and a heart rate they were pretending was normal.

The hall smelled of ginger and steam. The long tables were dressed in red. The aunties were already seated in their bench formation, and the cousins' children were already being measured against the doorframe, and there, at the head of the second table, was a place card in fountain-pen calligraphy reading *GIDEON* and, beside it, a second card reading *DANIEL (BOATS).*

"Mei," Gideon said, arriving at her elbow with the noodles and the flat voice of a person at the end of their rope. "We talked about this."

"We talked about many things." Mei beamed and steered them toward the seat. "Daniel's just over there. He brought his own chair. He's very self-sufficient."

Gideon looked across the hall. Daniel was, in fairness, exactly as advertised—steady, bearded, trustworthy-looking, currently helping Wren open a soda with the competence of a man who fixed things for a living. Wren was already telling him something about otters. The trap had been sprung with the efficiency of a woman who had been running family reunions since before Gideon was born.

The aunties began to arrive at the table one by one, settling in with their tea, their eyes warm and terrible. Aunt Mei raised her glass for the toast. The hall quieted.

"To family," Mei said, "to the ones we've lost, and—" her gaze landed on Gideon with the precision of a targeting system— "to the ones who are *still writing their story.*"

Forty faces turned toward Gideon. Daniel smiled his trustworthy smile. Wren looked up, curious. Somewhere in the kitchen a lid clattered, and the silence stretched, and Gideon felt the whole weight of the family's hope settle on their shoulders like a coat made of lead and love, and heard their own mouth open, and heard—before any part of their brain could intervene, before the increments could catch it—

"I can't sit with Daniel," Gideon said. "I'm seeing someone."

The words went off in the hall like a firework.

Mei's teacup stopped an inch from her lips. The aunties leaned in as one organism. Wren's eyebrows performed a paragraph.

"You're *what,*" said Mei.

"Seeing someone," Gideon said, and the lie, once out, did what lies do—it grew legs. "It's new. Very new. You wouldn't know them."

"New," Mei repeated, in the tone of a customs officer finding something undeclared in your luggage. "Who. What's their name. Where are they from. When do we meet them."

And Gideon, standing in the middle of forty waiting faces, with no name, no story, and no idea what they had just done, opened their mouth to answer.

The hall doors opened behind them.
