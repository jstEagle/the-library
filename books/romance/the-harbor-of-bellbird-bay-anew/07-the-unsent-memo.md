## 7. The Unsent Memo

The email arrived at 6:47 a.m., while the harbor was still the color of pewter and the fishing boats were just beginning to knock against their moorings like restless sleepers.

**Hale:** File's due Friday. Festival weekend is your window — council will be distracted, sentiment will be high, and I want the acquisition package on Whitcombe's desk before they can organize. Don't come home without it.

Marisol read it three times, standing barefoot in the kitchen of her rented cottage with coffee going cold in her hand. *Don't come home.* As if Bellbird Bay were a posting, a tour of duty. As if she hadn't spent six weeks learning the particular creak of the bookshop's third floorboard, the way Wren Zhou pronounced "amphibious" as "am-FIB-ous" with total confidence, the exact shade Gideon's face went when they laughed — a slow flush starting at the ears, like dawn coming up over water.

*Home* was a word that had started to mean something else entirely, and that was precisely the problem.

She set down the mug. On her laptop, open from the night before, sat the memo. The honest one. Forty pages of due diligence, annotated in her own cramped handwriting: *pier pilings original 1923 construction, load-bearing compromised but restorable; block seven contains two businesses operating continuously since pre-war; community attachment documented, extensive; acquisition feasible but socially catastrophic.*

She had written it the way she'd been trained to write everything — clean, clinical, defensible. And then, somewhere around page twelve, the clinical language had begun to rot from the inside. She'd caught herself typing *the pier holds no structural significance* and deleting it, because it was true in an engineering sense and a lie in every other way. She'd caught herself cataloguing the ledger — Gideon's ledger, the one with its water-stained cover and its hundred years of names — as an *asset of negligible market value,* and had closed the laptop so hard the screen had flickered.

That draft was still there. Waiting. Honest, damning, and due.

Her phone buzzed again. Not Hale this time. A text from Gideon:

*Wren wants to know if you're coming to story hour today. She's told everyone you do the seagull voice better than I do. No pressure. (Enormous pressure.)*

Marisol laughed — actually laughed, alone in a rented kitchen at dawn — and felt the laugh crack something open on its way out. Because here was the arithmetic she could no longer avoid: on one side of the scale, a promotion she had spent nine years earning, a corner office, a salary that would finally let her pay off her mother's medical debts in full. On the other side, a seagull voice. A child who saved her a seat. A person who looked at her across a crowded reunion table and made a lie feel like a memory of something real.

And underneath both sides, like the bedrock under the harbor itself: the fact that she had come here to buy the ground out from under all of them.

* * *

Hale arrived Thursday, unannounced, in a silver rental car that looked absurdly sleek against the salt-bleached storefronts of Harbor Street. Marisol saw him before he saw her — saw him pause outside the bookshop, consult his phone, and frown at the hand-painted sign advertising the festival, as though the whole town were an obstacle someone had failed to clear from his path.

"You look terrible," he said, by way of greeting, when she met him at the cottage door. "Coastal air not agreeing with you?"

"It's agreeing with me fine."

He stepped past her into the kitchen without being asked, dropped a leather portfolio on the table, and helped himself to the chair. Hale Voss had been her director for four years. He was not a villain; that was the exhausting part. He was a man who genuinely believed that waterfronts were wasted on waterfront towns, that sentiment was a line item, that anything beautiful which couldn't be monetized was simply inefficient. He had promoted her twice. He had once told her, drunk at a holiday party, that she was the only person in the firm who understood that *everything is negotiable, Marisol, everything, that's the whole secret.*

"The package," he said now, tapping the portfolio. "Walk me through it."

"I'm still finalizing—"

"You've had six weeks." He smiled, and it was almost kind. "Marisol. You know what this deal means for you. Principal. Your name on the door by spring. You didn't grind through nine years to stall out over a fishing pier."

"No," she said. "I didn't."

But her voice did something strange on the second sentence — thinned, wavered — and Hale's eyes sharpened the way a hawk's do when the field mouse moves wrong.

"Something you want to tell me?"

The honest memo sat on her laptop ten feet away. The falsified one lived only in her head, half-built, monstrous and merciful at once. She thought about Wren's question from Sunday — *will you still be here after the festival?* — and how Gideon had answered for her, smoothly, *of course she will,* and how Marisol had said nothing, because what was there to say that wasn't either a lie or a confession?

"There's a complication," she heard herself say. "The community attachment is stronger than projected. There's a historical registry — a private ledger, a century old — that documents continuous family ownership of the harbor lots. If it surfaces during review, our environmental and heritage assessments are exposed. I need until festival's end to neutralize it."

It came out fluent. That was the horror of it — how fluently the lie assembled itself, how naturally her mouth produced the language of the firm even while the rest of her stood at a distance watching, appalled, like a woman seeing her own reflection in a window she'd meant to walk through.

Hale leaned back, satisfied. "Neutralize it. Good. That's why you get the corner office." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and paused at the door. "One thing, though." His tone hadn't changed, but the temperature had. "Whitcombe mentioned the Zhou file. The bookseller. Says our scout has been *seen* around town with them. Festival date, apparently. Very public."

Marisol kept her face still. Somewhere behind her ribs, her heart was doing quiet, catastrophic work.

"Relationships are intelligence," Hale said mildly. "You taught me that. Just make sure when the file lands, the relationship doesn't complicate the narrative. If the bookseller turns out to be a problem—" he smiled again, that almost-kind smile, "—well. Conflicts of interest have ended careers, Marisol. Yours included. Friday. Five o'clock."

The rental car pulled away, trailing gravel. Marisol stood in the doorway for a long time, listening to the gulls argue over the tide line, feeling the shape of the trap close around her with a click she could almost hear.

* * *

Thursday night she walked the pier.

She hadn't planned to. Her feet decided, carrying her down Harbor Street past the darkened shop — Gideon's lamp still burning upstairs, a warm square of gold above the awning — and out along the boards where the festival crew had already strung their lights, hundreds of small glass bulbs waiting for Friday dusk like held breath.

This was where it had happened, Gideon had told her, that first walk after the reunion. Right about here, near the third piling, where the wood was worn smooth. Daniel had proposed with a ring hidden in a hollowed-out copy of *Persuasion* — because Gideon had never finished it, always stopped before Captain Wentworth's letter, unable to bear it, and Daniel had said *you'll finish it someday, and when you do, I'll be asking you a question.* Gideon had found the ring on page 213 and cried so hard they couldn't speak, and the tide had come in around their shoes, and neither of them had moved.

Marisol stood on that spot now and understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what her memo would erase. Not a structure. An inscription. The pier was a page, and every scuff and patch and salt-bitten rail was handwriting, and her employer wanted to pave over the text and pour concrete.

She took out her phone and opened the honest draft one last time. Read the clinical sentences. *Acquisition feasible. Community resistance manageable. Sentimentality factor: moderate.*

*Moderate.* She thought of Gideon's face going slowly pink with laughter. Of Wren asleep against her shoulder during the movie, drooling slightly on her cardigan. Of the ledger's flyleaf, where some long-dead shopkeeper had written, in faded iron-gall ink: *Let this book hold what the sea might otherwise take.*

She thought of Hale's voice: *conflicts of interest have ended careers.*

They had. They would. She knew exactly what Friday at five o'clock would cost her if she chose wrong — the promotion, the career, the nine years, the mother's debts unpaid, the whole architecture of the person she had built herself into being. Discovery meant ruin. Not metaphorical ruin. The real kind, the kind with lawyers in it.

Marisol stood on the pier where a dead man had once hidden a proposal inside a novel, and she asked herself the only question that mattered:

*Which side am I on — and when, exactly, did I choose it?*

Because that was the thing. She had already chosen. She'd chosen weeks ago, maybe — the night she silenced Hale's call on the seawall while Gideon showed her the ledger by flashlight. Maybe the night she offered to play the date, before she knew anything except that this person's loneliness recognized hers. Every day since had just been the choosing continuing, quietly, one unsent memo at a time.

The lights overhead flickered on — someone testing the circuit — and the whole pier lit up gold around her, and Marisol Vega stood in the middle of it and let the last version of her old life go.

* * *

Friday morning, in the cottage kitchen, she opened the honest draft one final time. Selected all forty pages. Deleted them — not to trash, but fully, holding the key down until the document was white space, until there was nothing left to subpoena, nothing left to betray her except herself.

Then she built the new memo from scratch. It took her four hours. She wrote it in the firm's language, the fluent dialect of her captivity, and she made it good — made it bulletproof — because a bad lie helps no one. She documented the pier's heritage status with citations she spent the morning digging out of the county archive. She attached photographs of the ledger's pages, framed as *evidence of adverse possession claims likely to stall litigation for years.* She wrote that the community attachment was not moderate but *severe and litigious,* that the acquisition timeline was unrealistic, that the firm should withdraw or expect a decade of costly resistance. She buried the truth in procedure the way a seed is buried in soil — deep, but alive, and pointed at the light.

At 4:52 p.m. she hit send.

At 5:01 her phone rang. HALE VOSS. She watched it ring eight times, then declined it. It rang again. She turned the phone face-down on the table, next to a postcard Wren had made her at story hour — a crayon seagull with enormous yellow feet, labeled MARI-SOL in wobbling capitals — and walked out into the festival evening, toward the lights, toward the harbor, toward the people she had chosen without ever being asked to choose.

Behind her, the phone kept buzzing against the wood like something trapped.

Ahead, down Harbor Street, the whole town was spilling toward the water, and she could hear the bookshop bell, and she could hear, faintly, Wren laughing.

She had until the truth surfaced. Days, maybe. Hours, if Hale flew in angry enough.

Marisol straightened her shoulders and went to spend whatever time remained exactly where she wanted to be.
