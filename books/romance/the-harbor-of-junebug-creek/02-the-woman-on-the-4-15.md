## 2. The Woman on the 4:15

The train began to slow eleven minutes before it reached Junebug Creek, and Delia Marsh felt every one of those minutes in her chest like a hand tightening around a fistful of thread.

She had counted them. Eleven minutes. She had been counting things for ten years — steps around the exercise yard, days between visits that never came, the number of times she'd rewritten a single paragraph until the paper went soft as cloth. Counting was how you survived a place built to erase you. But now, watching the valley unspool past the window — the creek first, silver-bright in the late afternoon light, then the water tower with its peeling paint, then the rooftops of the town she had left at twenty-three in handcuffs she hadn't earned — counting felt less like survival and more like terror wearing a practical coat.

Eleven minutes to decide who she was going to be.

The woman across the aisle, a grandmotherly type with a knitting bag the size of a small dog, leaned over. "Visiting family?" she asked, nodding at the window, at the creek sliding by below.

"Moving home," Delia said. The words tasted strange, like fruit from a tree she'd planted in someone else's yard.

"Well, isn't that lovely." The woman smiled, warm and uncomplicated, and Delia understood that this was what freedom actually looked like — not the gates opening, not the bus ride, not the crisp new clothes they'd returned to her in a paper sack, but this: a stranger's easy smile, offered without knowing anything at all about you. It would not last. Nothing lasted. But she let herself hold it for a moment anyway, the way you cup water in your hands before it drains through.

Her suitcase sat in the rack above her head. One suitcase. Everything she owned fit inside it with room left over, which was either poverty or discipline or both. But the box — the box rode on her lap, both hands flat on its lid, the way other women held sleeping infants. Cardboard, soft-cornered, tied with twine gone gray from handling. Inside were four hundred and twelve letters. Every letter she had written to Jonah Ravensworth in ten years. Not the ones she'd sent — those had gone out on birthdays, unsigned, one each year, ten in all, thin as prayers. These were the others. The ones written at two in the morning, written after bad nights, written when the guilt got so loud she couldn't hear the television down the block. *Dear Jonah. Today I found the second ledger page. I don't know why I'm telling you this when you'll never read it.* Unsent. Unsendable. A decade of confession addressed to a man who thought his father had died a thief.

The train shuddered. Junebug Creek station slid into view — the same green awning, faded almost white now, the same bench where her father used to wait for her after school with two sodas sweating in his fists. Her father. Elias Marsh, whose name was carved into the vineyard sign on the ridge road, whose signature was carved into a lie that had outlived him.

Delia stood too fast. The knitting woman wished her luck; Delia heard it from very far away.

* * *

The platform smelled the way it always had — creosote and creek water and, faintly, someone frying onions somewhere up the hill. Ten years, and the world had changed everything except the smell of a small town at dinnertime. That was the cruelest thing about coming back, she thought. Not the stares she was braced for, not the whispers. The sameness. The way the station clock still ran four minutes slow. The way the bench still had the initials some kid had carved in 1987. The whole town had simply continued without her, indifferent as weather, while she had turned herself inside out trying to earn her way back into it.

A porter set her suitcase down on the platform and asked if she needed a cab. She said no. The apartment was six blocks away, and she intended to walk every one of them, because she had promised herself she would arrive on her own feet or not at all.

The box was heavier than it had been on the bus. Or her arms were weaker; ten years will do that. She shifted it against her hip and started up Station Road.

Junebug Creek in October was gold light and long shadows, porches with pumpkins, a dog asleep in a truck bed. People nodded at her. One woman — Mrs. Halloran, who used to run the church bake sale — squinted at her for three full seconds, and Delia watched the recognition move across her face like a cloud crossing a field. Delia lifted her chin and kept walking. She had rehearsed this. *Yes, that's me. Yes, I'm back. No, I won't apologize for existing.* She had rehearsed it so many times the sentences had worn smooth, but putting them into practice made her palms sweat inside her gloves.

Six blocks. At the corner of Harbor Street, she stopped.

There it was. Ravensworth Café.

It was smaller than she remembered, or maybe memory had inflated it the way memory does with places that matter. Green trim, slightly peeling. A chalkboard sign out front advertising *soup of the day: tomato basil*. Warm light spilled across the sidewalk, and through the window she could see a broad-shouldered man moving behind the counter, flour to his elbows, laughing at something an old man at the corner table had said. Jonah Ravensworth. Thirty-four years old now. He had been nineteen the last time she saw him, at the funeral, standing beside the casket of a father the whole town believed had stolen from hers. Jonah hadn't cried. He'd just stood there with his jaw set like a boy determined to become a wall.

He had no idea she was looking at him. He had no idea she had been looking at him, in one way or another, for ten years.

Delia's legs nearly gave out on the sidewalk. Not from weakness — from the sheer weight of the moment arriving. This was the plan. It had always been the plan. Write the letters, serve the time, come back, tell the truth. Simple as a recipe. But plans are things you make in one life and execute in another, and the woman who'd made this plan — young, furious, certain — seemed almost laughably naive to the woman standing here now, holding a box of sins on a public street.

Because here was what the plan hadn't accounted for: he looked happy. Tired, sure, there were shadows under his eyes even from across the street, and when he rang up the old man he checked the register twice like a man who counted everything. But he laughed easily. He moved through his café like it was an extension of his body. And she was about to walk into his life carrying the knowledge that could crack all of it open — or bury him under it again.

*You don't have to do it today,* she told herself. *You don't have to do it ever, if—*

No. That was the coward's door, and she'd spent ten years learning to recognize it. Her father had walked through it every day of his life, smiling, pouring wine, signing other men's names. She would not be Elias Marsh's daughter in that way. She would be his daughter in only one way: she knew how to find a paper trail, and she never forgot a number.

She crossed the street.

* * *

The rental listing had been pinned to the board outside the hardware store for three weeks — she'd had her caseworker confirm it by phone — *APARTMENT FOR RENT ABOVE CAFÉ, HARBOR ST., INQUIRE WITHIN.* She had told herself it was coincidence, the universe being efficient. But standing in front of the door with her hand raised to knock, she admitted the truth: there was no such thing as coincidence in Junebug Creek, only gravity. Everything rolled downhill toward the harbor eventually. Including her.

She knocked.

The laughter inside stopped. Footsteps, the jingle of a bell above a door, and then it opened and Jonah Ravensworth was standing in front of her with a dish towel over his shoulder and flour on his forearm, and his face did something complicated — polite confusion first, then a flicker of something older, something searching, the look of a man trying to place a melody he half-remembered.

"Evening," he said. "You looking for the apartment?"

"I am." Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I called last week. Delia Marsh."

Something happened to his face. It was small — a stillness around the eyes, a fractional pause — but Delia had spent ten years studying men's faces for the moment they decided what you were, and she caught it. He knew the name. Of course he knew the name. Everyone in this town knew the name; it was on the winery labels stacked in every store, on the festival posters going up for harvest season, on the deed to half the waterfront.

But he didn't say anything about it. He stepped back and held the door. "Come in. Coffee's fresh, if you drink it. If you don't, I've got tea my mother bought in 2011 that I'm afraid to throw out."

The joke was automatic, hostly, and it cracked something loose in her chest. "Coffee," she said. "Black."

"Course it is." He led her through the café — she catalogued it as she went, the way she'd learned to catalogue rooms: exits, windows, the heavy cast-iron pan hanging by the stove that could stop a door — past tables being wiped, past the old man at the corner who looked up at her with frank, unhurried curiosity. The stairs to the apartment were narrow and smelled of cinnamon. Everything smelled of cinnamon and bread, and it occurred to her, absurdly, that she might cry from the smell alone. Bread was the smell of before. Before the trial, before the letters, before any of it, when she was a girl who came down to this harbor on Saturdays and bought a roll with coins her father gave her, coins that were clean because nobody had looked too closely at where they came from.

The apartment was small and clean and had a window overlooking the harbor, and the harbor at dusk was doing its best impression of heaven. Jonah stood in the doorway, not entering, keeping the threshold between them like a man who understood boundaries instinctively.

"Rent's six hundred," he said. "Includes heat, which matters, because the radiator sounds like it's dying but it does die loudly rather than quietly, if that makes sense. Month-to-month. I'm not fussy about tenants as long as they don't burn the place down and don't mind flour dust drifting up through the floorboards at five a.m."

"It makes sense," she said. "And I don't mind flour."

"You bake?"

"No." The word came out harder than she meant it. She softened it. "Not anymore."

He studied her for a beat — that searching look again, the melody he couldn't place — then nodded and didn't push. She liked him for that immediately, fiercely, the way you like a stranger who doesn't ask about the scar.

"I'll take it," she said. "If you'll have me."

"It's yours." He dug a key off a ring in his pocket and held it out, and when she took it their fingers didn't touch, but the key was warm from his pocket, and that warmth traveled up her arm like a rumor. "Welcome to Junebug Creek, Ms. Marsh."

*Ms. Marsh.* There it was. The name laid out between them like a card on a table. He knew. He had to know. And he'd handed her a key anyway, because that was the kind of man his father had raised him to be before the town took the father away — decent to a fault, generous past the point of sense.

Delia carried her box upstairs alone. She set it on the kitchen table beneath the window, where the last of the light lay across its lid like a blessing or a sentence, and she untied the twine with fingers that shook only a little.

Four hundred and twelve letters. Somewhere downstairs, a man was locking up his café, whistling, unaware that the woman above him had spent a decade writing him the truth in installments she never mailed.

One drawer at a time, she told herself, closing the flaps again. One truth at a time. You didn't survive ten years by rushing the ending.

Downstairs, the bell above the café door chimed. Through the floorboards she heard Jonah's voice greeting someone — warm, tired, kind — and then, cutting through it, another voice. Male, polished, amused, the voice of a man accustomed to rooms falling silent when he entered.

"Jonah Ravensworth? Cade Marsh. We should talk about your lease."

Delia went cold from the soles of her feet up.
