## 3. Letters I Never Sent

The letters had come every year on the twenty-third of March.

Not the day his father died — that was October, gray and wet and wrong in every direction — but the birthday, the one that still smelled of burnt sugar if he let himself remember hard enough, because his father had always insisted on making the cake himself even though he couldn't bake to save his life, and every year it came out of the oven lopsided and weeping frosting, and every year Jonah had loved it more than anything else on earth.

So on the twenty-third of March, for ten years running, an envelope had arrived at Ravensworth Café with no return address and handwriting that slanted left like a person leaning away from something. Inside, always, a single page. Unsigned. Unexplained. The first one had come when he was nineteen, three months after the funeral, when the café was a week old and he was sleeping on a cot in the back room because he couldn't bear to sleep in the house where his father's coat still hung by the door.

*Your father did not take that money,* the first letter said. *I know this is worth nothing coming from a stranger, but I need you to know it. He was an honest man. I am sorry.*

Jonah had read it forty times. Had taken it down to the police station, once, hands shaking, and been met with the tired patience of a desk sergeant who'd known his father for thirty years and was sorry, kid, really sorry, but the case was closed, the books were closed, the Marsh family had declined to press further charges out of what everyone called mercy and what Jonah's mother, before she left, had called something else entirely.

He'd written back anyway. That was the thing nobody understood, the thing he'd never told anyone — he wrote back. Every year. To a return address that didn't exist, care of nothing, sealed and stamped and sent into the dark. *Who are you? How do you know? Why won't you tell me?* Ten years of questions stacked like cups in a cupboard, and every March the answer came back the same: another page of apology, another fragment of a truth offered without its shape, another signature withheld.

*I can't tell you who I am yet. When I can, you'll wish I never had.*

That line had kept him up more nights than the money ever had.

* * *

The woman upstairs had been living above the café for six days before Jonah figured out what her footsteps sounded like.

That was how he knew people, when he bothered to know them at all — by their weight on his ceiling. Old Man Petrosian shuffled, dragging his left heel. The Hendersons' boy ran everywhere, a small thunderstorm at seven each morning. But the new tenant moved the way water moves under ice: deliberate, quiet, controlled, as though she'd learned somewhere that being heard was dangerous.

She'd taken the apartment on a Tuesday, cash, no references, and Ruthie at the rental office had called him that afternoon in a flutter — *you're the closest neighbor, Jonah, I just thought you should know, she's got some kind of history, they don't say what, but you watch yourself.* Junebug Creek loved two things above all others: pie and other people's sins, and it consumed both with equal appetite.

Jonah had watched himself. He'd watched her carry a single suitcase up the exterior stairs in the rain, refusing the umbrella the hardware store man offered. He'd watched her set down a cardboard box so heavy she had to rest twice on the stairs, and he'd watched her look at the café sign while she caught her breath — not at the apartment, not at the street, but at the sign, RAVENSWORTH CAFÉ, EST. 1998, with the particular attention of someone reading a sentence she already knew by heart.

He should have felt the cold walk up his spine then. Instead he'd felt something worse: recognition, faint and unplaceable, like a song heard through a wall.

It came to him on the sixth night, standing in his kitchen at eleven o'clock with flour to his elbows, proofing dough for the morning because sleep had become a country he could visit but not stay in. Above him, the floorboards creaked their slow, careful pattern — kitchen to window, window to kitchen. And the light from her window fell across the alley between the buildings and lay on the bricks below in a long pale stripe, and Jonah stood looking at that stripe of light and thought: *she leans left.*

The handwriting leaned left.

He dried his hands. He took the tin box down from the shelf above the refrigerator — the one behind the emergency candles, the one he told himself he kept there for practical reasons — and he lifted out the top letter, the tenth one, the one that had arrived eight months ago, and he held it under the kitchen light beside nothing at all, because he didn't need anything beside it. He knew that hand. He had spent a decade knowing that hand. The way the *y* dropped low and hooked, the way the *t* was crossed with a single impatient stroke, the way whole words seemed to lean away from the right margin as if the writer were trying to get as far from what she was saying as the page would allow.

*When I can, you'll wish I never had.*

He looked up at the ceiling, at the place where the footsteps paused by the window.

"Oh," he said, out loud, to the empty kitchen, and the word came out small and strange, like something a much younger man would say.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He baked instead — a double batch of the honey buns his father used to make badly, which Jonah made well, which was its own kind of grief — and at six-forty in the morning, with the case filled and the ovens ticking as they cooled and the first gray light coming up over the creek, he heard her door open above him.

He was out the back and up the exterior stairs before he had decided to be, which was unlike him; Jonah Ravensworth decided things, weighed them, turned them over like stones. But his feet took the stairs two at a time and his knuckles were on her door before his courage caught up, and then it was too late, because the door opened.

Delia Marsh stood in the frame with her hair pushed back and a mug in her hand, and up close, in the flat early light, she looked exactly like what she was: a woman who had been away for a long time and had not yet decided whether she was staying. There were shadows under her eyes that looked structural rather than temporary. Her cardigan was buttoned wrong by one.

"Mr. Ravensworth," she said. Not surprised. That was the first thing he noticed — not surprised, and not pleased either, but braced, the way you brace for weather you've been promised.

"You know my name."

"I know everyone's name. It's a small town." She said it evenly, but her thumb pressed white against the ceramic of the mug.

"It's a small town," Jonah agreed. "Which is funny, because I've lived here my whole life, and I don't know yours. Except I do." His voice came out lower than he intended. "Don't I. Delia."

Something crossed her face — not fear exactly, and not guilt, or not only guilt. It was the look of a person watching a door close that they had been holding open for a very long time at great cost.

"Ruthie talks," Delia said.

"Ruthie talks about tenants. Ruthie doesn't talk about—" He stopped. Breathed. The smell of honey and warm yeast drifted up the stairwell behind him, absurdly domestic, and he hated it, hated how ordinary the morning wanted to be. "Ten years," he said. "Every March. Twenty-third. No return address. No name. 'Your father did not take that money.' 'I'm sorry.' 'When I can tell you who I am, you'll wish I never had.'" He watched her face while he said it, watched each sentence land like a stone dropped into still water, ring after ring after ring. "I answered them. Do you know that? Every year I wrote back. Who are you. Why won't you tell me. What do you know about my father."

"I know," she said quietly.

"You *know*?" The word cracked open in his mouth. "You stood up there reading them — you stood on these stairs six days ago and read my name off that sign, and you knew you'd been writing to me since I was nineteen years old, and you said nothing?"

"Yes."

"That's it? *Yes?*"

"What would you like me to say, Mr. Ravensworth?" She set the mug down on the windowsill inside the door, carefully, precisely, the way people do when they're afraid of what their hands will do otherwise. "You're right. I wrote the letters. I'm sorry. I'm sorry for all of it — for the years, for the silence, for standing here now with your coffee smell in my nose and your father's name in my mouth. If you want me gone from this apartment, I'll go today. But I won't give you the story. Not because I don't owe it to you. Because I do — all of it, every page — and you deserve better than a confession delivered on a stairwell before seven in the morning by a woman you have every reason to hate."

"Hate." He laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "You think that's what this is? You think I climbed these stairs because I hate you?" He dragged a hand through his hair and found flour in it, and that undid him a little, the sheer stupid domesticity of it. "Do you know what those letters were? Do you have any idea? My father died in October. In November they buried him under a lie — embezzlement, theft, *thief*, that was his name in this town, that was the name on his grave as far as everyone was concerned. And in March, when I was nineteen years old and alone in a building full of ovens I didn't know how to run, an envelope showed up saying *he was honest, I'm sorry.*" His voice was shaking now and he let it. "Those letters were the only thing in ten years that said my father wasn't what they said he was. You want to know what I did with them? I memorized them. I built a café on top of them. I *answered* them, every year, into nothing, like a man talking to a grave — except the grave talked back, and now the grave is standing in front of me with her cardigan buttoned wrong asking me what I'd like her to say."

For a moment she said nothing at all. Then, very softly: "It was buttoned wrong?"

"*That's* what you—"

"No." She closed her eyes briefly. "No. I'm sorry. I do that — I reach for the small thing when the large thing will drown us both. It's a habit." She opened her eyes, and whatever armor she'd assembled was thinner now; he could see the woman through it, tired and resolved and frightened in a way that had nothing to do with him. Or everything to do with him. "You asked why I said nothing on the stairs. Because I've been rehearsing this conversation for ten years, Mr. Ravensworth, and every version of it ends with you looking at me the way you're looking at me now. I wanted five more days of not having earned that look."

"You've had them. You've had ten years of them."

"I've had ten years of earning them," she said. "There's a difference. There's a difference, and it matters, and I know it doesn't matter to you right now, and it shouldn't." She stepped back from the door — not retreat, exactly, but the creation of distance, the drawing of a line in the narrow space between them. "Here's what I can give you today. The letters were true. All of them. Your father was an honest man, and the world is going to know it eventually, and I am going to be the reason or I am going to die trying. That's not a confession. It's a promise. The rest — the who and the how and the why-now — you'll get it, all of it, when I can give it to you whole. Broken pieces are how they got your father. I won't hand you pieces."

"They?" Jonah seized on it. "*They* who? Delia — is that even your name?"

"It's my name." Something flickered there, some old wound flexing. "It's the only thing I've got that's mine, so yes. It's my name."

Below them, faintly, the bell over the café door rang — the first customer, old Mrs. Alvarado probably, wanting her corn muffin and her gossip in equal measure — and the sound fell into the space between them like a coin into a well. Jonah became aware of the hour, the light, the ovens cooling behind him, the whole ordinary machinery of a morning that had stopped being ordinary somewhere around the second stair.

"The twenty-third of March," he said. "Why the birthday? Of all the days. Why that one?"

Delia looked at him for a long moment, and he saw her decide — saw the decision move through her like weather — and what she finally gave him was almost nothing, and somehow it was the most she'd given anyone in a decade.

"Because it was the last good day," she said. "Before. I checked the date against the ledgers. Everything before your father's forty-fourth birthday was clean. Everything after—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening. "That's a piece. I said no pieces."

And then she reached inside the door, past the mug on the sill, and came back with an envelope — worn soft at the corners, handled a thousand times, and Jonah knew it before she turned it around, knew it the way you know your own reflection: his own handwriting, nineteen years old and furious, *WHO ARE YOU* pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn.

"You kept it," he said. "All these years. You kept my letters."

"I kept everything," Delia Marsh said. "Keeping things is the only talent I have left. One day soon I'm going to show you all of it — every letter, every page, everything I've carried. And when I do, you're going to understand why I couldn't do it on a Tuesday morning in a doorway." Her voice didn't waver, but her hand, offering him his own letter back, was not quite steady. "Until then, I'll be upstairs. Being quiet. Being sorry. Both of those I'm very good at."

Jonah took the envelope. His fingers brushed hers, and she flinched — actually flinched, a small involuntary thing, as though kindness were a voltage she'd forgotten how to ground — and he stepped back down one stair, then another, because suddenly the landing was too small for both of them and everything unsaid.

At the bottom he turned around. She was still in the doorway, watching him, the morning light catching the shadows under her eyes.

"The honey buns come out of the oven at noon," he heard himself say, and didn't know why he said it, and hated that he said it, and knew with terrible certainty that he meant it. "First ones are free. For neighbors."

Her face did something complicated — grief and gratitude and guilt all trying to fit through the same door at once.

"Mr. Ravensworth," she said gently, "you shouldn't be kind to me."

"Yeah," Jonah said, climbing the stairs back down toward his father's café, toward the ovens, toward the ten years of letters hidden behind the emergency candles. "I've been told that before."

Above him, the floorboards began their slow walk — kitchen to window, window to kitchen — and he stood in the alley in the flour-smelling dawn, holding his own nineteen-year-old rage softened at the corners by a stranger's keeping, and thought: *when she tells me, I'll wish she never had.*

He went inside and turned the ovens back on.
