## 7. Terms of Tenancy

The man from the bank had smelled like pencil shavings and pity, and Jonah Ravensworth had spent the rest of that Tuesday trying to bake the memory out of his hands. It didn't work. Kneading dough was a prayer he'd learned at his father's elbow — *push through it, son, push all the way to the bottom and back* — but even prayer couldn't change what was printed on the notice folded in his apron pocket: **THIRD NOTICE. FINAL REMEDY PENDING.** Three missed payments. Sixty days, maybe less, before the harbor block's oldest tenant became a story old-timers told over coffee about how things used to be.

So when the black sedan slid up in front of the café at half past two on a Thursday afternoon — too polished for Junebug Creek, too clean for the salt air — Jonah knew before the driver opened the door that the day had come to collect its interest.

Cade Marsh unfolded himself from the back seat like a man who'd never once worried about fitting into a space. He wore a suit the color of wet slate, no tie, collar open just enough to say *I'm relaxed* without meaning it. His hair was sun-streaked gold going silver at the temples, and his smile arrived a full second before the rest of him did, crossing the sidewalk ahead of his shoes.

"Jonah Ravensworth." He extended a hand. "I don't think we've met properly. Cade Marsh."

Jonah wiped flour onto his jeans and took it. The grip was warm, dry, calibrated. "I know who you are. Everybody knows who you are."

"All the more reason I should introduce myself anyway." Cade glanced past Jonah's shoulder at the café window, where the day's sourdough loaves were cooling in ranks behind the glass. "May I? I've heard your father's rye is famous as far south as Astoria. I'd hate to leave without knowing whether the reputation holds."

*My father's rye.* Something turned over in Jonah's chest, slow and cold, like a boat shifting against its mooring. He stepped aside and let the heir of Marsh Vineyards walk into the café his family had helped empty.

* * *

Cade ordered nothing. That was the first tell. He stood at the counter while Jonah poured him a coffee he hadn't asked for — because you didn't let a man stand in your shop empty-handed, not even this one — and he looked around the room with the unhurried attention of someone taking inventory. The tin ceiling. The counter his grandfather had planed by hand. The photograph behind the register: Elias Ravensworth, younger than Jonah ever knew him, sleeves rolled, laughing at something outside the frame.

"That's him," Cade said. Not a question.

"That's my father."

"He came out to the estate once, years ago. I must have been twelve." Cade wrapped both hands around his cup like a man warming himself at a fire. "I remember watching him talk to my father in the barrel room. Your father had a way of standing — square, unbothered. My father didn't like people who stood like that." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "It's a shame what happened to him. The accusations. Terrible business."

Jonah set down the coffee pot with exaggerated care. "Was there something you wanted, Mr. Marsh?"

"Call me Cade." He smiled again, and this time it reached all the way to his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Actually, yes. There is something I want. And I suspect it's something you want too."

He reached into his jacket and produced an envelope — heavy cream stock, expensive paper, the kind of stationery that announced its own price before you read a word of it. He laid it on the counter between them, squared its corners to the edge, and left his fingertips resting on it.

"I'm acquiring properties along the harbor," Cade said. "Quietly. A revitalization project — boutique hotel, tasting room, a boardwalk worthy of the creek. The kind of investment this town has been begging for since the mill closed. Most of the block has already signed." He tapped the envelope once. "Your lease is the last piece. And I understand — I genuinely understand — that you're in a difficult position with the bank. So I'm prepared to make this easy. Generously easy. The buyout figure inside would clear your debts twice over, with enough left to start whatever you wanted next. Bakery in Portland. Culinary school in Paris. A long vacation somewhere the fog doesn't reach. You'd be free, Jonah. No notices in the mail. No inspector walking through your kitchen with a clipboard. Just a check and a clean horizon."

The café was very quiet. Out on the water, a gull screamed once and gave up.

Jonah looked at the envelope. Cream stock. Heavy. Probably cost more per sheet than a loaf of rye earned in a week. He thought about the notice in his apron pocket, the weight of it he'd been carrying for three weeks like a stone sewn into the lining. He thought about sixty days. He thought about Paris, which he had never once wanted, and about the fog, which he loved the way you love a stubborn relative.

Then he thought about Delia Marsh, three floors up, who had moved in six weeks ago with one suitcase and a box of letters, and who had shown up at midnight with sandbags when his kitchen nearly burned down around him, working beside him until dawn without asking for a single explanation or offering one either.

"Why now?" Jonah asked.

Cade tilted his head, amused. "Why now what?"

"The harbor's been dying for ten years. The mill closed, the cannery closed, half these storefronts are held together with paint and prayer. Where was the revitalization project when my father was alive?" Jonah nodded at the photograph. "Where was it last year?"

"Timing is everything in business." Cade said it lightly, but something flickered behind the charm — a shutter drawn halfway down a window. "And frankly, the timing isn't about you. It's about the harvest festival. New era for the Marsh name. We want the waterfront to reflect that."

*We want the waterfront to reflect that.* Jonah turned the phrase over. It sounded less like a vision and more like a coat of paint over a crack.

"And if I say no?"

Something shifted in Cade's posture — barely anything, a degree of temperature, the difference between a handshake and a grip. "Then I'll be disappointed. And the bank will be the bank. But I want to be clear, Jonah, because I respect you, and I respected your father: this offer expires. Opportunities like this don't wait. Neither do banks." He pushed the envelope an inch closer. "Take some time. Read the number. Then call me."

"I don't need time."

"No?"

Jonah picked up the envelope. It was lighter than it looked. Everything expensive was. He held it out across the counter, back toward the golden boy of Marsh Vineyards, and watched the smile finally leave Cade's face — not all at once, but the way frost leaves a window, in patches.

"I appreciate you coming down here personally," Jonah said. "That took effort, and I don't think you waste effort. Which tells me my lease matters to you more than you're saying, and I've been lied to by a Marsh before. My whole life was built on it." He set the envelope down on Cade's side of the counter. "So no. I'm keeping my lease. I'm keeping my father's ovens. And I'm going to figure out the bank on my own terms, same as he would have."

For a moment, Cade Marsh simply looked at him. And there — underneath the tailoring and the timing and the terrible patience — Jonah saw something almost human flicker past: surprise, maybe, or envy, or the ghost of a boy who'd once watched a square-shouldered man laugh in a barrel room and wished he could stand like that.

"You're making a mistake," Cade said quietly.

"Probably," Jonah agreed. "But it's mine."

Cade straightened his cuffs, reclaimed his envelope, and left a twenty-dollar bill under the untouched coffee. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and glanced back at the photograph of Elias Ravensworth.

"He stood like that too, you know," Cade said. "Unbothered. Right up until the end." The bell above the door chimed his exit, soft and final, and the black sedan pulled away carrying the smell of money out of the café and back up the ridge road.

* * *

Jonah lasted four hours before he cracked.

Not about the money — he'd expected the money to tempt him, and it had, the way drowning men are tempted by air. What cracked him was the other thing, the thing he kept circling like a tongue finding a chipped tooth: *why had he refused?* He'd had no plan, no lawyer, no counteroffer. He'd had a feeling, and feelings didn't make payments to First Coastal Bank.

At seven o'clock, with the CLOSED sign turned and the ovens ticking as they cooled, he climbed the outside stairs to the apartment above the shop and knocked on Delia Marsh's door before he could talk himself out of it.

She opened it wearing a gray sweater, her hair down, a book facedown on the table behind her. Up close, in lamplight, she looked older than thirty-eight and younger than her file photo, whichever of those Jonah had seen first. Her eyes went from his face to the flour still dusting his forearms and back again.

"You look like a man who's been arguing with someone," she said.

"Cade Marsh came by today."

Something happened in her face then that Jonah would think about for a long time afterward. Not fear exactly. Recognition — the grim, weary kind, like weather arriving on schedule. She stepped back from the doorway. "Come in."

The apartment was spare and orderly: one chair, one lamp, the box of letters on the shelf where it always sat, spine out like a book she hadn't finished writing. She poured tea without asking, and they sat across from each other at the small table, steam rising between them like a border neither had crossed yet.

"He offered me a buyout," Jonah said. "Generous. Twice what I owe. Told me to take a check and a clean horizon."

Delia's hands went still around her cup. "And you said—"

"No."

She closed her eyes briefly, and when she opened them, they were flint. "You should have taken it."

Jonah blinked. Of all the responses he'd rehearsed on the stairs — gratitude, curiosity, the argument he'd already lost twice in his own head — he hadn't rehearsed that one. "Excuse me?"

"You should have taken the money, Jonah." She set her cup down with a click that sounded like a verdict. "Do you know what that man does to obstacles? Do you know what his family does? They don't knock twice. They don't take no for an answer and go home sad. They wait, and they get patient, and then they get thorough. You have a bank breathing down your neck and a health inspector who found 'irregularities' in wiring that was fine for forty years, and you turned down a lifeline because—" She stopped herself. Breathed. "Because why? Tell me why. Because I'd honestly like to know, since I can't find the reason myself."

Jonah looked at her — really looked — and understood that she wasn't angry at him. She was frightened for him, and fury was just the shape her fear took. It was, he realized, the most honest thing anyone had shown him in years.

"Because of you," he said.

The words landed in the room like a dropped glass. Delia went very still.

"When that fire started, you showed up with sandbags at midnight. You worked till dawn. You never asked me for anything, never explained yourself, never once made me feel like a charity case or a mark. And then this morning a man in a thousand-dollar suit walks in, smiles at my father's picture, says *terrible business* about the worst thing that ever happened to my family, and offers to rescue me." Jonah shook his head slowly. "I don't trust rescue that arrives wearing cologne. I trusted the woman with the sandbags. I don't know your whole story, Delia. I know pieces, and the pieces don't add up to anything good. But I'd rather bet on somebody who's carried her own weight in this town's rain than on the man whose family name is on every bad thing that ever happened to mine."

"That's not a reason," she whispered. "That's a feeling."

"It's the only kind I've got left."

They sat in silence. Down below, the harbor slapped against the pilings, patient as ever. Delia stared into her tea like it might show her something — the future, or just her own reflection, worn thin.

"If you stay," she said at last, quietly, "he won't stop at offers. You understand that. What happened to your father wasn't a rumor, Jonah. It was a machine. And machines like that don't care about your rye bread or your grandfather's counter or your stubbornness. They grind." She lifted her eyes to his. "I have spent ten years learning exactly how they grind. Take the money. Reopen somewhere else. Let me finish what I came here to do alone."

"No," Jonah said again, gentler this time. "You said it yourself — they don't stop. So there's no somewhere else. There's just here, and whatever's coming." He rose, drained his tea, and paused at the door with his hand on the knob. "Whatever you came here to do, Delia — you're not doing it alone anymore. That's not a feeling either. That's a fact."

He let himself out before she could answer, down the stairs into the dark of the closed café, where his father's ovens ticked as they cooled like a house settling, like a heart deciding.

Behind him, upstairs, a light burned late into the night. And in the morning, pinned to the café's front door where the whole harbor could see it, someone had left a note in handwriting Jonah had known for ten years without ever seeing it:

***The ledgers were only the beginning. Come to the ridge road Sunday. Bring nothing. Trust no one — especially not me.***

*D.*
