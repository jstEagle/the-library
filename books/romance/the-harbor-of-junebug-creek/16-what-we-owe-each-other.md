## 16. What We Owe Each Other

The smoke smell had worked itself into the walls of Jonah's life the way it had worked into the café's plaster — everywhere now, in everything, inescapable. He could taste it when he woke. He could taste it in the coffee he brewed on the single working burner of his apartment above the ruined shop, standing at the window at six in the morning and looking down at the blackened doorway where eleven years of his father's recipes and his own stubborn hope had gone up in a failure that wasn't electrical, whatever the insurance investigator's careful language wanted to imply.

*Pre-existing wiring deficiencies. Improperly permitted repairs. Coverage void.*

He'd read the letter four times before he understood it, and then he'd read it a fifth time hoping he'd misunderstood, and then he'd sat down on the kitchen floor with his back against the cabinet and laughed — one dry, cracked sound that didn't have any laughter in it at all.

Now, three days later, he stood in the wreckage with a crowbar he hadn't yet found the strength to swing.

The morning light came through the front windows in long dusty columns, catching the char on the ceiling beams, the blistered paint along the counter where Delia Marsh had once set down two cups of coffee and told him about sandbags and steady hands. The booths were salvageable. The espresso machine was not. His grandmother's recipe box — tin, dented, older than the building — sat on the pass-through shelf where he'd left it, untouched by fire or water or anything else, as if some small mercy had been assigned specifically to guard it.

Jonah picked it up. Held it. Put it back down.

"You're going to want gloves for that."

She was in the doorway. Of course she was. She'd been showing up like this all week — early, quiet, work boots laced to the ankle, hair tied back with whatever was nearest, carrying things. A thermos. A roll of contractor bags. Once, absurdly, a mop. She moved through his ruin like someone who had already decided it was hers to help carry, and he had let her, because there was nothing left in him to refuse.

"I'm not salvaging," he said. "I'm just standing here."

"I can see that. You're very good at it."

He almost smiled. It hurt too much to complete the motion, so it died somewhere around his mouth and became something else — an exhale, a surrender. He turned to face her fully.

Delia looked tired. Not the pretty, cinematic kind of tired; the real kind, the kind that lived under the eyes and in the set of the jaw. Ten years inside had taught her how to hold her face still, but he'd learned her tells by now: the way her thumb pressed into her own palm when she was blaming herself, the way she catalogued exits even in rooms she loved. She was doing both right now, standing in the burned doorway of his café, pressing her thumb hard against her hand.

"Say it," he said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on since the insurance letter came. You've been circling it for three days like a moth at a screen door. Say it, Delia."

Her chin came up. He watched her decide — watched the war move through her, quick and silent, the same war he'd seen her fight the night she'd told him to take Cade's money, the night she'd read him ten years of Tuesdays, the night she'd stood in this same doorway with flour on her forearms and looked at him like he was something she couldn't afford to want.

"He did this," she said quietly. "Cade. Or he paid someone to do it, which is the same thing with better posture. And he did it because of me. Because I wouldn't disappear. Because I brought the war to your door." Her voice held steady through all of it, which is how he knew what it cost her. "You should hate me, Jonah. Any reasonable man would hate me. Your father's name, your mother's recipes, your savings, your lease — I walked all of it into your life wearing an apology and called it penance. And you keep letting me in anyway, and I keep coming, because I'm selfish enough to keep coming, and I need you to know that I know exactly what I am."

The words landed in the smoky air between them and hung there.

Jonah set the crowbar down against the counter. Carefully, deliberately, the way his father used to set down tools — *a man who respects his tools respects his work* — though his hands were not entirely steady, and the gesture was less about respect than about giving himself something to do while his chest rearranged itself.

"Cade offered me a buyout," he said. "Six weeks ago. Before any of this. Do you remember what I said?"

"You said no."

"I said no. And you told me to say yes. You stood right there—" he pointed at the spot near the register, scorched now, "—and you told me to take the money, because you thought you were poison and you were trying to save me from you. And I said no again. So let's be clear about something, Delia, because apparently I have to be clear about everything in my own damn café."

He crossed the room. Three steps. Four. Close enough to see the pulse jump in her throat.

"I am not a reasonable man," he said. "I never have been. My father wasn't either — a reasonable man would have taken the plea deal, would have signed whatever they put in front of him, would have died quiet instead of dying honest. Instead he lost everything and went to his grave with his name dragged through the mud of this whole valley, and everyone in Junebug Creek believed the lie because believing it was easier than fighting the Marsh family. And I grew up on that. I grew up watching my mother count change at the register and pretend she wasn't counting it, watching her smile at customers who whispered. And every morning since I was nineteen years old, I have gotten up at four-thirty and baked bread in a building I was terrified of losing, because losing things is the only inheritance the Ravensworths ever got."

His voice cracked on the last of it. He let it.

"So don't stand there and tell me what I should feel. Don't do my grieving for me. I've spent my whole life protecting what little I had — the shop, the name, the routine, the four-thirty alarm, the regulars who don't know my middle name. I protected it so hard I built my entire life out of walls. And then you moved upstairs with a suitcase and a box of letters, and every wall I had started coming down brick by brick, and I hated it, and I fought it, and somewhere in the middle of it — somewhere between the sandbags and the ledgers spread across table six — I stopped wanting the walls back."

Delia hadn't moved. She was looking at him the way people look at something they're afraid will vanish if they blink.

"What are you saying?" she asked. Barely a whisper.

"I'm saying the café is gone." He gestured at the ruin around them — the char, the water damage blooming black across the ceiling, the smell that would live in these walls for years if anyone ever bothered to rebuild them. "My savings are gone. The lease is worthless. There is nothing left to protect, Delia. Nothing. For the first time since I was nineteen years old, I am completely, absolutely free — and it took a fire to do it, which is the most Ravensworth thing that has ever happened to me."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

"It's not." But her mouth trembled at the corner, betraying her, and he caught it, and something loosened in his chest that had been clenched for days.

"Here's what I'm actually saying," he said, and now he made himself slow down, made himself mean every word, because he had spent his whole life being a man who meant things and this was the most meaning he had ever packed into a single breath. "I choose you. Not instead of the café — there is no instead anymore. I choose you over safety. Over the easy version where I take whatever settlement I can scrape together and open a sandwich shop in Corvallis and never think about vineyards or ledgers or the Marsh family again. I could do that. It's sitting right there. It's warm and safe and it doesn't come with a woman who reads exit routes in every room."

"Don't—"

"I choose you *with* the exit routes. I choose you with the guilt and the ledgers and the dead accountant's daughter and the war you brought to my door. I choose the war. Do you understand? If we finish this, we finish it together — the evidence, the exposure, the festival, all of it, whatever it costs. And if you walk away, if you get on the 4:15 tomorrow and ride out of here to protect me from yourself, then fine. Then I'll grieve you too, and I'll add you to the list of things the Ravensworths lost. But I am done losing things without a fight, and I am done pretending that what I feel for you is friendship or gratitude or some complicated debt. It isn't any of those things."

He reached out and took her hand — the one with the thumb pressed white against the palm — and unfolded her fingers gently, one by one, until her hand lay open in his.

"I love you, Delia Marsh."

The words came out of him plain and unadorned, no poetry, no performance, because he had never learned to perform and wasn't going to start now, standing in the ashes of everything he owned.

"I love you. I have loved you since the night you showed up with sandbags and didn't ask permission. Maybe since the letters — God help me, maybe I loved the voice in those letters for ten years before I ever knew whose voice it was. And loving you is not a debt I'm paying and not a penance I'm serving and not a mistake I'm making. It's the first uncomplicated thing I have ever felt in my entire complicated life, and I will not apologize for it, and I will not let *you* apologize for it either."

* * *

For a long moment she didn't speak, and the silence had weight, had texture, had the quality of held breath.

Then Delia Marsh — who had survived ten years of concrete and barbed wire, who had faced down her brother across a general store counter, who had read forged ledgers aloud to herself in the dark until she had them memorized — began to cry.

Not gracefully. Not the way she did everything else, controlled and rationed. This was ugly and sudden, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on her face, her shoulders shaking, her free hand pressed flat against her sternum as if holding something in place that had come loose.

"I practiced leaving," she said, wrecked. "On the train. Last night. I had the bag packed. I had the timetable memorized. I stood at the platform gate for forty minutes and I couldn't make my feet do it, and I hated myself for that too, because staying was the selfish thing, staying meant the fire was for nothing—"

"The fire was not for nothing."

"—and then you say *I love you*, just like that, standing in your own ruins, and I don't—" She shook her head. "Nobody has ever chosen me, Jonah. Not once. My father chose the estate. Cade chose the name. The state chose ten years of my life. And you're standing there choosing me like it's easy, like it's obvious, like I'm not the reason your whole world is charcoal—"

"You're not the reason." He stepped closer, both her hands in his now. "Cade is the reason. Greed is the reason. A lie my father didn't tell is the reason. You're the only person in this whole sorry story who's been trying to fix it since before I even knew there was something broken. You wrote letters for ten years to a stranger because you couldn't live with what your family did. That's not poison, Delia. That's the opposite of poison. That's the bravest thing anyone's ever done in my direction."

She laughed through the tears — a real one this time, wet and disbelieving. "You have terrible taste in women."

"I have excellent taste in women. Ask anyone. Ask literally no one, because I haven't dated anyone in six years, but I'm confident."

And then she kissed him.

It was not gentle. Eleven years of restraint and ten years of letters and one burned-down café all arrived at once, in the middle of the wreckage, flour tins toppled and water-stained receipts drifting off the counter like dirty snow. Her hands came up to his face and his went around her back and the crowbar clattered somewhere behind them and neither of them cared. When they finally broke apart, breathless, her forehead resting against his, she was still crying a little, and he was too, though he'd deny it later to anyone but her.

"Together, then," she said. "We finish it together."

"Together or not at all."

"Not at all isn't on the menu anymore."

"No," he agreed. "It's off the menu. Permanently. Like the lavender scones."

"God, the lavender scones. I still think about those scones."

"I'll make them again. When we reopen. Which we will." He said it like a fact, and discovered, saying it, that he believed it — that somewhere beneath the ash and the voided policy and the arithmetic of ruin, a plan had already been forming without his permission. "But first—"

He crossed to the pass-through shelf and picked up his grandmother's recipe box, and then, from beneath the register, the dented metal cash box where he'd kept — because he was his father's son, because some part of him had always known this day might come — the copies. Every page Delia had given him. Every ledger line, every photocopied signature, every note in a dead accountant's cramped hand. He'd hidden them in the least likely place in Junebug Creek: inside a cash box in a cash-poor café, beneath a false bottom that had held his grandfather's poker winnings for forty years.

He set the box on the counter between them.

"They didn't find these," he said. "Fire marshal went through everything. Never touched it. Either Cade's man was sloppy, or he was looking for financial records and didn't think to check the register of a failing business." He flipped the lid. The papers sat there, crisp and intact, smelling faintly of metal and old ink. "Which means we still have everything. The daughter's page is missing, but everything else — the pattern, the dates, the second set of books. Enough to build the case. Enough to bury him at that festival."

Delia looked at the documents, then at him, and he watched the grief in her face transmute — visibly, alchemically — into something harder and brighter. Purpose. The thing he'd first glimpsed in her on the ridge road, the thing that had carried her through a decade of concrete.

"The accountant's daughter," she said slowly. "Ruth Calloway. Portland. I have an address. I was going to go alone."

"You were going to go alone," he repeated flatly.

"I was going to go alone," she admitted.

"Well, that's over. We go together. Tomorrow. First train out." He closed the cash box. "But tonight—" he looked around at the ruined café, at the booth by the window where they'd spread their first photocopies, at the pass-through where the recipe box kept its vigil, "—tonight we eat dinner on the floor of my café like a couple of arson survivors, and I make you the last decent meal this kitchen will produce for a while, and then we pack."

Delia wiped her face with the heel of her hand and smiled — the full, unguarded smile he'd only seen twice, the one that remade her whole face.

"Jonah Ravensworth," she said, "you are going to be a terrible fugitive from your own life."

"I've been practicing for years. I'm very attached to it."

They gathered wood scraps from the salvage pile and built a small fire in the old brick oven — the one thing in the building the flames had spared, as if it had already survived its burning decades ago and considered itself exempt. He found eggs and butter and the last bag of good flour in the apartment upstairs, and he cooked for her the way his father had taught him, simply and seriously, and they ate off paper plates at table six with the evening light coming gold through the broken-back windows.

At some point she reached across the table and took his hand, and neither of them spoke, and nothing needed saying.

Later — much later, when the dishes were done and the oven banked and the town lights coming on down along the creek — Delia stood at the window looking north toward the dark bulk of the ridge road and the vineyard beyond it, and her expression had gone quiet and far away.

"She'll say yes," Jonah said, coming up behind her. "The accountant's daughter. Ruth. Whoever she is. People keep secrets like that because they're waiting for someone to ask."

Delia leaned back against him. "How do you know?"

"Because my father waited twenty years for someone to ask too." He turned her gently by the shoulder, kissed her forehead, and nodded at the cash box sitting on the counter, patient as a loaded gun. "Tomorrow we ask."

Outside, far off toward the depot, the 4:15 came through — the last train of the day, the one she'd once arrived on, the one she'd almost left on. They listened to it pass, together, and neither of them moved toward the sound.

Neither of them ever would again.
