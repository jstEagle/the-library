## 18. The Reading of the Names

The morning of the harvest festival, Delia Marsh woke before dawn and lay in the dark above Ravensworth Café, listening to the building breathe.

She had not slept. She had tried — she had lain down at eleven with her clothes folded over a chair like a woman who intended to be reasonable about the next day, and she had stared at the ceiling until the first gray light came through the window and turned the water stain in the corner into a small gray continent. Somewhere below her, around four o'clock, Jonah had come up from the kitchen and stood for a long moment on the stairs, and she had known he was deciding whether to knock on her door. He hadn't. In the morning there was a thermos of coffee outside it, still warm, and a note in his blunt carpenter's handwriting: *Whatever happens today, I'm proud of you.* She had read it four times and put it in her pocket, where it pressed against her hip all day like a second heartbeat.

By seven the harbor was transformed. The Junebug Creek Harvest Festival had swallowed the whole waterfront — white tents along the boardwalk, strings of bulb lights sagging between the lampposts, a stage built out over the water on rented scaffolding so that whoever spoke upon it would have the creek itself glittering behind them like a backdrop painted by someone with more money than taste. Banners in Marsh burgundy and gold hung from every available surface: *ONE HUNDRED YEARS OF THE MARSH LEGACY*. There were cider presses and pie contests and a fiddle band tuning up near the fish market, and children ran everywhere with paper cups of free grape juice, sticky-handed and shrieking, and the whole town had turned out in its good clothes to be flattered by the family that owned half of it.

Delia watched it all from the ridge road first, alone, the way she had once watched everything that mattered about this town. From up here the festival looked small and bright and fragile, a birthday cake set out on a table that could be flipped with one hand. Below it, the vineyard spread down the hillside in its autumn rows, the grapes heavy and dark, and the great house sat at the top of it all with its windows catching the sun — the house where she had been born, where she had found the ledgers at seventeen, where her father had died still insisting to the end that he had done nothing that any sensible man wouldn't have done.

*Any sensible man,* she thought. That was the phrase. That was the whole disease, distilled. Her father had never believed he'd committed a crime; he'd believed he'd performed maintenance. A leak in the family's hull, and Jonah's father was the patch he'd nailed over it.

She got in the car and drove down into town, and the folder rode on the seat beside her like a passenger.

* * *

Jonah met her behind the café at nine, in the alley that smelled of yeast and creek water, and for a moment neither of them spoke. He looked terrible and beautiful — he hadn't slept either, she could see it in the bruised skin under his eyes, and he'd shaved carelessly and missed a patch at his jaw, and he wore the one good shirt he owned, the blue one, buttoned wrong at the collar so that she reached up without thinking and fixed it, her fingers brushing the warm skin of his throat.

"You don't have to do this," he said. It was the fourth time he'd said it. "You understand that, right? You've done your ten years. You paid. Nobody would say you didn't pay."

"That's exactly why I have to." She smoothed the collar flat. "Paying isn't the same as telling the truth. I paid for being quiet. Today I stop being quiet."

He took her hands then, both of them, and held them against his chest, and through the blue cotton she could feel his heart going fast and hard, faster than hers, which surprised her — she had assumed hers would be the runaway one today. "My father," he said quietly, "used to say that the truth is like bread. It doesn't keep. You have to serve it while it's real."

"Your father," Delia said, "sounds like he would have liked me."

"He'd have loved you," Jonah said. "That's what scares me."

They had agreed on the plan weeks ago, and then re-agreed it nightly, and then abandoned and rebuilt it like a sandcastle against the tide, because there was no plan that survived contact with Cade Marsh. The centerpiece ceremony was at noon. Every year of her childhood it had been the same: the patriarch or his heir standing on the stage over the water, reading aloud the names of the vineyard's founders, honoring the families whose labor had built the Marsh empire — the pickers, the cellar men, the accountants, the field hands, generation after generation, their names recited like scripture while the crowd bowed its head. It was the Marsh family's favorite ritual, the one they used to launder a century of other people's sweat into something called *legacy*.

Cade had made it the crown jewel of the festival. There would be press there. Cameras. A reporter from the county paper, two from the city, a television crew from Channel 6. Cade wanted witnesses. Well, Delia thought, adjusting the strap of her bag one last time. He was about to get them.

"I'll be in the third row," Jonah said. "Left side, near the aisle. If anything goes wrong—"

"Nothing's going to go wrong."

"—you walk toward me, and we go out past the fish market, and my truck's right there."

"Jonah." She waited until he looked at her. "I spent ten years learning how to walk into rooms where everyone hates me. This is the first time I'll ever walk into one knowing I'm right."

Something moved across his face — grief and pride tangled together, inseparable, the way they had been ever since the night he'd read all her letters. Then he kissed her, briefly, hard, like a man sealing a letter, and went to take his place among the townspeople, and Delia stood alone in the alley for another minute, breathing, letting the fear move through her and out, the way she'd learned to do in the visiting room at Alderson when the guard called her name.

Then she walked toward the stage.

* * *

The ceremony began at noon exactly, because Cade Marsh was a man who believed punctuality was a form of power.

Delia stood at the back of the crowd, beneath the shade of the fish market awning, and watched him work. And he was working — there was no other word for it. He stood at the podium in a suit the color of good whiskey, sunlit, handsome, his voice pitched to carry across the water, and he told the story of the Marsh legacy the way it had always been told: the immigrant grandfather with cuttings sewn into his coat lining, the lean years, the triumph, the generosity. Behind him, on a screen, photographs changed in slow dissolve — the great house in 1920, the first crush, her father shaking hands with farmworkers, young Cade himself in a christening gown. The crowd murmured at each image like a congregation at a familiar hymn.

"And so," Cade said, "as my grandfather did, and my father after him, we honor the names. The names are the legacy. Not the buildings — buildings fall. Not even the vines — vines can be replanted. But a name, given honestly, endures." He lifted a leather-bound book, old and cracked, the founders' register. "We begin, as always, with the families of the founding crews."

He began to read. Okafor. Lindqvist. Reyes. The old names, the picker families, some of whom still worked the rows, and Delia watched an elderly woman in the front row weep openly at the sound of her mother's name spoken by a Marsh, and felt the full obscene genius of the ritual settle over her: this was how the family had kept the town loyal for a hundred years. They didn't buy silence. They gave people their ancestors back, once a year, beautifully wrapped, and charged nothing but obedience.

Cade read on. And then, without changing his tone at all — without a hitch in his voice, though his eyes lifted from the page and swept the crowd once, quick and cold, a searchlight looking for one face — he said:

"We also honor those who served this community in other capacities. The accountants who kept our books honest. The partners who shared our risks." A pause, measured to the millimeter. "And the Ravensworth family, whose contributions to this valley were... significant."

A ripple went through the crowd. Delia saw heads turn, saw people glance toward the third row on the left, where Jonah stood very still in his blue shirt with his jaw set. *Significant.* One word doing the work of a eulogy and an insult at once. Cade was daring them. He was standing on a stage built over the creek where Edmund Ravensworth had drowned himself — or so the town believed — reading his name off a list of honored dead, with the forged ledger that destroyed him locked in a safe in the great house, and smiling while he did it.

Delia had planned to wait for the closing prayer. She had planned to walk up during the applause, politely, apologetically, and ask for a moment, and let Cade decide whether to cause a scene — betting, correctly, that he wouldn't dare, not with cameras running.

But hearing her father's crime dressed in the language of honor, hearing *significant* fall from that beautiful lying mouth — something in her chest closed like a fist, and the plan dissolved, and what remained was only the truth and her voice to carry it.

She walked forward through the crowd.

People stepped aside without quite knowing why — the crowd parted the way water parts around a keel. She heard her own name moving through them ahead of her, whispered, passed hand to hand like contraband: *Marsh. The Marsh girl. That's the one who was inside. The daughter.* By the time she reached the steps of the stage, three hundred people had gone silent, and the fiddle band had stopped mid-tune, and Cade was looking down at her with an expression of such perfect, glacial courtesy that only she could see the fury underneath it, banked like coals.

"Miss Marsh," he said pleasantly. "We're honored. Family always sits front row." He gestured, expansive, welcoming, the host to the end. "Come up. Let everyone see you."

It was a trap, of course — bring her up, embrace her, fold her back into the family portrait where she'd be one more repentant Marsh, safely absorbed. So she smiled at him, and climbed the steps, and let him extend his hand, and did not take it.

"No," she said clearly, and the microphone caught it, and carried it out over the water. "Thank you, Cade. But I'm not here as family. I'm here as a witness."

She turned to the crowd. Three hundred faces. The television camera's red light burned steadily at the edge of the stage. Somewhere in the third row, left side, Jonah Ravensworth stood with his hands at his sides, and did not look away from her, and that was enough. That was the ground she stood on.

"My name is Delia Marsh," she said. "Eleven years ago, my father, Aldous Marsh, framed a man named Edmund Ravensworth for embezzlement. He forged Edmund's signature on falsified ledgers and moved the Marsh family's losses onto his books, and when the auditors came, Edmund Ravensworth took the blame for debts he never incurred, lost everything he had, and six months later he walked into Junebug Creek and did not walk out. My father knew. My father let the town believe Edmund was a thief for the rest of his life, and my family has profited from that lie every single day since — including today, including this festival, including the words you just heard spoken over his name."

The crowd made a sound — not a gasp, something lower, a collective intake like wind through a cornfield. On the far side of the stage, Cade had gone very still, and his voice, when it came, was soft and lethal: "Delia. Whatever you think you know—"

"I know exactly what I know." She opened her folder. Her hands were steady. Ten years of steadiness, finally spending itself all at once. "Because I found the original ledgers when I was seventeen, in my father's study, and I have spent eleven years gathering the rest. These are certified copies of the Marsh estate accounts for the fiscal year Edmund Ravensworth died. This page—" she held it up, high, turning slowly so the cameras could find it, the photocopied ledger sheet with its columns of figures and the signature block at the bottom, "—bears the signature of Edmund Ravensworth, dated March the fourth. I hold the bank records proving Edmund Ravensworth was in a hospital in Ashford on March the fourth, recovering from surgery, unable to hold a pen. His doctor's affidavit is here. The deposition of Ruth Calloway, the accountant's daughter, whose father kept the true originals rather than the copies my father circulated, is here. Her father hid them because he couldn't live with what he'd helped do, and he died carrying them, and his daughter gave them to me because the truth deserved a better grave than a shoebox."

She laid the pages on the podium, one by one, deliberately, like dealing cards, like setting a table. "I am going to read the entry in my father's own hand. Dated February the nineteenth. It says: *'Transfer Q4 losses to R. accounts per plan. E.R. will sign — he trusts us. No one will look twice at a bookkeeper.'*" Her voice did not break. She had expected it to break; it didn't; it came out level and clear and terribly calm, the voice of a woman reading a weather report for the end of the world. "*No one will look twice at a bookkeeper.* That was my father's opinion of your neighbor. Of the man who fixed your roofs and coached your sons and baked bread for this town for thirty years. A bookkeeper. Someone no one would look at twice."

She looked up, out at the faces — Mrs. Okafor weeping now too, the old woman beside her gripping her arm; the hardware store owner gone gray; the schoolteacher with her hand over her mouth; the reporter from the county paper already writing, writing, writing. "For ten years I wrote letters to Edmund's son confessing this, because I didn't have the courage to say it out loud, and because I owed a debt I couldn't pay. Today I'm saying it out loud. My father, Aldous Marsh, committed fraud and manslaughter by degrees, and the Marsh fortune was built on the ruin of a good man, and I am done carrying it for them." She turned then, at last, and faced Cade directly, and the microphone carried even the softness of her voice. "You knew. You found the missing pages in the estate office and you burned the originals in your father's fireplace two nights ago. But it's too late, Cade. Truth doesn't burn. It just waits for someone willing to carry it."

The silence afterward lasted three seconds, maybe four — long enough for the creek to be heard lapping under the stage, long enough for a gull to cry somewhere out over the water. Then the crowd broke like a dam. Voices rose from every direction — outrage, disbelief, demands, questions, a woman shouting Edmund's name like a prayer, someone else calling for the sheriff — and Cade grabbed for the microphone, shouting for order, for security, for everyone to remain calm, his beautiful composure cracking down the middle like river ice in spring, and above the roar of three hundred people discovering in public that their history was a forgery, Delia Marsh stepped back from the podium.

She walked down the steps into the crowd, and the crowd parted again, and this time it parted the way a sea parts for something it cannot decide whether to worship or destroy. Hands reached toward her — some to strike, some to grasp, some merely to touch her sleeve as if to confirm she was real. She kept walking. Toward the third row, left side, near the aisle.

Toward Jonah.

He met her at the rope line, and took her arm, and the last thing she heard before the noise of the crowd swallowed everything else was Cade Marsh's voice behind her, raw and stripped of all its tailoring, shouting her name across the ruined ceremony — and beneath it, faintly, unmistakably, the sound of the county paper's reporter dictating into her phone: *"Get me the sheriff. Get me the sheriff now."*
