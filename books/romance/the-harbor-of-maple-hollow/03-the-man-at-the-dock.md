## 3. The Man at the Dock

The bus let him off at the edge of town, where the road narrowed and the salt smell started, and Callum Reyes stood there for a long moment with a cardboard box under his arm like a man holding something that might bite him.

Ten years. That was the arithmetic. Ten years since he'd driven out of Maple Hollow at nineteen with everything he owned in the bed of a truck he'd sold two states later because he needed the money more than the memory. He'd told himself he'd never come back. He'd built a whole life on that promise—warehouse work in Providence, then a foreman's badge, then an apartment with a view of nothing but another building. He'd gotten good at not looking back. It was a skill, like anything else. You practiced.

Then his father died, and the skill failed him all at once.

They'd cremated Marco Reyes in the city where he'd spent his last decade, alone except for a hospice nurse who called Callum twice a week to report on a man who mostly stared out the window. *He asks about the harbor,* she'd said once. *He asks if the water's still blue.* Callum had lied and said yes. The water had been blue when they left. He preferred to remember it that way, unspoiled, the way you prefer to remember anything before it's taken from you.

The box under his arm was his father's. It had arrived by mail three days after the funeral Callum hadn't been able to afford to fly home for—not that there was a home to fly to—and inside were letters. Dozens of them, rubber-banded into bundles by year, going back further than Callum had been alive. Letters between his grandfather and someone in Maple Hollow whose name appeared only as initials on the envelopes: E.K.

Elena Kavanagh. Jude's grandmother.

Callum hadn't read them yet. He'd carried them from Providence to Boston to Portland and finally onto this bus, telling himself he'd burn them somewhere along the route, and every time he got as far as a trash barrel outside a rest stop, his hands wouldn't open. His father had kept these letters through poverty and illness and the slow erosion of a man's pride, kept them when he'd thrown out everything else, including photographs of his own wedding. Whatever was in them, Marco Reyes had thought it mattered. And whatever mattered to Marco Reyes usually had a grudge attached.

So here he was. Back in the town that had eaten his family alive, carrying the evidence in a box with a coffee stain on one corner, telling himself he'd stay one night. One night at the Anchor Inn, which was now the Saltgrass Motel, which meant even the inn had changed its name to survive. Then he'd read the letters or burn them, collect whatever his father had left in the ground here—a plot in the cemetery on the hill, bought back in better years—and leave before anyone could look at him too long.

That was the plan. Plans were also a skill. He'd practiced those too.

* * *

Maple Hollow had repainted itself the way small towns do—new shutters on the old houses, a farm-to-table sign where the bait shop used to be—but underneath it was the same skeleton. He knew the streets by their smells: diesel and brine near the working dock, cut grass and apples up the hill, woodsmoke everywhere come evening. The Whitmore house still sat at the top of Harbor Road like a crown on the town's head, white columns gleaming, and Callum felt his jaw tighten at the sight of it, an involuntary reflex, the body remembering what the mind had tried to file away.

His father's boat was gone, of course. Sold at auction the same month the lawsuit gutted them—the *Marisol*, no, that wasn't right, the *Esperanza*, named for his mother, hauled out of the water while Callum stood on the dock and watched strangers inventory her like she was a seized shipment. He'd been seventeen. His mother had been four years gone by then, so it was just him and his father in the house on Larkspur Lane, and then it wasn't even that, because the bank took the house too, and the founding families of Maple Hollow stood around at the church afterward saying what a shame it was, what a terrible shame, in voices pitched exactly loud enough to be heard across the pews.

Whitmore Marine Group v. Reyes Fishing Co. That was the official name of the thing that ended them. A contract dispute over mooring rights, the lawyers called it. Callum's father called it theft with paperwork. The town called it nothing at all, because the town had decided long ago which family mattered and which one was ballast.

Callum walked down toward the harbor without deciding to. His feet remembered the way. That was the cruelest part of coming home—you didn't choose your routes; they chose you.

The dock was half-empty in the late afternoon light, a few lobster boats knocking gently against their slips, gulls arguing over something near the fish house. And there, at the far end, past the fuel pump, a woman crouched over a plastic crate with a clipboard balanced on her knee, writing fast, hair tied back, sleeves pushed up, sunburn starting along the backs of her arms in the particular way that happened to people who forgot sunscreen existed because data mattered more than skin.

He knew her before he saw her face. He knew her by the way she held the pen—gripped too tight, always, like the words might escape otherwise. Jude Kavanagh. Writing things down since she was eight years old, keeping notebooks about tide pools and bird counts and, once, in a notebook he probably should never have read, a list titled *Reasons Callum Reyes Is Annoying*, which ran to eleven entries and which he had memorized anyway, because being annoying to Jude Kavanagh had been the great project of his adolescence.

She looked different. Sharper somehow, or maybe just worn to a finer edge. There was a ring-shaped tan line on her left hand and no ring on it, and he hated himself for noticing, and noticed anyway.

His first instinct was to turn around. Clean, animal instinct, the kind you don't argue with—walk back up Harbor Road, take the next bus, let the letters rot in a landfill somewhere, keep the last ten years intact. She was the one person in Maple Hollow he'd never figured out how to hate properly, and that made her more dangerous than all the Whitmores combined. You could guard against enemies. Jude had gotten in under the fence when they were both children and never left, and he'd spent a decade trying to evict her from the parts of himself where names live.

His second instinct was worse. It was to walk down the dock and say her name and watch her face do whatever it was going to do.

He stood at the head of the dock with the box against his ribs and let the two instincts fight it out. The gulls screamed. A boat engine coughed to life somewhere behind the fish house. The water did its work on him, the way it always had—that slow blue pull, the smell of it reaching into some sealed room inside him and opening the windows. His father had asked if the water was still blue. Standing here, Callum understood that the question had never really been about the water.

Jude closed her clipboard, stood, stretched, and turned toward shore.

She saw him from maybe forty feet. He watched it happen—the recognition arriving in stages, disbelief first, then something that might have been relief, then a hard shutter coming down over all of it so fast he almost missed what was underneath. She went very still, the way she used to go still when a teacher asked a question she knew the answer to but didn't want to give.

"Callum." Not a question. Just his name, set down carefully between them like something breakable.

"Kavanagh." He made it flat. He'd rehearsed flatness on the bus, in motel bathrooms, in the long gray hours between cities. *You're not there to see her. You're there for your father. Keep it business.* "I heard you were back in town."

"Seasonal survey." She lifted the clipboard slightly, as if to prove it. "Turtle counts. I'm here until September."

September. Ninety-some days. The number snagged on something Marisol Vega had written in the letter that had chased him through three forwarding addresses—the letter about his father's estate, about property, about a deadline. He'd skimmed it on the bus and hated every word of it, because it meant lawyers, and lawyers meant the machinery that had destroyed his family grinding back to life with him inside it.

"You look good," she said, and then visibly wished she hadn't, color rising in her face.

"Do I." He heard how it came out—like a blade laid on a table—and watched her flinch. Good. Let her flinch. Except it didn't feel good; it felt like pressing on a bruise to prove it still hurt, which any fool knew only made it worse. "Sorry. Long trip."

"How long are you staying?"

The honest answer was *I don't know*, and under that, *I never stopped wanting to come back, and I've hated myself for ten years because of it*, and under that, deeper than either, the thing he would have said at nineteen if he'd had the words: *You were supposed to come find me. You were supposed to pick up the phone. I waited at that bus station in Rockland for two extra hours because I thought you'd come.*

"I'm settling my father's affairs," he said instead. "Then I'm gone."

Something moved through her face at that—quick, unreadable, gone. "Right," she said. "Of course."

A silence opened up between them, wide as the harbor. Ten years of it, actually, and all the smaller silences inside it: the night of the graduation bonfire when she'd turned away from him to talk to Preston Whitmore's circle and he'd stood there holding two cups of bad punch; the morning his father signed the papers surrendering the boat; the last afternoon, packing the truck, when Jude had walked past the end of his street without turning in, and he'd watched her go and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had chosen. Chosen the town. Chosen her family's place in it. Chosen safety over him, and never said a word about it, and never explained, and the not-explaining was the wound that never scabbed.

He'd built his whole resentment on that afternoon. It was load-bearing. If he took it out, he wasn't sure what was holding the walls up.

"I heard about your grandmother," he said, because the silence was becoming unbearable and cruelty was easier than kindness. "I'm sorry."

Her jaw tightened. "When did you hear?"

"This morning. The woman at the motel."

"Small towns," Jude said, and there was something bitter in it that surprised him. He'd expected her to defend Maple Hollow. She'd always defended Maple Hollow, the way you defend a parent everyone else criticizes—fiercely, and with private doubts you never say aloud.

"They bury people fast here," he said. "News, I mean. And other things."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means some things get buried on purpose." He shifted the box higher against his ribs. Her eyes dropped to it, lingered. "Family papers. My father's. I'm taking them back with me."

"To Providence?"

"That's the idea."

She nodded slowly, looking past him at the water, and for a second her composure slipped and he saw the girl underneath—the one who used to sit on this same dock with her sneakers off, feet in the harbor, reading marine biology textbooks for fun while he skipped stones and pretended not to be studying her profile. "Callum," she said quietly. "Why did you really come back?"

And there it was. The question with teeth. Because the true answer involved a dead man's letters and a grudge he'd nursed like a pilot light, and possibly—though he couldn't yet prove it, though he only suspected it from the shape of the envelopes and the dates and the way his grandfather's handwriting deteriorated across the final bundle—something that the Kavanagh family and the Whitmore family had done together in the dark decades ago, something Elena Kavanagh's initials were all over.

"The estate," he said. "Like I told you."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you get."

He regretted it immediately. Not because she deserved gentleness—he wasn't sure anymore what she deserved—but because he could see the hurt land, could see her decide to match his coldness degree for degree, and some stupid buried part of him howled at the waste of it. Ten years, and their first real conversation was a fencing match on a dock that smelled of diesel and low tide.

"Fine," she said. She picked up her crate of equipment. "Welcome back to Maple Hollow, Callum. I hope you find what you're looking for."

"I already know what I'm looking for."

"No," she said, shouldering past him up the dock, close enough that he caught salt and shampoo and something underneath that was purely her, unchanged, infuriating. "You know what you're looking *for*. You don't know what you'll find."

She walked up the ramp without looking back. He watched her go the way he'd watched her go ten years ago, and hated the symmetry of it.

* * *

The Saltgrass Motel room smelled of pine cleaner and old cigarettes, and Callum sat on the bed with the box open in front of him and the bundles of letters fanned out like a losing hand of cards.

He'd meant to wait. He'd meant to sleep, drive to the cemetery in the morning, stand at his father's plot for the allotted respectful interval, and read the letters on the bus home where they couldn't cost him anything.

But the dock had wrecked his discipline. Seeing Jude had cracked something loose, and the letters seemed to know it, seemed to lean toward him out of the box.

He untied the oldest bundle. The paper was soft as cloth, typed on a machine with a failing ribbon, the ink gray in places. His grandfather's name in the salutation—*Dear Tomás*—and the return address was the orchard on the hill. Orchard Hill Road. The Kavanagh orchard, the one that still stood above town, half-wild now according to the motel woman's gossip, though the trees had been legendary once.

*Dear Tomás. They came again about the north parcel. Eleanor's father says the harbor commission will rule against us regardless, so we should consider what is offered rather than lose everything. I told him what I always tell him. But Tomás, I am tired, and Ruth is frightened, and I cannot fight the whole town. Tell me what you want to do.*

Callum read it three times. The date was 1974. His grandfather would have been thirty-one. Thirty-one years old, fighting the harbor commission, fighting the Whitmores, and writing to Elena Kavanagh about it like she was the only person in Maple Hollow he trusted.

There were forty letters in the bundle. He read until the light went out of the window and he had to turn on the lamp, and the story assembled itself in fragments, in half-sentences, in the careful coded language of people who suspected their mail was being watched: a parcel of waterfront land the Reyes family had held since before the town had a name. A transfer. A signature. An amount of money that made Callum laugh out loud, alone in the motel room, a short ugly sound—because it was a fraction of what the land had been worth even then, and the land was worth a hundred times that now, and the marina plans in the current town newsletter sat on exactly that footprint.

And in the middle of it, dated the winter after the transfer, a single line in Elena Kavanagh's own hand, the handwriting looser, hurried:

*Tomás—I signed what I was made to sign. I have not slept since. If you can ever forgive me, forgive me, and if you cannot, then hate me, but do not hate yourself. What was yours was taken, and I helped them take it, and I will spend what's left of my life trying to make it right.*

Callum set the letter down. His hands weren't steady.

*Made to sign.* Signed what? By whom? And what did *trying to make it right* mean—meanwhile, while his father worked himself into an early grave in a city that never learned his name?

He thought of Jude on the dock, saying *you don't know what you'll find.* He almost laughed again. She had no idea. Or—cold thought, arriving unwelcome—maybe she did. Maybe the Kavanaghs had known all along what their orchard was sitting on top of, whose labor and whose loss had paid for its foundations.

Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, strung along the dark water like a town pretending to be innocent. Callum gathered the letters back into their bundles, retied them, and put the box on the nightstand where he could see it from the bed.

One night, he'd told himself. Settle the affairs, then gone.

But in the morning there was a lawyer to call—Marisol Vega, per the letter—and an estate to sort out, and apparently, buried somewhere in the paperwork his father had never mentioned and the lawyer's letter had only hinted at, a piece of property with his name on it. His father's name first. His father's name, on land in Maple Hollow, ten years after the banks had supposedly stripped the Reyeses down to nothing.

Callum lay in the dark listening to the gulls and the water, and the resentment in his chest, the load-bearing one, shifted slightly—just slightly—like a wall realizing its foundation had moved.

He'd come for answers. He was starting to understand he might have come for something else entirely, something with a clipboard and a sharp tongue and sunburned shoulders, and that terrified him more than any Whitmore ever had.

Tomorrow, he'd call the lawyer. Tomorrow, he'd find out why his father had kept paying taxes on a piece of Maple Hollow for fifty years.

Tonight, he allowed himself one memory: Jude at sixteen, feet in the harbor, laughing at something he'd said, the water blue, the future unbuilt. Then he shut it away, locked it, and slept badly, and dreamed of signatures.
