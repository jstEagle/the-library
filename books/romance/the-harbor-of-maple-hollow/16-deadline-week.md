## 16. Deadline Week

**JUDE**

Six days.

That was what Marisol said when she called at seven in the morning, her voice stripped of its usual warmth, and Jude stood in the orchard in her pajamas with dew soaking through her socks, listening to the number repeat itself in her head like a sonar ping. Six days to file the challenge or the deed stood forever, and the Whitmore trust took the orchard, and everything they'd dug out of the root cellar became a story two people told themselves in the dark.

"Are you okay?" Marisol asked. "You're not saying anything."

"I'm counting." Apples, trees, days. "I'm here."

"Don't come to the office today."

Jude's stomach went cold in the way it did before bad survey data came back. "Why?"

"Because yesterday afternoon a man from the county counsel's office visited my firm. Very polite. Very concerned about my 'conflict of interest,' given that my late husband's family sold timber to the Reyes farm in 1968." A pause, dry as August. "My husband has been dead eleven years, Jude. They had to dig back half a century to find a thread to pull. Which tells me they're scared, and scared people with money don't pull threads gently."

"What do you need?"

"Time. Copies of everything are already in three places, so don't panic about that. But I want you and Callum to lie low until the filing. No courthouse visits, no historical society, no heroic gestures. Can you do that? Can both of you do that?"

Jude looked across the rows toward the root cellar's door, where Callum was already up, already working, his silhouette moving behind the fogged glass of the storm windows they'd installed last month. He'd been sleeping in the farmhouse for two weeks now—officially because the cottage roof leaked, unofficially because neither of them had suggested he leave.

"I can only speak for one of us," she said.

"That's what I was afraid of."

* * *

**CALLUM**

Callum found the first sign of trouble without knowing what he was looking at.

He'd gone into town for irrigation fittings, and while he waited at the hardware counter he watched Dale Pruitt—the same Dale Pruitt who'd shown up with a chainsaw after the storm, who'd clapped him on the shoulder like they were boys again—talking to a man Callum didn't recognize by the register. The man wore a county windbreaker. Dale was nodding. And then Dale saw Callum watching, and something crossed his face that Callum had spent ten years learning to read in strangers: the flicker of a man deciding which side of a line he stood on.

Dale bought his parts and left without saying goodbye.

At the counter, the man in the windbreaker smiled at Callum like they were neighbors. "Reyes, right? Heard you've been fixing the old place up. Town appreciates that."

"Does it," Callum said.

The smile held a beat too long. "History matters to some folks around here. The right history." He paid for his coffee and left, and Callum stood there with his fittings in a paper bag, understanding that he had just been warned in the friendliest possible language.

By the time he got back to the orchard, Marisol had called twice, and Jude met him at the truck with her phone in her hand and her face pale under the sunburn.

"The courthouse records room," she said. "The 1974 transfer file. Marisol sent a paralegal this morning to make certified copies before the filing."

"And?"

"It's gone. The whole folder. The clerk says it was checked out three weeks ago by someone from the trust's title company and never returned. There's no record of a checkout." Jude's voice stayed level the way it did when she was reading data she hated. "Three weeks ago, Callum. That was before we even knew what we were looking at. Someone's been ahead of us since the beginning."

Callum set the paper bag down on the tailgate very carefully, the way his father used to set down tools when the alternative was throwing them. "Then we use the copies. The archivist's photographs. Tom Eldridge's testimony."

"Marisol says the originals matter. Certified copies from the courthouse would have anchored everything. Now it's our word and an archivist's analysis against a missing file and a title company that will swear it never existed." She exhaled. "She also said—and I'm quoting—that if anyone offers us a friendly cup of coffee this week, we should count the spoons."

* * *

**JUDE**

They spent the day doing the unglamorous work of building a case out of what remained: scanning photographs, cross-referencing dates, drafting declarations. Jude worked at the kitchen table with her laptop and three legal pads; Callum sat across from her with the letters spread out in their plastic sleeves, and somewhere around hour four she realized they'd stopped being two people forced together by a deed and become something like a team, and she didn't know when that had happened, and she was afraid to examine it too closely with five days left on the clock.

At dusk, Ruth Ann Corliss appeared at the end of the driveway in her Buick, which was how bad news traveled in Maple Hollow—faster than fire, wearing perfume.

Jude walked down to meet her. Ruth Ann rolled down the window and looked past Jude at the lit kitchen windows, at Callum's shadow moving inside, and her mouth did something complicated.

"You should know," she said quietly, "that Eleanor had tea with Pastor Donnelly on Tuesday. And Wednesday she had lunch with the bank president. And today my husband got a call suggesting that Corliss Marine Supply might find its dock lease 'under review' if certain families keep making noise about old paperwork." She gripped the wheel. "I'm not telling you this to scare you. I'm telling you because my mother worked for your grandmother thirty years, and I liked your grandmother, and somebody has to say out loud what everyone's whispering."

"Thank you," Jude said.

"Don't thank me yet." Ruth Ann's eyes went to the root cellar door, just visible past the barn. "Whatever you've got stored down there—maybe don't store it down there anymore."

* * *

**CALLUM**

He moved the letters that night anyway—not because of Ruth Ann, but because he'd been carrying the warning from the hardware store around all day like a stone in his boot, and stones in boots either get shaken out or they work through skin.

He boxed everything: the correspondence, the archivist's report, the photographs, his grandfather's ledger. Two boxes. One went into the cab of Jude's truck. The other he carried to the boathouse and wedged behind the panels of the old skiff his father had never finished restoring, under a tarp, in the dark, with his heart going hard the whole time because this was his grandfather's handwriting and his father's grief and ten years of a family erased, and it fit in two cardboard boxes, and men with county windbreakers could erase it again if he let them.

When he came back up the hill, Jude was standing on the porch with a flashlight, and the relief on her face when she saw him made something in his chest turn over.

"You were gone twenty minutes," she said. "I counted."

"I know. I could hear you counting from the boathouse."

"I wasn't—" She stopped. Started over. "Ruth Ann came by. She thinks we should be careful."

"Ruth Ann's right." He climbed the steps. In the porch light her face was tired and stubborn and beautiful, and he was so tired of not touching her that the not-touching had become its own kind of architecture, load-bearing, holding up the whole structure of them. "Five more days. Then whatever happens, happens in the open."

She nodded. But she left the porch light on all night, and he heard her check the lock twice, and once, around two in the morning, heard her bare feet on the floorboards, going to the window, looking out at the dark orchard like the dark might be looking back.

* * *

**JUDE**

The fire started at 3:40 in the morning.

Jude knew the time precisely because her phone lit up with the alert from the smoke detector they'd finally installed in the cellar—a box Callum had bought in September and she'd teased him about, *what's going to burn down there, jars?*—and she was out of bed and down the stairs before her brain finished the word *fire*, and Callum was already ahead of her, already at the cellar doors with the garden hose, and the smell reached her before the light did: burning paper and old wood and something chemical underneath, something that hadn't come from any jar of preserves.

The flames were small. That was the terrible part—they'd been meant to be small, meant to smolder, meant to eat the shelves and the papers and maybe creep up the stairs toward the house while everyone slept. Callum killed it with the hose and a shovel of dirt and then stood in the black doorway breathing like a man who'd run a long way, and Jude put her hand on his back and felt it shaking.

"They went through the lock," he said. "Pried it. Look at the jamb."

The jamb was splintered. Inside, the far shelf—the one that had held the letters, the ledger, the photographs—was charred to the frame. Empty frames. Whoever set it had known exactly which shelf, exactly which boxes, had come prepared with a plan and a accelerant and the confidence of someone who believed the orchard's owners would be asleep, or cowardly, or both.

"They knew what was down there," Jude said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Not guessed. Knew."

Callum turned and looked at her, and in the flashlight beam his face was gray with smoke and something worse than anger. "Everyone knew what was down there. We talked about it at the diner. We talked about it at the hardware store. We talked about it in front of Dale Pruitt with a chainsaw in his hands." He laughed, short and awful. "We trusted this town, Jude. Four days early."

Sirens, far off, coming closer. Jude looked at the burned shelf, at the wet ash, at the splintered jamb, and felt the last soft thing in her go hard and clear. Ten years ago she had kept silent to protect her family's place, and the silence had cost her Callum, and now someone had brought fire to her grandmother's cellar to protect a secret that was never theirs to keep.

"Call the fire department back and tell them to slow down," she said. "And then call Marisol. It's four in the morning and she said don't call unless—"

"Unless what?"

"Unless someone stopped being polite."

* * *

**CALLUM**

The volunteer fire crew arrived to find a dead fire and two very awake property owners, and by six a.m. there were tire tracks photographed and a partial boot print cast in the mud by the creek path—the creek path, which meant whoever came had walked in from the water side, away from the road, away from Ruth Ann's Buick and every window in town.

Marisol arrived at seven with her coat over her pajamas and took one look at the cellar and said the thing Callum had been thinking since the hose went cold:

"This stops being a property dispute the moment someone commits arson. This is now evidence of consciousness of guilt, and I am going to use it." She crouched at the splintered jamb. "But understand what it means. They're not trying to win anymore. They're trying to survive until Friday. Desperate people in the next ninety-six hours are more dangerous than they have ever been, and they know we know, and that makes them unpredictable."

"So we file tomorrow," Callum said. "First thing. Why wait?"

"Because filed is public, and public is a target, and I want every document certified, every witness sworn, and Tom Eldridge somewhere safe before this becomes a headline." She straightened up, wincing. "Which brings me to the uncomfortable part. Tom called me an hour ago. Somebody knocked on his door last night. Didn't give a name. Told him fires are contagious in old houses."

The morning birds had started up, indifferent and bright. Callum looked at the orchard—the trees his grandfather had planted, the ground his family had been cheated out of, the woman beside him who had chosen, finally, irrevocably, to stand in the smoke with him—and felt the grudge he'd carried for a decade transmute into something simpler and harder.

"They want us scared," he said. "Fine. Let's give them something else to think about."

Jude was already dialing. When Tom picked up, she didn't waste words. "Tom, it's Jude Kavanagh. Pack a bag. You're staying at the orchard tonight—my grandmother kept that place as a refuge for forty years, and it's about time it remembered the job."

There was a pause on the line, and then the old man's voice, thin but steady: "Thought you'd never ask."

Callum caught Jude's eye over the phone, and for the first time since the smell of smoke, something that wasn't fear moved through him. They had four days, a burned cellar, a frightened witness, and a town full of people choosing sides in the dark.

And somewhere out there, past the creek, a man with a gas can thought he'd ended the story.

He hadn't. He'd just given it a deadline.
