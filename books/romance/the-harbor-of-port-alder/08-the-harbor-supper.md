## 8. The Harbor Supper

Marta did not ask. That was the first thing to understand about Marta, and the thing that took June three weeks of Sunday teas to learn: Marta did not issue invitations, because invitations could be declined, and Marta had apparently never in her life permitted a decline.

"You'll come Thursday," she said on Tuesday, setting down a second cup of tea June hadn't asked for but had, evidently, already been assigned. "Community supper at the hall. Fish pie, if Dora Kettle's cooking, and if it isn't Dora then it's the Henderson boys, and God help us all."

"I have chapters to transcribe," June said, which was true, and also the kind of thing a person said when they wanted to be told no.

"Transcribe them Friday." Marta stirred her tea with the particular violence of a woman who considered stirring an argument won. "Elias Thorne has kept you shut up in that cottage for a month. You've got the look of someone who's been talking to herself so long she's started answering."

June nearly laughed—nearly, and caught it just in time, because the truth was that she *had* been talking to herself, or rather to him, silently, constantly, arguing with every sentence he dictated as though he were in the room. Which was its own problem, and not one she intended to examine over tea.

"It's a working arrangement," she said instead. "He's ill. The book matters."

"The book will still matter Thursday." Marta looked at her over the rim of her cup with those librarian's eyes that seemed to catalog everything they landed on and file nothing away carelessly. "You know what I did before I ran the library? Twenty years behind the fish counter at Alder & Sons. You learn to read people faster than books, standing next to a slab of ice. And you, June Marsh, are a person who has forgotten how to sit at a table without a purpose."

The observation landed somewhere beneath June's ribs and stayed there, uncomfortable, like a stone swallowed whole.

"I sit at tables," she said weakly.

"When you're taking notes." Marta stood, collected the cups, and delivered her verdict to the sink rather than to June, which somehow made it worse. "Thursday. Seven o'clock. Wear something that isn't gray."

* * *

Thursday arrived wearing rain, which June took as a sign, though signs were not something she believed in anymore; she'd spent her belief elsewhere, unwisely, fifteen years ago. She tried the excuse of the weather on Marta by telephone and was informed that rain was "character-building and besides, Dora made the pie."

So June walked down Harbor Lane under an umbrella the wind kept trying to confiscate, past the chandlery and the old ice house, past the community center—

She didn't look at the community center. Fifteen years of practice went into not looking at it, into keeping her eyes fixed on the middle distance the way you do passing a house where something happened. But the building sat there at the edge of her vision anyway, brick and salt-stained windows, the same notice board, and her step quickened without her permission, and by the time she reached the hall her heart was going like a gull in a storm and she had to stand a moment with her hand on the door, breathing.

*It's a supper,* she told herself. *Fish pie. Old people. Nothing here can touch you.*

The lie held long enough for her to go inside.

The hall smelled of woodsmoke and boiled potatoes and wet wool, and it was loud—not the hostile loudness of London, all elbows and phone voices, but a rolling, generous noise, the sound of a hundred people who had known each other so long that conversation had become less about information than about rhythm. Someone had strung paper lanterns along the beams. A child was chasing another child between the tables with a bread roll held aloft like a torch. At the far end, someone had begun badly tuning a fiddle.

"There you are." Marta materialized at her elbow, took her umbrella, and steered her through the crowd with the efficiency of a tugboat. "You're sitting with us. End table. I've put you next to Tom Weller, who will tell you about his knees, and across from Peter Quill, who will pretend he isn't listening to everything, because that's what Peter does."

"And what will you be doing?"

"Minding everyone," Marta said, as if this were a job title, which in Port Alder it possibly was.

The table received June the way tide receives a piece of driftwood: without ceremony, but without any intention of letting go. Tom Weller told her about his knees within ninety seconds, as promised, in exhaustive detail. Peter Quill, a dry stick of a man with spectacles he cleaned more often than necessary, said almost nothing while visibly missing nothing. Dora Kettle herself came round with the pie, a broad woman with flour on her forearms and the satisfied air of an artist among admirers.

"You're the one renting the Marsh cottage," Dora said, serving her a portion that could have fed a family of four. "You eating properly out there? You look like a comma."

"A—"

"All elbows and no middle. There. Tell me that's not better."

It was, infuriatingly, better. The pie was extraordinary—golden-crusted, rich with cream and dill, the fish flaking apart—and June realized halfway through her second helping that she had stopped monitoring the room. Somewhere between Tom's knees and Dora's pie, her shoulders had come down from around her ears for the first time in a month. The fiddle struck up something jaunty and unskilled. A baby two tables over was beating a spoon against a pot in perfect time. Nobody here knew who she was. Nobody here was trying to take anything from her.

For one evening, she thought, I am almost a person, and not a pen.

She should have known better than to think the word *almost*. The universe in Port Alder, she was learning, had a sense of humor, and it was not a kind one.

The door opened. Rain gusted in with the smell of cold sea, and a hush moved through the hall the way wind moves through wheat—the particular hush of a small town recognizing someone famous, followed immediately by the louder, warmer noise of a small town deciding to pretend it hadn't.

Elias Thorne stood in the doorway, dripping, hatless, thinner than the photographs, his collar turned up against the weather and an expression on his face suggesting he had lost a bet with himself and come anyway.

"He never comes," Tom Weller whispered, awed, to June specifically, as though she might not know. "Thorne. Years, it's been. Recluse, they say."

"Mm," said June, whose heart had performed some maneuver she chose not to name.

Marta, naturally, had already risen. Naturally, she was waving. Naturally, there was exactly one empty chair left at their table, and it was beside June, because Marta had arranged the seating on Tuesday and June understood this now with terrible clarity: she had been placed. She was a piece on a board, and the player was carrying a teapot.

Elias crossed the hall nodding to people, deflecting greetings with a gruffness that everyone present clearly interpreted as affection. He sat down heavily in the empty chair, shook rain from his sleeves like a dog, and said, to the table in general:

"Dora. If that pie is the Henderson boys' recipe, I want it noted that I came under protest."

"It's mine, you ungrateful wretch, and you'll have seconds whether you like it or not." Dora was already loading his plate. "Look at you. Skin and bone. Sit still."

"I am sitting."

"Sitting *thinly*."

Laughter around the table, and Elias submitted to being fed with the resigned dignity of a cat being bathed. Then he turned, and saw June, and something flickered across his face—recognition, yes, but something underneath it, a quickening she couldn't read and didn't trust.

"Miss Marsh," he said. "They let you out."

"They dragged me," she said. "Under protest. Apparently we had that in common tonight."

Something like approval moved through his eyes. "Dora," he said, not looking away from her, "your pie is wasted on this woman. She writes for a living. She'll analyze it."

"I will not," said June.

"She will," Marta confirmed happily. "She analyzed my scone recipe last week. Said it had 'structural integrity issues.'"

"It collapsed," June said. "I stand by the analysis."

"*Structural integrity,*" repeated Peter Quill, from behind his spectacles, in the tone of a man filing something away forever.

And that was how the evening tipped over, though none of them saw it go.

* * *

It began, as these things do, innocently. Tom Weller, having exhausted his knees, asked Elias when the new book was coming, and Elias said there wasn't one, and Tom said surely a man couldn't just stop, and Elias said watch me, and then Peter Quill—quiet, observant, dangerous Peter—set down his teacup and said:

"It's because novels are finished, and he isn't the sort to write endings he doesn't believe."

The table went quiet in the way of tables sensing sport.

"That's either very flattering," Elias said slowly, "or an accusation."

"Both," said Peter placidly. "Your last three books ended with everyone forgiven and the fog lifting. Fog lifts in Port Alder maybe four days a year. You've been writing us as we'd like to be, not as we are. A man loses faith in that eventually."

"I write *people*, Quill, not meteorological reports."

"You write people *in* weather. It's not the same."

June should have stayed out of it. Every instinct honed over fifteen years of professional invisibility said: stay out of it, you are here as a guest, as a ghost, as nobody. But Peter had said *fog lifts four days a year* with such precision, and Elias's jaw had set in that particular way she'd learned to recognize through a hundred hours of transcription—the way he set it when someone was wrong and he knew it and hadn't yet found the sentence to prove it—and before she could stop herself she heard her own voice say:

"He's right about the fog, actually."

Every head at the table turned. Elias looked at her with an expression she would spend the rest of the night failing to decode.

"Oh?" he said.

"In your last book. *The Salt Year.* The ending—it's beautiful, everyone says so, it *is* beautiful—but it's a lie of light. You give them the harbor at dawn like a gift, and the whole book earned grief, and grief doesn't cash out in sunrises. You wrote the ending the characters deserved instead of the ending they'd built." She heard herself continuing, helpless, the way water finds a crack. "You forgave them too early. Forgiveness on page three hundred is cheap. It costs nothing unless it's withheld until it hurts."

Silence. Tom Weller looked between them like a man watching tennis. Dora had stopped mid-pour.

Then Elias laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, rusty, the laugh of a man surprised by his own chest. "Withheld until it hurts," he repeated. "Listen to her. Dora, where did you find this person?"

"She found herself," said Marta, "at my kitchen table, analyzing my scones."

"You want cost?" Elias leaned toward June, and there was color in his face now that hadn't been there an hour ago, and she hated how much she noticed it. "Fine. You think forgiveness is cheap at page three hundred? Withholding it is easy. Anyone can withhold. Cruelty gets mistaken for honesty in every workshop in England. The hard thing—the genuinely hard thing—is deciding people have suffered enough and letting them off. That's not a lie of light. That's mercy, and mercy is expensive, because half your critics will call it sentiment."

"Mercy given early isn't mercy," June shot back. "It's impatience. You wanted to be done with them."

"I wanted them to *rest*."

"People don't need to rest. They need to be *seen*—"

"—and seeing means ending the book with them still bleeding—"

"—it means not lying about the bleeding—"

"*More pie?*" bellowed Dora, holding the dish over the table like a referee breaking up a fight, and the whole table roared, and June realized she was on her feet, or nearly, one hand braced on the table, and that she had just publicly argued literature with Elias Thorne in front of the entire population of Port Alder's Thursday supper, and that she could not remember the last time she had raised her voice about anything, because ghosts don't raise their voices, ghosts don't argue, ghosts transcribe.

But the table wasn't scandalized. The table was *delighted*. Peter Quill was polishing his spectacles to hide his face. Tom Weller was saying "go on then, go on" like a man at a match. Marta wore the expression of a woman watching a plan come to harvest.

Elias sat back down. He was still laughing, or something adjacent to laughing, and he pulled June's chair out beside him with one hand—an outrageous, presumptuous gesture—and said, "Sit down, Miss Marsh, before you hurt yourself. You argue like someone who hasn't practiced in years and misses it terribly."

The accuracy of it stung. She sat.

"For the record," he said, quieter, just to her, under the table's resumed roar, "you're wrong about the ending. But you're wrong in the only way worth being wrong. Most people aren't wrong *inside* the work. They're wrong outside it, at a polite distance." He picked up his fork. "You got in."

It was, she understood dimly, the most intimate thing anyone had said to her in fifteen years, and she had no idea what to do with it, so she ate her pie.

* * *

The evening loosened after that, the way evenings do when the worst has already happened and survived. Tom told a story about the flood of '09 that required three reenactments and a napkin diagram. Dora revealed that Elias, aged nineteen, had worked one summer on her father's boat and been seasick every single day and lied about it in his first novel, claiming he'd loved it. "Loved it!" she wheezed. "Green as weed, he was, hanging over the rail!"

"That book won a prize," Elias protested.

"The prize should've gone to the rail."

Even June laughed at that—laughed fully, undeliberately, the sound escaping before she could vet it, and she felt Elias glance sideways at her when it happened, quick and unreadable, filing it away the way Peter filed things away.

Later, when the fiddle struck up something slow and the children had been corralled, Marta appeared at June's shoulder with two cups of tea and pressed one into her hand. "Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"You know what. You argued with Elias Thorne in front of forty witnesses and lived."

"He started it."

"Mm." Marta sipped her tea, watching the dancers with librarian's patience. "Fifteen years I've watched that man sit at these suppers saying nothing to anybody, being pleasant the way a lighthouse is pleasant, and tonight he argued about *fog* like a boy. Whatever you're doing in that cottage, girl, keep doing it." She paused. "Or stop. One of the two. Something's happening either way."

June looked across the hall. Elias was standing near the fire talking to old Mrs. Penhale, and even across the room she could see the tiredness in him, the way he held himself carefully, the illness he hid from the press showing itself only in the economy of his movements, the way he conserved himself like a man rationing fuel. And yet he had come. Under protest, he'd said. In the rain. To a fish pie and a fiddle and a table of people who called him skin and bone.

Nobody had made him come. That was the thing she kept circling back to, later, walking home alone under a sky finally wrung dry. Nobody had scheduled him, contracted him, paid him. He had simply come, the way the tide comes, without authorization, and the town had closed around him like water around a hull.

And she had sat at a table and been a person. Not a pen, not a ghost, not a false name in a rented cottage. A woman who argued about endings and laughed too loudly and was served too much pie. Belonging had arrived the way Marta's tea arrived—unasked for, already poured, impossible to return.

She was halfway up Harbor Lane, past the dark shape of the community center, when she heard footsteps behind her and turned.

Elias stood in the lamplight, breath fogging, hands in his pockets, looking—for the first time since she'd met him—uncertain.

"Miss Marsh," he said. "A question. About the memoir."

"Yes?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. Rain dripped from a gutter somewhere, counting seconds.

"How much of yourself," he said at last, carefully, "do you plan to leave out of my book?"

The question hung in the cold air between them, and June—who had rehearsed a hundred answers to a hundred questions over fifteen years, who had an answer ready for publishers and neighbors and librarians alike—found, to her horror, that she had absolutely nothing prepared.
