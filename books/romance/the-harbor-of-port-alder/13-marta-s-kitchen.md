## 13. Marta's Kitchen

The rain came in off the harbor that Thursday like it had a grudge to settle, and I arrived at Marta's door with wet hair, a loaf of bread going stale under my arm, and the particular exhaustion of a woman who had spent three hours transcribing Elias Thorne's memories of his mother's funeral and then cried in her car about a man who wasn't even dead yet.

Marta took one look at me and said, "You'll want tea. The good kind, not the dust they sell at the shop."

"I brought bread," I said, holding it up like an offering to a small, formidable god.

"Day-old," she observed, taking it anyway. "You can toast it. Come in before the sea comes in after you."

Her kitchen was the kind of room that made you understand why people stayed in Port Alder their whole lives. It wasn't large. The wallpaper had been fashionable sometime around the invention of the steam engine, and the table wobbled in a way that suggested it had been doing so for decades out of pure habit. But there were plants on every windowsill—basil gone leggy, mint staging a quiet takeover of its pot—and a kettle that had clearly been boiled ten thousand times and intended to be boiled ten thousand more. A cat named Admiral occupied the third chair as if he held title to it.

I'd been coming here on Thursdays for six weeks now. Tea, mostly. Sometimes soup. Once, a spectacular argument about whether Daphne du Maurier counted as literature (Marta: yes; me: obviously yes; we argued anyway because it was enjoyable). She was the town librarian, sixty-something, built like a lighthouse and about as easy to move once she'd decided where she stood. She had never asked me a single prying question, which in a town like Port Alder was either indifference or discipline.

I had assumed indifference.

I was wrong.

* * *

We'd finished the toast and were on our second pot when Marta set down her cup with a small deliberate click and said, "I found something in the archive last week. While you were at Thorne's house."

"Oh?"

"Mm." She got up, went to the dresser, and came back with a manila folder, which she placed on the table between us but did not open. "Before I show you, I want to say something, and I'd take it kindly if you let me finish."

My stomach did something complicated. "All right."

"You're not June Marsh."

The rain filled the silence. The Admiral opened one eye, assessed the situation, and closed it again, unimpressed by human drama.

"Marta—" I started.

"Let me finish," she said, and I let her, because there are voices you argue with and voices you sit very still for. "June Marsh is a perfectly nice name. You answer to it quick enough, but you answer quick because you've practiced. Nobody practices their own name." She folded her hands. "Fifteen years ago there was a girl in this town who volunteered at the community center. Bright girl. Wrote things that made the rest of us look twice. And one winter something happened to her that this town should be ashamed of, and is, quietly, the ones who remember. She left on the morning ferry and nobody ever said her name again out loud, which is how cowards apologize—not saying anything at all and calling it peace."

The room had gone very far away and very close at the same time. I could hear my own pulse. I could hear the kettle ticking as it cooled.

"Your name is Juniper Brightwater," Marta said. "And I have known that since your second week here, when you signed the cottage rental ledger and your handwriting shook."

"That's not proof of anything," I whispered. "People's handwriting shakes."

"Not like that. Not like a person signing away a name they buried." She turned the folder around and opened it. "This is proof."

Inside were photocopies—old ones, soft-edged, handled carefully. A manuscript submission form from the community center's writing program, dated fifteen years back. A letter on library letterhead. And pages of a story titled *The Salt Year*, typed, with handwritten corrections in two different inks.

"What am I looking at?" My voice came out strange, thin, like it belonged to someone across the room.

"The truth," Marta said. "Which has been sitting in my archive for fifteen years because nobody asked me for it, and because the one time somebody did ask, they asked me to lose it instead." She tapped the typed pages. "This is your story. Your draft. Dated, logged, submitted to the program anthology in November. I know because I logged it myself. I was running the archive then, same as now."

"And the other version—the one everyone said I copied—"

"Appeared in January. Two months later. Under someone else's name." Marta's mouth went flat. "With your sentences in it. Not similar sentences. *Your* sentences. I've read both until I could recite them. There's a paragraph about gulls fighting over fish guts behind the cannery that exists word for word in both drafts, and I will tell you something, Juniper Brightwater: no two writers independently write 'gulls fight like creditors' and mean it."

A sound came out of me that was half laugh and half something else entirely. "That line. I forgot I wrote that line."

"Well, I didn't. Librarians forget nothing; it's the whole job." She pushed the folder toward me. "There's more. The submission log. Witness statements—I took them myself, that winter, from three people who saw the mentor with your draft before you ever read hers aloud. They wouldn't speak publicly. They were scared, or loyal, or tired. But they signed statements, and I kept them. Sealed. Waiting."

"Waiting for what?" I asked. "Fifteen years, Marta. Why keep it all that time? What were you waiting for?"

She looked at me over her cup, and her eyes weren't hard at all, which somehow made it worse and better simultaneously.

"For you to come back," she said simply. "Or for someone to come asking. Whichever happened first. I didn't think it would be you. People don't come back to places that hurt them. But then a woman called June Marsh rented the harbor cottage, and flinched at the community center like it owed her money, and knew the tide tables without being told, and read du Maurier like she was defending a doctoral thesis—and I thought, well. Either June Marsh is the world's most dedicated impersonator, or the Brightwater girl finally ran out of ocean." She refilled my cup without asking. "So. Which is it?"

I looked down at the folder. At my own handwriting, younger and angrier, correcting a sentence I no longer remembered writing. Fifteen years I had built a life out of not being seen, and here was a woman I'd known for six weeks handing me a mirror and a cup of tea in the same motion.

"Why?" I managed. "Why hide it? Why protect me? You didn't even know me."

"No," Marta agreed. "But I knew what was done to you. I watched it happen. I watched grown adults—a whole town, mind, not three or four bad apples, a whole *town*—decide a nineteen-year-old girl was guilty because deciding otherwise would have been inconvenient. Because the mentor was beloved. Because the family she stole for was old money. Because it was easier to lose one girl than to lose the comfortable story." She set her cup down harder than necessary. "I kept the file because keeping it was the only honest thing I could do at the time, and I have been ashamed of that *only* for fifteen years. So don't thank me. Thanking me lets me off too easy."

The Admiral, sensing the moment required him, jumped down from his chair and installed himself on my lap, heavy and warm as a sandbag. I put my hand on him so I'd have something to do besides fall apart.

"I'm not the same person," I said finally. "Whoever she was, that girl—"

"She's right here," Marta said. "Drinking my tea. Stealing my cat's affection. Writing a book next door to the only man in this town who ever defended her without knowing her name."

My head came up. "What?"

"Oh, don't look at me like that. Everyone knows you're ghosting Thorne's memoirs; Mrs. Penhale at the post office knows everything that happens within a mile of water." She waved a hand. "But I'll tell you something else I know, since we're being honest tonight, and honesty is exhausting so let's get it all done at once. That winter, after you left—Elias Thorne wrote a letter to the *Harbor Gazette*. Never published. The editor sat on it—pressure from above, you understand. I have a copy of that too." She slid a second sheet from the folder. "He wrote it under his own name. He said the plagiarism case was a miscarriage. He said he had read both manuscripts and any competent reader could see which was the original. He said, and I quote because I've had fifteen years to memorize it: 'This town did not lose a plagiarist. It lost the best writer it ever produced, and it did so on purpose, because the truth was heavier than our pride.'"

The kitchen blurred. I pressed my knuckles against my eyes and breathed until the blur became manageable, and when I could see again, Marta was watching me with an expression I couldn't name—something between tenderness and triumph, as if she'd been waiting years to watch someone come home and had rehearsed the welcome.

"He never told anyone why he cared," she said gently. "Never said how he'd read your work. Just that he had. Some of us wondered. Most of us didn't dare ask. A man like Thorne doesn't explain himself; he just stands somewhere and is right, loudly."

I thought of the letters. Of course I thought of the letters—the anonymous ones, postmarked nowhere local, arriving through my worst year like weather from a kinder climate. *You are not what they decided you were. Write anyway.* I had never connected them to Port Alder. I had never dared.

"Elias," I said, and stopped, because finishing the sentence aloud would change everything, and I wasn't ready, and also I was more ready than I had ever been in my life.

"Elias," Marta agreed, watching me with librarian's patience, missing nothing. "Now. Here's the practical part, because sentiment is fine but paper is better." She drew a final document from the folder—a sworn statement, dated, signed, witnessed, the ink faded but legible. "My testimony. The log copies. The witness statements. The letter. It's yours. All of it. I'm not giving it to you to bury in a drawer—I assume you'll do something with it eventually, though God knows what, given you're living under a false name writing a true book for a dying man, which sounds less like a career and more like a Greek tragedy with better weather."

A laugh escaped me, wet and startled. "It does sound like that when you say it."

"It sounds like that however it's said." She reached across the table and covered my hand with hers, brief and firm, the way you'd steady something valuable on a shelf. "Juniper. Listen to me. I watched this town fail you once. I am an old woman and I do not intend to watch it happen twice. Whatever comes—if the truth gets out, if people talk, if Mrs. Penhale talks, which she will—you will not face it alone. Do you understand me? There are people here who kept quiet because they were afraid, and some of them are sorry, and some of them aren't, but there are enough of the sorry ones to make a wall around you if it comes to that. Me first. Me always."

I looked down at the cat, at the tea, at the folder full of my own rescued past, and felt something in my chest unlock that had been locked so long I'd mistaken it for architecture.

"I spent fifteen years making sure no one could see me," I said. "I got very good at it. It's the one skill I never put on a résumé."

"And how's that working out?" Marta asked dryly.

"Honestly? Terrible. Someone keeps seeing me anyway."

"Mm. Happens in harbors. Things wash up." She stood and began gathering cups, conversation over, kindness delivered in the manner of a woman who considered emotion a thing to be served alongside refreshments and cleared away before it went sticky. "Stay for soup. It's barley. It's not exciting but it's hot, and you look like a woman who's been living on ambition and toast."

"I have been living on ambition and toast."

"There, you see. Sit."

I sat. The Admiral reclaimed my lap. Rain worked at the windows, and Marta moved around her kitchen humming something tuneless, and I understood that I had just survived the thing I had feared for fifteen years: being known. Being seen, fully, by clear eyes, in daylight—and finding that the floor held. That the sky stayed up. That soup still got made.

Halfway through the barley, Marta paused with the ladle in the air and said, casually, in the tone of a woman mentioning the price of flour:

"One more thing. That publisher fellow of Thorne's—he telephoned the library yesterday. Asking questions. About June Marsh. Where she came from, whether anyone local could vouch for her, whether the town had any... history with her." Her eyes met mine over the pot. "I told him I'd check our records and get back to him."

"Marta. What did you—"

"I told him," she said, serving me a bowl with great ceremony, "that our records indicated June Marsh was born in this town, grew up on Harbor Lane, and was known to every soul here as honest, gifted, and much wronged—which is all technically true, if you know which names to read between." She sat down across from me, and there was steel under the warmth, the way there is under all the best warmth. "But he struck me as a man who doesn't stop asking because a librarian gives him a tidy answer. He struck me as a man who smells a story and wants to sell it. So you should know, Juniper: whatever you're going to do about your name, your book, and that man next door—I'd do it soon. Before he does it for you."

Outside, the rain was easing. Somewhere down the lane, a light burned in the window of a cottage where a dying novelist was probably awake at this hour, writing something he hadn't shown me yet.

I picked up my spoon. My hands, I noticed, had stopped shaking.

"All right," I said. "Tell me everything else you know. Start with the publisher. And then—" I hesitated, and heard myself say it before I could stop it, "—then tell me about Elias Thorne, fifteen years ago. Where was he? Who did he know? How did he read my manuscript, Marta?"

Marta smiled slowly, the smile of a woman who had been waiting all evening for exactly that question, and reached for the folder one more time.

"Now *that*," she said, "is a longer story than the soup will cover. Lucky for you, I made plenty."
