## 9. The Appraiser Calls

The phone rang at seven in the morning, and Edmund Whitlock was already awake, already dressed, already standing in the kitchen with a cup of black coffee going cold in his hand because sleep had become a negotiation he kept losing.

He watched the phone ring the way he watched everything now — with the detached attention of a man reading someone else's accounts. The landline was a relic; he'd never replaced it because replacing things cost money, and money had long ago stopped being a substance and become a discipline. The phone rang twice more before he picked it up.

"Whitlock."

"Mr. Whitlock, good morning. This is Daniel Okafor, with Halloway & Finch, agricultural appraisals." A pause, the sound of paper shuffling. "I'm calling to update your appointment."

Edmund set his coffee down. "The letter said March."

"The schedule's moved up. We've had cancellations up the coast — storm damage assessments, mostly. I can be at Sandpiper Point in ten days. The fourteenth."

"Ten days." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. Ten days meant the appraisal report would land on the bank's desk by early February, which meant the bank's decision would come while there was still frost in the ground, while the vines were still sleeping, while everything he'd spent three years rationing himself down to would be weighed and priced like furniture in an estate sale.

"I know it's short notice," Okafor said. "If it's a problem—"

"It's not a problem." It was never a problem. Problems were for people who expected things to go well. "Ten days is fine. I'll have the records ready."

"Excellent. I'll want the last five years of production figures, debt schedules, equipment inventories, and access to the cellar and the fields. Oh — and Mr. Whitlock? If you have any documentation of restoration work, replanting, soil amendments, anything of that nature, include it. Land value isn't just acreage. It's what's been done to the land."

Edmund looked out the kitchen window at the vineyard, white to the tree line, the rows buried under a foot of snow that had stopped falling sometime in the night. What's been done to the land. His mother had done things to this land. He had done things to it too, though most of them were subtractions.

"I'll have everything," he said, and hung up.

* * *

He spent the morning in the office above the barrel room, pulling files, and it was there — elbow-deep in three years of receipts, his hands gray with ledger dust — that Julian found him.

Edmund heard him on the stairs. He'd learned Julian's tread in college, twenty years ago: quick, light, always arriving as if he'd been invited. Some things didn't change. Edmund didn't look up from the file cabinet.

"You're up early," he said.

"I'm always up early. You just never used to see it." Julian leaned against the doorframe. Through the frosted window behind him, the storm-light made him look younger than he was, or maybe just less finished. "Clara's still asleep. I've been up since five reading your mother's—" He stopped himself. "Never mind. Who was on the phone?"

"The appraiser. Moved up. Ten days."

Something crossed Julian's face — something fast, quickly filed away. "That's soon."

"That's the point. Banks don't extend deadlines out of kindness. They compress them so you make mistakes." Edmund closed the drawer and turned to face him fully. "Which brings us to you. You said you were scouting the property for investors. I assume they'll want to know the number before anyone makes an offer. So let's not waste each other's time. What are they offering?"

Julian pushed off the doorframe. He was wearing one of Edmund's spare sweaters — a fact Edmund resented more than he could reasonably justify — and he looked, for a moment, like a man about to say something difficult. Then the moment passed, the way such moments always passed between them, and Julian smiled instead, that old easy smile that had won over every professor and every girl and every scholarship committee from Providence to Portland.

"Sit down, Edmund."

"I'm standing."

"Fine. Stand." Julian dragged a chair around anyway and sat in it backward, arms folded across the backrest, the way he used to sit in lecture halls. "Here's the thing. I've been circling how to tell you this for two days, and every version sounds like a lie, so I'm just going to say it plainly and you're going to call me a liar, and then we'll get past it. Or we won't. But I'm saying it."

"Say it."

"There are no investors."

Edmund waited. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below them, in the cellar, a barrel settled against its staves with a wooden groan.

"What there is," Julian went on, "is a cooperative. The Harbor Coast Agricultural Trust — conservation-minded, grant-funded, run by people I've worked with for six years on land-preservation projects in the Willamette. They buy working farms in distress, forgive a structured portion of the debt, and convert ownership to a partnership model. The land stays agricultural in perpetuity. No development. No condos, no tasting-room theme park, no golf course with a vineyard view. The vines keep growing. Someone keeps farming them."

Edmund stood very still. Outside, wind moved through the bare rows and died somewhere near the glasshouse.

"And you represent them," he said finally. "You."

"I'm their regional director. Have been for four years."

"You told me you were scouting for investors."

"I told you what I thought you'd hear." Julian's smile had gone away entirely now, and without it his face looked strangely undefended. "Because if I'd opened with 'cooperative,' you'd have shut the door. If I'd opened with 'I want to help you,' you'd have shut the door faster. I know you, Edmund. I've known you for twenty years, including the fifteen we didn't speak. You don't take help. You take transactions. So I came here to transact."

"A transaction," Edmund repeated. The word tasted like copper. "You fly into my house on the heels of a snowstorm, eat my food, burn my firewood, and now you inform me that the whole visit has been a negotiation conducted under false pretenses. And I'm supposed to believe the pretense was *generous*."

"I'm telling you the truth now. That has to count for something."

"Why?" Edmund heard his own voice rise and made himself lower it. That was the training, the lifelong regimen: feeling was expenditure, expenditure was loss. "Why does any of it count for something, Julian? Give me one reason. One verifiable reason to believe a single word that comes out of your mouth."

Julian unfolded himself from the chair and stood. For a moment the two of them faced each other across the desk with its drifts of paper, and Edmund saw something in Julian's eyes he hadn't seen since they were twenty-five and everything between them broke: not anger. Hurt. Old, patient, well-kept hurt, the kind a man maintains the way Edmund maintained his ledgers.

"Because I came here to save your vineyard," Julian said quietly, "and I knew you'd rather lose it than be saved by me. And I came anyway."

Then he left. Edmund heard his footsteps go down the stairs, cross the barrel room, fade into the cold wing of the house where the guest rooms were.

* * *

He should have gone after him. He knew that even as he stood there not moving. There was a version of Edmund Whitlock — a better one, presumably, living some untroubled parallel life — who would have followed Julian down those stairs and said *tell me about the terms*. That man would have sat at the table with the paperwork by noon. By evening the three of them might have been drafting something together, the way they once drafted things together, badly and joyfully, before everything curdled.

Instead Edmund did what he had trained himself to do for twenty years. He sat down at the desk, opened the debt schedule, and began to work.

The numbers were familiar enough that he could have recited them. Total secured debt: $1.4 million against an appraised value that, in the best of all possible worlds, might reach $1.1 million. Operating line exhausted. Equipment loans current but immovable. The bank's forbearance agreement, signed in October, gave him until February 28 to demonstrate either refinancing capacity or a credible sale. Ten days to appraisal. Six weeks to judgment.

He worked through the morning the way a monk works through matins — not because the prayer changes anything, but because the discipline is the point. He itemized. He cross-referenced. He found an arithmetic error in the November fuel reconciliation and corrected it, and felt nothing, and noted that he felt nothing, and entered that too, somewhere, in the running audit of himself.

It was Clara who interrupted him, mid-afternoon. She appeared in the doorway with two mugs of tea and set one on the corner of the desk without asking, the way she used to in the library at college, when she'd appear beside his carrel with coffee and sit with him through whole evenings without requiring conversation. He had forgotten that she did that. Remembering it now felt like finding a splinter.

"You've been up here all day," she said.

"There's work."

"There's always work. That's rather the architecture of you, isn't it." She pulled the other chair around and sat, cradling her mug. Snow-light came through the window and lay along her jaw. "Julian told me. About the trust."

"Did he." Edmund kept his eyes on the page. "And what did he tell you?"

"That it's real. That he's been doing this work for years. That he wanted to help you and knew you'd refuse if you knew he was the one helping." She paused. "He also said you called him a liar."

"I asked him for one reason to believe him. He declined to provide one."

"He provided the only one there is, Edmund. He showed up." She leaned forward. "I've read about this trust. I looked it up this morning on my phone — we have signal again, incidentally, the tower must be back. The Harbor Coast Agricultural Trust. It's legitimate. They've done eleven properties in six years. There's a vineyard outside Newport they restructured in 2021; the family still runs it. I can show you the articles."

"I don't need articles."

"No," Clara said softly. "You need a reason not to want it to be true. That's different."

He looked up at her then. Her eyes were steady and kind and absolutely without pity, which was the only reason he could bear them. Twenty-five years ago — no, twenty — he had loved this woman with the whole untrained ferocity of his heart, before he learned that hearts were liabilities. She had loved him back, or he had believed so, and then Julian had — except Julian said otherwise, Julian said the whole story was different, and Edmund had spent two days refusing to open that door because behind it was either a betrayal or an apology, and he wasn't sure which would be worse.

"If I accept their terms," he said, "I stop being the owner. Do you understand what that means? Everything I've done — the three years, the selling, the going without — it was all to keep this place mine. Not the bank's. Not the county's. Mine. You hand it to a cooperative and I'm a — a tenant with a title. A steward. An employee of a nonprofit."

"Or a partner."

"Partners leave." The words came out before he could stop them, and he saw her flinch, very slightly, and hated himself with a completeness that was almost restful. "Partners die. Partners marry other people. Ownership is the only thing that stays."

"Your mother owned this place," Clara said. "Where did staying get her?"

The silence after that was long enough to hold a funeral.

"She stayed," Edmund said at last, evenly. "She stayed until the day she died, in her own house, on her own land. That's more than most people manage."

"And was she happy?"

"That's not a line item."

"No," Clara agreed, rising, taking her mug. "It never is, with you." At the door she stopped, and did not turn around. "The appraiser comes in ten days. Julian's offer doesn't expire tomorrow, but it isn't infinite either. Think about which wall you're building, Edmund. And who you're building it against."

* * *

That night he took the lamp down into the cellar, because the cellar was the only room in the house where his thoughts arranged themselves.

The barrels stood in their long ranks, breathing. His grandfather had laid some of these staves; his mother had filled others in the last desperate vintages, the ones the journals apparently recorded — Clara and Julian had been reading Marguerite's notebooks for two days, passing them back and forth like scripture, and Edmund had not asked what they found there because he wasn't sure he could survive the answer. He knew the outline. Everyone in the valley knew the outline: Marguerite Whitlock mortgaged the vineyard twice, bet everything on a frost-tender varietal the market wasn't ready for, and lost nearly everything except the land itself. Love over solvency, Clara would probably call it. Edmund called it what it was: a woman who couldn't stop spending what she didn't have, and a son who learned the lesson too well.

He put his palm flat against the nearest barrel. Cold oak, faint sweetness seeping through the wood. Under his hand the wine went on becoming itself, indifferent to appraisals, to banks, to the pride of men.

Ten days.

He tried to make himself feel relief — Julian's revelation should have been relief, should have been a door swinging open after years of bricked-up windows. A cooperative. Debt forgiveness. The land saved, the vines saved, no developers, no ruin. Every rational faculty he possessed lined up behind the obvious conclusion.

And underneath the lining, older and stronger than any rational faculty, ran the other current: *if you accept, you owe him.* If the trust saved Sandpiper Point, it saved it through Julian Marsh — through the man who, as far as Edmund had believed for twenty years, took Clara from him in a single summer and never once looked back. To sign those papers would be to stand in a room with that history and hand it the keys. It would mean the ledger was wrong. Not a line item. Not a column. The whole book — twenty years of entries, every sacrifice priced and posted, the entire architecture of his austerity — marked *void*, written off, worthless.

He could not afford that. It was the one asset he had left.

So he stood among his barrels with the lamp burning low and built the wall, brick by brick, the way he'd built it a hundred times before. The trust was real, probably. The terms were generous, probably. Julian was sincere, possibly. None of it mattered, because accepting help was a debt that compounded invisibly, and Edmund Whitlock did not take on invisible debt. He would meet the appraiser alone. He would present the numbers alone. If the number came back short, he would sell to whoever paid — developer, speculator, the devil in a linen suit — and he would walk away with a cleared balance sheet and nothing else, and nothing else was a thing he had trained himself never to need.

Above him, faintly, through the floorboards, he heard laughter. Clara's, and then Julian's, answering. They were in the kitchen, probably, with the journals spread across the table, reading his mother's handwriting aloud to each other by lamplight while the storm's last breath rattled the windows.

They sounded, he thought, like people building something.

He turned off the lamp and stood in the dark among the barrels, holding his wall up with both hands, and told himself it was load-bearing.

Upstairs, a phone rang — the landline, shrill in the quiet house — and then stopped, and then Clara's voice, muffled and rising, calling his name down through the floor.

"Edmund. Edmund, you need to come up. It's the bank."
