## 17. Running for the Platform

### Edmund

He heard her packing from the kitchen — the soft, terrible sounds of a suitcase being closed.

Zippers. The click of a latch. A drawer pushed shut with more force than necessary, as if she were angry at the furniture for holding her things. Each sound landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He stood at the counter with a cup of coffee he had no intention of drinking and counted the noises the way he'd once counted debts: methodically, hopelessly, watching the total climb toward something he couldn't pay.

The maintenance train would pass through Sandpiper Halt at 9:40. Julian had confirmed it by phone at dawn, standing in the doorway in one of Edmund's borrowed sweaters, his frostbitten fingers still wrapped in gauze, and he'd said it gently, which was worse. *Roads are clear to the coast road at least. Slush the whole way, but clear.* As if he were reporting weather and not an execution date.

Edmund had said nothing all morning. That was the discipline, wasn't it? Twenty years of saying nothing. He had trained himself so thoroughly that now, when the words finally existed — when they sat in his throat like stones of their own, warm and alive and desperate to be spoken — his jaw simply would not open. The muscle refused. The heart's obedient servant, keeping its master's silence.

Clara came down the stairs with her coat over her arm and her hair pinned back for travel, and she looked, God help him, exactly as she had at twenty-five on the platform at Ashford, except that then she'd been arriving and now she was leaving, and both times he had stood in the same paralyzed silence while she made the decision for him.

"The car?" she said.

"It'll start." He set down the untouched coffee. "It always starts."

"That's not what I asked."

It wasn't, and they both knew it, and he picked up her suitcase instead of answering because his hands needed work and his mouth could not be trusted.

* * *

### Clara

The drive was forty minutes of gray slush and wipers fighting a losing war against the melt.

She watched him from the passenger seat — the hands at ten and two, precise as ever; the eyes fixed on the road with the concentration of a man defusing something. He drove the way he did everything, as if error were a form of moral failure. She remembered being twenty-five and finding that quality steadying. She remembered, later, understanding it was a wall dressed up as competence.

"You could say something," she said, when the vineyard had fallen away behind them and the hedges began, the fields going silver with runoff.

"I'm driving."

"You know that's not what I mean."

His jaw worked. The wipers thumped. Somewhere under the ice on the fields, she thought, the vines were waking whether anyone asked them to or not. That was the thing about thaw — it didn't wait for permission. It didn't consult the ledger.

"Edmund." She turned in her seat to face him fully. "I'm getting on that train in half an hour unless you give me a reason not to. And I want to be very clear about what I'm not asking for. I'm not asking you to promise me anything. I'm not asking for a proposal, or a plan, or a five-year projection with margins. I spent eleven years married to a man who gave me projections instead of presence, and I will not do it twice. I'm asking for one true sentence. That's all. One."

The car hit a patch of slush and shuddered. He corrected without seeming to notice.

"And if I can't find it?" His voice came out low, scraped.

"Then I'll know it isn't there," she said. "And I'll get on the train, and I'll survive it, because I've survived worse. But you'll spend the rest of your life doing the arithmetic of this morning. I've seen you do it. You're already doing it."

He pulled over.

Just like that, no warning — the tires crunching onto the frozen shoulder, the engine idling, the wipers stopping mid-arc so that the windshield filled with a blur of melting white. He put his hands flat on the wheel and stared through that blur at nothing, and she saw his shoulders rise once, hard, like a man surfacing from deep water.

"Clara." Her name in his mouth sounded like a word in a language he was relearning. "I have spent twenty years being very good at not wanting things. Do you understand? It's the only skill I have. I can price a barrel of Pinot Noir to the cent. I can tell you to the week when the bank will call. I can go without — heat, help, company, sleep — indefinitely, and I built my whole life on being able to do it, because the year I was nineteen I learned that wanting things is how you end up with nothing." His breath fogged the glass. "And then you walked out of that storm and every single discipline I have failed at once."

She didn't move. She barely breathed.

"I'm not going to ask you to stay," he said. "I've been trying to make myself ask you for two days and I can't do it, because asking is the thing I don't know how to do. Asking means admitting there's something I can't replace if it's taken from me. So here's what I have instead. Here's my one sentence, and it's not elegant, and it isn't a proposal, and you should absolutely board that train if it isn't enough." He turned and looked at her, and his eyes were wet and furious and utterly undefended, and she understood she was seeing something no one had seen since his mother died. "I want you to stay. Not need. Not require. *Want*. Every morning since the snow sealed the road, I have woken up in my own house and been glad — glad, Clara, I didn't remember what glad felt like — and it's because you were downstairs. And tomorrow you won't be, and I will deserve it, because I let you pack in silence."

The wipers started again, someone's hand or some mercy, sweeping the blur away. The world came back sharp: the silver fields, the black hedges, the road running east toward the coast and the platform and the train.

"Say it again," she whispered.

"I want you to stay."

"Not the want. The rest."

"I let you pack in silence." His voice cracked clean through. "I hate myself for it. I have hated myself for it since the first zipper, and I kept doing it anyway, because silence is the only language my fear speaks fluently."

She reached across the console and took his hand off the wheel and held it, cold fingers around colder ones, and neither of them said anything for a long moment while the engine idled and the slush ticked against the undercarriage like a clock.

"Drive," she said finally. "We're going to miss it."

He looked at her — really looked, searching her face for the trap, the fine print, the clause that would cost him everything later — and found none, and something in his expression broke open like ice going out of a river.

"Is that—" He stopped. Started again. "Does that mean—"

"It means drive, Edmund. Before I change my mind about making this easy for you."

He drove. He drove faster than he had ever driven anything in his careful life.

* * *

### Edmund

Sandpiper Halt was a single platform, a wooden shelter with a leaking roof, and a sign reading SANDPIPER POINT — 2 MILES in letters faded to ghosts. The maintenance train sat idling on the single track, breathing diesel smoke into the cold bright morning, three cars and a caboose, going nowhere fast and everywhere eventually.

They came out of the trees at 9:31 and he took the last curve too hard and the car slewed through six inches of slush and stopped at an angle that would have scandalized every driving instructor alive. Neither of them cared. Clara had her door open before the engine died.

He got out and the cold hit him and he realized he was shaking — not from the cold. From the speed of his own blood. Twenty years of controlled pulse, and now this: his heart hammering like a fist on a locked door, demanding entry into its own house.

The platform was nearly empty. An old man with a bicycle. A woman with a crate of seedlings bound for the coastal nurseries. A conductor checking a pocket watch with the boredom of a man who had watched a thousand departures and expected nothing from any of them.

Clara set her suitcase down at the yellow line and turned to face him.

"So," she said. "Here we are."

"Here we are." His voice sounded strange to him. Unarmored. "You could still get on. I want to be clear — I said what I said, and I meant it, and it doesn't come with conditions. If you get on that train, I will drive home and I will not pretend it didn't matter. But I won't stand here and tell you it's enough, because I don't know what's enough. I've never done this. I don't have a ledger for it."

"No," she agreed. "You really don't."

"I'm asking," he said, and the word came out of him like a tooth coming out — painful, relieving, leaving a strange new space behind it. "God help me. I'm actually asking. Stay. Not for the vineyard, not for the cooperative, not because Julian needs your expertise on rootstock. Stay because I am better in a house where you are. Stay because I burned my debt schedule in the fireplace two nights ago and laughed while it went up and I don't recognize myself and I think — I think I like who's underneath. Stay because staying is the one thing I have never asked anyone for, and I am asking you, here, in front of this train and this conductor and God and everyone."

The old man with the bicycle smiled into his collar. The conductor consulted his watch again.

Clara looked at him for a long time. The wind came off the water and moved her hair loose from its pins, one strand at a time, until she looked like the girl on the Ashford platform, except older and harder-won and infinitely more real.

"Ask me again," she said softly. "In the spring. When the glasshouse is rebuilt. Ask me when there's something to stay *for*, not just something to run from. Because if I step back from this train right now, Edmund, part of me will always wonder if I stayed out of love or out of momentum, and I have made decisions out of momentum before, and it cost me eleven years." She stepped closer, close enough that he could see the wind-bright tears she wasn't letting fall. "But hear this, and take it with you: I am not deciding today. I am deferring. There's a difference, and the difference is that I'm coming back. The next train after thaw, I'll be on it. Whether there's a glasshouse or a ruin. Whether you ask me or lose your nerve again. I'm coming back, and then you can ask me properly, and I might even answer."

The whistle blew — long, impatient, indifferent to the affairs of hearts.

"That's my train," she said.

"I know."

She picked up the suitcase. Set it down again. Then she kissed him — quick and fierce, right there on the platform, in front of the bicycle man and the seedlings and the whole indifferent machinery of departure — and stepped back, and handed him the suitcase instead of boarding with it.

"Hold this," she said. "So you have a reason to be holding something when I come back."

The doors hissed open. She climbed aboard. Through the grimy window he watched her find a seat, watched her press her palm flat against the glass, and he pressed his own palm up to meet it, and then the train pulled away down the single shining line of track, taking his heart eastward at forty miles an hour, and he stood on the empty platform holding her suitcase and laughing — actually laughing, alone, in public, at nothing and everything — because for the first time in twenty years, letting something leave didn't feel like loss.

It felt like a promise with a return date.

He was still standing there when he heard footsteps behind him on the boards, and turned, and found Julian Marsh walking up from the gatehouse road with his hands in his pockets and an expression Edmund had never seen on that face before — not mockery, not rivalry, but something almost shy.

"Missed it, did she?" Julian said.

"No," Edmund said, hugging the suitcase to his chest like a fool, like a man, like a partner in something not yet written. "She'll be on the next one. We both will."
