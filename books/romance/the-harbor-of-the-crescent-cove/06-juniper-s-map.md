## 6. Juniper's Map

The thing about Dad's bookshop is that it's full of everybody's stories except ours.

Juniper Brightwater had figured that out by October. She'd counted, actually, because counting was something she was good at, like Dad was good at words and the harbor lady was good at shouting. There were 4,112 books in Brightwater Books, give or take the ones Dad hadn't reshelved yet, and every single one of them had people in it who belonged to somebody. Kids in books had grandmothers who baked things and uncles who owned boats and cousins who showed up at the door during storms. Juniper had a dad, and a cat named Biscuit who mostly ignored her, and a bookshop that smelled like paper and the lavender spray Dad used when he was nervous.

That was it. That was the whole family.

She'd tried to explain it to Dad once, back in September, and he'd gotten the look — the one where his face went smooth like a pond with nothing thrown in it — and said, "We've got each other, Junie-bird. That's not nothing." And it wasn't nothing. She knew it wasn't nothing. Mrs. Okafor at school had a dad who was gone in the worse way, the forever way, and Juniper had cried about that for a whole evening and made Dad a card that said SORRY ABOUT DADS in wobbly letters. So she knew. Having Dad was a big something.

But it was only one something. And some somethings you couldn't make cards for.

* * *

It was a Tuesday, which was the day Dad let her stay in the shop after school because his helper, Mr. Aldous, came in to fix the register that had been broken since before Juniper was born, probably. Juniper sat in her spot — the big armchair by the window with the blanket Dad had knitted wrong, all lumpy on one side, which made it better — and she watched people through the glass.

The cove in October was a town getting ready to hold its breath. Dad had said that at dinner. *Off-season's coming, Junie-bird. Town'll go quiet as a held breath.* She liked that. She wrote it down in her notebook, because Dad said writers stole good sentences from everywhere and that was legal.

Through the window she watched the harbor lady walk past.

The harbor lady's real name was Ms. Vega, and she was the boss of the whole water, which was the best job Juniper had ever heard of. She wore a jacket with a badge on it and she walked like the sidewalk was a boat she was steering. Everybody at school said she was scary. Marcus Pell said she'd once made a fisherman cry. But Juniper had seen her do a secret thing: two weeks ago, on the dock, when she thought nobody was looking, Ms. Vega had picked up a seagull that was hurt and held it against her jacket, all gentle, like it was a baby, and carried it somewhere. Juniper had watched the whole thing with her face pressed to the shop glass. People who were secretly gentle with seagulls were not actually scary. They were just carrying something heavy. Dad carried heavy things too — she could tell by how he stood, like the top of him was holding up the bottom of him.

The harbor lady walked past the bookshop without looking at it. She never looked at it. Juniper had a theory about that, but theories needed evidence, and Dad always said don't guess, go find out.

Then, further down, near the end of the street where the houses got small and crooked, Juniper saw Uncle Theo.

He wasn't really her uncle. That was the thing about the cove — everybody was somebody's uncle. Uncle Theo was Ms. Vega's brother, and he lived in the yellow house with the garden that was going wild, and he used to come into the shop every Saturday and buy a paperback and say maybe ten words, and Dad would say *good to see you, Theo* and mean it, and then one day about a year ago he stopped coming. Dad said he was "at the care facility now," said it in his smooth-pond voice, and Juniper knew that meant it was one of the heavy things, so she didn't ask. But she missed him. Uncle Theo had once shown her how to fold a paper boat that actually floated, and he'd said, *your dad's got the writing hands, you know. Whole family of makers.* Nobody had ever said Juniper was part of a family of makers before. She'd kept the paper boat on her windowsill until it got soft and then she'd kept the soft boat anyway, in her treasure box.

She watched him being wheeled along the boardwalk by a nurse, his face turned toward the water. Even from the shop she could tell he was looking at the harbor the way Dad looked at the harbor — like it owed him an apology.

Juniper looked down at her notebook. Then she looked at the crayons in her backpack, the big box, the one with the sharpener built into the side.

She knew what she had to make.

* * *

Maps were serious business. Juniper had checked out three atlases from the library and one book about pirate charts, and she'd studied how real mapmakers did it: the water got wiggly lines, the land got names in fancy letters, and important things got stars. So she spread out on the shop floor, behind the poetry shelf where nobody browsed in October, and she drew the cove the way it actually was — the hook of the harbor like a crescent moon, because that was the town's whole name and Dad said names mattered — and then she got to work on the important part.

She drew the bookshop first, because it was the middle of everything. She drew Dad in the doorway, with his glasses and his apron and his hair doing the thing where it looked like he'd argued with a pillow. Under him she wrote, in her best fancy letters: **DAD. KEEPS ALL THE STORIES. WORRIES A LOT.**

Then the harbor. She drew the docks, all the fingers of wood reaching into the water, and she drew the harbormaster's office with its flag, and she drew Ms. Vega standing on the end of the pier with her badge jacket, and under her: **HARBOR LADY. BOSS OF THE WATER. SECRETLY NICE TO SEAGULLS. SHOULD BE FAMILY.**

She stopped. Her crayon hovered. Should she write that? That people *should be* family?

Juniper thought about it the way she thought about everything, which was thoroughly. Here was the truth, and the truth was the whole reason for the map: a family wasn't just who you got. She'd learned that from books. In books, families collected people — the orphan got the lighthouse keeper, the lonely girl got the whole crew of the ship, and by the last chapter everybody was somebody's. Nobody in books ever said, *sorry, we're full.* Families were like harbors. There was room for more boats. That was the entire point of a harbor.

So she kept the words. SHOULD BE FAMILY. And she drew a star next to it, a big gold one, because important things got stars.

She worked until the shop lights went dim and the window went from blue to black, and Mr. Aldous's radio played the station with all the static, and Dad's footsteps went up and down the aisles doing his closing rounds. She drew Mrs. Okafor from school, who saved her the good seat at lunch. She drew Mr. Aldous, who knew everything about boats and nothing about registers. She drew the baker lady who gave her the burnt croissants for free because "burnt is just extra flavor, sweetheart." She drew the librarian, and the nurse who pushed Uncle Theo's chair, and even Biscuit the cat, labeled **CAT. COWARD. LOVES US ANYWAY.**

And at the yellow house at the end of the crooked street, she drew a small figure in a garden, and under it: **UNCLE THEO. TEACHES PAPER BOATS. REMEMBERS DAD'S BOOKS. QUIET NOW. SHOULD BE FAMILY TOO.**

Then, because the map was true and maps had to be true or they were just pictures, she drew one more thing. In the corner, out past the harbor mouth where the water got deep and dark on the page, she drew a little boat with a tiny person in it, and she didn't label it, because she didn't know its name. She just knew there was a piece of their family out there somewhere — a grandmother maybe, or a mom, or whoever it was that made Dad go smooth-pond-faced whenever the harbor bell rang. She'd asked once, a long time ago, before the cove, and Dad had said *some stories are still being written, Junie-bird,* which was a writer's way of saying *not yet.*

She wrote in the corner of the map, in fancy letters: **THE HARBOR OF THE CRESCENT COVE. WHERE EVERYBODY WHO SHOULD BE FAMILY CAN COME IN.**

Then she folded it up, because it was a secret the way wishes were secrets — if you told them too soon they got embarrassed and died — and she put it in her backpack, and she went to help Dad turn off the lamps.

* * *

The map lived in her backpack for four days. That was the plan. You let a wish sit, like dough, like the bread Mrs. Okafor taught them to make in the school kitchen. But wishes in backpacks got creased, and on Friday morning Juniper pulled it out at the kitchen table to smooth the folds, and that was the exact moment Dad came down the stairs.

She saw it happen in slow motion, the way things happened in the good books. Dad had his coffee mug. Dad had his glasses halfway on. Dad said, "Junie-bird, have you seen my — " and then he stopped, because his eyes had landed on the map, on the fancy letters, on the stars.

"Dad, it's not done — "

But he'd already picked it up. He'd already read it. She watched his eyes go down the page the way people read in books, fast and then slow and then fast again, and she watched his face do something she'd never seen it do. It didn't go smooth. It went the opposite of smooth. It went all crinkled and wet at the same time, like he was smiling and it hurt.

"Juniper," he said, and his voice came out funny, like it had a rock in it. "What is this?"

"It's a map." She said it like it was obvious, because it was. "Of the cove. Of who should be family."

He sat down across from her. He was still holding it with both hands, careful, like it was one of the old books in the glass case. His thumb was resting right on the star next to HARBOR LADY.

"The harbor lady," he said. "Ms. Vega."

"She's secretly nice to seagulls. I saw her."

"You saw her."

"Two weeks ago. On the dock. She carried it like a baby." Juniper leaned forward, because this was the evidence part of the theory. "Dad, people who are mean aren't nice to seagulls. I've done the research."

Dad made a sound that was half laugh and half something else, and he pressed the back of his hand against his mouth for a second, and Juniper realized with a jolt that he was doing the thing she did when she was trying not to cry at sad chapters — and that made her chest go tight and hot, because dads weren't supposed to do that at maps. Maps were supposed to make people happy.

"Uncle Theo teaches paper boats," Dad read quietly. "Remembers Dad's books." He looked up. His glasses were fogged at the edges, which happened when the kitchen got steamy, but the kitchen wasn't steamy. "Junie. Why'd you make this?"

And there it was — the question, the real one, the one the whole map was the answer to — and Juniper found that her throat had gone small. She looked at her hands. She looked at the window, where the harbor was doing its morning sparkle like nothing was ever wrong anywhere.

"Because in books," she said, "nobody's family is just one person. And ours is just you. And you're the best one." She said it fast, before it could get embarrassed. "But there's all this room, Dad. A harbor has room. That's the whole point of a harbor. And the harbor lady walks past our shop every single day and doesn't look at it, and Uncle Theo stopped coming on Saturdays, and I didn't even do anything wrong, so I don't know why everybody's so far away, and I thought if I drew it all with stars, maybe — " She ran out of air. "Maybe the map could make it true. Like in books."

The kitchen was very quiet. Down the street, faintly, the harbor bell rang, and she saw Dad's shoulders do their tiny flinch — the one he thought she didn't know about, the one he did every single time, like the bell was a hand reaching out of the water for him.

Then Dad got up, and she thought he was going to make more coffee, escaping, but he didn't. He came around the table and he knelt down next to her chair, which put his face level with hers, glasses crooked, eyes still shiny, and he said, "Juniper Brightwater, you are the bravest person in this whole cove, and I am including the harbormaster."

"She'd win," Juniper said.

"She'd lose," Dad said, and he hugged her, and he held on a long time, longer than dads usually held on at breakfast, and Juniper held on back and felt something pass between them that didn't have words yet, the way some chapters don't.

When he let go, he smoothed the map out flat on the table between them, right next to the jam, and he looked at it for a long time — at the harbor lady with her star, at Uncle Theo in his wild garden, at the little unnamed boat in the dark corner water.

"Tell you what," he said finally, and his voice was almost steady, almost his reading-aloud voice. "Some of these people on your map — they're carrying heavy things. Heavy things from before we even came here. And I haven't been brave enough to help them carry." His jaw did a thing. "But you're right about harbors. There's room. There's supposed to be room."

"So we can invite them?"

Dad opened his mouth. And before any answer came out — before yes, before *not yet,* before any smooth-pond anything — there was a knock at the shop's front door below, sharp and official, three raps like a gavel, and through the floorboards they both heard the bell over the door jangle anyway, because some people didn't wait to be let in.

Dad stood up. Through the kitchen doorway, down past the stairs, Juniper could see the front of the shop — and she could see, through the glass, a figure in a badge jacket standing at the counter, posting a notice on the community board, her jaw set like a cleat in a storm.

The harbor lady. In their shop. For the first time in a year.

Juniper looked at the map. She looked at the star. She looked at her dad, who had gone very still on the stairs, holding a map full of wishes against his chest like a shield.

"Go on," she whispered, and pushed the star side of the map toward him, like a good-luck charm.

Dad took a breath, tucked the map gently onto the bookshelf behind her — safe, for now, like all the best unfinished stories — and went down to meet the water.
