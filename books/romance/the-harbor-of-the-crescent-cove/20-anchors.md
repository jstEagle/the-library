## 20. Anchors

Marisol Vega had faced down forty-knot gales with steadier hands than the ones currently strangling a dish towel in her own kitchen.

It was Saturday, and she was going on a date.

The word sat in her mouth like a fishhook. She was forty-one years old, she ran the busiest harbor on this stretch of coast, she had pulled drowning men out of black water with her bare hands—and she could not decide what to wear without feeling like a cadet before her first inspection.

She'd laid out three options on her bed an hour ago and hated all of them. The navy dress said *trying too hard*. The flannel and jeans said *I don't care*, which was a lie so transparent it embarrassed her. In the end she'd compromised: the good jeans, the soft gray sweater Theo had given her two Christmases ago, boots that could survive a dock if a dock demanded surviving. Because a dock would demand it. A dock always demanded it. That was the whole problem with dating a man whose life was anchored to hers by geography.

*Go slowly,* they'd agreed, three nights ago, standing in the bookshop after everything had been said that needed saying—after the fury and the tears and the long, shaking silence where forgiveness finally found its footing. *Slowly,* Wren had repeated, like a man testing ice, and she'd laughed because he looked terrified, and because she was too, and because terror shared is at least half as heavy.

So: dinner. At the Anchor Line, the one restaurant in town with tablecloths, because Juniper Brightwater had staged a full intervention in the shop that morning, hands on hips, eight years old and merciless.

"You can't just keep *talking* to each other," she'd informed them both while shelving picture books with the authority of a foreman. "Talking isn't a date. My friend Sadie's mom says you have to *go somewhere*."

"Juniper," Wren had started, ears going pink.

"I'm not finished," she'd said, and honestly, Marisol had fallen a little bit in love right there. "You have to go somewhere and sit across from each other and be nervous. That's what makes it real." She'd paused, then added, with devastating gentleness, "You're allowed to be happy, Dad. You know that, right?"

Marisol had looked away fast, blinking hard at a shelf of maritime histories, because there are some things a harbormaster cannot be seen surviving in public.

Now she checked her phone for the fourth time in ten minutes. No cancellations. No emergencies. Just a text from Wren sent twenty minutes ago:

*I'm outside. I've been outside for six minutes because I didn't want to seem eager, but I also didn't want to seem late, and now I'm just a man loitering near your truck. Please rescue me.*

She laughed out loud, alone in her kitchen, and grabbed her coat.

* * *

He was leaning against the harbor fence when she came out, wearing a jacket she suspected he'd bought this week—the tags were gone but the creases weren't—and holding a paper bag that smelled, unmistakably, of the bookshop's cinnamon candles.

"Peace offering," he said, offering it. "For the years of hostility. It seemed insufficient, so I also brought—" he patted his jacket pocket, "—a list of conversation topics in case we run out of things to say, which, statistically, has never once happened to us."

"We've never once run out of things to say," she agreed, taking the candle. "We've just sometimes preferred shouting."

"The Anchor Line will be disappointed. They bill themselves as a quiet establishment."

They walked the three blocks along the water because neither of them suggested otherwise and both of them wanted to. The evening was doing its best October impression: copper light on the swells, gulls arguing over something in the shallows, the ferry *Cormorant* riding at her moorings with her running lights just coming on. Marisol found herself narrating without meaning to—pointing out which skiff belonged to the Delgados, which mooring buoy she'd replaced herself last spring, where the channel marker leaned two degrees off true and drove her crazy every single morning.

"You know," Wren said, "most people on a first date talk about themselves."

"This *is* me talking about myself. The harbor is me. There's no separating us; ask my exes."

"Your exes," he said carefully, "must have been idiots."

"One of them was. One of them was just tired." She glanced sideways at him. "You?"

"My ex-wife wasn't tired. She was *right*, which is worse." He said it lightly, but she heard the old flinch under it—the same flinch she'd spent three years mistaking for arrogance. "I disappeared into a manuscript and called it devotion to craft. She called it abandonment. The courts sided with her, mostly."

"And now?"

"Now I set an alarm to stop writing. It feels absurd. It also works." He smiled, small and real. "Juniper made me a chart. Green days and red days. We're on eleven green."

"Eleven green," Marisol repeated, and something in her chest went warm and unsteady. This was the danger of him, she was discovering—not the sharp tongue or the stubbornness, but this: the way he wore his repair work openly, like a man rebuilding a boat in his front yard, letting everyone watch the seams.

They reached the Anchor Line and were seated by a window, and for the first ten minutes it was exactly as awkward as Juniper had promised. They ordered wine and then both reached for the bread basket and then both withdrew their hands and then both laughed at themselves, and the laughter broke something loose, and after that it was easy—or would have been, if Marisol's radio hadn't crackled on the table between them.

*"Harbormaster, come in."*

She closed her eyes. "I turned it down to two."

"It's still audible at two."

*"Harbormaster, we got a situation at slip fourteen. The Petrel's lines are chafing and Mr. Okafor can't get his fenders reset, his shoulder's acting up again—"*

Wren was already signaling the waitress for the check. "Go," he said. "I'll walk you."

"You'll ruin your jacket."

"It's a jacket, Marisol. It signed up for this when I bought it."

* * *

Slip fourteen was chaos in miniature: the *Petrel*, a thirty-foot sloop, surging against lines worn nearly through, old Mr. Okafor hopping uselessly on the finger pier with his arm in a sling, and dusk coming down fast. Marisol was moving before her boots hit the dock—radioing her deckhand Ruiz, calling for chafe gear, reading the surge pattern off the water the way other people read headlines.

What she did not expect was Wren rolling up his sleeves, stepping onto the pitching pier, and holding out his hands for the fender line like a man who'd done it before.

"You've handled lines," she said, mid-task, not looking up.

"I owned a sailboat for four disastrous years. I named her *Metaphor*, which tells you everything about why the marriage failed."

She barked a laugh that startled a gull off a piling. "You did not."

"I did. She sank, incidentally. Off Point Reyes. Very poetic, very expensive."

Together they worked the fenders into position, Wren taking direction without bristling—a novelty she noted and filed away—while Ruiz arrived and doubled the chafed lines with proper splices. Twenty minutes, start to finish. When it was done, the *Petrel* rode quiet and easy, and Mr. Okafor was pressing five dollars into Wren's hand ("for the young lady's dinner, tell her it's from the harbor") and Wren was laughing, actually laughing, salt spray in his hair, and Marisol stood there with a coil of line over her shoulder and thought: *oh.* Not the dramatic *oh* of novels. A quieter one. The *oh* of recognizing a sound you'd forgotten the harbor used to make.

They ate late, back at the Anchor Line, their window table surrendered and a corner booth granted instead. Their dinners arrived cold-ish and neither cared. Somewhere between the main course and coffee, the conversation slid past the guarded shallows into deeper water, the way conversations do when both people have already seen each other shipwrecked.

"I keep waiting for it to feel strange," Wren admitted, turning his cup. "That it's you. That we wrote all those letters to each other while actively disliking each other."

"Do you still dislike me?"

"I never disliked you." He said it simply. "I feared you. There's a difference. You were the voice of everything I believed about myself. Hating you would have been easy. Believing you—that was the injury."

Marisol looked at him for a long moment. "Theo asked me yesterday if I'd forgiven myself yet. For three years of blaming the wrong man. I told him forgiveness isn't a door you walk through, it's a tide. It comes in slowly. Some days it goes back out."

"That's going in a book."

"Don't you dare."

"Too late. Already composing. Chapter one: *The Harbormaster Speaks in Tides.*"

She threw a piece of bread at him. He caught it and ate it, insufferable, grinning, and she thought: *slowly. We agreed slowly.* And then she thought: *but not glacially,* and reached across the table and took his hand, and held it there on the scarred wood between the salt shaker and the candle, and neither of them said anything about it, and neither let go until the waitress brought the check.

Walking home along the fence line, the *Cormorant*'s lights green and red against the dark water, they negotiated terms like the reasonable, battle-scarred professionals they were.

"Dinner again next week," Wren proposed.

"And Sundays. Juniper wants to teach me to bake the lemon thing. She says I need 'supervised practice.'"

"She's been planning this since September. There's a binder."

"There's a *binder?*"

"Color-coded. She inherited her organizational instincts from her mother, thank God."

They stopped at the corner where their routes divided. The pause stretched—not awkward this time, just careful, two people who knew exactly how much weight a moment could hold. Wren leaned in and kissed her cheek, brief and warm as a struck match.

"Slowly," he murmured.

"Slowly," she agreed, and walked home floating slightly above the pavement, and did not radio anyone about it, even when Ruiz texted asking why she was smiling at her phone like a teenager.

* * *

Sunday afternoon, Marisol came by the bookshop to collect Juniper for baking supervision and found the girl cross-legged on the floor behind the biography section, tongue between her teeth, drawing.

Not drawing—*revising*. Spread out around her was the map. Marisol recognized it instantly; Wren had told her about it weeks ago, during the storm night, his voice cracking on the details: the cove rendered in colored pencil, every person Juniper wished were family marked with a little symbol. A lighthouse for Mrs. Okonkwo next door. An anchor for Theo. A wave for the harbor lady.

But the map had changed. Marisol crouched down to look, and her throat went tight.

The harbor lady's wave had been redrawn—now it was a lighthouse too, connected to the bookshop by a dotted line labeled *walks here sometimes*. And beside the bookshop itself, new symbols crowded in: a second anchor, smaller, drawn twice because the first attempt had been erased. A lemon. A stack of books with a heart on top. And in the corner, in Juniper's careful block letters, a legend that read:

*FAMILY (CURRENT)* — and beneath it, a longer list than the page could comfortably hold.

"You added people," Marisol said.

Juniper looked up, entirely unembarrassed. "The map's supposed to change. That's how maps work. If nothing changed it would just be a picture." She pointed her pencil at a smudge near the harbor. "I had to erase the storm cloud I drew over your house. From when you were mean. It didn't fit anymore."

"Juniper," Wren said from the counter, mortified.

"What? It's accurate. Maps have to be accurate." She rolled up the map with great ceremony and handed it to Marisol. "You can borrow it. But I want it back. It's a working document."

Marisol carried it home that night and pinned it above her desk in the harbormaster's office, where her deputies pretended not to notice it and where she looked at it more often than she looked at the tide tables.

* * *

Moving day for Theo fell on the last mild weekend of October.

It took three trips in Marisol's truck and one in Wren's ancient Volvo, and it took Theo's opinion on every box, because Theo Vega had opinions the way the sea had salt. The apartment above the bookshop was small—one bedroom, a kitchen with a view of the masts, stairs that groaned—but when they wheeled him in on the first morning, he was silent for a long moment, looking out the window at the harbor he'd worked his whole life and could only visit now.

"Well," he said finally. "At least the commute's short."

They'd built the ramps together, Wren and Marisol, one long Saturday of measuring and sawing and arguing amiably about load ratings, and when it was done Theo had tested it himself, refusing help, gripping the rails, making it to the top on his own power while Juniper cheered from below like it was a championship final.

His room filled slowly with the artifacts of a life: the model schooners, the framed photos of crews long dispersed, the battered copy of *The Harbor of the Crescent Cove*—Wren's first novel, the one Theo had read four times and annotated in pencil like a harbormaster grading charts. Wren spotted it on the nightstand and went very still.

"You kept that?"

"Kept it?" Theo snorted from his chair by the window. "Son, I defended it. Whole marina book club said the ending was too sad. I told them they didn't know anything about endings." He fixed Wren with the look that had once kept entire decks in line. "This one better not be too sad."

"I'll do my best."

"You'll do better than your best. You've got material now." He nodded at Marisol, at the sounds of Juniper thundering up the stairs to show Grandpa Theo her binder of baking recipes. "Look at all this. Nobody writes a sad ending with *that* downstairs."

That evening, the four of them ate takeout noodles at the big table in the shop's back room, surrounded by boxes not yet unpacked, and Juniper insisted on a toast with apple juice in teacups. "To family current," she announced, mangling her own legend, "and to everybody being on the map now."

"To anchors," Theo said, clinking her cup. "Good ones drag a little. That's how you know they're holding."

Later, when Juniper had been put to bed and Theo had wheeled himself to his window to watch the night ferries, Marisol and Wren stood in the darkened shop among the shelves, not quite touching, looking up at the faint glow under the apartment door.

"He's going to rearrange your inventory within a week," Marisol said.

"He's going to rearrange my *life* within a week. He already told me chapter three of the new book is structurally cowardly."

"Is it?"

"...Yes."

She laughed softly, and in the dark his hand found hers, and they stood there a while, listening to the building settle around its newest resident, to the harbor breathing outside, to the whole improbable architecture of a family assembling itself out of wreckage and second chances.

"Slowly," Wren said, but there was no fear in it anymore. Just wonder.

"Slowly," Marisol agreed—and then, because honesty was the whole point of them now, she added, "but I'm keeping the toothbrush question on the table for spring."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, they heard Theo's voice, dry as driftwood, carrying perfectly in the old building:

"*Spring's too slow. I give it till February.*"

And from Juniper's room, half-asleep and triumphant: "*I said February! I said it FIRST!*"

Marisol pressed her face into Wren's shoulder to muffle her laughter, and upstairs the map hung on the office wall with its dotted lines still growing, and out in the cove the *Cormorant* sounded her horn for the last crossing of the night—two notes, steady and sure, like a heartbeat that had finally found its rhythm.

But three blocks away, in the darkened harbormaster's office, the printer was humming to itself, spitting out the season's first official notice, and the words on the page would set the whole town talking by morning:

**CRESCENT COVE WINTER LETTERS — SECOND SEASON. PAIRINGS OPEN NOW. THIS YEAR, YOU MAY REQUEST YOUR PARTNER BY NAME.**
