## 3. A Stranger at the Counter

Elena Marsh had been driving for two hours before she admitted to herself that she was not lost.

The GPS on her phone had given up trying to reroute her twenty minutes ago, its patient voice suggesting she make a legal U-turn in a place where the road had narrowed to a single gravel lane hemmed in by blackberry bramble. She had silenced it. Now the phone sat face-down in the passenger seat like a scolded child, and the harbor opened up below her in a long silver crescent, and she understood — with the peculiar clarity that sometimes arrives only when you have done something slightly irresponsible — that she had been running all morning.

Her father's voice was still in her ear, even with forty miles between them. *The waterfront package closes by fall, Elena. I need my daughter in that room when it does.* Not his lawyer. Not his vice president of acquisitions. His daughter, because Richard Marsh had learned long ago that a soft face opened doors that contracts could not, and Elena had learned to hate how well she fit the description.

She pulled into Millbrook just past eleven, into a space at the end of Main Street where the asphalt gave way to old cobblestones worn smooth as river stones. The town looked like something from a photograph her grandmother might have kept in a drawer — a crooked row of salt-bleached storefronts, a church steeple, fishing boats knocking gently against a pier that needed paint. A hand-lettered sign outside the hardware store advertised a chowder supper for the volunteer fire department. Someone had tied a ribbon around the lamppost in front of what appeared to be the only bookshop for fifty miles.

Elena sat in the car with her hands still on the wheel.

She had told her assistant she was taking the day to review site surveys. She had told her father she'd be reachable. She had told herself, somewhere on the coastal highway with the windows down and the wind ruining her hair, that this was reconnaissance — that a good acquisitions director walked the ground before she drew the lines on the map. But the truth was simpler and more shameful: she had seen the name of the town on a list of parcels, and something in her chest had clenched, and she had gotten in her car before she could talk herself out of it.

*Millbrook.* Even the name sounded like an apology.

She got out. The air came off the water smelling of salt and diesel and, faintly, of frying onions from somewhere down the street. Her heels were wrong for these cobblestones; she could feel every stone through the thin soles, and after half a block she stopped caring. A man mending a net on the pier nodded at her without curiosity. A dog asleep in a doorway did not lift its head. No one knew her here. No one was watching.

It was the closest thing to freedom she had felt in years, and it frightened her how small a thing it took.

* * *

The bell over the door of the bookshop made a sound like a spoon striking a teacup — bright, brief, a little out of tune.

Inside, the light came down in dusty gold shafts from a skylight nobody had cleaned in a decade, and the shelves rose higher than any chain store would ever allow, leaning toward each other overhead like old friends conspiring. The smell hit her first: paper and glue and cedar and something older underneath, the way a church smells, or a sea chest. Elena stood just inside the door and breathed it in and felt something in her shoulders unknot that she hadn't known was knotted.

"You can browse as long as you like," said a voice from behind the counter, "but fair warning — the poetry section is load-bearing."

He was shelving with his back to her, a stack of hardcovers hooked in one arm, and he was not what she expected. She had imagined, without quite meaning to, someone ancient and bent, a keeper of dust. Instead he was perhaps her own age or a few years older, dark-haired, sleeves rolled to the forearm, with the kind of face that photographs badly and lives beautifully — a little worn at the edges, a little amused at the eyes. He turned, saw her, and did the thing people always did when they looked at her: he noticed the coat first. The cashmere. The watch. She watched him revise whatever he'd assumed about her, and she watched him decide, generously, not to care.

"Load-bearing," she repeated. "Is that a joke or a structural assessment?"

"Both. That shelf hasn't been moved since 1987 and I'm fairly sure the building would notice." He set the last book in place and came around the counter, wiping his hands on a rag that had once been white. "Tourist?"

And there it was — the question, easy and innocent, and the moment where she could have said anything. *I'm here about the waterfront. My father's company is buying your harbor out from under you, shelf by shelf, boat by boat, and I've read the appraisal files and I know exactly what your building is worth per square foot.* She could have said it. She should have. It would have been cruel and honest and it would have ended everything before it began, which was perhaps why the other answer came out instead.

"Just passing through," Elena said. "I got off the highway."

"Everyone does, eventually. Or no one does, and then they stay thirty years." He smiled. "Can I point you anywhere? We're organized according to a system only I understand, which is either charming or infuriating depending on how much time you have."

"I have—" She glanced at her phone out of habit and then, deliberately, didn't look at it. "Time."

"Then start anywhere. That's the whole secret of the place."

She drifted. That was the only word for it — she drifted the way she had not drifted since childhood, without agenda, without a quarterly objective, letting her fingers trail along the spines. The shop was deeper than it looked from the street, rooms opening into rooms, a ladder on brass rails, a window seat in the back with a cushion gone soft as bread in the middle. There was a children's corner with a rug printed like a road map and a crayon drawing taped to the wall at knee height — a lighthouse drawn with furious purple enthusiasm, signed *JUNE, AGE 6* in letters that marched downhill. Elena found herself smiling at it before she could stop.

"That's the artist herself," the bookseller said, appearing beside her with two cups of coffee she had neither ordered nor expected. "She does commissions. Crayon only. Very exclusive."

"She's good," Elena said, and meant it. "The lighthouse is accurate, too. Point Marsh?"

His eyebrows went up. "You know the coast."

"My family used to summer north of here. A long time ago." Another lie, or close enough to one — the Marshes summered nowhere; they acquired. But she had studied these charts until she could draw them from memory, and apparently some part of her had loved them even while filing them under *assets*. "Point Marsh is my favorite. It's the best light on the whole seaboard around sunset."

"It is." He said it quietly, the way people say things they've thought many times. "My wife used to say the same thing."

The past tense landed in the room between them, and Elena heard it, and he saw her hear it, and for a moment neither of them reached for the usual bandages. He just looked at her — a frank, tired, unguarded look, the look of a man who had stopped performing for strangers — and then handed her the coffee.

"It's terrible," he warned. "The machine is older than the poetry shelf. But it's hot, and it's free, and those are the two pillars of hospitality."

She took it. It was terrible. It was also the best coffee she'd had in a year, because no one had brought it to her wanting anything, and because the cup was mismatched china with a chip on the rim, and because he drank from its twin without ceremony, leaning against the shelf beside her as though they had done this a hundred times.

"Casper," he said. "Casper Voss."

"Elena." Just Elena. The omission slid out of her so smoothly it barely registered as a choice, and that was the part that would keep her awake later — not that she lied, but how easily the lie wore the shape of truth. *Just passing through. Just Elena. Just a woman in a bookshop.*

"Pleasure, Just Elena." He deadpanned it so dryly that she laughed — actually laughed, a real one, surprising them both — and something shifted in the dusty gold light of the room, some small tectonic adjustment, the feeling of being *seen* rather than assessed. When had anyone last looked at her without calculating? Her father calculated. The board calculated. Even the men who flirted with her at fundraisers were, in some ledger she couldn't see, entering figures. Casper Voss looked at her the way he presumably looked at a book he hadn't read yet: with patience, and no assumptions, and a willingness to be surprised.

"What do you read?" he asked. "Actually — don't tell me. Let me guess." He studied her with theatrical seriousness, coffee cup raised like a jeweler's loupe. "You're going to say literary fiction, because everyone says literary fiction, but you're lying. You want a mystery. Something with a body in the first ten pages and a detective who's bad at relationships."

"That's insulting."

"But accurate?"

"...Accurate," she admitted, and he grinned like he'd won a bet, and led her three rooms deep into the shop to a shelf crammed with paperbacks whose covers promised murder in tasteful seaside villages. "How did you know that?"

"People tell me things in here. It's the coffee. Terrible coffee makes people honest." He pulled a battered paperback free and pressed it into her hands — a detective with a broken marriage stalking a killer through a rain-soaked harbor town, of all things. "This one. The plot's nonsense but the author knows what grief smells like. Salt and old wood. Nobody else gets that right."

Elena looked down at the book and felt the strangest sensation, as if he had handed her something other than a five-dollar paperback — as if he had handed her a mirror with the glass angled just slightly away from her face, showing her a woman she might have been in a life she hadn't lived. A woman who wandered into towns by accident. Who drank bad coffee. Whose father was nobody's empire.

"How much do I owe you?"

"For that? Four ninety-five. For the coffee, nothing. For the advice, you owe me a report — come back and tell me if I was right about the ending."

*Come back.* He said it lightly, the way shopkeepers say it to everyone, a coin tossed to every customer. But standing there in the paper-smelling gold light with the detective novel in her hands, Elena felt the words land somewhere real, and she hated — briefly, fiercely — the knowledge sitting in her briefcase in the car, the parcel maps and the phase-one schedules and the letterhead that bore her own surname. This shop was on page fourteen of the acquisition summary. Parcel 7-B. *Structure: mixed-use, retail below, residential above. Condition: poor. Sentimental value: n/a.*

*N/a.* As if a thing could be weighed and found to have none.

She paid him. Their fingers touched briefly over the change — four singles and a nickel, actual coins, she couldn't remember the last time she'd handled coins — and he didn't linger, didn't perform, just rang the till and wished her safe travels with the same unguarded ease as before. At the door she paused with her hand on the bell.

"The poetry shelf," she said. "Which poem is holding up the roof?"

He considered. "'Wild Geese.' Mary Oliver. It's earned its keep."

"You do not tell the water / to be less water," Elena quoted softly, before she could think better of it, and watched something move across his face — surprise, and beneath the surprise, a flicker of recognition, the look of a man seeing a stranger become suddenly, confusingly familiar.

"You're full of contradictions, Just Elena," he said.

"So I'm told." She let the bell ring behind her.

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had turned honey-colored, and the harbor lay flat and shining, and Elena walked to her car carrying the paperback like contraband. She sat behind the wheel and did not start the engine. Through the windshield she could see the whole curve of the town, the boats, the crooked street, the bookshop with its hand-tied ribbon, and she took out her phone at last and looked at it properly: eleven missed notifications, three from her father, one from the project office marked *URGENT — PHASE II TIMELINE*, and a calendar reminder for Monday's stakeholder call where her voice, her reasonable, persuasive, well-bred voice, was scheduled to help close the deal that would turn all of this into condominiums and a marina for people who owned boats they never used.

She opened the paperback instead. The detective was already miserable on page one, walking a beach at dawn, and the author wrote: *The town looked the same, which was the cruelest thing a town could do.*

Elena closed the book. She would never finish it — she knew that with certainty, the way she knew she'd never take up the piano again or call back half the people she owed apologies to. Some things you bought just to hold the possibility of them.

But as she pulled out onto the coast road, leaving the harbor in her mirrors, she caught herself doing something she had not done in years: rehearsing a return. *Come back and tell me if I was right about the ending.* She could come back. She could invent a reason — follow-up research, a site visit, a coffee. She could be Just Elena for another hour, another afternoon, and tell herself the rest of it — the parcels, the phase schedules, the signature pages waiting for names — belonged to some other woman wearing her coat.

Behind her, the bell of the bookshop rang for the next customer, a sound like a spoon against a teacup, bright and brief and a little out of tune, and Elena drove north with the unread novel on the seat beside her and the first lie of many settling over her like something almost gentle.

She did not yet know that the man in the shop had a reunion to survive, a sentence he'd rehearsed for ten years, and a proposition in him that would change everything — or that within a week, she would be the one giving him a reason to use it.
