## 6. The Harbor Road

The morning of the reunion arrived wrapped in fog so thick it seemed the harbor had been erased overnight — as if some careful hand had lifted the water, the boats, the far gray line of the breakwater, and left only a white page behind. Casper stood at the kitchen window above the bookshop with his second cup of coffee going cold in his hand and watched June's school bus dissolve into that whiteness at the end of Harbor Street, its taillights two smudges of red, then nothing.

He had told himself all week that this was a simple thing. A drive up the coast. A party. A woman on his arm who was, by every practical measure, a stranger wearing a borrowed role. He had rehearsed the logistics the way he once rehearsed inventory orders: leave by ten, arrive by noon, stay through dinner, be home before June woke Sunday morning worried about anything more serious than whether Mrs. Albee would let her feed the gulls again.

Simple. Except that his hands were not steady on the coffee cup, and except that when he'd laid out the blue shirt — the one Nora had bought him three birthdays ago, still soft from a decade of washing — he had stood over it on the bed for a full minute, unable to pick it up.

He picked it up now. He put it on. In the mirror, a forty-one-year-old man looked back at him with his father's jaw and his mother's tired eyes, and Casper said to him quietly, the way you'd speak to a nervous child, "It's one afternoon. You can do one afternoon."

* * *

Elena was waiting outside the shop when he came down at quarter past ten, standing under the faded green awning with her hands in the pockets of a linen jacket the color of sea glass. She had dressed down for the occasion — deliberately, he suspected, the way an actor dresses down for a role about ordinary people — but she wore it well, the way she seemed to wear everything, including the lie they had built together out of coffee cups and invented history.

"You're punctual," she said, smiling. "I wasn't sure. Bookshop owners strike me as people who live on their own clock."

"We live on everyone else's clock," Casper said, unlocking the truck. "We just pretend otherwise." He held the passenger door open for her, an old habit from a courtship older than June, and saw something flicker across Elena's face when he did it — surprise, maybe, or the recognition of a courtesy she hadn't expected from a man in a bookstore apron. She climbed in, tucking her skirt beneath her, and the cab filled suddenly with a scent like cold air and something floral underneath, and Casper had to concentrate on the ignition to keep his thoughts where he could see them.

"Fair warning," he said as they pulled onto Harbor Street, the fog thinning ahead of them into a pale promise of sun. "My family is not a small thing. Forty-odd Vosses, give or take the ones who've stopped speaking to each other. My mother will interrogate you within four minutes. My brother Daniel will make at least one joke that isn't funny, and my Aunt Ruth will tell you the story of how I fell off the mill house roof when I was nine."

"The mill house," Elena said. Just that. The word itself, returned to him like a coin placed carefully on a counter.

"You'll hear a lot about it today," Casper said. "Half this town thinks the Voss family *is* the mill house. My great-grandfather ran the gristmill until the shipping trade died, and then we did what Millbrook families do — we stayed, and got poorer, and called it loyalty." He turned north onto the coast road, where the fog was lifting off the water in long ribbons and the harbor opened below them, gray-green and patient, dotted with lobster boats rocking at their moorings. "There's a shortcut along the shore. It adds twenty minutes, but—" He hesitated, surprised by his own impulse. "I always take it. If you don't mind."

"I don't mind," Elena said, and turned her face toward the window, toward the water.

* * *

The coast road climbed and dipped along the headland, and the fog burned away as they drove, until the whole world was blue and gold and glittering. The road narrowed past the shuttered cannery, past the salt marsh where egrets stood in the shallows like punctuation marks, past the little white chapel whose steeple leaned two degrees eastward and had leaned that way since anyone could remember. Casper found himself talking more than he meant to — pointing things out, naming coves, telling stories about boats that had sunk and boats that had almost sunk and one boat, the *Marguerite*, that had sunk twice and refused to learn. Elena laughed at the right places. She asked questions that showed she was actually listening, which unsettled him more than any flattery could have, because he kept catching himself wanting to answer honestly, wanting to give her the true weight of things instead of the polished version.

"So your family reunion," she said, as they rounded the point above Gull Cove. "Is it a happy occasion? Or one of those obligations everyone dreads and no one skips?"

Casper considered the question with more care than it probably deserved. "Both," he said finally. "My mother started it after my father died — twelve years ago now. She said if the family wouldn't gather for joy, we'd gather anyway, and joy would have to catch up. Some years it does." He drummed his fingers on the wheel. "This year it'll be watching me like hawks. Ten years, and I've never brought anyone. Not once. They've stopped asking directly, which means they've started asking indirectly, which is worse."

"And you decided this was the year."

"You decided with me," he reminded her gently. "Over terrible coffee. I hadn't finished the sentence before you said yes. I've been meaning to ask you if you regret it."

Elena was quiet for a moment. Below them, a fishing trawler cut a long white seam across the bay. "No," she said at last, and there was something in her voice he couldn't name — not regret, not quite fear, something adjacent to both. "I don't regret it. I needed—" She stopped. Started again. "My weekends aren't usually my own. This one is. That's worth a few awkward questions from your Aunt Ruth."

"That's the spirit," Casper said. "Ruth only bites on alternate Sundays."

But he heard what she hadn't said, the way you learn to hear silences when you've spent ten years living inside one. *My weekends aren't usually my own.* There was a story there, whole and shadowed, and she had closed the door on it with a smile, and he had let her, because he was a man who understood better than most that some doors stay shut for good reasons.

* * *

He didn't plan to stop at the mill house. It happened the way these things happen — the road curved, the ruin appeared between the pines the way it had appeared a thousand times, gray stone rising out of the bracken above the rocky beach, and his hands simply turned the wheel onto the gravel pull-off before his mind caught up.

"Oh," Elena said softly, as the truck crunched to a stop. "What's this?"

"The mill house," Casper said. His voice came out strange, and he cleared his throat. "Or what's left of it. Come on — I'll show you. It's five minutes, and it's the best view on this coast, and I find I want you to see it before my family gets their hands on you and tells you everything wrong about it."

She followed him up the path through the pines, needles soft underfoot, the smell of resin and salt thick in the air. The ruin opened before them gradually — first the foundation walls, waist-high and furred with moss, then the great broken arch of the millrace where the water had once turned the wheel, then the falls themselves, dropping thirty feet into a black pool that fed the creek down to the harbor. The sound filled the clearing, constant and ancient, a sound that had been here before the town and would be here after.

"It's beautiful," Elena said, and her voice had gone quiet in a way that made him look at her. She was standing at the edge of the foundation, one hand resting on the mossy stone, looking at the falls with an expression he couldn't read — something intense and private, as though the place had spoken to her in a language he didn't know. "People lived here?"

"My great-great-grandfather built it in 1847. Ground grain for the whole coast, once. When the mill died, the family kept the land." He walked to the corner of the foundation where the wall rose highest, where someone — generations of someones — had carved initials into the granite. "We used to come up here when I was a kid. My brothers and I. We'd pretend it was a castle, a ship, a fort. Daniel broke his arm jumping off that wall and swore for years it was my fault." He smiled, but the smile felt fragile, and he knew what was coming, because it always came when he stood in this spot. The place pulled things out of him like tide pulling sand.

"Elena." He turned to face her. The falls roared behind him. "There's a reason I wanted you to see this. And I'm going to say it badly, so bear with me."

She waited. The light came through the pines in slats and lay across her shoulders.

"Ten years ago, in June, I proposed to my wife right here. On that wall, actually — the low part, because I'm not stupid enough to propose on the high part after what happened to Daniel." He heard his own voice steadying itself around the words, the way you steady yourself on a ladder. "Nora and I drove up the coast road on a Sunday, exactly like this. Fog in the morning, sun by noon. She loved this spot. She said it was the only place in Millbrook where you could hear the water working." He looked down at the carved initials — *C.V. + N.V.* — weathered nearly smooth now, but still legible if you knew to look. "She said yes before I finished the sentence. She always said I took too long to get to the point."

"Casper—"

"She died that same year. October. There was an accident on the harbor road, coming home from Portland, and—" The sentence snagged where it always snagged, the place where ten years of rehearsal had worn a groove. He breathed through it. "June was four. She doesn't remember her mother's voice, only stories. And I have spent ten years being the man who runs the bookshop and raises his daughter and never brings anyone to the reunion, and my mother thinks it's grief, and my brother thinks it's stubbornness, and the truth is it's both, and neither, and I don't entirely know what it is. But I wanted you to know why this place matters. Before today. Because today you're going to meet forty people who will tell you I'm a tragedy, and I'd rather you heard it from me, standing where it actually happened, than from them over potato salad."

The silence after was filled only by the falls. Elena had not moved. Her hand was still on the stone wall, and her face — he studied it carefully, because he was beginning to think he was learning to read her — her face had gone very still, very pale beneath the sun-warmth, and there was something in her eyes that looked almost like pain.

"That's why you rehearse a sentence," she said quietly.

"What?"

"In the bookshop. The other night. You said—" She stopped herself, and he watched her decide, visibly, to step back from whatever she'd been about to say. "Never mind. Thank you for telling me. About Nora. About this place." Her thumb moved against the mossy stone, a small unconscious motion, tracing the edge of the granite. "It matters. That you brought me here."

He nodded, throat tight, and let the silence stand, because some silences are honest and deserve to be left alone.

What he could not see — what no one standing in that sunlit clearing could have seen — was the interior landscape of the woman beside him: the blueprint spread across a conference table in Boston eleven months ago, the rendering of a luxury resort complex stamped *MARSH DEVELOPMENT — PHASE II*, and at its center, rendered in tasteful watercolor, the old mill house ruins restored as the resort's "heritage centerpiece," the falls channeled and ticketed, the foundation walls rebuilt as a restaurant terrace. He could not see how precisely she had recognized this place the moment the pines opened — not from photographs, though there had been photographs, but from the drawings themselves, from hours spent studying this exact view through the dispassionate geometry of site plans and setback lines and projected revenue per square foot. He could not see her father's voice in her memory: *That ruin is the crown jewel, Elena. People pay for authenticity. We'll give them the authentic experience.*

And he could not see the moment, there among the moss and the carved initials and the sound of the water working, when Elena Marsh understood exactly what she was doing on this coast road, in this truck, on this man's arm — and chose, for one more hour, one more day, to keep driving anyway.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. She silenced it without looking.

"We should go," she said, and her smile was perfect, and Casper thought only that she must be nervous about the family, and offered her his arm up the path, and did not notice how carefully she avoided touching the wall again on the way out.

* * *

They drove the last stretch in easy talk — or what sounded like easy talk, two voices moving over the deep water of everything unsaid. Elena asked about June; Casper lit up like a lamp and told her about the crayon maps, the gull census, the way his daughter organized her bookshelf by "how the covers feel." Elena told him about growing up in Boston, in careful, edited strokes — the museums, the winters, a boarding school in New Hampshire — and every fact was true and none of them were the truth, and she was startled to find how much it cost her to say even the safe ones, sitting beside a man who had just handed her the most expensive thing he owned.

"There it is," Casper said, cresting the final hill. "Voss Hollow."

Below them, the old farmhouse and its sprawl of outbuildings sat in a fold of green pasture, already crowded with cars, with folding tables, with the small moving figures of a family assembling itself for another year. Smoke rose from a grill. Someone had strung paper lanterns between the maples, absurdly early, the way Aunt Ruth always did. A dog ran loops through the yard for no reason at all.

"They look nice," Elena said. Her hands were folded in her lap, very still.

"They're loud, opinionated, and they will love you by dessert," Casper said. He put the truck in gear and rolled down toward the house. "Just — one thing. Whatever they ask you, however they ask it, you don't owe anyone the whole truth today. Half of it works fine. It's what the rest of us run on."

He meant it as a joke. He heard, too late, how it landed — saw her turn her face to the window, saw her press her lips together against something.

At the bottom of the hill, his mother was already standing on the porch steps, shading her eyes against the sun, watching the truck come down the lane with an expression ten years in the making.

"Well," Casper murmured, killing the engine. "Here we go."

Beside him, Elena Marsh took one breath — deep, deliberate, the breath of a diver at the edge of the board — and reached for the door handle.
