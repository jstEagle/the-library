## 8. What She Didn't Say

The kitchen smelled of cinnamon and burnt sugar, of a hundred Voss reunions layered into the walls like sediment. Elena stood at the counter with a half-empty glass of wine she had no memory of pouring, watching Casper across the room where he was laughing at something his brother had said — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she suspected he rationed carefully and spent only in small rooms with people he trusted.

She should have been happy. That was the strange part. She *was* happy, in a way that frightened her more than any board meeting ever had. Three hours at this table and she had already learned that June slept with one sock on and one off, that Casper's mother called him Cass when she'd had two glasses of wine, that his grief lived in him not as a wound but as a kind of architecture — load-bearing, invisible, holding up everything he built.

And she had learned nothing he could afford for her to know.

Her phone sat in her coat pocket by the door like a stone. She had felt it there all evening, through the toasts and the potato salad and June's fourth request to be lifted up "so I can see over the grown-ups," through the moment under the table when Casper's hand had found hers — not staged, not for anyone's benefit, just found it, the way a hand finds a railing in the dark.

His brother had said the name an hour ago. *Marsh Development.* Said it casually, the way people say the names of weather systems or distant wars — something happening to other people, somewhere else. And Elena had watched Casper's face do a thing she would remember for the rest of her life: it closed. Not all the way. Just enough. A door pulled to but not locked, out of politeness, because this was a family dinner and some doors you didn't open in front of the casserole.

He hadn't known. Sitting beside her, letting her hold his hand, telling his mother they'd met at the shop — he hadn't known who she was.

Or he had, some treacherous voice whispered, and let himself not know it, which was its own kind of lie. But no. No, she had watched his face. He hadn't known.

She slipped out through the side door while the table argued about whether the good china came from Casper's grandmother's side or his father's, a dispute conducted with the passion of people who had never once worried about anything that mattered. The screen door sighed shut behind her. The evening air off the harbor was cool and smelled of salt and diesel and, faintly, of low tide — the smell of Millbrook itself, honest and unglamorous, a town that had stopped pretending to be prosperous sometime before she was born.

She walked down the gravel drive until the porch light was a smudge behind her, then took out the phone.

Three missed calls from her father. Two texts: *Call me.* And, forty minutes later: *Elena.*

Not *sweetheart*. Not even her full name. Just *Elena*, flat as a signature on a demand letter.

She looked at the harbor while the phone rang. From here, in the blue hour, the water was the color of hammered pewter, and the boats knocked gently against their moorings, and the whole shoreline lay quiet and lit here and there like a string of beads about to be cut. Somewhere along that shore was the old mill house — she had seen it that afternoon, driving in, Casper's hand loose on the wheel, his voice going soft in a way she now understood had nothing to do with her. *I asked her there,* he'd said. *My wife. Right on those steps, if you can call them steps anymore.*

She had recognized it instantly. Of course she had. She had studied the site plans for eleven months. She knew the mill house the way she knew her own signature: every elevation, every structural assessment, every line item in the demolition budget. Phase One. Weeks, not months. The schedule had been approved in March.

"Elena." Her father's voice came through crisp and impatient, the background hum of his car. "Where are you? You're supposed to be at the Delgado thing tonight."

"I told you I was taking the weekend."

"You told me you were *away* for the weekend. Away has coordinates." A pause, the sound of a turn signal ticking. "You're in Millbrook."

It wasn't a question. Of course it wasn't. The car service had logged the trip; the driver reported everything; her father's world ran on information the way other men's worlds ran on money, though to Richard Marsh the two had long since become the same substance.

"I'm at a friend's family thing," she said.

"A friend." She could hear him smiling, and hated that she could hear it. "Casper Voss."

The name in her father's mouth sounded wrong, like hearing a hymn played on a cash register. "Dad—"

"Relax. I'm delighted. Genuinely." The turn signal stopped. "Do you know what your bookseller is sitting on? Waterfront parcel, deep-water access, original timber frame if you care about that sort of sentimentality. The last piece we don't control between the marina and the north bluff. His lease is up in fourteen months and he doesn't know it yet — the landlord's already talked to our people. We'll get it either way. But it would go faster, and cheaper, if the tenant were inclined to be reasonable."

"He would never sign," she said, and heard too late how much she had just admitted.

"No," her father agreed pleasantly. "He wouldn't. Which is why I'm glad my daughter is dating him instead of me." A beat. "I'm not asking you to trick him, Elena. I'm asking you to be present. To be the reasonable voice in the room when the time comes. People sign things for the people they love that they'd never sign for a stranger. It's practically the foundation of the industry."

"The industry," she repeated. "You mean taking a man's shop."

"I mean building a harbor that will still exist in fifty years, which is more than that town is going to manage on its own. Sentiment is not a business plan. Neither is grief." He said the word lightly, testing it, and she understood that he knew — that he had always known — what the mill house was to Casper Voss, and had filed it away as leverage, the way he filed away everything. "There's a demolition notice going out next week. Mill house site, Phase One. Thirty days. I wanted you to hear it from me before you read it on a bulletin board."

The water went very still in front of her, or she did. She couldn't tell which.

"Thirty days," she said.

"Twenty-eight, actually, accounting for the council's procedural window, which we've already handled." Handled. The word sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. She thought of the easement documents she'd signed off on in February without reading past the summary page — the access easement across the mill property, the one with the odd clause about abandonment she'd flagged and been told not to worry about. *Legal's got it,* her father had said. Legal always had it. "So. Enjoy your weekend. Enjoy your bookseller. Just keep in mind which side of the paperwork you're standing on when it comes time to choose."

"Dad." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "The mill house. Is that — is that really necessary? It's falling down anyway. It's not worth—"

"It's worth exactly what someone will pay for the view from it," he said, "and right now that someone is us. Goodnight, Elena. Say hello to Voss for me."

The line went dead.

* * *

She stood by the water for a long time after that. Long enough for the first star to come out over the breakwater, long enough for her wine-warmth to fade and the cold to work into her shoulders. She tried to assemble the evening into something she could carry back inside — tried to find a version of herself that could walk up that gravel drive, sit back down at that table, take Casper's hand again, and say nothing.

Here is what complicity actually feels like, she thought. Not the grand, cinematic guilt of novels. Not a knife in the dark. It feels like this: standing in the cold doing arithmetic. Twenty-eight days. How many dinners did that buy them? How many mornings in the bookshop, dust motes turning in the light, June cross-legged on the floor with a picture book she'd already read? How many times could she laugh at his dry, reluctant humor before the laughter became theft?

She thought about walking to her rental car, driving to the ferry, and disappearing. It would be clean. It would hurt him — she allowed herself, honestly, to know that it would hurt him, which meant she had already stopped pretending this was fake — but it would hurt him once, cleanly, instead of slowly, in installments, with interest.

Then she thought of June's face at dinner. The way the girl had claimed the chair beside Elena within ten minutes, as though seating charts were a birthright. The way she'd said, mouth full of bread, *"Are you coming to story hour? Papa does voices."*

Twenty-eight days. Or — and here was the thought she had been circling all evening, the one that finally landed — twenty-eight days was also time. Time was a currency too; her father had taught her that without meaning to. Twenty-eight days was enough to learn what she needed to learn. About the easement. About the council votes she'd heard mentioned in hallways and never examined. About why a project this size, in a town this small, had moved this fast without a single public objection on record.

Towns like Millbrook objected. Towns like Millbrook always objected. Unless they couldn't.

She turned the idea over carefully, the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath, knowing you might not like it. If something was rotten in the file — and something was, she could feel it, the way you feel a draft from a door that should be shut — then she was not merely complicit. She was positioned. She had access no journalist, no lawyer working pro bono, no stubborn bookseller could ever get. She had her own signature on half the documents.

That was the terrible arithmetic of it: the only person who could burn this project down from the inside was the person who had helped build it.

She pressed her palms against her eyes until she saw sparks. Then she smoothed her hair, checked her reflection in the black glass of her phone — practiced, briefly, the smile — and walked back up the gravel drive toward the light.

* * *

Inside, the reunion had moved on to pie. Someone had put music on — something old and scratchy-sounding even on good speakers, a song about harbors, because of course it was — and June had fallen asleep against her great-aunt's shoulder, one sock on and one sock off, exactly as advertised. Casper looked up when Elena came in, and the look he gave her undid her a little: easy, warm, entirely unguarded. He patted the chair beside him. As if she belonged there. As if belonging were simple.

"There you are," he said quietly. "I was about to send a search party. My mother's threatening to give you the family recipe for disaster pie if you stay much longer."

"Disaster pie?"

"It's a long story involving 1987 and a fire extinguisher. You'd have to be family to hear it." He said it lightly, teasing, and then seemed to hear what he'd said, and a flicker of something crossed his face — apology, or fear, or both — and she watched him decide to let it stand.

Family. The word went through her like a splinter finding depth.

She sat down. She took the fork he handed her. She ate pie she couldn't taste and laughed at jokes she half-heard and let her knee rest against his under the table, and every second of it she catalogued with the desperate precision of a woman memorizing a house she knew was scheduled for demolition. The chipped blue glaze on the serving bowls. The way his mother touched his shoulder passing behind him, wordless, a habit older than grief. The photograph on the sideboard of a younger Casper, sunburned, holding a baby with June's exact frown.

"So," said the brother — Daniel, the loud one, the one who'd said the name earlier — leaning across the table with the cheerful ruthlessness of younger siblings everywhere. "Elena. What do you actually *do*? Because Cass won't tell us anything, which means either it's boring or it's classified."

"Daniel," Casper said, warning in it.

"No, it's fine," Elena said, and smiled her armor-smile, the one that had gotten her through a hundred boardrooms and was, she realized, the first fully dishonest thing she had done all night. "I work in development. Real estate development, mostly waterfront projects."

She watched it land. Watched Casper go still in a way that was almost imperceptible — the wineglass pausing halfway to his mouth, the half-second delay before his smile resumed. Watched him decide, visibly, consciously, to believe the best interpretation: that the coincidence was a coincidence, that the woman who held his hand under this table had nothing to do with the notices taped to his door.

He chose to trust her. That was the unbearable part. Given almost nothing, he reached for the generous reading, because that was apparently who he was — a man who had lost enough that he refused to live in suspicion, who met the world open-handed even though the world had taken his hand's worth twice over.

"That's funny," Daniel said, oblivious. "There's a company sniffing around the harbor. Marsh Development? Big outfit. Everybody says the whole waterfront's getting bought up. You know them?"

"Everyone in the industry knows everyone," Elena said. "It's a small world."

It was. That was the joke of it, the cruel private joke: it was such a small world. Small enough that she was sitting in it, eating its pie, wearing its trust like a borrowed coat.

Under the table, Casper found her hand again. His thumb moved once across her knuckles, absent, habitual already — three days and already habit — and she had to look down at her plate and breathe.

Later, when the dishes were stacked and the goodbyes were beginning, his mother caught Elena at the door and held both her hands. She was a small woman with Casper's eyes and a spine like a mast.

"He laughs around you," she said simply. "I haven't heard that in ten years. Whatever this is — however new it is — thank you for it."

Elena held on to those hands a moment longer than grace required, because it was the closest she would ever come to confessing.

Driving back along the coast road afterward, Casper's headlights sweeping the black water, Elena watched the ruins of the old mill house rise up on the left — pale bones against the darker dark, the broken steps where a man had once knelt with a ring and a future. Twenty-eight days, her father had said. She counted them silently as they passed, the way you count the waves between you and the thing coming for you.

"What are you thinking?" Casper asked. "You've gone quiet."

"Just tired," she said, and put her hand over his on the gearshift, and told herself that the lie was temporary, that she would fix it, that there was still time.

Outside her hotel, he kissed her cheek — soft, unhurried, asking nothing — and drove off toward the harbor, and Elena stood in the parking lot with her phone in her hand, opened the folder of project files she had sworn she'd stop opening, and began, methodically, to read.
