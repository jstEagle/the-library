## 10. The Second Date

Casper Voss had not planned to see her that evening. That was the whole point of it, he realized later — the best things in Millbrook had never been planned. The fog came in off the water around six, softening the harbor into something out of an old etching, and he was locking up the shop when he saw her standing at the end of the pier, alone, watching the fishing boats come in with their lights already burning yellow against the gray.

She wasn't wearing the reunion dress or the business clothes she'd worn the day she'd first wandered into his shop pretending to be a tourist. She wore jeans and a sweater the color of sea glass, her hair loose, and she looked — there was no other word for it — unarmored.

He should have gone home. He should have climbed the stairs above the shop, made June's dinner, read her two chapters of *The Wind in the Willows*, and let whatever this was with Elena Marsh stay safely contained inside the fiction they'd built together: the fake date, the rehearsed history, the lie with a beginning and an end like any well-made story.

Instead he found himself walking down the pier.

"You're going to ruin your shoes," he said by way of hello, because the boards were slick with spray and she was standing right at the edge where the waves broke hardest.

Elena turned, and something in her face shifted when she saw him — a loosening, like a knot giving way. "They're already ruined," she said. "I've been standing here for an hour."

"Waiting for someone?"

"No." She hesitated, and he watched her decide to tell the truth, watched it cost her something. "Hiding from someone. My father called twice today. I didn't pick up."

"Ah," Casper said. "The family reunion hangover."

"Something like that." She smiled, but it didn't reach all the way up. "Your family liked me. That's the strange part. They liked me without knowing anything about me at all."

"They liked you because June adored you," Casper said. "And my mother liked you because you laughed at her story about the raccoon in the church basement. In this town, that's a character reference that would survive a background check."

Elena laughed then, really laughed, and the sound carried out over the water, and Casper thought: *I am in trouble.* Not the kind of trouble that could be solved. The other kind.

"I was about to walk up to the lighthouse," he heard himself say. "It's better in the fog. Everything's quieter. If you wanted—"

"Yes," she said, before he finished asking.

* * *

The path up to the lighthouse wound through gorse and wild roses gone leggy with neglect, and the fog moved around them like something alive, closing behind and opening ahead. Elena walked half a step behind him, close enough that their hands brushed once on the steep part of the climb, and neither of them acknowledged it, and both of them felt it.

"This is where I come when the shop gets too quiet," Casper said. "Which sounds backwards, but a bookshop's quiet isn't peaceful. It's the quiet of unsold inventory. This—" he gestured at the white blur of the tower emerging from the mist above them, "—this is the quiet of things that don't need me."

"When did you start coming here?"

"After Sarah died." He said it simply, and was surprised how simple it was to say. "First year, mostly. I couldn't sleep past four in the morning, so I'd walk up here and watch the light turn. It seemed like the only honest thing in town. Everything else was pretending the harbor was fine, that I was fine, that June understood why her mother wasn't coming back."

Elena said nothing for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful. "Do you still come at four in the morning?"

"Only when I've done something wrong," he said, and heard her breath catch beside him, though he couldn't have said why.

The lighthouse itself was decommissioned — automated decades ago, its lamp replaced by a buoy further out — but the town kept it painted and the door locked against vandals and romantics alike. There was a bench on the seaward side, old cedar gone silver, and they sat down on it together with the careful choreography of two people leaving exactly one hand-width of space between them.

Below, the fog swallowed the harbor whole. They might have been sitting at the edge of the world.

"I keep thinking about dessert," Elena said suddenly.

"Dessert?"

"At the reunion. Your brother's apple thing. The way June fell asleep against my arm during coffee." She was looking straight out at the fog as she spoke, as if the words were easier to say to the horizon than to him. "I've been to a hundred dinners that mattered more — deals, negotiations, things worth millions — and I can't remember a single one of them. But I can remember exactly what June's hair smelled like. Shampoo. The cheap kind, with the cartoon character on the bottle."

"Strawberry unicorn," Casper said. "She'll defend it to the grave."

"Strawberry unicorn," Elena repeated softly, like a word in a language she was only just learning.

The space between their hands on the bench had shrunk. Casper couldn't have said who had closed it. His little finger rested against hers, and the contact was so slight it might have been accidental, except that neither of them moved away, and the fog pressed in around them, and somewhere below a boat horn sounded, long and low, like the harbor itself clearing its throat.

"Elena." He said her name and she turned to look at him, and her face in the gray light was open in a way that frightened him with its honesty. "This wasn't supposed to be real. We agreed. A date for a date. A favor for a favor. And now I'm sitting on a cold bench wanting—" He stopped himself.

"Wanting what?" she asked. Her voice was barely above the sound of the water.

"You know what."

* * *

Later, Elena would replay this moment the way one replays a car crash — frame by frame, looking for the exact instant where it could have gone differently.

Because she did know what. She knew it the way she knew the weight of the contracts in her briefcase back at the hotel, the way she knew the demolition schedule for the mill house that lived folded in her coat pocket like a stone. She knew what he wanted, because she wanted it too, and wanting it was the most dangerous thing she had done in years.

Casper leaned toward her. Slowly, giving her every chance to move away, the way a man approaches a bird he doesn't want to frighten off. And she didn't move away. The fog was cold on her cheeks and his hand came up — she felt it hover near her jaw, not quite touching, asking permission in the oldest language there is — and she tilted her face up toward him, eyes half-closing, and the distance between them shrank to almost nothing, to breath, to warmth—

And then she pulled back.

It wasn't a dramatic retreat. It was small — a turning of her head, a few inches of air reclaiming the space between them — but she felt it land on him like a blow. His hand dropped. He sat back. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the lighthouse loomed above them, blind, its lamp long extinguished, a tower built for guiding people home that could no longer do even that.

"I'm sorry," Elena said.

"Don't apologize." His voice was steady, but she could hear the effort underneath it, the way you can hear the strain in a rope holding weight. "You don't owe me anything. We invented this. We shook hands on it, more or less."

"That's not—" She stopped. Pressed her palms flat against her knees. *Tell him,* said some voice in her, the voice that had been getting louder for days, since June put the crayon map in her hands, since dessert, since the moment on this same coast road when he'd shown her the mill house ruins and she had recognized them from her father's blueprints and said nothing, nothing, nothing. *Tell him who you are. Tell him what's coming. Let him hate you now instead of later.*

But the voice that won was older and colder, the voice of survival, and it said: *Not yet. You'll lose this. Whatever this is, you'll lose it tonight if you say it.*

"Casper." She turned to face him fully, and she gave him the truest thing she could afford. "When we met — really met, in your shop, when I bought that novel I'm never going to read — I wasn't being honest with you. About who I am. What I..." Her throat closed. "There are things I haven't told you. Things about why I'm in Millbrook. And I need you to know that none of it — none of it — has anything to do with how I feel when I'm with you. With June. With your ridiculous family and their raccoon stories."

Casper studied her for a long time. The fog beaded in his hair; the light was failing; somewhere below them the harbor went on existing, oblivious, full of boats and nets and a bookshop with a notice taped to its door.

"You're going to tell me eventually?" he asked. Not angry. Worse than angry — patient. Kind. The kindness was unbearable.

"Yes," she said, and hated herself for how easily the lie came, how practiced her voice sounded even to her own ears.

"Then I'll wait," Casper Voss said. "I've gotten good at waiting. Ten years of practice." He stood, and offered her his hand, and after a moment she took it, and they walked down together through the fog, not touching again, the almost-kiss hanging between them like the lamp that no longer burned in the tower above.

At the bottom of the path, where the lane split toward the hotel and the harbor, they paused. Neither said goodnight. Then Elena leaned forward and kissed his cheek — quick, warm, gone — and walked away into the mist before he could say anything at all.

In her coat pocket, folded into quarters, the demolition order for the old mill house rustled faintly against her hip with every step, like a second heartbeat.

Three weeks, it said. Three weeks until the wrecking crews came to the place where Casper Voss had once knelt in the grass and asked a woman to marry him.

Elena walked faster, as if she could outrun the paper, knowing all the way back to the hotel that she could not.
