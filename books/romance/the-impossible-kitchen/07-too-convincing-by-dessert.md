## 7. Too Convincing by Dessert

I've lied for a living before. Not the way you're thinking—appraisal isn't lying, exactly, it's just deciding what a thing is worth and defending that number against everyone who wants it to be something else. But I've sat across tables from people who wanted me to say things I didn't believe, and I've learned to keep my face still while my brain runs the arithmetic underneath.

So when Ada Nakamura slid her hand into mine on the porch of her aunt's estate and said, "Ready?" like we were about to walk into a board meeting instead of a family reunion, I thought: fine. This is fine. I've done hostile rooms before.

I had not done *this* room.

* * *

The house was the kind of money that doesn't apologize for itself—white columns, a lawn mowed within an inch of its life, a fountain in the front circle that burbled like it was being paid to. Hollow Creek, the sign said, though the creek was nowhere in evidence and neither, I suspected, was any actual hollowing. Everything here looked maintained. Preserved. The vineyard I'd spent two days crawling through looked like this estate's neglected cousin who'd been left out in the rain.

Ada's grip tightened as we crossed the threshold. "Aunt Carol will ask what you do," she murmured. "Say winemaker-adjacent. If she hears 'bank,' she'll start asking about your portfolio."

"And if she asks about my portfolio?"

"Then you're doing it wrong. You're supposed to be a man with no portfolio. A man of mystery and modest means whom I'm dating against all good judgment."

"That's the role?"

"That's the role." She squeezed my hand once—a signal flare, a last transmission—and then her face did something remarkable. It smoothed over. All the tension, all the dry wit, packed away behind a pleasant hostess smile, and suddenly I was looking at a stranger wearing Ada's clothes.

I understood, in that moment, that I'd been watching the unguarded version of her for three days. The woman who swore at irrigation lines and ate cold noodles standing over the sink. This other one—the performed one—I'd never met.

I found I liked the first one better. Noted it. Filed it under irrelevant.

* * *

The relatives arrived in waves, and they catalogued her the way I catalogue assets. I recognized the technique because it's mine: quick inventory, condition assessment, depreciation noted. Her aunt Carol took in the dress, the posture, the plus-one, and ran the numbers behind her eyes. An uncle with a firm handshake and a golf tan asked about the vineyard in the tone of a man asking about a terminal diagnosis. A cousin's wife complimented Ada's "brave little project" while refilling her own wine glass from a bottle that was, I noted professionally, a Central Coast pinot retailing at maybe fourteen dollars, served at least ten degrees too warm.

"You're the boyfriend," said a silver-haired man, materializing beside me at the bar with the confidence of someone who has never once been unwelcome anywhere. He stuck out his hand. "Gerald. Carol's husband. So. What's your line of work?"

"Winemaking," I said. "Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"I consult. Vineyards, mostly small ones. Ones people have given up on." I watched Ada across the room, laughing at something her sister Ren said, the laugh not reaching her eyes. "The ones nobody wanted."

Gerald made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a hiccup of contempt. "Well, there's a growth industry. Nakamuras never met a losing proposition they didn't fall in love with." He nodded toward Ada. "Her grandmother was the same. Sweet woman. Terrible head for business. That place should've been condos twenty years ago."

Something in my chest went tight and hot, which was inconvenient, because I agreed with him. Professionally. On paper. That was literally why I was here—that overgrown Syrah block, those failing trellises, the debt-to-asset ratio that read like an obituary. Gerald was describing my report. I'd written most of it already in my head.

So why did I want to put him through the tasting room window?

"Condos," I said, "on that slope? The drainage alone would kill you. South-facing hillside, forty-year-old rootstock, morning fog off the valley floor. Do you know how hard it is to find old-vine Syrah in this state that isn't a parking lot now?" I heard my own voice getting quieter, which is what it does when I actually care about something. "That block is worth more every year it survives. Everyone else tore theirs out. Hers is still standing."

Gerald blinked at me, recalibrating. "You've seen it?"

"I've spent three days in it." Three days on my knees in the dirt with a refractometer and a growing suspicion that the numbers in the file were wrong—not fraudulently wrong, just *cruelly* wrong, the way numbers get when nobody's been able to afford to make them better. "It's a mess. It's also the best fruit I've touched this year, and I don't say that to be polite, because I'm not capable of it."

Across the room, Ada had gone still. She'd caught the tail end of it—I could tell by the way she was holding her wineglass, slightly too tight, slightly too high. Looking at me like I'd spoken a language she'd only ever heard her grandmother speak.

Gerald recovered with a chuckle. "Well. Romantic and delusional. She's found her match." He clapped my shoulder and drifted off toward the shrimp.

I stood there holding a drink I hadn't tasted once, wondering why my pulse was up. Defending an asset's value was my job. I did it in conference rooms weekly. There was nothing—

Ada appeared at my elbow. "You didn't have to do that."

"Do what."

"The block speech. The—" she waved a hand vaguely, "—the *rootstock* thing. Gerald's been calling that vineyard a mistake since I was twelve. You just told him it was the best fruit you'd touched all year."

"It's true."

"I know it's true. That's not—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Nobody says it out loud. In this house."

"Well," I said, and reached for something wry to defuse whatever was happening in my chest, "I'm contractually obligated to be convincing. Consider that a free sample."

She laughed—her real laugh, the short surprised one—and I felt it land somewhere below my sternum, and I thought, with the detached professionalism I bring to all disasters: *that's going to be a problem.*

* * *

Dinner was a long table and a longer gauntlet. I learned the family taxonomy by survival instinct: Aunt Carol, the general; Uncle Gerald, artillery; Cousin Derek, who sold insurance and talked about his boat the way some men talk about their children; Ren, Ada's sister, sharp-eyed and harder to read than anyone else in the room, watching me watch Ada with an expression I couldn't price.

I played my part. Hand on the back of Ada's chair. Refilling her water without being asked. The small choreography of established couples, which I knew from observation if not much practice. And the strange thing—the thing I kept noticing like a loose thread—was that none of it required acting. My hand went to her back because her spine was rigid and I wanted it less so. I remembered how she took her water because I'd been watching her for three days and apparently retained everything.

At some point she leaned into me during a lull, just slightly, her shoulder against mine, and I realized she wasn't performing either. She was leaning because she was tired, and I was the nearest solid object, and somewhere in the last seventy-two hours I had become furniture she trusted.

*Fatal*, whispered a voice in my head that sounded suspiciously like my risk-assessment department. *This is what fatal looks like.*

Then dessert came out, and Uncle Gerald loaded the cannon.

* * *

It was a lemon tart, and he aimed it over the top of it like a man toasting. "You know," he said, pleasantly, to the table at large but with the trajectory clearly plotted, "it's wonderful, seeing Ada settled. After everything. The divorce, and then running off to that vineyard—it's nice she's finally letting someone take care of her." A smile at me. "Brave man. Taking on a fixer-upper."

The table did the thing families do—half a laugh, half a flinch, everyone checking whether it landed as joke or wound. Ada's fork stopped moving. I watched her face do the smoothing-over thing again, the hostess mask sliding up, and I watched her start to build the gracious non-answer, the one that would let it slide, the one she'd been building since she was twelve years old in this same house.

And I heard myself say, "Actually, she takes care of me."

The table turned. Ada turned. Somewhere in my peripheral vision, Ren set down her wine glass with great precision.

"She rebuilt an irrigation system in two days that a contractor quoted her four thousand dollars to do," I continued, because apparently I was doing this now, mid-sentence, with no exit strategy, "and she did it better than the quote. She knows every row of that vineyard—what's sick, what's strong, what's worth saving. I've worked with vineyards run by teams of ten who don't know half of what she carries around in her head." I picked up my wine, mostly to give my hands a job. "The divorce isn't a character flaw. Neither is the vineyard. And for the record, nobody's taking care of anybody. We're partners. It's considerably more annoying and considerably better."

Silence. The specific silence of a room recalculating.

Gerald smiled the smile of a man conceding a hole to a better putt. "Well. Good for you both."

Under the table, Ada's hand found my knee. Not a performance touch. A grip. Like she needed an anchor and I was the nearest solid object again, and God help me, I liked being it.

* * *

Later—dessert cleared, coffee poured, the family dispersed into smaller firing squads—I found myself alone with her on the side porch, string lights overhead, moths conducting their futile audit of the bulbs. She had her arms crossed and was looking at me sideways in a way I couldn't parse.

"That was quite a speech," she said.

"It got away from me."

"Partners. It's considerably more annoying and considerably better." She repeated it slowly, tasting it. "Where did that come from? We didn't workshop that."

"No. We workshopped 'we met through work' and 'it's new.' I improvised the rest."

"Why?"

It was a fair question and I owed it honesty, or as close to honesty as my current situation permitted, which was a narrow corridor with doors closing on both ends. Because he was cruel. Because you were about to absorb it politely. Because I have spent three days watching you fight for something everyone calls worthless and I can't stand watching you get hit for it.

What I said: "Because I'm method. Fully committed to the role. Ask any actor."

"Mm." She looked out at the dark lawn. "My mother used to say my grandmother could taste a lie in a glass of wine. Said she'd know if you'd watered the blend, sweetened it, whatever. Just—knew." A pause. "Ren does that too. Stares at people until the truth falls out."

"Your sister's been staring at me all night."

"What did you tell her?"

"Nothing yet. She hasn't finished the appraisal."

That earned me a real laugh, brief and low, and then she went quiet again, and the quiet stretched into something that wasn't uncomfortable, which was itself alarming, because quiet with another person is usually where I start tallying exits.

"You know what's strange?" she said finally. "All night, I kept waiting to feel like I was lying. That's the whole design of this—you and me, the script, the rules. And I kept waiting for the moment it would feel fake, and it—" she gestured vaguely at the space between us, at the whole evening, at the hand-shaped warmth on my knee that I was still cataloguing, "—didn't. It felt like the truest room I've been in, in years. Which is a problem."

"Yeah," I said. "It is."

We stood there, two professionals confronting a scope creep neither of us had budgeted for. The moths threw themselves at the lights. Down the hill, thirty miles east, her vineyard sat in the dark doing the slow work of surviving, and I thought about the file in my car, the draft report with its tidy columns, the number I was going to write that would decide whether any of it—any of it—continued to exist.

I thought about telling her. Right then, on the porch, with the string lights and the moths and her shoulder six inches from mine. *I'm the one who decides. The bank sent me. The number is mine.*

I didn't. I told myself it was because tonight wasn't the night, because she deserved the truth sober and unhurried, because there were rules and the rules said one weekend, no real feelings, and certainly no real disclosures. All true. All reasonable.

Also, underneath, quieter: I didn't tell her because if I said it out loud, I'd have to watch her look at me the way she'd looked at Gerald all these years—as another person sent to appraise her and find her wanting. And I discovered, standing on that porch, that I would do a truly unprofessional amount of anything to avoid that look.

Which meant the lie wasn't the fake relationship anymore.

The lie was everything I wasn't saying.

Ada shivered in the cooling air, and I took off my jacket and put it around her shoulders without thinking, and she let me, and neither of us remarked on it, and that was the most damning data point of the entire weekend.

"One more day," she said. "Then we go home and it's over, and you write your report, and I—" she stopped herself, smiled the hostess smile, the one I now hated on sight. "Well. Then we'll see."

"We'll see," I agreed, and shook her grandmother's ghost off my shoulder, and failed completely.

Inside, her phone lit up on the hall table. I caught the sender's name before she flipped it face-down: *Meridian Bank — Customer Relations.*

The subject line was visible for exactly one second.

**Re: Site Inspection — Moved Up.**

Ada turned the phone over, and her smile stayed perfectly, terribly intact, and I understood that whatever was coming had just started coming faster than either of us had planned for.
