## 17. Reunion, Interrupted

*Ada*

The final dinner of the Nakamura family reunion was held in my aunt Carol's dining room, which seated twenty-four under a chandelier that had opinions about everyone. I knew this because I'd spent enough childhood dinners beneath it to learn its moods. It caught the light like a judge catching a lie.

I had dressed for battle in the only armor I owned: a black dress Ren called "funereal" and Theo called "effective." He'd said it while adjusting his cufflinks in the guest room mirror, and something in his tone made me suspect he wasn't talking about the dress.

"You're quiet," he said now, as we stood on Carol's porch waiting to be let in. The house smelled through the screen door of roast pork and lilies and money that had never once been counted out loud.

"I'm rehearsing."

"The toast?"

"The eulogy."

He looked at me sideways. "Ada."

"I know." I smoothed the front of my dress where the letter sat folded against my ribs, soft as cloth after three days in my pocket. I'd carried it everywhere since finding it—into the shower stall, propped on the sink; into bed, read by phone light while Theo slept beside me with the trusting breathing of a man who didn't know what I was about to detonate at his feet. "I know what this costs."

"Do you?" His voice was gentle, which was worse than an argument. Gentle meant he could see all the way down into me, and I was still deciding whether that was a gift or a wound. "Because you've been carrying that letter like a grenade, and grenades don't care whose hand they're in when they go off."

"That's rather the point."

"Is it liberation you're after, or revenge?"

I turned to look at him fully. In the porch light his face was all angles and patience, and I hated how much I'd come to rely on that patience, how it had become load-bearing architecture in my week.

"Can't it be both?"

"It can," he said. "But you should know which one is driving before you get behind the wheel."

The door opened. My aunt Carol stood there in her hosting face—the one that said *welcome* and *behave* in the same breath—and behind her, the dining room glowed like a stage set for a family that had never actually existed.

"Ada," she said. "And Theo. You found us again."

"As if we could get lost," Theo said warmly, and stepped past me into the light.

That was the thing about Theo Marchetti I still hadn't reconciled: the man who arrived at Hollow Creek with a clipboard and a foreclosure timeline was also the man who could charm my aunt within four seconds flat. Somewhere between those two men was the truth of him, and I hadn't located it yet. I suspected neither had he.

* * *

*Theo*

Dinner was a performance, and I'd been performing so long I no longer trusted my own reviews.

Carol had seated Ada at the far end of the table, near the kitchen door—a placement so surgically precise it might as well have come with a memo. I was seated mid-table between a cousin named Doug who sold insurance and an uncle named Richard who sold nothing but certainty. Within ten minutes, Richard had covered the vineyard's failure, the divorce, and the general theme of Ada's life as a cautionary tale, all in the tone of a man discussing weather patterns.

"And the bank's still circling, I hear," Richard said, ladling gravy over everything including the conversation. "Shame. Though honestly, Mika's mother always did bite off more than she could chew. That whole operation ran on stubbornness and denial."

"Runs," I said.

Richard blinked. "What?"

"Runs. Present tense." I refilled my water glass with the calm of a man defusing something. "The Syrah block came through the frost. You'd know that if you'd visited."

"We're not really vineyard people."

"No," I agreed. "You're appraisal people. Everything gets valued from a distance."

Doug laughed into his potatoes. Under the table, Ada's knee pressed briefly against mine—not affection exactly. Acknowledgment. *I see you spending your credibility on me,* it said. *We'll discuss the exchange rate later.*

Across the table, Carol presided. She'd been gracious all weekend in the way of people managing a situation rather than hosting guests—redirecting conversations away from certain topics, steering relatives away from certain rooms. Away from the east wing. Away, I now understood, from anything that might lead back to a locked study four hours north containing thirty years of unsent letters.

I'd read three of them by then. Ada had given them to me in the truck on the drive down, wordlessly, the way you hand someone evidence. I'd read them at a gas station outside Medford while she bought coffee she didn't drink, and by the time she came back I understood two things: why her mother had left, and why Carol had spent decades making sure no one asked.

Mika Nakamura hadn't abandoned her family out of selfishness. She'd fled a sister who was quietly bleeding the vineyard dry and a mother who'd discovered it too late—and when Mika tried to blow the whistle, Carol had framed her as unstable, ungrateful, running off with a man. The family myth. The one Ada had swallowed whole at eight years old, along with the belief that her mother's leaving was somehow a draft she'd be expected to answer someday.

The last letter was different. It was addressed to no one—or to everyone. It named Carol's accounts, the loans taken against land she didn't own, the insurance payout redirected, the slow patient theft dressed up as stewardship. And it ended with a line I'd been turning over for two days:

*I am telling the truth now because I will not be here to tell it later, and silence is the only inheritance I refuse to leave my granddaughter.*

Ada had that letter folded against her ribs right now. I could tell by the way she kept one hand near her sternum, casual as adjusting a necklace.

Dessert arrived—lemon tart, Carol's signature, served with the triumph of a woman whose narrative had survived another reunion unscratched. Toasts began. Someone toasted Carol's generosity. Someone toasted the family. Richard toasted "to those of us who stayed grounded"—a remark aimed across forty feet of tablecloth with sniper precision.

And Ada set down her fork.

* * *

*Ada*

Here is what nobody tells you about the moment before you burn a room down: it's very quiet. The chandelier hums. Someone's fork scrapes porcelain. Your own heartbeat sounds like footsteps in an empty house.

I stood up, and the sound of my chair was small and enormous.

"Before we finish," I said, "I have a toast of my own."

Carol smiled the smile she'd been using on me since I was six years old, the one that said *sit down, sweetheart.* "Ada, honey, you don't have to—"

"To Grandmother," I continued, "who left me a vineyard, a mountain of debt, and—" I drew the letter from my dress, and the paper looked impossibly thin in my hands, "—this. Which I found last week in her study. Her last letter. Unsent. Addressed, I think, to whoever finally opened that door."

The table had gone still in the particular way of families sensing weather change. Ren, at the opposite end, had stopped mid-sip. Her eyes were wide and fixed on the paper, and I realized with a strange floating clarity that my sister—who'd warned Theo that I run, who'd armored herself in pragmatism years ago—was holding her breath.

"Grandmother wrote it eleven months before she died," I said. "It's about the vineyard. About where the money went. About why my mother really left."

"Ada." Carol's voice had dropped its warmth entirely, and underneath it was something older and colder. "Whatever you think you found—"

"'Carol has been drawing against the estate since 1994,'" I read. "'The Harrow Lane parcel was collateralized without my knowledge. The insurance settlement from the '09 frost—forty-one thousand dollars—was deposited to an account I did not open. When Mika confronted me with the ledgers, I did not want to believe her. That failure cost me my daughter.'"

The words came out steady. I'd expected my voice to shake. It didn't, and that steadiness frightened me more than shaking would have, because it meant some part of me had been ready for this for a decade and simply hadn't known.

"When my mother left," I said, looking up, "this family told me she abandoned us. Ran off. Chose herself. I was eight, and I believed it, and I carried it for thirty years like it was mine to atone for. She was trying to save the vineyard. From Carol. And when she wouldn't stop asking questions, this family made her the villain so the thief could keep the keys."

The room fractured. Not loudly—that was the eerie part. No screaming, no thrown glasses. Just a sound like ice shifting on a pond: Doug setting down his fork very carefully, a cousin whispering *is that true?*, Richard's face cycling through disbelief and recalculation and, finally, the awful dawning arithmetic of a man realizing every loan he'd ever co-signed with Carol had passed through stolen ground.

Carol stood. "This is a forgery. This is—Ada, you are a *guest* in my home—"

"I'm your niece," I said. "And I'm standing in the ruins of a story you built to protect yourself. Grandmother documented everything. Dates, account numbers, signatures. There's a lawyer in Portland who'll be receiving copies Monday morning, along with the bank, who I understand has been appraising assets that were mortgaged twice over without anyone's knowledge." I paused. "You didn't just steal from her, Aunt Carol. You stole from all of us. You stole my mother."

"She *chose* to go—"

"She chose to survive being disbelieved by everyone she loved. I know exactly how that feels." My voice cracked at last, on that, and I let it. "I spent thirty years thinking I was the one who ran. Turns out running is the family business—but you cornered the market."

Silence. The chandelier hummed its judgment.

And then, strangely, I felt it happen: the weight lifting. Not all at once—more like a tide going out, revealing things that had been submerged so long I'd forgotten they were there. Thirty years of swallowing. Ten years of apologizing for existing near my own history. The divorce I'd treated as proof of defectiveness. The debts I'd accepted as my natural inheritance. All of it suddenly legible, suddenly *not mine.*

I felt lighter than I had in years. Lighter, possibly, than I had ever felt standing up.

Ren was crying, quietly, furiously, wiping her eyes with her napkin like the tears were an inconvenience she intended to fire someone over. Later, I thought. We would talk later, my sister and I, about the years she'd stayed and the armor that staying required. But something in her face had unlocked, and I knew she'd heard it too: none of this had ever been ours to carry.

Theo caught my eye from mid-table. He didn't smile. He gave me a single nod, grave and certain, the nod of a man watching an appraisal come in exactly at value.

Carol sank back into her chair. Around her, the family was already rearranging itself—conversations splitting, alliances recalibrating in real time, the whole ecosystem of a dynasty discovering its foundation was hollow. Someone had their phone out. Someone else was asking Doug, in a low urgent voice, whether he still did estate law.

I sat down. My hands, I noticed, were perfectly steady around the letter.

"Well," Theo murmured beside me, leaning close, his breath warm against my ear. "For the record, that was the most effective thing I've seen all year. And I've seen a lot of foreclosures."

"Was that a compliment?"

"That was a warning." His eyes were serious beneath the dryness. "Monday, when the bank sees what's in that letter, everything changes. For the vineyard. For—" he hesitated, and I watched him decide not to say *for us*, "—for a lot of things. Are you ready for that?"

I looked down the ruined table, at the wreckage of the story I'd been raised inside, at my aunt staring at her lemon tart like it contained the answers, at my sister crying and laughing at once.

"No," I said. "But I'm done letting that stop me."

His hand found mine under the tablecloth and held on, and I let it, and outside the windows the first real cold of autumn was moving in over the hills—toward Hollow Creek, toward the vines, toward whatever came next.

I just didn't yet know that the bank's letter was already in the mail.
