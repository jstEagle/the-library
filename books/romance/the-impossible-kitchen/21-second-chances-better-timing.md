## 21. Second Chances, Better Timing

I rehearsed the apology eleven times in the truck.

Not out loud—God, no. In my head, where all my best work happened and none of it ever got delivered. The first draft was formal. *Ada, I take full responsibility for the report.* Sounded like a deposition. The second draft was romantic. *Ada, you're the only thing I've appraised correctly in my entire career.* Sounded like a greeting card written by a man having a stroke. Drafts three through nine variously involved poetry, statistics, and one deeply regrettable metaphor about terroir that I will deny to my grave.

Draft ten was just: *I'm sorry. I was a coward. I love you.*

Draft eleven added: *Also I brought wine, which is either charming or wildly presumptuous given the circumstances, and I've decided not to think about which.*

The bottle sat on the passenger seat like a co-conspirator. One of six cases we'd bottled from the frost-surviving rows—the harvest nobody thought possible, pressed by two people who'd stopped pretending somewhere around hour three of running smudge pots in the dark. Ada had labeled them by hand: *Hollow Creek, First Frost, 2024*. Underneath, smaller, in her grandmother's style of wry understatement: *Vines remember. So should you.*

She'd given me a bottle at the bank hearing. Not handed it to me exactly—more like set it down in front of me with the deliberate care of someone placing evidence. Then she'd walked out before I could say anything, which was fair, because everything I had to say had already been said by a letter she'd found in a locked room and read aloud to her entire family while I stood there complicit in my own silence.

That was three weeks ago.

Three weeks of amended findings, heritage-vineyard restructuring hearings, a regional director who called me "either the bravest or stupidest appraiser in the district" and then, grudgingly, approved the loan. Three weeks of drafting emails to Ada and deleting them. Three weeks of driving past the vineyard turnoff twice—once on a Tuesday, once at eleven p.m. on a Friday, both times getting as far as the gravel shoulder before my nerve failed and I told myself tomorrow.

Tomorrow is a coward's favorite word. I learned that from watching myself use it for twenty-one days straight.

So tonight I didn't give myself an exit. I turned in at the gate with my headlights off, which was dramatic and also nearly put me in the irrigation ditch, and parked under the old walnut tree where her truck used to sit back when I was appraising this place instead of losing my whole heart inside its property lines.

Her kitchen light was on.

Of course it was. Ada Nakamura did not sleep like a normal person; she slept like a woman keeping watch over something, which she'd been doing her whole life without anyone thanking her for it. Through the window I could see her silhouette moving—dishes, maybe, or another round of the cellar inventory she'd started now that the vineyard was legally hers again, debt restructured, future tentatively green.

I sat in the truck for four minutes. I know because I watched the clock do it, the way I used to watch appraisal deadlines, except this time the thing being measured was whether I had any spine at all.

Then I got out, tucked the bottle under my arm, and walked to her door like a man reporting for judgment.

* * *

The porch boards still creaked in the same places. Third step, left side. I knew that because I'd catalogued every sound this house made during those weeks of harvest, the way you memorize anything you're afraid of losing. The screen door stuck. I lifted it the way she'd taught me—left hinge up, don't force it—and knocked.

From inside: silence. Then footsteps, unhurried. Then the door opened to the width of a hand.

Ada looked at me through the gap. Hair down, sleeves pushed up, flour on one forearm—she'd been baking, which meant she'd been thinking too hard, which meant she'd probably been thinking about me, which I clung to like a man clinging to driftwood.

"Marchetti," she said.

"Nakamura."

"You're standing on my porch."

"I am."

"At eight o'clock at night. Unannounced."

"I called ahead," I said. "In my head. Eleven times. You never picked up."

Something twitched at the corner of her mouth. She killed it immediately, but I saw it. I'd spent enough hours studying this woman's face to know the difference between her anger and her almost-amusement, and right now they were sharing custody.

"You have flour on your arm," I said, because drafts ten and eleven had apparently fled the building and my mouth was improvising.

"I'm aware." She looked at the bottle under my arm. Her eyes narrowed. "Is that what I think it is?"

"First Frost. Bottle forty-seven of two hundred and sixteen."

"You kept count."

"I keep count of things that matter. It's a professional flaw."

She opened the door another inch. Not an invitation. An audit. "You have ninety seconds, Theo. I'm timing you."

"That's generous. Your sister gave me thirty."

"Ren likes you. That was Ren being fond." She leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed, and God help me, even mid-reckoning she was the most beautiful thing I'd seen in three weeks of nothing but spreadsheets and self-loathing. "Start talking."

So I did. No contract this time. No pretext. No file folder, no terms, no trade. Just me, on her porch, with the truth I'd rehearsed until it wore smooth.

"I submitted the original report because it was safe," I said. "Because liquidation was the defensible answer, the answer that would keep my job and my reputation and my clean little life intact. And then I stood in your cellar holding letters your grandmother wrote over forty years, letters proving everything this place is, and I chose the spreadsheet anyway. I told myself I was protecting you from false hope. That was a lie I told so I wouldn't have to admit I was protecting myself. You deserved better than a man who loved you in private and betrayed you in triplicate."

She didn't move. The ninety seconds were burning down and I could hear them going.

"I'm not asking you to forgive me tonight," I said. "I'm not even asking for a second chance yet. I'm asking for the chance to earn one. Slowly. With paperwork, if necessary—I know how you feel about documentation. I'll sign anything. I'll write terms. Rule one: no more hiding reports. Rule two: no more hiding anything. Rule three—"

"Theo."

"—rule three is whatever you want it to be, I'm flexible, I've learned flexibility, it only cost me everything—"

"Theo." She stepped out onto the porch and closed the door behind her. The click of the latch sounded final as a gavel. "You're rambling."

"I'm aware. It's a coping mechanism."

"You rehearsed this."

"Eleven drafts."

"What happened to drafts one through nine?"

"One involved a sonnet. We don't talk about the sonnet."

And there it was—the laugh. Small, reluctant, escaping before she could stop it, the way the first frost had escaped her forecast and saved us anyway. She pressed her lips together, but the damage was done, and we both knew it, and for one suspended moment the porch felt like the tasting room during the blackout, when we'd sat on the floor sharing cold noodles by candlelight and pretending the contract between us was still holding.

It hadn't been holding for a long time. We'd both known that too.

"You stood in that doorway for three weeks' worth of courage," she said quietly. "What took you so long?"

"Fear," I said. "The usual vintage."

"And the report? The real one? The one you actually believe?"

"I believe in the vines," I said. "I believed in them the first day I stood in your dying Syrah block and told you it was worthless, and I was wrong, and I've been wrong about the important things ever since. The numbers work now. But that's not why I'm here. I'd be here if the loan had been denied. I'd be here with worse wine and better excuses."

She studied me for a long moment. The porch light caught the flour dust on her arm, the tired line of her mouth, the eyes that had spent a decade learning not to hope and were now, visibly, against their better training, hoping.

"You hurt me," she said.

"I know."

"Not the report. Well—the report. But mostly that you decided for me. Again. Like everyone always has. The bank, my aunt, my family—they all decided what my story was, and you—" Her voice caught, steadied. "You were supposed to be the one person who asked."

"You're right." My throat was tight. "I should have shown you the draft. I should have let you fight beside me instead of in front of you. It's the same mistake your whole family made—deciding silence was kindness. It isn't. It never is."

"No," she said softly. "It isn't."

A long pause. A cricket somewhere in the vines, keeping time. The bottle sweated in my grip.

"So," she said. "Here's what happens now. You're going to stand there for another minute or so. Long enough to sweat properly. Consider it penance."

"This is penance?"

"Consider it interest accrued." The corner of her mouth was definitely winning now. "And then, if you've learned anything at all in twenty-one days, you're going to say the thing from draft ten. The real one. Not the sonnet."

My heart performed a maneuver that would have alarmed a cardiologist. "How do you know about draft ten?"

"Because I've spent three weeks imagining every version of this conversation, and mine had eleven drafts too, and draft ten was the one where I stopped being angry and started being honest." She unfolded her arms. "Say yours first. I want to hear it in your voice."

I set the bottle down on the porch rail. Carefully, the way you set down the last thing between you and the truth.

"I'm sorry," I said. "I was a coward. I love you."

There. Smaller than it had sounded in my head, and infinitely larger. The words hung in the dark between us, in the smell of frost coming and grapes ripening and flour and night air, and I stood there sweating exactly as instructed, waiting to find out whether love declared honestly was enough or merely necessary.

Ada looked at me for five full seconds. I counted. It's a professional flaw.

Then she reached out, took a fistful of my shirt, and pulled me across the threshold into the flour-dusted, lamplit, impossible kitchen, and kissed me like a verdict being overturned.

* * *

Later—much later, when the candles she'd lit for no one had burned down to stubs and the dough she'd abandoned had risen and fallen and risen again in neglect—we sat on her kitchen floor with our backs against the cabinets, sharing the bottle of First Frost. It was good. It was absurdly, improbably good, fruit and smoke and something bright underneath, the taste of a night when two people ran into a freezing dark to save something everyone said was dead.

"We should enter this in competitions," I said.

"We should drink it at our wedding," she said, and then went very still, the way she did whenever she surprised herself.

I turned the bottle in my hands, buying time, savoring it. "Bold of you to assume I'd plan something that far out. I once took three weeks to drive up a driveway."

"You took three weeks because you're careful," she said. "It took me ten years to open a locked door. We're both slow. It's fine. Slow vines make better wine."

"Is that true?"

"My grandmother wrote it in a margin somewhere. Everything she wrote turns out to be true eventually. It's very annoying."

I reached over and took her hand. Flour-rough, wine-sticky, steady. "For the record," I said, "draft twelve says I'd marry you in this kitchen, barefoot, with your relatives crammed in around the table and your mother crying into the punch. But draft twelve can wait. I'm done rushing the important things."

Ada leaned her head on my shoulder. Outside, the vines stood in rows under a sky just beginning to consider autumn, alive against every prediction, patient as memory.

"Stay for breakfast," she murmured. "I'm making pancakes. It's what I bake when I'm happy. The bread was for the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"Deciding whether to answer the door." She tilted her face up toward me, smiling in a way I intended to spend the rest of my life earning. "Next time, knock sooner."

"There's going to be a next time?"

"There's going to be a lifetime of next times, Marchetti. Get used to the porch."

And I laughed, and she laughed, and the empty bottle caught the candlelight like something rescued—which, we agreed much later, was exactly what it was.

But upstairs, on the desk where she worked, her phone screen glowed once in the dark: a message from a number she hadn't heard from in fifteen years.

*Mom: I found the letter. I'm coming home.*
