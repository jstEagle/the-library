## 8. Letters, Unsent

The storm had a sound now. Jonah had lived through enough of them to know the vocabulary—first the whisper, then the hiss, then this: a long white roar against the windows like the whole sky had leaned its shoulder into the glass and decided to stay. It was the kind of sound that made a building feel smaller from the inside, as if the walls were drawing in their breath.

He'd sent Eli and Priya to bed an hour ago. The cot in the storeroom for Priya, who had accepted it with the gracious efficiency of someone used to sleeping in airport lounges; the bench cushions from the window seats stacked into something resembling a mattress for Eli, out front by the hearth Jonah kept burning more for himself than for them. He could still hear the storm through two walls. He doubted either of them could sleep through it either.

So he did what he always did when he couldn't sleep. He worked.

The office was a closet masquerading as one—a desk his father had bought at an estate sale before Jonah was born, a filing cabinet with one drawer that only opened if you lifted the corner just so, shelves of binders going soft at the spines. The lamp threw a cone of yellow light across the desk, and outside that circle the room went brown and close, smelling of paper and old vanilla and the faint scorched sweetness that never quite left the walls no matter how many times he repainted. Sugar, baked into the building's bones. Danny's sugar.

He needed invoice paper. That was all. The power had flickered twice since midnight and he wanted tomorrow's invoices written out by hand while the generator still hummed, because Marisol would ask where they were, because the bank would ask where they were, because there was a comfort in the ritual of it—the pen moving, the columns adding up to numbers that were bad but *known*, known being the closest thing to safe he had left.

He pulled open the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet, the one that stuck, the one he hadn't opened since—

Since.

His hands stopped before his mind caught up. That was the thing about the body; it remembered ahead of you. It knew what was in that drawer the way a tongue knows a chipped tooth, mapping it without looking.

He should have thrown them out years ago. He'd told himself he would, a hundred times, standing right here with the drawer half-open, telling himself *tonight, tonight*—and every time, some cowardly arithmetic had intervened. They were only paper. Paper weighed nothing. Paper cost nothing to keep. And besides—and here was the lie he'd polished smooth over ten years—it wasn't sentiment. It was just that he hadn't gotten around to it.

Jonah Vasquez did not keep mementos. Jonah Vasquez trained his heart the way he trained his hands: repetition, discipline, no wasted motion. Grief was a muscle and love was a muscle and he had spent a decade teaching both to hold still. He had a system. He had routines. He had a café named after a dead man that he never explained to anyone, and a drawer he never opened, and those things were not contradictions, they were *infrastructure*.

He opened the drawer anyway.

The invoice paper was there, on top—he saw it immediately, a clean brick of white under the lamp light, exactly where it should be. All he had to do was take it and close the drawer and go back out to the desk and write his numbers and let the night pass over him like weather.

Instead he lifted the brick of paper aside.

Underneath it, wrapped in a plastic grocery bag gone brittle with age, tied with the twine he used for the pastry boxes, was a shoebox. Not even a good shoebox. Cardboard softened at the corners, the lid dented where something heavy had sat on it once, years ago. His own handwriting on the top in marker, faded almost to nothing: *DO NOT OPEN*, which was, he understood with a clarity that arrived about four years too late, the single most effective instruction he had ever issued to himself.

The storm roared. The lamp hummed. Somewhere out front, a log settled in the hearth with a soft collapse, and Jonah stood in his office holding a brick of invoice paper he no longer wanted, looking down at a box he had spent ten years not opening.

He told himself he was just moving it. Relocating it. The drawer stuck, the cabinet was dying, it made practical sense to consolidate. His hands untied the twine with the same deftness they used on pastry knots, muscle memory betraying him again, and the bag came away with a dry whisper, and the lid came off with no resistance at all, as if the box had been waiting so long it had given up pretending to be shut.

Letters.

Dozens of them. Envelopes without stamps, addressed in his own hand—*Eli Marchetti* over and over, the name repeated down through the stack like a prayer said wrong, or a prayer said too many times, or maybe there was no difference. Some envelopes fat, some thin as a single page. One with a coffee ring stamped across the corner. One with a crease where it had been folded and unfolded so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth. The earliest ones were dated—because he'd been methodical then, because methodical was how he survived everything—in a slanting hand from the autumn ten years ago, and the later ones weren't dated at all, because at some point he'd stopped believing in timelines.

He hadn't read them since he wrote them. He was almost sure of that.

He took out the first one. The envelope tore slightly under his thumb, along a seam already weakened by a decade of the drawer sticking, and the single page inside slid into the lamplight, and there was his own handwriting, younger, rounder, less disciplined, and the first line hit him like stepping into a cold lake:

*Eli — I know you're waiting for me to call. I can see you waiting. That's the worst part, that I can see it.*

He remembered writing that. He remembered the kitchen table in the apartment above the old bakery, three months after the funeral, the pen dragging because his hands wouldn't warm up anymore, grief living in him then not as sadness but as cold, a cold no amount of heat touched. He remembered thinking: *if I write it down, I won't have to say it.* And then he remembered the moment the letter was finished, sealed, addressed—and the moment after that, when he stood at the mailbox at the end of the block with the envelope in his hand and understood, with total certainty, that mailing it would mean saying it. Out loud. To another person. In the world. Where it could be answered.

He hadn't mailed it. He'd walked home and started a second letter instead, because surely the second one would say it better, and the second one would be the one he sent.

There were forty-one letters in the box. None of them had been good enough to send. That was the truth he'd built a life on top of, floor by floor, and now he stood in the basement of it, holding the first failed draft of a confession he had never finished making.

*I'm not writing to ask you to come back,* the letter said, further down. *I'm writing so that someday you'll know it wasn't you. It was the part of me that died in that hospital corridor and didn't have the decency to take the rest of me with it.*

Jonah set it down on the desk very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

The storm pressed against the building. He should stop. He should put them back, tie the twine, bury the box under the invoice paper, and never—this time he would make it true—never open the drawer again. He was good at not doing things. It was his finest skill. He had not called Eli for ten years; he had not sold the café; he had not cried at the grave or anywhere else; he had perfected the art of the unmailed, the unsaid, the unopened drawer.

But his hands were already in the box.

The third letter was longer. Four pages, written the winter after, when the café was new and failing in the ways it still failed now. *You'd laugh at the menu,* he'd written. *Half of it is yours. The cardamom thing is yours. I put your name nowhere and your fingerprints everywhere, and I tell myself that's not a message, but Priya—*

No. Not Priya. There was no Priya then. He'd written *but anyone with eyes*—anyone with eyes would look at that menu and see a man who had lost someone and rebuilt his whole life as a shrine and called it a business plan.

He flipped through the others without reading them fully, letting fragments rise: *the train platform, I still take the long way so I don't walk past it.* / *Danny liked you better than he liked me, and he was right to.* / *I dreamed you were at the counter and I made your order wrong on purpose so you'd stay and correct me.* / *It's been six years. I'm not writing to you anymore. This is just a list of things I'll say if I ever see you again.* / *It's been eight years.*

The last one was recent. That was the part that shamed him most—not the young grief, which at least had the excuse of being raw, but this: a letter written fourteen months ago, in his own current hand, steady and adult and no braver. *Eli — I heard you might come through town for the Marchetti wedding. I practiced saying hello. I got as far as the door.*

He had gotten as far as the door. He had stood in it, actually, the night of the wedding, coat on, keys in hand, and then he had turned around and prepped tomorrow's dough and told himself the feeling in his chest was indigestion.

Forty-one letters. Ten years. A man could build a prison out of less.

A board creaked out front.

Jonah's whole body went still—the animal stillness of guilt, faster than thought. He listened. The hearth ticked. The storm roared its long white roar. Then, unmistakably, footsteps: the particular uneven rhythm of someone navigating a dark room they don't know, a hand finding furniture, a low breath.

Eli.

Of course it was Eli. Priya slept like a woman whose conscience required nothing of her. But Eli had never slept well in strange places—or familiar ones, Jonah amended, remembering a decade of mornings finding him already awake, already watching the ceiling, already somewhere slightly ahead of the day.

And he was walking toward the office.

Jonah looked down at the desk. The box sat open under the lamp like a wound. Letters fanned across the surface—one in his hand, three on the wood, the torn envelope gaping. The lamp lit all of it like a stage. There was nowhere to put it. There was a filing cabinet two feet away and a shelf within reach and Jonah's body, which could laminate dough in the dark and pull a tray at the exact second of perfect gold, simply stopped working, hands hovering, mind blank, ten years of composure dissolving into the single dumb panic of a man caught exactly where he swore he'd never be caught.

The footsteps stopped outside the door.

For one absurd second Jonah considered turning off the lamp and standing in the dark among the boxes, as if darkness had ever once hidden anything from Eli Marchetti, who had spent two years learning the geography of his silences.

The door opened.

Eli filled the frame, backlit by the faint ember-glow from the front room, hair flattened on one side, wearing the sweater Jonah had lent him without comment an hour ago—a sweater that had hung in the office closet for years because throwing it out had also been a thing Jonah hadn't gotten around to. His eyes went to Jonah first. Then to the desk.

Jonah watched him read the scene the way he read everything: quickly, completely, and without any visible reaction until it was too late to pretend otherwise. The box. The envelopes. The name on them, repeated forty-one times, in handwriting Eli would know anywhere, because he'd once received actual letters in that hand, back when Jonah still believed words could be sent.

Neither of them moved.

"I heard the drawer," Eli said finally, quietly. "The one that sticks."

"It sticks," Jonah agreed. His voice came out level. He was almost proud of it. "I was getting invoice paper."

"You were getting invoice paper." Eli's gaze drifted, unhurried, over the fanned pages, the torn envelope, the marker ghost on the lid: *DO NOT OPEN*. Something moved through his face—too fast to name, gone before it landed, swallowed the way Jonah himself swallowed everything. "In the dark. At three in the morning."

"The storm keeps me up."

"Yeah," Eli said softly. "Me too."

He didn't ask. That was the unbearable part. He stood there in the doorway with all that paper between them—ten years of a man talking to him from inside a locked drawer—and he didn't ask, didn't reach, didn't even lean. He just looked at Jonah with an expression Jonah recognized from the platform ten years ago, from the hospital corridor, from every doorway Eli had ever waited in: patient, wounded, done waiting.

"Goodnight, Jonah."

"Goodnight," Jonah said.

And Eli turned and walked back toward the hearth, and Jonah stood alone in the yellow cone of lamplight with his heart slamming and his hands shaking and forty-one unsent letters spread out behind him like evidence—which was, he understood dimly, exactly what they were.

He gathered them up fast, hardly breathing, shoved them into the box, dropped the lid, wound the twine twice, and slid the box onto the highest shelf, behind the binders, at an angle he told himself looked casual.

It did not look casual. It looked like a man hiding a box at three in the morning from a houseguest who had already seen it.

Jonah turned off the lamp and stood in the dark, listening to the storm, listening to the hearth settle, listening to the silence from the front room where Eli lay awake on a pile of cushions, knowing now—not guessing, *knowing*—that he had been written to, and written to, and written to, and never once reached.

Tomorrow, Jonah thought. Tomorrow he'd move the box somewhere better.

He knew, even as he thought it, that he wouldn't. And he knew, with a colder certainty rising through him like water through a floor, that the box was no longer the only thing in danger of being found—because Priya Nair missed nothing, because the drawer stuck loudly, and because in twelve hours she would be awake, and curious, and alone in this café with both of them.

The storm roared on, sealing the doors, keeping its secrets—for now.
