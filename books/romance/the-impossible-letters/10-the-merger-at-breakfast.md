## 10. The Merger at Breakfast

Eli woke to the smell of batter and something burning, and for three disoriented seconds he was twenty-six again, in a kitchen with a skylight and a man who sang off-key while he cooked.

Then memory reassembled itself: the storeroom cot, the wool blanket that smelled of cinnamon and dust, the storm still pressing its white shoulder against the windows. The café. Jonah's café. He sat up too fast and his neck protested, a crick he'd carry through the morning like a small accusation.

The main room was lit low and gold. Snow had drifted against the front glass in a smooth curve, waist-high, sculpted by wind into something almost architectural—a dune, a wave frozen mid-break. The world outside had been erased so thoroughly that the café felt like the last room on earth, and Eli stood in the doorway of the storeroom for a moment longer than necessary, watching Jonah at the griddle.

He'd forgotten how Jonah cooked. That was a lie—he hadn't forgotten anything, that was the whole problem—but watching it again was different from remembering it. The economy of motion. The way he held the spatula like an extension of his wrist, flipping pancakes without looking, his attention split between the heat and the window as if the storm might try something. He wore yesterday's shirt with the sleeves rolled to the forearm, and there was flour on the inside of his left elbow, a white crescent he clearly didn't know about, and Eli had to look away from it because some details were too much before coffee.

"You're up," Jonah said, without turning. Of course he'd heard him. Ten years ago Eli couldn't sneak up on him either; ten years from now, presumably, the same.

"Your cot has opinions about my spine."

"It's held worse." A pause, and then, dry: "It's held me."

Priya was already at the counter, cross-legged on one of the stools like she owned the lease, her phone propped against the napkin dispenser, scrolling through something with the focused calm of a person who had decided the apocalypse could wait until after breakfast. She'd claimed the corner booth last night—the only seating not buried under storage boxes—and made her bed out of coats and dignity. She looked, impossibly, rested.

"There's coffee," she said, nodding toward the machine. "Jonah made a full pot at six and has been guarding it like a dragon. I've been rationing myself."

"I heard that," Jonah said.

"You were meant to."

Eli poured himself a cup. The mug was heavy, diner-grade, chipped at the rim, and when he lifted it he saw the logo—*The Copper Kettle*, faded gold lettering—and felt the strangest vertigo, because this had been their mug. Or mugs exactly like it. He'd drunk a thousand mornings out of cups like this one, back when mornings were a thing they had together, and now here it was in his hand again, warm and ordinary, as if the decade between had been nothing but a long walk around the block.

"Sit," Jonah said. "Pancakes are almost—" The smoke alarm chose that moment to chirp once, preemptively, and Jonah glared at the ceiling as if it were a traitor. "Almost ready."

"You still burn the first one," Priya observed.

"The first one's a sacrifice," Jonah said. "To the pan gods."

"To the pan gods," Priya repeated solemnly, and Eli caught it—the flicker of something in Jonah's face, surprise maybe, or the beginning of a laugh he swallowed. She did that, Priya. She found the seam in a person within minutes and pressed her thumb against it gently, just to see what came out. It was why she was brilliant at negotiation and terrible at letting things go.

Eli took the stool at the end of the counter, the one farthest from the griddle, and told himself the distance was about giving Jonah workspace. It wasn't. It was arithmetic. Proximity to Jonah Vasquez had always had a cost curve, and Eli had spent ten years learning what happened when you stopped tracking it.

* * *

The pancakes arrived on mismatched plates—three plates, because Jonah had set three places without being asked, without making it a thing, and Eli watched him do it and thought: *he still sets the table like we're staying.*

They ate. For a few minutes there was only the sound of forks and the wind worrying the eaves and the particular silence of three people pretending the fourth presence in the room—ten years of history, sitting at the counter like an uninvited guest—wasn't there.

Priya broke it the way she broke everything: cleanly, without apology.

"So," she said, spearing a piece of pancake. "Lisbon."

Eli's fork paused. "Priya."

"What? I'm making conversation." Her tone was innocent in the way a scalpel is innocent. "We're snowed in, there's no signal worth mentioning, and I have four days of agenda items I can't action. Consider this a working breakfast."

"We're eating pancakes in a blizzard."

"Pancakes are not a jurisdictional barrier to due diligence." She turned to face him fully, and Eli recognized the posture—the slight forward lean, the pen she'd produced from nowhere held loosely between two fingers. Boardroom Priya. He'd seen her deploy it across a table from men twice her age and watch them fold. "Walk me through your timeline again. You fly out Thursday?"

"Thursday," Eli said. "Signing's Monday. Lisbon time."

"And the relocation clause kicks in within thirty days of signature. You'll need housing sorted by March, the transition team wants you embedded by the second quarter—"

"Priya." He said it more firmly this time, and felt Jonah go very still beside him, the way a person goes still when they've realized they're standing in the blast radius of something. "This isn't the venue."

"This is the *only* venue. We're trapped in a snow globe." She gestured with her fork at the white wall of the window. "Unless you'd like to step outside and continue this at forty below."

Jonah set down his fork. The sound it made against the plate was very small and very loud.

"Four days?" he said.

Eli turned. Jonah wasn't looking at Priya. He was looking at Eli, and his face had done something complicated—the civility of the last eighteen hours peeling back to show the machinery underneath, the calculations running behind his eyes.

"The signing is Monday," Eli said. "I told you I was—"

"You said you were in town for business." Jonah's voice was level. Carefully, expensively level, the way a floor is level right before it gives way. "You didn't say the business was leaving the country."

"It's a merger. There's a European arm. Lisbon is the hub." Each word came out flat, factual, defensive. Eli heard himself doing it—hiding inside the language of contracts because the other language, the human one, had failed him once and he'd never fully recovered the nerve. "I signed on to lead the integration eight months ago."

"Eight months." Jonah repeated it like he was testing the weight of it. His eyes moved—not to Priya, not to the window, but to the middle distance, where math happens. Eli could see him doing it. Counting backward. Eight months ago was spring; eight months ago Eli had already known, had already been living inside the decision, had probably already packed some internal version of his bags. Eight months ago Eli had walked into a conference in Boston and given a keynote and answered questions and smiled at strangers, all while carrying a departure date in his pocket like a stone.

And somewhere in those eight months, Eli had come back to this town. Driven past this café. Twice.

"Right," Jonah said. He picked up his fork again. Put it down. Stood, instead, and carried his plate to the sink with the exaggerated calm of a man who needed his hands occupied or they'd do something else entirely. "More pancakes?"

"No," Eli said.

"I'll have more," Priya said, which was either kindness or strategy, and with Priya it was never possible to say which, though Eli suspected it was both, always both, the generosity and the maneuver fused at the root.

Jonah scraped batter onto the griddle. The sizzle filled the silence, and Eli understood suddenly, viscerally, what was happening in the other man's head—not jealousy, or not only jealousy, but arithmetic. He could see it happening the way you can see weather coming across a flat field. Jonah was running numbers. Four days. Eight months. Ten years. The cost of one more departure weighed against the cost of caring that it was happening. And Eli knew, with the sick certainty of someone reading his own autopsy, exactly which column Jonah would make balance.

Because that was the thing about Jonah that Eli had never been able to explain to anyone who hadn't loved him: he didn't run from feeling. He *audited* it. He sat down with his heart like a ledger and he found the line items that didn't justify themselves and he cut them, cleanly, without malice, and called it discipline. Danny died and Jonah audited his own grief and concluded that love was a liability he could no longer carry, and he'd closed that account and walked out of the funeral and never reopened it. Not for Eli. Not for anyone.

And now the ledger was open again, and Eli could feel himself being entered into it—*asset or liability, asset or liability*—and he hated how much he wanted to know the answer, and hated more that he already did.

"Eat your pancakes," Priya said quietly, to Jonah, to the room. "Cold pancakes are a crime in at least nine jurisdictions."

Jonah brought the fresh ones over. He set Eli's plate down with a precision that was its own kind of message, and sat back down at the counter—not at the far stool this time, but directly across from Eli, close enough that their knees nearly touched under the counter's overhang, and Eli couldn't tell if it was a challenge or a surrender or simply that the far stool had gotten cold.

"Lisbon," Jonah said. "How long is the assignment?"

"The initial term is three years." Priya answered before Eli could, smoothly, mercilessly. "With renewal options."

"Three years." Jonah nodded slowly, absorbing it, filing it. "And after?"

"After," Eli said, "it depends on results."

"Everything does."

The words landed between them with a weight that had nothing to do with mergers. Eli held his gaze and Jonah held his, and for a moment the counter between them might have been a negotiating table, might have been a kitchen table ten years gone, might have been the aisle of a church where neither of them had ever managed to stand.

Priya, mercifully or cruelly, looked down at her phone. "Signal's flickering," she murmured. "One bar. Maybe two."

"Enjoy it while it lasts," Jonah said, and his voice had recovered its evenness, its trained calm, and Eli wanted to shake him by the shoulders and say *stop doing that, stop making yourself smooth, I remember what you look like when you're rough*—

But he didn't. Because he was leaving in four days. Because he'd already left, eight months ago, in every way that counted. Because the ledger had spoken and the ledger was rarely wrong.

He ate his pancakes. They were perfect. Of course they were perfect. The man could burn sugar and laminate butter into something like silk and flip pancakes without looking, and none of it, none of it, had ever been enough to make him stay.

Outside, the wind shifted, and something struck the window—a branch, ice, the world trying to get back in. One bar of signal bloomed on Priya's phone, and she glanced at it, and then, deliberately, Eli saw her turn the phone face-down on the counter.

She caught him looking. She raised one eyebrow, almost imperceptibly, and said nothing at all.

And Eli, who had spent ten years learning to read silences, understood that he had just been given something—a reprieve, a warning, a door left open a crack—and that whatever Priya Nair had decided she saw in this snowbound room, she was not finished with any of them yet.
