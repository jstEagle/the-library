## 7. Terms of the Merger

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Theo would later think was fitting—nothing about the Winterbournes ever happened on a day that mattered. It came by courier, not post, in an envelope heavy enough to feel like a verdict. Cream stock, embossed crest, his name typed rather than handwritten. *Mr. T. Marsh.* Not even the courtesy of ink.

He was still at the archive when it found him, elbow-deep in a crate of 1940s seaside cards that smelled of salt and mildew and other people's summers. The courier waited while he wiped his hands on his trousers and signed for it, and Theo understood before he broke the seal that this was not correspondence. This was a transaction.

Inside: a single sheet, and beneath it, a check.

The figure had five zeros. He read it twice, the way one reads a word in a foreign language hoping repetition will translate it. The accompanying letter was brief, elegant, and surgical. *In light of your valuable work with the Brightwater Historical Society, Mrs. Vivian Winterbourne wishes to fund a permanent endowment for the archive—on the condition that certain materials relating to the Winterbourne family estate be transferred to private custody and that you accept a research position abroad, effective immediately. The Society's board has been made aware of this generous opportunity.*

*Generous.* Theo set the page down on the reading table with exaggerated care, as if it might detonate. Outside the tall windows, the harbor did its patient afternoon glittering. A gull hung motionless over the water. Somewhere behind him, the dehumidifier hummed its one flat note—the sound the archive made when nothing was wrong.

He thought about Bram's face at dinner three nights ago, the way she'd laughed at his terrible joke about Dewey decimal numbers and then gone quiet all at once, like a radio losing signal. He thought about the letters in the estate box—Elias Winterbourne's firm hand, the merger papers dated weeks before the accident, the sentence he'd copied out and then sat staring at until the lamp buzzed: *the girl will be told what serves us.*

They knew. That was the thing that made his hands cold. They didn't suspect he'd found the letters; they *knew*, which meant someone had been watching him, which meant the corruption wasn't a buried thing at all but a living one, tended, watered, defended. And now they were offering to buy him the way you buy out a minority shareholder—not because he mattered, but because he was inconvenient.

He didn't touch the check. He photographed it instead, front and back, and slid everything back into the envelope, and carried it home like evidence, because that was what it was.

* * *

Vivian Winterbourne chose her battlegrounds the way she chose table linens: nothing accidental, everything coordinated. So Theo was not surprised—but was nonetheless unsettled—to find her waiting for him two days later at the archive itself, seated in the reading room with her hands folded, wearing pearl gray and an expression of serene benevolence, as though she owned the building. In a sense, she was about to try to.

"Mr. Marsh." She gestured to the chair across from her. "Thank you for seeing me."

"You didn't ask to see me. You just arrived."

A smile, thin as tissue paper. "I find appointments invite refusal."

He sat anyway. Up close she looked like Bram in the way a portrait looks like a person—correct in every line, animated by nothing. Her eyes were the same gray-blue, but where Bram's flickered and doubted and burned, Vivian's simply assessed. He felt catalogued. Filed. Deaccessioned, almost.

"I'll be direct," she said. "My daughter is going through a difficult period. She's always been imaginative. Grief does that—it lets people rewrite things." She smoothed an invisible wrinkle from her skirt. "You've been a comfort to her, I'm sure. But the reunion is over, the wedding approaches, and Bram needs stability, not... archaeology."

"Is that what we're calling it."

"I'm calling it what it is." Her voice stayed pleasant; her eyes did not. "You've been into my father-in-law's papers. I know about the letters. I know what you think you've found." She let that sit between them like a dropped glass. "And I'm here to tell you what you've actually found: a young woman's tragedy that this family managed with dignity, and a business decision that saved four hundred employees' livelihoods. You can call it corruption from your little room with your little cards. I call it stewardship."

"The merger was signed six weeks before the accident," Theo said quietly. "Daniel Hale ended the engagement by letter. Bram never ran anyone off any road. There was no accident at all—at least not the one the family tells."

Something moved behind Vivian's eyes—fast, quickly caged. "There was a car in a ravine, Mr. Marsh. There were surgeries. There are scars."

"There's a version of events everyone agreed to," Theo said. "Including, eventually, Bram herself. That's not dignity. That's a hostage situation with better catering."

For a moment—one unguarded half-second—Vivian looked tired. Ancient, even. Then the armor came down again, polished and seamless. "Here is what will happen," she said. "The endowment is real. The position in Edinburgh is real. A respected institution, your own archive, your name on the door. Or—" she rose, gathered her gloves, "—you can stay, publish whatever fantasy you've assembled, and discover how small a town's memory is when the family that funds it asks it to forget you. The Historical Society answers to its donors. Its donors answer to me. You'd be surprised how quickly a man can become unlisted."

"You'd erase him," Theo said. "Like you erased her."

Vivian paused at the door. For a heartbeat she was almost human. "I kept her alive," she said. "That's more than Daniel Hale left me to work with." And then she was gone, pearls clicking against the doorframe, leaving the reading room smelling faintly of vetiver and threat.

Theo sat for a long time afterward, watching dust turn in the window light above the postcard crates. The impossible card lay in its mylar sleeve in the drawer at his elbow—the woman in yellow, laughing on the Brightwater lawn, dated three days before a crash that never happened the way anyone said. He touched the sleeve once, lightly, the way you touch something you're afraid of losing.

Then he called the Society's director, and learned that his access to the estate collection had already been suspended pending review.

* * *

Bram called him at nine that night, and he knew before she spoke. He could hear it in her breathing—the particular controlled calm of a woman holding something broken very carefully so it doesn't rattle.

"They cut off my credentials today," she said. "The firm. Marguerite called it an 'administrative review.' My own clients are being reassigned. The Ashford wedding—my sister's wedding, Theo—they've assigned a co-planner. Someone to 'support' me." A laugh with no humor in it, thin as ice over water. "Three days before the event. They're severing me in installments so it won't look like what it is."

"What is it?"

"A message." She was quiet a moment. "It's for both of us, isn't it? Whatever they said to you—I can hear it in your voice too."

Theo looked at the envelope on his kitchen table, the check inside it fat as a folded bird. He thought about telling her everything—the offer, the threat, the way Vivian had said *I kept her alive* with something that had almost been grief. But she sounded like a woman standing on a ledge already, and he was done making decisions for other people. His mother used to say the truth was a gift you wrapped in timing, and he'd never learned the wrapping part.

"Yes," he said. "They offered me money to disappear. And a career to go with it."

Silence. Then, small and steady: "Are you going to take it?"

"No."

Another silence, longer. When she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, stripped of polish, the voice under all her voices. "Theo. Before the wedding. Before Saturday. If there's anything else—if there's anything you haven't told me—I need you to say it now. Because tomorrow I'm going to stand in my mother's kitchen and ask her to tell me the truth, and I need to walk in there knowing exactly how much worse it can get."

His chest tightened. He thought of the letters. *The girl will be told what serves us.* He thought of her aunt going pale at the mention of a postcard. He thought of Bram asleep against his shoulder on the porch swing at Brightwater House, trusting him with her whole unconscious weight.

"Bram—"

"Don't protect me." Fierce now, cracking open. "Everyone has protected me. That's the whole problem. Everyone decided what I could survive, and I lost three years to their arithmetic. Don't you dare do the math too."

He closed his eyes. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one along the quay, small and stubborn against the dark.

"There's a letter," he said. "From Daniel Hale, ending the engagement. Dated six weeks before the accident. Your mother kept it. And there's a memo in your grandfather's hand about the merger, and a line in it I can't stop reading." He made himself say it plainly, no cushioning, because she'd asked for plain. "*The girl will be told what serves us.*"

The line went so quiet he checked whether she'd hung up. Then, barely audible:

"Saturday," Bram said. "Her toast is second. Mine is third."

She ended the call before he could answer, and Theo stood alone in his kitchen with the check and the dark and the sound of a woman deciding, three hundred miles of phone line away, to burn her life down—and sounding, for the first time since he'd met her, entirely awake.
