## 2. Small Safe Steps

There is an order to the mornings here, and I have learned to trust it the way other people trust God.

First the kettle. Then the stairs, all eighty-seven of them, counted in the dark so my body knows where it is before the light does. Then the lamp room: check the lens, wipe the salt haze from the glass with a rag dampened in vinegar, log the barometer. The sea gives its report. I write it down. For fifteen years this has been the whole of my conversation with the world, and I have found it more honest than most.

This morning the barometer reads low and falling. The gulls are inland, sitting fat and silent on the breakwater like jurors waiting for evidence. I write *storm coming* in the log, and my hand is steady, and I am proud of that, the steadiness, the way a woman who once shook so badly she couldn't hold a cup is proud of a steady hand.

My grandfather taught me the routine. He taught it to me the way you teach anything to someone who is hiding: without questions.

* * *

His name was Elio Marchetti, and he was the last man who ever called me by my real name.

I arrived on this island in the spring I was twenty-four, with two suitcases and a parole paper folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth. The ferry captain didn't look at me. The islanders didn't either, which was the point. My grandfather had made arrangements the way he made everything—quietly, with rope and salt and no wasted words. A granddaughter, he told the harbor committee. His son's girl, orphaned now, needing work and sea air.

He never asked what happened. That was the miracle of him. Other people would have asked, and their asking would have been a knife whether they meant it or not. Elio just handed me a rag and pointed at the lens and said, *Salt gets in everything. You fight it daily or it wins.*

I understood he was talking about more than salt.

The name I left behind had been in the newspapers for eleven months. *Restaurant fire. Arson. Young woman convicted.* The name had a weight to it, a smell, a shape in the mouth that made strangers flinch. So I buried it the way you bury anything you can't cremate—deep, and in the dark, and with a marker over the grave so you can visit but not dig.

Rafael. He chose it himself, my grandfather, testing it in his mouth at the kitchen table while I sat with my hands around a mug of tea I couldn't drink. *Rafael. A good name. Means God has healed.* He said it like a man laying a first stone. *It doesn't have to be true yet. Names can grow into themselves.*

I have spent fifteen years growing into it. Some days I fit. Some days the name hangs on me like a coat cut for a braver woman.

* * *

The present: I make coffee in the cottage kitchen, and I do the small safe steps of my life. Feed the gull who thinks he owns the porch—I call him Senator, because he takes bribes and delivers nothing. Mend the hinge on the storm door. Walk the gallery rail with my hand on the iron, checking the bolts the way my grandfather taught me, one by one, because the sea works at everything and only vigilance is a match for it.

The island is small enough that invisibility is a craft you can master. There are forty-one year-round residents and I know all their routines and they know mine, and what they know is this: the keeper's granddaughter, quiet, keeps to herself, writes her little stories, bakes bread on Thursdays. Mrs. Okafor gets the cinnamon loaf. The Delgados get plain. The postmistress, who knows everything on this island except the one thing that matters, sets aside the writing magazines for me and asks each month, with the patience of a woman who has asked for a decade, *When do we get to read one?*

*Soon,* I say. *Soon.* It is the most fluent lie I speak.

The truth is the manuscripts go out and come back. The truth is sitting on my desk upstairs, opened yesterday, dated the anniversary, as if the universe has a sense of timing I resent. *Too honest about guilt,* the editor wrote, and I have turned the phrase over all morning like a stone with something underneath it. Too honest. As if honesty were a flavor you could overdo. As if the problem with my book were that it told the truth instead of a prettier lie.

I could write a prettier lie. I've had fifteen years of practice living one.

* * *

Here is what I remember about prison, and I remember it in pieces, the way you remember a shipwreck—by what floated.

I remember the sound the cell door made. Not the clang they put in movies. A softer thing, a heavy sliding, like the sea deciding. I remember that I learned to sleep with my back to the wall because it meant no one stood behind me. I remember a woman named Dot who taught me to braid hair and never asked why a twenty-two-year-old had stopped crying and started counting. I remember counting. I counted everything—steps, days, the cracks in the ceiling, which numbered forty and I could have drawn them from memory for the rest of my life.

I remember that I did not cry at the sentencing, and that my not crying was reported in the paper as coldness. *Showed no emotion.* What the reporter didn't know—what no one knew—was that I had made a promise, and the promise had a price, and the price was my mother's care, and a woman watching her mother die one ward at a time learns that tears are a luxury billed to someone else.

I set no fire. I want to write that here, in ink, in my own hand, in the journal I keep locked in the drawer with my grandfather's logbooks: *I did not set that fire.* But the fire took the restaurant, and the restaurant was the Vane brothers' inheritance and my family's whole history—my parents had cooked there, met there, and I had grown up in its back booth doing homework in the smell of garlic and scorched tomatoes—and someone had to burn for it, and the someone was arranged to be me, and I was twenty-two and in love with one brother and believed by the other, and I said yes.

Not *I did it.* I said *yes* to the shape of it. To the plea. To the impossible promise that if I swallowed the fire, my mother would be cared for until the end, and the man I loved would never have to know what his brother was.

I have rehearsed the confession so many times it has grooves worn in it, like a stair. *I need to tell you what really happened.* Eight words. I have said them into the mirror, into the sea, into the dark of the lamp room where only the light and I could hear. I have never once said them to a living soul.

* * *

My grandfather died in my eleventh winter here, quietly, in the chair by the window, with the logbook open on his knee and the pen still in his hand. The last entry in his handwriting reads: *Wind SW, moderate. Lamp steady. Granddaughter capable.*

Granddaughter capable. Three words. It was the whole of his will and the whole of his blessing, and when the lawyer read the rest—*the light and its keeping, to my granddaughter, with all duties and all love*—I understood that Elio Marchetti had known, all along, exactly what he was leaving me. Not just the light.

The lie. The lie came with it, packed like china. Because the light needed a keeper, and the keeper needed a name, and the name needed a story, and by then I had lived the story so long it had calluses.

I buried him on the north point where he could watch the channel forever, and I came back down and climbed the eighty-seven stairs and lit the lamp, because that is what keepers do, and because grief is just another kind of weather and the routine holds against it. I have lit that lamp every night for fifteen years. The Coast Guard automated it four years ago—the bulb turns itself now, the sensors do the watching—but I still climb, still wipe the glass, still log the barometer, because a thing you tend by hand stays yours, and I needed one thing in my life that was mine all the way through.

The light is honest. It doesn't know my name. It just turns, and turns, and tells the truth to ships: *here is the danger, here is the edge, here is where you must steer away.* I have sometimes thought it was the only confession I was capable of. *Here is the danger. Here is the edge. Steer away from me.*

* * *

Tonight I write in the journal, the one nobody will ever read, the one where the sentence lives.

I write the date first. Fifteen years. I write the weather. Then I sit with the pen above the line, and I write the beginning of it, the way I have written the beginning of it a hundred times:

*I need to tell you what really happened. Fifteen years ago I—*

And there it stops. It stops the way it always stops, at the edge of the drop, my pen hovering over the truth like a gull over open water. Below the unfinished line I write what I always write, the small safe step, the sentence that lets me close the book and sleep:

*Tomorrow.*

I blow out the lamp on the desk. In the window, the lighthouse beam sweeps past, once, twice, patient as a heartbeat. Somewhere out there the barometer is falling and the gulls know it and the sea is gathering itself, and tomorrow the harbormaster will come up the path with his collar up and his face grave, and I will log the weather, and I will not know yet that the weather is not the worst thing coming.

Tomorrow, I write. And for fifteen years, tomorrow has kept the promise that I couldn't.

The wind rises against the glass like a hand testing the latch.
