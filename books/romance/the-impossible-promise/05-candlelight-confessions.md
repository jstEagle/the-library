## 5. Candlelight Confessions

The lights died at 9:47.

Marcus Vane would remember the exact time later, because he was looking at his watch when it happened—watching the second hand sweep toward the hour the way he'd been watching it all evening, waiting for something to go wrong. It was a habit of his, expecting the worst. It had kept him solvent through two bankruptcies and one marriage. It had never once prepared him for the actual shape of disaster, which always arrived sideways, wearing a friendly face.

The lamps along the gallery rail flickered once, twice, and went out. The cottage went dark except for the red pulse of the emergency beacon in the tower above them and the white chaos of the storm beyond the windows.

"Great," Julian said from the armchair by the cold fireplace. "Trapped on an island with no power, no ferry, and you."

"The feeling's mutual," Marcus said.

He heard rather than saw the keeper's granddaughter move through the kitchen—a soft shuffle, the clink of glass—and then a match flared, and she lit three candles in quick succession, cupping each flame with her hand like someone who had done this many times before. The light came up amber and small, and her face floated out of the dark: pale, watchful, thirty-something, forgettable. That was the word that had snagged in him since they'd arrived. *Forgettable.* And yet there was something underneath it, some current running too deep to see, the way you can stand in shallow water and feel the pull of something enormous below.

"There's a generator," she said, "but my grandfather only ran it for the lamp mechanism. Fuel's scarce. Candles will do."

"Candles will do," Julian repeated softly, and Marcus caught the look his brother gave her—the look Julian gave every woman who ever handed him anything, warm and grateful and slightly wounded, as if kindness were a surprise he'd never learned to expect. Fifteen years and the man hadn't changed. He still collected small mercies like other men collected debts.

Marcus took the whiskey bottle off the counter without asking. It was a decent single malt, better than he'd expected to find in a lighthouse cottage, and he poured three fingers into the first glass he found and didn't offer any to anyone.

"Help yourself," the woman said dryly.

"I intend to."

She didn't smile. She took the second candle and climbed the stairs to the lamp room, and Marcus watched her go, watched the flame climb the spiral stair and shrink to a spark and vanish into the tower's throat, and felt the strangest sensation of having seen this exact image before—the light ascending, the dark closing behind it—though he couldn't say where, or when, or why it made the back of his neck prickle.

* * *

They drank in silence for an hour while the storm tried to take the island apart.

It was a sound Marcus had never heard before and hoped never to hear again: not wind exactly but a sustained assault, the sea hurling itself against rock with a rhythm like artillery, and between the volleys a high whistling shriek through the window frames that made conversation impossible anyway. So they sat in the amber pools of candlelight—Julian reading one of the keeper's paperbacks, a water-stained thriller with a cracked spine; Marcus working through the bottle at a pace he'd have called moderate if anyone had asked—and the silence between them filled up with all the things fifteen years had deposited there.

Their father's estate, unsettled. The restaurant name, sold. The house in Brookline, emptied of everything but arguments. Their mother, dead four years now, having spent her last decade asking each son privately what the other had said about her, and believing neither.

"You could have come to the funeral," Julian said finally, not looking up from his book.

"I did come to the funeral."

"You came late and left early and stood by your car."

"It was raining."

"It was raining," Julian agreed, and turned a page.

Marcus drank. This was how it went with them now—conversations conducted entirely in fragments, each man lobbing half-sentences across the gulf and watching them fall short. Their father used to do the same thing at dinner, Marcus thought. Two wars' worth of things unsaid, passed down like china nobody wanted but nobody would throw away.

He should stop drinking. He knew that. Whiskey loosened him in the wrong direction—not toward warmth but toward honesty, and honesty was the one asset he'd spent fifteen years liquidating.

Outside, something heavy struck the side of the cottage—loose shutter, maybe, or a piece of the boathouse roof—and both of them flinched, and then pretended they hadn't.

"She handles it well," Julian said. "The storm. You'd think she'd be nervous, alone up here with strangers."

"She's not alone. She's home." Marcus heard his own voice come out strange, thoughtful. "People who live in lighthouses don't get nervous during storms. Storms are just weather to them. It's the calm that makes them jumpy."

Julian looked over. "That's oddly specific."

"It's nothing." But it wasn't nothing, and Marcus knew it even as he said it. There was something about the woman—Rafael, she'd said, an odd name, an old-fashioned name—that kept snagging him like a fishhook in a sweater. The way she moved through the dark without hesitating. The way she'd frozen, just for a half-second, when Julian introduced himself at the door. *Vane,* he'd said, and her face had done something brief and terrible and gone, like a match struck underwater.

He told himself it was coincidence. He was very good at telling himself things.

* * *

By eleven the bottle was half gone and the storm had settled into its long grind, and Julian set down his book and said, quietly, in the voice he used when he was about to be decent about something, which was somehow more infuriating than if he'd been cruel:

"Fifteen years ago tonight."

Marcus's glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Don't," he said.

"Somebody should say it. Mom never could. Dad never would. Somebody has to mark it, Marcus, or it just—" Julian gestured vaguely at the dark window, the roaring sea. "It just keeps happening every year in silence until we're dead."

"And saying it out loud fixes what, exactly? Brings the restaurant back? Makes us brothers again?" Marcus laughed, and hated the sound of it, wet and thin in the candlelight. "You want to memorialize the night our family burned down. Fine. Light a candle. Say a prayer. Just don't ask me to hold hands with you over the ashes."

"I'm not asking you to hold hands. I'm asking you to tell me the truth for once in your life."

"And what truth is that?"

"That you know more about that fire than you've ever admitted."

The words landed in the room and lay there, glowing, dangerous, like a coal dropped on the rug. Marcus made himself breathe. Made himself keep his face arranged in its usual expression of bored contempt, the one he wore to board meetings and funerals alike.

"You've been nursing that theory for fifteen years," he said. "The insurance payout, the missing ledger, whatever else your lawyer brain has stitched together from nothing. I've told you a hundred times. I was in Boston that night. I have alibis."

"Alibis can be bought."

"So can lawyers. Ask yours."

Julian leaned forward. In the candlelight his face looked older than fifty-two, carved into deeper lines than Marcus remembered, and his eyes had the patient, terrible steadiness of a man who had been waiting a long time and intended to keep waiting. "I don't want to destroy you, Marcus. God knows I've had fifteen years to try and haven't. I want to know why our father died without speaking to you. I want to know why you flinch every year on this date like a dog that's been kicked. I want to know what it costs you, because I've watched it cost you everything, and you won't let anyone help you carry it."

"Maybe it doesn't weigh anything," Marcus said. "Maybe I'm just a bad person. Have you considered the simple answer?"

"I've considered every answer." Julian sat back. "Except one."

The wind screamed against the glass. Somewhere above them, faintly, the lamp turned—*sweep, sweep, sweep*—the great lens rotating through its endless circuit, throwing light out over black water whether anyone needed it or not. Marcus stared into his glass. The whiskey had gotten past his defenses; he could feel the truth pressing up against the back of his teeth like a tide against a seawall, and the wall was old, and tired, and cracked in places he'd stopped inspecting years ago.

And maybe it was the whiskey. Maybe it was the date. Maybe it was the strange quiet woman somewhere overhead in the dark, keeping her light burning. But he heard himself speak before he decided to.

"They caught someone," he said.

Julian went very still. "What?"

"For the fire." His voice sounded far away to himself, flat, recited. "Someone was convicted. Arson, felony murder—the night watchman's death got pinned on it too, eventually. Fifteen years. She's still inside, last I heard. Or she isn't. They let them out early sometimes, when they're dying."

The room tilted slightly. Julian had set down his book. "A *she*?"

"A girl." Marcus turned the glass in his fingers, watching the candle flame double itself in the amber. "Young. Twenty-two, twenty-three. Nobody we knew. A—employee's daughter, something like that. She confessed. Open and shut. The lawyers were falling over themselves, case closed, insurance settled, everyone free to grieve properly." He smiled, and it felt like a wound opening. "Everyone free."

"Why have I never heard this?" Julian's voice had gone very quiet, which was worse than loud. "Fifteen years, Marcus. Not one word. Not from you, not from Dad—"

"Dad knew."

The admission slipped out before he could catch it, and Marcus watched it land, watched his brother's face change—the blood leaving it in stages, like a tide going out.

"Dad *knew*," Julian repeated.

"He paid for the defense, at first. Then he stopped. Then he paid for other things." Marcus drained his glass and poured another, his hand steady in the way of a man performing steadiness. "He made me promise never to discuss it. Family matter, he said. Private grief. He said the girl made her choice." The words tasted like ash and copper. "He said that. *Made her choice.* As if—"

He stopped. Closed his mouth. The seawall held, barely, groaning.

"As if what?" Julian asked.

"As if she had one," Marcus finished, very softly.

For a long moment neither brother spoke. The storm filled the silence for them, and the lamp swept its light overhead, and the candles bent and straightened, bent and straightened, like men bowing to something unseen.

"What was her name?" Julian asked at last.

"No."

"Marcus—"

"*No.*" It came out harder than he meant, hard enough to crack against the walls. He stood, too fast, and the room swayed with him. "You don't get her name. You don't get to make her real. She's a sentence in a court record, that's all, that's all she ever was, and if you dig into it—if you go poking at that with your righteous face and your appeals and your justice—"

"If I dig into it, what?" Julian rose too. The brothers faced each other across the candlelight, close enough that Marcus could smell the whiskey on both of them, close enough to see that his brother's hands were shaking. "What happens, Marcus? What are you afraid I'll find?"

*Everything,* Marcus thought. *You'll find everything. You'll find me at nineteen, standing in the parking lot with a can of kerosene I told myself I wouldn't use. You'll find the phone call I made afterward, and the man who answered it, and the price of that call compounding annually for fifteen years like the worst debt of my life. You'll find her face when they led her out of the courtroom, and how she looked at me—only me, not Dad, not the judge, *me*—and how she didn't say a word, because I'd promised her mother's care would continue, because I'd promised, because she believed me.*

*You'll find that she loved you,* he thought, and the thought arrived unbidden and unbearable, from some sealed room in himself he'd bricked over long ago. *Not me. Never me. She loved you, and I used it.*

"Nothing," he said aloud. "There's nothing to find. Forget I said anything. I'm drunk and it's the anniversary and I said too much."

"You said almost nothing," Julian said. "That's the problem. You said almost nothing, and it's the most you've given me in fifteen years."

Above them, the floorboards creaked.

Both men looked up. The stairwell door stood open at the top of the short flight, and the darkness beyond it was total—but Marcus had the sudden, sickening certainty that the darkness was not empty. That the keeper's granddaughter, Rafael, whose name snagged at him like a hook, whose face struck matches underwater, had been standing in that dark long enough to hear every word.

Then the floorboards creaked again, retreating, and a door closed somewhere above, soft as breath, and the lamp swept its light around and around, and outside the sea threw itself against the rocks as if trying to reach them.

Marcus sat back down and put his head in his hands.

"She heard," Julian said quietly.

"She heard nothing. There was nothing to hear."

But even as he said it, Marcus was remembering the way she'd frozen at the door when Julian said their surname. The way she'd climbed those same stairs with a candle, silent as smoke. The way she'd asked, earlier, so casually, pouring coffee: *Are either of you from the city originally? I know so many families from the city.*

He looked at his brother, and his brother looked back, and between them the candles guttered in a draft from the stairwell—as if something had just passed down it, going the other way.
