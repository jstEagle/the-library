## 11. Trapped Together

The phone died at 4:17 in the morning.

I know because I was awake to hear it—not the silence itself, which came without ceremony, but the small mechanical gasp the old landline made as it went, a sound like a held breath finally released. Fifteen years of living alone with that phone and I had never once heard it die. It had survived hurricanes. It had survived my grandfather's death, when I sat beside it for three days waiting for a call that never came from anyone who mattered.

Now it was dead, and so was every line to the mainland, and outside the kitchen window the world had been erased.

I stood at the sink with cold water running over my hands—I hadn't turned the tap on for that purpose; my hands had simply gone there, the way they always did when I needed something to do that wasn't thinking—and watched the storm take the harbor apart. The sea had come up over the seawall in the night. The moored dinghies were gone or submerged; I could see only the top of the harbormaster's flagpole, whipping like something alive trying to escape its own root.

Three people asleep upstairs. Three people who should have been on the other side of the water, and weren't.

I dried my hands on a towel that had belonged to my grandmother and told myself the truth I'd been circling since the brothers Vane arrived: the storm was not the worst thing in my house.

* * *

By seven the power was flickering. By eight it failed entirely, and the lighthouse went onto its backup generator with a shudder I felt through the floorboards—the old girl clearing her throat, reminding everyone she'd been doing this longer than any of us. The house dropped into candle-and-lamplight. The refrigerator fell silent. And the radio became the most important object on the island.

My grandfather's radio sat on the shelf above the desk in the front room: a marine band set, heavy as a Bible, dials worn smooth by forty years of his thumb. He'd taught me its language before he taught me to read properly. *Weather first, chatter second, mayday always.* I turned it on out of habit more than need, letting the static wash through the room while I lit lamps, and it was Julian who noticed what the static was saying.

He appeared in the doorway with two candles, one already guttering, and nodded at the radio. "Is that supposed to sound like that?"

"Like what?"

"Listen."

I listened. Under the white noise, a voice. Then another. The coast guard channel, patched through some relay that was holding on by its fingernails, and threaded through both of them, a third voice—calm, professional, repeating itself the way voices do when they're reading something official into an indifferent wind.

"—appeal filed Tuesday on behalf of Daniel Okafor, fifty-eight, serving twenty-five years for the 2010 arson of the Vane family restaurant in Harborview. Okafor's counsel cites newly surfaced evidence. Medical records entered this morning indicate Okafor is suffering from advanced congestive heart failure. His physicians estimate—"

The voice broke up. Static took it whole for ten seconds, fifteen, and I stood there with a lamp in one hand and my heart going like a trapped bird against my ribs.

*Daniel.*

Danny Okafor. Who had fixed the restaurant's walk-in freezer twice a year for as long as I could remember, who whistled show tunes badly and called me *piccola* because I was small and quick, who had a daughter in third grade who drew him pictures of the restaurant with all of us standing in front of it smiling. Danny, whose lawyer had begged him to take a plea and who refused because taking a plea meant saying he did it, and he hadn't done it, and Danny Okafor did not lie even when lying would have saved him.

Danny, who had looked at me across the courtroom—once, only once, on the day of sentencing—and not with hatred. With confusion. That was the unbearable part. Not hatred. Confusion, like a man trying to read a word written in a language he thought he knew.

"—estimate he may not survive the winter," the radio finished, when it found its voice again. "The appeals court has granted an expedited review. New evidence must be submitted within—"

Static again. Julian set the candles down on the desk and leaned close to the dial, tuning with fingers far steadier than mine would have been. Marcus stood behind him in the doorway, and I made myself look at him, made myself watch his face as the radio said the name *Okafor* again.

Marcus's face did nothing. That was the thing about Marcus; it was always the thing about Marcus. His face was a door painted on a wall. But his hands—he was holding a coffee mug he hadn't drunk from, and his knuckles were the color of the candle wax, and when the announcer said *newly surfaced evidence*, the mug trembled, a fine fast tremor, like a struck bell too small to hear.

He felt me looking. Our eyes met over his brother's shoulder, and Marcus set the mug down with exaggerated care, the way a man does when he's afraid of what his hands will say if he lets them talk.

"Bad reception up here," he said, to no one, to everyone.

"The appeal," Julian said, still bent to the radio. "They said new evidence. What evidence? Whose?"

Nobody answered him. The radio answered instead, fighting through: "—within fourteen days of today's filing—"

Fourteen days. The number landed in the room like a stone dropped down a well. I heard it hit bottom somewhere inside me.

Fourteen days, and the phone lines were dead, and the ferry had stopped running, and the storm outside was the worst in thirty years, and the only man who knew the truth about the fire was standing six feet away from me holding an empty mug, and the only man who could make him tell it didn't yet know why he should.

And me. I knew everything. I had known everything for fifteen years, and I had written it all down in a manuscript hidden in the boathouse, and I had rehearsed a confession in a journal until the ink wore grooves in the page, and none of it—none of it—had ever reached a courtroom, or a lawyer, or a dying man named Danny Okafor who whistled show tunes and called me *piccola* and was now, according to the radio, running out of winters.

* * *

Julian wanted to drive to the point where the signal might be better. There is no point on the island where the signal is better; I let him find that out himself, watching from the window as he stood on the gallery rail landing with the handheld receiver raised toward the mainland like an offering, rain driving sideways into his coat. He came back soaked and grim and said, "There's a gap in the weather Sunday. Maybe Monday. The harbormaster's skiff—"

"It's a fourteen-foot aluminum boat with a nine-horse motor," I said. "In open water. In January."

"In a gap," he said. "Rafael." My name in his mouth did something to the air between us. "That man is dying. If there's evidence—if there's anything that could clear him—we can't sit here."

*I know,* I almost said. *I know exactly what could clear him. I've known for fifteen years. It's sleeping in your brother's conscience and my boathouse.*

What I said was: "The coast guard knows we're here. They'll run a rescue window when they can. You don't cross thirty miles of winter ocean in a rowboat because you're impatient."

"I'm not impatient." He wrung out his coat at the door, and his voice dropped low enough that Marcus, in the other room, couldn't have heard it. "I'm afraid. There's a difference. Impatience is about you. Fear is about him—that man, Okafor. Rafael, I keep thinking—" He stopped. Started again. "Fifteen years ago, someone burned our restaurant. Someone went to prison for it. And I was twenty-four and grieving and I never once asked whether they'd caught the right person. I just let it be true, because it was easier than it being nobody, or worse, somebody I—" He cut himself off again, and I understood what he wasn't saying. *Somebody I loved.* Because he had loved me then, whatever he believed now, and if he ever learned what I'd carried all these years—

No. One catastrophe at a time.

"The appeal will happen whether we're there or not," I said. "Lawyers file papers. That's what lawyers do."

"Not without evidence, they don't." He hung the coat on its hook and looked at me, and there it was again, that inexplicable familiarity he kept almost reaching for, like a man patting his pockets for keys he'd lost years ago. "Counsel said *newly surfaced*. Surfaced from where? From whom?"

From me, I thought. Or from Marcus's blackmailer. Or from the slow rot of a lie that's been buried so long it's finally pushing bones up through the ground.

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truest sentence I'd spoken in three days.

* * *

Marcus didn't eat dinner. He sat in the front room with the lamp turned low and the radio murmuring, and he listened to the news cycle repeat itself the way a man listens to his own heartbeat when he suspects something's wrong with it. Every time the announcer said *Okafor*, I watched him flinch. He thought he was hiding it. He'd always thought he was hiding things well. That had been his gift and his ruin: Marcus Vane could commit a sin and then construct, brick by brick, a version of himself that had never sinned at all, and live in it, and call it a life.

I brought him tea he didn't ask for. I don't know why. Habit, maybe. Fifteen years ago I'd have brought him whiskey and sat close enough that our shoulders touched, and he'd have put his arm around me and told me I was the only real thing in his world, and I'd have believed him, because I was twenty-two and he was beautiful and grief makes excellent camouflage for appetite.

"You should eat something," I said now.

"I'm not hungry."

"Marcus." His name. I hadn't said it aloud in front of Julian yet, and I saw him register that—saw the small shock of it, the way a man registers a hand on his throat. "The man on the radio. Do you remember him?"

The lamp flame stood very still between us. Outside, the wind found a loose shutter somewhere and worried it, rhythmic, patient, like a question asked over and over.

"Why would I remember him?" Marcus said.

"Because he fixed your freezer. Because he had a daughter who drew pictures of the restaurant. Because he's dying, and he's dying in the wrong place, and you know it."

I hadn't planned to say it. It came out the way water comes through a cracked dam—all at once, having waited years for the crack. Marcus rose from the chair slowly, and for one naked moment his face was not a painted door but the wall behind it, and what was written there was terror.

"You don't know what you're talking about," he whispered.

"Don't I?"

"Rafael." He glanced toward the hallway, toward the room where Julian was, and lowered his voice until it was barely breath. "You want to be careful. You want to be very careful. There are people who—there are arrangements that were made, and if you start pulling threads—"

"What arrangements, Marcus?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Fifteen years passed through the space between us: a restaurant burning, a courtroom, a prison visiting room where I sat across from him once, only once, and he'd said *keep quiet, keep quiet, keep quiet* until the guard made him leave.

"They'll kill you," he said simply. "Or worse. They'll make you into a liar in public. A crazy woman. A stalker with a grudge and a novel." Something moved behind his eyes—shame, maybe, arriving late to its own funeral. "I'm trying to protect you. That's the only true thing I've said in years, and you won't believe it, and you shouldn't."

Then he walked past me to the stairs, and paused on the second step without turning around.

"He was good to you," Marcus said quietly. "Okafor. I know. I remember the pictures his daughter drew." His hand tightened on the banister. "You think I don't carry it? You think there's a night I sleep?" He laughed, a small terrible sound. "There isn't. There has never once been a night I sleep."

And he climbed the stairs and was gone, and I stood in the lamplight with the radio murmuring the dead man's name, and I understood that the clock had started—not the fourteen days the court had given, but a smaller, crueler clock, the one ticking inside Marcus Vane, the one that had been ticking for fifteen years and was now, at last, audible.

* * *

I wrote in the journal that night by candlelight, because the journal was where I put things when they got too large to hold.

*Danny Okafor is dying,* I wrote. *Fourteen days. The phone is dead. The ferry is dead. The truth is in this house, and it is in me, and it is in a man upstairs who sleeps no nights.*

I stopped. The candle guttered. Down the hall, Julian's door was closed, but under it, light—a lamp still burning, and the faint scratch of a pen, because Julian Vane was also awake, also writing, also keeping a record. Of what, I wondered. Of what he remembered. Of what he was beginning to suspect. Of the girl he'd known who'd become a woman he couldn't place and couldn't dismiss.

I turned to the last page, where the sentence lived—the sentence I had started a hundred times and never finished, the sentence that began *I am the one who* —and I picked up the pen, and my hand shook, and I wrote:

*I am the one who knows.*

Not the confession. Not yet. But closer than I had ever come, and the difference between those two sentences, I understood suddenly, was only a matter of days now. Maybe hours. The storm would break. The lines would come back. The world would come asking, and Danny Okafor's lawyer would come asking, and Julian would come asking, and there was only so long a woman can stand in a house with a locked room inside her before she opens the door herself—or before someone else breaks it down.

The radio hissed softly to itself in the dark front room, keeping its vigil. Somewhere beyond the storm, a dying man was waiting on evidence, and the evidence was me.

I blew out the candle and lay listening to the wind, and beneath the wind, faint and steady, I heard footsteps overhead—Marcus, pacing his room, pacing, pacing, a man walking the perimeter of his own cage.

The clock had started. And every hour it ticked louder.
