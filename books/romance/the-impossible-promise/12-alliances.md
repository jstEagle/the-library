## 12. Alliances

I didn't sleep. None of us did, I think, but I was the only one pretending otherwise.

By four in the morning the storm had settled into a rhythm that felt almost deliberate—wind hammering the north face of the lighthouse, then a held breath, then the wind again, like something enormous pacing outside and deciding whether to come in. The generator had died with the power lines two days ago. We were down to candles, a wood stove, and the emergency radio in the lamp room, which Rafael checked every hour on the hour with the discipline of someone who had been waiting for bad news her whole life.

She was in the kitchen when I came down, sitting at the table with her hands wrapped around a mug she wasn't drinking from. The candle between us threw her shadow up the wall, tall and wavering.

"You should sleep," she said.

"So should you."

"I don't, much. Never learned how." She turned the mug a quarter turn. "Julian. If you're going to say what I think you're going to say, say it now. Before he's awake."

Marcus was still upstairs in the room he'd refused to share with me—the room he'd barely left since Rafael laid those manuscript pages on the table and watched him go white as the foam below the gallery rail. My brother. The boy who'd taught me to skip stones off the harbor breakwater. The man who had burned our family's restaurant to the ground and let a girl go to prison for it, and let an innocent handyman rot in it for fifteen years while he built a life on the ashes.

"I'm choosing her," I said.

Rafael closed her eyes. Not surprise. Something closer to grief, as if even the right choice cost something.

"He's my brother," I went on, because it needed saying out loud, in front of a witness, so I couldn't take it back at dawn. "He's the only family I have left, and I've spent fifteen years grieving him without knowing what I was grieving. But if it comes down to Marcus or the truth—to Marcus or you, or Marcus or that man dying in Fairview Correctional—I choose the truth. Every time."

"Fairview," she repeated softly. "You looked him up."

"Danny Okafor. Fifty-one years old. Pancreatic cancer, diagnosed eight months into a fifteen-year sentence." I sat down across from her. "His final appeal is next week. It hinges on new evidence. Which means it hinges on evidence that exists."

"And ours is a confession nobody wrote down, some letters Marcus hasn't given anyone, and a novel."

"A novel that matches the case file detail for detail," I said. "Rafael. Listen to me. That book isn't just your confession. It's corroboration. You described the back door of Marchetti's, the oil drums behind the kitchen, the way the fire moved east to west against the wind. You could only know that if you were there—and the prosecution's whole theory was that you set it. Your book doesn't just clear you. It points at whoever else was there that night."

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the wind found a gap somewhere and made the whole tower hum, a low note like a struck glass.

"When I was writing it," she said finally, "I told myself it was fiction. I changed the names. I changed the town. I thought I was being clever." Her mouth twisted. "I was building evidence and calling it art, because art was the only place I was allowed to tell the truth."

"Then we get it out there. All of it. The manuscript, whatever Marcus will give us, his confession if we can record one—"

"He'll never confess."

"Not willingly. But he already has, in every way but words on a record. He saw those pages and went gray, Rafael. Guilty men don't go gray over fiction."

* * *

Marcus came down at seven, moving carefully, like a man testing whether the floor would hold him. He'd shaved. That was the first thing I noticed—that after two days of collapse he'd stood at some mirror and put his face back together, because Marcus Vane would rather be executed than seen disheveled.

"Coffee?" Rafael asked, and her voice was perfectly level. I marveled at it. Fifteen years in prison hadn't taught me that kind of calm; it had taught her.

"Just coffee." He took the mug without meeting her eyes. "Where's the radio?"

"Lamp room. Why?"

"The coast guard broadcast. I want to hear the marine forecast." He drank, wincing like the coffee was a formality. "If there's a window tomorrow, I'm taking the boat to the mainland."

"There is no boat," I said. "The harbormaster's skiff is half-swamped at its mooring, and the dory in the boathouse has a cracked transom."

"The dory can be patched. And the engine on the keeper's launch—" He stopped. Recalibrated. I watched him do it, that smooth recalculation I'd mistaken all my life for competence. "The keeper's launch might make it in a following sea."

"It's Rafael's boat," I said. "It goes when she says."

Something flickered across his face—there and gone, fast, but I caught it because I was finally, finally watching. Not guilt. Calculation. My brother stood in a stranger's kitchen holding stolen warmth in his hands and he was doing arithmetic.

"Of course," he said mildly. "Whenever she says."

* * *

We planned it in the lamp room, because it was the only room with a lock and the only room where we could hear anyone coming up the stairs. Rafael showed me the transmitter—a squat yellow box bolted to the console, part of the automated-reserve system her grandfather had installed before the Coast Guard modernized the light.

"Emergency traffic only, officially," she said. "But it records everything that goes through it onto a backup drive. Grandfather was paranoid about losing logs during storms. Insurance reasons." A ghost of a smile. "Paranoia runs in the family. I come by mine honestly."

"If Marcus confesses within range of this thing—"

"Then there's a recording with a timestamp, stored on a drive I can pull and hide anywhere on this island. In a storm like this, no one's flying in to check frequencies." She touched the drive slot the way other people touch talismans. "But he won't confess in front of us. He knows better now."

"Then we make him think confessing is the safe option."

She looked at me. In the gray light through the lantern glass, with the sea exploding white against the rocks forty feet below, she looked both younger and older than her years, worn smooth and hard at once, like sea glass.

"How?"

"Crane." I said the name and watched her flinch. "He's squeezing Marcus. Has been for fifteen years, if the letters are anything like what you've described. Which means Marcus isn't protecting himself anymore—he's protecting himself *from* Crane. Right now, in his head, we're a second threat. Two threats pointing at him. So we become the lesser one. We make him believe that if Crane goes down, Marcus can survive it—but if Crane wins, Marcus drowns too."

"And how do we convince him of that?"

"We show him Danny Okafor's appeal filing." I'd pulled it up on my phone before the signal died entirely, saved every page. "The state's case is built on your conviction. If the appeal succeeds, the conviction vacates, and the whole file reopens—and the first thing any competent investigator does is ask why a twenty-two-year-old waitress with no motive confessed to a fire with a documented insurance angle. Marcus's name is in that insurance paperwork, Rafael. As beneficiary-adjacent. His signature is on the loan renewal his father signed three weeks before the fire."

Her breath caught. "You knew?"

"I suspected. I've suspected for years that Marcus knew more about the fire than he ever said—I just never let myself finish the thought." I heard my own voice go flat and cold. "Finishing thoughts about my brother was a skill I avoided."

* * *

We brought it to him that afternoon, in the kitchen, with the candles lit because the daylight was the color of dishwater. Rafael sat with her hands flat on the table. I stood by the stove, between Marcus and the door—not because I expected him to run, but because I wanted him to feel it.

I laid the printed pages down in front of him.

Danny Okafor's face looked up from the first sheet, older than his fifty-one years, hollowed by illness and injustice. Below it, the summary of the pending appeal: newly surfaced inconsistencies, the timeline problems, the absence of any physical evidence linking the confessor to accelerants.

Marcus read it the way a man reads his own autopsy. I watched his eyes move down the page and stop at the paragraph about reopened investigations.

"This doesn't mean anything," he said. "Appeals fail all the time."

"This one won't," Rafael said quietly. "Because I'm going to submit my novel as a statement. Under my real name. Rafael Marchetti." She let the name land in the room like a dropped glass. "And when the investigators come to me—and they will come, Marcus, they always come—they're going to ask who else was at the restaurant that night. Who had keys. Who benefited. Who threatened me afterward."

"You'd perjure yourself—" He caught himself, because there was no perjury in telling the truth, and we all three knew it, and the knowledge sat between us like a fourth person.

"I'd testify," she said. "For the first time in fifteen years, I'd actually testify."

Marcus set the pages down. His hands weren't steady. For one naked moment he looked less like a lawyer than like the boy I'd grown up with, the one who cried when our dog died and swore me to secrecy.

"They'll come for me either way," he said, very low. "That's what you're not getting. Either way, Crane—"

"Crane is one man," I said. "One man with a paper trail, a partner who's ready to talk, and a victim dying in Fairview whose lawyers would trade their teeth for new evidence. You help us, Marcus. You give the letters, you give a statement, you stand up in that courtroom and say what happened—and you go in as a cooperating witness instead of a defendant. Or you sit here and wait for Crane to decide you're a liability too. Because that's what he does with liabilities. You know that better than anyone."

Silence. The stove ticked. Somewhere below us the sea worked at the rocks with the patience of a debt collector.

"I need to think," Marcus said.

"Think fast," Rafael said. "Danny Okafor doesn't have slow."

My brother looked at her then—really looked, maybe for the first time since he'd walked into her lighthouse out of the snow. Whatever he saw in her face made him look away first.

* * *

He agreed to nothing that night. He agreed to nothing at breakfast. But at ten the next morning he knocked on my door—knocked, like a stranger—and said, "Show me the letters you think exist," and when I described them, the dates, the postmark from Crane's office, he didn't deny they existed. He said, "If I give them to you, they can't leave this island until I decide. Swear it."

I swore. Rafael swore. And for six hours, six hours, it seemed possible. Marcus sat at the kitchen table with a legal pad and actually drafted a proffer statement, his pen moving in the quick confident strokes I'd envied our whole lives, laying out the insurance scheme, the night of the fire, Crane's role in planning it, Crane's role in burying it. Rafael read over the pages with tears running silently down her face, and Marcus didn't look at her, but he slowed down whenever he reached a part about her—about the threat to her mother's care, about the promise he'd extracted from a terrified girl—and I understood that slowing down was the closest thing to apology my brother's architecture allowed.

At four o'clock he said he wanted air, and took the flashlight, and went down toward the boathouse alone.

Rafael and I looked at each other across the table, across the pages that smelled of ink and fifteen years.

"Someone should go with him," she said.

"He asked for air."

"He asked for a head start," she said, and she was already moving.

We reached the boathouse in time to see nothing incriminating—of course we did; Marcus was careful, Marcus had always been careful. He was standing over the keeper's launch with the engine cover open and grease on his hands, and he said, "Fuel line's fouled. Salt water in the tank, probably. This boat's been sitting too long."

Rafael crouched beside him, lifted the fuel line free of its clamp, and held it up to the flashlight beam. The line was clean. Bone dry. New-cut, even—the end freshly sliced, the blade mark bright.

"That's strange," she said, in the same mild voice she used for weather reports. "Because I winterized this engine myself in October. Fresh fuel, stabilized, line capped." She set the severed line down on the deck between them, gentle as setting down a sleeping child. "Funny how it cut itself."

Marcus's face did something complicated and final. When he spoke, his voice was the lawyer's again, smooth as poured concrete.

"Storm damage," he said. "These things happen."

"No," Rafael said. "They don't. Not to a capped fuel line."

She straightened up and looked at him for a long moment, and I watched my brother understand, all at once, that the woman he'd burned was not afraid of him anymore—and that he had just shown her exactly how afraid of her he was.

"Nobody leaves the island," she said quietly, "until the truth does."

Behind us, out past the point, the storm dropped its voice for a moment—and in that sudden, unnatural hush, we both heard it: an engine. Low, distant, wrong. No ferry ran in this weather. Nothing should be moving on that water at all.

Rafael turned toward the sound, and the color left her face.

"Somebody's coming," she said.
