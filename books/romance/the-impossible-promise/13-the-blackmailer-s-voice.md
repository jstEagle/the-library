## 13. The Blackmailer's Voice

I didn't sleep. That was the first thing I noticed about the morning after Julian read her pages back to me like a verdict—no, not a verdict. Verdicts end things. This was worse. This was the beginning of something, and beginnings are where men like me die.

The storm had eased to a steady grind against the seawall, the kind of sound you could almost mistake for silence if your whole body weren't listening for it. The generator had coughed back to life around dawn, and now the cottage hummed with electricity I hadn't earned, lights burning in a house where a woman I'd helped bury alive was making coffee for three.

She moved like someone who had rehearsed this kitchen for years. Everything within reach without looking. The kettle set down without a clank. She'd built herself into this room the way roots build into rock—slowly, invisibly, and with a patience that shamed me.

Julian sat at the table with his hands wrapped around a mug he wasn't drinking from, watching her the way he used to watch the door of our father's restaurant on the nights our father came home late—braced, hopeful, braced again. My brother had always loved people at full volume. It was his most dangerous quality.

And me. I stood by the window with my coat still on because taking it off felt like settling in, and I was not settling in. I was passing through. I had been passing through for fifteen years.

"Marcus." Rafael's voice. Not cold. That was the unbearable part. She poured a fourth cup she had no business pouring and set it on the counter near me, an offering or a trap, and said my name again like it was a hand extended across a table. "Sit down."

"I'm fine standing."

"You've been fine standing since Tuesday," Julian said. "It's Thursday."

I looked at the coffee. Steam rose off it in a thin, honest line. Somewhere under the floorboards the sea worked at the island's foundations, patient as a lawyer.

*Tell them,* said the voice in my head that had been talking all night. *Tell them about Crane. Tell them what he has. Tell them why you can't.*

*Because,* said the older voice, the one with seniority, *because if you tell them, you're not a coward who got squeezed anymore. You're a man who chose. And choices have consequences you can't bill to anyone else.*

Rafael turned from the counter. Her eyes were the same gray as the water past the glass, and they held no hatred, which frightened me more than hatred would have. Hatred I knew how to survive. This was something else. This was a woman who had spent fifteen years in a cell for a fire she didn't set, looking at the man who let her, and finding him pitiable.

"Last night," she said carefully, "you said the novel was fiction."

"It is fiction."

"You said it was *slanderous* fiction."

"That's what slander is. Fiction with consequences."

Julian set his mug down with a click. "Marcus."

"Don't." I held up a hand. "Don't do the lawyer thing. Don't do the big-brother thing. I've had fifteen years of both and neither one ever kept me warm."

"Then what did?" Rafael asked quietly.

And there it was. The question. Fifteen years of insurance settlements and silent partners and quarterly envelopes and a phone that rang at bad hours, distilled down to five words asked by a woman in a lighthouse keeper's sweater.

What did keep me warm? Aldous Crane's voice on the line, telling me everything was handled? The penthouse I couldn't sleep in? The second restaurant we opened on the ashes of the first, with its tasteful plaque commemorating the "tragic accident," which I walked past every morning on my way to an office where I signed things I didn't read because reading them would have meant knowing them?

"The money," I said. It came out flat and true. "The money kept me warm. That's the answer you're looking for, and I'm not going to dress it up for either of you, because dressing it up is half of how I got here."

Rafael didn't flinch. Julian looked away.

"But it wasn't only the money," I heard myself say, and my own voice surprised me, the way a floor surprises you when it gives. "That's the part nobody believes. That's the part I don't believe, some mornings."

* * *

Here is what I told them, standing at that window with my coat still buttoned, while the storm scratched at the glass like something wanting in.

After the fire, after Rafael's arrest, after the trial I attended in a good suit with my grief arranged just so on my face—after all of it, there was a man named Aldous Crane who appeared in my life the way mold appears in a wall: already there, already feeding, just becoming visible.

He'd been our father's insurance broker. Then our father's lawyer. Then, when Julian went east to law school and I stayed to hold together the ruins, Crane became mine. He handled the claim. He handled the settlement. He introduced me to the investors who rebuilt Marchetti-Vane into Vane Hospitality Group—he'd already smoothed the name, already filed the papers, already done everything except ask me for anything in return.

For two years, nothing. Two golden years. We were the phoenix story the business press loves: brothers rise from tragedy, younger one shows real grit. I believed it. God help me, I believed I had grit.

Then, one night in my office, Crane closed the door and sat down across from me and put a single photograph on the desk between us.

It was a receipt. A hardware store, fourteen miles outside the city, dated nine days before the fire. Accelerant, extension cords, a galvanized can. Signed with a name I didn't recognize—which was the point; it took me four days and a private investigator I paid in cash to learn the name belonged to a man who had died the following spring, a man whose signature Crane had borrowed the way you borrow a coat from a friend who won't notice.

"I don't understand," I told him.

"Yes, you do," Crane said. He had a way of smiling that never reached anything above his cheekbones. "You understand perfectly. You've understood since the night of the fire, Marcus. You're the only one who understood, and you've been so brave about it. I admire that."

I want to be exact here, because exactness is all I have left to offer anyone: I did not know, before that moment, that Crane had set the fire. I want that on the record, even though the record is a kitchen table on a dying island and the only court in session is made up of the two people I have hurt worst.

But I knew *something*. That's the sentence underneath every other sentence. On the night of the fire I knew that Crane had called me at eleven and told me to leave the office early, that he'd handle the closing paperwork himself, that I should take Rafael home—I remember saying that, *take Rafael home*, as if she were a package, as if she were mine to deliver—and I knew that when the call came about the flames, my first feeling, before horror, before grief, was a cold bright flash of arithmetic. The building was insured. The building had been failing. My father's death had left debts dressed up as legacy, and Julian was gone, and Rafael—

Rafael loved Julian. Everyone knew it except Julian, who was twenty-four and dense as oak about anything he wanted too much. And Rafael was kind to me. Kind in the specific, devastating way that beautiful people are kind to the brother who isn't chosen. I had built a whole cathedral out of that kindness, and I knew, with the arithmetic still flashing, that if the restaurant burned, the cathedral burned with it, and maybe then—

No. I'm not going to soften it. Maybe then I could have her.

I never set the fire. I never bought accelerant, never struck a match, never hired the dead man whose name is on that receipt. But I stood in Crane's office two years later and understood that I had wanted it, that my wanting had been visible to a man like Crane the way heat is visible to a snake, and that he had simply done the thing I lacked the nerve for, and then—this is the part that will follow me into whatever cell they give me—then I had let a twenty-two-year-old girl go to prison rather than let the arithmetic show.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked him that day. My voice sounded like someone else's.

"Because the investigator you hired is thorough," Crane said, "and because from now on, thoroughness runs through me. Quarterly disbursements to certain accounts. Silence at certain board meetings. And Marcus—" he gathered up his photograph, his little paper ghost, and squared its edges against the desk, "—if you ever think about being brave, remember that the state convicted someone. The state is satisfied. If you reopen this, you're not confessing, you're admitting you knew for years and profited. There's no version of that story where you're the tragic brother. There's only the version where you're the accomplice."

He was right. That's the hell of it. He was right then and he's right now, sitting in whatever marble office he keeps, waiting for my quarterly silence like a crop coming due.

Fifteen years. Envelopes with no return address containing photographs of receipts, of meeting minutes, once—God, once—a photograph of Rafael's parole hearing, taken from the gallery, her face turned toward the camera by accident, twenty-six years old and trying to look harmless. With a note in Crane's neat hand: *Remember how far we've come.*

We. As if we were partners. As if we'd built something.

* * *

When I finished, the kitchen was very quiet. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Out past the point, the sea threw itself against the rocks and threw itself again, committed to a task with no end.

"So he's been bleeding you for thirteen years," Julian said at last. His voice was strange—level, careful, the voice he used with hostile witnesses. "Payments. Silence. Leverage."

"Yes."

"And you paid. Every time."

"Every time."

"Why not go to the police?" Rafael asked. "After the first envelope. After the photograph. Why not walk into a precinct and say *he did it, arrest him*?"

"Because of exactly what he said," I told her. "Because the moment I spoke, I stopped being the grieving brother and became the man who knew. Because they'd ask why I waited two years. They'd ask about the payments. They'd ask about the night of the fire, and Rafael—" I made myself look at her. It cost something. Let it cost something. "They'd ask what I suspected, and when I suspected it, and whether I said anything to you before the trial. Whether I visited you in county. Whether I wrote."

"You didn't visit," she said. No accusation in it. Just inventory.

"No. I didn't."

"Why?"

"Because you looked at me at the sentencing," I said, "like you were trying to figure out whether I'd done it. And I couldn't bear to sit across glass from that question every week. So I let the question stay unanswered instead of answering it wrong." I laughed, and it came out broken. "That was my great moral act. Protecting myself from your eyes."

Julian pushed back from the table and stood at the window beside me, and for a moment we were two boys again in our father's kitchen, close enough to touch, separated by everything.

"He'll come for the letters," Julian said. "If you have letters, he'll come."

"There are no letters. He's too careful for letters."

"Everyone is too careful until they aren't," Julian said. "Fifteen years is a long time to run leverage on a man. Something slips. A note. An email. A check that shouldn't exist. Think, Marcus. Think like the man who built a company out of ash. What do you have?"

And I thought. I stood there with my cooling coffee and my coat still buttoned and I thought, and what surfaced wasn't a document. It was a drawer.

"My house in the city," I said slowly. "There's a safe behind the paneling in the study. After the third year, I started keeping copies of everything he sent. Photographs, notes, the disbursement schedules I copied out of the company books. I told myself it was insurance." I shook my head. "I told myself a lot of things were insurance."

"You have evidence," Julian said. "Actual evidence. In a safe in Boston."

"In a safe in Boston, yes. Which might as well be on the moon, because the ferry's dead, the roads are dead, and the storm's got another day in it at least."

"Then we wait out the storm," Rafael said.

I turned to look at her. She had both hands around her mug, and she was watching me with that terrible gentleness, and I realized she had misunderstood—or rather, she had understood something I hadn't said yet, something truer than what I'd said.

"Rafael," I said. "The letters are yours. When we get off this island, they go wherever you say. To the appeals court, to the police, to a newspaper. I'm not negotiating. I'm not asking for anything." I swallowed. "But I need you to hear me say one thing before any of that happens, because you may never believe me after, and I'd rather you have it now, undiluted."

"Say it."

"I didn't set the fire." My voice cracked on *fire*, and I let it crack. "Crane set the fire. But I knew what he was before he ever lit it, and I said nothing, and I watched you carry it, and I let the money make it bearable. That's my confession. It's not the one that frees anyone. It's the smaller one. But it's true, and it's mine, and I'm giving it to you because it's the only payment I have that you might accept."

Rafael set down her mug. For a long moment she said nothing, and the sea filled the silence, and I stood in it like a man in surf, letting it move me.

"Partial payment," she said finally. "That's what this is, Marcus. Not absolution. Partial payment. There's a balance outstanding, and the currency it gets settled in isn't words."

"I know."

"Do you?" She stepped closer, and up close I could see the fine lines at her eyes, the fifteen years I'd given her. "Because the man in prison—the handyman, Daniel Okafor—he's dying. Did you hear the radio yesterday? His final appeal needs new evidence, and it needs it fast, and you're standing here handing me *photographs* like that's a gift. Photographs I could have used in 2011. Photographs that mean nothing unless they reach a judge before he does."

"They'll reach a judge."

"How? The storm—"

"I'll get them there." Julian spoke from the window, quiet and absolute. "However I have to."

Something in his tone made me turn. My brother was looking out at the gray violence of the channel with an expression I recognized from childhood—the expression he wore before he did something reckless and magnificent and usually half-insane. The gap in the weather. The boat. I saw the whole plan assemble behind his eyes before he said a word of it aloud, and fear moved through me, cold and specific.

"Julian," I said. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm thinking," he said, "that Daniel Okafor doesn't have another day, and neither does the truth." He looked at me then, and there was no warmth in it, but there was something better than warmth: purpose. "Get me those letters, Marcus. Whatever it costs."

Outside, far across the water, past the point, past the shoals, a shape was forming in the squall that none of us saw yet—a hull, running dark, coming our way.

But that's ahead of the story. All I knew, in that kitchen, was that I had finally handed over the first installment of a debt fifteen years past due, and that the collector was already on the water.
