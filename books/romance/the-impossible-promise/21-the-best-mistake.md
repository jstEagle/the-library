## 21. The Best Mistake

Spring came to the island the way forgiveness came—slowly, then all at once.

Julian noticed it on a Tuesday in April, standing at the window of the cottage he'd rented from the harbormaster, watching the water change from slate to something almost blue. The trial had ended in March. Marcus was in a facility upstate, cooperating, awaiting sentencing. Crane was in a cell with no windows and no deals left to make. The innocent man—Walter Finch, seventy-one years old, twenty-two years stolen—was home with his sister, eating breakfast in a kitchen he'd been denied for half his life.

And Rafael Marchetti had published a book.

*The Impossible Promise* sat on Julian's nightstand, spine cracked from a second reading. Her real name on the cover. Her face on the back flap, unsmiling, unhidden, photographed against the white wall of the lighthouse she still kept, even now that the lamp ran on automation and the Coast Guard had no more use for a keeper. The reviews called it "devastating" and "necessary" and "a novel that reads like a confession because it is one." Julian had read it three times and wept at different passages each time. The third time, he'd wept at the ending, because the ending was different from what had happened, and he understood now that the difference was mercy. In the book, the girl chose herself and walked into the light alone. In life, Julian was still standing at the edge of that light, waiting to see if he was allowed in.

They had been careful since the trial. Careful the way two people are careful when they've survived something together and don't trust themselves to name it. She'd come to his brother's sentencing hearing and sat two rows behind him, and when it was over she'd touched his elbow in the courthouse corridor—just his elbow, two seconds, the lightest pressure—and said, "Call me when you're ready to not be sad."

He hadn't called. Not because he wasn't ready. Because he kept rehearsing what to say, and everything he rehearsed sounded like the old Julian—the one who loved with both hands and no brakes, who had loved her at twenty-two with a ferocity that had helped ruin everything, because love that desperate makes people do desperate arithmetic. *If she marries me, she'll be safe. If she marries me, Marcus will back off.* He had been twenty-six and already thinking in bargains.

He was thirty-seven now. He wanted to do it differently or not at all.

So on that Tuesday in April, he called the harbormaster, asked about the ferry schedule, and was told the weather was, in the harbormaster's words, "boring. Best kind. Flat calm, high clouds, nothing happening anywhere."

Perfect. Ordinary weather. He bought a ticket.

* * *

She met him at the dock, and the first thing he noticed was that she looked different in daylight without a storm behind her. Smaller, somehow, and more solid at the same time. She wore a canvas jacket over a gray sweater, and her hair was down, and she was carrying a thermos like a woman who had decided in advance that this visit would last a while.

"You look terrible," she said.

"I've been sleeping fine, actually."

"Liar. You have trial face. My book has trial face on the cover, so I know it when I see it."

He laughed, and it surprised him how easily it came. "It's over, though."

"It's over." She handed him the thermos. "Coffee. I made it strong because you always under-sleep before you come here. You did it in March too."

"You noticed that."

"I notice everything about you. It's an occupational hazard of having loved you at an impressionable age."

She said it lightly, but not *carelessly*. That was the difference between the Rafael of fifteen years ago and this one—the old Rafael would have said it and then flinched, watching his face for damage. This Rafael said it and just kept walking, up the dock ramp, toward the path that wound along the shore toward the lighthouse. As if the truth were a thing you could carry in open hands.

He fell into step beside her. The path was muddy in the way of early spring, and the gulls were making their idiot arguments overhead, and none of it was dramatic. None of it was a storm. That was the point, he realized. She had chosen an ordinary day on purpose.

They walked for a while without talking, which was its own kind of fluency. Then she said, "How's Marcus?"

"Better. Worse. Both." Julian turned the thermos in his hands. "He wrote me a letter last week. Four pages. The first three were about the facility, the food, the group therapy. The fourth page was one sentence." He paused. "*Tell Rafael I'm not asking for anything. I just wanted her to know the fire was the best mistake I ever made, because it taught me what I actually was.*"

Rafael stopped walking.

"That's a horrible sentence," she said.

"It's a Marcus sentence."

"It's a horrible, *honest* sentence." She started walking again, slower. "I got one too. I haven't answered it. I don't know if I will. Forgiveness isn't a door you can just—prop open. It opens when it opens or it doesn't."

"You don't owe him a door."

"No," she agreed. "But I owe myself the truth about whether I want one, and I don't know yet. That's allowed, right? Not knowing?"

"It's allowed."

"Good. Because I'm about to do a lot of not knowing, and I'd like it on the record that it's a deliberate choice."

She said it looking straight ahead, at the lighthouse, white against the pale sky, and Julian understood that the conversation had arrived at its real beginning, and that she had been walking them toward it since the dock.

* * *

They sat on the gallery rail's bench—the same rail where he'd almost told her everything in a storm, where he'd nearly drowned trying to reach the mainland, where she'd hauled him back with her grandfather's lifeline and he'd said *I've loved you since before the fire* with seawater in his lungs. The lighthouse behind them was dark in the daytime, doing nothing, needing nothing. The sea below was doing its eternal indifferent work.

Julian set down the thermos and said, "Can I ask you something I've been carrying since the lamp room?"

"You can ask me anything. You know that by now."

"What would we have been? If not for the fire."

She didn't answer right away. She looked out at the water, and her hands were still in her lap, and he watched her do the thing she did—turn the question over, test its weight, decide whether it was a trap or a gift.

"I used to know," she said finally. "At twenty-two I had a whole answer. White house on the coast, you practicing law in town, me writing in the mornings. I had it *furnished*, Julian. I'd picked out the dishes." Her mouth curved. "And then the fire burned that too, along with everything else, and for fifteen years the answer to your question was a wound. Not a possibility. A wound."

"And now?"

"Now it's a question again." She turned to face him. "Which is better. A question you can live inside is better than an answer you can't."

"I don't want to rush it," he said, and heard his own voice go careful, the way it did in court when the stakes were highest. "That's what I came here to say, actually. I had a whole speech on the ferry. I was going to be very eloquent about second chances and—"

"Julian."

"—and how the past doesn't have to define—"

"*Julian.*"

He stopped.

"Give me the speech later," she said. "For my birthday, maybe. I want to hear the whole terrible eloquent thing. But right now I want to say my part, because I've been rehearsing it for two months and if you keep talking I'll lose my nerve, and I have spent enough of my life losing my nerve to last several lifetimes."

He shut his mouth. He would have shut it for a year if she'd asked.

She took a breath. "Here's what I know. I know that you loved me without conditions, and I didn't know what to do with that, because everything I understood about love came from Marcus, and Marcus's love was a ledger. Everything cost something. So when you offered love for free, I assumed I hadn't seen the price tag yet." Her voice was steady. Fifteen years of practice at steady. "I know that you came back, storm after storm, and never once made my past the price of your present. I know that when you read my book—the book where I chose *nobody*, where I walked away from both of you—you didn't get angry. You told me it was the truest thing I'd ever written. And I know that a man who can read that and stay, without trying to renegotiate the ending—"

She stopped. Started again.

"I don't want a promise," she said. "I spent fifteen years inside an impossible promise. It nearly killed all three of us. I don't want vows or bargains or forever, said in a storm, when we can't tell fear from love. I want—" she gestured at the day, the flat calm water, the boring sky, the gulls, the mud on the path, all of it gloriously undramatic—"I want *this*. Daylight. Ordinary weather. We find out what we could have been the slow way. One Tuesday at a time. No promises either of us can't keep. If it fails, it fails in the light, where I can see it, and I'm not afraid of the light anymore."

Julian looked at her for a long moment.

"You rehearsed that," he said.

"Two months. In the journal. I finished the sentence this time."

"Was it a good sentence?"

"It was the best one I've ever written. Better than the book." She was smiling now, and there was nothing guarded in it. "Your turn. No speech. One sentence."

He thought about it. He was, by profession, a man of many sentences, and she was asking him for one, and he understood that this too was the point—that love, the real kind, the kind that didn't keep ledgers, could be said briefly or not at all.

"I don't want to find out what we could have been," he said. "That's a ghost's question. I want to find out what we *are*. Starting now. In boring weather."

Rafael laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and then she leaned over and kissed him.

It was not a storm kiss. There was no wind trying to kill them, no confession pending, no gun in anyone's hand, no clock running down. It was a Tuesday in April, with mud on their boots and coffee going cold in the thermos, and the lighthouse dark behind them because it didn't need to be lit in the daytime, and the kiss was slow and ordinary and completely unhurried, and when she pulled back she was still smiling.

"Well," she said. "That was aggressively unremarkable."

"The best thing that's ever happened to me."

"Same." She picked up the thermos. "Come on. I'll show you the cottage. I'm turning the keeper's room into a study, and I want your opinion on paint colors, because I've decided to write the next book here, under my own name, and I refuse to write it in a room I hate."

They walked down from the tower together, and the sea kept on, and the sky stayed boring, and nothing in the world was on fire.

At the bottom of the path, her phone buzzed. She glanced at it, and Julian watched her face do something complicated—surprise, then something softer, then something like fear, but the old kind, the ordinary kind, the kind that belongs to living people.

"It's Walter," she said. "Walter Finch. He's never called me." She looked up. "Julian—he says he wants to visit the island. This week. He says—" she read, and her voice caught—"he says he has something he's never told anyone about the night of the fire. Something he *saw*."
