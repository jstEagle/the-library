## 5. The Man at the Bake Sale

October came to Marrow Bay the way it always did—not all at once, but in increments, the way grief had come to me. First the light went thin and gold, like tea held up to a window. Then the wind found its teeth. Then the town began, in its small industrious way, to decorate for a festival I had spent three years pretending not to dread.

The bunting went up on a Tuesday. I watched from the bookshop window as two men from the harbor committee wrestled a ladder against the lamppost outside, orange and cream cloth snapping in the wind like something alive and trying to get loose. Wren pressed her forehead to the glass beside me, her breath fogging a small circle.

"They're doing the lights this year," she said. "Vivian Ash said so at school. She does a whole assembly about it. She's very intense."

"Is she."

"She made a fourth-grader cry about string placement. In a *nice* way, though." Wren considered this. "Mostly nice."

I said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wasn't a lie, and I had gotten very good at not lying to my daughter, which meant I had gotten very good at saying nothing at all.

The bell over the door chimed, letting in a gust that smelled of salt and cold iron. Mrs. Pell, my most reliable customer and least reliable gossip, shook rain from her cardigan and beelined for the new releases she never bought.

"Bake sale's Saturday," she announced, as though I had asked. "For the festival fund. Vivian Ash has opinions about the table layout. Vivian Ash has opinions about everything, God love her. You're contributing, I assume? The shop always does."

"The shop always does," I agreed, because it was easier than explaining that the shop had never done anything, that *I* had, once, under protest, and that the woman whose name made my chest tighten like a too-small coat had been signing my letters for three weeks now—letters I had read so many times the paper had gone soft at the folds.

*Dear Stranger, you write like a man who's been interrupted mid-sentence for years and has finally decided to finish it anyway.*

I still didn't know how she'd seen that. I still didn't know why it hadn't made me angry.

* * *

Saturday morning I baked. This was not a thing I advertised. There had been a time when I'd been photographed holding things—awards, guitars, champagne—and a time after that when I'd learned that the safest life is one where nobody knows what you're good at. But Wren liked my lemon loaves, and the bake sale was for the school roof, and there are limits even to cowardice.

I carried the tin down Harbor Street at nine, Wren trotting ahead in her yellow raincoat, and the town was already transformed. Stalls lined the seawall like a fleet at anchor. The air was cider and woodsmoke and frying onions. Somewhere a fiddler was tuning, the notes hanging in the cold like questions.

And at the center of it all, directing traffic with a clipboard held like a baton, stood a woman in a green coat.

I knew her the way you know a scar. Vivian Ash. Three years older than the last time I'd seen her—thinner through the face, hair shorter, but the same upright carriage, the same way of standing as though the world were a room she'd been asked to organize. She had testified at the hearing. She had sat in the second row with her hands folded and her voice steady, and she had said the words that ended me, and I had watched her mouth move and thought, absurdly, *she has nice penmanship, you can tell.*

I had not spoken to her since. Marrow Bay is small but it is very good at letting people orbit each other without collision, and we had orbited, she and I, for three careful years.

Until Saturday, when the orbits failed.

* * *

It started with the tables. The bake sale tables were supposed to be along the seawall rail, but someone—Mrs. Pell, I'd have bet my last unsold paperback—had dragged them into a horseshoe facing the bandstand, and Vivian Ash arrived at the horseshoe like a general arriving at a battlefield she had specifically ordered not to exist.

"No," she said, to the tables, to the town, to the morning. "No, no. The wind comes off the water straight into the rail line. Everything gets sandblasted. Who moved these?"

"Committee voted," said a man I didn't recognize, with the shrug of a man who had already won.

"The committee voted on the *plan I wrote*—"

"Plans change, Viv."

I might have walked past. I intended to walk past. But Wren had spotted a friend and peeled off toward the cider stall, and my path took me directly through the horseshoe, directly past the woman in the green coat, and directly into the sound of her voice saying, to no one and everyone:

"If one more thing about this festival happens *to* me instead of *because of* me, I am going to lie down in the harbor."

It was such an exactly, catastrophically familiar sentence that I stopped. Because my pen pal—my impossible, perceptive, maddening pen pal—had written nearly the same thing three days ago. *Some days I want to walk into the sea just to get one hour where nobody needs anything arranged.*

"You could try delegating," I heard myself say.

She turned. Up close she was taller than I remembered, or I had shrunk; three years of making yourself small will do that. Her eyes went over my face with the quick cataloguing glance of someone who organized people too, and I watched her fail to place me, and I felt the strange double sensation I'd been living with all month: relief and disappointment arriving in the same envelope.

"I'm sorry?"

"Delegating," I said. "The tables. If the committee voted, the vote is the thing to fight, not the men who moved furniture. You're shooting the messengers, and there are at least six of them."

Something flickered across her face—irritation, then interest, then a kind of reluctant delight, as if I'd played a chord she didn't know she knew. "And you are?"

"Nobody," I said. "I run the bookshop. I brought lemon loaf."

"Lemon loaf," she repeated, as though I'd confessed to arson. Her eyes dropped to the tin under my arm. "The school roof fund?"

"The school roof fund."

"Fine. Table four, far end, away from the fiddler—the vibration gets into the frosting, don't ask me how I know. And for the record, I *do* delegate. I delegated the signage and it came back spelled 'Fesitval.' Twice. I have become the kind of person who corrects banners at seven in the morning, and I blame this town entirely."

"Blame is very efficient," I said. "It requires no evidence."

She laughed. It came out of her like something escaping—a short, startled, genuine sound—and then she caught it, pressed it back down, and looked at me with narrowed eyes as if I'd pickpocketed it.

"You're very annoying," she said.

"I've been told. Repeatedly. In writing."

Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under a boat—a shadow, a recognition of the phrase, of *in writing*—but she couldn't have known, couldn't possibly have known, and it passed.

"Table four," she said, and turned back to her war.

* * *

I set up the loaf at table four. The fiddler's vibration did, in fact, get into the frosting, or into something; I felt it in my teeth, a low persistent hum, and I stood there cutting slices for Mrs. Pell and the harbor men and a stream of children with sticky coins, and I listened to the woman in the green coat fight her battle two stalls down, and I found myself—this is the part I keep circling, the part I don't want to look at directly—*enjoying* it. Her voice carrying over the crowd. The exasperated tenderness in it. The way she said *no, the OTHER left* to a man carrying a banner.

"You're smiling," said Wren, materializing at my elbow with cider on her chin.

"I'm working."

"You're smiling *and* working. That's new." She studied me with the frank, merciless attention of an eleven-year-old. "You know Vivian Ash?"

"I've met her."

"She's the festival lady. She's kind of amazing. She stayed late at school helping the chorus even though the chorus is *terrible*, and she told Marcus his solo was 'a crime against melody' but then she taught him how to fix it, and now he's less terrible." Wren took a slice of my loaf without asking, which was her right. "She'd like you. You're both bossy."

"I'm not bossy. I'm quietly right."

"That's what bossy people say." She chewed, considering. "You should come to the festival more. You used to like music. You never like music anymore."

The fiddler sawed into a reel. The vibration hummed through the table, through my hands, through a place in my chest I had boarded over like a window in a condemned house.

"Music's fine," I said. "It's just loud."

Wren looked at me the way she'd been looking at me more and more lately, the way children look at a locked door they've decided they will eventually open. Then she shrugged and went back to her friends, and I stood at my table in the wind and the noise, and I did the thing I had not done in three years.

I hummed.

It was barely anything. Four notes, maybe five—the opening phrase of a piece I had written in another life, in a city that no longer claimed me, for a record that no longer existed. It came out of me the way the laugh had come out of Vivian Ash: startled, escaping, immediately regretted. I clamped my mouth shut so fast I nearly bit my tongue.

But Wren had heard it. I saw her stop mid-stride, ten feet away, her back to me, her whole body going still the way a deer goes still, and I saw her turn her head just slightly, listening, the way you listen when a bird you thought had flown returns to the branch.

She didn't say anything. She just smiled at her friends and glanced back at me once, and filed it away, in the place where she filed everything, in the drawer she would open some night over dinner when I least expected it.

* * *

I closed the shop early that evening, the bake sale tin empty, the festival lights coming on down the harbor one strand at a time until the whole bay wore a necklace of orange and cream. I made tea. I sat at the counter with the day's letter—my pen pal's letter, which had come that morning, which I had read four times already and was now reading a fifth.

*Dear Stranger. Today I argued with a man about tables and lost, and then argued with the sea about wind and lost, and then a stranger at the bake sale told me blame requires no evidence, and I laughed in front of the whole town, which hasn't happened in years, and I don't know whether to thank him or bill him for the embarrassment. He had lemon loaf. He was insufferable. I keep thinking about what he said, which is annoying, because I have a festival to run and no time to be annoyed by a bookshop man with good frosting and a dangerous mouth.*

I set the letter down.

The shop was quiet. Outside, the lights burned, and the tide came in with its long cold breath, and somewhere across town Vivian Ash was probably at her kitchen table writing to me—writing to *me*, complaining about *me*, laughing at a joke I'd made without knowing I was making it.

I should have felt trapped. That's what I told myself, sitting there in the lamplight with my heart doing something complicated against my ribs: *you should feel trapped.* The woman who ended my life and the woman who was unending it, letter by letter, were the same woman, and when the festival ended and the names came out—and names always come out, that's the one law Marrow Bay has never repealed—everything I had built here would come down with it. The shop. The quiet. Wren's small safe world.

I should have stopped writing. I knew that. I had known it for a week.

Instead I took out a pen, and pulled a fresh sheet toward me, and wrote: *Dear Stranger. Today I met someone who argued with a whole town and lost, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about her laugh.*

And I signed it, as I always did, with a name that was almost true.
