## 1. The Muscle That Lies

The nesting beach at 4 a.m. was the only church Orsen Hartwell still believed in.

He walked the tideline with his headlamp off, letting his eyes adjust to the moon's thin silver, counting by feel and habit. Loggerhead tracks stitched the sand like tire treads from some enormous, patient machine — eleven crawls since dusk, nine with nests. He logged them in his head first, then in the waterproof notebook strapped to his chest, because numbers were the one language that never lied to him. *Eleven crawls. Nine nests. One false crawl. One turtle still on the beach.*

She was there now, forty meters up from the waterline, hauling herself through the dune grass with the slow, ancient determination of something that had been doing this since before continents looked the way they do. He crouched at a respectful distance and watched her dig. Sand flew in dark arcs. Her flippers worked like oars, like wings, like grief given a body and told to keep moving.

*The heart is a muscle,* he recited, the creed he'd built brick by brick over ten years. *Muscles can be trained. Trained muscles can be contained.*

It had taken him three years to make the sentence feel true and seven more to stop hearing how it sounded — like something a man tells himself at two in the morning when the alternative is remembering the sound the water made when it took his brother.

*Containable. Everything containable. The data goes in the notebook. The feeling goes nowhere.*

The turtle finished her chamber and began to drop her eggs into the dark of the earth, and Orsen did what he always did: he counted those too. Ninety-one. Ninety-two. Each ping of a shell hitting sand a small, measurable mercy. You could not save them all. You could count them all. Counting was its own kind of prayer, and unlike praying, it had never once failed him.

When she turned back toward the sea, dragging her improbable weight down the slope, he followed at a distance to log her return crawl, and that was when he saw the supply boat's light bobbing in the anchorage, hours early. Which meant mail. Which meant the consortium had finally sent the thing he'd been dreading for six weeks: the pen-pal assignment.

*Peer Support Initiative for Isolated Field Researchers.* He'd read the brochure twice just to sharpen his contempt. A program that paired lonely scientists with anonymous correspondents so they could "process the emotional realities of remote conservation work." As if processing were something you did instead of something you refused to do until it killed you quietly. He'd tried to decline. The grant administrator had replied with cheerful bureaucratic immovability: participation was a condition of the consortium's renewed funding, effective immediately, no exceptions.

So they were going to make him write letters. Fine. He would write letters the way he counted turtles — accurately, minimally, without giving anything away.

* * *

The package sat on his desk under the solar lamp, looking smug.

Orsen made coffee he didn't want and opened it with the flat efficiency of a man defusing something. Inside: a form letter, a confidentiality agreement, a single sheet identifying his correspondent only as **Anonymous B**, and one letter, already written, already waiting.

He almost didn't read it that night. He told himself he'd read it tomorrow, after the morning nest checks, after the data entry, after the day had been properly contained. But the sanctuary was quiet in the way it got in late June — the volunteers asleep, the generator humming, the whole island holding its breath between tides — and the letter was right there, and reading it was, at minimum, data collection.

*Dear Anonymous A,*

*I'm told we're supposed to exchange "honest reflections" about our work. I'll be honest, then: I don't believe in this program, and I suspect you don't either. So let's skip the part where we pretend.*

*I'll go first. My honest reflection is that I've spent my career watching people like my correspondent — I assume you're a field researcher, since the consortium said you're "remote" — mistake data for devotion. You count things. You log things. You build beautiful little spreadsheets around suffering and call it stewardship. I've watched sanctuaries run exactly that way die anyway, slowly, politely, while their directors congratulated themselves on their objectivity. Numbers don't fight for anything. They just watch.*

*If that stings, good. Honesty was the assignment.*

*— B*

Orsen read it twice. Then a third time, standing up, which he hadn't realized he'd done.

His first feeling was fury — clean, hot, startlingly specific. His second feeling, arriving half a second behind it like a shadow, was something worse: recognition. Because whoever Anonymous B was, they weren't wrong. That was the obscenity of it. Ten years ago he'd have written that exact letter himself, back when he still believed feeling was a thing you could afford. Back before the accident taught him what feelings cost.

And beneath the fury, faint as bioluminescence in a wake, was a third thing he refused to name: the letter had made him *feel*. Not much. Just a spark. But sparks were how fires started, and he was a man who lived on an island made entirely of dry wood.

He sat down and wrote a reply before he could stop himself, pen pressing hard enough to emboss the page.

*B,*

*You're right that I don't believe in this program. You're wrong about nearly everything else.*

*Numbers don't fight, you say. Come to a hatching beach at dawn and tell the eighty percent survival rate that counting is passive. Every nest I locate gets screened against predators. Every disorientation risk gets mapped and mitigated. Your kind — lawyers, I assume, since you write like someone billing by the adjective — prefer grand gestures and press releases. I prefer hatchlings that actually reach the water.*

*You want honesty? Here it is: contempt delivered anonymously is cowardice with good vocabulary.*

*— A*

He read it over once, felt the strange electric satisfaction of having meant every word, and sealed it. It was the most alive he'd felt in months, possibly years, and he distrusted that completely.

* * *

Three days later, the second letter came, and with it, the beginning of the end of his peace.

*B again. Cowardice with good vocabulary — I'm having that framed. Let's establish terms, since you clearly need rules to function: I'll match your bluntness and raise you specificity. This season I'm advising a coastal committee reviewing development permits near a critical nesting habitat. The developer's name is Halvorsen, and if you know that name, then you know why I don't sleep. If your little island is anywhere in his crosshairs, you should be losing sleep too. Tell me where you are. Or don't, and keep being safe. Safety looks good on you, I imagine. Like a cage fits a bird.*

Halvorsen.

The name landed in Orsen's chest like an anchor dropped through thin ice. Halvorsen Development Group. The permits. The consortium's funding renewal — contingent, the paperwork had mentioned in passing, on "cooperation with regional stakeholder initiatives." He'd skimmed that clause the way he skimmed everything that wasn't data.

And the writing itself — God, the *writing*. The scalpel precision of it. The way the sentences leaned forward like they were about to cross-examine him. He'd heard this voice before, out loud, four years ago, in a conference room in Charleston, when a young conservation attorney with storm-grey eyes had stood up in front of the Gulf Coast Marine Trust and dismantled his funding proposal piece by piece, arguing that his sanctuary's methodology was "quantitative theater" — that he'd turned conservation into accounting and called the ledger compassion.

Wren Calloway. He'd hated her for an hour and then, in a way he'd never admitted to anyone, including himself, he'd thought about her for a year.

She'd lost that fight — his funding survived — but she'd won something else, something he'd carried out of the room like a splinter working toward his heart: the suspicion that she might be right.

Now she was Anonymous B. Now the consortium — whether by cosmic joke or clerical error or deliberate cruelty — had wired him directly to the one person alive who could see straight through everything he'd built.

He should report the conflict of interest. Request reassignment. Contain it.

Instead, Orsen Hartwell sat down at his desk under the solar lamp, pulled out fresh paper, and wrote:

*Calloway.*

Just the name. No salutation, no signature. He stared at it for a long moment, heart doing something uncontainable, and then added:

*You want to know where I am? I'm on the island Halvorsen wants. And if you're on that committee, then next month you'll be here too — the consortium named us co-leads of the nesting-season response. Check your calendar. Bring your cage metaphors. I'll bring the data you love to hate.*

*Let's see who breaks first.*

He sealed it, and out past the breakers, unseen in the dark, the first nest of the season began to stir.
