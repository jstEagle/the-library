## 3. Nell Asks a Question

The thing about my dad's desk is that it's not really a desk. It's a lifeboat.

That's what I decided when I was maybe eight, the first time I climbed up into the flat above the shop while he was downstairs ringing up some tourist who wanted three copies of the same lighthouse book for reasons I still don't understand. The flat is small — kitchen, bathroom, his room, my room, and the little landing where he keeps the desk that used to be our dining table before we decided eating at a counter was easier. The desk faces the window, and the window faces the harbor, and everything on the desk faces away from me like it's shy.

There's a lamp with a green glass shade he won't replace even though the switch sticks. There's a mug of pens that are all dead except one. There's a stack of invoices held down by a piece of sea glass the size of a plum, frosted white, which I found on Crescent Beach when I was six and gave him because it looked like a moon that had given up.

And there's the paper pinned to the wall above the desk.

I'd seen it a hundred times without seeing it. That's the trick of living somewhere — your eyes develop calluses. The paper had gone the color of weak tea, soft at the corners, one pin holding it up by its top edge so it hung forward slightly, like something tired of standing. It was a ferry schedule. Merrow's Reach to Harborton, printed in that gray-blue ink the ferry company uses, times listed down the left side in a column: 6:15, 8:40, 11:05, 2:30, 4:40, 7:15.

But it wasn't this year's schedule. It wasn't any schedule from my lifetime, actually, though I didn't know that yet. What I knew, the afternoon I finally saw it — really saw it — was that someone had circled one of the times in pen. The 4:40. And next to the circle, in handwriting I knew better than my own, because it was the handwriting on every birthday card and every note in my lunchbox and every margin of every book he'd ever read to me, was a date.

September 14th.

No year. Just the day and month, like a birthday for somebody who wasn't born.

* * *

I want to explain something about how my brain works, because Dad says I get it from him, which is funny, because he also says I get my stubbornness from "the other side," whatever that means, and you can't get both from the same place unless the same place is just him being contradictory.

My brain does this thing where once it notices something, it can't unnotice it. Marisol at the post office calls it "the Nell effect." She says I'm like a hermit crab that's decided your shoe is a shell now, good luck getting it back. Once I fix on a question, I carry it around until I find an answer or the question wears through and falls apart. Mostly they wear through. Mostly questions about Dad fall apart, because Dad has this talent — and I mean *talent*, like a magician's talent, like something he's practiced — for answering a question without answering it.

Example. When I was nine I asked him why we don't have a car. He said, "Because the island doesn't believe in them," which sounds like an answer but isn't, because obviously the island believes in cars, Mrs. Pruitt has one, it's just not allowed on the ferry more than twice a year. When I was ten I asked him why he never watches the news. He said, "Because the news is just weather with worse manners." When I was eleven I asked him where my other parent was, the way kids in books ask it, casual, over cereal, and he said — and I've turned this sentence over so many times it's gone smooth as beach glass — he said:

"Some people are tides, Nell. They come into a harbor, and then the water pulls them back out, and it isn't anybody's fault. It's just what water does."

And then he ruffled my hair, which is his escape hatch, and went downstairs to open the shop, and I sat there with my cereal going soggy thinking: that's not an answer, that's a *poem*, and poems are what people use when the truth would take too long.

But here's the thing about the tide answer. It worked. It worked for years. Because I was a kid, and kids accept poetry the way they accept magic — gratefully, without auditing it. It was only this September, age twelve, standing in front of his desk with the light coming in sideways off the harbor, that I looked at the schedule and thought: tides come back. That's the whole deal with tides. They leave and they *come back*. If she was a tide, where's the return?

* * *

Let me back up to the actual afternoon, because I'm doing the thing Dad does, narrating in circles instead of arriving.

It was the third Saturday of September, which meant inventory, which meant Dad spent the whole day downstairs counting books and I had the flat to myself, which at twelve is basically a national holiday. My plan was: make tea I wasn't allowed to have after four o'clock, eat the emergency biscuits, and read on his bed because his window gets the last of the sun. Innocent plan. Boring plan. A plan with no hermit crabs in it anywhere.

Except when I went to open his curtains — he keeps them half-drawn even in daytime, another thing I never questioned until I started questioning everything — I had to stand right in front of the desk, and the schedule hung there at eye level, tea-colored, sagging off its pin, and the sun came through the window behind it and lit it up like a museum exhibit.

I read it. All of it. The times. The little ferry company logo, a wave inside a circle. The fine print about bicycles and livestock. And the circle around 4:40, and the date beside it in his handwriting, pressed hard enough that the pen had nearly torn the paper: *Sept 14.*

I did the math standing there. I'm good at math, which nobody in my family will admit to, since Dad counts on his fingers and pretends it's charming. Eleven Septembers ago. I am twelve. Which means the date on that paper is older than me by about a year. Which means my dad pinned a ferry schedule to his wall before I existed, circled a boat, wrote down a day, and then never took it down. Through eleven years of dustings and repaintings and the time the roof leaked and soaked half the flat. Through all of it, that paper stayed, hanging there like a door he kept meaning to walk through.

Kids aren't supposed to notice things like that. But here's what grown-ups forget: we're short, not blind. We spend our whole lives looking at the parts of rooms you stop looking at. We see the backs of radiators and the undersides of tables and the papers pinned above desks. You build your whole mystery up high where you think we can't reach, and meanwhile it's hanging at exactly our eye level, waiting.

I stood there for a long time. Long enough that my tea went cold, which for the record I drank anyway.

And then — because I'm my father's daughter, whatever that means, whichever side that comes from — I did what he does. I put the question in my pocket and carried it around.

* * *

I carried it for two weeks.

Two weeks of watching him differently. Not suspiciously — I want to be clear about that, because when I tell this story later, to Marisol, she'll say *you were spying on him*, and I'll say no, spying is when you want to catch somebody. This was the opposite. I wanted to *not* catch him. I wanted him to turn around and say, oh, that old thing, it's nothing, here's the boring explanation, and I could hand the question back and go back to being a kid whose father answers things with poems because poems are easier.

So I watched the way you watch weather. Small things.

He still made two cups of coffee every morning. He drinks one. The second sits on the counter until it goes cold and then he pours it out, every single day, without ever appearing to notice he's done it. I asked him about it once, months ago, and he said "habit," and I said whose habit, and he laughed and changed the subject, and I let him, because I was ten and the question hadn't worn through yet.

He always knows when the ferry's coming before it rounds the point. Not from the schedule — the real ones, the current ones, live on his phone like everyone else's. He just knows. He'll be mid-sentence shelving paperbacks and he'll say, "Boat's early today," and ninety seconds later it appears, and I used to think it was a superpower. Now I think it might be a wound. There's a difference between watching the water because you love it and watching it because you're waiting for it to give something back.

And once — this is the one that got me — once, I found him standing at the shop window with a paperback in his hands, not reading it, just holding it open at the same page, and when I came in he closed it so fast and so naturally that if I hadn't been watching for exactly that kind of motion I'd have missed it. The book was called something about harbors. I checked the shelf later. He'd reshelved it spine-in, backwards, which he never does, because he says books should face the world.

You face the world or you don't, Dad. Pick one.

* * *

I asked him on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays are slow and slow days make people honest, or at least slower at lying.

He was at the desk upstairs, doing the accounts, which mostly involved him sighing at the calculator like it had personally wronged him. I pulled the spare chair over — the wobbly one, the one that means *this conversation might take a while* — and sat down across from him, and he glanced up with the expression he gets when I bring home a permission slip, which is love and dread in equal parts.

"Whose chair is that," he said. "That's the interrogation chair."

"It's a chair."

"That's the chair you sit in when you want something."

"I don't want anything." I nodded past his shoulder, casual, so casual it probably looked like a neck injury. "What's that paper?"

I watched him not look at it. That's the part I'll remember longest, I think. He didn't look. His eyes went to the window, to the calculator, to his tea, everywhere the paper wasn't, the way you don't look at the one loose stair. Eleven years of practice moving through that room without ever letting his eyes land on that wall, and I watched him do it in real time, and something in my chest went cold and small.

"Ferry schedule," he said.

"Yeah, but it's old."

"Is it?"

"Dad. The ferries don't run at 4:40 anymore. Nothing runs at 4:40. I looked."

He set down his pen. Here's the thing about my dad: he's handsome in a rumpled way, gray coming in early at the temples, hands always smelling faintly of paper and glue from the repairs he does on old stock. When he's about to deflect, he does this thing where he takes off his glasses even though he's wearing contacts half the time — no wait, that's not right, he doesn't wear contacts, he wears the glasses, and when he's about to deflect he *cleans* them, slow, both lenses, giving himself eight full seconds to compose a poem.

He cleaned his glasses.

"You know what a neap tide is?" he said.

"No."

"Course you do, I've told you."

"You've told me a lot of things."

That landed harder than I meant it to. His hands stopped on the glasses. For a second — one real second, I saw it — his face did something unguarded, like a shutter catching wind. Then it closed again and he smiled, and the smile was warm and true and completely beside the point.

"A neap tide," he said, "happens when the sun and the moon are pulling at right angles to each other. Neither one wins. So the water barely moves. High tides that hardly rise, low tides that hardly fall. The sea just sort of... holds its breath." He hung the glasses back on his collar. "Some days are neap tides, Nell. You keep a schedule around to remind yourself the water's still there, even when it's not going anywhere."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a very good answer."

"It's a poem."

"Nell—"

"Why do you keep it?" I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted, and I hated that, so I pushed it bigger. "Eleven years, Dad. You circled a boat. You wrote a date. Whose date? Who was on the 4:40?"

And here is what happened, and I need to write it down exactly, because exactness matters, Marisol says so, Marisol who has run the post office for thirty years and says the whole truth of a town lives in its exact details.

My dad looked at me. Really looked, the way he almost never does, like I'd grown a foot and he was recalibrating. And his mouth opened — I swear it opened, there was a word coming, I saw the shape of it start in his chest — and then the phone rang downstairs. The shop phone, the old bell one, jangling up through the floorboards like a fire alarm.

He stood up so fast the chair skidded.

"That'll be the distributor," he said. "Hold that thought."

"I've been holding it two weeks."

"I know," he said, already at the stairs. And then, over his shoulder, with a smile that was trying so hard to be light that it broke a little in the middle: "Tides, Nell. Everything's tides."

His footsteps went down. The bell phone got answered. His voice went into its shop voice, bright and easy, the one for tourists and distributors and everyone who isn't me.

I sat alone at the desk, in the interrogation chair, under the tea-colored paper. The 4:40. *Sept 14.* The pen-press so hard it had nearly torn through, like whoever wrote it had been angry, or scared, or both, and had needed the date to stay.

Everything's tides, he said.

But I'd started paying attention to the actual water that summer, walking the harbor wall after school, and I knew something my dad's poem left out. Tides don't just move water. Tides move *everything*. They lift boats off mudflats that have been stuck for hours. They carry in things from far away — glass, wood, messages, people — and set them down on shores that had given up expecting anything.

And sometimes, if you watch long enough, the water starts rising whether anybody circled a schedule or not.

Downstairs, Dad laughed at something the distributor said. Upstairs, I reached out and touched the corner of the paper, just once, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you weren't sure was friendly.

Two days later, Marisol put a notice in the post office window, and by Friday the whole island was talking about it, and nobody — not me, not Dad, not even Marisol, who knows everything — saw what was coming.

The ferry route was being decommissioned. Next season, there would be no 4:40 at all.
