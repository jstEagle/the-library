## 4. Decommissioned

The notice went up on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Bad news should arrive on Fridays, Jonah thought, so you could at least drink through the weekend before facing it. Instead it was Tuesday morning and Marisol had taped a single sheet of paper to the post office window, and by noon the whole island had read it twice and memorized it once.

*Notice of Route Review: Merrow's Reach–Mainland Service. Public consultation meeting, Thursday, 7 p.m., Harbour Hall.*

Route review. The mainland had a genius for language that said everything by saying nothing. Nobody reviewed a route they intended to keep.

Jonah read it standing in his own doorway with a mug of coffee going cold in his hand. Behind him, Nell was at the counter doing her homework, pencil moving in that fast, certain way of hers, and he found himself angling his body slightly, the way you do when you want to shield someone from weather that hasn't arrived yet.

"What's it say?" she asked without looking up. Twelve years old and she could feel him reading over things now, some new sense she'd grown that autumn.

"Council stuff," he said. "Ferry business."

"Is it bad ferry business?"

"It's ferry business," he said, and heard how little that answered, and hated himself for it, and did it anyway.

* * *

Harbour Hall had been built to hold two hundred people and an optimism nobody on the island had ever possessed. On Thursday night there were three hundred of them, folding chairs dragged in from the church hall and the fire station, standing bodies along the walls, old Duncan MacRae's dog lying across the doorway like a furry barricade. The smell was wet wool and instant coffee and the particular electricity of a crowd that has already decided to be angry.

The mainland sent two people: a man named Prentice from the transport authority, grey suit, grey voice, and a younger woman who took notes on a laptop and never looked up. They sat at a trestle table under the hall's one working radiator while Councillor Ada Fenn stood beside them holding a microphone that crackled every time she breathed.

Prentice began with slides. That was the first mistake. He put up charts showing ridership figures declining over eleven years — eleven years, Jonah noted, as if the number itself were neutral — and cost-per-crossing calculations, and a projection line that bent downward like a gull diving.

"Seasonal demand simply doesn't sustain year-round service," Prentice said. "The route has been subsidized for—"

"Subsidized." Old Nan Corrigan, eighty-one, from the third row, in a voice that carried the way fog carries. "My husband built that slipway with his hands. My son drives the supply truck. You'll tell me about *subsidized*."

"I appreciate the history of the community—"

"You appreciate nothing. You've been here forty minutes."

There was laughter at that, hard and glad, and Jonah felt the room gather itself around Nan like a tide coming in. He sat near the back, as he always did, in the chair by the door that let him leave first if he needed to. He'd been leaving first for eleven years. It was practically a skill.

Ada Fenn tried to steer. "What the community wants to understand, Mr. Prentice, is what happens after this season. To medical transfers. To school kids sitting exams on the mainland. To freight."

"There will be a transition period," Prentice said. "A helicopter charter arrangement is being explored for emergencies—"

"A *helicopter*," someone called out, and the word went around the hall like something thrown.

Jonah watched it all with the strange double vision he'd developed since the flood — since the basement, since the water-stained envelope currently hidden inside a first edition of *Treasure Island* on the shop's middle shelf, spine cracked, waiting for him the way unexploded ordnance waits. He watched the island rage, and he raged too, quietly, in the place where he kept all his loudest feelings. But underneath the anger ran another current entirely, cold and private and shameful:

*The last ferry means the last chance.*

Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't stop doing, even while Prentice droned about seasonal variance. If the route ended, Wren Okafor would fly away from the mainland job she'd surely have by then, and the island would be reachable only by helicopter charter and weather, and whatever window had opened — however narrow, however terrifying — would close like water over a stone.

He should have wanted the ferry saved for Nell. For the school runs, the hospital runs, the ordinary dignity of not being cut off. And he did want that; God, he did. But if he was honest — and honesty had been arriving lately in small, unwelcome installments, like letters surfacing from a flooded basement — part of him understood that the fight in this room tonight was also, secretly, about a sealed envelope and a sentence he'd rehearsed for a decade and never once delivered.

The deadline had walked into Harbour Hall wearing a grey suit.

* * *

It got worse before it got better, the way meetings do.

A fisherman named Callum Brodie stood up and gave a speech about islanders being sacrificed again, forgotten again, and half the room thumped chairs. A woman from the farm co-op asked pointed questions about livestock freight that Prentice answered with the phrase "logistical solutions," which caused actual booing. Then Ada Fenn said, "Perhaps we should hear from residents directly," and the microphone began its slow journey down the rows.

Jonah knew the microphone. The microphone was a trap with a foam cover. In eleven years of island meetings he had spoken perhaps four times, always about bookshop zoning, always briefly, always in the past tense where possible.

The mic reached Nan Corrigan again. "I've buried a husband and a son on this rock," she said, "and I'll tell you what keeps the living alive out here. It's not the mainland's money. It's knowing there's a boat. Knowing you're not alone. You take the boat away, you might as well tell us we've drowned already and just haven't noticed."

More chair-thumping. The dog barked once, decisively.

And then, three rows ahead, a figure stood up that made Jonah's whole body go still.

Wren Okafor.

She'd been back nine days. Nine days of interim piloting, nine days of Jonah arranging his mornings around crossing times he didn't need to know, and here she was at a public meeting taking the microphone with the same unhurried grip she'd used on a wheelhouse throttle, and the hall quieting for her the way it hadn't quieted for anyone all night.

"I fly this route," she said. "Interim. Since the ninth." Her voice was low and even, the kind of calm that makes other people calmer or much less calm depending on their guilt. "I want to say what the numbers don't show. This route isn't seasonal. It's structural. Take it away and you don't lose a convenience, you lose the island's pulse. People plan lives around crossings. Babies get born around crossings. Letters—" and here, absurdly, impossibly, her eyes moved through the crowd and passed over Jonah without stopping, though he felt them pass like a hand over a flame, "—letters get posted around crossings."

The hall murmured. Prentice shifted papers.

"So my question is simple," Wren finished. "When you decommission a route, who signs the order? Because I'd like that person to come stand on the dock in February and explain it to the faces."

She handed the mic back. She sat down. She did not look at Jonah again, and Jonah sat in his chair by the door with his heart going like a bilge pump and understood two things with total clarity.

First: she was magnificent, and he had spent eleven years telling himself a story in which walking away from her had been some kind of mercy, and the story collapsed completely in the time it took her to say *letters get posted around crossings*.

Second: she didn't know. About the letter. About any of it. And every day the route survived was a day the letter stayed possible, and every day it died was a day it didn't, and either way he was going to have to become, at long last, a man who did things instead of a man who almost did them.

* * *

Afterward the hall emptied slowly, the way grief empties. Ada Fenn worked the crowd, collecting names for a petition. Prentice and the laptop woman escaped to their car like people leaving a dentist's. Outside, the harbour lay black and still, the ferry tied up at the slip with its deck lights on, and Wren stood alone at the rail end of the dock looking at the water.

Jonah could have gone home. Home was four hundred metres away and Nell was at Marisol's doing homework-with-supervision, an arrangement that had more to do with Marisol's biscuits than supervision. He could go home and shelve books and not speak to anyone, which was his native language.

He walked onto the dock instead.

Not all the way. Halfway. Close enough to be heard, far enough to retreat. Eleven years of practice in one stride length.

"Good speech," he said.

Wren turned. Deck light caught the side of her face. Up close she looked tired in the specific way pilots look tired, weather and hours and responsibility layered like sediment.

"Didn't expect you to come," she said. "You avoid these things."

"I came."

"So I see." A pause with eleven years inside it. "How's the shop?"

"Flooded in September. Recovering." He heard himself performing normalcy and couldn't stop. "You?"

"Flying a dying route." She looked past him at the ferry. "They'll kill it, you know. Doesn't matter what anyone said tonight. Committees like that arrive with the decision already made; the meeting's just anesthesia."

"Probably."

"Probably." She turned back to him then, and her eyes were steady and unreadable and it cost him something enormous to hold them. "Funny thing, though. Everyone in that hall fighting for the boat. Like it's the beating heart of the place." A beat. "Some things left this island a long time ago and nobody fought for those at all."

The words landed exactly where she aimed them, or maybe she wasn't aiming at anything, maybe he was just standing in the one spot on earth where every stray bullet had his name on it.

"Wren—"

"Don't." Quiet. Not angry. Worse than angry — settled. "It's late, Jonah. I've got a 6:40 sailing."

She walked past him toward the boat, and the distance between them closed and then reopened, and he stood on the dock in the dark listening to her boots on the planks and thinking: *the last ferry means the last chance.* The season would end. The route would die. And somewhere between now and the final sailing there was a window, narrow as a lock gate, and he was going to have to decide whether he was a man who stood on docks watching things leave, or a man who got on them.

Behind him, up the hill, the lights of Tidewrack Books burned in the front window, and somewhere in its middle shelf a first edition held its breath along with him.

He started up the hill, and the notice paper flapped on the post office board behind him — *route review, public consultation, transition period* — civic language for a countdown, though only he seemed to hear it ticking.
